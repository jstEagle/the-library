## 9. Storm Warning

The barometer began its descent on a Tuesday, which I only mention because Tuesdays were the days the supply boat ran, and there is a particular cruelty in watching your last link to the mainland lower itself into the sea on schedule.

I stood at the kitchen window with the glass in my hand — the instrument, not the window, though I was tempted by the second — and watched the needle slide down through *Change* without so much as a pause for breath. Below it went *Rain*, below that *Much Rain*, and then into territory marked only by numbers, as if the maker had run out of words for what came after.

"Storm coming," Alistair said from his chair by the stove. He didn't look up from the compass card he was burnishing, a circle of brass no wider than a supper plate. He'd been working it for three days now, patient as erosion, and he had begun to let me do parts of it, which was either trust or surrender or simply an old man's hands tiring. With Alistair you rarely got to know which.

"I can see that."

"Then why are you asking the barometer?"

"Because the barometer doesn't argue with me."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh in a younger man, or in a man built differently, and went back to his polishing. Outside, the sea had gone the color of tarnished pewter, and the gulls had stopped their complaining and left altogether. Gulls know. They'd known since Monday, probably, wheeling out over the water and then vanishing inland in long ragged lines, sensible creatures that they are. Only we and the lighthouse stayed.

The supply boat was due at eleven. At eleven-fifteen I was still at the window, and at eleven-thirty young Mr. Fenn brought the *Merrow's Gift* around the headland just far enough for me to see that she was riding light — running low on coal, running low on everything, because the storms of November had eaten our stores and the mainland had sent half of what we'd asked for — and then she turned about.

She never even dropped her dinghy. Fenn stood at the rail and cupped his hands and shouted something the wind took apart before it reached me. I raised one arm so he'd know I'd seen him, seen the turning, understood. Then the *Merrow's Gift* fell away south and was gone behind the point, and I stood in the kitchen doing arithmetic.

Coal: perhaps two weeks if I rationed the lamp generously and us meanly. Flour, enough. Oil for the light itself — enough, barely, if the winter behaved, which it had already shown no intention of doing. Salt fish, potatoes, a ham Alistair had brought ashore in December like a man who knew something. We would not starve. But we would be alone, truly alone, cut off until the weather broke or the bridge reopened, whichever came last.

"They turned back," I told him.

"I saw." He set down his cloth. "How long?"

"The glass says three days. The glass has been wrong before. It says things like *change* when what it means is *the end of the world as you arranged it*."

"That's very nearly poetry, Petra."

"My husband wrote poetry. Look how that ended."

The words were out before I could weigh them, and they landed between us heavier than I meant. Alistair's hands stilled on the table. For a moment — I have thought about this moment many times since — his mouth opened, and something rose in his face like water coming up a well. His eyes went to the workshop door, then to me, then down to his own knuckles, white where they gripped the table edge.

"Alistair?" I said. "What is it?"

And the water sank again. He cleared his throat. "Nothing. Only that Dominic did write bad poetry. Terrible stuff. About the moon, mostly. His mother kept every page." He gathered up his cloth and his brass and his patience and said, "We should batten down," and walked out into the wind before I could ask him anything else.

That was the first time.

* * *

Battening down is a liturgy, like everything else in a keeper's life. Shutters, hatches, lashings on the boat and the woodpile and the rain barrels. Every object on a headland must be either fastened down or resigned to God, and the trick of a long life on the coast is knowing which is which.

We worked through the afternoon while the wind climbed behind us like a debt accruing interest. Alistair took the shed and the workshop; I took the tower and the house. Tomas, who is ten and therefore useful in exactly the ways nobody plans for, carried lines and cleats and asked questions the whole time, questions with the relentless logic of a boy who has decided that fear is boring.

"Why do the shutters go on the seaward side first?"

"Because the sea comes there first."

"But the wind comes from everywhere."

"The wind hasn't decided yet. Give it till midnight."

"Will the workshop roof hold? Papa said it wanted new beams."

I stopped with a cleat in my hand. "When did he say that?"

Tomas shrugged, hauling his line. "Last autumn. Before. He said the roof was older than Grandfather and twice as stubborn, and that we'd fix it in spring." He looked up at me with Dominic's own grey eyes, which felt less like inheritance than like ambush. "Spring never came, though. Did it."

"No," I said. "It didn't. Tie that off tighter."

By dusk the sky in the northwest had gone a strange bruised yellow-green, the color the old fishermen call *the sky thinking*. The swell was coming in longer and deeper, sets of waves arriving like messengers ahead of an army, each one larger than the last, and when they struck the headland the whole cliff rang underfoot. I have lived on this rock my entire married life and most of my girlhood, and I will tell you the truth about great storms: they do not arrive. They announce themselves, sometimes for days, and you live inside the announcement.

Supper was salt fish and potatoes and the last of the apples, which Alistair divided with a surgeon's fairness. Tomas ate and talked and was sent to bed without much protest, being tired from hauling line all afternoon, and then the house held two people and a silence.

Alistair sat looking at the fire. I sat looking at him looking at the fire. This had become, over the weeks of our enforced alliance, a kind of conversation.

"The light," he said finally. "You'll keep it through it all."

"I always do."

"In '81 the keeper at Marrow Head didn't. Went down to secure his boat and the stairs flooded behind him. Light went dark four hours. Two ships lost on the shoals that night." He said it flatly, a man reciting inventory. "Fourteen drowned."

"You've told me this before."

"Because I want you to understand that it matters. That night matters. Lights matter." His voice had acquired a strange urgency, entirely out of proportion to my competence, which has never been in question. "Petra. If anything should—" He stopped. His jaw worked. He looked at his hands, those precise old craftsman's hands, and I watched him decide something and then un-decide it in the space of a breath. "If anything should happen to the lamp mechanism tonight. The spare parts are in the green chest. Marked."

"There's nothing wrong with the mechanism."

"There's nothing wrong with a great many things," he said, "until suddenly there is," and he wished me good night and went up to the loft bed like a man climbing toward a verdict.

I sat by the dying fire a long while after, listening to the wind begin its work on the shutters, and turned over what he'd almost said. *If anything should happen.* Happen to the lamp? To the light? Or to whatever it was he carried, that thing I had come to feel in him the way you feel a stone in your shoe — not large, but present with every step.

That was the second time. Though I did not yet understand it as the second time; I only counted one.

* * *

The storm arrived at midnight the way debts arrive — all at once and worse than expected, despite every warning.

I logged it, because logging is prayer in my church: wind rising through the small hours from southeast, shifting east by two o'clock, gusts by dawn that the anemometer couldn't properly credit. By morning the world outside the glass was a gray chaos in which the sea and the sky had dissolved their ancient border dispute and merged into a single howling element. The headland took each wave with a concussion I felt in my teeth. Spray went over the lamp room, sixty feet up, and the tower hummed like a struck bell.

We kept to the house. There was nothing else to keep to.

Tomas, to his credit, was more fascinated than frightened, though I caught him once at the window slit with his face gone pale, watching a wave explode against the rocks below. He didn't speak of it. Boys learn early here to swallow fear whole, and I hate that he learns it, and I am proud of him for it, and both of these feelings can occupy a mother at once without consulting her.

Alistair and I fell into the rhythm of storm days: checking lashings between squalls when it was safe to open a door, keeping the stove fed, minding the boy. And in the gaps, because grief and dread abhor a vacuum, we worked on the compass. He'd carried the card and the tools up to the kitchen table, and there under the lamp we bent over brass while the house shook around us.

"Lighter strokes," he said, guiding my wrist. "You're engraving, not defending yourself."

"I hadn't noticed any difference in my life lately."

"Humor me. Long, even cuts. Let the tool find its line." His hand over mine was light and dry and steady, and I thought, absurdly, *so this is what a father's guidance feels like*, and put the thought away where I keep the ones that cost too much to look at.

We worked. The wind screamed at the north shutter. Tomas read aloud from his schoolbook, doing all the voices, until the lamp guttered and we let him stop.

And somewhere past noon, with Tomas asleep on the settle and the storm at full voice, Alistair set down his graver and looked at the compass card for a long time, and said, quietly, as if continuing a conversation I hadn't heard the beginning of:

"He came to me in September. Before the crossing."

My hand stopped. The wind filled the pause.

"Came to you," I repeated.

"To the shop on the mainland. Stayed two days. He had papers with him. Questions." Alistair's thumb moved along the edge of the brass card, back and forth, a man soothing himself with an object. "He asked me things about the firm. About Harrigan Brothers Shipping. Whether I remembered certain voyages. Certain nights." His voice was level, careful, a man crossing ice, testing each step. "I told him the past was the past. I told him—"

He stopped. Utterly. As if a door had swung shut inside him and locked. He looked at me then with an expression I had never seen on that proud old face — not guilt exactly, nor fear, but the terrible arithmetic of a man weighing what truth costs weighed against what silence costs, and finding the sums won't resolve.

"Yes?" I said. My heart was behaving badly. "You told him what?"

The wind took the house then, a gust like a fist flat against the seaward wall, and the lamp flame bent sideways, and in the confusion of shadow Alistair rose, collected his coat, and said, "I'm going to check the shed lashings," which was madness, no sane person went out in that, and went out into it anyway, and I sat alone with a half-engraved compass card and the absolute certainty that I had just watched a confession drown.

* * *

He came back soaked and half-frozen and would accept no sympathy, which suited me, as I had none to offer. I gave him tea instead and sat across from him and studied him as I might study a chart of waters I'd sailed a hundred times and suddenly found unsounded.

Two near-confessions in two days. A man does not nearly confess by accident, the way a clock does not nearly chime. Something was working its way up in him, pressure seeking release, and the storm outside was only the visible half of it. I thought of the ledgers in the workshop with their payments from a shipping firm I'd never heard of. I thought of Dominic, two days ashore on the mainland in September, asking his father about certain voyages, certain nights. I thought of the compass commissioned by strangers, wrapped in oilcloth, unfinished for over a year.

And I thought: *my husband did not drown. Not merely drown. Something happened in September, and something happened in November, and this old man knows the shape of both and is holding the knowledge the way you hold your breath.*

I did not press him. Pressing Alistair Kavanagh is like pressing a knot; the wood only tightens. But I changed my arithmetic. Whatever bargain we had struck — finish the compass, then part forever — had been made between two people who each believed they knew what the compass was. I no longer knew what it was. I suspected he had known all along.

Evening came down black and early. I lit the lamp, and its beam swung out into the murk and was swallowed, and I watched it go with the particular ache I carry every night: that I keep a light to bring strangers home safe, in a world that had let my own husband drown within sight of one.

At nine o'clock, with Tomas asleep and the storm still hammering, Alistair appeared at the foot of the tower stairs in his nightshirt and coat, holding a candle, looking ten years older than he had at supper.

"Petra." His voice was strange. "There's something you need to know about Dominic. About why the compass—"

The light went out.

Not the candle — the great lamp above us, the light itself. Its beam, which had been sweeping past the stairwell windows in its patient rhythm, stopped sweeping. Darkness crashed down complete, and beneath it came the sound every keeper knows and no keeper forgets: machinery failing, metal meeting metal somewhere it must never meet, the clockwork drive seizing with a shriek that traveled down the tower's bones into the floor.

Above us, in the dark where the light had been, something heavy settled and groaned.

Alistair set his candle down very slowly, and when he spoke again, the confession was gone from his face as if it had never been there, replaced by something older and simpler.

"The green chest," he said. "Come on."
