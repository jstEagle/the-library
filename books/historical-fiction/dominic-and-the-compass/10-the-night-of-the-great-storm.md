## 10. The Night of the Great Storm

### Petra

The barometer had been falling all day, the way a man falls down stairs — slowly at first, then all at once. By noon the glass read lower than I'd seen it in eleven years on the headland, and by two o'clock the sea had turned that particular shade of green-grey that means it has stopped being scenery and become an adversary.

I had kept lights through storms before. Dominic and I had kept our first one together three weeks after our wedding, and I had learned then that a gale is not a single event but an argument — the wind states its position, the sea contradicts it, the tower holds its opinion in the middle, trembling. You do not fight a storm. You outlast it, the way you outlast a long winter or a difficult relative.

We had both under one roof now, so there was no outlasting anything.

"Should we move the workshop things?" Alistair asked, standing at the kitchen window with his hands behind his back as if reviewing troops he did not trust.

"The roof's held through worse."

"Has it."

It was not a question, and we both knew it wasn't, because the truth was that the roof had been mended twice since Dominic died and once badly, by me, with whatever the supply boat brought and my own stubbornness supplied. I said none of this. "Bring the ledgers and the tools up," I said instead. "The card especially. The brass can be replaced. The work cannot."

He went without argument, which frightened me more than the barometer. Alistair argued the way other men breathed; when he fell quiet, it meant he had looked at something and found it worth worrying about. He came back up the rock path with the oilcloth bundle pressed to his chest like an infant, and behind him the first squall came off the sea in a white sheet and erased the bridge from the world entirely. Not that there was a bridge to erase. There hadn't been since October. But the sea erased the place where it had been, and somehow that felt deliberate, as if the water were reminding us what we were to each other now: everything.

By four o'clock we had shuttered the kitchen windows, hauled Tomas's cot into the passage — the safest room in any tower is the one with the most stone around it — and lit the stove. Tomas himself was a study in suppressed excitement, the way boys are about storms, wanting the drama and dreading it in equal measure.

"Will the light go out?" he asked me.

"The light does not go out," I said. "That's rather the entire point of me."

He grinned, and I kissed the top of his head, and sent him to the passage with a book he would not read, and went up to the lamp room to begin the night.

I will say this for terror: it simplifies the mind wonderfully.

* * *

### Alistair

I have watched weather for seventy years and I had never watched anything like that.

It came in off the Atlantic not as wind but as a solid thing, a moving wall of water and air with a sound beneath it like a train crossing an iron bridge that never ends. The tower took the first blow and leaned — I felt it lean, a slow sickening tilt and recovery, like a man catching himself on a table — and then stood, and I understood in my bones why my son had loved this place and why it had killed him anyway.

Petra went up to the lamp room at dusk. Through the hour that followed, the light swept overhead every few seconds, that great patient wheel of it, and below me the world dissolved. Rain moved horizontally. The sea came over the headland in sheets, finding the rocks it had wanted for ten thousand years.

I sat in the passage with my grandson asleep against my shoulder — he had fought sleep and lost around nine o'clock, mid-sentence, in the way of children who cannot admit exhaustion — and I listened to the tower speak. It groaned. Stone should not groan. And beneath the groaning, the workshop below us made sounds I did not like at all: creaks, and then cracks, and then, somewhere past midnight, a long tearing roar that I felt through the floor before I heard it.

The roof. Gone, or going.

I laid Tomas down and went to the foot of the lamp room stairs and shouted up into the din.

"Petra!"

Nothing. The wind ate my voice.

I climbed. The lamp room in a storm is a lesson in humility — the glass trembles in its frames, the whole lantern seems to breathe, and the noise is beyond describing, a sound you feel in your teeth. She was at the clockwork, winding the rotation mechanism by lamplight, sleeves rolled, hair coming loose in dark ropes across her face. She looked at me and read the news off my face before I spoke, because she had learned to read faces during a year when no one would speak plainly to her.

"How bad?" she shouted.

"Gone, or nearly. The north end at least."

"The ledgers?"

"In the passage. Safe. The boy's asleep."

She nodded, wound the last quarter-turn, checked that the weight was descending true, and then did the thing that I have thought about every day since. She took off her lighthouse apron, hung it on its peg — hung it up, neatly, in a hurricane, because order was how she prayed — and said:

"Then come down and help me save the rest of it."

"You can't leave the light—"

"The clockwork runs ninety minutes on a full wind," she said. "I've left it before in emergencies and it has never once failed me. The workshop will not wait ninety minutes."

* * *

### Petra

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it keeps a ledger of its own. Every object tied to the dead person carries a small charge, and you learn to handle them carefully, the way you handle charged glass. Dominic's chisels. Dominic's bench. Dominic's coat still on its hook because I could not decide whether a coat counted as something to keep or something to bury.

So when Alistair and I came down the spiral stair together and I saw the workshop door bowing inward under the wind, and beyond it the north half of the roof lifting like a lid being prised off a tin, I felt the ledger open itself, every entry at once.

We went in anyway.

The wind had found the gap where the roof met the wall and was widening it with the patience of water. Slate was coming off in armfuls somewhere above us; I heard one strike the rock outside with a crack like a rifle. Rain came through in ropes. The tarpaulins we'd stored against exactly this were in the corner, and we got the first one over the bench where the compass lay disassembled in its careful rows — the card half-engraved, the jewelled bearings in their paper envelopes, the needle wrapped separately — while Alistair held one end and I held the other and the storm tried to take the middle from between us.

"Hold!" he roared, though I don't know if he meant the tarpaulin or himself.

"I am holding!" I roared back, and for one absurd moment, in the flashing dark, with salt rain running down both our faces, we were laughing. Not happily. Laughing the way people laugh at funerals, because the body must do something with all that terror or burst.

We got the second tarpaulin over the tool chest. We dragged the ledgers' crate into the driest corner. It took perhaps six minutes and cost us perhaps six years, and when the worst of the immediate salvage was done we stood with our backs against the stone wall under the surviving half of the roof, breathing hard, watching the rain fall past the doorway in grey curtains.

And that is when he said it.

Not all at once. Nothing true ever comes all at once with Alistair. It came in pieces, the way the roof came off.

* * *

### Alistair

There is a kind of cowardice that looks exactly like patience. I had been practicing it for months. That night, with the roof torn open above my son's unfinished work and the woman who would have been my daughter if God were kinder standing beside me soaked to the bone and shaking — not from cold — I ran out of patience to hide behind.

"He came to see me in June," I said. My voice sounded strange in the din, flattened by wind. "Last June. Eleven months before he drowned."

Petra went very still. Stillness, in her, is not calm. I have watched enough weather to know a clear sky can mean the worst part of the front hasn't arrived yet.

"What are you talking about?"

"Dominic. He took the ferry across. Told you he was buying stock for the shop in Falmouth." I had not known until that moment that she didn't know. Some old men are cowards in large ways and honest only by accident. "He came to my rooms. He had papers with him. Ledger pages, copied out in his own hand. He was frightened, and he was furious, and he had worked out what the Meridian Shipping Company actually was."

The name landed between us. I watched her understand it — watched her think of the payments she'd found, the firm she couldn't place, the page she had kept — and I watched her refuse to understand it, the way a hand pulls back from a hot iron.

"Wreckers," she said. The word came out flat.

"Coastal insurance fraud dressed up as bad luck. False lights on dark nights, ships encouraged onto the Aldermoor rocks, cargo written off, goods walked away inland before the excise men could ride out. It's older than either of us. Older than the light." I swallowed. "Dominic had traced payments. Names. Dates going back thirty years. He had copied everything, and he'd hidden the originals, and he told me—" My voice failed. In seventy years it has failed me twice, both times on this subject. "He told me he'd been approached to finish a commission. A compass. For a gentleman of the firm. And that he knew now what the compass was for, and that he was going to give his evidence to the magistrate at Falmouth regardless of whose money had built my grandson's cradle."

The wind screamed through the ruined roof. Somewhere above us a beam shifted, and neither of us moved.

"He was going to testify," Petra said slowly, as though translating from a language she had once known. "In June. Before he drowned in November."

"Yes."

"And you let him get back on the ferry and you said nothing. To me. To anyone."

"I told him to wait. I told him a man with a family doesn't stand up in a courtroom on paper copies and a conscience." I heard how it sounded. I had heard how it sounded for eleven months. "I thought I was protecting him. I thought if he waited, cooled down, the firm might—"

"Might what? Retire?"

"I don't know. I have asked myself every night since, and I don't know. But I know what I've done since I got here, Mrs. Kavanagh, and it wasn't clearing the workshop for the surveyors. I came to find those originals and burn them, because dead men can't testify and neither can their paper, and the men he was going to name are still alive and still shipping and I thought—" I stopped. There was nothing left that could be dressed as duty. "I thought some truths cost more than they're worth."

The light wheeled overhead, visible through the broken roof, steady as a heartbeat. Ninety minutes. She had maybe twenty left before she had to climb again.

Petra turned and looked at me, and her face in the lamplight from the passage was the loneliest thing I have ever seen, and also the angriest, and I understood that I had just done to her the very thing I had spent five chapters of silence trying not to do.

* * *

### Petra

You imagine you know your husband. Eleven years married, one child, one shared grave waiting — you imagine the shape of him is settled in you like a fossil in stone, and that stone is finished.

I stood in my father-in-law's confession with the rain coming through the roof of the room where my husband had spent his last year building a gift for wreckers, and the fossil broke.

Because here is what shattered, and I want to be exact about it, because exactness is all I have left tonight. It was not the knowledge that Dominic had known. Men know terrible things; knowing is cheap. It was that he had chosen. He had stood in some room in Falmouth with papers in his hands and fear in his throat, and he had chosen the harder thing — the right thing, God help me, the brave thing — and he had told his father and not his wife. He had planned to walk into a courtroom and put our family in the path of men who lure ships onto rocks, and he had told Alistair and not me. And then he had gone out on the water in November with fittings that I will now spend the rest of my life examining, and I had spent a year mourning a drowning, and I had been wrong about the shape of the story I was drowning in too.

"You should have told me," I said. "In June. After. Whenever. You should have told me."

"Yes," Alistair said. No argument. That was somehow worse.

"I defended him to you. The ledgers, the payments — I called it nothing. I lied to my son about it last week with my own mouth." My voice cracked down the middle like the slate coming off the roof. "I have spent a year keeping his light and polishing his memory and you're telling me he was going to blow the whole rotten coast wide open and didn't think I deserved—"

She stopped herself. I watched her stop herself, jaw tight, hands fisted in wet wool, and I realized she was doing arithmetic — the same arithmetic I had done in June and botched. What does the living wife owe the dead husband's secret? What does anyone owe?

"The boat," she said finally. Quietly. The storm seemed to lean closer to listen. "His boat. The fittings. You said he was going to testify, and then he died, and everyone said the sea, the season, the usual."

"I don't know," I said. "God forgive me, I don't know, and I have been afraid to find out."

Above us the beam groaned, and the light through the hole in the roof swung round and round, and Petra Kavanagh looked up at it the way a woman looks at a mirror when she no longer recognizes the face in it.

Then she straightened her shoulders, and wiped her face with the back of one wet hand, and became, before my eyes, the keeper again — except that nothing in that face was the keeper I had bargained with over cards and compass parts. This was someone new, or someone I had never troubled to look at properly.

"The clockwork needs winding," she said. "Stay with Tomas. When I come down, you and I are going to go through every page of those ledgers, and every fitting on that boat if I have to dive for it myself. And then, Alistair—" she paused at the stair, and the wheel of light passed over her face once, twice, like a searchlight finding something "—you are going to tell me everything you know about the men my husband died naming."

She climbed into the dark, and the storm closed over the tower like a fist, and I sat down on my son's tool chest with my heart going like the clockwork and understood that the roof was not the only thing that had torn loose tonight, and that what had come off us both could not be nailed back down by spring.
