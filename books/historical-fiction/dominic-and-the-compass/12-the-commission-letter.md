## 12. The Commission Letter

I had built furniture with more honesty than Dominic kept his secrets.

That was my first thought, going through his desk in the grey of the morning — that I had dovetailed joints which told fewer lies than this desk did. The top drawer held what a top drawer ought to hold: spare nibs, a tin of stamps gone brittle at the edges, an account book kept in his small careful hand. The second drawer held string and wax and a lens cloth. The third held nothing but the smell of him, which is not nothing, but it was not what I was after.

Petra watched me from the doorway with her arms folded, and I could feel her deciding whether to help or to hover. She had been up before me, of course. She was always up before me. She lit the lamp, fed the boy, set porridge in front of us both as though feeding a man who had come to take her house apart were simply another item on the keeper's list, between trimming the wick and counting the oil. There is a discipline to this household that I admire and resent in equal measure, which I suspect is how discipline works.

"The false bottom," she said, "if you're looking for one, will be under the blotter tray. He made this desk when he was nineteen. He thought I didn't know about the compartment because he thought I didn't know anything about joinery."

"And do you?"

"I know he used sliding dovetails because he bragged about them for a decade," she said. "Push the back panel left."

I pushed. The panel slid, smooth as the day he fitted it, and there it lay: a packet wrapped in the same oilcloth as the compass, tied with the same waxed twine, because my son was a man of exactly one good idea and applied it everywhere.

I sat with it on my knees for longer than dignity allows a man to sit with anything.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about me, since we have come this far together and you deserve to know what sort of man has been narrating your storms: I am a coward of the respectable kind. I have never cheated anyone, never struck anyone, never lied under oath. My sins are all administrative. I have looked at ugly things and arranged my face into the expression of a man looking at wallpaper. For forty years I signed what needed signing and said nothing where saying something would have cost me, and I called this prudence, and prudence is a very warm coat in a cold country.

Dominic was not a prudent man. That was the quarrel between us, all his life — not money, though we quarreled about that too, and not the lighthouse, though God knows we quarreled about that. The quarrel was that he believed a thing should be said because it was true, and I believed a thing should be survived because it was true, and neither of us ever convinced the other, and now one of us is out of arguments permanently.

He wrote to me eleven days before he drowned.

I read it four times that morning, once aloud for Petra's benefit, three times silently for mine, and by the fourth reading my hands had stopped pretending to be steady.

*Father —*

*I have found what the firm pays for. Not freight. Lights. Ours and others'. A light shown from the wrong place at the wrong hour, and ships come ashore where men are waiting with wagons. The payments in the ledger are not for salvage. They are fees. Mine among them.*

*I have been paid to make a compass. You will know the hand of the client when you read the name. They want it as a gift — so they say — for a master they mean to reward. But I have seen their charts. A gift like that is not gratitude; it is a leash. It will carry their soundings and their private marks, and every mark is a door opened onto our rocks.*

*I have written to the magistrate at Kirkwell. I go to the mainland on the 14th to give what I know. If anything happens to me, the ledgers matter less than the letter I have hidden, and the letter matters less than this: do not let them have the compass finished. Unfinished, it is only brass and a dead man's name. Finished, it is theirs.*

*I am sorry for everything I did not say to you. Tell Tomas the sea took me if the sea must be blamed for something, but do not believe it yourself.*

*Your son,*
*Dominic*

The 14th. He drowned on the 12th.

* * *

Petra read it standing, both hands, the way one reads a telegram. When she finished she did not weep and I want that recorded, because everyone in this story keeps being credited with more composure than they possess and she least of all deserves the reputation. She read it twice and then set it down on the desk very flat, as if it might blow away, as if the window were open, as if any wind could reach it through two feet of stone.

"He went to the magistrate," she said. "He was going to testify."

"So he says."

"You don't believe him."

"I believe he wrote it," I said. "Believing a man's letters and believing a man are different trades." And then, because silence between us had become a currency we spent too freely: "I knew some of it. Not this."

She turned. Slowly, the way weather turns.

"How much," she said, "is *some*."

* * *

I will tell you what I told her, and then you may judge me, and I will wait here while you do, because I have been waiting forty years already and am practiced at it.

The firm — Marrow & Vane, shipping agents, respectable to the point of tedium, pillars of two chapels — had been running wreckers' lights off this coast since before I had grey in my beard. Not constantly. Occasionally. A bad winter, a ship too rich to pass by whole, a boat sent out with a lantern rigged to look like a harbor light or a neighbor's sector, and the sea did the arithmetic the rest of the way. My own father knew. His father likely did. In the villages here it was the sort of knowledge that is not spoken because speaking it makes you part of it, and not knowing it makes you a stranger to it, and everyone chose the first door.

When Dominic took the commission for the compass I suspected nothing worse than vanity in the client. When the payments began arriving — regular, generous, far beyond the work — I asked him once, and he gave me an answer about materials and gilding, and I accepted it because accepting answers is what I do.

But I knew what the firm was. I had known since I was a young man and watched a brig called the *Annabelle* come apart on the Skerries with a light burning on the headland that had no business burning, and I watched the wagons come down at dawn, and I signed a receipt for salvaged cargo the following week because a receipt is a small thing and my signature weighs nothing, doesn't it, until you stack forty years of them in one place.

"I came here to bury it," I told her. It was the truest sentence I had said aloud in decades and it tasted like swallowing a coin. "The orders for the sale — those are real, but they are not mine alone. I moved for the survey. I wanted the workshop cleared and the land sold and every scrap of paper in it burned before spring. I told myself it was for Tomas. So the boy would inherit a name instead of a case file."

Petra said nothing for a long while. Outside, the gulls were making their argument with the wind, which the wind was winning.

"You came to burn evidence," she said finally, "in the house of the woman whose husband it killed."

"When you put it in that order—"

"It's the only order it comes in."

"Yes," I said. "All right. Yes."

And here is the thing I cannot explain except by telling it plainly: she nodded. She did not forgive me — forgiveness takes a generosity I saw no sign of and deserved less — but she filed me, in that moment, somewhere new. Not ally. Never that yet. But a known quantity. A coward, yes, but a coward who had walked into the storm on purpose, which is the only interesting thing cowards do.

* * *

We laid the letter on the workbench beside the compass, and I wish I could describe the way those two objects argued with each other without my saying a word, because they did.

The compass sat half-made in its cradle of soft pine where Dominic had left it: the bowl spun and trued, the gimbal rings cut and bored, the card — the beautiful, accursed card — roughed out but unfinished, its rose unpainted, its points unnumbered. Beside it, the client's papers. Because of course there were client's papers. Dominic hid things in layers, like an onion with a conscience. Under the oilcloth lining of the tool chest we found the specification: the soundings to be engraved, the private marks, the deviation table computed for a particular class of vessel, and the name of the master for whom it was intended, and I will not write that man's name here even now; the court records have it, and let the court records carry it.

"Do you understand what this is?" I asked Petra, and heard my own voice come out older than usual. "It is not a bribe. A bribe you pocket and forget. This is a *trap dressed as a gift*. They commissioned it from the widow's husband — from the one craftsman on this coast whose work carries weight — so that when the compass went aboard a ship with his name engraved on the case, every captain would trust it. His reputation was to be the seal on their forgery. Every mark on that card is a lie drawn by a man whose whole trade was truth. Soundings shifted by a fathom here, a shoal omitted there. Ships would steer by it and call it care. And the rocks would do the rest."

Petra had picked up the card. She held it the way she held everything — competently, at the balance point — and looked at the faint pencil lines where the rose would go.

"He refused," she said. "In the letter. He refused to finish it."

"He intended to refuse. He intended a great many things by the 14th." I took a breath. "But understand the position we are in, woman, because I have been understanding it since dawn and it has not improved with practice. The orders stand. Surveyors come at thaw. If the workshop clears and the compass goes to auction or into a crate, it goes *unfinished*, and an unfinished compass is a curiosity — but the specification travels with it, and the specification is worth more to the firm than the brass. If we finish it — if you and I, with these hands, complete every line Dominic drew — then we build their instrument for them. We finish what the sea started. We become, for the price of a month's honest labor, wrecker's men ourselves."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below, Tomas was doing sums at the kitchen table, badly, cheerfully, the sums of a boy whose father was still a hero because his mother had made him one at cost to herself.

"There is a third thing," Petra said.

"There is always a third thing. It is usually worse."

"Then we burn it all. Compass, specification, ledgers, letter." Her voice was level and it frightened me more than shouting would have. "Let the firm have nothing. Let history have nothing. Tomas inherits a clean name and a locked box of ash."

"And the men who cut your husband's fittings — if they were cut — keep their ledgers on their side of the water, and no one living can prove a word."

She flinched. Good. I had meant it to land; cruelty delivered honestly is still a service, though no one thanks you for it.

"That's the whole ledger of choices, is it?" she said quietly. "Hand them their weapon, burn the proof, or—" and she stopped, because she had seen it, I watched her see it, the same thing I had been circling since the fourth reading of the letter, and I knew that whatever she was about to propose I would spend the rest of this winter regretting having taught her enough joinery to think in structures.

But she did not say it. Not yet. Instead she folded the letter along Dominic's creases, put it inside her apron against her body, and looked at the compass the way a keeper looks at a sea she has decided to cross anyway.

"Not today," she said. "Today we eat lunch. Today Tomas does his sums. Today nobody decides anything, because decisions made before noon in a house full of grief come out poisoned." She was already moving toward the door, and then she stopped, with her hand on the frame, and did not look back at me. "But Alistair. Last night you nearly told me something twice and swallowed it twice. Whatever it is — whatever is still under your tongue after *all this* — you will say it before this storm season ends. I am done learning my husband from drawers."

She went down to her son and her stove, and I stood alone in the workshop with the finest instrument on this coast lying unfinished in front of me, poisoned at the root, gorgeous in its promise, and understood that I had not given her everything after all.

Because there was one more page. One more layer, in the onion, under the specification — a note in Dominic's hand, smaller, later, dated the 11th of December, the day before the sea took him. He had tucked it inside the cradle itself, glued beneath the felt lining where no honest search would find it, and I had found it only because my thumb caught the edge while lifting the bowl.

I had not told Petra about it yet. I am telling you, and God help me I will tell her, but not before noon.

It said only: *The card is wrong on purpose. Check the east marks against the Admiralty chart. Whoever finishes this should know the client's own soundings are the confession.*

The client, in other words, had dictated false charts to my son.

And my son had copied them faithfully — into the one place they could never be unmade.

Outside, the barometer was falling faster than any instrument ought to fall, and the supply boat was not coming, and winter had only just begun to mean it.
