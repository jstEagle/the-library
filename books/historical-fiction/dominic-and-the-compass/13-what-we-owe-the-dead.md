## 13. What We Owe the Dead

The commission letter sat on the workbench between us for the better part of an hour before either of us touched it again, which I have since decided is the natural behavior of people who have discovered a live coal in their kitchen and are waiting to see who will be fool enough to pick it up.

Alistair had gone back to his chair by the stove — the same chair he'd occupied every evening since the bridge closed, as if sitting in it were a job he'd been hired for and could not resign from — and I stood at the bench with Dominic's last words to no one folded into their original creases. *The card is yours to finish or not. Forgive me the rest.*

"He didn't write that like a guilty man," I said at last.

"He wrote it like a frightened one." Alistair did not look up from the fire. "There's a difference, but it's a narrow one, and the dead don't get to explain which side of it they stood on."

"You knew." It was not an accusation, or not only one. We had been through this already, the night of the storm, when he'd given me the shape of the truth in exhausted pieces: the firm, the false lights, Dominic's discovery. But knowing a thing in the dark, half-shouted over wind screaming under the door, is not the same as knowing it by daylight with your hands steady. Daylight is where things become real. "You knew what the compass was for, and you came here in January to help bury it."

"I came here," Alistair said, with the particular care of a man choosing between two equally bad doors, "because I didn't know what else to do. That is not a defense. It is merely the truth, and you've made it plain enough lately that we're trading in nothing else."

I set the letter down. My hands were not entirely steady — I'll admit that here, where no one can see them — but they were steady enough.

"Then tell me plainly. What do we owe him?"

* * *

It is a strange thing, to argue with a man about your dead husband while standing in your dead husband's workshop, surrounded by your dead husband's tools, breathing your dead husband's sawdust into your lungs with every breath. The whole room took sides. The lathe leaned toward Alistair's view of things — order, silence, the dignity of leaving well enough alone. The unfinished compass, clamped in its cradle of soft pine where Dominic had left it eleven months ago, leaned toward mine. Or perhaps I only wanted it to. Grief does that. Grief drafts allies out of brass and wood and makes the furniture testify on your behalf.

Alistair's position, stated formally, was this: destroy the compass. Melt the case, burn the letter, feed the ledgers to the stove, and let spring come without evidence. The wreckers' ring was old money and older silence; Calloway had proven that much simply by appearing at our table with clean gloves and cleaner manners. Finishing the instrument — even altered — would put my son in possession of a thing that men had possibly killed to keep unfinished.

"It's a poisoned inheritance," he said. "You don't hand a boy a cup because the cup is handsome."

"And you don't smash it and pretend the poison was never brewed." I pulled the oilcloth off the bench and began, without quite deciding to, working the clamps loose. "Tomas has already read the ledger, Alistair. He asked me why his father took wrecker money while I was buttering bread. Poison doesn't stay in the cup. It's already in the house."

"So you finish it and engrave names inside the case." He said it the way a man reads back a diagnosis he doesn't accept. "A compass that testifies. Do you hear how it sounds?"

"I hear exactly how it sounds. It sounds like the only honest thing in this room."

He rose then, and came to the bench, and looked down at the half-made instrument the way other men look at a coffin. The case was nearly complete — Dominic had been a careful craftsman even in treachery, or in service of it; I still couldn't decide which word fit and suspected neither did — but the card was blank, the needle unmounted, the glass unfitted. A compass with everything but a soul.

"The firm commissioned this," Alistair said quietly. "Every rivet in it was paid for with money earned by luring ships onto rocks. Men drowned so that this could sit on some shipowner's desk as a trophy. And your proposal is to polish it?"

"My proposal is to finish it." I kept my voice level, mostly because Tomas was asleep thirty feet above our heads and both of us had learned, in these weeks, to fight in the register of whispers. "There's a difference too, and it's narrower than you think."

* * *

We argued past midnight, and I will not pretend it was a grand debate with positions elegantly maintained. It circled. It doubled back. It went cold, warmed, went cold again. At one point Alistair called me stubborn in three different tones of voice, each more respectful than the last, which is how I knew I was winning.

His arguments were good ones, and I will record them fairly, because fairness became something of a religion in that house that winter.

First: danger. Calloway had come and gone, but gone was not gone forever; the thaw would bring the surveyors and who knew what else wearing respectable coats. An engraved confession inside a compass was a lamp in a window for every man who wanted it dark.

Second: futility. A court might not care. Names in brass were not signatures on paper; a clever barrister would call it sentiment, or forgery, or grief-driven spite from a lonely widow. We could ruin ourselves and change nothing.

Third — and this one he delivered looking into the fire, not at me — mercy. "Dominic carried this alone," he said. "Whatever he meant to do, he never got to choose how it ended. You're proposing to choose for him. To make his last work a witness against him. Let the man keep one secret, Petra. He paid for the right to it with his life."

That third argument found its mark, I won't lie. I sat with it a long while. There is a version of love that is only protection — of the dead, of the living, of ourselves — and it wears the face of mercy, and it is very hard to distinguish from cowardice until you hold it up to a light and check what it casts.

Here is what I held up instead.

I thought of the night of the great storm, when the workshop roof tore loose and Alistair told me, in pieces, that Dominic had been going to testify. Going to. Not forced, not complicit to the end — going to, with a boat fitted out in secret and a magistrate's name written somewhere I had not yet found. The commission letter proved what the compass had been born as: a bribe, a trap, a gift from criminals. But it did not prove what Dominic intended it to become. A man ordered to build an instrument of lies does not write *forgive me the rest* unless the rest is something he means to undo.

"They gave him the brass," I said finally. "They didn't give him the meaning. Meaning is the maker's, Alistair. Whoever pays."

He turned that over. I watched him do it — this old man, this proud, frozen, formal man who had crossed a dying bridge to bury his son's shame and found instead that shame makes poor mortar — and I saw the exact moment his argument lost its footing. It wasn't surrender. It was more like watching ice go out of a river in spring: nothing breaks, everything shifts.

"If we finish it," he said slowly, "and it's found — and it will be found, Petra, instruments get sold, houses get searched, boys get curious—"

"Then let it be found telling the truth."

"And if Calloway comes back first?"

"Then he finds a finished compass," I said, "and no reason to burn anything, because the thing he wants buried will be the thing shouting."

* * *

Somewhere in the small hours the argument stopped being an argument and became something closer to a wake, which is, I suspect, what most Irish arguments are underneath anyway. Alistair brought out the bottle he'd ferried across in January — medicinal purposes, he'd claimed, and none of us had laughed — and poured two measures with the ceremony of a man administering last rites to his own position.

"When Dominic was twelve," he said, turning his glass without drinking, "he took apart my best barometer to see how it worked. Didn't ask. Just did it, on a Sunday, while I was at church." A sound escaped him that in a younger man would have been a laugh. "I thrashed him for it. Then I spent the next month teaching him what I should have taught him the day he was born: that a mechanism tells you the truth whether you want it or not, and a craftsman's whole honor is in building it true. Weather instruments, navigation instruments — he grew up on that catechism. *Build it true.*"

He looked at the compass.

"And then men bought it out from under him, and he built it anyway, because a man will do terrible things to feed his family, and then he spent — how long? Years? — trying to find a way to build it true after all." He drank at last, one swallow, like medicine. "Your proposal is cruel, Petra. It finishes his penance for him in front of God and everyone."

"Or it completes his testimony," I said. "The one he never got to give. You testified tonight yourself, remember. In a workshop, to an audience of tools. It counts."

"Does it?" he asked, genuinely asking, and I understood suddenly that this whole winter had been Alistair asking that question in a hundred forms: *does a father's silence count against him, does showing up count for him, is there any act left that balances the ledger.* I had thought him merely difficult. He had been doing arithmetic.

"Yes," I said. "It counts. All of it counts. That's rather the point of engraving."

* * *

We settled it the way all real settlements are settled: not with a triumph but with terms.

The compass would be finished. Properly finished — card, needle, glass, gimbal, the whole of it, to a standard even the firm's shipowner would have envied, because shoddy work in service of honesty is just a second kind of lie. And inside the case, where only an opened instrument could see, where a thief or an heir or a magistrate would have to turn it over and pry it apart to find it, we would cut the truth: every payment, every date, every name in Dominic's ledgers, engraved in letters small enough to hide and deep enough to outlast everyone who wished them forgotten.

"A compass shows you where you are," Alistair said, when the terms were agreed and the candle was guttering. "Yours will show some future fool where *this* place stood. Who paid. Who drowned. Who looked away." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger. "My son will hang from a letter he never sent."

"No," I said. "Your son will hang from the truth he reached for. Those aren't the same rope, Alistair. I've been holding both this past year, and I can tell you the difference by weight."

He didn't answer. But he rose, and crossed to the bench, and picked up the graver — Dominic's graver, the one with the worn rosewood handle Tomas's small fingers had fit so naturally that first afternoon — and laid it in front of me with the formality of a knight presenting a sword.

"We start with the dates," he said. "Dates are hardest to dispute. And we practice on scrap brass first, because your hand shakes when you're angry and engraving forgives nothing."

"My hand doesn't shake."

"It shook the entire time you argued with me."

"That was conviction," I said. "It looks similar."

Something happened to his face then that I had not seen all winter — the corners of it moving in the general direction of humor, tentative as the first thaw. "God help the mainland," he murmured, "if that woman ever gets a courtroom."

* * *

I did not sleep after he went up. I sat at the bench with the compass in my hands and the letter open beside me and the lamp turned low, and I talked to Dominic, which is a habit I had mostly broken and now resumed without apology.

"You were going to tell them everything," I said to the dark. "I know that now. I've stopped defending the parts of you that need no defense and started defending the part that does — the part that found out, that planned, that reached. That's the man I'm married to. The other man, the one who took their money and kept quiet — he was frightened, and hungry, and I'd have done the same with Tomas's stomach in the balance, and I will not pretend otherwise, but I will not *build him a monument* either."

The needle mount caught the lamplight. Eleven months it had waited, half-born, wrapped in oilcloth like a body prepared for burial that turned out to be only sleeping.

"So here is what we owe you," I told him. "Not silence. Silence is what they bought from you, and look what it cost. We owe you the ending you were walking toward when the sea — or something worse wearing the sea's face — stopped you. We finish it true."

Outside, the wind had eased to something almost gentle, the storm spending itself the way storms do, without conscience and without memory. Below the headland the sea worked at the rocks, patient, indifferent, keeping its own counsel about what it had swallowed and on whose behalf.

I banked the lamp and climbed the stairs to bed, passing Tomas's door, where his sleeping breath made its small steady weather. Tomorrow we would begin cutting names into brass. My hand, whatever Alistair said, would be steady.

It was three days later, when the supply boat that shouldn't have been able to cross the ice-choked channel came anyway — flying no colors, running no signal — that I understood the thaw, and everything riding on it, had started early.
