## 14. The Firm Sends Someone

There is an art to arriving uninvited, and I have made a study of it. The secret is to arrive as though you were expected — indeed, to half-believe it yourself. Men who come with apologies get searched at the threshold. Men who come with certainty get handed a towel and asked if they'll take tea.

I rowed the last quarter-mile because the fishing skiff's owner had opinions about his hull and my destination, and I paid him enough that his opinions became silence. The headland rose out of a grey sea like the back of something sleeping, and the lighthouse stood at its crown, white paint gone the color of old teeth, the lamp room glinting dully under a lid of cloud. A working light. I noted it the way I note all things: as an instrument that might be used.

The dinghy scraped shingle. I stepped out with both boots into six inches of February water, which was the correct choice over one boot into twelve, and hauled her up beyond the reach of the tide. Then I stood a moment and let the wind have my coat, because a man who arrives wind-bitten looks honest, and honesty is the first of my costumes.

The house sat behind the tower, low and stone, smoke rising from its chimney in a thin, obedient line. Beyond it, half-hidden in the lee of the hill, a workshop with slate windows. That was the reason for my journey. That, and a certain ledger, and a certain unfinished brass object that the firm had paid for in good coin twenty years ago and never received.

I am not a sentimental man, but I will confess to a professional appreciation for unfinished things. An unfinished thing is a confession written in someone else's hand.

* * *

The woman opened the door before I knocked, which told me she'd watched me cross the water from the lamp room — sensible of her; keepers watch the sea the way bankers watch ledgers. She was younger than I expected and more composed than I hoped otherwise. Widow's black worn practical rather than mournful: sleeves turned back, collar high, no jewelry except a wedding band gone thin as a promise kept too long. Her eyes did the arithmetic of me in perhaps two seconds — boots, coat, hands, eyes — and arrived at a sum she chose not to share.

"Mrs. Kavanagh," I said, and gave her my name, and watched it land on her without weight, which meant either she didn't know it or was very good. "Calloway. Of Harrow & Vane, shipping agents. I'm afraid the weather has inconvenienced everyone, myself most of all." I produced a letter of introduction, folded once, corners square. "The firm has interests on this coast. When we heard the bridge was condemned, Mr. Vane thought someone ought to see to the family's welfare."

"Did he," said Mrs. Kavanagh.

It was not a question. It was a door held very slightly ajar so she could look at what wanted entering. I smiled the smile I keep for customs men and clergy.

"He is famously kind," I said. "May I come in out of the wind? I promise I don't bite before luncheon."

She considered me for exactly as long as courtesy permitted and one second longer, then stepped back. "You may. Boots there."

A woman who makes a stranger of quality remove his boots is a woman who has decided where power lives in her house, and it is not with him. I approved. I removed my boots.

Inside was warm, scrubbed, and orderly in the manner of households governed by grief rather than habit — everything in its place because its place had been assigned by absence. A boy sat at the table doing sums, ten or eleven years old, with his father's jaw and his mother's habit of looking at strangers as though they might owe him money. He looked up when I came in. I gave him the small nod one gives children, the one that says *I am harmless*, which is true in the sense that a knife in a drawer is harmless.

"Tomas," said his mother, "this is Mr. Calloway. He's come from the mainland."

"I've come," I corrected gently, hanging my coat, "to help. There's a difference."

"Not always," said Mrs. Kavanagh, and put the kettle on.

* * *

Old Kavanagh found me within the hour, as I knew he would. Alistair Kavanagh is a man built like the furniture of a church: hard, dark, intended to outlast the congregation. He came into the parlor where I'd been installed with tea I didn't want, stopped in the doorway, and went still in a way I have only ever seen in soldiers and debtors recognizing their collector.

"Calloway," he said.

"You know me." I rose, hand extended. He did not take it. "Well. That saves us the pleasantries, though I confess I enjoy pleasantries. They're like oil on a hinge. Nobody notices them until they're gone."

"How did you get across? The bridge—"

"The bridge is down. The sea, however, remains open to the enterprising." I sat again, unhurried, and let my gaze travel the room: the mended netting in the window, the brass clock wound and running, the photograph of a young man in keeper's uniform with the boy's jaw. "I've come about the estate. The firm was a creditor of your son's, among other things. When the surveyors come in spring to settle the workshop land, we'll want our property accounted for. Tools, stock, materials paid for and never delivered. It's a simple matter of inventory."

"Inventory," said Alistair.

"Inventories are the soul of commerce, Mr. Kavanagh. Everything in its column. Nothing left lying about to confuse the assessor." I let that sentence sit in the room like a guest who refuses his hat. "Naturally I'll need to see the workshop."

"Naturally," he said, in the tone of a man agreeing to his own autopsy, and I understood then that he knew precisely why I'd come, and that knowing it would make him easier to manage, not harder. Guilty men guard doors. Innocent ones forget they have them.

* * *

They gave me the boy's trundle for sleeping and the good chair for evenings, and I settled into the household the way silt settles into a harbor: quietly, and everywhere. By the third day I had learned the rhythms of the place. The lamp lit at dusk and trimmed through the night in watches. Breakfast at seven, supper at six, grace said by the old man in a voice like gravel poured slowly. The widow kept her own accounts in a canvas-bound book and balanced them nightly, and I do not mean the household expenses. I mean the light. Oil consumed, rotations logged, weather entered in a fair hand. A woman who writes things down. I find such women either the easiest or the most dangerous company in the world, and the first three days did not tell me which.

On the fourth day they let me into the workshop.

Let me describe it, because rooms are testimonies and this one swore falsely. A joiner's bench under the north window, tools racked above it in oiled rows — his father's discipline imposed on a dead man's habits, or perhaps the dead man's own. Shavings on the floor gone grey with age. And on the corner of the bench, apart from everything, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with twine like something between a relic and a patient, the compass.

I did not go to it at once. That is rule one: never go to the thing you came for. Go instead to the window and remark on the view. Pick up a plane and admire its iron. Ask after the lathe. Let your host relax his vigilance by degrees, the way one lets air out of a cask, until the moment comes when crossing the room means nothing at all.

"The firm's records show outstanding work," I said, admiring a set of chisels. "A commission accepted, materials advanced, delivery promised and never made. Twenty years is a long time to carry a debt, Mrs. Kavanagh. It weighs on the books."

"My husband carried a great many things," she said. Her hands did not stop moving — she was sorting screws by size into paper twists, and her fingers had the sureness of long practice. "I didn't know he owed yours money."

"A technicality of bookkeeping. The advance was against the finished piece." I set the chisel down precisely where I'd found it. "Which appears to be nearly finished itself. May I?"

She hesitated. It was a small hesitation, well-mannered, entirely proper — and I have spent thirty years reading hesitations the way other men read tide tables. It was not the hesitation of *may I show you*. It was the hesitation of *what will you see*.

She untied the twine herself and folded back the oilcloth, and there it lay. Brass bowl, gimbal rings bright-cut, the card half-engraved — points marked to south and east, west unfinished, the graver's path stopping mid-stroke as though the hand had been called away forever. A beautiful thing. A thing built to be trusted absolutely, which is what makes any instrument useful, and what had made this one valuable to men whose trade requires trust placed exactly where it will do the most harm.

"It's fine work," I said, and meant it, which surprised me. Sentiment is a leak in the hull; I bailed promptly. "A credit to the family. The firm would be pleased to see it completed and delivered — I could arrange carriage in spring. There'd be a settlement. Enough to carry the widow comfortably to the mainland, perhaps enough for schooling."

The boy appeared in the doorway at the word *schooling* the way gulls appear at the word *fish*, and his mother saw him, and something closed in her face like a drawer.

"We'll see," she said. "The compass goes to Tomas. It was his grandfather's craft and his father's making. It isn't for sale."

"Everything," I said pleasantly, "is for sale. Some things merely haven't met their price. It's why markets exist, Mrs. Kavanagh — to bring the patient and the surgeon together."

"That's a strange way to describe a market."

"I deal mostly in strange descriptions. It keeps the work interesting." I smiled at the boy over his mother's shoulder, and the boy, to his credit, stared back at me without a flicker, the way dogs stare at men who smell wrong.

* * *

That evening I wrote my report by the parlor fire while the family pretended I wasn't there, which is a skill families acquire faster than you'd think. I reported arrival, access, and condition of assets. I reported that the widow was intelligent, the father-in-law hostile but manageable, the boy observant. I recommended retrieval before thaw and used the phrase *storm damage* twice, because the firm likes phrases that can bear two readings, and so do I.

What I did not report — because reports travel, and ink is a traitor — was my growing suspicion that the ledger I'd been sent for no longer lived in the workshop. A room tells you when something has been taken from it. The dust speaks. The gaps in racks speak loudest of all, and there were gaps in Dominic Kavanagh's tool rack shaped not like tools but like papers: a shallow rectangle of clean wood where years of grime should have framed whatever rested there. Someone had lifted documents recently and often, and recently replaced them somewhere else, somewhere less obvious than a bench drawer.

Somewhere, possibly, with a lamp overhead at all hours and a lock on the door.

I blotted the page and considered the fire. In my experience there are two kinds of houses: houses where things are hidden from strangers, and houses where things are hidden from each other. This, I suspected, was becoming the second kind, and those are delicate houses. Delicate houses burn, sometimes, when all other arrangements fail. I did not want it to come to burning. Burning leaves questions, and questions leave magistrates, and magistrates leave depositions, and depositions are the one genre of literature I truly fear.

No. Better the quiet way. Wait for the household's rhythm to open a seam — the old man's nap, the widow's watch below, the boy's school of the shoreline — and lift the ledger and the compass both in an afternoon, and row away owing nothing to anyone. Courtesy is patient. Courtesy, given time, opens more doors than crowbars ever have.

The widow came in just then to bank the fire, and caught me watching her, and did not look away.

"You write a great deal, Mr. Calloway," she said.

"Correspondence is the rent I pay for my keep."

"And what do you say in it?"

"The truth, mostly. It's less suspicious than the alternatives." I held her gaze with the mild expression I wear for photographs. "I've said the light is well kept, the family hospitable, and the boy clever. None of it will embarrass you."

"Then you've said more true things tonight than most travelers manage." She set the poker down. "Goodnight, Mr. Calloway. The lamp needs trimming at two. You'll hear me pass your door."

"I'll hear you," I agreed, and meant more by it than she knew yet: I would learn her footsteps, their weight and intervals, until I could tell from the sound alone whether she slept. Every house gives itself away eventually, in footsteps and hinges and the hours its dog stops barking. Hers would too.

After she'd gone I sat a while longer, listening to the wind organize itself against the tower, and admitted to myself the one thing I had kept out of my report: the widow frightened me a little, and I am rarely frightened, and being frightened by the right person has saved my life twice and cost me a fortune once, and I have never reliably told the difference in advance.

Above me, faint through the ceiling, I heard the boy's trundle creak — he was awake, past ten o'clock, and moving about his small domain with elaborate quiet. Moving toward the toy chest, if my ear was true, which it generally is.

Children, I thought, tucking my report into my coat. Children hide things in toy chests, under floorboards, inside the false backs of drawers their fathers made for them. Children hide things badly and bravely.

In the morning, I decided, I would ask to see the boy's toys.
