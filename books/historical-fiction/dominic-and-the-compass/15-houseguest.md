## 15. Houseguest

There is a particular kind of guest who arrives with a compliment already in his mouth, like a coin held between his teeth for tipping. Mr. Calloway was that kind. He had been in our house four days, and I had counted eleven compliments, all of them landing on things that did not belong to him — the bread, the view, the boy's manners, the compass.

"You must be very proud," he said, for the fourth time, standing over the workbench where the compass case sat open under the window's gray light. "A thing like this. Dominic's hand is all through it."

"He had good instruction," I said. "His father's."

Alistair, mending a net by the stove, did not look up. He had perfected, in four days, the art of being furniture with opinions.

I had learned something about myself in those four days, and it was not a comfortable thing to learn. I had learned that I could smile at a man I did not trust while measuring, the whole time, the distance between him and the door. That I could pour his tea with a steady hand while my mind ran the house's inventory — where the ledgers lay, where the card lay, which floorboards creaked, which window latched. Fear, it turns out, is a kind of fuel. It burns clean if you ration it.

* * *

The card was finished. That was the fact I carried through every one of Calloway's compliments like a stone in my boot.

Petra, you'll want to know — and I confess I wanted someone to know, even if only this page — the card was finished, and it was beautiful. Alistair and I had worked the brass thin as a wafer, and I had painted the points in the old style, north in gold leaf, the intercardinals in black, and around the rim the thirty-two winds with their fine old names: NNE, ENE, the whole litany of directions a soul might need when the world went dark. Dominic had cut the pivot before he died. All that remained was to seat it, and I had not yet, because seating it felt like a kind of marriage vow, and I was not yet certain whom I was marrying — the compass, or the truth, or neither.

So the card lived in Tomas's toy chest, wrapped in the oilcloth his father had wrapped it in, lying among a wooden horse with one ear, six marbles, a kite that had never flown, and a tin of soldiers Tomas had stopped playing with the year his father drowned. It was, I reasoned, the last place a man like Calloway would look. Men like Calloway look in desks. They look under floorboards and inside mattresses. They do not look among the toys of children, because they have decided, somewhere along the way, that children are furniture too.

I did not feel clever about this. I felt like a woman hiding a Bible in a flour sack. But cleverness is not required for survival. Only stubbornness, and a good hiding place.

* * *

Calloway inspected the workshop on the second morning, and he did it the way a customs man inspects a hold — thoroughly, cheerfully, and with his hands behind his back so that nothing he touched could be tied to him.

"May I?" he asked, at every drawer, every chest, every shelf, and I said "of course" at every one, and we were both lying the entire time with the ease of old dance partners.

He admired the lathe. He admired the arrangement of the files by size. He held Dominic's smallest chisel to the light and said, "German steel. He had an eye." He ran his finger along the bench and looked at the dust on it, and I understood that he was not inspecting the workshop. He was inspecting us. A workshop in active use looks one way. A workshop being searched looks another. He was trying to determine which ours was.

It was both, of course. That was the joke of it, the joke I kept behind my teeth.

"The ledgers," he said, casual as weather. "Alistair mentioned the firm's old accounts were kept here. I've come to collect whatever's outstanding, you understand. Housekeeping. The new owners will want a clean slate."

"The new owners," I said, and smiled, and handed him a rag to wipe his finger. "I hadn't heard a sale was settled."

"Nothing is settled until the thaw." He smiled back. His smile was like the light on the water — pleasant, and no indication whatsoever of what lay beneath. "But firms plan ahead. As should you, Mrs. Kavanagh. As should you."

I told him the ledgers had gone to the mainland with the autumn ferry, for the solicitor. He thanked me as though I'd offered him a biscuit, and I knew he didn't believe me, and he knew I knew, and we went on being gracious about it. There is a whole country of conversation that exists between people who are lying to each other, and I had never known I held a passport to it. Grief had taken me there, apparently. Or Calloway had. Either way, I found I could live in it.

* * *

That evening he made his offer.

We were at supper — Tomas excused early, Alistair silent over his plate, Calloway eating my bread and praising it, which is a particular offense — when he set down his fork and folded his hands and became, for a moment, almost human.

"Mrs. Kavanagh. May I speak plainly?"

"Mr. Calloway. You've spoken plainly since you climbed out of that dinghy. It's one of your charms."

He took the hit without flinching, which told me he'd been hit before. "The firm is prepared to be generous. Passage for you and the boy — anywhere you like. Glasgow, if you have people. Canada, if you want a new start. A sum to establish yourselves. In exchange, the workshop contents pass to the firm as agreed, and the estate is settled cleanly, and no one — " he spread his hands, a man releasing birds — "no one needs to be troubled by any of it. The past is heavy cargo, Mrs. Kavanagh. I'm offering to carry it for you."

"And what does the firm imagine is in the workshop that's so heavy?"

"Ledgers. Correspondence. Instruments in unfinished states." He said *unfinished* the way a surgeon says *malignant*. "Sentimental value to the family, no doubt. Of no use to anyone else."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the surveyors come at the thaw, and the lawyers come after, and everything is handled in the ordinary way, which is slower, and noisier, and harder on the boy." He looked at me with what I can only call kindness, and it was the worst thing I had seen all winter, because it was practiced. "You've been alone a year, Mrs. Kavanagh. I've watched you these four days. You're tired. You're keeping a light for strangers while — forgive me — your own house stands in the dark. The firm isn't your enemy. The past is. Let us take it off your hands."

And here is what I discovered about myself, the thing I told you I was learning: I smiled. Not the tight smile of a woman cornered. A real one, warm at the edges, the kind I'd given ferry captains and visiting inspectors all my married life, the keeper's smile, the one that says *all is well* whether or not it is. I poured him more tea while I did it.

"That's a generous offer," I said. "I'll consider it."

Alistair's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. He knew — he had to know — that I was lying, and that I knew he knew, and that the knowing was itself the message: *we are agreed on nothing, and we will say nothing here.*

"Take all the time the weather allows," said Calloway pleasantly.

Which was, we both understood, not very much time at all.

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I lay in the dark doing sums — how many days to the thaw, how thick the ice on the crossing, how fast a dinghy travels when its passenger is in a hurry — and listening to the house. Calloway had been given the small room off the kitchen, and somewhere around two in the morning I heard his door, and his weight on the stairs, and — this was the part that made my blood go quiet and cold — the workshop door, opening and closing on its oiled hinge without a sound.

He was searching at night. Of course he was. The day's inspection had been theater; this was the performance that mattered.

I lay in bed and listened to him move through Dominic's workshop for the better part of an hour, and I want to be honest, because I am trying, in this account, to be honest about everything: I was afraid. Properly afraid, the kind that sits in the chest like a stone. A man was walking through my dead husband's work, touching his tools, opening his drawers, and I lay still and let him, because stopping him would tell him where to look, and not stopping him was its own kind of answer, and I could not find a third thing to do.

When his door closed again, I got up and went down and stood in the workshop among the disturbed dust, and I checked — the ledgers were safe, moved days ago to the lamp room, where no guest goes at night for fear of the stairs; the card was safe, in the toy chest, under the one-eared horse. Everything of value was exactly where a searching man would not think to look, and I stood there in the dark and felt something I had not felt since Dominic died, which was not peace exactly, but its cousin. Competence. The cold, steady knowledge that I could keep a thing burning through a bad night.

I had done it for a year with the light. I could do it with the truth.

* * *

The days that followed had a shape to them, the shape of a card game where only two players know they're playing. Calloway helped Tomas with his knots in the mornings — the man was good with the boy, which I hated him for, because it was not feigned, or if it was feigned, it was feigned so well that the difference stopped mattering. He asked Alistair about old wrecks with the enthusiasm of a hobbyist. He complimented my bread again. He walked the headland in the afternoons, alone, and I watched him from the lamp room and noted that he always walked it the same way: past the workshop's seaward wall, slow, looking up at the roofline, at the shuttered windows, at the lie of the land.

He was not admiring the view. He was measuring it. For what, I would not let myself say. But I started filling the wash copper in the evenings, and I moved the lamp oil from the shed to the kitchen, and I told Alistair to sleep in his boots, and Alistair, who had spent a lifetime pretending not to understand things, understood this immediately and said nothing, which between the two of us had become a whole language.

On the sixth day he found me in the workshop, seated at the bench, doing nothing — I had learned to be found doing nothing, it was safer than being found doing something — and he leaned against the doorframe and said, "You know, I read the shipping news. The ice is thick this year. The thaw may run late."

"Then you'll have more bread," I said.

"And you'll have more time to consider my offer."

"I'm considering it now."

"Are you?" He came further in, and looked at the empty bench where the compass case had sat until two nights ago, when I'd moved it to the bottom of my own clothes chest, because I'd begun to suspect he was watching which rooms I entered carrying things. "Where is it, Mrs. Kavanagh? The compass. The famous unfinished compass that you and Alistair have been so faithfully — what did he call it — *seeing to*."

"Finished," I said, and heard myself say it, and could have bitten my own tongue off.

The silence lasted three seconds. In it, I watched him decide not to believe me, and decide also that I had perhaps made an error worth having, because an error could be worked with. A woman who admits the thing exists can be made to trade it.

"Finished," he repeated, soft as snowfall. "Then it's only a matter of where."

"It's a matter of nothing," I said, and stood, and gave him the keeper's smile, the one that says *all is well* to strangers in the dark. "It's Dominic's. It goes to my son. That was always the arrangement, and it's the arrangement still. Your firm can have the ledgers, the land, and my very best wishes, Mr. Calloway. The compass stays."

I walked past him out of the workshop with my heart going like a gull in a gale, and I did not look back, because looking back is how you tell a man he's won ground.

That night I checked the toy chest twice. The card lay where I'd hidden it, brass-bright under the oilcloth, among the marbles and the one-eared horse, pointing — as it always would, as it was made to — toward north.

And downstairs, very late, I heard the kitchen door, and then the shed, and then the small glassy sound of a lantern being set down on stone, and I lay in the dark with my hand on Tomas's door and understood that the time for hiding things was nearly over.

The wind had come around. It was going to be a windy night.
