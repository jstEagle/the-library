## 16. The Ledger in the Lamp Room

There are three things I know about grown-ups, and I learned all of them that winter.

First: they think you don't listen. Second: they think you don't look. Third: when they smile too much, it means somebody is about to lose something.

I learned the third one from Mr. Calloway, who smiled more than any person I had ever met. He smiled at breakfast. He smiled at the porridge, which in my opinion did not deserve it. He smiled at my mother in a way that made her back go straight as a mast, and he smiled at Grandfather Alistair in a way that made Grandfather's hands curl up like dead leaves. And he smiled at me and asked me questions — was I fond of the sea, did I help my mother tend the light, where did I keep my toys — and every question sounded friendly and felt like a hand going through my pockets.

So I listened, and I looked, because nobody had told me not to.

* * *

It started on a Tuesday, or possibly a Wednesday; the days in winter at the light are all gray brothers of each other. I had gone up to fetch my boots from beside the kitchen stove and stopped at the door because Mother and Mr. Calloway were in there, and you can learn more standing still than moving, which is the fourth thing I know but doesn't count because I learned it from Grandfather.

"—so generous," Mr. Calloway was saying. "Passage on the *Corinne*, first week of the thaw. Cabin passage, Mrs. Kavanagh. The boy would see the mainland properly."

"The boy sees the mainland properly enough," said Mother.

"For schooling. For prospects." A spoon clicked against porcelain. He took tea with us every morning and I had never once seen him asked to. "A place like this is fine for keeping a light, ma'am, but it's poor ground for growing a future."

I looked through the crack of the door. My mother was standing at the stove with her back to him, doing something to the porridge that did not need doing. She does that — keeps her hands busy so her face can be somewhere else.

"His future," she said, "is his father's workshop, and his grandfather's trade, and a compass that isn't finished yet."

"Ah," said Mr. Calloway, "yes. The compass. You must let me see it again before I go. Such work. Dominic had a gift."

"He did," said my mother, and the porridge spoon stopped for half a second, and I knew — the way you know a wave is going to break before it breaks — that something was wrong. Because Mr. Calloway had never once said my father's name before. He'd said "your late husband," "the former keeper," "poor Kavanagh," the way men say things when they're handling them with tongs. Saying *Dominic*, warm and easy, was like watching a man put down the tongs and pick up the thing with his bare hands.

"And the ledgers," Mr. Calloway went on, pleasant as weather. "The old man has been at them day and night, I hear. Family accounts, I suppose. Housekeeping, coal, oil."

"Housekeeping," agreed my mother. "Coal and oil."

"Wonderful," said Mr. Calloway. "Wonderful." And he laughed, a small round laugh like a coin dropped on a table, and I backed away from the door before either of them saw me, and stood in the cold hall with my heart going like the fog bell.

Because I had seen the ledgers.

* * *

Here is what I knew, and how I knew it, and I'll set it all down straight because this is the part where I did the thing I did and you should know exactly why.

The ledgers lived in the workshop, in the third drawer of the bench under the window, wrapped in a rag. I wasn't supposed to open the third drawer. Nobody had ever said so out loud, which is how I knew it mattered. Grown-ups guard things two ways: locks for strangers, and silence for family, and the silence is stronger.

I had opened the drawer in January, when the storms were loud enough that nobody could hear anything above a shout. My father's handwriting was in those books. I'd know it anywhere; he used to write my name on my slate on my birthday, big loops, the T crossing through both syllables like it was showing off. And next to his handwriting, in other handwriting — fat, careful, money-handwriting — were sums. Page after page. Sums paid *to* him, marked with dates, and dates I knew, because one of them was two weeks before he drowned.

And there was a word written small in the margin of one page, in my father's hand, pressed hard enough that the pen had nearly gone through the paper. I hadn't understood it then. I understood it now, because I'd heard Grandfather say it in his sleep one night through the wall, and then heard Mother say it once to Grandfather in the kitchen with her voice flat as a ironed cloth:

*Wreckers.*

My father took money from wreckers. Or my father found wreckers. Those are not the same thing, but they live on the same page, and Mr. Calloway wanted that page.

* * *

That afternoon I did what boys do, which is nothing anyone notices. I carried water. I split kindling. I walked the gallery rail with my mittens frozen stiff, and I watched Mr. Calloway the way the gulls watch you when you have chips.

He went into the workshop twice while Mother was up in the lamp room. Both times he came out with his gloves on, though the stove was lit in there and everyone else's gloves hung by the door. The second time, he came out and stood a moment and looked back over his shoulder, and I dropped flat behind the rain barrel so fast I bruised my knee on the stone, and when I dared look again he was smiling to himself. Nobody to smile at. Just smiling, alone, on our doorstep, in the wind.

That was the day I decided.

Not decided like a magistrate decides. Decided like a wave decides — the whole sea moves and the wave just happens. I lay awake that night listening to the house talk to itself, the beams ticking, the wind worrying the shingles, and I thought: they're managing it. Mother and Grandfather. They're holding it like a full cup, walking careful, and Mr. Calloway is walking careful too, and one day two careful people are going to meet on the stairs and the cup is going to go over. And the ledgers would be in the workshop when it did. In the third drawer. Where he had already been, twice, with his gloves on.

The lamp room, though. Nobody wanted anything in the lamp room. The lamp room was mine and Mother's. Even Mr. Calloway climbed that stair slow and respectful, taking off his hat, like the light itself might take offense. And the light room had a thing no other room had: a locked service cabinet built into the wall, iron, meant for spare wicks and signal flags and the emergency lantern, with a key that hung around my mother's neck on the same cord as her whistle.

I couldn't get the key. But I didn't need the cabinet.

Under the floor plates of the gallery walk, where the lens machinery hums at night like a sleeping animal, there is a void between the inner and outer walls where Dad showed me once how the counterweights run. "Mind your fingers there, Tommo," he'd said, "the weights don't care whose fingers they are." It's dry. It's dark. It's the one place in the world where my father's voice still lives in the actual wood and iron, telling me to mind my fingers.

I would put the ledgers where the weights ran. Where the light lived. If Mr. Calloway burned the workshop — and I had heard Grandfather tell Mother, very low, two nights past, *"he'll fire it and call it the storm, mark me"* — then everything in it would go. But nobody burns a lighthouse. You'd have to be mad. The whole coast depends on it; even wreckers need it, the way thieves need the moon to be honest about where the rocks are.

* * *

I waited for the right hour, and the right hour came two evenings later.

Mr. Calloway had gone down to the landing at dusk, saying he expected a boat, and Mother went with him because she never let him near the water alone — I'd watched her do it every time, casual as anything, leaning on the rail like she liked the view. That left Grandfather in the workshop, working the compass case with the little file, his spectacles down his nose. Grandfather at his work hears nothing but the work.

I took the rag-wrapped bundle from the third drawer and put in its place a folded blanket, weighted with two of Dad's hammer heads, so the drawer would slide the same if anyone pulled it. Then I rolled the bundle inside my coat, which made me look like I'd swallowed a goose, and went out through the yard in the last gray light with the wind shoving me sideways like a big rude cousin.

The lamp room stair goes up through the middle of the tower, ninety-odd steps, and I counted them the way Dad taught me, because counting keeps fear in a box. At step forty I heard the kitchen door below and froze with my back against the cold brick, one arm clamped over the lump in my coat, and Mother's voice came floating up — she was calling Grandfather to supper — and then his footsteps, and his muttering, and then the two of them talking low in the kitchen, and their voices wrapped around me like another coat, and I went up.

Step seventy. Step eighty. The hum of the clockwork grew until I felt it in my teeth. Then the hatch, and the lamproom, and the great lens turning its slow gold turn, throwing its beam out and hauling it back, out and back, patient as breathing.

You feel small in the lamp room and tall at the same time, which I can't explain any better than that. The whole sea is out there black and boiling, and the light goes over it like a hand smoothing a blanket, and you're the finger of the hand. Dad used to lift me up to the sight-line. "Everything we show them is true, Tommo," he said once. "Rocks are rocks. Water is water. That's the whole job." I didn't understand him then. Standing there with wreckers' money against my ribs, I understood him all the way down.

The floor plate over the weight channel is held by four butterfly nuts. I'd watched Dad undo them a hundred times; my fingers knew the motion even if they'd only done it small. They fought me — brass nuts in cold air will do that — and I skinned one knuckle and bled a little on the plate and wiped it with my sleeve, and got the plate up, and the channel yawned dark and oily below, and far down I could hear the great weight ticking on its chain.

I unwrapped the rag. The ledgers looked smaller without the drawer around them. Two books and a folded paper I hadn't seen before, tucked in the wrapping — I made myself look at it, because if I was going to hide a thing I should know what I was hiding. It was a letter, or part of one, in the fat money-handwriting, and the words I caught were *the instrument* and *by spring* and *no further payments*, and my stomach went cold and quiet, like the sea gets just before it does the worst thing.

Out and back went the beam. Out and back. Rocks are rocks.

I wrapped it all tight, tied it with the rag's own string, lowered it into the channel as far as my arm reached, and laid it flat against the inner wall where the weights don't run, and put the plate back, and did up the nuts, and stood there breathing hard with my heart banging like surf, and that is when the stair creaked.

* * *

Nobody creaks that stair soft. There's no way to do it; the treads announce you like a churchwarden. But some people try, and the trying has a sound of its own — a pause, a shift of weight, a held breath you can almost hear through the brick — and that is what came up the stair while I stood in the gold sweep of the light with my skinned knuckle bleeding and my mouth tasting of pennies.

I looked at the hatch. The hatch is a trapdoor in the floor. There is nowhere else.

The service cabinet was ten feet away and locked. The gallery door was bolted, because the bolts are drawn at dusk, and I had drawn them myself an hour ago. And the stair was already announcing its second creak, closer now, and I thought with a strange clear calmness: *it's fine, Tommo, you're supposed to be here.* Boys climb the tower. Boys sit with the light. There is no rule against a boy in the lamp room, and no reason on earth for guilt, except that guilt doesn't need a reason, it just needs a staircase.

The hatch lifted.

Mr. Calloway came up into the gold light with his hat in one hand and his gloves on, and for one long moment neither of us moved. The lens turned. The beam swept out over the black sea and hauled itself back, and in the returning glow his face was all warmth, and his eyes were all arithmetic.

"Why, Master Tomas," he said. "Up past your bedtime."

"I help keep the light," I said. Which was true. Which was the best lie of all, a true one.

"Do you?" He stepped off the hatch and let it close softly behind him, and crossed the lamp room unhurried, looking at everything — the clockwork, the burner, the logbook open on its stand — looking everywhere, in fact, except at me, which was worse than looking at me. "Your father helped too, I'm told. Kept the light beautifully, your father."

"He kept it true," I said.

Something crossed his face like a shadow of a gull — fast, and gone, and you couldn't say afterward whether it had really been there. "True," he repeated, tasting it. "Yes. Well." And then he did the terrible thing, which was to crouch down to my height, and smile, and say gently: "And what else do you keep up here, hmm? Anything? Anything you'd like to show me?"

And because God looks after boys and fools, and sometimes confuses the two, that was the exact moment the clockwork struck the quarter-hour and the striking works engaged — the great clatter and ring of it, four strokes on a bell the size of a washtub — and under cover of it came Mother's voice up the stair, sharp as the whistle on her cord:

"Tomas? *Tomas.*"

She came up through the hatch with the wind pouring in around her and the lamp oil smell of the tower all stirred up, and she saw me, and she saw him, and I watched her read the whole room in one pass the way she reads the horizon — quick, complete, missing nothing. Me, white-faced. Him, crouched, gloved, smiling. The quarter-bell dying away. The distance between us.

"There you are," she said to me, mild as milk. "Grandfather wants you to fetch the small vise from the workshop. The little one. Go along now." And then, to Mr. Calloway, with a smile I had never seen on her before, a smile with frost on it: "Shall I fetch you down some tea, sir? It's a long climb for nothing."

"Not at all," said Mr. Calloway, rising, resetting his hat. "The boy and I were just getting acquainted." He patted my head once, lightly, the way you'd pat a lock to check it's shut. "Fine lad. Watchful. Takes after his father, I shouldn't wonder."

He went down. We listened to the stairs count him out, all the way, and the kitchen door, and then the yard wind swallowing him.

Mother knelt and took my chin in her hand, gentle, and turned my face to hers, and her eyes went over me again — knuckle, sleeve, the smear of blood on the floor plate, the butterfly nuts sitting a half-turn prouder than they should.

"Tomas," she said, very quietly. "What did you move?"

And upstairs in the gold turning light, with the sea booming on the headland below like something enormous shifting in its sleep, I looked at my mother — who lied to me once about my father, and was lying right now with her smile for Mr. Calloway, and had been carrying the whole truth alone up and down these stairs for a year — and I made the decision every child makes eventually, the one that turns you from a kept thing into a keeper.

"Nothing," I said. "Yet."

But her hand tightened on my chin, and her eyes went to the floor plate, and I saw her count the butterfly nuts the way she counted everything, and I knew she'd already done the sum.
