## 17. Calloway's Patience Ends

There is a particular kind of patience required of a man in my profession, and I like to think I possess it in abundance. Patience is the whole of the trade, really. The ledgers, the courtesies, the small gifts at Christmas to men whose names appear in no ledger at all — all of it is patience wearing a good coat. A firm like Harrow & Vane does not survive forty years of profitable shipwrecks on audacity. Audacity gets men hanged. Patience gets them pensions.

I had been patient for eleven days at the Kavanagh light.

I had been patient through Mrs. Kavanagh's tea, which she served in a manner I can only describe as aggressively correct — the cups polished, the milk fresh, and not one degree of warmth in any of it. I had been patient through the old man Kavanagh's silences, which were longer and more eloquent than most men's speeches. I had been patient through the boy, Tomas, who watched me with the frank, unblinking appraisal of a young animal deciding whether I was the sort of creature that bit. I had complimented the compass. I had praised the boy's manners. I had offered the widow money, passage, a fresh start on the mainland, and she had smiled at me the way a lighthouse smiles at a ship it has no intention of guiding home.

And I had been patient through the weather, which was the true insult. The storm that had closed the bridge had been meant to be the season's overture, not its whole symphony. Instead the sky had settled into a sulk — grey, windless, drizzling with the enthusiasm of a paid mourner — and the wind, my wind, the wind I needed, had gone off somewhere to sulk on its own account.

Because here is the arithmetic, and I did it every morning at the window of the room they'd given me, the one with the view of the workshop: the thaw was coming. The bridge would open. And when it opened, the first cart across it would carry a magistrate.

I had counted the days twice. I am careful with numbers; numbers are the only honest things in my line of work.

* * *

Let me say something in my own defense, since no one else will ever be moved to offer it. I did not begin that morning intending fire.

Fire is a vulgar instrument. Fire is what a man reaches for when his patience has been spent down to the wick, and I had been raised — in a manner of speaking — on the gospel of the unspent wick. My trade, done properly, is almost gentle. A payment here. A light shown where no light ought to be, or withheld where every light ought to burn. A ship's owner paid three times the value of his hull to look the other way, the cargo insured twice over, the underwriters in London none the wiser. Nobody drowns who doesn't have to. That was the firm's founding principle, if you like: *nobody drowns who doesn't have to.* We wrecked ships, not men, whenever the arithmetic allowed. The sea did the drowning, and the sea has never once been indicted.

But Dominic Kavanagh had been a complication, and complications breed complications, and here I was, eleven days into a house where every surface seemed to have his fingerprints on it.

I made my morning inspection of the workshop while the family took breakfast. This had become routine, and routine is the other half of my trade — a man who is expected somewhere is a man who is not suspected anywhere. The door was locked now. It had not been locked when I arrived. I noted that the way I note everything: quietly, in the ledger behind my eyes.

I did not force it. Forcing a lock tells a man's hand. Instead I walked the outside of the building, hands clasped behind my back, a gentleman taking the air, and I read the structure the way I read a hull.

It was an old building. That was the first thing. Timber frame, shingled roof that had been patched more than replaced, and the whole of its western side facing the sea like a cheek turned for a slap. The storm in November — the one that closed the bridge — had already taken a bite out of the roofline. I could see the newer timber, pale as a scar, where someone had spliced in fresh boards. Dominic's work, probably. The man had been competent; I'll grant the dead their due. Competent men are the most dangerous kind, because they believe competence is a form of armor.

There was a chimney, stone, sound. A stove inside — I'd seen it through the window on my first tour, before the locking began. Coal in a scuttle. And along the north wall, stacked under oilcloth, the offcuts and shavings of a winter's brasswork. Curled ribbons of metal, sawdust, a barrel of linseed oil, rags.

I stood looking at that barrel for a long time.

A workshop is a building that has spent its whole life rehearsing for a fire. Every element of the thing is present and willing: fuel, air, a source of spark. All it lacks is a decision. Most buildings never receive the decision. They live out their long dull lives and die of rot instead, which is the death of a saint compared to what I was considering.

I want it understood that I weighed the alternatives. I was not a man who leapt. I was a man who descended stairs carefully, one hand on the rail.

The alternatives were these. One: wait for the thaw, cross with the surveyors, and simply take what needed taking — but the ledgers were no longer where I had seen them, the compass was finished or nearly so, and a magistrate arriving to find a completed compass and a widow prepared to talk was a different animal entirely from a magistrate arriving to find a cleared workshop and a grieving family. Two: take the compass by force and go over the wall of the headland to the sea. The dinghy was mine and hidden; the crossing in this season was a coin-toss with the coin weighted against me, and even if the sea spared me, a stolen compass and a vanished agent was a confession written in brass. Three: do nothing, and let spring do its work.

Spring's work, I had come to understand, was the worst of the three. I had watched Mrs. Kavanagh for eleven days. Grief had made her still; suspicion was making her *precise*. She had stopped asking me polite questions and started asking me specific ones — when had I last been at the mainland office, who had commissioned the compass originally, did I know a firm called Merrow & Sons. I answered each one beautifully. I have never once in my life answered a question beautifully and had it be enough, and I knew it would not be enough here. She had her husband's ledgers. Somewhere in this house was a dead man's arithmetic, and arithmetic, as I have said, is honest.

And there was the old man. Alistair Kavanagh had come ashore claiming the right to oversee the workshop, and I had assumed at first that he was the firm's problem made flesh — the father who would want the land sold, the past cleared, the quiet bought. But I had watched him at the table across from the widow, and I had seen something that unsettled me more than suspicion ever could: the two of them were beginning to agree. Not warmly. God, no. They agreed the way two fronts agree, meeting over the boy's head, over the ledgers, over the locked door. Grief had made them allies, and allies were the one thing my trade had no instrument for.

So. The alternatives, weighed. And the scale tipped, as scales do, toward the vulgar instrument.

* * *

I spent the afternoon being charming, which is to say I spent the afternoon establishing my innocence in advance.

I helped the boy with a kite he was trying to fly in wind too sullen to lift it. I complimented the widow's stew. I told the old man a story about a storm in '81 that I had no business surviving, and he listened with his jaw set like a man biting down on a tooth he refuses to have pulled, and I thought: *you know something, old man. You've known it for years, and you came here to bury it, and now you're not sure whose grave you're digging.* That, at least, was a comfort. A guilty man is a predictable man. I had been a guilty man myself for thirty years; I knew the breed.

At supper I mentioned — lightly, the way one mentions weather — that the long-range forecast from the mainland papers spoke of a system coming up from the southwest. "Three days, perhaps four," I said. "The barometer's been falling since noon. You'll want everything battened."

Mrs. Kavanagh glanced at the barometer in the hall, because she was a keeper's wife and then a keeper's widow and barometers were her scripture. "It's been falling all week."

"Faster now," I said, and smiled, and the lie sat in my chest as comfortably as a swallowed stone. There was a system coming — there is always a system coming, off that coast, in that season; I could have thrown a dart at a calendar and hit a gale — but I did not know when, and I did not especially care. I only needed the household to believe that the wind was coming *to them*, on a schedule, so that when it arrived and did its work, the arrival would look like prophecy fulfilled rather than opportunity seized.

This is the whole secret of blaming a storm: you must not fight the storm's reputation. You must borrow it. A workshop burns on a still night, and men ask questions. A workshop burns on a night when the wind has been screaming for six hours and the whole coast is littered with the storm's other victims, and men shake their heads and say *the sea takes what it likes* and pour a drink. The sea is the most reliable accomplice in the world. It has never once talked.

The boy looked at me across the stew. He had that look again, the appraisal, the young animal deciding. I smiled at him. He did not smile back. I made a note of that, too, in the ledger behind my eyes, and I confess the note troubled me more than the locked door did. Children cannot be bribed with the things that buy their elders, and they cannot be reasoned with, because they have not yet learned that most truths are negotiable. The boy had found something — I was nearly certain of it. Ledgers had a way of walking in that house. I had seen the widow's eyes go to the lamp room once, twice, at nothing, at the ceiling, the way a woman's eyes go to the place where a thing is hidden when she forgets herself.

The lamp room. Of course. The one room in the house I could not enter without an escort, because it was the one room in the house that was holy.

* * *

That night I could not sleep, which was unlike me. I have slept soundly through the whole of my career; conscience, in my experience, is a thing one can furnish around, the way one furnishes around a bad view.

I sat at my window with a candle and watched the headland. The light turned above me — the widow kept it herself now, I had learned, climbing the tower on her own schedule, feeding the lamp with her own hands — and its beam went round and round, laying its white stripe across the workshop roof every seven seconds. Seven seconds of illumination, then darkness, then the sea, then the stripe again. I found I had begun counting without meaning to. One, two, three — light — dark.

It occurred to me, sitting there, that I had been counting the wrong thing all my life. I counted payments, hulls, percentages, the honest arithmetic of the dishonest. But the widow counted seconds of light, and the boy counted his father's tools, and the old man counted the years of his silence, and every one of them was counting toward something, while I had only ever counted *against* — against discovery, against exposure, against the day the ledger behind my eyes would be read aloud in a courtroom.

The workshop stood black under the turning light, and I made the decision properly, the way one makes a decision that will need to survive being looked at later: I made it three times.

Once as necessity. The thaw was coming; the magistrate would come behind it; the compass was finished or nearly, and a finished compass in a widow's hands was a signed confession with a glass face. It could not be allowed to exist in a form anyone could read.

Once as mercy — and I do ask you to believe I reached for that word without irony, because irony is a luxury of men whose houses are not on fire. A clean loss to the storm would close the estate, settle the land, end the widow's dangerous precision. She would grieve the building and be done. Grief, I have found, is the most effective solvent in the world; it dissolves questions that money only smears around.

And once — the third time, the true time — as fear. I will write the word plainly, since no one is left to read this who matters. I was afraid. Not of the widow, nor the old man, nor even the boy. I was afraid of the *arithmetic*. Somewhere in that house was a page with my firm's payments written in a dead man's hand, and pages do not drown, and pages do not burn themselves, and pages, unlike men, have never once agreed to look the other way.

The wind came up around midnight. Not the great wind — a small wind, a rehearsal, worrying the eaves and dragging a shutter somewhere below. I stood at the window and watched it lean on the grass and the gorse and the black shoulder of the workshop, and I felt the old familiar cold satisfaction, the tradesman's pleasure at the arrival of materials.

*Soon*, I told the building. *Soon, and blamelessly.*

* * *

The next morning I did what a careful man does: I prepared my alibi before I prepared my crime.

I asked the widow, over breakfast, whether I might walk the cliff path to the north landing — "for the exercise; I'm a poor sailor and poorer guest" — and she assented with that correct, temperatureless courtesy, and I went, and I was seen going. The boy saw me. I made sure of it; I waved. Witnesses are not only for courtrooms. A man seen walking north at two o'clock cannot have struck a match at the south end of the headland at midnight, and the sea, my accomplice, would swear to nothing.

On the path I did my last inspection. The oilcloth over the offcuts had come loose at one corner in the night's small wind. The barrel of linseed stood where it stood. The stove chimney rose clean and warm-looking against the grey sky, and I thought: a spark up that flue, a chimney fire, a dry old roof in a gale — why, the thing might almost do itself. Storm damage. An act of God. God has taken blame for my profession's work since before my profession existed; He has never once invoiced us.

I stood there in the wind with the sea going white and black below me, and I confess that for one moment — one — I saw the building as they saw it. As the boy saw it. A room that smelled of brass and oil and a dead man's pipe, where a father had taught a son to hold a chisel, where a compass had been begun in love and finished in defiance. It was, by the standards of the trade, merely an obstacle. By every other standard I could summon, it was a shrine.

I am not a man who hesitates at shrines. I noted the hesitation, filed it under *fatigue*, and walked back down the path to be charming at lunch.

The barometer was falling. I had not lied about that; I never lie about what can be checked. The system was real, or one very like it was — the southwest sky had the bruised, piled look of a thing gathering its weight, and the gulls had gone inland, and Mrs. Kavanagh stood at her window reading the sea the way I read ledgers, and I knew she knew a storm was coming, and she did not know what I intended to send ahead of it.

Three days, I told myself. Perhaps four. Wait for the wind to rise past the pitch of ordinary prudence, past the point where any keeper would bar her door and keep her family close and never think to look at a workshop until morning. Wait for a night when the light itself would be fighting for its life and every soul in that house would have hands too full to hold a lantern anywhere but the lamp room.

And then a spark, and a flue, and a dry roof, and a gale to carry the blame.

I went in to lunch and complimented the stew.

That evening, the wind began in earnest — the true wind, the one I'd been promised, hauling around into the southwest with a sound like a train crossing a trestle — and I watched the household close itself up, shutters and straps and the boy sent to secure the woodshed, and I felt the clock in my chest begin to run. Tonight, perhaps. Tomorrow at the latest. Before the wind had time to think better of its appointment.

And it was then, passing the lamp room stair on my way to my room, that I heard the boy above me — a small scuff of feet, a pause, the creak of a man's weight where no man should be standing — and the widow's voice, low and sharp, saying words I could not make out but whose *shape* I knew the way one knows the shape of a knife through a coat pocket.

I stood at the bottom of the stair in the rising dark, and I counted the seconds of the turning light above, one, two, three, and I understood that I had run out of patience at exactly the moment they had run out of time.
