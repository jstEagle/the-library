## 18. Fire Watch

I did not sleep that night, and I would like to report that this was owing to motherly vigilance or a keeper's instinct for weather, but the truth is less flattering: I lay awake composing the conversation I ought to have had with Mr. Calloway at supper and did not have, in which I said all the sharp things three hours late, arranged them just so, and won every exchange. It is a common enough occupation for widows. We are excellent at retrospective courage. It costs nothing and arrives too late to be of use, rather like insurance.

The wind was rising while I lost these imaginary arguments. It came off the water in long shoves, rattling the kitchen shutters, then easing, then shoving again, the way a man tests a door before deciding whether to break it. By two o'clock the light above me was turning through spray-haze, and the sound of the sea had changed from speech to argument.

It was Alistair who noticed the smell.

We met on the landing at half past two — neither of us pretending anymore that we'd merely come down for water — and I might have made some dry remark about the whole household keeping insomniac hours when he lifted his head like an old hound and said, quietly, "Do you smell that?"

I did. Underneath the salt and the lamp oil that always lived in the walls of this house, underneath the peat smoke, there was something thinner and sweeter. Mineral. Petroleum.

Lantern oil. And not ours burning in the lamp room, which has its own particular reek I could identify blindfolded in a crowd.

Alistair looked at me. I looked at Alistair. Somewhere below us, in the workshop where my husband's tools hung in their careful rows and his compass sat half-finished under its cloth — no. Not half-finished anymore. Finished now, save for what we had put inside it, which was the reason anyone wanted it burned.

"Tomas," we both said, which tells you everything about the order of love in this house, and then we were moving, and I will note for the record that Alistair Kavanagh, sixty-eight years old and proud of it in the way only a fit man of sixty-eight can be proud, went down those stairs faster than dignity strictly allowed.

* * *

The workshop stood apart from the tower by twenty paces of wet stone, close enough to be convenient and far enough, I understood now, to burn without taking the light with it. Dominic had chosen the site. I wondered, not for the first time that winter, how much his father had told him and when, and hated myself for wondering, and wondered anyway.

The door stood open six inches.

We kept that door latched. There was nothing remarkable about the latch itself — a simple iron hook, the kind you'd find on any shed from here to the mainland — but I had taken, in the past fortnight, to checking it the way other people check their pulse, and I knew it was thrown when I went up to bed. Now it hung loose and the door breathed in and out with the wind like something sleeping.

Alistair put one finger to his lips. I nearly laughed — a silent gesture, in a gale, outside a building already betraying us by smell — but I nodded, and we eased through the gap.

The interior was dark except for what the tower's beam lent us every few seconds, swinging through the seaward window in great pale strokes. In the first stroke I saw him: Mr. Calloway, coatless, sleeves rolled with the terrible tidiness of a man who intends to leave no fingerprints worth mentioning, kneeling at the north wall with a five-gallon tin of lantern oil tipped carefully against the base of the timber framing. He had laid a trail of it already along the floorboards, neat as a gardener sowing a drill, running from the wall to the workbench and from the workbench to the door. Methodical. Courteous, even. He would have apologized to the fire if he thought it would reflect well on the firm.

In the second stroke of light I saw the compass case sitting on the bench where we had left it, brass catching the beam and holding it, and I saw that Calloway's trail of oil passed directly beneath it.

He was not going to steal the compass. I had assumed theft — the ledgers, the instrument, tidy hands making tidy things disappear across the water. But he was going to burn it. Burn the ledgers' twin if copies existed, burn the commission letter, burn the engraving we had spent three nights cutting into the inside of the case, burn the whole testimony in brass, and call it storm damage in the morning. The wind was doing his paperwork for him. Every soul on this headland would be battened down fighting the gale, and the workshop would go up, and come spring there would be a shrugging man in good boots telling the surveyors what a shame it was, what weather, what a loss.

"You'll want to put the tin down, Mr. Calloway," I said.

* * *

I wish I could tell you my voice did not shake. It shook. But it carried, which in this family has always counted for more.

He rose slowly, setting the tin upright with a small precise click, and turned to me with an expression of genuine, warm regret — the same expression he'd worn offering me money over the tea things a week ago, the same expression he wore saying *what a tragedy about your husband, Mrs. Kavanagh*. He had a face built for condolences. I expect he practiced in mirrors.

"Mrs. Kavanagh. You should be asleep. Big night for the light."

"It is." The beam swung through the window and crossed his face, and for one flash he was lit like something pinned to a board. "And yet here we both are."

"Storm damage," he said, gesturing gently at the oil with one hand, as though presenting a finished piece of work. "Terrible thing. These old outbuildings. Dry as tinder, the pair of you know that better than anyone. The firm will want to help, of course. Rebuild. We've been very concerned for your welfare all winter."

"The firm," I said, "has been concerned for our welfare since before my husband drowned."

The words came out steadier than I expected, and I watched them land. Something behind Calloway's courtesy shifted its weight — not anger exactly. Reassessment. The look of a man discovering the invoice is larger than quoted.

"Mr. Kavanagh," he said pleasantly, finding Alistair in the dark by the door. "You're up late. At your age."

"I'm told," Alistair said, "that grief keeps strange hours."

"Mm." Calloway took one step sideways, casual, putting himself between us and the bench — between us and the compass — and I saw his hand move toward his coat where it hung over the chair back, and I saw the weight in the pocket of that coat, and I stopped calculating and simply moved.

Later, Alistair would tell me I shouted. I don't remember shouting. What I remember is the oil under my boots, treacherous as black ice, and Calloway turning with the speed of a man who had done violence before and found it tedious but sometimes necessary, and his fist arriving against my shoulder and spinning me into the workbench, and the pain arriving a half-second later, white and astonishing. Tools rained off the wall. My father-in-law's pliers. Dominic's dividers. Twenty years of careful arrangement hitting the floor in a single second.

And Alistair — Alistair, who had spent a winter standing on ceremony, who confessed his silences in increments like a miser paying out coins — threw himself bodily between me and the compass case.

Calloway swung. I don't believe he meant anything by it but clearance, the way a man shoves furniture aside. But Alistair did not duck. He leaned *into* it, wrapped both arms around the brass case, and took the blow across the temple and cheekbone with a crack like green wood splitting. He went down against the bench and stayed down, and stayed holding the compass, and the sound he made was not a cry but a short furious exhale, the sound of a man inconvenienced by his own body.

"Alistair—"

"I'm fine." Blood came through his fingers where he touched his face. "The boy's inheritance isn't. Mind him."

Calloway had the coat. He had it half-on, and his hand was in the pocket, and the wind chose that moment to hit the workshop broadside with a shove that groaned the whole frame and put out the last of my certainty along with it. Outside, the sea was no longer arguing. It was roaring. And I understood, in the way one understands things in the middle of the night with blood on the floorboards, that he did not need to win. He needed only to leave. Leave us bleeding in a burning building on a night when no boat could cross and no magistrate would come for months, and let the fire and the sea settle accounts between them.

There was a lantern on its hook by the door. Ours. Filled that morning, by my own hand.

"No," I said.

Calloway paused, one arm in his coat, and favored me with the regretful face again. "Mrs. Kavanagh. You've had a fall. Both of you. Confusion in the dark, in a storm — these things happen. Come spring, the firm will say so, and the firm's word carries."

"My word carries too," I said. "I am the keeper of this light. Have been since the Crown signed the paper, though they were careless enough to sign it to a dead man's name. When the bridge opens, I will walk across it to the magistrate's office and I will say your name, and the names in that ledger, and I will say them in a courtroom where the firm's word must stand next to a widow's under oath. Do you understand the arithmetic, Mr. Calloway? Your employers sent you to erase a witness, and instead you've made two more, and one of them is ten years old and writes everything down."

That last part was bluff. Tomas writes everything down because I make him practice his penmanship. But Calloway couldn't know the proportion of bluff to truth, and I watched him do the sum anyway, and watch it come out wrong for him.

Because here is the thing I understood in that moment, standing over my fallen father-in-law with my dead husband's tools scattered at my feet: Calloway was not afraid of the ledgers. Ledgers can be disputed, dismissed, explained as bookkeeping. He was afraid of *testimony*. A living voice, sworn, local, respectable — a keeper's voice, the very voice the firm had spent forty years arranging wrecks around. They had bought silence in this family once, cheaply, from men who told themselves it was prudence. They could not buy it twice, and everyone in the room knew it, including the wind.

Calloway put the coat on properly. Slowly. Giving me every opportunity to see that he was choosing, not retreating.

"There will be other nights," he said.

"There will," I agreed. "But I'll be awake for them."

He stepped over the oil trail — carefully, courteously, minding his boots — and went out into the gale, and the door slammed behind him with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

Then it was just the three of us: me, Alistair, and the compass, in a room soaked in lamp oil with a lit lantern hanging four feet from the puddle, which I remedied immediately and with language my son will never hear about.

Alistair sat on the floor with his back against the bench legs, holding a rag to his cheekbone, and refused assistance in the manner of a man who considers fainting a character flaw. The cut was long but shallow, running up through the grey of his eyebrow, and it bled the way head wounds do, enthusiastically and without consequence.

"You should have let him take it," he said, meaning the compass. "Brass and glass. You could have been killed over brass and glass."

"It isn't brass and glass." I knelt and took the case from his arms and set it on the high shelf, out of the oil, out of reach. "It's a witness. Witnesses get protected. That's rather the entire point of tonight."

He was quiet a while. The wind worked at the roof, and somewhere overhead the tower turned its light through the murk, steady, indifferent, faithful. Then he said, in a voice I had not heard from him all winter — smaller, and older, and honest:

"Dominic took that blow's shape from me. Did you know that? Same jaw. Same stubborn way of putting his body between a thing he loved and the world." His hand shook slightly, adjusting the rag. "When he was born I swore I'd keep him away from the water and away from the firm, and I managed the water for perhaps a year."

"You didn't keep him from the firm," I said. "You kept his secret from him until it killed him. That was the opposite direction entirely."

"Yes." He did not flinch from it. That, at least, the winter had given him. "Yes, it was."

I sat down on the floor beside him, because my legs had discovered what the rest of me had been too busy to notice, and we sat together in the wreckage of the tool wall, two people bleeding in various degrees, guarding a box.

"We finish it," I said. "Tomorrow. Tonight, if the wind gives us an hour. Every name. Every payment. Before he finds another night."

"He'll come back."

"Of course he'll come back. Men like that always come back." I reached up and took the compass case down again and held it in my lap, and the beam swung through the window and filled the brass with light for one long breath. "But he'll come back to a finished thing. Let him see what he's up against."

Alistair laughed then — a rusty, disbelieving sound that turned into a wince halfway through — and pressed the rag harder to his face, and said, "God help me, woman, you sound like my wife."

"She sounds like good company."

"She was," he said. "She was exactly that."

Outside, the gale gathered itself for the long night's work, and the light turned, and turned, and turned.
