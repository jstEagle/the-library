## 19. Engraving the Names

The storm left the way a drunk leaves a funeral: noisily, apologetically, knocking over half the furniture on its way out. By morning the wind had swung round to the west and gone quiet, and the island lay scoured and gleaming under a sky so clean it looked guilty.

I stood in the doorway of the workshop with two cups of tea going cold in my hands and counted what the night had cost us. Half the roof felted across the yard like a shed skin. The window Alistair called *your husband's extravagance* was a starburst of absence, snow drifting gently onto the bench where Dominic used to cut his gaskets. And Alistair himself was sitting up on the cot I'd dragged in there three days ago, his ribs strapped, his forehead mottled the purple of a bad plum, watching me count.

"You'll want to sweep before you inventory," he said. "You've got a roof in your wool."

"There's a roof in my tea as well." I handed him his cup. "We're eating it all week."

He drank, wincing at the grit and pretending not to. That was our arrangement now, mostly — pretending not to notice things while noticing everything. Three nights ago the man had put his body between a lantern-oil-soaked floor and Mr. Calloway's fist for the sake of a brass case the size of a dinner plate, and neither of us had said one word about why since. Some things you don't thank a man for out loud. It makes the debt feel too much like an accusation.

"Calloway's boat," I said. "Have you looked?"

"Looked." He said it the way he said everything, flat as a filed edge. "Gone, and half the oil from the store, and your good handspike with it, which is a loss to navigation if nothing else. Man rowed into that swell like he owed the sea money." A pause. "He does, of course. Just not to the sea."

"He'll come back when the thaw comes."

"He'll come back before the thaw comes if he can find a way across. Men like Calloway don't wait on bridges, Petra. They wait on opportunity, which is worse-tempered and keeps different hours." He set down the cup. "Which means we have less time than the ice says we do."

I looked at the compass then. It sat where it had sat through fire-scare and storm-shock and an old man's blood spilled near it, wrapped now in Dominic's oilcloth like a patient waiting for surgery. The card was finished. I had finished it myself, my first and only, under Alistair's grudging eye — the rose cut and balanced true, sixteen points, the needle jeweled and floating sweet. Alistair had tested it against his own pocket watch and the light above us both and pronounced it, in the largest compliment he'd issued since arriving, *not embarrassing*.

The case was finished too. Cleaned, polished, hinged right, the little glass dome seated without a bubble. Everything a compass ought to be.

Everything except honest.

* * *

We had argued about it for two days after I found the fittings cut — argued the way family argues, which is to say with the gloves off entirely and the windows rattling. Finish it clean and sell it or sink it. Break it for scrap and let the sea keep its secrets. Alistair had wanted, with real passion for a man of his temperature, to simply let it go unfinished, the way Dominic left it, the way everything about Dominic was left.

"It's poisoned work," he'd said. "Commissioned by wreckers, paid for out of drowned men's pockets. You finish it, you finish their instrument."

"And then it sits in a drawer being finished," I'd said, "and nobody ever knows, and in fifty years some other Kavanagh polishes it up and sells it to somebody's parlor, and it goes on guiding ships somewhere with nobody the wiser. That's how poison works, Alistair. It doesn't stop being poison because you put it away nicely."

It was Tomas, in the end, who'd settled it without meaning to — the way children settle most adult questions by simply saying the obvious thing out loud. He had come into the workshop looking for wire for a trap he was building for imaginary rabbits, picked up the ledger page I'd copied out fair, and read the names aloud in the careless voice children use for hard words they don't yet know are heavy. *Merrick. Ossian Drew. The Harrow & Vane line.* Then he'd looked up and said, "If you write them small enough, they'd all fit inside the case."

And that was that. There was nothing left to argue after a child has shown you the door.

So on the fourth morning after the storm, with the workshop swept of its roof and the cold coming in off the water like an unpaid bill, I laid out the tools on the bench in the order Alistair had taught me, and I opened the ledger to the first page, and I began.

* * *

Engraving brass, if you have never done it, is nothing like writing and everything like prayer. You are not making marks; you are persuading metal to hold still for them. The graver wants to run. The brass wants to spring. Your breath fogs the surface and you learn to work between heartbeats, and if your mind wanders even the width of a hairline, the letter comes out crooked and you cannot unmake it, only repair around the wound.

Alistair sat propped on the cot with his ribs strapped, dictating. That was the division of labor his pride would permit: he could not lean over a bench with those ribs, and God knew he'd have rather died than ask me to stop and fetch him anything, so he did the next best thing to holding my hand. He read the ledger aloud.

"Merrick, J. Payment rendered. Fourteen pounds, ten shillings. November the twelfth, 1861." His voice caught slightly on the year — 1861, the *Corriedale*, nine men, though the ledger never says so. The ledger just says fourteen pounds ten, as though the sea were a tradesman to be settled with quarterly.

I cut it into the inside face of the case, small and deep, in letters fine as stitching. Around the rim the numbers would march, date after date, payment after payment, thirty-one years of a shipping firm's quiet gratitude to the rocks beneath my window.

"Your eyes," Alistair said, when I sat back to blink. "Rest them every quarter hour. Engraver's rule."

"Whose rule?"

"Mine. As of this moment." But he passed me the cloth soaked in weak tea — his own remedy, imported from Edinburgh along with his opinions — and I pressed it to my lids and listened to the workshop breathe.

It sounded different with a hole in the roof. Wind came through in slow exploratory drafts, stirring the shavings, and the gulls had already begun their audit of the damage. Somewhere under all of it, underneath the cold and the drip and the gulls, the room still smelled of oil and brass and, faintly, of pipe smoke that had soaked into the wood twenty years ago and never left. Dominic's room. It had been his room the whole year I couldn't bear to enter it, and now here was I bent over his bench with his father-in-law coaching me through his craft, and I found — this is the part I still can't say plainly, so I'll say it crooked, the way true things usually get said — I found I wasn't sad in here anymore. Not the way I'd feared. The grief hadn't gone. It had changed trade. It had become something worked with the hands.

"Dominic cut his first card at eleven," Alistair said, apropos of nothing, or possibly of everything. "Ruined it. Cut the south point clean off and cried over it. I told him a compass that lies is worse than none, and he looked at me—" Alistair stopped. I kept my eyes shut behind the tea cloth and waited. "He looked at me the way you look at weather you're deciding whether to sail into. And then he started again and didn't cry, and it was true, that second card. Every point of it."

"He was twelve years finding out his father was right," I said.

"He was twelve years finding out I was a hypocrite," Alistair said. "Go on. Merrick again. January, 1862."

* * *

We worked four days. I want to be exact about that, because exactness is the whole point of the exercise — dates, sums, names, all of it cut into brass where no tide can take it and no courteous agent with gloved hands can lift it out. Thirty-one pages of the ledger, reduced to their bones. The firm's name. The ship names, which I made him give me even though the ledger omitted them, because Alistair knew them, because Alistair had known them all his life, because — he told me this on the second day, in the flat voice of a man reading his own sentence — his own brother had carried cargo for Harrow & Vane once, one season, long ago, and never asked what the extra money was for. Never asked. That was the sin Alistair had come north to bury: not Dominic's discovery, but his own decades of convenient deafness.

"I signed nothing," he said. "Did nothing. Only heard, and arranged my hearing accordingly." He watched me cut the *Ossian Drew* entries, the worst cluster, the winter of '71 when the sea gave back so few. "There's room in that case for me too, I suppose."

"No," I said, and I surprised us both by how quickly and how fiercely I said it. "The case carries the guilty. You're carrying enough already."

He didn't answer. But he passed me the loupe without being asked, which from Alistair was roughly equivalent to an embrace.

Tomas came in each evening after his chores and held the lamp for me, and I taught him what I'd learned — how to seat the graver against the thumb, how the brass tells you when it's ready to take a line. He was careful the way Dominic had been careful, and quick the way Dominic had been quick, and on the third evening he cut his first full word without a slip: *witness*. We put it at the top of the inner lid, above all the names. His word. Let it be his. An inheritance isn't the thing you leave someone, I've decided; it's the thing you teach them to make.

* * *

On the fifth day the case was full.

Not crowded — Alistair insisted on spacing, on dignity, on the whole record being legible to any magistrate with a loupe and a conscience — but full, the inner faces of that beautiful poisoned compass covered in small true lines. The payments. The dates. The names of the men who took them, Merrick and Drew and Harrow himself and six more whose names Alistair recited like a rosary he'd been carrying in his mouth for thirty years. At the bottom, beneath everything, I cut one line of my own, in letters a shade larger than the rest, because some things deserve to be read without squinting:

*This instrument was commissioned to mislead ships. It has been finished instead to testify.*

Then I closed it, and oiled the hinge, and balanced the needle, and watched it settle — north, steady, untroubled, pointing at absolutely nothing but north. That was the joke of it, the beautiful joke. All those names in its belly, all that guilt engraved finer than a spider's argument, and outwardly it was the soul of innocence: a fine brass compass, a gentleman's piece, the sort of thing a magistrate could hold in his palm and turn over twice and see nothing but good craftsmanship.

Until he opened it.

"Let him open it," I said to Alistair, wrapping the thing in Dominic's oilcloth for the last time. "Let him open it in a courtroom, under oath, in front of the whole mainland. Let it be the politest accusation anyone ever made."

Alistair was silent a while. The wind had come round again, softer now, thawing-wind, the first treacherous mild breath of a season that meant surveyors and carts and consequences. When he spoke, his voice had something in it I hadn't heard before — not warmth exactly; Alistair doesn't do warmth; it was more like weight, set down.

"Dominic wanted to testify," he said. "That's the whole of what I withheld from you, and it was cowardice, and I'm sorry for it in front of you and the boy and whatever's left of him listening. He was going to stand up in a courtroom and say all of this out loud. And now it will be said anyway, by you, holding a thing he made and you finished." He looked at the wrapped compass in my hands the way other men look at relics. "It's a better instrument than he planned. Don't tell him I said so."

"He knows," I said. Because I believed it, and because there was no one left to be embarrassed by my believing it.

That night I set the compass on the mantel between Dominic's photograph and the lamp, and Tomas sat with it a long time, turning it over, not opening it — he knew what was inside; he'd cut half of it — just holding the weight of it, learning it. Outside, the ice on the channel boomed once, far off, a great slow cracking sound, the sound of a season changing its mind.

Alistair, who had gone to the window with his tea, said quietly: "Bridge ice breaks loose three days before the crossing's safe. Mark me. Someone will cross too soon."

And far down the dark channel, where the mainland lights should have been, I saw — or thought I saw — a lantern moving. Too early. Too low. Coming on.
