## 20. The Bridge Opens

### Petra

The ice went out of the strait on a Tuesday, with a sound like a giant clearing his throat.

I had heard it described often enough — the groan and crack of the thaw — but hearing it from the lamp room at four in the morning was another matter entirely. It came up through the rock itself, through the tower's iron bones, into the soles of my feet. I stood with my coffee going cold and listened to a winter's worth of imprisonment breaking apart below me, and I confess my first feeling was not joy. It was something closer to stage fright.

For five months the sea had been our wall. It had kept Calloway out, and it had kept the magistrate out, and the surveyors, and the ferryman, and every other instrument of the mainland's opinion. We had lived in a country of three citizens — myself, Alistair, and Tomas — with one prisoner of war sleeping in the guest room and pretending to be a helpful uncle. Now the wall was melting, and everything we had done behind it would have to stand in daylight.

"You'll wear a groove in that floor," Alistair said from the doorway. He had taken to wearing his good coat around the house since the storm, as though dressed for an occasion that kept refusing to arrive.

"It's already worn," I said. "Dominic wore it first."

He grunted, which from Alistair was a eulogy, a blessing, and an apology all compressed into one syllable. He had grown thinner since the blow Calloway gave him, but the bruise had faded from purple to the yellow of old paper, and his hands had steadied. He still could not grip a burnisher properly, so the final polishing of the compass case had fallen to me, and I had done it badly and then less badly, and then, on the last night of March, well.

The compass sat on the workshop bench under its oilcloth, finished at last. Finished twice over, really: once as Dominic had planned it, all brass and bone-white card and careful needle; and once as we had remade it, with every name and date and sum engraved inside the case in letters small enough to require a jeweler's loupe and honest enough to survive one. *J. Merrow, £14, October 1871. H. Calloway, agent, £30, March 1889.* Forty-one entries. Forty-one little confessions circling a needle that pointed north no matter who wished otherwise.

Tomas came clattering up the stairs before the sun was properly up, which was his habit and always had been, as if sleep were a tax he resented paying.

"Is it today? The ice is out. Mr. Dunmore said when the ice goes out the cart comes."

"Mr. Dunmore," I said, "is a ferryman who has been wrong about the weather, the tides, and the number of his own children. The cart comes when the surveyors say the bridge will bear it."

"The ice is *out*, Mam."

"I heard it." I looked at him — ten years old and tall for it, with Dominic's jaw and, God help him, Dominic's talent for asking exactly the wrong question at exactly the right time. "And what do you say we let the bridge decide for itself?"

What I did not say: that I had lain awake half the night imagining the crossing. Not the danger of it — the bridge had been condemned in autumn and repaired by no one, and there was genuine danger — but the strangeness of seeing wheels on it again. Of hearing a horse. Of the world arriving with its paperwork.

It arrived two days later, on an afternoon so mild and ordinary it seemed almost insolent. I was in the garden turning the frozen compost when Alistair called from the gate in a voice pitched carefully between announcement and alarm, and I straightened up with dirt to my elbows and saw them coming across the span: a gray mare, a cart, two men in good coats, and a third man walking beside the wheel with the rolling gait of someone whose business was measuring things.

The magistrate was a Mr. Prewitt, round and pink and apologetic about his own importance. The surveyor was a Mr. Lachlan, who shook my hand with dirt-and-all indifference and immediately began staring at the bridge's stonework as though it had personally offended him. And behind them, unannounced, unlooked-for, came a constable in a blue coat, young enough that his cap sat on him like a borrowed thing.

"Mrs. Kavanagh," Prewitt said, removing his hat. "Forgive the intrusion. The thaw opened earlier than forecast. We took the liberty."

"Of course," I said. "You're fourteen days late by your own letter. I'd begun to think you'd drowned."

Alistair made a sound that might have been a cough. Prewitt blinked, decided I was joking, laughed, and then noticed that I had not been, and stopped.

They came into the house because there was nowhere else to come, and I set out tea because my mother had raised me to set out tea even during the apocalypse, and perhaps especially then. Tomas hovered. Alistair sat very straight with his hands flat on his knees like a man awaiting sentencing. And I found, to my surprise, that my hands were steady pouring, and my voice level, and that some part of me had been rehearsing this afternoon all winter without telling the rest of me.

"We're here regarding the estate of Dominic Kavanagh," Prewitt said, "and the disposition of the workshop land. There is a dispute—" He paused, delicately. "There has been a dispute raised by a shipping interest. A firm calling itself Merrow & Vane."

"Yes," I said. "I know."

Prewitt looked at me over his teacup. "You know."

"I know rather more than that, Mr. Prewitt. But I think you should hear it in order, or you'll think I invented it, and I'd hate to be thought inventive. I'm a keeper. We deal in facts and fixed intervals."

I fetched the ledgers first — three of them, wrapped in oilcloth, carried in from their winter hiding place in the lamp room where a boy with quick feet and better instincts than most constables had guarded them through the worst of it. I laid them on the table between the teapot and the sugar bowl, domestic objects flanking evidence.

Then I unwrapped the compass and set it down last, and opened the case, and turned it toward the light.

### Alistair

I have sat through funerals, bankruptcies, and one wedding that should have required a magistrate instead of a minister, and I tell you none of them prepared me for watching Petra Kavanagh hand over her husband's compass to a stranger with a notebook.

She did not hover over it. She did not explain it defensively, the way I might have — the way I had spent forty years explaining things I was ashamed of. She simply turned the case to the window light and said, "Read the inside cover," and then sat back and drank her tea while the magistrate read forty-one names aloud in a voice that grew quieter with each one.

That is not grief, I thought, watching her. That is what grief looks like after it has been put to work.

My own part came later, and I will record it here plainly because I have earned the discomfort of plainness. When Prewitt asked who could speak to the firm's older practices — the false lights, the wrecking seasons of the sixties and seventies, the payments my son had found recorded in his father-in-law's — in *my* — old correspondence, I stood up.

"My name is Alistair Kavanagh," I said. "Dominic was my son. I knew of the firm's practices for thirty years and said nothing, because they paid for my house and my silence cost me nothing but my sleep, and I told myself my sleep was a fair price. It was not. My son found what I had helped bury, and he went out on the water to do something about it, and he did not come back. I am prepared to swear to all of it, in writing, before God, and before whatever court will have me."

The room was quiet. Petra did not look at me with forgiveness or with anger. She looked at me the way one craftsman looks at another's joinery — checking whether it would hold.

It would hold. I had built it to.

The constable wrote it all down in a hand like a spider drowning. Prewitt asked questions — careful ones, the questions of a man realizing his tidy land dispute had opened onto something with a great many more bodies in it than expected. The ledgers were inventoried. The compass was examined, and Lachlan the surveyor, who had until then shown interest in nothing but masonry, bent over the engraved names for a long time and then straightened and said, "My grandfather was lost off Carrick Head in '72," and no one said anything at all, because there was nothing to say that the inside of a brass case hadn't already said better.

It was near dusk when the cart was loaded — ledgers boxed, my written statement signed, the compass wrapped in its oilcloth and placed, at Petra's instruction, in the magistrate's own custody, receipt given and taken. She kept the receipt. Of course she kept the receipt. That woman would keep the receipt for her own funeral.

"And the man Calloway?" Prewitt asked, packing his papers. "The firm's agent. Is he — I understood he may have been on the island."

"He left," Petra said, "the night of the great storm. In a dinghy, against advice, in weather that killed three sheep and half my fence."

Prewitt winced. "Then he—"

"Is either at the bottom of the strait," Alistair said grimly, "or on the mainland road, and I know which I'd bet on."

Petra said nothing. But she looked out the darkening window toward the water, and I saw in her face that she was not betting on the bottom of the strait at all.

### Petra

Here is what I did not tell the magistrate, because it was not evidence, only instinct: that Calloway had not drowned. Men like Calloway do not drown. They are buoyed by some property of their own composition — self-regard, perhaps, or the firm's money sewn into their coats. He had rowed away from this island in a storm because staying meant facing a keeper's testimony, and men like Calloway always choose the water over the witness. It is the one voyage they reliably make.

So when, three days later, the supply boat came across with the mail and the butcher's order and a newspaper gone soft with damp, I read it standing on the dock with the wind pulling my hair loose, and I was not surprised. I was, unexpectedly, moved almost to tears, but not surprised.

*MAINLAND ROAD, NEAR GLEN ARROW — TRAVELLER DETAINED.* The account was brief and deliciously mundane. A gentleman answering the description of one H. Calloway, agent, had been stopped at the Glen Arrow tollhouse on suspicion of passing a forged bank draft, the tollkeeper having become suspicious of a man in fine clothes paying a penny toll with a note worth fifty pounds. Constables attending had found upon his person papers "of interest to the magistracy," including correspondence referencing the island, the workshop, and — I read this sentence four times — "certain instruments of navigation."

Certain instruments of navigation. As if the compass were a weapon. Well. I suppose it was.

He had walked off my island into a tollhouse trap set by his own vanity. Justice had not come for him with trumpets. It had come on ordinary wheels, drawn by a gray mare, delayed by mud, initiated by a penny toll. I laughed until I had to sit down on the dock crates, and the ferryman's boy looked at me with concern, and I waved him off and wiped my eyes and laughed a little more.

Alistair read the article afterward in silence, twice. Then he folded the paper along its crease with the exactness of a man squaring a joint.

"He'll talk," he said. "Men like that always talk when the alternative is hanging alone. He'll give us Merrow, and Vane, and the whole rotten chain back thirty years." He paused. "He'll give them me, too. Everything I just swore to, plus thirty years of detail I left out."

"Yes," I said.

"You could have stopped me. At the table. You could have let an old man keep the last of his name."

"Alistair." I took the newspaper out of his hands and set it down. "You crossed a condemned bridge in winter to bury your son's secret, and you stayed to engrave it into brass instead. Whatever name you have left, you spent it on the right thing. Let the court have the rest."

He looked at me for a long moment, and his eyes were wet, and neither of us remarked upon it, because we were, by then, family of the sort that doesn't need to say so.

* * *

The surveyors came back the following week and condemned half the bridge again, which everyone agreed was very like them. Repairs would take until summer. But the causeway path was passable at low tide now, and the mail ran, and the world arrived daily in small parcels instead of all at once, which suited me. I had learned this winter that I preferred my catastrophes in installments.

On an evening in late April, with the light burning and the swallows returned to the gallery rail, I unpacked the magistrate's return parcel. Inside, wrapped in new oilcloth with an official seal broken and re-tied — Prewitt's courtesy, or the court's efficiency — lay the compass.

Returned. Released as evidence no longer needed, its testimony copied into the record, its work in the world formally concluded. Beneath it, a note in Prewitt's round hand: *The court has no further claim on this instrument. It occurs to me that its proper custody was never in question.*

I carried it inside. Tomas was at the table doing sums he was pretending were harder than they were, and Alistair sat by the fire mending a net he had no use for, because his hands wanted occupation and his pride wouldn't allow idleness.

I set the compass on the mantel above the hearth, where Dominic's portrait used to hang before I moved the portrait to the bedroom, where a man can be complicated in private. I opened the case. The firelight caught the engraved names and made them flicker, forty-one small witnesses warming themselves.

Tomas slid off his chair and came to look, the way he always came to look, and I braced for the question — *why did Papa take their money* — the question I had once lied to him about, the lie that had cost me something I never got back and didn't entirely want to.

But he didn't ask that. He had stopped asking it weeks ago, after the night he moved the ledgers to the lamp room, after the night he learned what his father had died trying to do. Instead he studied the needle swinging to its rest, pointing north, pointing true, and said:

"When I'm bigger, will you teach me?"

"Teach you what?" I asked, though I knew.

"To make things that tell the truth."

Behind me, Alistair's net-mending had gone very still. I put my arm around my son and looked at the compass — at the inheritance we had finally finished, not the one Dominic left us but the one he'd tried, in his last months, to build — and I said:

"Yes. First lesson tomorrow. It starts with keeping a light."

Outside, the sea ran easy against the headland, and the lamp turned above it, steady as a promise, mine now, lit in my own name.
