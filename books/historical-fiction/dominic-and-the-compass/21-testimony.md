## 21. Testimony

The mainland courtroom smelled of wet wool and lamp oil and the particular staleness of a room where men have been arguing since before breakfast. It was smaller than I had imagined. I had built it up in my mind over the winter — a great cathedral of judgment with high windows and marble pillars — and instead it was a converted customs house with a stove that smoked and a magistrate who kept wiping his spectacles as though the truth might smudge them.

I found this oddly comforting. Cathedrals are for awe. Customs houses are for business. And what we had come to do was business: the settling of accounts, long overdue.

Tomas sat between Alistair and me on the hard bench, wearing his good coat, which had grown short over the winter in the way of boys who refuse to stop growing out of spite. He had wanted to come. I had considered leaving him at the inn with Mrs. Penhale, the landlady, who had offered to keep him "amused," a word that made me suspect she owned a deck of cards. But he had carried those ledgers through a storm and nearly lost himself to Calloway's patience to protect them. He had earned his seat.

Besides, I did not want him hearing about his father secondhand. Not today.

The courtroom was fuller than I expected. Word travels strangely on the mainland — faster than ferries, slower than shame — and half the town had turned out to look at us. The lighthouse family. The widow and the old man and the boy. I felt their eyes move over us like hands checking goods for damage. Let them look. I had spent a year being looked at with pity; pity is a kind of blindness, and I preferred curiosity. Curiosity at least leaves the door open.

Calloway was not there. They had taken him on the mainland road two days before we crossed, at the toll bridge, sitting in a hired carriage with two ledgers in his valise that were not ours — copies, the magistrate said, though where a man gets copies of things he was never meant to see is a question I leave to wiser heads than mine. He had not resisted. That was the detail that stayed with me, that gnawed like damp in a wall. He had simply handed over his papers and asked, politely, whether there would be time to send a letter before the assizes. Courtesy, to the last. Some men are hanged by their manners; Calloway was merely inconvenienced by them, so far.

But his principals were there. That was why the benches were full. The firm had sent counsel — three of them, gray men in gray coats, arranged along the barristers' table like cutlery in a drawer — and the senior one, a Mr. Voss with a voice like dry paper, had spent the morning doing what lawyers do: making the sea sound complicated.

"The coast of Cornwall," he told the magistrate, "is treacherous by nature. Ships founder. Lights fail. Payments recorded in a dead man's workshop may signify bookkeeping, charity, salvage fees — any manner of legitimate transaction. We submit that grief has been permitted to dress itself as evidence."

Grief dressed as evidence. I wrote that phrase down in my notebook because I suspected I would want it later, when I needed to be angry on purpose rather than by accident.

Then the magistrate called my name, and I stood up, and my knees informed me — briefly, privately — that they had opinions about all this.

* * *

I had rehearsed my testimony perhaps four hundred times. In the kitchen, in the lamp room, walking the gallery rail with the wind trying to steal my words. I knew the shape of it: measured, orderly, beginning with the ledgers and ending with the boat fittings, each fact laid down like a stone in a causeway, so that anyone could walk across it without getting their feet wet.

What I had not rehearsed was the oath. There is something about swearing on a book in front of strangers that strips a person down to the studs. My voice came out steady enough. It was everything behind the voice that trembled.

"Mrs. Kavanagh," the magistrate said. He was kinder than I'd expected, or perhaps only tired. "Take your time."

"Thank you, sir. I've had a year of taking my time. I'm rather good at it now."

Someone in the back laughed, then thought better of it. The magistrate almost smiled. I decided we would get along.

I began where I had promised myself I would begin: with the ledgers. I described the workshop, the oilcloth, the drawer beneath the lathe where Dominic kept the payments from Harrow & Vane Shipping — thirty-one entries over nine years, sums that grew after every wreck on our coast. I gave dates. I matched wrecks to payments the way you match a key to a lock, and I watched the gray men at the table begin, very slightly, to rust.

Voss rose to object twice. Both times the magistrate said, "Overruled. The lady is establishing a pattern." The lady. I have been called worse things by the sea.

Then came the part I had dreaded, and I will tell you honestly: I had planned to soften it. All winter I had drafted versions of the next passage in which Dominic appeared as an innocent deceived, a good man tricked into bad company, his name wrapped in cotton wool and set gently aside. That was the gentler memory. That was the story I had told Tomas in December, the first lie I ever told my son, and it had sat in my chest for months like a swallowed stone.

And then, somewhere between the fourth hundred rehearsal and the ferry crossing, I had understood that the stone was the point. You do not get to choose truth and keep the comfortable parts. Truth is not a market where you haggle.

So I said it plainly.

"My husband knew what the payments were for. He discovered it sometime in the autumn of 1891 — I believe from a man named Tregose, who served as the firm's shore agent before Mr. Calloway. Dominic confronted the firm. He was paid to make a compass — a fine one, brass-cased, commissioned as a gift — and he learned that the gift was intended for the master of a wrecking vessel, that the instrument itself was part of the machinery by which ships were drawn onto our rocks. False lights. Moved lights. Lights lit where no light should be." I paused. "He was going to testify. He told his father so. Ten days later, his boat was found hull-up off the Carrick shoal, and the fittings on his mast step were cut. Not worn. Cut. Cleanly, with a blade."

The courtroom went very quiet. Even the stove seemed to smoke more respectfully.

Voss was on his feet. "The witness offers speculation. Boats are lost in storms—"

"In November of 1892," I said, "there was no storm. The log of my own station records a clear night, wind southwest at twelve knots. I know, sir, because I was keeping the light, and I have kept it every night for eleven years, and I can tell you the character of the weather on any night you care to name since 1882. That is not speculation. That is my profession." I turned to face him fully, and I confess I enjoyed the next sentence more than was strictly Christian. "You people should have chosen a widow who couldn't read her own logs."

The magistrate's spectacles came off entirely. "Mr. Voss, sit down."

* * *

They cross-examined me for the better part of two hours, and it was less brutal than I had feared and more tedious than I had imagined, which is, I suppose, the general ratio of dread to reality in most human affairs. Voss tried to establish that I hated the firm, that I had motive, that a woman alone with a mortgaged light might invent villains for sympathy or coin. I answered with dates. I answered with figures. I answered, once, with a description of the cut in the mast-step fitting so precise that the magistrate asked me how I knew such things, and I said, "Because I have repaired every fitting on that boat myself, sir, and because my husband taught me that metal tells the truth even when men don't. It was the last thing he taught me. I've tried to be a diligent student."

When I stepped down, my legs were shaking and my hands were not, and I counted that a victory.

Alistair testified next.

I had not known until that morning whether he would. He had been strange and silent on the ferry crossing, standing at the rail the whole way in the cold spray, and I had thought: he is deciding. All winter he had been deciding, the way a man decides about a tooth. On the stand, he looked smaller than he does at home — the courtroom shrank everyone, even the guilty, even the grieving — and his voice came out rough as unplaned timber.

He confirmed the ledger entries. He confirmed that Dominic had come to him in October of 1891 with what he'd found. And then he stopped, and looked at his hands, and the whole room waited, and I understood that whatever came next had not been rehearsed at all.

"I'll tell you what I am, sir," Alistair said to the magistrate, "since no one's asked and someone ought to. I'm a coward of long standing. Thirty years in the trade. My son came to me with proof that men were lighting false lamps to drown sailors on my own coast — sailors, some of whom I'd drunk with, dined with, buried. And I told him to let it be. I told him the firm was powerful, that we were nobody, that some rocks are best left unturned because under them you find nothing but more rock." His jaw worked. "Dominic argued with me. It was the only argument we ever had, God help me, and I won it, and winning it killed him. Because he went quiet instead of going loud. Quiet men drown quietly. And I have spent fourteen months telling myself the sea did it, because the sea is blameless, and the alternative was looking in the glass."

He raised his head then and looked directly at the gray men at the table, one by one, the way you'd inspect faulty workmanship.

"I named my silence here today so no one else can hide inside it. Whatever comes of these proceedings, that debt is mine and I've paid it publicly. I'd advise the gentlemen opposite to consider making similar arrangements while the terms are still generous."

The magistrate had to call a recess after that, mostly, I think, because of the noise the spectators were making.

* * *

In the corridor during the recess, Tomas found my hand without being asked about it. He had gone pale during Alistair's testimony — boys that age understand confessions better than verdicts — and I knelt down on the cold flagstones, heedless of my skirt, and looked at him straight.

"You remember what I told you in December," I said. "About your father and the money."

He nodded. He didn't trust his voice yet.

"That wasn't true. Not all of it. I lied because I was afraid, and because I loved him, and because lying seemed kinder. It wasn't. Your father took money from bad men, and then he found out what they were, and then he tried to stop them, and they killed him for it. That's the whole of it. He wasn't perfect. He was brave too late and honest too quietly, and he was ours, and I am done protecting a memory at the expense of a truth." I squeezed his hand. "You carried the ledgers through a storm. You've already done the bravest part. I just didn't want you hearing the rest from strangers."

Tomas considered this with the terrible thoroughness of ten-year-olds. Then: "Grandfather cried."

"He did not cry."

"He did. When you were testifying. About the mast step. He did that thing with his handkerchief like he had something in his eye."

"Alistair Kavanagh," I said, "has had something in his eye since roughly 1860. Don't tell him I said so."

And my son laughed — a real laugh, cracked and startled out of him — in the corridor of a customs house, and I thought: there. That is what the truth buys. Not peace, exactly. But laughter in corridors, afterward, which is a currency I had forgotten existed.

* * *

The verdicts came the following afternoon, and they arrived the way justice usually arrives: partially, belatedly, and on ordinary wheels. The firm's charter was suspended pending investigation; Tregose, conveniently deceased two years past, absorbed a satisfying share of the blame; two of the gray men were committed for trial and one, remarkably, turned king's evidence before the day was out, which tells you everything about the loyalty of men who buy compasses for wreckers. Calloway would stand trial in the spring assizes. The magistrate, delivering his findings, used the phrase "a conspiracy against the lives of seafaring men," and I watched Alistair write it down in his notebook, in his precise draftsman's hand, as though it were a measurement he intended to keep.

We walked out of the customs house into a March afternoon that couldn't decide between rain and sun and settled, characteristically, on both. The harbor lay gray-green and glittering. Somewhere beyond the headland, invisible from here, my light stood waiting on its rock, dark in the daylight, patient as an unpaid debt.

Alistair fell into step beside me. For a while neither of us spoke. Gulls argued overhead about something; gulls always have a grievance.

"Well," he said finally. "That's the end of it, then."

"It's the end of the testimony," I said. "It's not the end of anything else. Trials don't raise the drowned, and they don't finish compasses."

He glanced at me sideways. "The compass is finished. You finished it. Engraved and cased and keeping time on your mantel like a saint's relic."

"The case is finished," I said. "The instrument isn't. A compass isn't finished when it's engraved, Alistair. It's finished when it's used — when it points somebody true. And there's one person it hasn't pointed yet." I looked ahead, down the harbor road, toward the ferry that would carry us home tomorrow, toward the headland, toward the light. "Tomas asked me something on the crossing over. He asked when I'd teach him the trade. I told him when he was older. He said he was older than yesterday, and technically, the wretch is right."

Alistair made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "You're going to teach a ten-year-old to keep a light."

"I'm going to teach him to keep everything," I said. "The light. The truth. The names in the case. All of it, lit every night, whether anyone's watching or not. That's the inheritance. It took me a year and a corpse and a courtroom to understand it, but there it is." I squared my shoulders against the wind coming off the water. "Come along, then. The ferry leaves at seven, and I intend to be home before dark — because tomorrow night, for the first time in eleven years, the Kavanagh Light gets lit by Petra Kavanagh, keeper in her own name, and I mean to sleep well beforehand."

Behind us, the courthouse clock struck four, unhurried, indifferent, correct.

Ahead of us, the road ran home.
