## 22. Keeper of the Light

The ferryman who took us across in April was not the same man who had brought Alistair's letters all winter, but he had the same profession's gift for silence, and I paid it the respect of matching it. We rode from the mainland dock with the spring water slapping the hull and Tomas hanging over the rail counting gulls, and Alistair sitting with his hands folded on his cane like a man attending his own trial and finding the verdict slow in coming.

He'd testified, mind you. That was the miracle nobody in the courtroom had thought to remark upon because they were too busy picking their jaws up off the floor. Alistair Kavanagh, seventy-one years old, standing in that witness box with his collar buttoned to his chin, saying in a voice like frost forming: *I knew what the firm paid for. I said nothing for thirty years, and my son found out, and I have regretted my silence every day since he went into the water.* Then he sat down, folded his hands, and looked as though he'd rather be flayed.

"You're staring," he said now, without turning his head.

"I'm admiring," I said. "It's different."

"It's unsettling. Stop it."

I didn't stop it. There are few enough pleasures left to a woman my age, and irritating Alistair Kavanagh had turned out to be one of the durable ones — like a good lamp, you could rely on it in any weather.

The headland came up out of the haze the way it always did, slow and then all at once: the black rock, the white tower, the keeper's house with its slate roof, and behind it the workshop with its new beams where Calloway's lantern oil had never got its chance. The bridge — the new bridge, thrown up by men who worked through winter on government money and grumbled about it the entire time — stood at the island's neck, pale timber still smelling of sap.

Tomas saw it first. "The light! Mam, the paint's peeling on the gallery rail!"

"Then we'll paint it," I said.

"Both of us?" He meant *you and me*. He had stopped meaning *you and Da* sometime over the winter, and neither of us had remarked upon the shift, because some griefs change shape quietly, the way ice does, and you don't tap them to check.

"Both of us," I said.

Alistair said nothing at all. But when we made the dock and I offered him a hand, he took it — took it, weight and all — and let me steady him onto the rock, and neither of us mentioned that either. We were becoming rich in things unmentioned that were, at last, the good kind.

* * *

The magistrate's letter had come ahead of us by the mail packet, which meant it had spent two days being read by everyone between here and the county seat, and Mrs. Trelawney at the harbour office had already congratulated me twice by proxy before I'd been ashore an hour. The substance of it was this: the appointment of Dominic Kavanagh as keeper of the Kavanagh Light was dissolved by death, as appointments are; the post had lapsed to caretaker status under his widow; and the Board of Trade, having reviewed the testimony given at the winter assizes and finding the keeper's service during the storm season — I quote — *exemplary beyond precedent*, did hereby appoint Petra Kavanagh keeper of the said light in her own name, her salary to begin retroactively to the first of March, and so forth, and God save the Queen.

*Exemplary beyond precedent.* I read it four times. Then I read it aloud to Alistair, mostly to see what his face would do.

His face did something complicated, the way it always did when officialdom accidentally praised someone he loved. "They've spelled your name wrong," he said. "Kavanagh with an extra A in the second clause."

"They have not."

"They have. Look again."

There wasn't one. There never is, with Alistair. It is his way of saying *I am proud of you* without the indignity of saying it. I have learned the language. It has only eleven words, and nine of them are corrections, but I'm fluent now.

The warrant itself — the actual paper, with the seal — I put up on the wall of the front room, next to the clock that had belonged to Dominic's mother. And then I went to the workshop, where the compass had lived wrapped in oilcloth since the courthouse, and carried it inside.

Understand what this object was. Four inches of brass, a card I had cut and balanced myself under Alistair's merciless eye, a needle set on jewels Dominic had chosen and never lived to mount. On the outside it was beautiful in the plain way of well-made things. On the inside of the case, engraved in letters small enough to require a jeweller's loupe and steady enough hands to shame a surgeon, were forty-one entries. Names. Dates. Payments. The wrecks they bought, the ships they lured onto our rocks with false lights while honest sailors drowned trusting a shore that lied to them. Mr. Renwick Halliwell of the Meridian Shipping Firm. His agents. His captains. Every one of them written into the brass by my own hand, with Alistair dictating the ledger figures from memory because his eyes had failed him for fine print years ago and he would die before admitting it, so I checked each figure against Dominic's ledgers twice and he pretended not to know that too.

The magistrate had wanted it for evidence. I had talked him out of it — or rather, I had pointed out that the ledgers existed, and copies existed in three courthouses now, and that the compass could serve the living better than the archive. Truth kept in a drawer goes stale. Truth kept where people touch it stays alive. He'd looked at me for a long moment and then signed whatever paper made it legal, and shook my hand harder than he needed to, which is how men say things they can't say.

I set it on the mantel between the clock and the photograph of Dominic that Tomas had chosen — young Dominic, grinning, before the sea or the firm or the secret had got into him — and I opened the lid so the names faced upward, and I stepped back.

"Well," said Alistair from the doorway. He had come in behind me without my hearing, which at his age should be impossible and yet is a thing he manages weekly. "That's an ugly thing to keep in a parlour."

"It's the truest thing in it."

"Yes." He came further in, leaned on his cane, and regarded the mantel the way other men regard altars. "Dominic would have hated it."

"Dominic wrote half those engravings' contents in his own notebook, hidden under the floor of his own shop, planning to testify with it."

"He'd have hated it *and* done it," Alistair conceded. "In that order. He was his mother's son." He was silent a moment. Then: "She'd have liked you. She'd have liked you enormously, and then she'd have told you your bread was overproofed."

"She'd have been right about the bread."

"She was always right about the bread." He turned to go, then stopped, and delivered the closest thing to tenderness his mouth permitted: "It sits well there. The room looks finished now."

After he'd gone I stood a while longer. Eleven years in this house, and I had spent the first one after Dominic's death measuring everything by what could be carried — that had been my private arithmetic, my cold comfort. Grief in portable units: his pipe, his coat, the compass in its oilcloth, small enough to hold. As though love were luggage. As though the way through loss was to shrink it down to hand-size so it couldn't drag you under.

But you cannot carry a light. You can only keep it. And keeping is a different verb entirely — slower, less dramatic, repeated nightly whether anyone watches or not.

* * *

Tomas found the compass within the hour, because of course he did. He is ten years old and the son of a man who hid ledgers under floorboards; discovery runs in his blood.

He stood before the mantel very straight, the way children do when they sense a room has become solemn and aren't certain of the protocol. Then he looked at me, asking permission with his eyebrows, and I nodded, and he lifted the compass down with both hands — both hands, always, the way Alistair had drilled into him — and carried it to the table.

He opened it. He read. I watched him read, and watched the understanding move across his face like weather: first the pleasure of the mechanism, the needle swinging true, the card balanced so finely it barely trembled — and then the slowing, the frown, the lips moving over the engraved names, and the moment when a boy works out that the beautiful thing in his hands is also a witness, and that witnesses exist because something happened that should not have.

"Mam," he said carefully. "These are the wreckers. The ones from the trial."

"Some of them. And the payments, and the dates."

"Why is it in here? In the compass?"

"Because a compass is for finding your way," I said. "And somebody ought to make sure this one remembers what happened to the people who lost theirs."

He turned the case in his hands. His thumb found the hinge Dominic had fitted and never finished, the one Alistair and I had finished together in the weeks after Calloway fled into the storm, working side by side at the bench like two people performing surgery on the same patient and refusing to discuss their feelings about it. Tomas's thumb knew that hinge. He'd watched us make it.

"Da started this," he said.

"Your da started it. Your grandfather and I finished it. And the truth in it belongs to whoever keeps it lit."

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, the spring wind came up the headland and rattled the kitchen window in its frame, an old argument between the house and the sea that the house had been winning, narrowly, for ninety years.

"I want to learn," Tomas said.

"Learn what, love? Supper's fish, same as always."

"The trade." He looked up, and he had Dominic's eyes, which is a cruelty the sea arranged without consulting anyone, and Dominic's stubbornness besides, which is worse. "The keeping. The light and the wicks and the weights, all of it. Grandfather says I held a chisel right when I was seven. He says Da taught me." His voice caught slightly on *taught* and steadied itself, and I let it catch, because in this house we had learned that letting a thing catch and holding it steady until it resumed was better than pretending it hadn't. "I want to learn all of it. Even the hard parts. Even the brass work."

I sat down at the table across from my son and his father's compass, and I thought about lying to him. Not about anything large — just the ordinary maternal lacquer, the coat of *of course, when you're older, it's dull work, heavy sacks and scraped knuckles* that mothers apply to keep children from wanting dangerous things. I had lied to him once before, in this same war between loyalty and truth — told him his father's ledger money was honest wages, and felt the lie cost me from the first syllable, and paid for it in the courtroom months later when the truth came out anyway and my son learned it from strangers.

Never again. Not in this house. We had buried enough in this house — a husband, a bridge, a comfortable story about a good death. The ground was full.

So instead I said: "All right. Here's how it begins."

He sat up.

"It begins tomorrow at dawn, with oiling the clockwork, and you'll do it badly for a month and I'll correct you every single day, because your grandfather corrected me every single day and I resented it and it saved my life in February when the great storm took the vent shutters and I had sixty seconds to fix them in the dark. It begins with learning that the light doesn't care if you're tired or grieving or afraid. It burns or it doesn't, and ships live or die on that, and there's no room at the top of the tower for anything but exactness." I reached over and closed the compass gently in his hands. "And it ends — no. It doesn't end. That's the whole lesson. Keeping doesn't end. You don't finish it and put it on a shelf. You do it again tomorrow, and the night after, and the night after that, whether anyone's watching or not."

"That sounds terrible," said Tomas, aged ten, with perfect honesty.

"It is," I agreed. "It's the best work there is."

He grinned then — Dominic's grin, God help me — and carried the compass back to the mantel with both hands, and set it down square to the edge of the shelf, and opened the lid so the names faced upward, exactly as I had. He had watched me do it and understood without being told: you leave it open. You don't hide the witness. You let it look at the room, and the room at it, and everyone who passes learns what this family keeps.

"Can I light the lamp tonight?" he asked. "Since it's your first night as keeper. I could carry the keys."

"You may carry the keys," I said gravely. "The lighting is mine tonight. Tomorrow we begin yours."

* * *

Dusk came late and clear, the way it does in April, taking its time about the dark as though the winter hadn't earned it. Alistair declined supper in favour of a chair by the fire and the newspaper from three days ago, and complained about the shipping news, and fell asleep mid-complaint, which was his way of saying he was content. I covered him with the good blanket and he pretended not to notice, and I pretended not to notice him pretending, and that is the whole of our marriage-in-law, and it has grown on me like ivy on a ruin.

At half past eight the dark came down off the water, and I collected the keys from my son, who had polished them twice out of nerves, and we climbed the tower together — Tomas ahead, because keys-bearers go first, a law we invented on the stairs and will doubtless observe for a century.

Up top, the lamp waited, cold and enormous in its glass room. Below us the headland ran out black toward the rocks that had taken Dominic, and past them the sea lay flat and pewter-coloured, innocent as a lie, waiting for dark to start its old work. Somewhere out there were ships. Somewhere out there, always, were men willing to buy their wrecks. The world had not improved because we'd testified; I was done expecting it to. What I had instead was a tower, a light, a name on a warrant, and a boy beside me breathing fast with the honour of the keys.

I trimmed the wick. I charged the lamp. I struck the match, and the flame took, and I turned the weight-train loose, and the great lens began to turn, and the Kavanagh Light swept out over the water — four flashes, pause, four flashes — the same signature it had spoken for ninety years, except that tonight it spoke in my voice.

Petra Kavanagh, keeper. In her own name. Lit every night, whether anyone was watching or not.

Beside me, Tomas pressed both hands to the glass and watched the beam walk out across the sea, and I watched him watching it, and I understood finally — a year late, a corpse and a courtroom too late, but arrived nonetheless, the way lights arrive at last over a horizon you'd given up on — that inheritance was never the objects. Not the compass, not the coat, not the pipe. Those were only the portable griefs, the hand-luggage of mourning. The true inheritance was this verb, *keeping*: the daily, undramatic, unending act of lighting the truth and aiming it at the dark and daring the dark to do its worst.

The lens turned. The beam swung wide and came round again.

And far out, at the very edge of the reach of the light, where the pewter water met the darker sky, I saw it — or thought I saw it, and after this year I have earned the right to trust the thought — a brief answering flicker. Nothing. Probably a fishing boat's lantern. Certainly nothing.

But I put my hand on Tomas's shoulder, and I noted it in the log the way a keeper notes everything, and I wrote beneath it, in my own name, in my own hand:

*Light established. All names accounted for. Watch continued.*
