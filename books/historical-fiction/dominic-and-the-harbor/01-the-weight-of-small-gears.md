## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Edmund Ashgrove would later remember as the last ordinary day of her life.

She was at the bench in the back of Hollis & Sons, filing a click spring to within a hair of its proper thickness, when the boy from the post came through the shop door with the wind behind him and a satchel of damp envelopes. Master Hollis sorted the mail with his reading spectacles pushed up into his thinning hair, and Edmund watched his face over the top of her loupe the way she watched everything — for the small betrayals, the flickers, the moments when someone looked at her a half-second too long.

"Apprentice." He said it the way he always did, without her name. In six years he had never once used her name where a customer could hear it, and Edmund had long ago decided this was mercy rather than contempt, though some nights she wasn't sure. "Letter for you. Vell postmark." He held it out at arm's length, the way a man might hold a fish he suspected of biting. "Who do you know in Vell?"

"No one, sir." True. Entirely true, and she said it steadily because she had practiced steadiness the way other apprentices practiced their scales.

"Hm." He gave it over and went back to his ledger, already losing interest, and Edmund slipped the envelope into her apron pocket unopened, because her hands were filthy and because a letter that mattered deserved to be read sitting down, and because — though she would not have admitted this under oath — she wanted a few more minutes of being an ordinary apprentice with an ordinary Tuesday stretching out ahead of her.

She read it at midday, alone in the loft above the stable where she slept, at the one small window that faced the gray harbor of her own city.

*To Mr. E. Ashgrove, journeyman clockmaker.*

The title made something move in her chest, warm and terrible. Journeyman. She had passed no examination, signed no roll, taken no guild oath — those doors were closed to her before she was born — but Master Hollis had written her a letter of standing three years ago calling her a journeyman of the trade, and the paper lived folded inside her coat lining like a second heart.

*The master regulator at the Vell Observatory has failed. It drives the harbor clock, the tide bell, and the pilot signals, and it has not kept honest time since October. Our own men have been at it twice and made it worse. The Harbor Trust requires a specialist. Your name was given to us by the Guild Registry.*

That last line she read three times. The Guild Registry. Somewhere in the ledgers of the trade, in ink she had never signed, her name existed — or rather *his* name did, Edmund Ashgrove, journeyman — and the machinery of the world had turned over once, slowly, and produced this: a summons north to Vell, to the observatory on the cliff, to the master clock that governed a harbor she had never seen.

*Remuneration generous. Passage and lodging provided. Winter approaches; the bridge road closes with the first hard freeze. Come quickly or not at all.*

Signed by a clerk of the Harbor Trust, in a hand like fence wire.

Edmund sat for a long while with the letter flat on her knees.

Come quickly or not at all.

She thought about her father — she thought about him every day, but some days the thinking had edges — a pilot on other waters, another coast, drowned before she was old enough to be useful to anyone. She thought about the word *observatory*, which meant precision, which meant the kind of work apprentices dreamed about and journeymen lied about having done. A master regulator was the top of the whole slow ladder of the trade. Nobody handed one to an apprentice. Nobody even handed one to a woman, and the world contained no shortage of people ready to say so.

And she thought about the rest of it, the part she could not put down: *come quickly.* If she went, she would go as Edmund Ashgrove, and she would live for months inside a town that had never seen her, and every day would be a held breath.

She was twenty-three years old and had been holding her breath since she was fourteen, when she had cut her hair with the kitchen scissors and bound herself flat and walked into Hollis & Sons with a forged letter of introduction and a voice she had practiced dropping into her boots. Six years. She knew exactly what six years of disguise weighed, because she carried it daily: the binding, the deliberate clumsiness of her manner, the way she never sang, never laughed fully, never let her wrists speak. Some days the weight was nothing. Some days it was everything she owned.

But the letter said *master regulator*, and Edmund Ashgrove had wanted, for as long as she could remember, to be good at something too completely for anyone to take it away.

She went downstairs and told Master Hollis she would take the commission.

* * *

He argued, of course. Not loudly — Hollis never did anything loudly — but with the long, wearing persistence of a man who believed he owned his apprentices down to their Sundays.

"Vell is nine days north," he said, turning his spectacles around in his fingers. "And it's a harbor town. Harbor towns keep close books and closer eyes. You'll be living among strangers who'll want to know your business."

"My business is clocks," Edmund said. "I expect I'll talk about little else."

"Hm." He set the spectacles down. "It's the chance of a lifetime, I'll grant you. That's what worries me. Chances like that come with teeth." He looked at her a moment longer than was comfortable, and Edmund felt the familiar cold slide along her spine — *does he know, has he always known* — before he went on. "You'll sign the commission as a journeyman of this house. Whatever you do up there, you do it as a representative of Hollis & Sons, so don't do anything foolish."

"No, sir."

"And write weekly. I don't like not knowing where my tools are."

So it was settled, and there followed four days of the particular arithmetic that ruled her life: what could be carried.

This was not metaphor. Edmund had measured her existence in portable units since the night she left her mother's house with a carpet bag and a stolen suit of clothes. Everything she possessed fell into two categories — things that fit in a bag, and things she would have to abandon if the wrong person ever pulled open a drawer — and the second category had grown over six years until it included nearly everything soft about her. Her mother's letters. A photograph. Two dresses, sold years ago, the money spent on files and loupes.

She packed the way she repaired a movement: everything in its place, nothing extra, total honesty about weight.

Tools first, because tools were the only part of her identity no one could question. Her father's old kit roll, oiled canvas gone soft as cloth-of-gold with handling; the pin vises, the broaches, the tweezers ranked fine to finer. Her staking set, disassembled into its box, each punch wrapped in waxed paper. The good loupe and the backup loupe, because a craftsman with one loupe was a craftsman halfway to blind. Oils in their stoppered vials, padded in wool. A whetstone. Spare brass stock. A book on escapements she had copied out by hand in her neatest false handwriting because she could not afford the printed volume.

Clothes next, and here the packing became strategy. Three shirts, collarless. The good coat, cut square across the shoulders to disguise what padding did the rest of. The binding — she rolled it tight and buried it in the middle of the bundle, beneath the shirts, beneath the linen, where no landlady airing a room would stumble on it by accident. She had a system for laundering it, refined over years: late at night, wrung dry in silence, hung inside a shirt of the same color. Systems were how you survived. Systems and dullness. Be competent, be quiet, be slightly boring, and the world's attention slid off you like water off waxed canvas.

Into the bottom of the bag, wrapped in oilcloth twice over, went the letter of standing, her coin purse, and the small tin photograph of her father she allowed herself to own — turned face inward, so that even her own luggage told her no truths she hadn't asked for.

Last, almost as an afterthought, a blank notebook and three pencils. She had a professional habit of documenting everything she touched, and besides — she wrote in it that final night, testing the pencil — *a clockmaker far from home should keep records. Records are the only witnesses we can afford.*

She did not know, then, how those words would come back to her. She sealed the notebook and slept badly, and dreamed of a clock the size of a room, ticking with two voices.

* * *

The coach north took nine days, exactly as promised, and every one of them taught Edmund something about the distance between towns and the closeness of strangers.

She shared a bench with a wool factor who talked for six hours about tariffs; a widow going to her sister's, who slept against the window and woke each time the coach lurched, murmuring half a prayer; and, on the fifth day, a young man of about her own age with a salesman's smile, who bought her gin at a change-station and asked friendly questions in a friendly rhythm that Edmund recognized the way a deer recognizes a stillness in the grass.

"Clockmaker, eh? Up to Vell? What's up in Vell needs a clockmaker?"

"The observatory," Edmund said, and yawned. "Big clock. Broken. They pay well."

"But your family, though — where are they? Ashgrove's a local name, isn't it? South coast?"

"It's a common enough name." She finished her gin in one swallow, which ended most conversations, and pleaded exhaustion. Lying awake afterward in the change-station's common bed, listening to the salesman breathe two feet away, she rehearsed the lesson she had learned a dozen times and would learn a dozen more: charm was a tollgate, and every friendly question was collecting coins toward the same bridge. People didn't need proof. People needed only a story that stopped fitting, and then they needed very little else.

On the ninth day the coach crested the last ridge in failing light, and Edmund saw Vell for the first time.

It sat in the cup of its bay like something the sea had made and couldn't decide whether to keep: a crescent of gray stone houses climbing from a black harbor, masts bare and rattling, gulls hanging motionless in the wind off the water. Above the town, on the headland, stood the observatory — a white dome catching the last light like a lamp — and below it, strung out along the cliff road, the long iron span of the bridge that connected Vell to the mainland. Even from here she could see the winter shutters stacked at its approaches, ready to be closed.

She counted the town's steeples, found none, and located instead the harbor clock, driven by the observatory's master, its face pale in the dusk above the customs house. It read five minutes fast, or the observatory's light did — she couldn't tell at this distance, and the discrepancy itched at her like a miscounted wheel train.

Five minutes. On a harbor clock. On a tide-driven system, five minutes was the difference between safe water and rock.

"First time to Vell?" the driver asked, hauling the bags down.

"Yes."

"Mind the ice," he said, which seemed to be the town's entire philosophy compressed into three words, and clicked up his horses.

Edmund stood a moment in the coach-yard with her bag in her hand, feeling the wind come straight off the water with nothing between Vell and the arctic to soften it, and made her usual inventory. Tools: accounted for. Papers: sewn and hidden. Voice: pitched low. Name: worn loosely, ready to drop at a run.

Somewhere beyond the customs house a bell rang the half hour — late, she noted again, late by minutes — and a knot of fishermen crossing the yard glanced up at the sound with expressions she would think about later, much later: not annoyance, but something older and warier, the look of men checking weather they didn't trust.

The landlady her letter had promised — a Mrs. Tarnow, of Tarnow House, on Rope Walk — was waiting under the yard lamp, a small square woman wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, holding a lantern up as though the arriving coach might contain anything better than tired travelers.

"Ashgrove?" she called. "Mr. Ashgrove, the clockmaker?"

"That's me." Two words. Keep them plain.

"Well then." Mrs. Tarnow raised the lantern higher, and its light crossed Edmund's face — travel-grimed, chin shadowed by six days without a razor, hat low — and paused there for exactly one heartbeat longer than courtesy required.

Edmund's pulse jumped, once, hard.

Then the landlady lowered the light, satisfied or pretending to be, and said only: "You look frozen through. There's soup on. Mind the third stair; it complains, but it holds."

Edmund followed the lantern up Rope Walk toward the lighted windows of Tarnow House, and behind her, out on the black water, the harbor bell rang the three-quarters — still wrong, still minutes adrift, a whole town keeping time to something broken on the hill.

She would climb to the observatory in the morning, she told herself. Tonight there was soup, and a locked door, and the small nightly labor of becoming nobody.

She did not yet know that in Vell, nobody stayed nobody for long.
