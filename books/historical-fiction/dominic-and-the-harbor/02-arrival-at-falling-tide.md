## 2. Arrival at Falling Tide

The ferry that would carry me to Vell was the last of the season, and it nearly left without me.

I came down the gangway at half past six in the morning with my case bumping my knee and the wind coming off the water like something with an opinion about me. The deckhand had already cast off one line. I shouted — a man's shout; I had practiced it for years in my mother's kitchen until it stopped feeling like theft — and he looked up at me across two feet of black harbor water and made his decision based entirely on the sound.

"All right, all right." He hooked the line back around the bollard without hurry, as though the season ending were no concern of his, which I suppose it wasn't. "You're lucky, friend. Another hour and you'd be wintering on this side."

"Then I'm lucky," I said.

He took my coin and looked at me the way men look at other men: briefly, dismissively, completely. Six years in my trade and four in my trade's disguise, and I still felt the small vertigo of it every time — the relief and the grief arriving together, twins, holding hands.

The ferry was called the *Maren B.* She smelled of diesel and herring and old paint. There were perhaps eight other passengers, all of them going home: men in oilskins who slept against their bags before we cleared the breakwater, a woman with a crate of chickens who talked to them more tenderly than she would ever talk to a person. Nobody was traveling *to* Vell in November except me. That fact sat in my stomach like a swallowed coin for the whole three hours of the crossing.

I used the time the way I use most time — working. I had brought a small valise of tools and a smaller satchel of parts, and I sat near the stern where the spray wouldn't reach and disassembled a broken pocket watch I'd bought off a pawnbroker in Dorn for twice what it was worth, because its escapement was the same pattern as the one described in the observatory's letter, and I wanted my fingers to know it before my eyes did.

That is the thing they never understand, people who think clockwork is about mathematics. It is about fingers. A hand that has set ten thousand pallet stones does not tremble. A hand that has not, cannot be talked out of trembling. I have known men with brilliant minds for gearing whose work was worthless because their hands flinched, and I have known dull ones whose hands were honest as plumb lines. My father taught me this when I was nine, holding my wrist over the works of a regulator clock while snow came down outside the shop window in Ashgrove Lane.

*Steady comes from doing, Eddie. Not from wanting.*

He called me Eddie. Everyone did. Even after — even now, especially now — when Edmund was not only my name but my architecture, my daily costume, my passport through every door in the world worth walking through. Edmund Ashgrove, apprentice of the Dornish Guild of Horology, fourth year, licensed for transport repairs and field maintenance. The license was real paper with a real seal. The guild master had signed it without once looking closely at my face, because the guild master had known my father, and grief makes men generous, and my mother's letter asking on her son's behalf had been written in her best hand.

The letter from Vell was folded inside my coat, over my heart, where I kept documents that frightened me. I had read it so many times I could recite it.

*To the Guild's attention: the master clock at Vell Observatory has developed a fault in the going train. Mr. Oskar Brant, our former keeper, is deceased. Our other candidates decline travel at this season. We require someone competent, soon, as the clock regulates the harbor bells and the tide tables posted at the customs house. We can offer twelve marks per day plus lodging. Come quickly, before the bridge closes.*

Twelve marks a day was triple my apprentice wage. Before the bridge closes — that was the part that mattered, the part I'd been turning over since October. Vell stood at the end of a causeway road that flooded twice daily at high tide and became impassable in ice; the ferry ran until freeze-up and then nothing crossed but the ice road itself, if the winter was cold enough to make one, and no one crossed that who didn't have to. If I took this job, I would be sealed into that town for months. Whatever was wrong with their clock, I would be alone with it, among strangers, under a name that was a lie stitched over a wound.

I went anyway. Ambition, the guild master would have said. And it was partly ambition — a master clock is to a workshop regulator what a cathedral is to a garden shed, and apprentices who work on master clocks do not remain apprentices long. But there was something else, something I didn't have a name for yet, a restlessness like a gear with a missing tooth, catching every rotation on the same absence.

My father had drowned five years ago off a coast I had never seen. Off rocks, the inquiry said, in weather the tide tables had promised was safe. He had been pilot-boat crew then, between engagements, taking whatever work the harbor offered. The wreck was called the Wrenwreck, after the pilot boat *Wren*, and it killed eleven men, and the town of Vell had held memorials, and my mother had received a letter of condolence signed by the harbor master himself.

She kept that letter. She read it sometimes at night, I think, though we never spoke of it. There was something in her face when she did — not comfort. More like a woman rereading a bill she suspected was wrong but couldn't prove.

I finished the pawnbroker's watch somewhere off the second marker buoy, and the little anchor escape wheel turned true, and I closed the case, and the coast of Vell came up out of the gray like a rumor becoming fact.

* * *

It was not a beautiful town. I want to be fair to it, and fairness requires saying so plainly.

Vell sat low along a harbor that opened east to the sea, backed by a hill too steep for building, crowned by the observatory — a white dome, salt-stained, with a lightning rod rising from its crown like a needle through the town's whole sky. Below it the houses climbed each other's shoulders in grays and browns, slate roofs furred with the first frost, chimneys already smoking. Everything about the place faced the water. Not pleasantly, the way resort towns face water, but the way soldiers face a border. Windows were small on the sea side. Boats were hauled up and lashed down with a thoroughness that looked less like maintenance than ritual. Every third wall seemed to bear a painted mark or a carved date, and later I would learn these were high-water records, flood lines, death lines — the sea's signatures collected by a town that could not stop watching for the next one.

Even the gulls sounded different here. Harder. They screamed like things with ledgers of their own.

We came alongside at the stone quay just as the light was failing into that particular northern afternoon that starts at two o'clock and gives up by four. The deckhands worked in silence. The chicken woman went first, then the sleeping oilskins, then me, and when my boots hit the quay I felt the eyes.

Not hostile eyes. Worse. Assessing ones. Two fishermen mending net by a shed paused with shuttles mid-air. A boy stacking lobster pots turned his head like a weathervane finding wind. A woman in a doorway drew her shawl tighter and simply watched, unblinking, the way herons watch water. In Dorn, strangers arrived weekly and nobody cared. In Vell, I understood within thirty seconds, a stranger was an event, catalogued immediately and discussed by nightfall. By morning, everyone in town would know that a young man had come on the last ferry with one case and fine tools, hired for the observatory clock, walking with his chin up as if he owned the frost.

Good, I told myself. Let them know the clockmaker came. That is exactly what a clockmaker should be.

But knowing a thing and being easy with it are different trades.

I asked directions from the pot-stacking boy, offering him a coin he refused with visible offense, and he pointed me up Harbor Street to Mrs. Tarnow's lodging house — *the* lodging house, apparently, there being no other — and then, because boys will, added commentary.

"You're the clock one, then. For the big clock, up top." He squinted at me with frank curiosity. "Oskar Brant died in June. Nobody thought anyone would come."

"Someone came," I said.

"Not many someones come anymore." He said it without self-pity, as plain fact, and went back to his pots.

Harbor Street ran parallel to the waterfront, cobbled, wet, lined with the honest ugliness of working buildings — chandlery, cooperage, the fish market shuttered for the season, its scales hanging empty like gallows. Halfway along stood the customs house, a squat brick affair with a flagpole and a board beside the door where the official tide tables were posted in glass. I slowed without meaning to. Habit. Clockmakers read anything with numbers on it.

High tide today: 4:41 in the morning, 5:03 in the evening. Neap range. The figures were chalked in a careful clerk's hand, dated, initialed. H.M.'s office, the notation read. Approved: C. Hale.

And beneath the board, screwed to the brick, a bronze plaque, green with age:

*In memory of those lost in the wreck of the pilot boat WREN, 14th October 1906. The sea keeps what it loves.*

Eleven names. I knew the tenth one before I found it, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. But I stood there in the wind and made myself read down the list properly, one name at a time, because it seemed like a cowardice to skip to his — and because I wanted, in some stubborn corner of myself, to see his name among strangers' names, in a strange town, and feel nothing but the ordinary cold.

*Asher Ashgrove.*

The wind came off the harbor and pushed my coat against my back like a hand hurrying me along, and I walked, and did not look at the plaque again.

* * *

Mrs. Tarnow's house was the third past the chandlery, narrow and tall, with a blue door that had once been a better blue. She answered my knock before I finished knocking, which told me the boy's news had outrun me already, or else she had been watching the quay through glass all afternoon, which I later learned she had.

"You'll be the clockmaker." She was perhaps sixty, built like a keel, with gray hair pinned in a crown and eyes the color of wet slate — quick eyes, that went over me once, head to boots, in maybe a second and a half. I felt the look pass over my wrists, my throat, the line of my jaw. I had learned not to flinch during such inspections. Flinching confirms suspicions the way scratching confirms fleas.

"Edmund Ashgrove," I said. "The guild sent word ahead?"

"It came Tuesday." She stepped aside to let me in. "Room's top floor, back, overlooks the yards. Bathwater's Tuesdays and Saturdays, and it's Saturday, so mind you don't waste it. Supper's at six. I don't cook twice and I don't wait."

"Understood."

"And you'll want to know—" She paused, and something moved behind the slate eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "You'll want to know the observatory keeper expects you tomorrow morning, not tonight. He keeps churchyard hours, that one. Up at dawn, abed at dusk, and God help whoever rings at either end of it."

"The keeper? The letter mentioned the keeper had died. Oskar Brant."

"Brant kept the stars. Corven Hale keeps everything else." She said the name flatly, but the flatness had weight to it, like a stone laid carefully rather than dropped. "Harbor master. He'll come round to look at you before the week's out. He looks at everyone. Comes to my table some Sundays — widower, eats like a man doing penance, tips the girl like a man buying forgiveness. You'll see him soon enough."

I carried my case up four flights to the room under the roof, which smelled of cedar and old soap and, faintly, of the sea, which I would learn was inescapable — it was in the plaster, the blankets, the very bread of Vell. From the window I could see neither the harbor nor the observatory dome, only a canyon of slate roofs and, far below, the yard of the cooperage where barrels lay like sleeping animals.

I unpacked methodically. Tools in the order of use. Spare linen. The letter of commission, moved from coat to under-floorboard. The pawnbroker's repaired watch, wound, keeping time on the sill like a small brave assertion that time could be kept at all.

Then I sat on the bed's edge and listened to the town settle into its evening: gulls, rigging, a bell — the harbor bell, cracked or badly adjusted, striking the hour with a sound like a spoon dropped on stone. Somewhere a dog. Somewhere a woman calling children in from the dark. A town buttoning itself up against the season, patient, watchful, waiting for ice.

I should sleep, I thought. Dawn start. Long walk uphill. A master clock with a fault in the going train, twelve marks a day, in and out before the causeway froze — that was the plan entire, simple as a lever, and I repeated it to myself the way one repeats a password crossing a border.

At six I went down to supper, and it was there, at Mrs. Tarnow's table, that I saw Corven Hale for the first time — though *saw* is too clean a word for it. He did not come to dinner. He came to the door.

* * *

I heard the knock, and Mrs. Tarnow went, and her voice dropped into a register I hadn't heard her use — softer, warier — and then she stepped back and he filled the doorway.

He was tall, mid-forties, built like the harbor pilings, with a beard going iron-gray and a heavy coat still bearing the damp of the quay. And the first thing — the only thing, really, for the first several seconds — was his face. It wore grief the way other men wore scars: openly, almost as ornament. His eyes were red-rimmed. His mouth was set in the particular downward curve of a man perpetually holding back some statement of sorrow. He looked, I thought, like a monument to loss that had learned to walk.

"Missus Tarnow. Forgive the hour." His voice was deep and slow, a voice for reading names aloud. "I won't keep you. Only — the ferry came in today."

"It did."

"Aye. It did." He stood there as if the sentence had cost him something. "Last one till spring, then. Last one." He passed a hand over his eyes, and I realized with a small shock that he was not performing; the grief was real, worn smooth by five years of handling, like a coin rubbed featureless. "The new clockmaker — is he in?"

My spine went rigid before my mind caught up and ordered it relaxed.

"He is. At my table, eating my mutton." Mrs. Tarnow's tone made clear that both facts were sacred.

"Of course. Of course. I wouldn't disturb his supper." And then he leaned slightly, deliberately, past her shoulder — and looked at me.

It lasted two seconds. Perhaps three. His red-rimmed eyes found mine across the lamplit room, over Mrs. Tarnow's shoulder, and in them I saw nothing hostile, nothing suspicious — only that vast, public sorrow, and beneath it, at the very bottom, something I could not then name. Something that did not match the sorrow above it, the way a shadow does not always match the shape that casts it.

He raised one hand — a small salute, courteous, harbor-masterly. "Welcome to Vell," he called to me, and the words carried strangely, formally, like the opening of a service. "Mind the stairs up the hill. They've taken better men than either of us."

Then the door closed on him, and Mrs. Tarnow came back to the table, and we ate mutton, and neither of us spoke of him, and the cracked bell struck seven below us like a dropped spoon.

I lay awake long after, listening to the wind organize itself for winter, turning his face over in my mind as I would turn a mechanism I couldn't identify. Grief-stricken, yes. Genuine, yes. And yet.

A man may wear mourning honestly, my father might have said, and still have done the burying himself.

Sleep came late and thin. At dawn I rose, dressed, bound my hair flat, checked my reflection in the spotted glass — a young man, pale from travel, competent, forgettable — and loaded my tool case.

Up the hill, then. To the observatory. To the heart of the great clock that kept Vell's time and rang Vell's bells and regulated, in brass and ink, the very tides that had taken my father.

I did not know yet what was waiting inside its case.

I would know by nightfall.
