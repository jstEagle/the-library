## 3. The Heart of the Observatory

The observatory keeper's name was Dagny Skov, and she did not believe in talking.

She let Edmund in at seven sharp the morning after her arrival, unlocked the tower door with a key on a chain around her neck, and said exactly four words: "Clock's on the top." Then she went back down the stairs and left Edmund alone with two hundred steps and a silence so complete it had texture.

Edmund climbed. The stairs spiraled inside the tower wall, stone worn smooth at the center by a century of feet, and every turn brought a narrow window and a wider view of Vell below — the gray rooftops, the harbor with its masts like a field of winter wheat, the breakwater where the sea broke itself apart over and over without complaint. She counted the windows to keep from counting anything else. Forty-one steps per revolution. Nine revolutions.

At the top, the clock room.

It smelled the way all good clock rooms smelled: oil, brass, dust warmed by friction, the faint mineral breath of old stone. But under that, something else. Something sharper. She stood in the doorway a moment, breathing it in, letting her instincts do what they were made to do.

Ozone, almost. Fresh filing. Metal cut recently enough to still hold an edge in the air.

The master clock dominated the room — a regulator built by some long-dead craftsman whose name was inlaid in brass above the dial, a case of black walnut tall as two men, its glass doors catching the gray light from the cupola above. This was the clock that kept time not for one town but for every ship's chronometer in the harbor, every tide table, every ledger entry, every almanac printed south along the coast. When this clock spoke, the sea listened.

Or it was supposed to.

Edmund set down her bag, rolled her sleeves, and looked at the pendulum.

It was swinging wrong.

Not stopped — stopped would have been honest. It was swinging *short*. A master regulator should describe a slow, dignified arc, seconds measured out in full amplitude, each swing identical to the last like breaths in deep sleep. This one moved in quick, shallow flicks, as if the clock were panting. A clock that panted was a clock losing amplitude. Losing amplitude meant losing isochronism. Losing isochronism meant that somewhere in the train, something was eating the energy that belonged to the pendulum, and the whole harbor was running fast or slow and nobody knew by how much.

She opened the case.

The lock was trivial — older than she was, and the keyhole gaped like a loose tooth. The glass doors swung wide on hinges that needed oil but hadn't been denied it; someone had maintained the outside of this machine with love. That was the first wrong note. Clockmakers are consistent creatures. A man who oils his hinges does not neglect his escapement.

Unless he wants the escapement neglected.

She got her lamp from her bag, lit it, and hung it on the case's inner bracket, and then she leaned into the works, and the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the season.

The escapement had been altered.

Not worn. Not gummed with old oil or chipped by accident. *Altered.* The pallet stones had been filed — both of them, symmetrically, expertly — reducing the impulse by perhaps a fifth. Someone had understood exactly how much they could take before the clock stopped entirely, and taken a hair less than that, leaving the machine alive and lying. And the work was good. Damnably good. Whoever had done it knew movements the way a butcher knows anatomy. Under the lamplight the fresh file marks caught in bright threads, silver-bright against the duller patina of fifty years, and Edmund could see the ghost of the original polish where the stones had sat before the steel touched them.

Within the season. Weeks, maybe less.

She sat back on her heels in the dark of the case and felt her heart do something complicated — a stutter, a drop, the physical signature of a thought assembling itself faster than she wanted it to.

Sabotage. In an observatory. On the master clock of a harbor town, five years after a wreck everyone in Vell spoke of the way people speak of weather that has killed them.

Why would anyone sabotage a clock?

Her hands, steady as always when they held tools, answered before she did. They went back in. They began, gently, systematically, to explore — the way she'd been taught, her father's low voice in her memory: *when something is wrong, don't look for the wrong thing, look for the thing that's hiding from you.*

She checked the maintaining power. Intact. The winding barrel — sound. The second wheel, third wheel, fourth — clean, oiled, correct. The motion work driving the hands — fine. Everything downstream of the escapement was cared for like a child. Everything upstream too. Only the escapement had been touched, and only just enough.

Which meant the sabotage wasn't meant to destroy the clock. It was meant to make the clock untrustworthy. To make its time approximate. To give whoever kept the official records a permanent, deniable margin — *the master clock runs fast, everyone knows it, we calibrate against observation* —

Observation. Tide observation.

Her father had calibrated instruments against harbor clocks for thirty years. He'd trusted them the way priests trust scripture.

She was reaching up to check the upper works — the part of the movement she could only reach by standing on the case's foot rail, arm buried to the shoulder — when her fingers found paper.

It was folded small, flat, wedged behind the great wheel's upper arbor where no hand would go short of full disassembly, wrapped in waxed cloth against the oil. Her whole body went still. Even her breathing. In the lamplight her own shadow lay huge across the ceiling of the case, bent over the machinery like a question mark.

She drew it out.

Cloth first, unwound. Then paper — several sheets, folded together, light as moth wings, and when she opened them her lamp flame shivered because her hand was no longer perfectly steady.

Tide ledgers.

Handwritten columns. Dates. Times. Heights in feet and inches. The meticulous, repetitive script of a man recording readings night after night — and she knew before she read a single number that these were not copies. These were originals. The ink had feathered differently page to page, the way ink feathers when it dries in real conditions, on real nights, in a real recording station with damp in the air. Nobody fakes that. Nobody bothers.

And the dates—

Five years back. Autumn. The pages ran forward through October toward November like a road, and Edmund walked down that road knowing exactly what waited at the end of it, because her father had died at the end of it, and she had spent five years telling herself it was weather, it was luck, it was the sea doing what the sea does.

The night of the Wrenwreck, these ledgers said the tide stood four feet lower than the official record claimed.

Four feet.

Four feet was the difference between a channel a pilot could trust and rocks wearing water like a disguise. Four feet was the difference between coming home and not. Her father had gone out that night on the official tide reading — high water at 9:40, safe passage over the Kettle Stones — and the Kettle Stones had opened the *Wrenn* like a tin of sardines, and nine men had gone into water cold enough to stop a heart in six minutes, and the official record afterward said the tide had been exactly where the almanac promised, an act of God, a tragedy of navigation, nobody's fault.

These pages said somebody's fault. Said it in ink. Said it in a hand that had shaken slightly on the last entries — she could see the tremor in the numerals now that she looked, the letters leaning as if flinching from what came next.

She read the final page three times. The columns marched to 11:20 p.m., the hour the Wrenn struck, and there the handwriting simply stopped, mid-row, the pen lifted, the rest of the line blank. As if the man writing had heard something through the walls of the recording station and set down his pen forever.

Below the ledgers, folded smaller, was a single sheet in a different hand — cramped, hurried, a letter begun and abandoned:

*I cannot carry this out of the harbor and I will not burn it. If I am found with it my sister loses everything. Whoever finds this, the official tide tables for the night of 14 October are false. The true readings are attached. I watched him change them himself the next morning. He knows I know. God forgive me for waiting this long.*

No name. No signature. Just that, and the wax cloth, and the dark of the clock to keep it.

Edmund sat down on the floor of the clock room with her back against the walnut case and the papers spread across her knees, and for a while she did nothing at all. The pendulum flicked its shallow lies beside her. Above her, through the cupola glass, the sky went on being gray.

Cold shock, she thought distantly. This is what it feels like. Not heat, not tears. Cold. Like touching metal in frost — the warmth leaving your hand so fast it burns.

She had imagined grief as finished. Five years is a long time; she had buried her father twice, once in the ground and once in the ordinary business of living, and she had believed the second burial took. But here was the past knocking through brass and steel, patient as any mechanism, and here was her heart opening the door.

He had done nothing wrong. That was the thing that broke her open, the thing she hadn't known she was waiting five years to learn. He had navigated perfectly by a false tide. He had trusted the harbor, and the harbor had lied, and the lie had been written down and certified and mourned over publicly by the very man who—

Corven Hale.

She saw him again as she'd seen him yesterday on the quay: the heavy shoulders, the face carved for sorrow, the black band still worn five years on. *A man married to his grief*, Mrs. Tarnow had said over supper, approvingly. And Hale kept the tide records. Hale certified the tide records. Hale's office printed the tables her father had steered by.

And Hale — or someone under his hand — had recently, carefully, expertly sabotaged the master clock that would have exposed any discrepancy between the official tide times and the true ones.

Because someone had found the ledgers. Or was about to. Because the clock was being repaired — she was here to repair it — and a clockmaker with the case open might put a hand into the dark and find more than a broken escapement.

The papers had been hidden where only disassembly would reveal them. The clock had been slowed just enough to matter. And she, Edmund Ashgrove, summoned from the guild for her skill and her silence, was the first person in five years to stand inside this machine with a lamp and a reason.

*He knows someone found the ledgers,* she thought. *Or fears it. And when the repair is done and reported—*

She stopped herself. One fear at a time. Fear, like a train of wheels, moved in order.

She looked down at the papers on her knees, and beneath the cold, beneath even the grief, something else was kindling — something she recognized from her father's workshop, from the moment a broken mechanism finally showed her its secret. Purpose has a feel to it. Clean. Precise. A click, like a pallet dropping into place.

Her father's name was in these numbers. Not written — but proven. Absolved. Somewhere in Vell, presumably, these same facts existed in their falsified form, signed and sealed, and here in her lap was the truth that contradicted them, line for line, tide for tide.

She refolded the papers along their original creases. Wrapped them in the waxed cloth. And then she paused, holding them, and thought about what happened next with the cold clarity her trade demanded.

If she returned them to hiding, they'd be destroyed within weeks — Hale would hardly leave a landmine under his own clock once the repairman's visit ended. If she carried them out openly, she'd be searched, robbed, and quietly drowned before the bridge closed; harbors are good at making things disappear.

If she copied them—

She stood, folded the cloth flat, slid it back behind the great wheel's arbor exactly as she'd found it, and closed the case. Let the clock keep its secret a little longer. Let the hunter believe his hiding place held.

Then she took out her notebook — the plain one, not the one with her tool lists — and began, in a small hand, to copy columns.

Outside, the wind came off the water and pressed against the tower, and the pendulum flicked and flicked, keeping its false time, and Edmund copied the true one, line after line, her father rising off every page like breath on cold glass.

Near noon, boots on the stair. Dagny Skov's voice through the door, flat as slate:

"Harbor master's coming tomorrow. Wants to watch you work."

The pen stopped.
