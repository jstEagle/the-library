## 4. Her Father's Name

Edmund did not go back to Mrs. Tarnow's that night.

She told herself it was the clock—that a master mechanism half-dismantled could not be left alone, that any apprentice worth her tools would sleep on a cot of gears before abandoning an open movement. It was even true. But truth and excuse had worn the same face for so long she could no longer tell them apart, and so she banked the observatory stove, hung her coat over the broken case like a curtain, and sat on the floor with three ledgers in her lap while the harbor wind worked at the shutters.

The lamp threw her shadow huge against the stone wall. She had the sudden irrational thought that the shadow was someone else—someone taller, broader-shouldered, the man everyone in Vell believed they'd hired—and that he was watching over her shoulder as she read.

*Read, then,* she told him. *Tell me if I'm wrong.*

The ledgers were tide books, the kind kept in every pilot office from here to the capital: date, hour, height of water, wind, remarks. The handwriting changed every few pages, which was correct—a station passed between clerks, each hand leaving its signature in ink. She knew ledgers. Her father had kept one at their kitchen table every Sunday night of her childhood, copying his fair book while she practiced cutting gear teeth into soap. He used to say a ledger was a clock made of numbers instead of wheels. *Wind it honest every week and it will never lie to you.*

She started five years back, the autumn before the wreck.

For two hours nothing happened. That was the cruelty of evidence—it looked like nothing. Columns of figures, weather notes, the small bored complaints of clerks (*herring run late; new boy useless; rain again*). She read the way she worked, one tooth at a time, and let the mechanism assemble itself in her head.

Then, on the ninth of October, the hand changed.

Not gradually, not the natural drift of one clerk laying down the pen and another taking it up. The entries simply stopped being written by tired men and became written by a tidy one. The slant went vertical. The numerals grew uniform, machine-perfect, the work of someone who had been taught bookkeeping properly and never once made an error since.

Edmund laid her finger under the first entry in the new hand.

*9 Oct. 06. High water 21:40, height 14 ft 2 in., wind SW fresh, channel clear.*

And beneath it, in the official register—the one published for pilots, the one her father would have copied into his own book before taking the *Wren* out—she remembered, with a violence that made her teeth ache:

*High water 22:10. Height 12 ft 6 in.*

Thirty minutes. Twenty inches.

Her father had gone out on the ebb believing the channel would float his boat. The true tide said it would. The published tide said it would not—that he had four more hours to wait, that the sandbar off Gull Teeth lay bare and waiting. Any pilot in the world trusts the printed tide table over his own eyes; that is what the tables are *for*. You cannot sound your way through black water in a rising gale when the book tells you there are fourteen feet under your keel.

The book had lied.

Edmund sat very still. The stove ticked. Somewhere below in the works of the master clock, the great pendulum swung on regardless, patient as geology, and she felt a kinship with it she had never felt with another living thing.

She turned pages with hands that did not shake. That was the gift of her trade—you learned to hold fear in your fingertips and keep them steady anyway, because a trembling hand ruins a train of wheels beyond repair. The pattern repeated through October. True readings in the tidy vertical hand, filed away where no pilot would ever see them. False readings in the same hand, entered in the public register with dates shifted, heights inflated, times moved by minutes or half-hours. Not every day. Only certain days. Days when the discrepancy would matter—when a ship needed to move and could not afford to wait, or when someone wanted a ship to move *now*, tonight, whatever the water actually said.

On the eleventh of October, the remarks line in the true ledger was not blank, as remarks lines usually were. Someone had written, in that same tidy hand, a single word:

*Wren.*

Edmund closed the ledger.

She stood up. She walked to the window, because standing up and walking somewhere was the only alternative to screaming, and screaming in the observatory would carry down the hill into Vell like a bell.

The harbor lay below, black and still, masts against the lamplight of the quay. Five years ago her father's boat had come apart on the rocks at the mouth of that harbor, and the town of Vell had held a memorial, and Harbor Master Corven Hale—then only assistant harbor master, she now understood, newly promoted into keeping the tide station himself—had stood at the front of the crowd with his hat off and his eyes wet, and the town had loved him for it.

*Channel clear,* the ledger said for the ninth of October. And for the eleventh, a word: *Wren.* As if the wreck were not an accident recorded but a delivery noted. As if her father's death were an item received into inventory.

Her father's name was Tomas Ashgrove. She had not said it aloud in four years—not since the guild examiner had asked for next of kin and she had given it in a voice pitched low, and watched the man write *father deceased* without looking up. Saying names aloud costs something. She had been spending everything else for years—her hair, her voice, her posture, seven years of never letting her shoulders relax—but the name she had hoarded, kept in the last sealed room of herself.

She said it now, to the dark window, to the harbor that had killed him.

"Tomas Ashgrove."

It came out as a whisper. It was enough.

* * *

She worked until dawn, and the work saved her, because grief unstructured is a flood and grief structured is a millwheel—you can make it grind something.

She ruled columns in the back of her notebook. For each falsified date: true reading, published reading, delta, weather, and—once she cross-checked the shipping pages of the *Vell Advertiser* she'd found lining a drawer—what vessel had sailed that night. Nine discrepancies in October of '06 alone. Fourteen across the following winter. The tidy hand appeared in the spring of '06 and ran unbroken through the wreck and long after; whoever kept the true station records from that month forward had also manufactured the public ones, and had done it carefully, competently, over years.

Corven Hale had been made acting keeper of the tide station in September of 1906, after the old stationer died. She'd heard it twice since arriving in Vell, always told the same way, always fondly: how Hale had stepped into the dead man's boots and kept the books so beautifully the port authority had made the post permanent within a season. *A tragedy brought out the best in him,* the ferry clerk had said. Everyone agreed the Wrenwreck had been hard on Hale. He'd known her father, people said. They'd been friends.

Friends.

Edmund looked at the word *Wren* again and understood something that made the room colder than the stove could answer. A stranger falsifies tides for money—a wrecker paid to lure ships onto rocks, an insurer's fraud, some sordid arithmetic of salvage. She could have understood that, in the way you understand anything done by strangers. But this was not the work of a stranger. This was a man who had sat at a desk with his friend's boat in his mind, entering fourteen feet where the sea offered eleven, and then walked down to the quay and stood beside the widow and the orphaned child with his hat in his hands, and had gone on doing it, month after month, year after year, because stopping would mean admitting the first time.

She found the motive eventually, or the shape of it, in the shipping notices. In the autumn of 1906 the *Merrow*, a grain ship under charter to a firm with heavy penalties for late delivery, had needed to leave Vell on the ninth of October on a falling tide. The true tide said wait until morning. Waiting cost money. And there was a second entry, three days later, a coastal steamer whose owner owed Hale's family something she could not reconstruct from paper alone—but the pattern was plain. Every falsified tide had cleared a vessel early. None of them had wrecked. Except one.

Except hers. Except the one night the man at the tide desk made his arithmetic serve the wrong boat—or failed to check which pilot had taken the job, or didn't care, or—

She stopped herself. She was building him a defense out of nothing, and she caught her hands doing it, the same hands that had trembled on the ferry coming in, wanting the world simpler. Wanting him to be a monster or a victim rather than both.

She ruled a final column instead: *evidence quality.* Which pages proved what. Which entries, extracted and sworn, would hang the falsification on the tidy hand beyond any argument. She marked nine pages with slips of paper torn from a blotter. Then, because nine pages in an observatory were nine pages a fire or a search or a convenient spill of oil could erase, she began to copy.

* * *

It was nearly light when she went back inside the clock case to fetch her calipers, and saw the sabotage properly for the first time.

She had noticed it yesterday, of course—had diagnosed the failure before her coat was off, because that was why they'd sent for her. The master clock drove the harbor's time signals: the drop-ball on the tower, the electric impulse to the quay clock, the bells. Its escapement had been failing, irregularly, in a way that mimicked wear. She had assumed wear. Clockmakers assume wear the way doctors assume age.

But she had spent the night among the ledgers, and the ledgers had taught her to look for intention in small deviations.

She took the pallet stones out and held them to the lamp. Wear polishes a stone evenly, along its working arc. These were polished unevenly—scored, actually, with fine scratches too regular to be accident. Someone had abraded them deliberately, with emery or fine sand, just enough that the escapement would lose time erratically: sometimes minutes a day, sometimes nothing, a fault no regulator adjustment could cure and no ordinary inspection would catch, because the stones still looked serviceable. Then, deeper in the movement, she found the second kindness of the saboteur's hand: the maintaining-power spring, the device that keeps a clock running during winding, had been bent a fraction out of true. Wind the clock carelessly—as a busy keeper winding a big mechanism at speed inevitably would—and the power would interrupt, the hands would stop, and the fault would look like the escapement's fault. Two defects, each covering the other. Elegant work. She might have admired it in another life.

And it told her the timing.

Because a sabotaged clock does not stay sabotaged. Escapement stones abrade themselves smooth with running; the maintaining spring would work-hardened back toward true with use. The faults were self-healing—slowly, over months. Whoever bent this clock had built a device that would fail convincingly through the winter and recover by spring, requiring a repairman, requiring the case opened, requiring someone to find what was hidden among the works—or requiring nothing at all, if the hidden thing could be quietly removed once the season of scrutiny had passed.

Hidden until spring. Destroyed before anyone thought to look.

Her eyes went to the cavity behind the strike train where she'd found the ledgers. It was not a natural space. Someone had cut it—recently; the solder joints were bright, newer than the rest of the work, done by a competent hand that was not a clockmaker's hand but was not careless either. And cut into the inner face of the plate, where no one would see unless the whole train came out, was a small chiseled mark. Not a maker's mark. An arrow, pointing inward. Toward the hiding place.

A reminder. His own signpost, so that come March he could find his buried papers without dismantling the movement blind.

Edmund stood in the cold heart of the harbor's clock with the lamp raised, and laughed—one short breath of it, no humor in it at all, fogging in the air. He had used her. Not personally; he didn't know her, couldn't have. But the summons to Vell, the commission, the urgent wire about the master clock failing—she would bet her best loupe that the repair request had traveled through channels Hale controlled. He had needed the clock opened by an outsider precisely because no insider could be trusted not to gossip, and an apprentice from two hundred miles away would fix the escapement, close the case, take his fee, and vanish before the ice melted. A traveling pair of hands. A temporary lid on his grave of papers, hired by the season, gone by thaw.

Instead he had sent for her.

*Tomas Ashgrove's daughter.*

She set the lamp down. She took out her notebook and wrote, in the small compressed hand she used for calculations that mattered:

*He does not know what he has summoned. Keep it so. Work well. Be dull. Be invisible. Copy everything. And when the ice road opens, walk out past all three checkpoints carrying his whole rotten history sewn into my coat.*

She read it twice. Then she blew out the lamp, because dawn was graying the east windows, and a clockmaker observed in an observatory at odd hours is a clockmaker asked questions.

Below the hill, Vell was waking. Smoke stood up from the chimneys, straight in the windless cold. Down on the quay, small and distant, a figure in a harbor master's coat stood at the mooring posts looking out at the gray water, and even at this distance Edmund knew the set of those shoulders, because she had seen them in the crowd at the ferry landing and again outside the customs house—Corven Hale beginning his morning ritual of being grieved and beloved.

She watched him for a long moment through the glass, this man who had written her father's name in a ledger between a herring note and a blank remark line, and she found that the fear was still there, exactly where it had lived for seven years, familiar as her own pulse.

But underneath it, something else had hardened overnight. Something with edges. Something that fit her hand.

She turned back to the broken clock, took up her file, and began to work—because the fastest way to stay in Vell unnoticed was to fix the thing brilliantly, and the fastest way to destroy Corven Hale was to do it slowly, patiently, tooth by tooth, the way any good clockmaker takes down a movement.

She had one season. She intended to spend every hour of it.
