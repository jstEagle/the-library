## 5. Three Checkpoints

Edmund drew the map on the back of a repair invoice, because that was what she had. The invoice was for a mantel clock she'd fixed in her second week in Vell — a family named Ostrow, three crowns, a worn pallet stone — and its blank reverse side had been waiting ever since for something worth writing on.

She sat at the narrow table in her room above Mrs. Tarnow's house with the lamp turned low and the shade angled toward the wall, an old habit from years of working after hours in shops where apprentices weren't supposed to be awake. Outside, the wind came off the harbor in long flat pushes, rattling the loose pane in the window frame. Somewhere below, Mrs. Tarnow's floorboards creaked their slow midnight litany: kitchen, stairs, landing, bed. Edmund had learned the rhythm of the house the way she learned the rhythm of any mechanism — by listening until she could predict it.

The map took shape in pencil strokes.

Vell sat on the harbor's north shore like a hand cupped around water. To the south, across the gray channel, lay the mainland — fields, then the coast road, then everything else in the world. In summer there was a ferry, twice daily, forty minutes. In winter there was nothing but the ice road, when the harbor froze hard enough to bear a sledge, and even that was not a road so much as a permission the sea granted grudgingly and revoked without notice.

And between Vell and the mainland, three doors.

She wrote them out, because writing made problems smaller. That was another thing her father had taught her, back before she'd understood he was teaching anything at all: *Name the parts, little one. A movement is only frightening while it's a mystery.* He'd been talking about a striking train she was afraid to disassemble. She'd been seven. She could still hear the exact pitch of his voice, which was both a comfort and, lately, a wound that reopened nightly.

*One,* she wrote. *Customs house.*

It stood at the landward end of the harbor quay, a squat granite building with a stovepipe and a flagpole, and it controlled the causeway path that led up to the cliff road — the only route off the north shore that didn't cross the ice. In summer you walked past its door and the customs officer might glance up from his ledger, might not. In winter, when the bridge closed and the town contracted into itself, the customs house became something closer to a border post. Nothing left Vell by land without passing its window. And nothing left Vell by ice without passing its yard, because the ice road began on the shingle directly below the customs steps, where the officer could stand in his doorway and count every sledge, every walker, every bundle that crossed.

Edmund had walked past it four times now, at different hours, hands in pockets, eyes forward, the way a person walks past a building they have no interest in. The officer was a heavy man named Brakke who wore his coat unbuttoned even in this cold and stood in his doorway smoking as if the doorway belonged to him personally rather than to the crown. He knew faces. That was his whole trade, really — knowing which faces came and went, and which ones had no business going anywhere at all.

An apprentice clockmaker had no business leaving Vell before spring. An apprentice clockmaker had been *sent for*, and sent-for men stayed until the work was done and the roads opened. If Edmund appeared at the customs house with a coat full of stolen harbor papers, Brakke would not need to be clever. He would only need to be curious.

*Two,* she wrote. *Ferry office.*

This one puzzled her until she'd thought it through twice. The ferry wasn't running; the ferry wouldn't run until the ice broke in March or April. But the ferry office still mattered, because the ferry office kept the manifest book, and the manifest book was the second half of the checkpoint. No one crossed the ice road — not a merchant, not a mail carrier, not a farmer with a sledge of turnips — without a chit signed at the ferry office and countersigned at the customs house. Two stamps, two ledgers, two chances for a name to be written down and remembered. The clerk at the ferry office was a boy, sixteen maybe, all elbows and earnestness, who had already brought Edmund a broken pocket watch and looked at her — at him — with the open admiration boys reserved for anyone who could make dead machinery live again. Piet, his name was. He talked too much. He worshipped Corven Hale the way boys worship men who stand at windows looking seaward.

A boy like that would remember anyone who asked questions about the ice road. A boy like that would tell Hale without meaning any harm by it, the way a bell tells the hour — simply because that is what it is built to do.

*Three,* she wrote, and stopped, and looked at the word a long moment before finishing it. *Hale's gate.*

Because the ice road did not run from the customs steps straight out to sea, as she'd first assumed. She'd confirmed it that morning, walking the shingle in the flat gray light with her collar up, pretending to study the mooring rings. The harbor froze unevenly — the inner basin first, where the water was still, then outward — and the safe crossing began not at the customs house at all but at the eastern point, where the harbor master's own property ended in a low stone wall and an iron gate. The ice road started on Hale's ground. Of course it did. Everything in Vell, sooner or later, started or ended on Corven Hale's ground. He owned the warehouse leases, held the pilotage contract, chaired the harbor trust, and — as of five years ago — had buried under his own signature the truth about the night the *Wren* went onto the Teeth with her father aboard.

So the way out of Vell ran through his front yard. She almost laughed when she'd seen it, standing on the freezing shingle with the wind trying to take her hat. It was the kind of joke a mechanism makes on its maker: the whole town's winter escape hinged on a gate belonging to the one man who could never let her pass carrying what she carried.

Three doors. Three places where a name could be spoken, a coat could be opened, a future could end between one heartbeat and the next.

Edmund set down the pencil and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until the dark bloomed orange.

* * *

The ledgers lay where she had re-hidden them, wrapped in oilcloth inside the master clock's lower case, behind the great barrel of the going train, in a cavity that existed because some observatory mechanic fifty years ago had cut it to clear a warped wheel and no one since had known it was there. She had found it the way she found most things: by feel, fingertips reading metal the way other people read print.

She went to them every night now. Not to read — she had the numbers memorized down to the last fathom and minute — but to confirm. To touch the oilcloth and know the evidence still existed, still breathed, still waited. It was a ritual she recognized and distrusted, because rituals were how clockmakers fooled themselves into thinking time was on their side.

Tonight she unwrapped them fully and spread the pages across the observatory's workbench under the shaded lamp, and made herself look again at the arithmetic that had ended her sleep.

Five years ago, on the night of the twelfth of November, the official tide ledger recorded a high water of nineteen feet at nine o'clock — deep enough, according to the pilotage guidance pinned beside it, for the *Wren* to clear the Teeth with two feet to spare. Her father had trusted that number. Everyone trusted those numbers; they were posted each morning in the pilots' room, copied from the observatory's records, initialed by the harbor master's own hand.

But the observatory's own tide gauge — the brass float in the well beneath the building, wired to a pen that drew the water's breathing on a paper drum — had recorded something different. Edmund had found the drum pages folded into the same oilcloth, brittle and foxed, the ink line unmistakable: a high water of barely fifteen feet, and falling fast, an ebb running early and strong. Fifteen feet meant the Teeth were bare-backed. Fifteen feet meant the channel her father had steered into was a corridor of hidden rock.

Someone had taken the true record and hidden it, and written a lie in its place, and initialed the lie, and sent eleven men out onto the water to die of it.

Her father had been the pilot. He'd gone down with the boat, or near enough — they'd recovered him two days later on the shingle below the cliffs, and Edmund, fourteen years old, had stood in the crowd with her mother's hand crushing hers and heard the town say *tragic, tragic, the sea gives and the sea takes,* and watched Corven Hale weep openly at the memorial service with a face like a man carved for grief.

The sabotage of the master clock explained the rest. Someone — Hale, or someone serving him — had discovered the old drum pages and the draft ledger still existed up here in the observatory, misfiled, forgotten. Rather than steal them outright and leave a gap any auditor could find, they had jammed the clock — a clever job, she'd give them that, a bent detent pin timed to seize the strike train — so that the observatory would send for an outside repairer, someone with no history in Vell, and the works could be quietly cleaned during the repair. The pages would vanish into a repairman's rag bag. By spring, no trace.

Instead they had summoned her.

Edmund sat very still in the lamplight and let that irony settle over her like snow. They had reached halfway across the country for a stranger's hands, and the stranger's hands had belonged to the daughter of the man they'd drowned.

She did not believe in fate. She believed in escapements, in cause and consequence, in wheels that turned because teeth pushed them. But sitting there among the cold brass with her father's killers' handwriting under the lamp, she felt something that a religious woman might have called providence and that Edmund called, more carefully, *the work presenting itself.*

The work, now, was copying.

* * *

She began that same night, there at the observatory bench, because the observatory was the only place in Vell where she could have paper, ink, and privacy in the same room. The keeper — a half-deaf old astronomer named Voss who slept through weather, sermons, and most of the century — retired at nine and did not stir before dawn. His tower was hers from ten until five.

Copying was slower than she'd estimated. That was the first lesson of the night, and it was a bad one. The tide drum pages especially resisted haste: the ink line wandered, doubled back, annotated itself in margins, and every mark mattered, because the whole power of the evidence lay in its precision. A court — if it ever came to a court — would not be moved by grief. It would be moved by the difference between fifteen feet and nineteen, measured to the eighth of an inch, in the observatory's own hand. She could not approximate that. She had to reproduce it.

By three in the morning she had completed two pages. Two. There were eleven drum sheets and six ledger leaves, plus the draft tide table with Hale's initials — *C.H.* in a small, confident, murderous hand — and the pilotage guidance sheet showing the fatal depth figure. Nineteen pages, minimum, each requiring an hour or more of close, dry-eyed work.

Nineteen hours of copying, minimum, and she could not do them in nineteen consecutive nights, because the observatory was not always empty, because Voss sometimes woke, because the harbor master visited the tower — she'd learned that in her first week — and because every night she carried the originals out of hiding and spread them under a lamp was another night the whole enterprise balanced on a deaf old man's sleep.

She worked out the schedule the way she'd work out a power train: what drove what, what could be sacrificed, where the strain would fall. Four nights a week at the observatory, two pages a night. Ten days for the drums and ledgers. Then the final assembly — matching copies to originals, checking figures twice, folding the packet flat enough to sew into a coat lining without printing through the wool.

Which raised the question of the coat.

She looked down at herself, at the gray wool coat hanging on the observatory's peg, and thought about linings and needles and the fact that she had never in her life sewn anything that needed to survive a search. She could learn. She'd learned everything else the hard way. But a bulge in a lining, a seam that sat wrong, a coat a man wouldn't wear but a woman might — the disguise itself was a fourth checkpoint, one she passed a hundred times a day and could fail in a hundred ways. Every day in Vell was a search she hadn't been caught in yet.

And underneath all of it, patient as rust, the deadline. The ice road would form within weeks — the fishermen said the inner basin usually locked by the new year, the outer channel soon after, and once the road opened, the season's traffic would begin, and with the traffic would come scrutiny, and with the scrutiny, eventually, someone would ask why the clockmaker from away hadn't gone home. Or worse: Hale would finish whatever quiet audit he was conducting of his own secrets, decide the ledgers were a loose thread, and come up the tower stairs himself.

She had until the ice. Maybe eight weeks. Perhaps six.

Edmund wrapped the pages back in their oilcloth, returned them to the cavity behind the barrel, and closed the clock's case with the tenderness she reserved for mechanisms that deserved better than their owners. Then she stood a moment in the dark of the tower with the stopped pendulum hanging silent above her, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing for three days:

Even if she got the copies out — even if she crossed three checkpoints with nineteen pages sewn into her coat and put them before a magistrate on the mainland — copies could be disputed. Copies could be called forgeries. The originals would still be here, in Vell, in Hale's reach, waiting to burn.

The copies were not the mission. The copies were the rehearsal.

Somehow — and she did not yet know how, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a weight hung on the mainspring — the originals themselves had to leave. All of them. Across the ice, past three doors, out of the one town in the world built to keep them.

* * *

She walked home along the quay at half past five, in the blue-black before dawn, and the map in her head unfolded itself street by street whether she wanted it or not. Customs house: Brakke, heavy, watchful, forty paces of open quay with no cover between his window and the shingle path. Ferry office: the boy Piet, kind, talkative, loyal to Hale, seated exactly where the ice-road chits were signed. Hale's gate: iron bars, a gravel approach visible from the study windows, and beyond it the frozen point where the road began.

Three doors, and behind her, when she imagined turning around, a fourth: the life she'd built, letter by letter, year by year — *E. Ashgrove, journeyman clockmaker* — which would not survive being opened any more than the coat would. Exposed at any checkpoint, she lost everything at once: the guild, the trade, the name, the disguise that had taken twelve years to build and would take one raised eyebrow to demolish. They would not even prosecute her, probably. Towns like Vell didn't prosecute women who dressed as men; they simply unmade them, politely, thoroughly, and told the story for decades afterward in lowered voices.

So the plan had to be perfect, and plans were never perfect, and somewhere between those two facts Edmund Ashgrove was going to have to find a version of courage she hadn't needed before.

She climbed the outside stair to her room. The house was dark. Mrs. Tarnow's kitchen chimney had gone cold hours ago.

But when she opened her door, she stopped.

On the table, where no table of hers had anything at this hour, sat a covered dish — bread, cheese, a jar of pickle — and beside it, laid square to the table's edge with a precision that made Edmund's chest tighten for reasons she couldn't name, a folded quilt. An old one, faded indigo, the stitching gone slightly yellow, the kind of thing kept at the bottom of a cedar chest for years.

There was no note. There didn't need to be. Mrs. Tarnow missed nothing in this house — not the hours Edmund kept, not the ink stains, not the way her lodger's eyes went red-rimmed in the mornings, not, perhaps, other things entirely.

Edmund stood in the doorway with the cold at her back and the warm bread smell reaching her across the dark room, and understood that the map on the invoice in her pocket was already incomplete. She had drawn three checkpoints between herself and the mainland.

She had not counted the ones already inside the walls.
