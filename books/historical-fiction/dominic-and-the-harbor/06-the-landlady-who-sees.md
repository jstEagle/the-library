## 6. The Landlady Who Sees

Edmund learned the house by its sounds.

The Tarnow house on Salt Lane was old enough to have opinions and honest enough to voice them: a tick of settling timber at dusk, the groan of water pipes when the downstairs tap ran, the particular creak of the fourth stair that announced any visitor before they knocked. Edmund had catalogued them all in the first week, the way she'd catalogue the works of an unfamiliar clock — because a house, like a movement, told you what it was doing before it did it.

Which meant she knew, three nights after copying the ledgers by candlelight, that Mrs. Tarnow had stopped on the landing outside her door and stood there for the better part of two minutes without knocking.

Edmund sat very still at her table, hands flat on the wood, the copied pages beneath her forearm. Through the door she could hear the landlady's breathing — slow, deliberate, the breathing of a woman listening. Then the stair creaked, descending, and the pipes sang in the kitchen, and there was only the wind off the harbor worrying the shutters.

She did not sleep after that. She sat until the candle drowned in its own wax, looking at nothing, seeing tide tables behind her eyes — columns of figures in two different hands, one true and one false, separated by five years and eleven dead men including her father.

In the morning she came down late, hoping the kitchen would be empty.

It was not.

Mrs. Tarnow sat at the head of the scrubbed pine table with the teapot before her and two cups out. Two. She was a small woman, sixty or thereabouts, built close to the bone, with white hair pinned in a knot so severe it looked load-bearing. Her hands were folded on the table as if she'd been waiting exactly long enough for waiting to become a statement.

"You've been taking your breakfast in your room," she said. It was not a question and not quite an accusation. It was an observation laid on the table like a card played face up.

"The work keeps irregular hours," Edmund said. She kept her voice where she always kept it — low, flat, a workman's voice, neither warm nor cold. Five years of practice lived in that voice. "I didn't want to trouble the household."

"It's no trouble." Mrs. Tarnow poured the tea without asking whether Edmund wanted any, which settled the matter efficiently. "Sit. Bread's from this morning but it toasts fine."

Every instinct Edmund owned said leave. Take the cup, plead the observatory, climb back into the locked room where the papers were and the eyes weren't. But refusing tea in a harbor town in December was itself conspicuous, and besides — she was tired in a way that went past her bones into something underneath them. She sat.

Mrs. Tarnow pushed the bread board across. "You're left-handed."

Edmund's hand stopped halfway to the knife. "Pardon?"

"The ink on your fingers. Right side of the right hand, smudged under, the way a left-handed man drags his sleeve." Mrs. Tarnow sipped her own tea, watching him over the rim. Her eyes were pale gray, unlovely, unhurried. "My husband was left-handed. I know the mess."

* * *

The silence stretched. Outside, gulls argued over something on the ice-glazed quay; the wind turned the sign of the chandler's shop down the lane on its bracket with a sound like a struck match. Edmund made herself reach for the bread and cut a slice, left-handed, deliberately, because correcting course now would confirm everything.

"Clockwork makes a man clumsy with his mornings," she said. "Fine work, small parts. Hands shake if you don't sleep."

"Mm." Mrs. Tarnow rose, took down the butter dish, set it beside him. "You don't eat like a man who doesn't sleep. You eat like a man who's forgotten how. There's a difference."

"I—"

"Your lamp burns past two." She sat again. "I see the light under your door when I come up to bank the fire. Some nights past three. And you've taken to wearing your coat indoors, which is either a cold room or a man carrying things he doesn't want to set down."

The tea went tasteless in Edmund's mouth. She had accounted for the checkpoints of Vell — customs house, ferry office, Hale's gate — and somewhere in the arithmetic of those three barriers she had let herself believe that inside her rented room she was alone with the truth. It had not occurred to her, or rather she had declined to let it occur, that the truth kept a light under the door that anyone might see.

"I keep odd hours for the observatory," she said. "Master clock's temperamental. It wants watching."

"It wants more than watching, the way you look." Mrs. Tarnow refilled his cup, and her hand did not shake, and her face gave away precisely nothing except patience. "I'm not asking, Mr. Ashgrove. I'll say that plainly so we're square. I'm not asking what you're about. A woman my age in this town learns which questions cost. I watched them ask questions after the Wrenwreck and I watched what the answers bought." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "I'm only telling you what I see, because a man ought to know when he's seen."

"And what do you see?"

Mrs. Tarnow considered him for a long moment. The fire popped in the grate. Down the hall, the tall case clock in the front parlor — a fat-bellied thing, thirty hours if it was an hour — ticked with a slight limp on the strike train that Edmund had been politely ignoring since her second night in the house.

"I see a young man doing sums he doesn't want found," she said. "And I see someone who flinches when the church bell rings, which is no way for a clockmaker to behave toward bells."

* * *

She should have packed that afternoon. That was the thought that followed Edmund up the hill to the observatory through the gray spit of morning sleet — *you should have packed, you should have taken the copies and gone over the wall at low tide and let the town keep its secrets.* The bridge was closed; the ferry was gone; but a man with a rope and a dark night and no attachment to being believed afterward could get down the cliff path to the shingle below the fort, and from there —

From there the papers died in the sea, or Edmund did, or both. And Hale would rebuild his ledgers' hiding place in spring and wind his penance clock every night until he died respectable, and her father would stay a drunk pilot who misread the tide, which he never had in twenty years of reading tides better than the almanac.

So she climbed instead of packing, and worked through the morning among the master clock's works with her sleeves rolled and her mind split three ways — between the escapement, the ledgers hidden in the observatory cellar under a floorboard she'd loosened and re-set, and the pale gray eyes of Mrs. Tarnow measuring her across a breakfast table.

The sabotage was elegant. That was the thing she kept returning to, the thing that gnawed. Whoever had done it had understood the movement deeply — had retarded the going train just enough to drift the town's time by four minutes over a fortnight, had done it with tools and knowledge of the maintenance schedule so the fault would look like wear. Four minutes. On the night of the Wrenwreck, the difference between the official tide record and the real one had been — she'd checked it against the almanacs in the observatory's reference shelf — forty-one minutes of slack water reported as high water. Time falsified twice: once in the ledgers, once in the clocks that all of Vell trusted without thinking.

Someone had wanted the observatory quiet and wrong through the winter. Someone who knew the records room would go untouched until the season's end, when — according to Piet, according to the ferry office gossip that came free with a repaired watch — Corven Hale burned the year's dead paperwork "for tidiness," as harbor masters here had done for thirty years.

She seated the new pallet stones, closed the case, and set the pendulum swinging. Below her, through the observatory's salt-fogged windows, Vell lay along its harbor like something beached: roofs glazed with rime, smoke standing straight up in the windless cold, and beyond the mole the sea already putting on its winter skin of slush ice, pan by pan, working outward from the shallows. Every day the ice crept. Every day brought closer both her trap and her road — because when the harbor froze solid, Vell's tradition was older than any checkpoint: the ice road opened, marked with brush brooms from the custom house steps clear across to the mainland, and anyone could walk it who paid the tolls and showed their face at the three stations.

Three stations. Three chances to be searched, questioned, remembered.

She wiped her hands and thought, not for the first time: *what can be carried.* Not what can be proven — proof needed witnesses, tribunals, men in offices on the mainland willing to open files that Vell preferred shut. What can be carried was smaller and heavier: the copied pages, the original ledger from the clock case, and whatever else she could gather before the ice road opened. It was not much to set against a town.

Unless it wasn't only her.

* * *

The thought was still sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal when she came back down Salt Lane in the blue dark of four o'clock and found the fourth stair creaking under her own weight and the kitchen door standing open on lamplight and the smell of onions and bay.

"You'll eat here tonight," Mrs. Tarnow said from the stove, without turning. Not a question either. Everything in this house that mattered was said as though it had already been decided, and perhaps in this house everything had.

"I have work—"

"The clock will keep." Mrs. Tarnow turned then, ladle in hand, and her face was as unreadable as ever, but she nodded at the table, where two places were set again. Two places, every evening this week, Edmund realized — she'd been taking her suppers in her room on a tray and hadn't noticed that the tray came down each morning and the table was always laid for two. "Sit down, Mr. Ashgrove. Soup doesn't care what hour it is. That's why I trust it."

Edmund hung her coat on the peg — she had stopped wearing it indoors, after the breakfast, a concession she hadn't examined too closely — and sat. The soup was fish and barley and too much pepper and the best thing she had eaten since Vell.

They ate mostly in silence, but it was a different silence than the one at breakfast. At breakfast Mrs. Tarnow had been laying cards. Now she seemed, if anything, relieved — as though some tension in the house had eased with Edmund on the near side of the table instead of barricaded above it.

"My daughter writes from the mainland," Mrs. Tarnow said at last, into her soup. "Every month, regular as anything. Asks when I'm coming to live with her and her husband in Warmouth, where it's sensible. I tell her I'll come when the house is sold." She paused. "The house won't sell. Nobody buys a house on Salt Lane anymore. Too near the water, they say. Too near remembering."

"The Wrenwreck," Edmund said quietly. It was the first time either of them had put a name to the thing moving beneath their conversations like a body under ice.

Mrs. Tarnow set down her spoon. For a moment she looked out the dark window, where the harbor lights rode at anchor in the black, and her reflection looked older than she did.

"Eleven boats' worth of families buried in six days that winter," she said. "And a twelfth name nobody says. You'd think a town that drowns its men that fast would learn to talk about it. Instead it learned to lower its voice." She picked the spoon up again. "I lowered mine. That's all I'll say about it tonight."

"That's more than most will say at all."

"Aye." The ghost of something that was almost humor moved at the corner of her mouth. "And you noticed I said it, didn't you. Sitting there eating my soup with your left hand and your careful voice." She looked at him straight. "Whatever you are, Mr. Ashgrove — whatever you're carrying and whoever sent you — you're not from the mainland government, and you're not from Hale's office, because neither of those would rent my back room and burn lamp oil till three in the morning over tide tables. So." She stood and began stacking dishes, briskly, the audience over. "There's a blanket chest in the hall closet that sticks in wet weather. If a person ever needed anything to stay dry and undisturbed in this house, that's where I'd tell them to put it. Hypothetically."

Edmund sat frozen, spoon halfway to her mouth.

"Hypothetically," Mrs. Tarnow repeated, and carried the plates to the basin, and that was the end of it, apparently — except that when Edmund climbed the stairs that night she found that the lock on her door, stiff since her arrival, now turned smoothly, freshly oiled, and that a stub of good beeswax candle and a box of matches had appeared on her table beside the inkwell, placed with a homemaker's precision exactly where the light wouldn't show under the door.

She stood in the middle of her room for a long time.

Being seen was the thing she had trained herself against for five years — trained her walk, her voice, her shoulders, her laugh, until the training ran deeper than thought. Being seen and surviving had one method: be seen less. Be smaller, duller, earlier to bed. And here was a woman who had seen everything and responded not with exposure but with oil for the lock and wax for the candle, an alliance offered entirely sideways, requiring nothing signed and nothing admitted.

It should have terrified her. It did terrify her. But underneath the terror was something she had almost forgotten the shape of: the specific warmth of being known by one person on purpose. Her father had known her — *my Ed, my best apprentice, man or no* — and he was five years drowned with a false tide written over his grave. Since then, nobody.

She took the copies of the ledger pages from under the floorboard of her trunk lining — she had moved them twice already — and carried them down the hall in the dark, and opened the sticking chest in the hall closet, and laid them in the bottom beneath the winter quilts, beneath a layer of camphor and a knitted counterpane gone gray with age. The quilts were heavy and smelled of another household, another generation. They smelled of safety of a kind she hadn't budgeted for.

As she closed the chest lid, the parlor clock struck seven — the fat-bellied thirty-hour, limping on the strike as always, half a beat late and laboring into it. Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would ask Mrs. Tarnow's leave to open the case and ease the strike train, a small repair freely given, the first honest transaction she'd conducted in Vell.

She was climbing back upstairs, lighter by the weight of the papers and heavier by the weight of the trust, when the fourth stair creaked under her and she heard, through the frosted glass of the front door, footsteps stopping on Salt Lane outside. Boots. Heavy ones. A pause — the particular pause of a man reading the house's nameplate by lamplight — and then a firm double knock on Mrs. Tarnow's door, at an hour when nobody in Vell called socially.

Through the hall window, past the hedge, Edmund saw the lantern raised, and the light of it caught the collar of a greatcoat, and the badge above it glinting: the silver wheel-and-anchor of the harbor master's office.

"Mrs. Tarnow!" a man called through the door, pleasant and carrying. "Corven Hale's compliments! Just a word about your lodger!"
