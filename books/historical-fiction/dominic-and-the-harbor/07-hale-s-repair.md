## 7. Hale's Repair

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy with chapped ears who didn't wait for a reply.

*Harbor Master C. Hale requests the clockmaker's attendance at his residence regarding a timepiece in disrepair. Payment offered at guild rates.*

Edmund read it three times by the light of Mrs. Tarnow's kitchen window, and each reading made the paper feel heavier in her hands. Guild rates. Hale knew her name — or rather, the name she had built like a small brass shell around herself: E. Ashgrove, journeyman clockmaker, licensed out of Aldermoor, references available. He knew it because he had signed the harbor office's authorization for her work at the observatory, one of four signatures required before anyone touched the master clock. She had watched him write it from across the room, a man with careful handwriting and a face arranged into something between dignity and exhaustion.

Now he wanted her in his house.

She thought about refusing. She composed the refusal twice — *regret that my obligations to the observatory prevent me* — and burned both drafts in the stove. A clockmaker who refused the harbor master's commission in a town the size of Vell would be remembered for it, and remembered was the one thing Edmund Ashgrove could not afford to be. Besides. Some cold, practical part of her, the part that had kept her alive through nine years of binding and posturing and sleeping with her boots near the door, whispered that a man who falsified tide ledgers did not invite tradesmen into his home without reason. Either he suspected nothing, in which case she was safe. Or he suspected everything, in which case refusing would only confirm it.

Either way, she would learn more inside his walls than outside them.

She told herself that was the whole reason she accepted. She was lying, and she knew it, and she went anyway.

* * *

Hale's house stood above the harbor on a shelf of rock, gray stone under gray sky, with windows tall enough to suggest money and shutters painted well enough to suggest care. A widow lived next door; Mrs. Tarnow had mentioned her, along with everything else in Vell that could be mentioned, in the tone of a woman cataloguing exits. The house looked out over the water the way a sentry looks out over a wall — not admiringly. Watchfully.

Hale opened the door himself. Up close he was taller than Edmund had guessed, and older, silver coming in at the temples in a way that seemed less like aging than like weathering. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they did the thing she hated most in the world: they moved over her quickly, cataloguing, and then away.

"You're the clockmaker." Not a question. "Ashgrove."

"At your service, sir."

"The study. This way." He turned without waiting, which was either rudeness or habit, and led her down a hall lined with charts. Tide tables, harbor soundings, the coastline rendered in ink so fine it looked embroidered. Her father had drawn charts like these. Her father had died because the numbers on charts like these had been lies.

The study smelled of pipe smoke and lamp oil. The clock stood against the far wall — a good piece, forty years old at least, walnut case gone dark with handling, a regulator of the kind shipping offices used to keep their chronometers honest. It had stopped. That was all Hale said about it. *It has stopped.* As though the thing had simply lain down one night and declined to rise.

"When do you need it running?"

"Before the ice closes the road." He said it flatly, and something in the flatness snagged on her attention. Before the ice closed the road. Everyone in Vell spoke of the closing as an ending, a burial, a season of entombment. Hale spoke of it the way a man speaks of a deadline.

"I'll need the afternoon," she said. "Possibly two."

He nodded once and left her alone in the room.

That was the first strange thing. She noted it the way she noted a gear tooth worn slightly out of true — not alarming yet, but wrong. A man with secrets did not leave a stranger alone among his papers. Unless the papers were already gone. Unless the room had been swept clean of anything worth finding, and leaving her in it was itself a kind of message: *look all you like.*

She looked all she liked.

* * *

The clock's trouble announced itself within ten minutes. The suspension spring had been bent — not broken, bent, carefully, with a tool finer than a household knife — so that the pendulum's swing decayed and the escapement starved. Someone had done this deliberately, and recently, and had done it knowing enough about clocks to make the failure look like fatigue rather than violence.

Edmund sat back on her heels and stared at the little twisted wire of steel.

Twice now. Twice she had found sabotage where there should have been simple wear. Once in the observatory, wrapped around a secret that had rearranged her life. And once here, in the private study of the man whose signature was all over that secret. Coincidence was possible. Clocks failed everywhere; springs bent; Vell was a hard town on its machinery. But coincidence, in her experience, was what people called patterns they hadn't finished counting yet.

She straightened the spring, oiled the train, let the pendulum find its rhythm. The tick filled the study, steady and stupid and indifferent, keeping time for a room full of grief it couldn't see. While it ran she worked the case open further than repair strictly required — a clockmaker's privilege, checking the strike train — and found nothing hidden in this one. No papers. No folded pages behind the dial. Whatever Hale kept, he did not keep it in his clocks.

Which meant he kept it somewhere else. Which meant she should leave. Which meant she stayed.

It took her another hour to find it, and she found it the way she found most things: by thinking like the person who hid them. Where does a careful man put a document he cannot destroy and cannot be seen owning? Not in the desk — desks are searched first. Not in the clock — too clever, too expected of a town full of sailors' superstitions. She checked the chart drawers anyway, and the map tubes, and the false bottom of the coal scuttle, and was beginning to feel like a burglar rather than a craftsman when her eye caught the bookshelf.

Navigation almanacs, twenty years of them, spines cracked with use. And at the end of the row, one volume standing slightly proud — the current year's tide almanac, which no one needed anymore because the observatory printed daily corrections. She pulled it out. It was heavier than it should have been.

The hollowed center held a ledger.

Not a copy. Not the observatory's interleaved sheets she'd found in the master clock, those damning fragments of corrected readings. This was the original — bound in oilcloth, the harbor office's own stamp on the flyleaf, entries in a hand she recognized from the authorization forms upstairs. Corven Hale's hand. Five years of tide observations, and then, on the nights surrounding the Wrenwreck, entries that matched nothing the official record claimed. The real heights of the water. The real times of the shift. Numbers that, laid beside the pilot boat's last logged course, explained everything: the channel that had been reported safe was not safe, and the man who reported it had known, and the report had been signed anyway.

Her father had steered into water that a truthful ledger would have condemned.

Edmund knelt on the floor of her father's killer's study and shook. Not weeping — she had trained that out of herself years ago, the way you train out any tell — but shaking, her hands useless around the oilcloth, her breath coming in the shallow sips of someone trying not to make noise in a silent house. The clock ticked on. Ten seconds a minute slow, she thought distantly, I'll have to regulate it again, and the absurdity of the thought nearly broke her open entirely.

She copied nothing. There was no time, and the ledger was too long, and her hands were not steady enough. Instead she memorized its location, replaced the almanac exactly as it had stood — spine proud by a finger's width, the pride of a book read often — and told herself she would come back. She had to come back. The ice road was weeks from opening, and Hale had said *before the ice closes the road*, and she was beginning to understand that both of them were racing toward the same date for opposite reasons.

She was repacking her tools when she saw the door.

It stood at the back of the study, half concealed by the chart cabinet — not locked, merely closed, the kind of door a house pretends isn't there. It opened onto a narrow room, windowless, lit by a single lamp left burning low. She should not have entered it. Every professional instinct she owned said walk away, pack your tools, present your bill, go home to Mrs. Tarnow's tea and her unasked questions.

She entered it.

* * *

The room held thirty-one photographs.

She counted them before she understood what she was counting, because counting was safer than understanding. Thirty-one framed portraits on a single shelf that ran the length of the wall, each with a candle stub before it, each candle burned down to a different height — some fresh, some puddles of wax months old. Sailors, mostly. Young men in oilskins, old men in dress uniforms, a woman in a harbor office coat. Names beneath each in copperplate: *J. Verlaine. T. Oduya. M. and S. Brandt.* The Wrenwreck dead, she realized. All of them. Every soul the harbor had lost that night, gathered here in a room with no windows, where no visitor would ever come.

And at the end of the shelf, apart from the others, set slightly forward as if it had been handled more:

A pocket watch.

It lay on a square of velvet, open, its case worn to bare brass at the hinge. Edmund knew that wear. She had grown up watching her father's thumb find that exact spot, worrying the brass smooth during long watches at the tiller. She knew the dent above the crown, acquired — so he'd claimed — by dropping it on a dock in Aldermoor while showing off for a girl. She knew the engraving on the inner lid, and she made herself look at it, made herself confirm it, because some part of her still hoped to be wrong.

*To Tam Ashgrove, who keeps better time than the sea. — R.*

Her mother's hand. Her mother, who had died believing her husband lost honestly, to weather, to chance, to the sea's ordinary cruelty.

Edmund picked it up. It was warm. That was the detail that undid her — not the watch, not the name, but the warmth of it, the evidence that Hale came here often, that he took the watch in his hands, that he wound it. She pressed the stem gently and felt the resistance of a mainspring kept alive. Somewhere in this house, every day or two, the man who killed her father wound her father's watch so that it would not stop.

She stood in the lamplight holding it and tried to arrange her heart into hatred and found the arrangement wouldn't hold.

Because here was the horror braided through the pity, tight as wire through rope: this was not the shrine of a man who felt nothing. Guilty men built shrines. Innocent men built nothing at all. Hale had spent five years keeping thirty-one candles and one watch alive in a windowless room, and none of it changed a single fact, and none of it brought back a single sailor, and none of it un-drowned Tam Ashgrove — and still he did it, nightly, the way other men prayed, a penance that pardoned no one and was not meant to. He had falsified the tides. He had also, apparently, never slept a whole night since.

Both things were true. That was the discovery that frightened her most. She had crossed the frozen miles to Vell carrying a simple story — a villain, a crime, a daughter's vengeance dressed as a journeyman's toolkit — and the story was dissolving in her hands like ice in spring water. Villains did not wind their victims' watches. Monsters did not bend a clock spring to hide a ledger they might have burned years ago and hadn't. He could have destroyed the evidence a hundred times. He kept it instead, hollowed into an almanac, close enough to touch. A man keeps what he intends to answer for.

Or a man keeps what he cannot decide to burn, she corrected herself, setting the watch down with fingers that were not quite steady. Mercy was a trap. Pity was how people like Corven Hale survived — not by denying what they'd done but by performing sorrow so thoroughly that the sorrow became the story. The town already told it that way; she'd heard it at Mrs. Tarnow's table, the grieving harbor master, the man who took every loss personally. Grief as armor. Candlelight as camouflage.

But her father's watch was warm, and the ledger was intact, and neither fact fit the simple story, and Edmund Ashgrove stood in the dark between them for a long time.

She memorized the room. Thirty-one frames, the order of the names, the position of the watch, the brand of candle, the draft under the door that kept the flames leaning east. Evidence of character was not evidence of guilt, but juries — if it ever came to juries, if it ever came past the checkpoints and the burning and the winter — weighed a man's whole weight, not just his crimes. And a confession written nowhere could sometimes be read in a room like this, if you could bring the room to them. Photographs could be carried. Testimony could be carried. Even, she thought, looking at the watch, at terrible cost, the watch itself.

No. Not the watch. She would not steal grief, even his. She had her own to carry.

She closed the door, aligned it with the shadow line the cabinet cast, and returned to the study, where the regulator ticked on, faithful and blind. She regulated it properly this time — two minutes' work, bringing it to true — and the small irony of it almost made her laugh: she was leaving the man's clock more honest than she'd found it. Let it keep perfect time. Let it count down the weeks to the ice road with flawless accuracy, for both of them.

Hale was waiting in the hall. She hadn't heard him come back, and the realization landed cold in her stomach — how long had he been standing there? How much did a house tell its owner about where a guest had been?

"Running?" he asked.

"True to the second," she said. "The spring was fatigued. I've set it right."

Something moved behind his pale eyes — not suspicion, she decided. Something more tired than suspicion. "You work quickly."

"Clocks don't reward slowness, sir."

"No," he said, and looked past her, down the hall, toward the study door, toward the door beyond it, and for one suspended moment Edmund felt the whole house hold its breath around them. Then he produced a coin purse and counted out her fee without haggling, which was its own kind of strangeness, and said, "You'll be here through the winter, then. Trapped like the rest of us."

"Until the road opens."

"Until the road opens." He repeated it slowly, tasting it, and handed her the coins, and his fingers did not quite touch hers. "Mind the ice, Ashgrove. It holds some and drops others. There's no telling, until it's too late, which is which."

She walked down the hill with the fee in her pocket and her heart going like an unbalanced flywheel. Behind her the house watched the harbor, and the harbor lay flat and gray and patient, and somewhere in a windowless room thirty-one candles leaned east in a draft no one else ever felt.

Mrs. Tarnow met her at the door with tea already poured and a question she finally, after six weeks, allowed herself to ask.

"Well?" she said. "What's he like, the harbor master?"

Edmund hung up her coat, and thought of warm brass, and said, "I don't know yet. But I know where he hides things."

Mrs. Tarnow set down the teapot very carefully, the way a woman sets down something she suspects will need both her hands free later, and pulled out the second chair.
