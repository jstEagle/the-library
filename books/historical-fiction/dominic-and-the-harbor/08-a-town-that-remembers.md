## 8. A Town That Remembers

The invitation came pinned to the observatory door on a card of good cream stock, printed in a hand too elegant for the town it served:

*In memory of those lost to the sea, five years since the Wrenwreck. Harbor Square, dusk. All of Vell is asked to attend.*

All of Vell. Edmund read it three times, looking for the exit clause. There wasn't one. In a town of four hundred souls, *all* meant her too — the clockmaker from away, lodged above Mrs. Tarnow, known to the harbor master by his first name now, or near enough to it. E. Ashgrove, apprentice of the Guild of Horologists, summoned to fix their master clock and staying on past all reasonable excuse because the master clock kept developing faults that only she could diagnose.

Because she was the one inventing them.

She had been slowing it by degrees, a quarter-minute here, a minute there, buying herself days she hadn't yet learned how to spend. The copies were made — forty-one pages in her own careful hand, hidden inside the ticking of a broken mantel clock in her room where no searcher would think to listen — but copies were not originals, and originals were not yet safe, and winter had not yet done its work on the harbor.

And now the town wanted her in a square at dusk, standing among them, remembering.

"You'll go," Mrs. Tarnow said, when Edmund mentioned it over supper. It was not a question. "Not going would be noticed faster than anything you could do on purpose."

"I don't belong here."

"No one belongs anywhere," Mrs. Tarnow said, buttering bread with terrible precision. "They just stop checking after a while. Eat your fish."

---

She went.

Harbor Square at dusk in December was a study in what lamplight could not fix. The town had turned out entire — fishermen with their caps in their hands, shopkeepers, the customs men out of uniform and awkward in their Sunday coats, children released from bedtime for once and running wild between the adults' legs like sparrows. Someone had set up a brazier, and someone else had carried down chairs from the church hall for the widows, and there were more widows than chairs, which told Edmund something about arithmetic before anyone said a word.

At the head of the square, facing the water, stood a temporary dais and on it a wooden frame draped in black cloth. Behind it, the harbor itself: black water, masted silhouettes, and beyond the mole the low dark teeth of the rocks where the *Wren* had gone to pieces on a falling tide five years ago this night.

Edmund found a place at the edge of the crowd, cap low, hands in pockets, and did what she did best around machinery she didn't yet understand. She watched, and she listened, and she mapped the works.

Corven Hale came to the dais at full dark.

She had seen him twice since the day in his study — once across the customs yard, once passing her on the quay with a nod so brief it might have been wind. He wore his authority the way other men wore wet wool: correctly, and with visible discomfort. Tonight, in front of all of Vell, he put on grief like a uniform instead, and it fit him worse.

He spoke briefly. Five names. Five lanterns lit, one for each lost — her father's name, Tomas Ashgrove, third in the list, read out in Hale's level voice as if it were any name, as if the syllables didn't weigh what they weighed. The crowd murmured the names back, the way congregations do, and Edmund's mouth stayed shut around hers until it ached.

Then Hale said the thing she'd come to hear him say — not because it surprised her but because she needed to know exactly how he said it.

"The sea keeps its own ledgers," he told them. "We keep ours. And on that night, every reading, every record, every line in every book said the tide would hold. It did not hold. I have reread those pages a hundred times. They were true, and they were not enough." A pause, measured like a pendulum. "I will not stand here and tell you I am blameless. I stood at that window. That is enough to carry."

And the square broke like a mechanism into its two trains.

She heard it happen in fragments, moving slowly along the crowd's edge with her head bowed, a ghost with a cap and cold ears.

"—said it himself, didn't he? He reads the pages every year. What kind of man torments himself five years over a mistake unless—"

"Unless there was no mistake," said another voice, lower. Old Brandt the netmender, she thought, though she couldn't see him. "Tides don't lie, but men who write tides down can. My cousin was on the pilot boat. Ask why the readings that week ran two hours proud of the moon."

"They *ran proud* all autumn, everyone knew—"

"Everyone *believed*. There's a difference."

"Corven Hale pulled six bodies out of that water himself. Six. With his own hands. You want to talk ledgers to a man who—"

"His hands were clean that night, maybe. Were his books?"

A woman's voice cut through, sharp with a grief that had curdled: "My Henrik is under that water because somebody's arithmetic failed, and I don't care whose. I care that it's *somebody*, and that nobody has ever been made to be him."

Edmund moved on. Near the brazier, a knot of younger men — ferry crew, customs juniors — talked a different language altogether.

"He's announcing the ice road soon, you mark me. First week of the new freeze, same as always."

"Piet says the office is already cutting passes. Piet hears everything."

Piet. She turned the name over. Ferry office boy — sixteen, maybe seventeen, quick hands, quicker grin; she'd fixed a carriage clock for him her second week in town and he'd paid her in gossip and admiration, most of it aimed past her at Corven Hale. *The harbor master remembers my name,* he'd said, glowing, as if the sun had noted him personally.

"Season's end, he's burning the old records," one of the young men said, and Edmund's blood went from cold to arctic. "Clean sweep, he told the office. Twenty years of tide books and cargo manifests, all of it, 'for tidiness.'"

"Tidiness," Brandt's voice said again, from somewhere behind her, dry as bone. "That'll be a tidy fire."

Laughter — some of it uneasy, none of it disbelieving. So the burning was public knowledge already, announced casually, planned openly. Of course it was. Hale was not a man who hid his intentions; he hid his evidence, and then destroyed the hiding place in broad daylight, with permission. She had been assuming she had until spring. She had weeks. Maybe days, once the ice road opened and he no longer needed the town's goodwill frozen in place.

She filed it, and kept listening, and began, methodically, to sort the town into gears and springs.

---

The memorial ended with the lanterns.

Five of them, lit from the brazier and carried down to the mole by five volunteers, set on the stone lip where the light would sit over the wreck all night. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it — the whole arrangement, the black cloth and the names and the little flames going down the steps two by two, was beautiful, and it worked, she could feel it working on her, pulling her grief out of the locked drawer where she kept it and holding it up to public warmth like a thing being blessed.

Her father's name had been read aloud by the man who killed him, and the town had murmured it kindly, and for one treacherous moment Edmund had felt *included*.

That was the trick of Vell, she understood then, standing apart in the dark while the crowd dispersed around her. The town offered belonging the way the sea offered a calm morning — sincerely, generously, without the smallest regard for what lay underneath. Grieve with us. Drink with us. Stand in our square and murmur our dead. Belong — on terms written by the man who sank your father, in a ledger you are not permitted to read.

She was still standing there, longer than anyone unnoticed should stand anywhere, when a voice at her elbow said:

"You're the clockmaker."

A girl — no, a young woman, mid-twenties, sharp-faced, a shawl that had been good ten years ago. She held one of the lanterns, unlit, tucked under her arm like a loaf.

"Ashgrove," Edmund said. "I'm repairing the observatory clock."

"I know what you're repairing." The woman looked at her with an attention Edmund found genuinely alarming — the attention of someone counting. "I'm Maren Brandt. Old Brandt's granddaughter. The one who talks about tide readings, if you've had your ears on tonight."

"Everyone's ears were on tonight."

"Mm. But yours especially." Maren turned the unlit lantern over in her hands, once, twice. "You've been here eight weeks. Nobody stays eight weeks in Vell in winter unless they're stuck or looking. The bridge closed behind you, so you're stuck. But you watch Hale like a woman doing sums."

Edmund said nothing. Saying nothing was a skill; she'd had a lifetime's instruction in it.

"I'm not warning you off," Maren said. "I'm telling you which end of the town you're standing in. There's two of us. The ones who need him to be guilty and the ones who need him to be good, and both of us need it so badly we'd each forgive a great deal to get it. Whichever way it breaks, half of Vell loses the story it lives by. Think about that before you decide what you're carrying around, and who you show it to." She set the lantern down on the seawall, deliberately, in front of Edmund. "My grandmother drowned that night too. Not in the water. In the waiting. Somebody in this town owes me an arithmetic, clockmaker. If you ever work it out—" she nodded at the lantern, "—light this."

She walked away. Edmund stood a long moment with the lantern's cold handle under her hand, feeling precisely the weight of it, and of everything else.

---

Mrs. Tarnow was awake when she got home, which was unusual, and had not poured tea, which was unprecedented.

"Well?" her landlady said. "How was the mourning?"

"Instructive." Edmund sat, stripped of her cap, letting the room see what the street could not. "Half of them would follow him off a pier. Half of them have been waiting five years for someone to prove the pier was sabotaged. And the burning's moved up — he's destroying the records at season's end, publicly. Everyone knows. It's not even a secret."

Mrs. Tarnow absorbed this the way she absorbed most things, with a small stillness that cost her visibly. Then: "The ice road opens when?"

"When the harbor freezes hard. Days, if the cold holds."

"Then you have days." Mrs. Tarnow folded her hands. "And three doors between you and the mainland, and I've lived in this town fifty years, girl, so let me tell you about doors while the tea's still in the pot. The customs house — Officer Lund. Dour, honest, and terrified of his superiors, which means honest only up to the point where honesty gets him reassigned. The ferry office — that's run by a clerk named Vasik with a heart like a receipt book, but he keeps a boy, Piet, who'd do anything for anyone who treated him like a man. And the gate at the harbor master's own house." She paused. "That door isn't locked by locks."

"No," Edmund said quietly. "It isn't."

"And you've been inside it."

It wasn't quite a question, and Edmund didn't quite answer it, and between the two not-quites passed the whole shape of the thing they were becoming to each other: conspirators who never used the word, bound by tea and silence and the growing inventory of things Mrs. Tarnow chose not to ask.

"There's a boy," Edmund said at last. "At the ferry office. Piet. He brought me his mother's carriage clock last month and came back twice to ask if it was done when it wasn't. He worships Hale. Talks about him like scripture."

"And you're wondering whether to use him."

"I'm wondering what it costs," Edmund said. "He's sixteen and he thinks the world is legible. I remember thinking that."

Mrs. Tarnow looked at her for a long moment, in the way she had — the look that made Edmund understand why being perceived could be terror and comfort in the same breath.

"Fifty years ago I was sixteen and legible too," Mrs. Tarnow said. "The world corrected me. Use the boy, if you must. But use him gently, and decide now whether, when it's done, you can look at him and tell him the truth. Because he'll deserve it, and because one day he may be the only person in Vell standing between you and a cell."

* * *

The next morning, Edmund walked to the ferry office under cover of legitimate business: the observatory needed a copy of the winter tide schedule posted beside the master clock, and only the ferry office issued it.

The office smelled of ink and paraffin. Piet was behind the counter, copying entries into a ledger with the fierce concentration of someone performing surgery on paper, and when he saw her his whole face performed relief.

"Mr. Ashgrove! Is it done? The clock — Mama asks every day, well, she doesn't, but I ask for her—"

"It's done," Edmund said, and produced it from her bag, cleaned and oiled and keeping perfect time, and watched the boy's delight as if it were a mechanism she was studying. Which she was.

"She'll cry. She cries at everything since Papa." He wound it immediately, reverently, then lowered his voice over the counter. "Did you hear? About the ice road? Harbor master was down at the quay last night — after the lanterns, he goes down alone every year, did you know that? Stands at the mole till midnight. Anyway, he told Lund the road could open inside the week. Inside the *week*." His eyes shone with the news as if it were Christmas rather than a calendar of thawless cold. "First crossing in five days, maybe less. I'm to cut the passes myself. He said, *Piet, I trust your hand with the stamps.* His very words."

"His very words," Edmund echoed.

"And after season starts he's clearing out the old records — the whole cellar, he says, twenty years of paper, burn it clean. He says a harbor shouldn't drown in its own paperwork." Piet laughed, easy and bright, and stamped the tide schedule, and slid it across. "You'll want the road early, I expect. Clockmakers always do. I could put your name first on the list, Mr. Ashgrove. First pass out of Vell, when it opens."

Edmund took the schedule. Her hands were steady; she'd trained them to be, years ago, in a guild workshop where a tremor meant discovery.

"That's generous of you, Piet."

"Harbormaster says favors make the town go round." He grinned, sixteen and legible, holding the stamp like a scepter. "So. First pass. Just say when."

Edund folded the schedule into her pocket, next to the weight of everything else she carried, and smiled the smile she'd practiced in mirrors until it stopped costing anything.

"Sooner than you think," she said.
