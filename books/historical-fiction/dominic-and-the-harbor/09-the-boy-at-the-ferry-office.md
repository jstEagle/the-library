## 9. The Boy at the Ferry Office

The ferry office smelled of tar and old paper, and the boy behind the counter was trying to look older than he was.

Edmund had passed him twice already that week — once on her way to the observatory, once coming back — and both times he'd sat up straighter when she came through the door, as though a customer were an event. The office itself was mostly ceremony in winter. The ferries had stopped running the day she arrived; the last one had crossed the gray water without her on it, and now the ticket window was shuttered, the timetable behind it curling at the corners. What remained was the checkpoint: a desk, a stove, a ledger for recording anyone leaving over the ice when it formed, and Piet.

He couldn't have been more than sixteen. He had a cowlick he fought with pomade and lost to daily, and a ferry company cap two sizes too large, and when Edmund set the broken pocket watch on his counter, his eyes went wide with the particular hunger of a boy who wanted, more than anything, to be trusted with something delicate.

"It's my father's," Edmund said. Which was true, though not in the way Piet would hear it.

"Elgin?" Piet picked it up with both hands, careful as a nurse.

"Waltham."

"Hairline crack in the crystal. Mainspring's—" He tilted it against his ear, shook it gently, frowned. "Mainspring's let go. You'd need a proper clockmaker for this."

"I am a proper clockmaker."

Piet looked up. Looked at her hands — she'd learned long ago not to flinch under that look — and then at her face, and whatever arithmetic he performed came out in her favor, or at least came out inconclusive. "You're the one fixing the master clock," he said. "Ashgrove. Everyone says you're from the capital."

"The capital has clocks too."

"How many springs do you replace in a year?"

"A hundred, maybe more."

Piet exhaled through his teeth, impressed despite himself, and set the watch down between them like a pledge. "I could get you tea. There's a kettle."

"There is," Edmund agreed, because there plainly was, sitting on the stove, and because she had come in here for a reason that had nothing to do with the watch, and the watch was only the door she was walking through.

She felt the falseness of it settle in her stomach, cold and specific, even as she smiled.

* * *

It took three visits before Piet talked about the ice road.

That was the rhythm of small towns; Edmund knew it from her own, years ago, before she'd cut its memory out of herself along with everything else. You didn't ask a question directly in Vell. You sat, and drank terrible tea, and let a boy show you his stamp collection or complain about his boots, and eventually the thing you needed came to you on its own feet, the way a cat comes to the person ignoring it.

On the first visit, Piet told her about his mother, who ran the boarding house near the fish market and had opinions about everyone. On the second, he told her he'd applied to the naval academy in the capital twice and been rejected twice, and that Corven Hale had written him a letter of recommendation the second time anyway, unprompted, which Piet described in a voice gone carefully flat, the way people describe things that matter most to them.

"He read my application himself," Piet said. "Marked it all up in pencil. Said my seamanship answers were strong but my mathematics would sink me." He turned his cup on its saucer. "Then he wrote the letter."

"That was kind of him."

"He didn't have to. Harbor masters don't write letters for clerks." Piet said it like doctrine. "He remembers the Wrenwreck. He pulled three men out of the water himself. My uncle saw him do it — went back in twice after his own boat was burning oil off the rocks." The boy's jaw tightened. "People in this town say things about him. They don't know anything. They weren't on the pier that night."

"No," Edmund said quietly. "They weren't."

She was aware, distantly, of her own heartbeat, steady and traitorous, keeping time like a good escapement while she sat in the warmth of a stove and mined a grieving boy for intelligence. She thought of the ledgers folded into the lining of her coat at Mrs. Tarnow's, the numbers in faded iron-gall ink that contradicted everything Piet believed. She thought of Hale going back into black water twice.

Both things could be true. That was the part no one warned you about — that a man could be a murderer and a rescuer in the same night, and a town could love him honestly, and a boy could carry his name like a lamp.

"When does it open?" she asked, as casually as she could fold the question. "The road. I'll need to get home eventually, and I'd rather know before everyone else does."

"Depends on the ice." Piet got up to feed the stove, grateful for something to do with his hands. "Harbor usually locks around the second week of the month. Then customs measures thickness at three points — off the breakwater, mid-channel, past the point. Needs eight inches minimum, and they won't post the date until they've measured twice." He glanced back at her. "Last year it opened the twenty-third. Year before, the nineteenth. Why?"

"I like to plan."

"You clockmakers." He laughed, and it was a good laugh, unguarded, and Edmund hated herself a little more. "Everything measured. My uncle says your whole trade is just people who can't stand not knowing what time it is."

"Your uncle isn't wrong."

* * *

By the fourth visit she had repaired two more clocks for him — his mother's mantel clock, and the office regulator, which hung crooked on the wall and ran six minutes slow, an insult Edmund could not bear to leave standing another day. She worked at the counter while Piet watched, asking questions she could answer honestly, and the honesty of it was the trap she'd built for herself. Because the work *was* honest. The boy's delight in watching the regulator come apart and go back together was real. When he held the pendulum bob like a relic and asked how she'd known the suspension spring was fatigued, and she told him *you listen for it, the beat goes uneven before the time does*, and he repeated it under his breath like scripture — that was real too.

She was lying to him with true things, and it was the most refined cruelty she knew.

"So if the cold holds," Piet said, watching her stake the regulating nut, "and customs measures next week, we're looking at the twentieth, maybe the twenty-first."

"You'll be first in line to leave?"

Something flickered across his face. "I can't leave until the season closes. Ferry office staffs the last checkpoint. Hale's gate is third, customs is first, we're second." He counted them off on his fingers, and Edmund kept her eyes on the movement and did not blink. Three checkpoints, laid out in a boy's open hand. "After the roads close for thaw, I get leave. I'm going to the capital in spring. Take the mathematics exam again."

"Third time."

"Third time." He grinned. "Hale said — he said, *the harbor doesn't care how many times you've failed, only that you come back to the wheel.* Do you think that's foolish?"

Edmund set down her pliers. Outside the window, Vell lay gray under low cloud, masts bare, gulls hanging motionless in the wind like things nailed to the sky. Somewhere beyond the breakwater the sea was beginning, night by night, to turn to stone.

"I think," she said, "that some people are worth failing for."

Piet looked at her strangely, and she realized the words had come out with more weight than she'd intended — more weight than a stranger owed a clerk — and she covered it by holding out her hand for the pendulum.

But the damage was done. She could see it happening in him, the way trust happens, quietly and all at once: the loosening of the shoulders, the way he stopped performing for her and started simply talking. By the end of that afternoon he had shown her the checkpoint log — *because you'd appreciate the record-keeping, Mr. Ashgrove* — and told her which customs officer signed off on measurements, and mentioned, in passing, that Hale's men had been asking questions around town about a stranger who asked too many questions.

Edmund's hands stayed perfectly steady on the regulator.

"Anyone in particular?" she said.

"Not you." Piet shrugged, sealing her fate with his carelessness. "Some surveyor type, people said. Or a journalist. Nobody knows. But Officer Brandt's been stopping people on the bridge road, checking parcels." He rolled his eyes. "As if anyone's smuggling anything out of Vell but dried cod."

"Imagine," Edmund said, "the scandal."

* * *

She walked home along the harbor front in the blue hour, when the lamps were lit and the water between the pilings had gone glassy at the edges, ice creeping inward from the shallows like a tide of milk. Her breath hung around her. Her feet knew the way by now; that itself was a betrayal, how quickly a place you meant to pass through became a place you knew.

Mrs. Tarnow had left a lamp in the window for her, and a plate of something under a cloth, and said nothing about either, which was her way of saying everything.

Upstairs, Edmund took out the copied pages and spread them on the table, and made herself look at them properly, the way she'd been avoiding since she'd begun this campaign of kindnesses. Piet's laughing face. His stamps. His *do you think that's foolish?* The letter of recommendation from a man who had falsified the tides and let a boat full of people die on the Wren Rocks, and who had gone back into the water twice — she believed that now, she believed it completely, and the believing changed nothing, forgave nothing, and yet it stood there in her chest beside the hatred like two clocks in one case, running at different speeds, refusing to be reconciled.

She had used a lonely boy's loneliness. She had wound it and set it ticking toward her purpose, and every hour he spent being glad of her company was an hour she was spending him.

Her father had taught her hands. Her father had taught her that a good repair leaves no mark, that the finest work is invisible. She had taken his teaching and turned it into a method for deceiving a sixteen-year-old who wanted to be a naval officer, and somewhere in the dark below her window the harbor was freezing over, inch by inch, building the road she would use to run.

*Don't,* she told herself, the way she'd tell a movement that wouldn't beat true. *Feel it later. There is no later until you're across.*

But the thought would not obey its train, and it was nearly midnight when she finally blew out the lamp, and the last thing she saw in the dark was the pocket watch on the table, her father's, still broken, mainspring let go — and beneath it, where she'd left them that morning and forgotten to hide them, the original ledger pages, and one page of Piet's checkpoint log copied in her hand, showing exactly which officer would be standing at which gate, and on what dates.

Downstairs, faintly, Mrs. Tarnow's floorboards creaked once, and went quiet — the sound of a woman awake in the dark, listening to someone else lie awake, and deciding, again, to say nothing yet.
