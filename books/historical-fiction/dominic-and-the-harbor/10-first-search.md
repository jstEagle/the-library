## 10. First Search

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, and it was wrong.

Edmund knew the sound of a knock before she knew anything else about a person — it was a habit of the trade, listening to how things struck other things. Mrs. Tarnow knocked twice, light, with the flat of her knuckles, always. Piet, when he came by with a broken watch and an excuse to talk, knocked three times quick as a boy skipping stones. But this knock was four beats, heavy, spaced like a man counting.

She was at the window with her coat already half on, the copies of the tide ledgers sewn into its lining against her ribs, because she had not slept properly in six days and had stopped pretending to herself that she would ever sleep properly again while the papers existed anywhere but against her own body. She looked down into the street.

Two men in customs gray stood on Mrs. Tarnow's step. A third waited by their cart, arms folded, watching the windows of the house the way a man watches a clock he suspects of lying.

Edmund took the coat off again, slowly, and laid it over the back of the chair as if it were nothing, as if her heart were not hammering against the very papers hidden inside it.

* * *

"They're asking for a thief," Mrs. Tarnow said through the door of Edmund's room, her voice pitched low and even, the voice of a woman reporting weather. "Harbor papers, they say. Stolen from the harbor office or the observatory, they weren't precise. They have a warrant signed by Corven Hale himself."

Edmund stood in the middle of the small room with her hands at her sides. The room was honest, at least. She had kept it honest for exactly this possibility: a bed made tight, a washstand, a trunk of clothes, a carpet bag with tools that were only tools — files, pliers, a loupe, oil cans. Nothing on the walls. No letters. A man's room belonging to a man who had nothing to hide.

"Let them look," she said.

"I intend to." A pause. Through the door, Edmund could hear Mrs. Tarnow's breathing, deliberate, the breath of a woman arranging herself. "But you'll want to know that they asked first for your name. Not 'the lodger.' Your name. Ashgrove. Someone gave it to them."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree and right itself. Only three people in Vell knew the name meant anything: Hale, who had known all along; Piet, who knew nothing; and whoever had written the anonymous tip that had set this whole machine turning. She thought of the observatory at night, of Hale winding the master clock with his head bowed like a man at a graveside, and she understood: he was not searching for papers. He was searching for proof of what he already feared. He wanted to know which shape his enemy wore.

"Mrs. Tarnow," Edmund said quietly, "if they find anything in this room, it's mine, and I'll say so, and you'll have no part of it."

The silence on the other side of the door lasted long enough that Edmund counted her own heartbeats against it. Then Mrs. Tarnow said, in the tone she used for discussing the price of coal, "Nonsense. Nothing in my house is yours until rent is paid, and rent is paid through the month. Whatever is in my house is mine to account for. That's the law and I'll thank them to remember it."

Footsteps moved away, firm on the stairs.

Edmund stood a moment longer. Then she did the thing she had decided not to do, the thing every instinct screamed against: she took the coat with the papers sewn into the lining, folded it once, opened her door, and crossed the landing to where Mrs. Tarnow stood smoothing her apron, and held it out.

For a heartbeat neither of them moved. The house smelled of bread and lamp oil and the salt that got into everything in Vell, even here, even high on the hill away from the water. Downstairs the knock came again, four heavy beats, patient as tide.

"You understand what you'd be carrying," Edmund said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "If they find it on you, it's not a fine. It's the magistrate. It's Vell deciding what kind of woman you are, and Vell has never once decided kindly."

Mrs. Tarnow looked at the coat. Then she looked at Edmund — really looked, the way she had looked that first week at Edmund's hands, saying nothing, seeing everything — and took it out of her arms as simply as taking in laundry.

"My sister's quilt is on the line," she said. "It wants bringing in before those men tramp snow through my hall. Go and stand by your window and be a young man mending a watch."

* * *

They searched badly, which told Edmund something important: whoever had tipped them had given names and suspicions but no particulars, because a tip with particulars would have produced a search with particulars. They went through her trunk with the thoroughness of men performing thoroughness, lifting shirts and letting them fall in wrinkles Mrs. Tarnow would suffer over for days. They tapped the walls of her room twice each, without conviction. The elder one, gray-whiskered with the portly authority of a man who had spent thirty years stamping manifests, picked up her tool roll and unrolled it across the bed with a clatter.

"Clockmaker," he said, not quite a question.

"Journeyman," Edmund said. "Ashgrove. I'm here under commission from the observatory. Master clock. You can verify it with the astronomer, Mr. Fenwick, or with the harbor master, who engaged me for his own study clock besides."

"We know who you are." He said it mildly, watching her face the way she watched a movement in a mechanism that ought to be smooth and wasn't. "That's rather the point of today."

"Then I'm glad to save you the trouble of learning it twice."

The younger officer laughed — one short syllable, quickly smothered — and the older one shot him a look that could have stopped a pendulum. Edmund kept her eyes level and her hands loose at her sides and thought, with a strange cold clarity, *this is what Papa must have done, standing on the deck of the Wren, reading the water that was lying to him.* The thought did not frighten her. It steadied her, the way a heavy flywheel steadies a train of gears.

They went through the carpet bag. They found the spare mainspring wrapped in oilcloth and the tin of French chalk and nothing else, because there was nothing else to find, because everything that mattered was upstairs in a quilt on a line that Mrs. Tarnow had brought in twenty minutes ago and folded, with great ceremony, over the rail of her own bed behind a door she had informed the officers led to her late husband's things, which she did not permit anyone to touch, and which they evidently did not dare.

The gray-whiskered one stood in the middle of the room at last, dissatisfied in the way of a man whose warrant has come up empty and who knows the emptiness will be reported upward. "You'll forgive the intrusion, Mr. Ashgrove."

"I forgive the necessity," Edmund said. "Papers get stolen in harbor towns. I'd want them found too."

Something flickered across his face — not suspicion; recognition, almost, of a sentiment he shared. "Aye," he said. "That we would." And then, at the door, with his hand on the frame and his back half turned: "Odd thing, though. The complaint named this house before it named any other lodging in Vell. Out of eleven houses that take lodgers." He let that sit in the air between them like a weight lowered onto a scale. "Someone knows this house. Someone knows you. Might be worth a careful man asking himself who."

He went down the stairs. The front door closed. The cart creaked away through the slush.

Edmund stood in her violated room among her unfolded shirts and did not move until she heard Mrs. Tarnow's footsteps on the stair, slow and unhurried, and the soft double knock of knuckles on her open door.

* * *

They sat in the kitchen, because Mrs. Tarnow said searches unsettled a house and the only cure was tea taken in the warmest room of it. The stove ticked. Snow slid off the eaves outside in soft thumps. On the table between them lay the coat, and beside the coat, folded into a square no bigger than a hymnal, the quilt — her sister's quilt, blue calico gone thin at the folds, stitched in patterns of stars that Mrs. Tarnow's sister had put there forty years ago in another town, for another life.

"You'll want to know how I did it," Mrs. Tarnow said, pouring. "In case you ever need to do it yourself."

Edmund wrapped both hands around the cup and said nothing, which was agreement.

"A quilt has a binding edge. An inch wide, if you make it generous, and my sister was generous. You pick the stitches along a hand-span of it — takes four minutes with a good needle, less if you've fingers like yours — and you have a pocket the width of the quilt and nobody alive thinks to look inside a quilt's binding, because nobody alive has ever made one." She smoothed the blue calico with one palm, an affectionate gesture, absent. "I put your papers flat along the bottom hem and restitched it after. My sister's stitch pattern isn't hard to copy. I've been copying it since she died, mending it. Nobody will see a difference, because there isn't one."

"You didn't ask what they were," Edmund said. "You didn't ask last night either, when you hid them without being asked at all."

"No."

"They could have been anything. Forged bills. Love letters. Money owed to dangerous people."

Mrs. Tarnow set down the pot. She looked out the window at the frozen harbor, white to the far shore, the masts of the icebound boats sticking up like winter wheat. When she spoke, her voice was quieter and older.

"I saw the Wren go down."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below the ice, the harbor groaned — a long, deep sound, the sea shifting under its lid, and Edmund felt it in the floorboards and in her teeth.

"Night of the ninth of November, five years back. I was at this window. I couldn't sleep — my hip, in the cold, it aches me still. I saw her lights come in wrong. Too far north, coming in too fast for the tide that was running, and I remember thinking, that pilot's been lied to, because I grew up with a father who read water and I know what a boat looks like when it trusts a chart that's false." She turned her gaze from the window to Edmund, and her eyes were dry and very steady. "Then the rocks took her, and the screaming carried all the way up the hill, and in the morning there were men at my door with money and a story about how the Wren's pilot had misread the tide tables through his own fault, drink perhaps, grief perhaps, and wouldn't it be a kindness to the widow and the children if the town agreed. And I took the money. Eleven years I've taken it, every season, quiet as rent."

Edmund's throat had closed. She made herself ask, though she already knew, though the answer had been waiting in her chest since the ledgers, since the observatory, since the summons itself: "Whose name did they give you? The pilot's?"

"Josiah Ashgrove." Mrs. Tarnow said it plainly, without ceremony, the way you set down something heavy so it can finally rest. "I've read your face a hundred times since you came, child. I've read your hands longer than that. I didn't know what I was waiting to be true. But when those officers knocked this morning asking after stolen harbor papers, I understood that whatever you carry, it's him you carry it for. And I decided before you ever handed me that coat that I'd sooner hang than hand Josiah Ashgrove's daughter to Corven Hale's men."

The kitchen was very warm and very still. Edmund looked down at her tea and found her vision blurred and did not fight it and did not apologize for it either. Five years she had carried her father's name like contraband — spoken to no one, written nowhere, folded small and hidden in the same inner pocket where she kept herself. And now it sat on a kitchen table between two cups of tea, in the open, in another person's keeping, and the world had not ended. The world had, in fact, become slightly more possible.

"The money," Edmund managed at last. "You kept taking it."

"Every season. And every season I wrote down the date and the amount and the name of the man who paid me, in a little book I keep inside the cover of my Bible, because I am a coward, Edmund Ashgrove, but I am a coward who keeps accounts." Something moved at the corner of her mouth that was not quite a smile. "There are fourteen entries. Fourteen seasons of silence, bought and paid for by the harbor master's own hand. If your papers need a witness, mine will testify."

Edmund stared at her. "You've had evidence against him for five years."

"I've had evidence and no use for it. Evidence needs someone willing to stand in front of a town and be destroyed for it, and I wasn't willing. I'm old and comfortable and afraid, and I chose those things, and I won't pretend otherwise now." She reached across the table and laid her hand, brief and hard, over Edmund's wrist. "But you're willing. I've watched you be willing for weeks. So take my book when the time comes. Take my testimony. Take whatever an old woman's word is worth against Corven Hale, because it turns out it's worth more than I thought, sitting here watching you not flinch."

* * *

That night Edmund sat at her window with the lamp turned low, working a watch that needed no working, and made herself think like a mechanism instead of a frightened animal.

The search had failed, but it had taught Hale three things, and she enumerated them the way she'd count the teeth of a wheel. One: the tip had named this house, which meant the tip came from someone who knew where she lodged — the ferry office list, most likely, or the observatory's own commission ledger. Two: the search had found nothing, which would not satisfy him; it would sharpen him. Three: the gray-whiskered officer had warned her, openly, at the door — a customs man warning the suspect. Which meant that somewhere inside the customs house there was at least one man who did not love Corven Hale, and that her father's name still carried weight in this town among men old enough to have known him.

Assets, then: Mrs. Tarnow's testimony, fourteen entries strong. A sympathetic officer, unnamed. Piet at the ferry office, loyal to Hale but hungry for arithmetic. The ice road forming day by day in the harbor, white and widening, the one road out.

Liabilities: Hale's certainty growing by the hour. Season's end coming like a falling weight. And the burning — she had heard it talked of in the market, heard it repeated with approval, the harbor master tidying the old records at last, closing the books on the Wrenwreck forever. Weeks, perhaps days.

Below her window the harbor lay silent under its ice, and far off, at the base of the observatory hill, she could see the small moving spark of a lantern making its way up the path at the usual hour, the usual pace. Hale, going to wind the master clock. Going to kneel before the machine where he had hidden his crime and where she had found it, night after night, performing his penance or his patrol — she no longer believed she knew which.

Edmund closed the watch case with a click that sounded, in the dark, very loud.

"He knows I'm here," she said aloud to the empty room, testing the truth of it in her mouth. "And I know he knows. And neither of us can move first."

Except that one of them had just gained a quilt full of testimony and an ally who kept accounts, and the other had only fear and fire.

She trimmed the lamp, lay down, and for the first time in six days slept — deeply, dreamlessly, the sleep of a gear that has finally found the tooth it meshes with.
