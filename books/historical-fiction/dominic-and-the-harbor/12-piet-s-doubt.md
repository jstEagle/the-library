## 12. Piet's Doubt

The ferry office smelled of tar and cold tea, the way it always did, but that morning there was something new under it — woodsmoke from the stove Piet had fed too eagerly, as if heat could make the winter pass faster. He sat behind the counter with his boots hooked on the rung of his stool, swinging them like a boy half his age, and when the bell over the door rattled he looked up with the same open face he'd worn every day for three weeks.

"Edmund! You're early."

"You're open." Edmund set her tool roll on the counter and unwound her scarf. "That's rarer than you think in this town."

Piet grinned. He was sixteen going on forty, with a clerk's neat hands and a harbor boy's chapped knuckles, and he kept the ferry office's one wall clock running better than it deserved because Edmund had shown him how to oil the train without drowning it. In exchange he told her things: which customs men drank at which tavern, when the ice road's surveyors were expected, what Corven Hale said at the harbor board meetings that Piet wasn't supposed to repeat.

Edmund had come to value those conversations more than she liked to admit. That was the trouble with using kindness as a tool — after enough days, your hand forgot it was holding anything at all, and just held on.

"The surveyors came yesterday," Piet said, leaning forward, dropping his voice as though the empty room might contain listeners. "Old man Voss walked the harbor edge with his pole and his auger. Tapped the ice all along the channel. My uncle says if the cold holds another week — maybe less — they'll flag the road."

"Maybe less," Edmund repeated. Her heart did something complicated she chose not to examine.

"That's good, isn't it? You'll want to get home before the deep winter." Piet frowned slightly. "You never say much about home."

"There isn't much to say." She took out the ferry office clock's pendulum, which she'd taken home two nights ago to true its beat, and began fitting it back onto its suspension spring. Work first. Work was the honest part of every visit. "Your escapement was dragging. The pallets were gummy. Someone's been touching this clock with harbor fingers."

"That would be me," Piet admitted. "I tried to clean it. With lamp oil."

"With—" Edmund closed her eyes briefly. "Piet. Lamp oil is not cleaning oil. It's glue that hasn't made up its mind."

He laughed, unashamed, and slid off the stool to watch her work, close enough that his breath fogged the brass. This was the other thing about Piet: he watched everything. Not suspiciously — hungrily. He wanted to know how clocks worked, how tides worked, how the world worked, and he asked questions the way other boys threw stones, constantly and without malice.

It was that hunger Edmund had been turning over for four nights now, the way she turned a difficult wheel in her fingers before committing to a repair.

She had copied the ledgers. All of them, by lamplight, in a hand disguised as her own disguised hand — smaller, rounder, a clerk's script rather than a craftsman's. Mrs. Tarnow had sewn the copies into the lining of a quilt without being asked, and Edmund had lain awake under that quilt feeling the weight of paper against her shoulder blades like a second spine. But copies were only half a weapon. Copies could be dismissed. *Forged*, Hale would say. *Made by some thief of harbor papers* — and the town had already heard that phrase whispered once, in the week after the customs search that found nothing.

What the copies needed was a witness. Someone inside Vell. Someone whose loyalty to Hale was not a debt but a devotion, so that when it broke, it broke loudly.

She had not decided to do it this morning. She told herself that, even as her hand finished seating the pendulum and her mouth opened on words that had been waiting behind her teeth for days.

"Piet. Can I ask you something about arithmetic?"

"Arithmetic?" He blinked. "I'm not the best at figures. That's why I'm a ferry clerk and not a merchant."

"Then I'll teach you while I ask." She pulled a blank page from the office's own pad — his pad, his pen, his ink, so that nothing of hers remained — and drew a simple column. "Say a pilot boat leaves the outer light at slack water. Say the tide tables for that night say the water stands at nine feet over the bar rocks."

"All right."

"And say the official ledger for that night — the one the harbor master signed — says the same. Nine feet. Safe passage." She wrote the numbers. "Now say a different record exists. A working ledger, kept hour by hour by the tide keeper himself, before anyone signed anything official. And that one says five feet."

Piet went still. Not dramatically. Just — still, the way a boy goes still when a game stops being a game.

"Five feet wouldn't clear the bar," he said quietly. "Not for a boat drawing six. Everyone knows the Wrenwreck water. We learn it as children. Five feet is the number that kills boats."

"Yes."

"But the official ledger said nine. It's in the memorial book. Master Hale read it out himself, at the anniversary, so no one would forget the truth of that night." Piet's voice had a defensive edge now, thin as ice near the shore. "He read the figures out loud, Edmund. Why would he do that if—"

"If he wanted people to check them?" Edmund set down the pen. "No. If he wanted people to *stop* checking them. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. A man who hides a number burns it. A man who reads a number aloud makes it holy. Nobody audits scripture, Piet."

The stove ticked. Outside, a gull argued with the wind over something neither would win.

"What are you saying?" Piet asked. His voice had gone careful. "Who showed you these figures?"

"A page came into my hands." True, and useless, and exactly as damning as silence. "I'm not going to tell you how, and I'm not going to tell you who gave it to me, because I don't know, and because knowing would put you in a position you shouldn't be in. I'm asking you something simpler. I'm asking you to do sums."

"I don't want to do sums." But he was looking at the page. Sixteen years old, hungry for how things worked, and she had laid a mechanism on the counter in front of him with one gear missing, and she knew — she knew the way she knew a mainspring was overwound — that he could not leave it alone.

"You do," Edmund said gently. "You've been doing them since I sat down. Look at the dates on the two entries. Same night. Same hour. Two different waters. One of them is wrong. So ask yourself the question any apprentice asks when two parts of a machine contradict each other: which part is lying, and why?"

Piet didn't touch the page. He looked at it the way he'd once looked at the inside of the wall clock when Edmund first opened it for him — as if it might bite, and as if being bitten might be worth it.

"The tide keeper wrote the working ledger," he said at last. "Old Brandt. He drowned two years ago. Winter fever."

"I know."

"Everyone said he'd gone strange at the end. Talking about the Wrenwreck. Saying the harbor master owed him." Piet's jaw worked. "People laughed at him. My father laughed at him."

"People laugh at men who tell uncomfortable truths right up until the moment they need the truth, and then they're furious it wasn't written down anywhere." Edmund kept her voice level, unhurried, a craftsman's voice. She would not push. Pushing a hesitant gear strips its teeth. "I'm not asking you to believe a stranger's page, Piet. I'm asking you to believe your own eyes and your own subtraction. Nine minus five. What does the harbor draw?"

"Four feet." He said it like a confession. "A boat drawing six sits two feet into the rocks."

"And whose signature is on the nine?"

The silence stretched long enough that Edmund began fitting the clock's case door back on, giving him privacy the way you give a wound air. When she glanced up, Piet had picked up the page — finally touched it — and was holding it at arm's length as if distance might change the ink.

"He signed it," Piet said. "Master Hale signed the nine."

"He signs everything. That's what harbor masters do. It doesn't mean he wrote it." Edmund surprised herself with that — the fairness of it rising out of her unbidden, five chapters of dread suddenly complicated by a memory of a study full of drowned men's watches, each one cleaned and oiled and running, grief maintained like a mechanism. "But someone wrote it. And someone let the real number sit in a drawer for five years. And someone recently hid that drawer inside a clock case where no honest person would ever look."

"Why are you showing me this?" Piet put the page down fast, as if it had grown hot. "Why me? You barely know me."

Because you're kind, Edmund thought. Because you idolize a man who may have killed my father, and faith that survives arithmetic is worth ten times faith that never meets it. Because I cannot carry this town's memory across the ice alone; paper convinces magistrates, but people convince towns.

"Because you keep the ferry office clock," she said instead, "and you noticed yourself that it ran slow all last month and nobody else did. Because you count things. Because when the ice road opens, someone will stand at that window and stamp papers for everyone leaving Vell, and I wanted — before that happens — for one person in this town to have done the sums."

Piet stared at her. Something moved behind his face that she recognized from her own mirror: the specific vertigo of a load-bearing belief beginning to crack. It doesn't shatter. It groans, and settles, and lets in drafts.

"My uncle was on the rescue boats that night," he said, very quietly. "He never talks about it. Except once. He'd been drinking, and he said the water was wrong, that the bar was showing white water where the charts said deep channel, and that when he said so at the time, Master Hale told him the charts were right and his eyes were tired." Piet swallowed. "I always thought it was the drink talking."

"Was your uncle a drinking man?"

"No." A pause. "That was the thing. He stopped entirely after that night. Five years. Never touched it again." Piet looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious at once, sixteen years old and forty at the same time. "You stop drinking when you can't forget something. Everybody knows that."

Edmund said nothing. There was nothing to add. Arithmetic had done its work; the rest was his.

* * *

She spent the afternoon at the observatory, ostensibly finishing the master clock's annual regulation, actually listening — because the clock itself had become her informant. Its tick carried through the tower's stone throat differently depending on whether the doors below stood open or shut, and twice that week she had heard Hale's uneven tread on the stairs after dark, his pause at the case, the small ritual sounds of a key and a winding crank. He came nightly now. He checked the drawer space behind the great wheel where the ledgers had hidden, though the ledgers were long gone into Mrs. Tarnow's quilt. He was looking for whoever had taken them, and finding nothing, winding the clock anyway, as if keeping time honestly might balance the books of everything else.

Hunter and hunted, exchanging places in the dark, both pretending the clock was only a clock.

When she came down at dusk, Piet was waiting outside the observatory gate, stamping his feet against the cold, and the sight of him there sent a cold of its own through her — had he talked? Had she misjudged?

"I did the sums again," he said, without greeting. "All evening. I made up other wrecks. Other tides. I tried to find a way the nine could be true and the five could be true at the same time." His breath steamed. "There isn't one. Unless the sea itself lied, and the sea doesn't lie. That's the whole point of the sea. That's why we have the ledgers at all — because the sea won't testify, so we write down what it says."

"Yes," Edmund said.

"So either the page is forged, or Master Hale is a liar, and I've thought about forgery, and the hand matches the tide keeper's hand — my father has an old receipt Brandt wrote him for mooring fees, and I compared them, I held them up to the lamp." Piet's voice cracked and steadied. "It's Brandt's writing, Edmund. It's really his."

She waited. The harbor behind him was sealing itself shut, sheet by sheet, gray ice creeping out from the pilings toward the channel like a slow white verdict.

"I'm not saying I believe everything," Piet said. "I'm not saying anything yet. But if there are more pages — if whoever gave you that page has more — then somebody should compare them properly. Somebody young, whose eyes are good. Somebody who works at a checkpoint and sees every document that leaves this town." He lifted his chin, defiant and terrified and resolved all at once. "Somebody like me."

Every instinct Edmund had honed for eleven years said *refuse*. Every year of passing, of trusting no one, of measuring her life in what could be carried, said that allies were liabilities with warm hands. But she thought of Mrs. Tarnow sewing papers into a quilt without being asked, and of her father's pocket watch ticking faithfully in a dead man's shrine, and of the fact that she had crossed half a country to give a name back to a drowned man and could not do it alone.

"There will be a packet," she said slowly. "Soon. Before the season ends. It has to reach the mainland magistrate, and it has to pass three checkpoints, and the third one belongs to Corven Hale himself." She met Piet's eyes. "I'm not asking you to help me. I'm telling you so you can decide, with your eyes open, whether you know anything at all. There's a difference between mercy and complicity, and I won't choose yours for you."

Piet stood very straight in the blue dusk, a boy at a window where every departing soul must be stamped, and Edmund watched him understand — visibly, like frost forming — exactly what she was asking, and exactly what it would cost.

"The ice road opens in four days," he said. "Voss flagged the channel this morning. And Edmund —" he hesitated, and the hesitation was worse than anything he'd said all evening — "there's a notice going up tomorrow. Master Hale ordered it posted at all three checkpoints tonight. Old harbor records are being destroyed at season's end. For tidiness, the notice says." His voice dropped. "Everything. The whole archive. Including the memorial book."

The ice in the harbor groaned, long and low, like a floor shifting under weight.

Four days. Edmund turned her collar to the wind and began, already, to count what could be carried.
