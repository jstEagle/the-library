## 13. The Ice Road Forms

The cold came down from the north like a verdict.

Edmund felt it first in the observatory, in the master clock itself — the pendulum swinging a hair slower each morning as the oil thickened, the escapement ticking with a faint reluctance, as if the machine understood what was coming and wished to be spared it. She adjusted the temperature compensation, cleaned the pivots a third time, and stood with her ear against the case listening to the heartbeat she had spent two months learning.

*Not yet,* she told it. *Hold together a little longer.*

Outside the observatory windows, Vell Harbor was dying by inches. The water at the inner basin had gone still and gray, then gray-white, then white. Slush ice formed along the pilings on Tuesday; by Thursday the fishing boats were locked in place, their rigging furred with frost, their owners walking out to them across ice that creaked but held. Children skated where the pilot boats used to run. An old man sat on an upturned dory at the harbor's edge, mending nets with fingers too stiff for the work, doing it anyway because that was what men in Vell did when the sea closed its hand.

And every day, the ice crept further out toward the channel. Every day, the gap between the harbor and the mainland shore narrowed.

Every day, the clock ran down.

* * *

She heard about the ice road before she saw it. Piet told her, breathless, in the ferry office where she'd come to return a repaired regulator he'd left with her — a small brass thing, his one possession of any value, traded for gossip as always.

"Three days," he said, holding the regulator like a rescued bird. "Maybe four. Old Sander walked the channel this morning with his measuring pole. He says the ice is fourteen inches past the point." Piet grinned, and for once there was no hero-worship in it, only the ordinary hunger of a boy who wanted winter to mean something. "Once Sander posts the notice, they'll mark the road with the black boughs, and then anyone can cross. First time since November."

"And the checkpoints?" Edmund kept her voice light, professional, a tradesman asking after logistics.

Piet shrugged. "Customs house opens a window on the ice itself. Ferry office does the same — I'll likely draw that shift, if I'm lucky." He said *lucky* the way other boys said *drowned*, but he said it. "Then the gate at the harbor master's yard, before the road climbs to the mainland track. Three stamps on your paper and you're through."

Three stamps. She'd known the shape of it for weeks, but hearing Piet recite it so simply — three doors between her and the truth, each one staffed by someone who could look at her hands, her throat, her eyes, and see what the whole town somehow hadn't — made her stomach tighten around nothing.

"You'll be standing in the cold all day," she said instead.

"It's the important shifts people fight over." Piet set the regulator carefully into his coat pocket, patting it. "Everyone wants to be the one who opens the road."

*Or closes it,* Edmund thought, and hated herself a little for thinking it, because Piet had been nothing but kind, and kindness was not the same as loyalty, and she was going to have to find out which one he had before the ice did its work.

She walked back through the town under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Smoke stood straight up from every chimney — windless cold, the kind that made sound carry strangely, so that she could hear, from three streets away, the gulls arguing over the fish market scraps, and beneath them, fainter, the groan of the harbor ice settling into itself like a large animal making ready to sleep.

At the corner by the chandlery, a knot of men stood reading something nailed to the post. Edmund slowed without stopping, the way she'd learned to slow everywhere in Vell — never quite halting, never quite hurrying.

A printed notice. The harbor master's seal at the bottom.

She read it in the time it took to pass: **BY ORDER OF THE HARBOR MASTER, ALL ARCHIVE RECORDS OLDER THAN FIVE YEARS SHALL BE INSPECTED AND DESTROYED BY FIRE AT SEASON'S END, IN THE INTEREST OF TIDINESS AND THE RECOVERY OF SPACE.**

Five years.

The Wrenwreck was five years old in March.

* * *

Mrs. Tarnow had the kettle on when Edmund came in, which she always did now at dusk, though neither of them had ever agreed to it. The ritual had assembled itself out of small permissions: a second cup appearing in the cupboard, a chair drawn closer to the stove, the lamp turned up a notch higher than thrift would allow. They drank tea in the front room where the window faced the harbor, and mostly they talked about weather, and in the space underneath the weather they conducted an entire other conversation that neither of them ever named.

"Tidiness," Mrs. Tarnow said, when Edmund mentioned the notice. She said the word the way you'd spit out a fishbone.

"That's what it says."

"I've lived in Vell sixty-one years." Mrs. Tarnow poured, her hands steady, her eyes on the harbor. "I've seen them burn driftwood and dead nets and one year the whole contents of the customs cellar, and every single time it was called tidiness, and every single time it was something else." She handed Edmund her cup. "Tidiness is what a man calls it when he's cleaning up after himself."

Edmund wrapped her fingers around the cup and let the heat sink in. Her copies of the ledgers were sewn into the lining of her work coat, thirty-one pages of a dead clerk's careful arithmetic, and she thought of them lying against her ribs like a second heart.

"When is season's end?" she asked. "Officially."

"Whenever the last boat leaves or the first sledge goes, whichever comes later. Then the harbor master closes the books." Mrs. Tarnow took her own cup and sat, and for a while the only sound was the tick of the cheap mantel clock she kept fifteen minutes fast and refused to correct. "You're counting days, girl."

It wasn't a question, so Edmund didn't answer it.

"I'm not blind," Mrs. Tarnow went on, mild as milk. "I've watched you copy papers by lamplight until your eyes go red. I've watched you measure the distance to the mainland shore every morning like a woman pacing a cell. And I've watched Corven Hale walk up to that observatory every night at nine o'clock to wind a clock that doesn't need winding." She sipped. "I don't ask. But I'll say this much for free: whatever you're carrying, you'd best carry it soon. Ice roads close as quick as they open, and men who burn things rarely wait for a convenient wind."

Edmund looked at her over the rim of the cup. "Why do you help me?"

Mrs. Tarnow met her eyes, and behind the mildness there was something old and hard, bedrock under snow. "Because I've been tidy myself once," she said, "and I didn't sleep right for twenty years."

She said nothing more, and Edmund asked nothing more, and the mantel clock ticked its dishonest fifteen minutes while outside the harbor groaned and settled, groaned and settled, the ice thickening in the dark.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep, so she worked.

She sat at the little table in her room with the lamp turned low and laid out everything she had, the way she'd lay out a movement on the bench before reassembling it: the copied ledger pages, numbered in pencil; her own notes on the discrepancies, cross-referenced to dates; the memory-map of Hale's study and the true ledger she'd found there, the one with her father's name entered in a dead man's handwriting on the night the *Wren* went onto the rocks. Originals were better than copies. Copies could be called forgeries, dismissed, laughed out of any inquiry. But the originals were scattered — most here, sewn into her coat, and the true ledger still hidden in Hale's own house, behind the shrine to the drowned, where she could not go again without ending the delicate fiction that kept her alive in this town.

Unless.

She turned the problem over the way she'd turn a stubborn gear, looking for the tooth that would catch.

Hale burned records at season's end. Season's end was when the ice road opened — days now. Which meant the burning would happen in the same narrow window in which she meant to cross. He would burn the archive; would he burn the study ledger too? A man who kept a shrine to his victims might keep their names safe forever, or might, in the end, feed even those to the fire, because guilt kept is guilt preserved, and guilt burned is — what? Absolution? Erasure?

She thought of his face in the lamplight of his study, the night she'd found the watch — her father's watch — sitting among the others like relics on an altar. She thought of him winding the master clock every night at nine, punctual as grief.

He knew. That was the thing she kept circling back to. Someone had found the ledgers in the clock — she was sure of it now, had been sure since the third night she'd watched him climb the observatory hill. The sabotage, the nightly visit, the announcement of a burning: it wasn't tidiness. It was a man hunting a ghost through his own house, burning the forest down to flush it out.

Which meant every day she waited was a day he moved closer. And every day the ice thickened was a day the road to the mainland came nearer — for both of them.

She blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the harbor. The groaning had changed tonight. Deeper, slower, with long silences between, like breathing before speech. By morning, she guessed, Sander would walk the channel again. By tomorrow evening, perhaps, the black boughs would go up along the ice, marking the road the way you'd mark a grave.

Three days. Maybe four.

* * *

In the morning the whole town turned out to watch the sea finish dying.

Edmund stood among them at the harbor wall, collar up, hands in pockets, one of forty gray figures in the gray light. Out past the breakwater the last lane of open water had narrowed overnight to a black seam, and as they watched, the seam whitened at the edges, shrank, and closed — not dramatically, not all at once, but with a soft grinding sigh, the way a door closes when someone means it to stay shut. A murmur went down the wall. Somewhere a child cheered and was hushed.

Old Sander was already out on it, absurd and brave in his long coat, walking the future road with his measuring pole and two boys carrying stakes. He went slowly, testing each stride, and the crowd tracked him in silence the way crowds track a tightrope walker. At the halfway point he stopped, drove a stake, and raised his arm.

The murmur became a cheer. The ice road would open. Two days, the word passed down the wall — two days, maybe three, and Vell would be joined to the world again.

Edmund stayed after the crowd thinned, watching Sander's small figure plant the black-boughed stakes one by one across the frozen channel, a dotted line drawn on white paper, an arrow pointing away from everything she knew.

Two days.

She went to the observatory. It was habit now, and cover, and refuge, all three. The master clock needed only its daily check, but she gave it an hour anyway, oiling, adjusting, listening, and at the end of it she stood with her palm flat against the case and felt the mechanism running clean and true beneath the wood, the finest work she'd ever done, keeping perfect time for a town that would remember her, if it remembered her at all, as the apprentice who fixed the clock.

"Hold together," she whispered to it, and couldn't have said whether she meant the clock or herself.

* * *

The summons came at noon, delivered by a harbor office boy who wouldn't meet her eyes: the harbor master requested the clockmaker's presence at the archive inspection, Friday next, to regulate the office chronometer beforehand. Standard practice. Nothing unusual.

Nothing unusual except that Friday was the day after the ice road opened, and the inspection would be followed, everyone knew, by the burning.

Edmund sent back her acceptance in her best forged journeyman's hand — *E. Ashgrove, at your service* — and then sat very still at the table, looking at the thirty-one pages sewn into her coat, and understood that Hale had just shown her his cards without meaning to, or meaning to, she couldn't tell which. He wanted her where he could see her on the last day. He wanted the clockmaker present when the records burned, the way you want the doctor present at an execution, to certify that everything was done properly.

Or he wanted her close enough to catch.

Either way, the schedule was no longer hers to choose. If she crossed on the opening day, she'd leave the true ledger behind in his study, half her evidence ash within the week. If she waited to take it, she'd be crossing with the burning already lit behind her and Hale watching the road.

There was one night between the two. One night when the ice would bear weight and the checkpoints would stand empty and the town would be asleep, and in that night she would have to get into Corven Hale's house, past his shrine, out again with a dead man's ledger, and across a frozen harbor to a customs window manned by a stranger — all without waking a single soul in a town that had spent five years learning to listen for exactly this.

She took out her pencil and began, methodically, dreadfully, to make a list.

Under the floor, Mrs. Tarnow's stove ticked as it cooled, and far out on the harbor the ice spoke in its new deep voice, over and over, like a clock counting down.
