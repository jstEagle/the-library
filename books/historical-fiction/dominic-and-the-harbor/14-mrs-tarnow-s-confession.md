## 14. Mrs. Tarnow's Confession

The list had three items by the time Edmund finished it, and every one of them required something she did not have: a key, a darkness deep enough to hide a body-shaped shadow, and luck. She was still staring at the paper when the knock came — two soft raps, the second one lower, as if the knocker had lost her nerve halfway through.

Mrs. Tarnow never knocked. In three months of lodgings she had come up the stairs with firewood, with soup, with complaints about Piet's boots on her porch, and each time she had simply opened the door as though the room were an extension of her own kitchen. Tonight she knocked.

Edmund folded the list into her sleeve and said, "Come in."

But Mrs. Tarnow only opened the door halfway and stood in the gap holding a lamp, and Edmund saw at once that this was not a visit about soup. The old woman had put her good shawl on, the one with the jet beads, the one Edmund had seen her wear to the Wrenwreck memorial. Her hair was pinned with unusual care. She looked, Edmund thought, like someone dressed for a funeral — her own.

"There's tea downstairs," Mrs. Tarnow said. "If you're willing."

It was past eleven. The harbor wind had settled into its winter register, that long flat note that made the shutters hum. Downstairs the kitchen was warm from a stove that should have been banked hours ago, and on the table were two cups, the teapot, and — Edmund's stomach tightened when she saw it — the tin of shortbread Mrs. Tarnow kept for occasions she considered worthy, which in five years of widowhood had amounted to perhaps four.

"Sit," Mrs. Tarnow said.

Edmund sat. She was aware of the papers sewn into her coat lining hanging on the peg by the door — copies, all of them now, but copies of things worth killing for, if Corven Hale's sabotage of the master clock meant anything at all. She had not told Mrs. Tarnow. She had been so careful. And yet here was the landlady pouring tea at midnight in her funeral shawl, looking at Edmund with those pale, unhurried eyes that had watched her all winter the way one watches a clock one suspects of running fast.

"You've been making lists," Mrs. Tarnow said. "I see your light under the door. Two, three in the morning. A man planning a job sleeps before he plans or after. You sleep never." She set the pot down. "And you've started walking to the point like a man who expects to be stopped."

"I don't know what you—"

"Maren."

Edmund went very still.

The name hung in the steam between them. Not Edmund. Not Ashgrove. *Maren* — her mother's name for her, used only in the last year of her life, spoken only indoors, only after checking the window. Nobody in Vell could know it. Nobody alive knew it.

Mrs. Tarnow lifted her own cup, drank, and set it down with the small precise click of a woman who had spent decades handling other people's porcelain without breaking any of it.

"Your father wrote to me," she said. "Five years ago. Three weeks before the *Wren*. Did you know that?"

"No," Edmund said. Her voice came out sanded flat.

"He didn't tell you because you'd just gone up to Grunstadt for your apprenticeship, and he didn't want to worry you, and — well." Something moved across her face, old and tired. "He wrote asking whether I'd keep my mouth shut a while longer, or whether I was ready to stop being paid to. That was the last letter. After the wreck there wasn't anything left to ask."

The stove ticked. Out on the ice the harbor groaned, that long tearing exhale Edmund had learned to hear in her sleep.

"Paid to," Edmund repeated.

"Fifty crowns a quarter, the first two years. Then thirty. Then nothing, which I took for contempt." Mrs. Tarnow refilled her own cup, though Edmund noticed her hand did not quite steady on the pot's handle. "My window faces the east channel, girl. Same view for thirty-one years. On the night of the ninth of November I couldn't sleep — bad knee, bad weather, same reasons then as now. I saw the pilot boat go out. I saw the signal lamps on the point. Green over white, green over white — the safe-channel signal, the one that says come in by the rocks." She looked up. "You'd know better than most what it means when a lamp says one thing and the tide says another."

Edmund knew. She had spent five years knowing it in brass and steel, in the falsified columns of ledgers hidden inside a clock, in the arithmetic of a night her father had drowned. But hearing it said aloud, in a warm kitchen, by a witness — it landed differently. It landed like weight finding a bearing.

"The tide was wrong," Edmund said quietly. "The readings published that morning were wrong by nearly two fathoms at low water. Hale entered them clean. The true ledger shows what the gauge actually read. He signed both."

"He signed his lie and hid the truth in a clock," Mrs. Tarnow said. "Yes. I know about the clock. I've known since a boy named Piet carried a crate up the observatory hill last month and told me the master clock had failed for the first time in forty years. Nothing in this town fails by accident, Miss Ashgrove. Or whatever else you are." She paused. "I saw the *Wren* turn in. I saw her take the rocks. I heard it — the sound carries over water at night, worse than seeing it. And the next morning Corven Hale came to my kitchen with an envelope and an expression I can only call apologetic, and he said: 'You were asleep, Mrs. Tarnow. You slept through the whole miserable business. It would be a kindness to the families if you remembered it that way.'"

"And you took the money."

"I took the money." No flinch. "I had a sick husband and a mortgage and no trade but washing other people's linens. You'll notice I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you the price of silence, because you've been calculating yours all winter and I want you to know it compounds." She pushed her cup aside and folded her hands. "Here is what changed my mind, since you're too polite to ask. Your light under the door. Every night, working. My husband was a cooper — barrels, hoops, honest staves — and he worked like that at the end, when we knew he was dying and there was a will to write. Men work like that when they're putting their affairs in order. Women too. So either you're leaving Vell forever, or you're going somewhere dangerous, and either way you'd go alone, because you trust nobody, because trusting nobody has kept you alive since you were fourteen years old and put on your first pair of men's trousers."

Edmund's breath caught. Mrs. Tarnow did not even pause.

"Don't look like that. I raised four sons. I know a bound chest when I see one, and I know it doesn't matter what's painted on the outside." She rose, went to the dresser, and came back with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string — the kind of parcel a fisherman's wife knows how to make, tight enough to survive a dunking. She set it in front of Edmund but kept her hands on it. "Inside is a written statement, dated, signed, witnessed by Father Anselm who has been waiting five years for permission to be useful. Inside is also the envelope with the first payment — I kept it, don't ask me why, women keep receipts for the injuries done them. Fifty crowns, marked in Hale's own hand on the corner where he thought ink wouldn't show on that paper. It shows."

Edmund stared at the parcel. All winter she had planned like an engineer, reducing an impossible crossing to forces and clearances, checkpoints and hours. She had accounted for ice, wind, lamplight, the habits of guards. She had never once accounted for this: that someone might simply *hand* her the missing piece, unprompted, out of nothing but a shared kitchen and five years of a guilty conscience.

"Why now?" she asked.

"Because you're going tomorrow night, or the night after, whichever the ice allows, and dead women make poor witnesses." Mrs. Tarnow said it the way she announced weather. "I've watched you carry this thing around like a stove burning inside your coat. I'm old. My husband's dead, my sons are scattered, and the worst thing Hale can do to me is stop paying me, which he already has. Silence stopped being an investment for me some time ago, girl. Now it's just a habit. Habits can be broken." She released the parcel and sat back. "Take it. Sew it wherever you sewed the rest."

* * *

For a while neither of them spoke. Edmund turned the parcel over. The oilcloth was stiff with age at the folds; the string was waxed; whoever had prepared it had done so carefully and, judging by the dust pressed into the cloth, done it years ago and kept it ready — a confession packed like provisions, waiting for a traveler.

"You knew what I was before tonight," Edmund said finally. "How?"

"Your hands." Mrs. Tarnow smiled faintly. "Men's hands change when they do fine work — they spread, they coarsen evenly. Yours are a woman's hands that have practiced being something else. Also you wound your watch at the same hour every night, always behind the same gesture of shielding it with your palm, which is a habit nobody keeps unless somebody once punished them for it. And you flinched the first time I called the upstairs lodgings 'the young gentleman's room.'" She shrugged. "I say nothing to anyone. What a person is, under the paint, is between them and God and their landlady."

Something in Edmund's chest unlocked — painfully, the way a frozen hinge gives. Three months of calculated solitude, of measuring every word, of eating breakfast in character, and it turned out she had been seen the entire time, catalogued kindly, and left undisturbed. It should have terrified her. Instead it felt like setting down a load she had forgotten she was carrying.

"The memorial," Edmund said. "You wore the shawl. You stood at the front."

"I stand at the front every year. Hale thinks it's respect." Her eyes were dry and bright. "It's accounting. I stand where I can see his face, and I remind myself what silence costs, and I decide again whether I can afford another year of it. This year I watched you standing at the back with your cap in your hands like everybody else, and I thought: there. There's the interest coming due on fifty crowns a quarter."

"My father—" Edmund stopped. Started again. "Did you know him well?"

"Well enough to lend him sugar and argue about the moon. He believed the tides were honest because the moon was honest, and money couldn't bribe either." Mrs. Tarnow's voice roughened, just slightly, the way rope frays before it parts. "He was wrong about the money part. He was right about the rest, eventually. Everything comes back around; it just comes back slowly." She stood, gathered the teapot, and paused with her hand on the handle. "One more thing, and then we both need to sleep, because tomorrow you have a harbor to cross and I have a town to lie to for the last time. The customs officer at the east house — Brandt. Your father pulled him out of the water when they were both young men, off the mole, drunk on festival wine. He's never forgotten it and never mentioned it. Men like that remember longest when they say least. If you get to his window, say the word 'mole.' Just that. He'll understand the rest."

She crossed to the door, then stopped, her back to Edmund, a small square woman in a beaded shawl with five years of bought silence finally spent.

"I'm not brave," Mrs. Tarnow said. "I want that understood. I waited until it cost me nothing. That's not courage, that's arithmetic." She opened the door onto the cold hallway. "You're the brave one. You're doing it while it costs everything. Try to survive the invoice."

Then she was gone down the stairs, and Edmund sat alone in the warm kitchen with the parcel in her lap and the taste of tea gone cold in her cup.

* * *

Upstairs, she did not sleep. She threaded a needle by lamplight and sewed the oilcloth packet into the lining of her coat beside the copied pages, feeling the coat grow heavier page by page until it weighed like armor or like ballast — she could not decide which, and supposed it did not matter, since she would need it to be both.

Through the window the harbor lay white and vast under a half moon, the ice road's dark line already traced by lanterns from yesterday's testing. Somewhere beyond the far shore lay the mainland, the railway, the guild hall, the ministry offices where a landlady's testimony could become a verdict. Tomorrow night, or the night after — whichever the ice allowed.

She tested each stitch, then bit the thread, then sat back and listened. Far below, in the sleeping town, a door closed softly — Mrs. Tarnow, checking her own latch for the last time as a woman who had chosen silence.

And then, closer, from the direction of the harbor, a sound Edmund knew the way one knows one's own pulse: the observatory clock striking the hour. Except the stroke came late — half a minute late, then corrected, then late again — and she realized, needle still in her hand, that someone was winding it tonight. Someone was climbing the observatory hill in the dark, in this cold, the way he had every night for five years.

Which meant Corven Hale was awake. And between her and the customs house lay his gate.
