## 15. Unmasked

The note came with the morning milk, folded into the wax paper like a knife wrapped in cloth.

*Master Ashgrove. The regulator in my study has stopped again. Come at your convenience. — C.H.*

At his convenience. Not hers. Edmund read it twice, standing in Mrs. Tarnow's narrow kitchen while the kettle muttered on the stove, and understood two things at once. First: there was nothing wrong with Hale's study clock. She had repaired it properly the first time, and she did not make mistakes. Second: he knew.

She had felt it building for days, the way you feel a spring tightening past its safe tension — the click of the escapement going wrong before the whole train locks. His visits to the observatory clock, nightly now instead of weekly. The way his eyes had found her across the memorial square. Piet's nervous chatter drying up whenever Hale passed the ferry office window. The town itself seemed to lean toward her, waiting.

"You've gone white as the milk," Mrs. Tarnow said, not looking up from her darning. "Sit down before you fall down."

"He wants me to come to his house."

"Then go." Mrs. Tarnow bit off her thread. "Refusing would be worse. A man who wants to talk doesn't want to be refused." She looked up then, and her eyes were the flat gray of the harbor in November. "Wear your coat. All the pockets."

Edmund did not ask what she meant. She went upstairs and took the copies from their hiding place beneath the floorboard — not all of them, only the three most damning pages, thin enough to sit flat against her ribs inside her shirt — and left the rest where they were, sewn into the quilt Mrs. Tarnow had never once mentioned. Then she checked herself in the glass the way she had checked herself every morning for eleven years: jaw bound, collar high, coat hanging square, hands still and steady. A young man. An apprentice. Nobody.

It was strange, how much effort it took to become nobody again when for so long it had taken no effort at all.

* * *

Hale's house stood above the harbor like a man who had climbed there to be seen and stayed because coming down meant questions. Edmund had been inside once before, for the study clock, and remembered the smell of it: pipe smoke, beeswax, and underneath everything, faint but permanent, the sea.

The housekeeper let her in without a word and disappeared. The corridor to the study was long and dark and hung with ship portraits, and Edmund walked it listening to the house the way she listened to a case she'd just opened — for the tick that didn't belong.

Hale was at his desk. He did not rise.

"Master Ashgrove." He gestured at the mantel clock, which was running perfectly. "You'll find it stopped."

"It isn't stopped."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

He let her stand there while he finished writing something, the pen scratching like a mouse in a wall. He was smaller than she remembered from the ferry dock, or perhaps she had grown larger. Five years of grief had carved him down to essential parts: the hands, the eyes, the voice. He wore no uniform today. Just a dark sweater with a darned elbow, and somehow that was worse — a man in mourning clothes pretending to be a man in plain clothes.

"Sit down, Ashgrove."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." He set down the pen. "Do you know how long I've worked this harbor?"

"I didn't come here to—"

"Twenty-two years," he said, as if she hadn't spoken. "I know every rock in Vell harbor by the sound the water makes against it. I know which fog means ice and which fog means drowning. And I have spent twenty-two years learning to read people the way you read a gauge — because in this town, the tide table is the truth, and men lie about everything else." He folded his hands. "So understand me when I tell you: I have known what you are since your ninth day in Vell."

The room did not change. The clock kept its perfect time. But Edmund felt something shift in her chest with an awful mechanical finality, a tooth slipping, eleven years of careful work releasing all at once.

"You're mistaken," she said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "I don't know what you think—"

"The way you carry weight," Hale said. "Men carry it in the shoulders. You carry it in the hips. The way you bind your boots — I noticed the second week, the wrapping under the laces. The way you listen." He leaned back. "And the way you looked at me at the memorial. Like a woman deciding whether to buy a knife."

Silence. Somewhere below, the frozen harbor groaned — that long tectonic complaint the ice made at dawn and dusk, the sound the whole town had learned to ignore.

"What do you want?" Edmund said.

"Now we're being honest." He rose, finally, and crossed to the window, and for a moment she saw him calculate the distance between them, the doors, the windows, the way any man does when he thinks about what happens next. Then he dismissed it. He didn't need it. That was the thing she hadn't understood until this moment: he had never needed force. He had the tide tables. He had the town. He had her secret, and her secret weighed more than she did.

"There's a boat out of Sornvik," Hale said, "that runs twice a month, winter schedule, papers be damned if you pay enough. The captain owes me. In four days it will carry whoever I say it carries." He turned around. "You could be on it. With your tools, your wages, and a letter of introduction to a guildmaster in Sornvik who asks no questions — I've written it already. It's in this drawer."

"And in exchange."

"You leave the papers. All of them. The copies, the originals, whatever you took from the observatory and whatever you found in this house." His voice did not rise. It lowered, if anything, until it was almost gentle, and the gentleness was the cruelest part. "You take the boat, you keep your name — *Ashgrove*, apprentice, guild member, a man, all of it, everything you built — and Vell keeps its peace. My peace."

"My father's name," Edmund said, "was Tomas Ashgrove."

Something crossed Hale's face. Not surprise. Confirmation. He nodded slowly, the way a man nods at a figure that matches his own arithmetic.

"Pilot of the *Maren*. Fifth of November, five years back." He said it without hesitation, and she understood that he had said those words to himself many times, in this room, probably at night, probably facing that window. "Yes. I thought it might be him. There aren't many reasons a clockmaker travels five hundred miles to service one clock in the season's last week."

"The clock needed servicing."

"The clock needed servicing," Hale agreed, "because I broke it. Because I put those ledgers where only spring and a thorough cleaning would find them, and I prayed — and I'm not a praying man, Ashgrove, but I prayed — that they'd rot before anyone read them." He came back toward the desk but did not sit. "So. Now we both know everything. Let me tell you what happens if you refuse, and then let me tell you why you'll accept."

He told her what happens first. It was clean, professional, and terrible. A magistrate who owed him. A charge of theft — *a thief of harbor papers*, the phrase already circulating, already doing its work. And then, laid on top like a second hull breach: the unmasking. An apprentice clockmaker revealed as a woman, fraud upon the guild, false registration, impersonation. "They don't hang women for passing," Hale said. "They do worse. They erase them. Every job you ever held becomes suspect. Every gear you ever cut gets reattributed. Your guild card burns, and with it the eleven years you traded your whole life for. You won't be punished, Ashgrove. You'll be *deleted*. There will be no record anywhere that you existed at all."

He paused there, watching her take the weight of it, and Edmund hated him for the accuracy. Because it was true. She had known since she was fourteen years old, since the first time she cut her hair and lowered her voice and practiced walking with her heels down, that the life she was building had no floor beneath it. One pull, and everything fell. She had simply arranged her days so she never looked down. He was making her look down.

"And now," Hale said, "why you'll accept."

He opened the drawer and took out the letter of introduction, and set it on the desk, and beside it — her breath stopped — her father's pocket watch.

She had seen it last the night before the *Maren* sailed, when he'd wound it at the kitchen table and said, *time you can carry is time they can't take from you.* She had been twelve. After the wreck, the harbor board had returned his effects to the family in a sealed box, and the box had contained his coat, his knife, his gloves — and no watch. Her mother had written to the board three times. The answers had grown shorter and then stopped. For five years Edmund had assumed it was lost, or stolen, or at the bottom of the harbor, and she had grieved it as a small thing beside the large thing, the way you grieve a button beside a body.

And here it was. In Corven Hale's desk. In Corven Hale's *house*, where she had stood a month ago among his shrine of drowned men's effects and felt pity move through her horror like warm water through ice — and had not looked closely at the watch on the second shelf, because she had never in her life imagined she would see it again.

"You took it off him," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "He was alive in the water. Wasn't he. Long enough."

Hale closed his eyes. Just for a second. "The ledgers were falsified," he said. "The tide readings, yes. Mine. To cover the *Karoline*'s late berth — I moved the pilot channel markers to save my own commission and it cost the *Maren* her line into the channel, and by morning four men were dead, and I falsified the records rather than lose everything, and I have regretted it every single hour since, and none of it — *none of it* — changes what happens to you if you refuse." He opened his eyes. "The watch goes with you. The letter goes with you. Your name, your trade, your future, intact. That is the offer. It expires when the ice road opens, because after that I can no longer protect you from what this town will do, and I am telling you plainly: I no longer wish to be the man who ruined a second Ashgrove."

Edmund looked at the watch. Gold case, worn to brass at one edge where his thumb had rubbed it for thirty years. Time you can carry.

That was the horror of it — the real horror, deeper than the threat. Because he was right about the shape of her life. She had spent eleven years buying her future with her past, trading pieces of herself away one at a time — her hair, her name, her voice, the right to be touched, the right to grieve openly, the right to exist on paper as what she was — and she had told herself it was strength, and it was, but it was also a ledger of debts, and Hale had simply read it aloud. He was offering to forgive the entire balance. Walk away. Keep the disguise that had kept her alive. Be Master Ashgrove, safe, employed, anonymous, forever. Let the dead stay dead. Let the record stay false. Let Vell keep its peace.

Let her father have died a drunk pilot's error, which is what the official finding said, which is what her mother had died believing, which is what her brother in the interior still believed, and what difference did it make now, truly, five years on, to anyone but her?

To everyone, said something in her chest, in the cold exact center of her, the part of her that heard a clock run wrong from three rooms away and could not rest until it was right. To everyone, forever. That's what a false reading is. It doesn't just mislead you tonight. It misleads every soul who consults it after.

"You knew since my ninth day," Edmund said quietly.

"Yes."

"And you said nothing for two months. You let me walk your streets, fix your clocks, eat in your town. Why?"

Hale was silent for a moment. When he answered, his voice had a crack in it, hairline, the kind you only hear if you repair clocks.

"Because you were the first honest thing to happen to me in five years," he said. "And I didn't want to know what you'd come to do. I wanted another month of not knowing."

Edmund picked up the watch. It was heavier than she remembered. Or she was stronger.

She put it in her pocket — her pocket, over her heart, where he used to carry it — and looked at Corven Hale across the desk, and saw him understand, in the slow way of a man watching a tide turn, that the answer was no. That it had always been no. That he had offered her erasure dressed as mercy, and she had recognized the stitching.

"My father carried this watch into the water," she said. "I'm going to carry his name onto the ice. You can decide, between now and then, which side of it you want to be found on."

She walked the long dark corridor back toward the door, and behind her, in the study, the mantel clock ticked on, keeping perfect time, and she could have sworn she heard it — faintly, under the tick — the sound of a man beginning, at last, to wind something other than his own silence.

Outside, the light was failing early, the way it did now, and down at the harbor mouth the ice had gone quiet — that particular held-breath stillness Mrs. Tarnow called *the night before the road*.

Tomorrow, or the day after, the crossings would begin. And someone in Vell, Edmund knew with a certainty colder than the harbor, had already sent word about a thief of harbor papers.

She had less time than Hale thought.

And now she also had a witness she hadn't counted on — because when she reached Mrs. Tarnow's gate, the old woman was sitting on the step in the dark, fully dressed, a packed carpetbag at her feet.

"I saw the Wrenwreck from my window," Mrs. Tarnow said. "Get inside. We need to talk before you do anything brave."
