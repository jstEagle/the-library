## 17. Betrayal at the Gate

The knock came at the ferry office an hour before dawn, and Edmund knew before Piet opened the door that something had broken.

She wasn't there to see it. She was across town in her room above Mrs. Tarnow's house, lying dressed on top of the blankets with her coat over her feet, listening to the wind worry the shutters. But she knew it the way a clockmaker knows a wheel has slipped its tooth — not by seeing the failure, but by hearing the tick go wrong.

She had shown him one page. One page, three weeks ago, in the back of the ferry office while the stove ticked and Piet polished the counter like it was an altar. She had watched his faith crack along its grain, arithmetic doing what argument never could, and she had told herself: this is not betrayal of him, this is rescue of him. He deserves to know what he worships.

She had also known, in the same breath, that a boy with a cracked faith does not stay quiet. A boy with a cracked faith goes looking for someone to mend it.

* * *

It was Mrs. Tarnow who brought the news, coming up the stairs faster than her hips allowed, her knuckles rapping the door frame twice — their signal, developed without discussion, that conversation was needed and could not wait for politeness.

"There's men at the harbor office," she said when Edmund opened the door. "Hale's men. They've pulled Piet off the ticket window and they're going through the ledgers there. The real ones." Her eyes went to the quilt folded at the foot of the bed. "And there's a sledge at Hale's gate with a stove on it. The kind you burn things in."

Edmund's hands were already moving — checking the seams of her coat where the copies lay flat against the lining, twelve pages sewn in with waxed thread, thin enough to pass a patting search, thick enough that a needle would find them.

"How do you know all this?"

"Grete Voss's boy works the fish dock. He sees everything and tells me half." Mrs. Tarnow stepped inside and shut the door behind her, which she never did. In the closed room she looked smaller and older and entirely steady. "What did you show the boy?"

"One page."

"Which page?"

"The tide table from the night of the Wrenwreck. The one where his own handwriting contradicts the official reading by nine minutes."

Mrs. Tarnow sat down on the edge of the bed as if her legs had filed a formal complaint. "Nine minutes," she repeated. "That's all it took?"

"Nine minutes is the difference between safe water and the Teeth. My father died of nine minutes."

The old woman nodded slowly, working something through. Then: "He didn't give you up."

"What?"

"Piet. If he'd given you up, they wouldn't be searching ledgers. They'd be here." She tipped her head toward the stairs, toward the town, toward the net closing everywhere but this one room. "He told them there were copied pages. He didn't tell them whose hand made them. That's what I heard, anyway, from a woman who hears things."

Edmund stood very still. Outside, the wind shifted and the harbor ice groaned — a long, deep note, like a bow drawn across the lowest string of an enormous instrument. Three nights now it had been making that sound. The ice road was forming. Everyone in Vell could feel it the way farmers feel rain in a knee.

"He's protecting me," she said. It came out flat, factual, and yet it landed in her chest like a dropped weight.

"Or he's protecting himself from knowing what he's done," Mrs. Tarnow said. "Boys that age can hold both. It's grown men who have to choose." She stood, took Edmund's chin in a dry, firm hand, and turned her face to the gray window light. "You look terrible."

"I haven't been sleeping."

"No one in this town has, since the ice started talking." She released her. "How long do you have?"

"Hale said end of season for the burning. But if he knows copies exist—" Edmund did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do. The search would take a day, maybe two, to move through the harbor offices. Then it would come to the lodgings. Then it would come to her, because a stranger in a small town is always the first suspect and the last one forgiven. "Tonight," she said. "It has to be tonight."

"The road won't bear weight until—"

"It doesn't have to bear a sledge. It has to bear me." She met Mrs. Tarnow's eyes. "One person, walking fast, is lighter than anything they'll send after me. And no one travels the ice at night except fools and fugitives, and I intend to be both."

* * *

She spent the morning doing the thing that kept her sane, which was work. There was a mantel clock in her room she'd promised to Mrs. Tarnow, and she sat with its small heart open in her hands, adjusting the escapement by feel because her eyes kept going to the window, to the harbor, to the dark line of Hale's warehouse where men moved like ants disturbing a nest.

The escapement, she thought. The part of a clock that lets the power out a little at a time so it doesn't tear itself apart. Every clock she'd ever built or mended had one. People thought the spring was the heart, but the spring was only rage; the escapement was the discipline that turned rage into time.

For five years her life had been an escapement. Let nothing out too fast. Release exactly as much as can be borne. Tick, and tick, and tick, and never once let the whole force of what you are and what you know escape at once, because it would destroy the mechanism entire.

Tonight she was going to break her own escapement deliberately. Let the whole spring go at once. She found she could not tell if she was terrified or relieved, and suspected the two had become indistinguishable.

Mrs. Tarnow sat across from her, sewing in steady strokes — not the quilt with the copies hidden in it, that quilt was staying behind, a decoy full of harmless household linen, but a second coat, heavier, which she was quietly transforming: doubling the inner lining, adding pockets that buttoned, reinforcing the shoulders.

"If they stop you on the road," Mrs. Tarnow said, not looking up, "you're a clockmaker called to Emerdale on an emergency. I'll write you a letter. Grete Voss's cousin's wife has a sick clock in Emerdale, or will have by tonight, God forgive me."

"You'd lie for me in writing."

"I've lied for worse men for money." The needle flashed. "Lying for a friend is a promotion."

Edmund set down her tweezers. "Why? Truly. You were paid to be silent about the wreck. You've spent five years being silent. Why break it for me?"

Mrs. Tarnow's needle paused. For a moment she seemed to look through the wall, through the town, out at the black water under the white ice.

"Because silence is a debt," she said finally, "and debts collect interest. I took coin to watch those men drown and say nothing, and every year at the memorial I stand in the square and listen to Corven Hale talk about tragedy and fate, and the interest compounds." She resumed sewing. "You're the first chance I've had to pay any of it down. Don't thank me. It's selfishness wearing a shawl."

* * *

Dusk came early, the way it did in December, and with it came Piet.

She saw him from the window first — crossing the square with his collar up, stopping twice as though reconsidering, then turning down her street with the doomed resolution of a man walking to his own sentencing. She was downstairs and opening the door before he knocked, because whatever this was, she would not have him saying it on the doorstep where the whole street could hear.

"Piet."

"Miss—" He caught himself. He was the only soul in Vell who sometimes slipped like that, and she had never corrected him, and he had never explained it, and between them the slip hung like a small unclaimed package. His face was terrible. Fifteen years old and his face was the face of a man who had done something irreversible. "I need to talk to you. Not out here."

Upstairs, Mrs. Tarnow poured him tea he didn't touch. He sat on the very edge of the chair with his cap in his hands, turning it, turning it.

"They came to the office this morning," he said. "Hale himself. Not the clerks — him. He asked me about papers. Copied papers. Someone told him the master clock's old ledgers had been handled, pages out of order, and that copies might exist." His knuckles were white around the cap's brim. "And I told him it was true. I told him I'd seen a copy. One page. The tide table from the Wrenwreck night."

The room held still. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Why?" Edmund asked, gently, because the answer mattered more than the accusation.

"Because it's true!" He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "Because I checked it, like you showed me, four times, five times, and it's true, and he lied, and my father worked the docks his whole life and knew two of the men on the *Wren*, and I thought — I thought if I gave him the page, if I admitted knowing about it, he'd think that was all there was. One page. A curiosity. He'd stop looking and I'd have warned—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I'd have warned whoever made the copy. Because they'd hear the search had narrowed and they'd run, and running proves guilt, but it also keeps them alive, and I thought — I thought alive and guilty was better than dead and honest, which is what happened to the pilots."

Mrs. Tarnow set down her cup with a click like a small gear engaging.

"So you sold one truth to buy another," Edmund said.

Piet flinched. "I know what I am. I'm not asking you to call it anything else." He straightened, and for a second she saw the man he would become layered over the boy he was. "Here's what you need. The search moves to the lodgings tomorrow. Hale's ordered every boarding house turned out starting at first light, yours first because you're the stranger. And the ice — I checked the markers at noon. The road's flagged as far as the middle marker. It'll bear a walker by midnight, maybe sooner, but the customs house checkpoint on the far shore isn't manned after ten. The officer goes to his supper and doesn't come back till dawn. Ten to dawn, the window's open." He stood, put on his cap, and his voice cracked on the last of it. "I'm on gate duty at the office tonight until midnight. I see nothing. I hear nothing. Whatever walks past the ferry office on the ice after ten, I was looking at my ledger."

"Piet—"

"Don't." He raised a hand, palm out, like a man holding back a tide. "Don't thank me and don't forgive me, either one. Both are more than I've earned." At the door he paused, back still turned. "He was a good man once. Hale. My father said so. Maybe he still is, somewhere under everything. But good men who drown other men don't get to decide who tells it." He left, and his boots went down the stairs slower than they'd come up.

* * *

They packed by lamplight, in silence, in the shorthand of people who have already agreed on everything. The originals — the true ledger from Hale's study, the observatory pages, Mrs. Tarnow's written testimony folded in oilcloth — went into the coat lining, redistributed across the new doubled seams so no single bulge would tell a story. The copies stayed in the quilt, laid flat, discoverable, damning enough to occupy Hale for days while she crossed the ice and beyond.

Edmund packed her tools last. The small hammer. The eye glass. The tweezers worn to the shape of her fingers. A screwdriver her father had ground down himself from a file, back when she was small enough to sit on his bench and watch, back when he'd taught her that a clock is honest in a way people aren't — that it cannot help telling the truth about its own works, if you only know how to open the case.

She wrapped them in flannel and left room in the bag for nothing else. Everything else could be carried in the place where things couldn't be confiscated.

At the window, the harbor lay black-and-white under a half moon, and the ice spoke in its slow language. Groan, and settle. Groan, and shift. Somewhere out there, under forty feet of frozen sea, lay the *Wren*, and her father, and five years of a lie wearing a town like a coat.

"Ten o'clock," Mrs. Tarnow said. "Then you go. Not before — the ferry office clerk changes shift at ten, and the new one's deaf as a post and hates the cold." She pressed the letter into Edmund's glove: *To whom it may concern, the bearer is a clockmaker of good standing called away on urgent business...* forged in a hand so ordinary it was invisible. "You'll walk to the middle marker and then keep the flag line on your left shoulder. If the ice booms under you, keep moving — it's settling, not breaking. If it cracks sharp and high, drop flat and crawl. And Edmund—" 

It was the first time she'd used the name whole, unguarded, in front of anyone. Neither of them remarked on it.

"When you reach the mainland," Mrs. Tarnow said, "when they ask you who sent these papers, who carried them, who lost everything to deliver them — you tell them your name. Your true one. Not for the record. For you. Names are the only inheritance some of us get, and yours has been sitting in a vault five years gathering interest." She smiled, thin and fierce. "Go collect it."

Edmund embraced her — quickly, hard, the way you embrace someone when embracing might undo you — and then the lamp was out, and the stair was dark, and the door of the house closed behind her for the last time.

The cold hit like a fact. The moon stood low over the harbor. Half past nine.

Across the black water, at the far edge of the ice, the lantern of the customs house checkpoint burned small and patient, and beyond it, thirty miles of frozen dark between her and the truth getting out alive.

She stepped off the cobbles and onto the sea, and the sea held.

Behind her, in the sleeping town, a bell tolled ten — and somewhere near the harbor master's warehouse, a second lantern came alight and began, unhurried, to move toward the shore.
