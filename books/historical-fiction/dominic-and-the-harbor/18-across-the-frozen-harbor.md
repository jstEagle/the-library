## 18. Across the Frozen Harbor

The knock came at half past eleven.

Edmund had been dressed for an hour, sitting on the edge of the bed with her boots in her lap, listening to the wind work at the shutters. She had planned to leave at two, when the town's lamps burned low and even the harbor master's watchmen dozed at their posts. Piet's betrayal had bought her hours, not days — Hale would move by morning, and the ice road would not officially open for three more nights. Which meant she had to take it unofficially, alone, in the dark, before anyone thought to guard it.

But the knock came at half past eleven, three soft raps from Mrs. Tarnow, and Edmund knew before she opened the door that the schedule was dead.

"They're at the ferry office," Mrs. Tarnow said without stepping inside. "Four men with lanterns. Asking after a thief of harbor papers again, but this time they have a description. Young man, slight build, foreign hands." Her mouth thinned. "That's you, girl. That description has always been you."

"Which gate are they holding?"

"Hale's own gate is manned. The customs house too, I'd wager — they'll assume you'll wait for the official opening like a sensible person. Nobody crosses early. Nobody crosses alone."

"Then that's exactly what I'll do."

Mrs. Tarnow looked at her for a long moment. The hallway lamp behind her made a halo of her gray hair, and Edmund thought, absurdly, of the saints in her mother's old prayer book, all of them rendered in cheap ink with their halos printed slightly off-center. She thought about how she had never prayed in her life and how she wanted someone to pray for her now.

"The quilt," Mrs. Tarnow said. "You're wearing it?"

"Sewn into the lining of my coat. All of it. The ledgers, the copies, Tarnow's testimony." She touched the left side of her chest, where the papers lay flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Every few minutes since sundown she had checked them, as if they might dissolve, as if the coat might simply refuse to carry them any further.

"Then you're carrying more than papers," Mrs. Tarnow said. "You're carrying me. Don't you dare drop me through the ice."

"I won't."

"Go out the scullery window. The lane behind runs down to the boat sheds, and the sheds are empty this time of year. From there it's fifty yards of shingle to where the ice starts solid." Mrs. Tarnow pressed something into her hand — a small flask, cold and heavy. "Brandy. For after, if there is an after. And Edmund—" she paused, and for once did not look away or soften whatever came next, "—if the ice takes you, those papers go down with you, and everyone in this town keeps believing a comfortable lie until they die of it. So don't let the ice take you."

"No," Edmund said. "I won't let it."

It was the easiest promise she had ever made and the hardest one she could imagine keeping.

* * *

The harbor at midnight was not a harbor. It was a country.

She had known, abstractly, that Vell's bay froze over every winter — she had heard the numbers often enough these past weeks, the thickness of the ice measured in fingers and hands and arm-spans, the ritual by which the harbormaster declared the road open each year. But knowing a thing in figures was different from standing at the edge of it in the dark. The frozen bay stretched away from her toward the far shore, where a single light burned in the customs house window, two miles distant across a plain of black glass. There was no path. There were no markers yet; Hale's men wouldn't stake the official route until the road opened. She would have to choose her own line across and trust the arithmetic of cold.

Above the town the observatory stood against the stars, and she could just make out its clock face, dimly luminous, keeping its repaired and faithful time. She thought of the escapement under her hands, the neat teeth of the escape wheel releasing one instant at a time. Time didn't flow. It fell, tick by tick, and you either spent it or it spilled away unused. Two miles of ice. One hour, maybe less, if the surface held.

Behind her, up in the town, a lantern swung along the lane above the boat sheds.

She went down the shingle on all fours, letting the stones take her weight, and stepped onto the ice at the point where the moored dories stood locked in it like flies in amber. The first ten yards were easy — thick, snow-dusted, groaning only the deep slow sounds of settling ice that meant nothing and lulled you into thinking everything meant nothing. Then the snow thinned and the surface turned glassy beneath it, and she could see down into the dark water through her own reflection, and her body understood before her mind did what she had actually undertaken.

Her father had drowned within sight of this shore.

She made herself walk.

* * *

The ice talked the whole way.

That was the thing no one had warned her about — not Piet, with his secondhand stories of the ice road festivals; not Mrs. Tarnow, who confessed she hadn't been past the end of her street in nine winters. The ice *talked*. It boomed like a struck beam, a sound so deep she felt it in her back teeth, and it cracked in long shrieking zips that ran from one horizon to the other, and it whispered, everywhere, constantly, a dry fibrous creaking like rope under strain or an old man turning in his sleep. Some of the sounds meant the ice was thickening. Some meant it was failing. There was no way to tell which was which, and after twenty minutes Edmund stopped trying, because trying was costing her her balance and her breath, and both mattered more than knowledge.

A quarter mile out, the snow vanished entirely and the wind found her. It came down the bay from the north with nothing between her and the open sea to blunt it, and it pushed at her like a large indifferent hand. She leaned into it and shortened her stride, the way you climb a hill, and kept her eyes on the customs house light, which bobbed and swam in the wind-tears and refused to come closer at any rate she could measure.

The papers shifted against her ribs with every step. She had sewn them in herself that afternoon, in Mrs. Tarnow's kitchen, using the landlady's good scissors and a sailmaker's stitch learned from watching harbor men mend their nets. Three layers of oilcloth, then the documents rolled flat, then another layer of oilcloth, then the quilted lining stitched back down over the whole of it. If she went into the water, the bundle might buy her a minute of buoyancy. It might also drag her under like an anchor. She had decided, kneeling on Mrs. Tarnow's floor with the needle in her hand, that she did not care which. There were things worth drowning with.

Halfway across, the ice began to change underfoot.

She felt it first as a softness, a faint give in each footfall, like walking on a frozen river in March instead of January. Then she crossed a pressure ridge — a seam where two vast plates had shouldered against each other and reared up in a waist-high wall of shattered slabs, blue-white in the starlight, razor-edged. She climbed it slowly, distributing her weight, and from its crest she saw the whole route ahead lit by nothing but sky, and saw, a hundred yards on, the dark line of open water.

Not open water. A lead — a crack in the bay's skin, running diagonally across her route, thirty feet wide, its edges steaming faintly in the cold. The black surface of it moved. Slowly, silently, plates of thin new ice ground together and separated, ground and separated, breathing with some tide she couldn't feel.

Edmund stood on the pressure ridge with the wind tearing at her collar and understood that the bay was not a road. It was a living thing doing what living things do — shifting, healing, breaking — and she had crossed onto it in the dark believing it would hold still for her.

For a moment — one whole, complete moment — she considered going back.

She let herself have the moment honestly. She stood there and imagined the boat sheds, the warm scullery window, Mrs. Tarnow's kettle, the shop in some other town under some other name, the long safe gray years of being nobody to anybody. She let the wanting have its full weight. And then she thought of her father's pocket watch lying in Corven Hale's shrine like a trophy, and of Piet's face when the arithmetic broke his faith in front of her eyes, and of Mrs. Tarnow saying *you're carrying me*, and the moment closed like a ledger.

She skirted the lead northward, walking parallel to its grinding edge, testing every step with her heel before committing her weight. Twice the ice spoke directly beneath her — a sharp crack like a pistol — and twice she stood absolutely still, arms out, breath stopped, until the sound faded into the general conversation of the bay and nothing opened under her boots. The third time, something did open. Not beneath her. Ten feet to her right, a fissure unzipped with a shriek and stopped, and she stood looking at a black seam six inches wide and knew with perfect clarity that it had chosen, arbitrarily, to stop six inches wide, and that her survival tonight had very little to do with her.

Keep moving. That was the only prayer available. Keep moving.

* * *

She heard the dogs first.

Or not dogs — she couldn't name it, a high thin carrying sound from the direction of the town, and she turned, and saw lights. Three lanterns strung out along the shore near the boat sheds, moving, and then a fourth already out on the ice, farther than the others, coming on fast. Sledge runners. Someone had brought a sledge, which meant someone believed the ice would bear weight at speed, which meant somewhere between her and the shore the route she had guessed at had been tested by men who knew this bay, and they were following her tracks in the snow-dusted stretches with the simple efficiency of hounds on a line.

They had found the scullery window. Or Mrs. Tarnow had lied beautifully and been believed anyway. It didn't matter now. What mattered was arithmetic: they had runners and fresh legs and lanterns, and she had two miles of bad ice and a coat full of paper.

Edmund ran.

It was not running as she understood the word. It was a lurching, sliding, weight-hating scramble, boots skidding on black glass, lungs seizing in the cold until each breath came in like a knife. The wind, which had fought her all the way out, now worked for her — at her back, shoving her toward the far shore, toward the customs house light that grew with horrible slowness, a hundred yards at a time. Behind her the runner-sound sharpened. Whoever drove that sledge knew what he was doing; he took the pressure ridges at angles she wouldn't have dared at a walk, and he was gaining.

The lead. The lead was still ahead of her, and it would be wider now — she could hear it breathing, a rhythmic crunch-and-hiss, and she angled toward its edge and ran along it and the sledge behind her gained and gained, and she understood with a cold, clear, almost calm part of her mind that she was going to reach the lead with the sledge forty feet behind her, and that the man driving it would watch her try to cross thirty feet of broken moving ice at night, and that he would not need to catch her at all. He would only need to wait.

The lead bent. She followed it around a shallow curve and saw, ahead, where the young ice had bridged most of its width overnight — a broad pane of gray, glassy-new ice stretching from shore to shore of the crack, trembling visibly when the wind pressed it. New ice. Hours old. An inch, maybe two, maybe less in the middle where the current ran.

Behind her, a voice — Hale's voice, carried impossibly far over the ice, stripped by distance of everything but its shape: *"Stop."*

She stepped onto the new ice.

It sang under her boots — a high, taut, terrible ringing, the sound ice makes when it holds by bending, and she spread her arms and kept her steps short and even and did not look down at the black water flowing an inch beneath her soles, and the singing rose around her with every step, and halfway across the whole pane flexed like a plank, a slow wave rolling from her front foot to her back, and somewhere behind her the sledge stopped, its driver unwilling, and Edmund walked alone onto the middle of a bridge that existed only because the night was cold enough and the sea was patient enough and she had not yet been permitted to drown.

Twenty steps. Fifteen. The singing deepened. Ten.

Her boot went through.

Not her whole weight — the ice cupped her leg to the knee and held, screaming, and the shock of the cold went up her thigh like a brand, and for one full second she hung there balanced on one leg over open black water with the papers pressing against her ribs, and she heard herself make a sound that was not a word. Then she threw her weight backward, onto sound ice, and dragged her leg out — one long tearing pull, the water giving her up grudgingly, her trouser leg instantly stiffening into armor of frost — and she stood gasping on the far edge of the lead with her heart slamming and the customs house light close enough now to see the shape of the building around it.

Behind her, across the singing bridge, the lanterns bunched at the lead's near shore. Shouts, thin with distance. No one else willing to cross.

Edmund laughed. It came out of her cracked and sobbing and it was the best sound she had ever made.

She covered the last quarter mile at a staggering trot, wet leg freezing solid, and pulled up at last under the Customs house eaves, where the door was already opening, spilling yellow lamplight and woodsmoke warmth into the night. A tall figure filled the doorway in a greatcoat, holding a lamp up, squinting against the dark at the wild-haired, frost-rimed creature staggering out of the bay.

"In God's name," the man said slowly. "Who—" And then the lamp tilted, and his face changed, and Edmund saw him recognize not her but something older: the set of her jaw, perhaps, or the way she planted her feet. She had heard of this man. Gerrit Vos, customs officer, twenty-five years at his post. Her father had drunk with him. Her father had trusted him.

"Master Vos," she said, and her voice came out ragged and steady at once. "My name is Edmund Ashgrove. My father was Tomas Ashgrove, pilot of the *Wren*, and I am carrying five years of stolen truth sewn into this coat — and there are men on that ice behind me who mean to take it off my body before sunrise."

She pulled the coat open herself, before they could decide to do it for her, and stood in the lamplight with the papers gleaming in their oilcloth against her heart.

Vos looked at the papers. Then at her face. Then out at the dark harbor, where four lanterns had gone very still at the edge of the open water.

"Get inside," he said, and shut the door, and shot the bolt.
