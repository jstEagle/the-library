## 19. Checkpoint One: Customs

The ice did not want her on it.

She had known that from the first step out of Mrs. Tarnow's back door, when the harbor groaned beneath her like a cathedral settling its bones. The sound was not the sound of solid things. It was the sound of a decision being reconsidered all night long — the sea underneath deciding, over and over, whether to keep holding her up.

Edmund walked anyway.

The papers rode against her ribs, sewn into the lining of her coat in three flat packets: the copied ledgers in Mrs. Tarnow's quilt-stitch, the original true ledger folded into oilcloth, and — pressed flat between cardboard she'd cut from a clock-crate — the final testimony, signed, that Mrs. Tarnow had written by lamplight with a hand that only shook twice. All of it together weighed less than a brass movement. Less than the smallest striking train. She kept telling herself that. She kept feeling it grow heavier with every hundred yards of black ice.

Behind her, Vell burned yellow at the windows. Behind her — she would not look again, she had promised herself, and then looked anyway — lanterns moved along the shore road. Not running yet. Searching. There was a difference, and the difference was time, and time was the only material she had ever worked in.

The customs house sat at the harbor's far jaw, where the channel narrowed toward the mainland. In summer it was a low stone building with a flagpole and a bell. In winter, when the ice road opened, it became a checkpoint: two men, a stove, a table, a barrier of tarred rope strung between iron posts across the lane of marked ice. Beyond it, four miles of frozen channel. Beyond that, the mainland, and the magistrate who could compel a ledger to speak.

Four miles. One checkpoint. Then the ferry office shed at the mid-channel mark. Then Hale's gate on the mainland spit, because of course he would keep his own gate; a man who falsified tides would not leave the last door to strangers.

One thing at a time. That was the whole of clockmaking, and she had made it the whole of her life. One wheel at a time. Do not look at the escapement while setting the barrel.

* * *

She heard the customs house before she saw it — the stove's iron tick, a man coughing, the wet slap of coffee in a tin cup. Then the lantern came up off its hook and swung its light across the ice, and she walked into it rather than away, because running was the one confession they would understand instantly.

"Hold there." A voice like gravel poured slowly. "Ice road's closed till first light. Nobody crosses after dark."

"I need to cross tonight."

"Everyone needs to cross tonight." The lantern steadied, and behind its glare she made out the shape of him: broad, gray-bearded, wrapped in a coat older than she was. He came forward onto the ice itself, boots crackling, which meant he trusted it, which meant the cold snap had done its work. "Turn back to town, lad. Whatever it is keeps till morning."

"It doesn't keep."

"They all say that."

He was ten feet away now, and the lantern light fell full on her face, and Edmund watched his own expression do something strange. It went from boredom to attention to a kind of baffled recognition, as if he were meeting someone he knew in a place the person had no right to be.

"Ashgrove," he said slowly. "Ashgrove the clockmaker. From the *Wren*. You'll be — the son, then."

Here it was, then. Standing on black ice at midnight with the truth sewn into her coat, she found the moment arrived not as catastrophe but as a door she had been carrying on her back for fifteen years, waiting for a wall to set it in.

"May I give you my name properly?" she said.

The old man frowned. "Properly?"

"Before you search me — and you're going to search me, I can see it in how you're standing — I'd rather say it myself. What I am and what I'm carrying. Because if you find it, you'll have to decide what it means, and I'd rather you decided knowing I told you freely."

The second man had come out of the customs house now, younger, heavyset, a truncheon hanging easy from his belt. He glanced at his senior. "Marek, what's this?"

"Quiet a moment, Joss." Marek set the lantern down on the ice between them, so its light climbed all three of them equally. His eyes had gone careful. Twenty-five years of checking manifests teaches a man when cargo is about to declare itself. "Say it, then."

Edmund opened her coat.

Not fast — nothing sudden, nothing that could be read as a weapon — but fully, both hands drawing the sides wide, turning slightly so the lantern light could find every seam. The lining gaped where she'd cut the stitches that afternoon and resewn them with long, pullable basting. The paper showed white against the dark wool. Oilcloth showed yellow.

"There are documents in this coat," she said. Her voice carried strangely over the ice; she heard it go out flat and hard toward the mainland like a thrown stone. "True tide ledgers from the night of the Wrenwreck. The originals, and copies, and a written account from a witness who saw the pilot lights moved. They prove the official ledgers were altered. They prove the *Wren* was lured onto the Widow's Teeth by a false safe-channel signal."

Joss took one step and stopped, looking at Marek, because everyone in Vell knew what the Widow's Teeth had eaten, and everyone knew whose job it had been to keep those lights honest.

"And my name," Edmund said, before they could ask, before the searching hands could take even this from her, "is Edmund Ashgrove. My father was Tomas Ashgrove, pilot of the *Wren*, drowned the ninth of December, five years ago. And I am his daughter."

The words went out of her like air out of a wound.

For fifteen years she had said a false name into a thousand ears — *apprentice, yes, master's son, yes, Edmund Ashgrove, junior, yes* — and every saying had been a small death she'd survived by not noticing. She had thought that speaking the true name aloud would feel like losing everything. Instead it felt like setting down a weight she had forgotten she was carrying, and finding her arms still worked.

"I've been passing as a man since I was twelve years old," she said, quieter now, because the worst was out and the rest was only detail, "because the guild takes no women, and the trade was mine, and my father taught me gears before he taught me letters. I came to Vell to repair your observatory clock. While inside it I found what someone had hidden there. That is all of it. Search me. Read them. But know that I gave them to you before you asked."

The ice groaned, long and low, underlining everything.

Joss had gone pale. Marek had not moved at all. He stood with his hands at his sides and looked at her for a long time, and Edmund looked back, and in that looking something passed between them that had nothing to do with checkpoints or papers.

"You knew him," she said.

"I crewed with Tomas Ashgrove nine winters," Marek said. "He talked me out of the water once off Gull Point when I was green and stupid, and he never once let me forget it." He bent, slowly, and picked up the lantern. When he straightened, his face was doing what faces in Vell did — grieving in public with the grief turned down to a manageable level. "He had a little girl. Used to sit on his knee in the pilot house and take apart the compass binnacle with her fingers."

"She still takes things apart," Edmund said. "It's the putting back together that took practice."

Joss made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by circumstance.

Marek didn't laugh. He came closer, close enough that she could see the frost in his eyebrows, and held the lantern up beside her face like a man verifying a coin.

"The guild papers you showed at the ferry office," he said. "Those said a nephew. An heir."

"Forged well enough to pass a clerk who wanted his dinner," Edmund said. "I won't insult you by claiming better."

"And Corven Hale knows? Knows what you are?"

"He has known since my first week. He told me so himself, two nights ago. He offered me passage out if I left the papers on his table." She held his eyes. "I refused. And he did not report me, which should tell you something about what he fears more than my disguise. And Piet — the boy at the ferry office — went to him tonight with word that pages were moving. Not my name. Not yet. But the search will narrow by morning, and the records burn at season's end regardless. That is why it's tonight. That is why it cannot keep."

Marek turned the lantern down. It was an odd gesture, deliberate, dimming the light on her as though to say: *I see enough.*

"Joss," he said. "Go stoke the stove. Make coffee you can actually drink."

"Marek—"

"Stove. Coffee. Now."

The young man hesitated, looking at Edmund with an expression she couldn't fully read — half suspicion, half something almost like awe — and went. The door of the customs house banged. Their footsteps faded into the tick of the stove.

Then it was just the two of them and the lantern and the breathing ice, and the four dark miles beyond.

"If I let you pass," Marek said slowly, "I hang for it. Letting papers through a checkpoint without inspection — that's the least of it. Consorting. If Hale comes asking who saw what, I'm finished in Vell. Pension, name, all of it." He rubbed his beard. "If I stop you, I'm the man who handed Tomas Ashgrove's daughter back to the man who killed him, on the word of a boy and a rumor. Also finished. Just finished cleaner."

"I wouldn't ask you to choose blind," Edmund said. "Read them. That's all I ask. You were a seaman before you were a clerk — read the tides like a seaman. If they lie, arrest me now and I'll say nothing in my defense, because I'd deserve it for forging them badly."

That almost got the laugh out of him. Almost.

He took the oilcloth packet when she offered it, and for a moment his hands — big, cracked, certain on ropes and stamps and manifest pens — fumbled with paper the way hers never fumbled with brass. He angled the lantern. He read.

Edmund stood in the dark and let him.

She had spent five years imagining this evidence as a weapon, and three weeks building it into one, but watching Marek read it she understood it was something else entirely. It was a message in a bottle that had finally reached a shore with someone on it. She watched his lips move through the columns. She watched him get to the ninth of December, to the hour of the flood tide, to the numbers that could not be made to agree with the official record no matter how generously you read them, and she watched his jaw set like a keel taking weather.

"Tide tables don't lie," he said at last, very quietly. "Men lie. Tables just stand there and tell the truth until somebody writes over them." He looked up. "These are real. This is the real book. God help me, girl, where did you—"

"The observatory clock. Hidden in the case. He couldn't destroy them five years ago and couldn't keep them either, so he buried them where only he would ever go, and then he sabotaged the clock so nobody else would either. Until spring. Until the burning."

Marek closed the ledger. He held it a moment longer, as if its weight surprised him every time he checked.

"My wife's brother went down on the *Wren*," he said. "Did you know that? Half this town lost someone and half this town decided overnight that they hadn't, if you follow me. Grief with a harbor master presiding over it. Easier to mourn clean than to ask questions." He tucked the ledger under his arm, and for one bad second Edmund thought he meant to keep it, to bury it deeper than she ever could — and then he held it back out to her, handle of the oilcloth toward her, an offering.

"But some of us asked questions quietly," he said, "for five years, with no answer coming. And here you come across my ice at midnight with the answer sewn in your coat." He exhaled, long, the breath steaming and vanishing. "Tomas Ashgrove pulled me out of the water. I suppose I always owed the family a crossing."

Hope hit her harder than fear ever had. She had to lock her knees to stand still under it.

"The road's marked to the mid-channel shed," Marek said, already moving, untying the tarred rope from the first iron post, coiling it with a seaman's economy. "Stay on the brush markers — the ice east of the third marker is new-frozen over the old channel and it will sing before it goes, but it will only sing once. At the shed you'll find the night clerk. Boy named Piet."

"I know him."

Something shifted in Marek's face. "Ah. That's the boy who ran to Hale tonight."

"He didn't give my name."

"They never do, the first time." He dropped the coil by the post and looked at her, and now his voice changed — lower, older, the voice of a man handing over cargo he considered more valuable than anything on his manifests. "Listen to me. The next gate is the ferry office, and the last gate is Hale himself, and I can't help you past either. What I can tell you is this: whatever happens at those gates, don't hide again. You've been invisible fifteen years and it very nearly worked. Tonight you stood on my ice and said your name out loud, and I watched two grown men forget how to breathe. That's worth more than any forged paper you'll ever carry. Whatever they take from you out there — don't give them the name back."

The cold bit her cheeks. Her eyes were wet anyway.

"He talked about you," she said. "My father. In his letters. He said the best man in Vell customs was a great ox of a fellow who pretended to be slow so people underestimated him."

Marek's face cracked open at last, grief and humor in the same expression, and he put out his hand, and she took it, and his grip swallowed hers whole.

"Go," he said. "Third marker, mind. And Edmund—" He used her name deliberately, the true compound of it, and she felt it settle on her like clothing cut to fit. "When they ask who passed you through customs, tell them Marek Ostrand checked your papers personally and found them in order."

"Found them in order," she repeated.

"In order," he said, "and their bearer likewise."

He hung the lantern back on its hook, and the customs house light fell behind her, and Edmund Ashgrove stepped onto the marked road alone with four miles of singing ice ahead, the papers against her heart, her name still warm in her mouth —

and from the darkness of the mid-channel shed, far out on the black, a small lantern already burned. Someone was awake at the second checkpoint.

Someone was waiting.
