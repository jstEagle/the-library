## 20. Checkpoint Two: The Ferry Office

The customs house was behind her. Her name was in the open air of Vell now, spoken by a man with a lantern and an old friendship, and there was no taking it back. Edmund ran.

Not the flat sprint of a thief. A runner's economy — coat pulled tight around the papers sewn into its lining, boots finding the packed snow between the ice-slick cobbles, breath measured the way she'd been taught to measure everything: in small, exact increments that added up to survival. The harbor lay to her left, a vast white plain under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and at its far edge, where the ferry road climbed toward the mainland causeway, stood the second checkpoint.

A lamp burned there. One lamp, one brazier, one figure standing in the orange circle of light with his hands jammed in his pockets and his collar turned up against the wind.

Piet.

She had counted on this and dreaded it in equal measure, and now that both feelings had arrived at the same moment, they canceled each other out and left only her legs moving and the papers pressing against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

He saw her coming from fifty yards. She watched him straighten — watched the clerk's posture come over him, the borrowed authority of the ferry office settling on his thin shoulders like a coat too large for him. He was sixteen. He had a chipped front tooth and a laugh that came too easily, and for three months he had brought her tea gone cold from sitting too long on his desk while she fixed the office regulator, and he had told her everything he knew about everything, because no one had ever listened to him before, and she had used every minute of it.

Behind her, faint across the frozen town, a bell began to ring. The customs house. Word spreading. Time shortening.

"Stop," Piet called, and his voice cracked on it, which somehow made it worse. "Miss — I mean — stop right there."

She stopped. Ten feet between them. Close enough to see that his face wasn't the face of a boy guarding a checkpoint. It was the face of a boy who had been crying and was furious about it.

"You know why I'm here," Edmund said.

"I know you're the one." He said it like an accusation and it landed like a wound. "I sat there last night after — after I went to Hale's gate, and I did the sums again. The ones you showed me. Over and over. And the tide doesn't lie, does it. Water doesn't care who's harbor master. It just rises when it rises." His jaw worked. "I checked the moon tables myself. Twice. I stayed up all night checking them like that would change something."

"It didn't change anything," Edmund said gently. "You already knew that when you checked."

"I idolized him." Piet's voice broke again, angrier this time. "When I was small my mother took me to the memorial and put me up on the sea wall so I could see him, Corven Hale, standing there with his hat off, and I thought *that's what a man is*. That's what I'd be." He wiped his nose with the back of his glove. "And then some stranger shows me a page of numbers and takes him apart in ten minutes."

"Not a stranger," Edmund said.

"No." He looked at her then — really looked, the way Mrs. Tarnow had looked at her hands, the way people see what they were never permitted to see. "Not a stranger. Edmund Ashgrove. That's your father on the Wrenwreck list. Third name. I've read that plaque a hundred times since I was eight years old and I never once wondered about the names, I just read them like they were weather."

The bell behind her had stopped. In its place: voices, far off, and the crunch of more than one set of boots on snow.

"They're coming," she said. "Piet. I don't want to make you choose—"

"You already made me choose." He said it without heat. "Last night, when you showed me that page. You made me choose and I chose wrong first, and now I'm choosing right, so let me do it properly." He stepped aside from the road and swept his arm toward the dark line of the causeway beyond. "Checkpoint's clear. Nothing to declare. Go."

Edmund didn't move. Something in her chest had seized — not fear; she knew fear by now, knew its weight and temperature — but something older and less familiar. She thought of every transaction she had ever made in Vell: repairs traded for gossip, silence bought with competence, kindness accepted as cover. She had spent three months using this boy the way the town used everyone, as a means to an end with a face attached.

"Piet." She pulled off one glove and held out her hand. He stared at it, then took it, his fingers small and cold inside hers. "What I did to you — the questions dressed up as conversation, the page I showed you like bait on a hook — that wasn't fair, and I'm not going to pretend it was. You deserved the truth plainly told, not arithmetic slipped into your pocket. I'm sorry."

He blinked fast. Sixteen years old, trying to be a man in a town that had taught him men didn't blink.

"You could've just run past me," he said. "That's what Hale would've done. That's what anyone with any sense would do — apologize later, if it was convenient."

"I've been invisible for eleven years," Edmund said. "Invisible people get very good at apologizing later. I'm finished with it."

Something shifted in his face — the last of the boy-guard falling away, and underneath it not a man, quite, but someone honest, which was better.

"He let you go at customs?" Piet asked. "Old Brenner? He's supposed to—"

"Brenner knew my father. Knew him before the Wrenwreck, before Hale. He hesitated, and then he didn't." She glanced back down the white road toward town, where lamplight was beginning to move. "But hesitation isn't forever. Neither am I."

"Then go." But he didn't step further back. He stood there working his jaw, and Edmund, who had learned to read mechanisms by their hesitations, saw the gear catch before it turned. "There's a third gate," he said. "Hale's own. Nobody goes through it this time of night unless he's standing there himself, and he will be, won't he. For you, he'll be standing there."

"I expect so."

"And I'm supposed to stay at my post." Piet looked at the little brazier, at the ferry office dark behind it, at the whole architecture of his employment and his childhood arranged neatly around a lie. Then he reached into the office doorway, took down the second lamp, and blew it out.

* * *

They walked the causeway together, and Edmund understood that she had acquired something no plan had accounted for: a witness.

"The ice road's sound tonight," Piet said, talking fast the way he talked when he was nervous, filling silence with facts the way other boys filled it with noise. "Cold snap's had five days. Eighteen inches minimum past the point, I heard the ice master say it Tuesday. Stay away from the eastern margin where the current comes under the point — the black patches aren't thin ice, they're deep water showing through cracks, and you'll go down before you know you're on one. Keep left. Left the whole way, even when it looks longer."

"You've walked it?"

"My father iced fished out there every winter till he died. He said the harbor talks to you. Groaning means it's shifting, not failing — groaning is fine. Silence is what kills you. If it goes quiet, stop walking until it starts again." He said it all without breathing much, and then, quieter: "I'm coming with you. To the gate. Not past it. Just to see."

"To witness," Edmund said.

"That's the word." He glanced sideways at her. "If it ends bad — if Hale stops you, or worse — somebody has to have seen it. All of it. The truth needs a witness or it's just paper, and paper burns. He taught me that too, in a way. Everything he ever hid, he hid by burning things."

Ahead, where the causeway met the shore road, a single lantern hung from the iron frame of the harbor gate, and beneath it, motionless, a tall figure in a dark coat.

Corven Hale.

Piet made a small sound in his throat. Edmund felt her heart begin to work harder, steady and heavy, like the master clock she had wound back to life — tick by tick, the mechanism of the last five years arriving at the hour it had been built to strike.

"Stay behind me," she said. "Whatever happens."

"No," Piet said. "Beside you. Witnesses stand where they can see."

They came on together across the frozen harbor, two small figures and one waiting one, and behind them the wind carried the sound of pursuit still distant, still uncertain, and the ice beneath their feet groaned like something waking — long, low, and turning over in its sleep, as the third gate opened onto the last of the road.
