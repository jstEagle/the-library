## 22. What Can Be Carried

The mainland in February was a country of gray light and long corridors. Edmund learned them all in the weeks that followed the ice: the corridor of the customs office where she'd sat wrapped in a borrowed blanket, her fingers too stiff to undo the stitches holding the papers in her coat lining; the corridor of the harbor commission, where men with clean nails passed the ledgers down a table like relics; the corridor outside the hearing room where Corven Hale sat for eleven days with his hands folded, answering every question, denying nothing.

She had expected him to fight. He didn't. When the commissioner asked whether the tide entries of the ninth of March, 1906, were in his hand, Hale said only, "Yes," and then — and this was the part Edmund would think about for years — he said, "You should also have a page more. The last one. Ask the young woman who brought you these."

He did not say *Edmund Ashgrove*. He did not explain what she had been to Vell or what Vell had been to her. He gave his testimony the way he wound the master clock: nightly, completely, without hope of being forgiven for it.

The verdict came down on a Tuesday. Hale was stripped of his office, fined to the limit of the statute, and referred for criminal proceedings on charges of falsification of records and manslaughter by negligence. Some of the commissioners had wanted more. Edmund had sat in the back row and found that she wanted nothing at all. There was no sentence that could be measured against her father's boat going down with three men aboard, and she had stopped expecting arithmetic from the world.

The newspapers were less restrained than the commission. THE WRENWRECK LIES TOLD, said the Vell Courier's mainland cousin, and beneath it a smaller headline that made her stomach drop in a public tea room: WITNESS IN HARBOR CASE "A WOMAN IN MAN'S DRESS," GUILD TO REVIEW HER STANDING.

*Review.* She had known it would come. She had opened her coat at the customs house precisely so that it would come, so that no one could ever take the truth from her by threatening to reveal it. But knowing a wave is coming does not keep the cold off your skin.

* * *

The Clockmakers' Guild of the Mainland summoned her in April, though *summoned* was generous — a letter on good paper inviting her to "clarify matters pertaining to her registration." Her registration read *Edmund Ashgrove, journeyman, apprentice of Master Hendrik Ashgrove, deceased.* Every line of it was true except the one nobody had thought to write down, because nobody had imagined it needed saying.

The hall smelled of oil and old varnish, the same smell as her father's workshop, and that undid her a little before the questions even began. Seven masters sat behind a long bench. The youngest of them, a woman named Verlaine whom Edmund had never met but whose reputation for fine escapement work had reached even Vell, sat at the far end and would not look at her — or rather, looked at her steadily whenever the others looked away, which was its own kind of testimony.

"You registered under a false identity," said the Master of the Roll. He said it without heat, which was worse.

"I registered under my own name," Edmund said. "It is the only name I have."

"Your name is not Edmund."

"My name is what I have carried for twenty-three years through workshops that would not have opened their doors otherwise." She heard her voice stay level and was grateful to it. "My father taught me the trade before he taught me to lie about it. He taught me both because he loved me and because the trade was the only inheritance we had. When he died, I kept the trade. I am here to ask if the Guild will let me keep the name too."

"The Guild admits women," someone offered from the middle of the bench, weakly.

"The Guild admits women to a separate roll, at half the fee, barred from the bench and the seal," Verlaine said, still not looking away from Edmund. "I have filed objections to that rule twice. They are in the record."

"This is not the occasion—"

"It is exactly the occasion."

But the vote was never close. Disbarment for fraudulent registration, effective immediately, her journeyman's mark struck from the rolls. The Master of the Roll looked genuinely sorry as he said it, which Edmund noted and filed away: the world was full of sorry men whose sorrow changed nothing.

She stood to go. At the door, Verlaine caught up with her, out in the corridor among the smells of oil and varnish.

"They're wrong," Verlaine said. "And they know it. The separate roll comes up again next congress. If it passes—" She stopped herself, embarrassed by her own hope, or by offering it to a stranger.

"If it passes," Edmund said, "I'll consider reapplying." Then, because it was true and because Verlaine had earned it: "Your recoil escapements run fast in cold weather. You could fix it with a bimetallic arm, but everyone says it can't be done cheaply enough."

Verlaine laughed, sudden and real. "Who are you?"

"Nobody, now," Edmund said. "Which turns out to be a kind of freedom."

* * *

Spring came to the mainland the way spring comes to coastal towns — reluctantly, then all at once. Edmund took a room above a chandler's shop on a street called Low Lane, three doors from the water, and spent the money she'd carried out of Vell — most of it untouched through five years of apprenticeship, because a person who measures her life in what can be carried does not accumulate much else — on a lease, a bench, and a window sign.

**E. ASHGROVE — CLOCKS & WATCHES REPAIRED.**

Not *Ashgrove & Son*. Not a male partner fronting the trade while she worked behind a curtain, the arrangement her father had once proposed with such shame that she had refused it before he finished asking. Just the name, in black on white glass, catching the harbor light every morning when she unlocked the door.

The first month, foot traffic slowed outside the sign the way traffic slows at an accident. People came in anyway, eventually, because the town had two broken tower clocks, a lighthouse mechanism nobody could tame, and more pocket watches per capita than anywhere Edmund had ever lived. Skill, she discovered, was a language that argued louder than prejudice. By June, the chandler downstairs was telling anyone who'd listen that the new watchmaker had fixed in an afternoon what the last man hadn't fixed in a year, and by August, half her customers had forgotten to be surprised by her and the other half had decided their surprise was worth paying to get over.

There were still the ones who came to stare, and the ones who left when they saw her hands at the works, and one old captain who stood in the doorway a long time and finally said only, "Ashgrove. Pilot boat Ashgrove?" and when she said yes, took off his hat, put it back on, and left without another word. He came back the next week with a ship's chronometer that needed real work, and paid without haggling, and after that he came every few weeks with something or other, mostly to sit on her stool and talk about tides.

She kept the shop small on purpose. One bench, good light, a shelf of tools her father had begun and she had finished. Above the bench, where other shops hung their guild certificates, she hung nothing. In the corner, on its own shelf at eye height, sat a piece of brass and steel the size of two fists: the broken escapement of the Vell observatory's master clock, dead-stopped, its escape wheel frozen mid-tooth.

Hale had let her take it. That was the strange grace note of the whole affair — at the gate, in the last hour, he had watched her walk onto the ice and then, when the hearing depositions began and the clock was dismantled as evidence, he had testified that the sabotaged escapement should go to "the technician best qualified to document the damage," and everyone in the room knew what he meant and let it stand. So it had crossed the ice in her coat in place of the papers, sewn into the same lining, riding over the water like ballast.

She could have repaired it. She had worked out how within a week of setting it on the shelf — a replaced pallet stone, trued teeth, four hours' work. She chose not to. Some broken things were more useful broken; they kept their question open. Customers asked about it sometimes, and she told the story in whatever length the moment allowed, and the story always ended the same way: *and the man who broke it handed me the confession himself, at the last gate, and I don't know what that makes him, and neither does anyone.*

* * *

Letters came from Vell.

Mrs. Tarnow wrote first, a page a week in a spidery hand, mostly weather and gossip, occasionally a line that landed like a dropped wrench: *They've named the memorial path after your father. It seems a town can only afford honesty in small doses, but it does pay eventually.* Piet wrote too — awkward, over-formal letters full of ferry schedules and complaints about his new supervisor, and once, in a postscript: *I told the commission everything I told you. I checked the arithmetic twice. I check everything twice now.*

In May, a letter arrived with no letterhead. Hale's handwriting, smaller than she remembered, the loops pressed thin as if the pen barely dared touch the page.

*I will not trouble you with apology, which you did not ask for and which would serve neither of us. I write to tell you the outcome only. I have pled guilty to all charges. My property in Vell passes to the town, save one item: your father's watch, which I have enclosed below and which was never mine to keep, whatever I told myself in the dark years. I wound it every night for five years. It kept better time than I deserved.

*You asked me once, at the gate, why I gave you the final page. Here is the whole answer: because a man should die in the trade he ruined. Mine was truth-telling, at the end, done badly and late. Yours is clocks. Keep yours honest.

*C.H.*

Wrapped in the letter, ticking faintly against her palm as she turned it over, was her father's pocket watch.

She sat with it a long time. Then she opened the case — the hinge still smooth, still oiled, evidence of those five years of nightly winding — and read the engraving inside the lid, the one she had known by heart since she was six: *To H. Ashgrove, who keeps us on time. From E., 1899.*

From E. From *her mother*, dead before the disguise began, dead before anyone had decided what Edmund could and couldn't be. Her father had never once suggested changing the engraving, even when he'd taught her to bind her chest and lower her voice. *Names are load-bearing,* he used to say, tightening the last screw on a movement. *You don't go altering them for convenience.*

She set the watch on the shelf beside the escapement, wound it, and listened to it run.

* * *

Winter came again. On the first hard frost, Edmund walked down to the water before dawn and watched the harbor begin to skin over with ice, and thought of a night crossing she had made a year ago with papers against her ribs and the groaning dark under her feet. She had believed, crossing, that she was carrying the truth to safety. Standing here now, she understood she had been carrying something else as well, something heavier and hers alone: the right to exist in one piece.

The guild letter came in December — the congress had voted. The separate roll was abolished. Women admitted to the full register, the bench, the seal. Verlaine had written across the bottom of the official notice, in ink too formal for her usual scrawl: *The seat opposite mine is empty. It has your name on it, if you want it. If you want it is the operative condition, and I suspect you'll take your time deciding.*

Edmund read it standing in the shop with the door open to the cold, letting the harbor air move through. Behind her, forty clocks ticked in imperfect agreement, the way they always did, each keeping its own time and none of them wrong.

She thought about the word *belonging*, which she had spent her life treating as a place you arrived or didn't: a workshop, a town, a roll of names. She had belonged to Vell for one winter and it had nearly eaten her. She had belonged to the Guild for ten years on false terms and it had discarded her the moment the terms showed. Every belonging she had ever been granted had come with a clause that could end her.

This was different. This was a door, not a home — an offer she could accept or refuse without disappearing either way. She was E. Ashgrove, Clocks & Watches Repaired, and the sign stayed up regardless. The name on the roll would just be the same name already on the window, already on the letters, already spoken aloud at a checkpoint in her own voice while officers stared.

She took Verlaine's letter to the desk, dipped a pen, and started the reply: *Dear Master Verlaine — I'll take my time, but yes. And when I sit that exam, I intend to bring a certain broken escapement with me as my thesis subject, because I have some thoughts on sabotage-proof pallet design that the Guild ought to hear...*

Outside, the first snow of the season came down over the water, softening the harbor edge by edge toward white. Edmund wrote until the light failed, then lit the lamp, wound the shop clocks one by one — her nightly ritual now, though hers was penance for nothing — and went on writing.

Somewhere across the ice, in a gray town that was learning, slowly and reluctantly, to tell itself the truth, a master clock stood repaired and running in a stone tower, keeping perfect time for a harbor that no longer trusted its keepers. She liked imagining it: the escapement she had rebuilt, dropping its beats into the dark, patient, exact, answerable to no one.

Like her. Like her.
