## 1. The Season of Closed Roads

There is a particular art to being overlooked, and I had spent nineteen years perfecting it.

It is not, as some might imagine, a matter of shrinking. Shrinking draws the eye the way a stutter draws mockery. No — the trick is to be *useful* in the flattest possible way. To carry the ledgers without dropping them. To refill the inkwells before anyone notices they are dry. To have a face like a Tuesday. In the counting house of the Corvane Trading Company, where forty clerks bent over forty desks and every one of them believed himself essential, I had achieved something close to perfection: Mr. Pellingsworth, who shared my row for six years, would have struggled to say whether I was called Rosalind or Rosemary or simply *the girl*. The company paid my wages to a fund I was never permitted to touch, against an indenture signed in my father's name when I was eight years old, and in all that time I had become as furniture — serviceable, unremarked, and owned.

I want it understood that I did not resent this. Resentment is a luxury for people who expect things. What I felt on the first of November, watching the last cart rumble across the tidal bridge before the winter closure, was something closer to satisfaction. The causeway road would flood twice daily now; the bridge gates would be chained until spring thaw; and Saltmere would contract into itself like a fist, the company compound sealed away from the world with me inside it. Safe. Contained. Invisible for another season.

I had not yet learned the lesson that every keeper of small fires eventually learns: that walls keep things out, but they keep you in, and sometimes what they keep in with you is trouble wearing another man's coat.

* * *

The closing of the bridge was the one day of the year the compound permitted itself ceremony. Factor Grell stood on the customs steps in his grey wool coat, reading the trade accord proclamation to a gathering of clerks and dockmen whose breath hung in the cold like a congregation of ghosts. He read well, Grell. He had the sort of voice that made even larceny sound like scripture, which was fortunate for him, given his profession.

"...and until the reopening of the causeway, the Corvane Trading Company assumes full custodial authority over all persons, goods, and vessels within the compound perimeter, as provisioned under Article Nine."

Article Nine. The article that made me, legally speaking, a kind of weather — present, accounted for, belonging to no one and therefore to the company. I stood at the back with the ledger under my arm and watched him fold the proclamation closed.

"The rival fleets will be frozen out of the northern grounds by mid-month," Grell said, and here he smiled, because he could afford to. "The Voss fleet has already withdrawn beyond the strait. Let them dive in their own waters and drown in their own waters. Saltmere's season belongs to Corvane."

A murmur went through the crowd — approval, mostly, and the particular silence of men who had seen what happened to disapproval. I looked down at my ledger and thought of nothing in particular. That was the safest thing to think.

That evening the wind came around to the north and stayed there, and winter began in earnest.

* * *

It was nine days later that everything I had built — this careful, dust-colored life — ended in a boathouse that smelled of tar and dead eels.

I had gone down to the water on an errand so trivial I cannot now recall it. Something about oiled canvas for the winter stores. The docks were empty in the way docks only are in deep cold, when even the gulls give up and sit facing away from the wind. The boathouse door was supposed to be locked for the season. It was not.

I remember standing in the doorway with the gray light behind me, letting my eyes adjust, and thinking first — absurdly — that someone had left the salvage netting in a tangle on the floor. Then the netting breathed.

He was wedged between the overturned skiff and the wall, and he had arranged himself with a dying man's practicality: out of the sightline of the door, near the rain barrel, with a boat hook laid across his lap like he still believed he'd use it. There was blood on him — old blood, browned along his left side from hip to ribs, and new blood, from a wound in his shoulder that he had bound with what looked like a strip of sailcloth tied one-handed. His face was familiar in the way a song is familiar when you haven't heard it since childhood: bearded now, hollowed, but the same heavy brows, the same crooked nose broken by a guide-rope in some summer neither of us had forgotten properly.

"Ilya," I said, before I had decided to say anything at all.

His eyes found me and did not widen. That was somehow worse than if he had lunged. He looked at me the way a man looks at a tide chart — calculating, resigned.

"Underhill's girl," he said. His voice was gravel steeped in brine. "You have her mother's chin. And your father's way of walking into rooms quiet as sin."

Every sensible instinct I owned told me to leave. Walk out. Bolt the door. Mention to no one, ever, that the boathouse lock was broken. I was indentured property of the Corvane Trading Company; the man on the floor belonged to the Voss fleet, which had been declared enemy to the trade accord in October with all the pomp Grell could summon; and harboring him was not a crime so much as a suicide arranged in installments.

"You're bleeding," I said, instead of leaving.

"It does that." He shifted and failed, entirely, to hide what it cost him. "How long until the bridge opens?"

"Not until thaw."

Something crossed his face then — not fear exactly. Arithmetic. A man totaling what he had left. "Then I am," he said, "in a great deal of trouble."

"Yes," I said. "You are."

He laughed, which turned into coughing, which spotted the sailcloth dark. I should like to report that I weighed the moral question like a heroine in a serial. I did not. What happened was smaller and stranger: I knelt, because my father had taught me to kneel toward bleeding men, and because kneeling toward this one felt like a debt coming due — though I could not yet have said for what.

He watched me untie the sailcloth with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be. "You don't ask how I got in."

"The fishing cut at low tide, past the chain, in one of the fleet's skin boats. You'd be frozen half to death, so you'd have shipped somewhere warm after." I nodded at his side. "And you've been here two days at least, because the fever's established. You knew about the bridge closure." I looked up at him. "You came here *because* of the closure. Because nobody leaves, and nobody looks."

His brows rose a fraction. "Underhill said you were clever. He said you were clever and hidden, like money sewn into a coat."

My father's name sat in the air between us, and I found I could not pick it up. Six years dead. Fever, they told me, in the northern station, quick and unremarkable, his effects sent south in a crate I was not permitted to open. I had buried whatever I felt about it in the same unmarked grave as everything else.

"He talked about me?" I said. My voice did something I didn't authorize.

"He talked about you the way misers talk about gold. Never loudly." He pressed his hand over the wound as I cleaned it, hissing. "In the skiff. Left pocket. I was told — only if you didn't send me away."

I told myself afterward that I already knew what I would find, that some part of me had known since the moment I said his name. But that is the vanity of hindsight. In truth my hands went into that coat pocket the way hands go into a fire.

The letter inside was wrapped in oilskin. The seal had been wax and my father's signet — the lantern-and-key he wore on his thumb for thirty years — pressed deep and clean. The paper was soft with handling. Four lines, in his blunt sailor's hand:

*Rosalind — if Voss comes to you, he is to have shelter, passage, and silence. He carried me once when I could not carry myself. What the company owes him it will never pay. You and I might. Forgive me the asking. Your loving father.*

I read it three times. The third time was not for comprehension. It was because my eyes kept snagging on *you and I*, written by a dead man to a daughter he had addressed as though she were already the person he believed she was — someone capable of shelter, passage, silence. Someone who existed outside the ledger.

"Well?" Ilya said quietly.

Outside, across the gray water, the harbor light was waking for the evening, and somewhere behind me the compound bell rang the change of watch, and none of it — not the ledgers, not Article Nine, not nineteen years of practiced invisibility — none of it had ever felt less relevant than it did at that moment.

I folded the letter and put it inside my shirt, against my skin, where it lay like something with a pulse.

"If anyone finds you here," I said, "we both hang. You understand that."

"They told me you were your father's daughter," Ilya Voss said, and shut his eyes. "I'm glad it's true."

I stood in the doorway a moment longer, doing the arithmetic he had done — supplies, fever, a hiding place, four months of winter — and understood, with a dread that felt almost like relief, that the woman who walked out of that boathouse could never again be furniture.

There was one place in the compound no one entered anymore. It had a lamp in it that I alone tended.

I went to fetch the key.
