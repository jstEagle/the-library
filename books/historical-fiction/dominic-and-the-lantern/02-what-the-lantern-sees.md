## 2. What the Lantern Sees

The lantern room had been dark for eleven years — not officially decommissioned, mind you, because the Corvane Trading Company never officially did anything it could instead quietly stop paying for. The light had simply been reassigned to the new tower on Gullspit Point, and the old harbor lantern had been left to gather salt like everything else in Saltmere the company no longer found profitable. Including, I was beginning to suspect, me.

It suited me fine, that room. I had been tending its dead wick since I was fourteen, climbing the ninety-one steps every few weeks under pretense of "inventory," which is a word that makes clerks' eyes glaze so thoroughly they never once followed me. Nobody audits a woman counting rusted lamp-glass. Invisibility has rules, you see. You must be useful but never interesting. Present but never noted. I had been practicing since I was seven years old, when my father signed me over to Corvane's books, and I had gotten very good at it.

Good at it right up until the night I led a bleeding enemy of the trade accord up ninety-one stairs by candlelight, praying to a God I only half believed in that the fog would hold.

* * *

By day, I smuggled him his life in a bread basket.

This required theater. I had always taken my supper alone on the seawall — clerks find solitary eaters unsettling and leave them be — so now I ate half and carried the other half up the tower wrapped in my shawl like leftovers, which is what it was, though not mine. The company surgeon kept laudanum in a locked cabinet whose key hung from a nail behind the ledger of outbound tin, because the company locked everything except the things that mattered. I borrowed it Tuesdays, when Mr. Fenn took his gin at noon. A thimble of laudanum, a roll of linen, a jar of the surgeon's carbolic stolen in quantities so small that the shrinkage read as mice. Saltmere's mice are famous thieves. I have never been prouder of an alibi I invented for creatures who never asked for the work.

Ilya Voss healed the way driftwood dries: crookedly, and all at once when you weren't watching. The wound in his shoulder was a week old when I found him and three weeks closing when he could finally sit up against the lantern's housing without going gray. By day he lay flat among the dusty lamp-glass while I worked the counting house two hundred yards away, transcribing freight tallies in a hand nobody ever praised, and I confess I thought about him the way you think about a lit match carried through a hay barn — constantly, and with dread, and with a strange attention to how the light moved.

Because here is what I had not told anyone, least of all myself: I liked hearing him talk.

He talked at night, low, when the searches were done. Grell had put men on the streets by the second week. Word had come down from the accord offices that a diver from the Voss fleet had gone into the water near the wreck of the *Meridian* before she burned, and had come out again, and was presumed hiding somewhere in the compound with whatever he'd found down there. Grell's men knocked on doors with a courtesy that frightened everyone, and searched trunks with a thoroughness that insulted them, and every evening I climbed my ninety-one steps with my heart going like a gull in a sack, wondering if tonight the tower seemed worth knocking on.

"They're not looking for a man," Ilya told me one night. We were sitting in the black mouth of the great lens, the real moon doing a poor imitation of the lamp it had replaced. "They're looking for papers."

"Papers don't bleed."

"Papers are worse." He turned his bandaged shoulder toward the dark window, toward the water. "Your father understood that."

I had been waiting eleven days for him to say my father's name, and when he finally said it, it went through me like a splinter I'd forgotten I was carrying — not new pain, exactly. Old pain, relocated.

"Then tell me," I said. "Tell me what he understood."

* * *

You should know what I knew before that night, so you understand the size of what I learned.

I knew my father, Tobias Underhill, had been a diver for Corvane for nineteen years, one of the men they sent down when cargo sank or harbors needed clearing or rival wrecks wanted salvaging before their owners arrived. I knew he died of fever in the winter of '82, because that is what the company ledger says — I have copied the entry four times in my years in the counting house, *T. Underhill, fever, no dependents*, which last part was a lie so tidy it should have embarrassed someone, as it failed to account for a dependent then aged nine, currently writing this sentence. I knew he had a partner named Ilya Voss whom I remembered only as a pair of hands lifting me onto a dock piling, and a laugh like a hatch opening. And I knew my father had once kept a letter sealed with his own ring, because I had seen him press the wax when I was very small, and I had assumed for twenty years that the letter was why Ilya had crossed half a frozen sea to die badly in our boathouse.

The letter was real. The promise in it was real. Those turned out to be nearly the only true things involved.

"There was a wreck that winter," Ilya said. "The *Lark*. Company ship, company manifest, company insurance. She went down off the shallows with her holds full, and Corvane reported the loss complete. Total. Everything drowned, everything lost, claim paid." His voice was flat the way water is flat before something comes up through it. "But your father went down on the last dive — unofficial, off the books, Grell's order — to see if anything was recoverable before the season closed. And he came up and told me what was down there."

I waited. In the counting house I am patient the way stone is patient.

"Nothing was lost, Rosalind. That's what he found. The *Lark* was scuttled. Deliberately, by her own crew, on Grell's instruction — holes bored below the waterline, cargo lightened first and rowed ashore over three nights. Then the ship sunk, the loss claimed, the insurance paid out twice over on the same goods. Your father saw the bore-holes. Clean, even, auger work. He came up and he said—" Ilya stopped, and I understood he was giving me the sentence gently, the way you hand someone something heavy edge-first. "He said, *someone has to write this down.*"

"And then?"

"Fever," Ilya said. "Very sudden. Very convenient. Two days after he reported what he'd seen to the factor's office. The doctor who signed the certificate was the same man who keeps the laudanum cabinet you've been robbing."

I sat with that. The lantern room smelled of cold iron and dead oil and eleven years of salt, and I sat in the middle of it and felt something in my chest go from grief — which is soft, which is weather — into something with edges.

For twenty years I had believed my father died of bad luck and worse climate, the ordinary cruelty of a hard town. It had made a kind of sense. Corvane was a wall; men died against walls; you learned to be small and the wall might overlook you. But my father hadn't died of weather. He had died of arithmetic. He had gone down into the cold and come back up carrying the truth in his hands like a recovered bell, and the company had weighed it, found it inconvenient, and dropped him over the side. Fever. *No dependents.*

"You're crying," Ilya said, not unkindly.

"I'm inventorying," I said, and wiped my face with the heel of my hand, and we both let that stand as the lie it was.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rage: it is wonderfully clarifying material, but it sets fast, and you get one pour.

I spent the walk down the ninety-one steps deciding what to make of mine. Grell's men were searching houses again — I could hear the knocks from the stair's arrow slits, methodical as a clock — and somewhere below me Mr. Grell, factor of the Corvane Trading Company, murderer-by-ledger, was sleeping soundly on a mattress paid for out of insurance fraud, having written *fever* in a book and called it medicine.

I want to be honest, because honesty is the one luxury I never stole from the company: I considered giving Ilya up. Not for long, and not seriously, but I considered it, the way you consider jumping from a height — your body does the math without asking you. One man, already condemned, traded for my warm corner of the wall. The court wouldn't call it murder. The court would call it loyalty.

But the court also called my father's death a fever, and I had stopped taking the court's word for anything around step sixty.

Justice, I was beginning to understand, was not going to arrive in Saltmere wearing robes. It would come the way everything came to Saltmere — smuggled, mislabeled, carried up dark stairs in a bread basket. If the law had a price and I couldn't pay it, then I would do what the law pretended not to know how to do: I would keep my promise, protect my witness, and find out what Ilya had actually pulled from the wreck of the *Meridian* that made an entire accord fleet declare one diver an enemy.

At the top of the stairs I paused on the landing where the old lamp used to hang, and looked at the great dark lens, and had the oddest sensation of being watched by it — by all the years of light it had thrown on water, keeping ships honest whether or not the town below was.

"You'll need to tell me about the *Meridian*," I said to Ilya, when I reached the top.

He was awake, propped against the housing, and he didn't ask how I knew there was more. He only looked at me for a long moment, the way you look at someone who has changed shape since supper.

"Tomorrow," he said. "There's something you should see first. Something your father sent me to find — and Grell knows I have it."

Below us, faint through the fog, came the sound of boots on the harbor stones. Not walking past.

Climbing.
