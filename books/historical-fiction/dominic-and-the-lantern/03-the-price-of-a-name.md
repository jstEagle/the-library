## 3. The Price of a Name

There is a particular arithmetic to fear, and I had become fluent in it. Three days since the search began in earnest. Two guards now posted at the bridge, though the bridge was closed and the guards mostly slept. One lantern room at the end of a rotting catwalk, holding one wounded diver, one letter with my father's seal, and one woman who had spent nineteen years teaching an entire compound not to see her.

The arithmetic was failing.

I knew it the morning Tamsin, the junior clerk, looked up from her ledger as I passed and said, "You've been coming in late, Underhill." Not unkindly. Just noting it, the way one notes weather. I had been late twice — once for Ilya's fever, once because I'd sat too long in the lantern room listening to him describe my father laughing, and could not make my legs agree to stairs.

"You've been counting wrong," I said, and she flushed, and I hated myself for it. The old Rosalind would have smiled and apologized and vanished. But invisibility, it turns out, is like any currency: spend it once, and you haven't got it anymore.

I worked the morning column of salt duties with hands that did their work while my mind ran the ledger beneath: bread from the kitchen before dawn, when Cook was still drunk enough to mistake me for anyone; laudanum bought off young Dr. Prue with money I did not have and promises I meant badly; the way Ilya's color had come back, which should have gladdened me and instead terrified me, because a man well enough to walk is a man who will be found walking.

"Underhill."

Mr. Grell's voice does not carry so much as arrive, already inside your ear, though he stands across the room. He stood now by the factor's dais, holding a folded paper that did not look like much, which is Grell's gift — making ruin look like correspondence.

"Factor wants the quarterly tallies read aloud," he said. "Come."

I followed him through the counting house, past the clerks who bent lower over their books in the manner of people hoping the water rises around someone else first, and into the small room behind the factor's office where disputes were settled and, less officially, where men who owed the company were reminded of what they owed.

There was no factor there. There was only Grell, the paper, and on the table between us — laid flat, smoothed out, almost tenderly — a strip of linen, browned at its center with dried blood.

I did not touch it. I am proud of that, later if never again. My body wanted very much to do something obvious — to gasp, to snatch it away, to deny — and some cold clerk-thing in me simply read it, the way I read a column: linen torn from a sheet, hospital-grade weave, not the harbor watch's coarse bandages. Found somewhere specific, or Grell would not be showing it to me.

"Do you know," Grell said, "in eleven years, I have never once had cause to speak your name aloud? I had to ask Tamsin what it was." He smiled. It was not a threat; it was worse, a compliment. "That's a kind of genius, Miss Underhill. I'd nearly forgotten you existed. And then this winter — late arrivals, missing bread, laudanum signed out against a debt nobody remembers incurring. You've become *legible*."

"I've been ill," I said. "So has half the compound."

"Mm." He turned the bandage over with one finger. "Found this burned in the grate of the old boathouse stove. Incompletely, as fire is lazy and so was whoever fed it. Company-issue linen, mind. We inventory our own sheets." He looked up. "A diver came ashore before the closure. Voss, they're calling him. Enemy of the accord. And someone in my compound is feeding him, dosing him, and burning his dressings like a housewife hiding a love affair."

"Then you should question the housewives," I said, and heard my own voice do the thing it had learned to do among clerks: flat, dry, faintly bored.

Grell laughed — genuinely, which unsettled me more than shouting would have. "Oh, you're your father's daughter after all. I wondered." He drew a second paper from his coat and unfolded it on the table beside the bandage, and I knew it before I read a word, because I have dreamed of it: my indenture. My name in a child's uncertain hand, witnessed, sealed, sold. Nineteen years old, that signature. I had been seven.

"The Corvane Trading Company," Grell said, "is prepared to tear this document in half."

The room went very quiet, the way rooms do when something enormous is being offered cheaply. Freedom. I had thought about freedom the way orphans think about mothers — constantly, abstractly, without expectation of delivery. And here it was on a table between a bloodied rag and a knife I hadn't seen yet but knew was coming.

"In exchange," Grell continued, "for information regarding the current location of Ilya Voss. A word. A direction. You needn't even point. Nod, and you are a free woman by the spring tide, papers gone, debt dissolved, a reference in company hand besides."

"And if I don't nod?"

He folded his hands, a patient man settling in. "Harboring an enemy of the accord is treason. Treason in wartime trade law is hanging. The law doesn't much distinguish between the hider and the hidden — it strings them side by side, and the magistrates come back over the bridge in thaw and find it all very tidy." He tapped the indenture paper. "Or. This."

I looked at the paper. That is the honest accounting: I looked, and I wanted it, and wanting it felt like drowning in warm water — pleasant right up until you understand no one is coming. I thought: sign nothing, say nothing, stall until the fog comes. I thought: he has a bloodied bandage and suspicions, but suspicions are not a diver in irons. Every day Ilya heals is a day closer to the ice bearing weight.

And beneath those thoughts, quieter, another ledger entirely, one I had been keeping without admitting it: my father's voice saying *we owe what we promise*, and Ilya's voice saying *he died asking whose rope had been cut*, and Grell's own ledgers, which I have kept for eleven years and which contain, on the third shelf, in a box marked MISCELLANEOUS DUTIES, the dive requisition dated the week my father drowned — authorized by G. Grell, factor pro tem.

He was offering to buy my freedom with the same hands that had priced my father's life at nothing.

"I'll need time," I said. "To consider."

"No," said Grell, pleasantly. "You'll need until solstice night. The feast. Everyone drunk, everyone accounted for, everyone watched — which cuts both ways, Miss Underhill. After the festival, if I have no answer, I go to the magistrates with what I have. A bandage. Bread. Laudanum. A name." He slid both papers toward me, the indenture on top, and set a pen beside them. "Some people find it clarifies things, seeing their whole life on one page."

I picked up the pen. This was expected; refusing outright would end the interview and begin the arrest, and I am not finished being useful when they think I'm deciding. I dipped it. I held it over my name — over the wobbly seven-year-old letters that had cost me everything and bought me nothing — and I let Grell watch me hover there, three seconds, five, a woman seriously considering selling a man's life for her own.

Then I set the pen down, uncapped.

"My eyes are tired," I said. "Solstice night. I'll give you your answer at the feast."

His smile thinned by a degree. "See that you do. Answers keep better than fugitives. Fugitives get sick, get seen, get stupid." He gathered the bandage but left the indenture lying on the table, deliberately, I understood — a leash laid out where I could see it. "Incidentally," he added, at the door, "the lantern tower's been dark two nights running at the top of the hour. Sloppy, Underhill. Fix it. Wouldn't want anything drawing attention to that end of the catwalk."

* * *

I did not go back to the counting house. I went up.

The stairs to the lantern room I know blind, and I took them blind, and I burst through the door with my heart going like the light itself, and Ilya was sitting up, gray-faced but upright, mending a net he'd found because apparently enemy divers cannot rest.

"He knows," I said. "Grell found the bandage. He knows, or he knows enough that knowing is a formality." I told it all — the offer, the paper, solstice night — while he listened the way divers listen, still, saving air.

When I finished he was quiet a moment. "Solstice," he said finally. "The fog comes off the shallows on the big tides. Your father always said—" He stopped, corrected himself. "*I* always said: on a solstice low tide, with fog, a man could walk out of Saltmere dry-shod if the ice held."

"Will it hold?"

"It held for me." He looked at me, and there was something in his face I distrusted immediately, because I had seen it on Tamsin that morning: the beginning of noticing someone. "Rosalind. Come with me."

And there it was, the second offer in one day, also enormous, also cheap. Across the ice, out of the cage, a life somewhere the name Underhill had never been spoken.

"No," I said, and was surprised how easily it came. "If I run, he wins by default. Everything stays exactly as he built it — my father drowned and unnamed, me vanished, the ledgers tidy." I looked out the lantern glass at the harbor, at the feast-torches already stacked for solstice, at the frozen shallows gleaming beyond the mole like a road no one believed in. "Besides. He expects my answer at the feast."

"Which is?"

I turned back to the great lamp, and checked the wick, and found myself smiling in a way I did not recognize — wider, hungrier, mine.

"A diversion," I said.
