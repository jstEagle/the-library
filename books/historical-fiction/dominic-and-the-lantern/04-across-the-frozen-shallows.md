## 4. Across the Frozen Shallows

The solstice festival came to Saltmere the way debt comes to sailors: announced for weeks, dreaded all the same, and impossible to escape once it arrived. By four o'clock the compound windows were already amber with lamplight, and the great hall had been hung with holly that Mr. Grell himself had inspected twice, because Grell believed even a branch of greenery might harbor sedition.

I stood at my ledger in the counting house and added other men's profits with steady hands, and inside me something turned over and over like a coin on a tabletop.

Tonight.

The plan was simple, which is to say it was terrible, and we had no better one. Every watchman, every clerk, every factor's man would be at the feast — Grell presiding over the roast goose and the company punch, making his little speech about loyalty and abundance while the tide went out beneath him. The shallows between the breakwater and the far channel froze solid in December, but only at low water, and only for two hours. Ilya could not swim with that shoulder. He would have to walk across the ice like a ghost walking on its own grave.

At half past six, I left the counting house without my coat. No one noticed. That was the whole architecture of my life, wasn't it — a girl built of gaps and oversights, a person you could lose in a room the size of a broom cupboard. For nine years I had been grateful for it.

I climbed the tower stairs two at a time, lungs burning, and lit the lantern three hours before dusk required it.

Then I did something I had never done: I let it be seen doing what it should not do. I turned the shutters wrong. I let the beam swing wide and wild, sweeping the compound walls like a searchlight looking for something. A keeper's error. An old lamp gone faulty. The kind of thing that would bring the watch up the cliff path grumbling about the woman who couldn't tend her own light.

Every eye on the tower meant no eye on the boathouse stair.

I met Ilya at the tideline where the fog was already coming in off the channel, thick as wool, smelling of salt and kelp and cold. He moved stiffly, his arm bound against his chest under a borrowed coat three sizes too large, and he looked at me the way he had looked at the letter with my father's seal — as if I were proof of something he'd stopped believing in.

"The ice," he said. "Rosa. It will hold?"

"It held for the gulls this morning. It'll hold for you." I handed him the satchel: bread, cheese, the last of the laudanum, and wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, everything I knew about the Corvane Company's ledgers that I probably shouldn't have copied out. "Give that to anyone who'll read it. There are people on the northern fleet who owe your family favors. Use them."

He took my good hand — the one not holding the storm lantern — and pressed it between both of his.

"Come with me."

The fog swallowed his voice and gave it back softened, almost kind.

"I can't dive anymore. You can't stay in this place. Rosalind, they know someone helped me. When they find out—"

"They won't find out tonight." I pulled my hand free and pointed out across the shallows, where the ice lay pale and waiting under the first threads of mist. "There. Follow the breakwater until you pass the second marker, then bear north by the channel light. Don't stop. Don't look back. And don't," I said, "argue with me. You're a slow healer and a worse debater."

He laughed — a short, cracked sound — and then his face did something complicated.

"Your father," he said, "would have been proud past bearing."

"My father," I said, "was proud past bearing. It killed him. Or rather, they did." I stepped back. "Go, Ilya. Walk soft. Live long. Make them sorry."

I watched him go until the fog took him — one dark figure moving carefully across white nothing, pausing at the second marker, turning once (he looked back; of course he looked back; men always do) — and then he was only the sound of boots on ice, and then not even that.

* * *

I did not go back down the cliff path.

I sat in the lantern room instead, in the dark I had made myself, listening to the foghorn moan and thinking about what it costs to send someone away and stay behind. It costs more than leaving. Anyone can run. Staying is the expensive thing.

Boots on the stairs, eventually. Two pairs. The watch, summoned by my broken lantern, arriving an hour late to a fault already mended.

"Keeper!" the older one shouted through the door. "Your light's gone wild tonight!"

"Old mechanism," I called back, opening the door with a yawn I did not entirely fake. "She sticks when it freezes. Come in and warm yourselves, if you've a mind, though I've nothing but tea and bad opinions."

They checked anyway. They always check. They found nothing but a woman, a lamp, and a ledger of light-hours kept in a neat hand, and they left grumbling about the cold, and I stood in the doorway watching them go and thought: *that* is what invisible looks like from the outside. I had worn it so long I'd forgotten it was a costume, not a face.

It was nearly midnight when I walked into the feast hall.

I had never done that either. The clerks' tables were for clerks; I took my meals standing in the pantry like a cat that has learned which rooms welcome it. But tonight I walked the length of that warm, roaring room with my father's letter against my ribs and the copy of the survey report — the real one, the one that said *depth unsafe, order rescinded* and then said *dive ordered, diver lost, cause: fever* in Grell's own hand — folded inside my bodice, and I felt every head turn.

Grell saw me from the high table. He set down his punch.

"Miss Underhill," he said pleasantly, crossing to meet me, and I noticed he used my name now. That was its own kind of warning. "A word outside."

"A hundred words," I said. "But indoors. I want witnesses."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the quick arithmetic of a man deciding whether he is holding cards or being dealt them. He had found the bloodied bandage three days ago; I knew he had; he had let me see him knowing. We had been circling each other since like dogs on a frozen pond.

"You have until the tide turns," he said softly. "My offer stands. Papers torn. Freedom. All you have to do is tell me where Voss crossed."

"He crossed nowhere," I said clearly, loudly enough for the nearest table to hear. "Because you have no proof any diver ever reached Saltmere, and I have this."

I unfolded the survey report and held it up, and I watched the hall go quiet around us, table by table, the way wind flattens grass.

"Signed," I said, "by E. Grell, Factor. Dated the ninth of October, the year my father died. Read it slowly. I've waited nine years; you may take your time."

Grell's face did not change. That was the frightening thing about Grell — he had been caught before, perhaps, and survived it, and survival had taught him that faces are negotiable.

"Burned papers make poor evidence," he murmured.

"So does a hanging factor," I answered, just as softly. "Shall we find out which of us the Company believes?"

* * *

Later — much later, when the feast had curdled into whispers and Grell had withdrawn with the senior partners and their lawyers, when the hall smelled of goose fat and scandal — the company solicitor spread a fresh document on the counting house table and asked me, with the weary courtesy of a man paid by the hour, to sign a statement.

I took the pen. My hand did not shake.

Nine years I had signed things: receipts, work-logs, confessions of small incompetence designed to keep myself beneath notice. Nine years of a signature shrunk small and slanted, tucked into corners of pages, apologizing for existing.

*Rosalind Underhill.*

I wrote it large. Across the full width of the line, every letter upright and certain, the R tall as a mast, the double-l standing straight as the tower itself. The pen scratched like something alive. When I finished, the name sat on the page looking less like handwriting than like a declaration of war.

The solicitor blinked at it. "That's... quite legible," he said, as though legibility were a crime.

"It's new," I said, and blotted the page.

Outside the window, the fog was thinning off the channel, and somewhere far to the north, I liked to think, a man with a bad shoulder was stepping onto solid ground. Behind me in the hall, Grell's voice rose and fell behind a closed door, arguing for his life.

Let him argue. I had a light to keep, a ledger to open, and nine years of accounts to settle — and for the first time in my life, every entry would bear my name.
