## 1. The Weight of Lead

The trouble with lead, Dominic Yarrow's father used to say, is that it wants to be something else. You heat it and it forgets itself; you pour it into the matrix and it becomes an *a*, a *g*, a long-tailed *q*, whatever letter the world was short of that morning. But cool it wrong, rush it, and it reverts — a misshapen lump, good for nothing but melting down again.

"Men are the same," he'd said, trimming the burr from a fresh sort with his thumbnail. "Most of them are only waiting for someone to give them their proper shape."

Dominic thought about this at half past six on a Tuesday morning in October, because she had just poured a bad cast and it sat in the cooling tray looking like nothing on earth — a slug with a limp, her father would have called it — and because it was easier than thinking about Madame Perrin, who was at this very moment climbing the outside stair to the shop door with the particular heaviness of a woman who intended to get answers.

She swept the failed cast into the scrap pot. She straightened her apron. She flattened, out of habit, the already-flat front of her waistcoat.

The knock came anyway. It always did.

"Monsieur Yarrow?" Madame Perrin's voice, pitched to carry through two inches of oak. "Monsieur Yarrow, I see the lamp in the window. I see the smoke from your chimney. I know you are in there."

Dominic counted to four, deepened her voice by a degree she'd practiced against the well-bucket until it stopped hurting her throat, and opened the door exactly far enough to be rude without being suspicious.

"Madame Perrin. Early."

"Not too early for rent," she said, craning past her like a goose trying to see over a fence. "And not too early to ask when I might finally meet my tenant face to face. Three years, Monsieur Yarrow. Three years I have rented my ground floor to a man made of lamplight and chimney smoke."

"My health," said Dominic, in Master Yarrow's voice. "The lungs. The physicians say—" and here she coughed, a dry, professional cough she kept oiled for exactly these occasions, "—forgive me. The air of company is bad for it."

"The air of *money* is good for it, though."

"Money has no smell at all, Madame. That is its finest quality." She produced the coins from the tin on the shelf, counted them into Madame Perrin's palm one at a time, slowly, the way her father had taught her: pay slow and people forget what they came to look at. "Twelve francs. Twelve, as agreed."

Madame Perrin bit one coin — habit, not suspicion; she'd have bitten the Archbishop of Vellamont — and squinted up at Dominic, who was, in fairness, squintable at. Nineteen years old, narrow as a composing stick, hair bound back under her cap, chin smudged with ink at all hours as though she wept the stuff. Her father had been a big man, barrel-chested, with hands like spades. She had his eyes and none of his mass, which meant that anyone who remembered old Yarrow saw him in her face and drew entirely the wrong conclusion, bless them.

"You've your father's look," Madame Perrin said, as she did every month. "Old Yarrow. God rest him. He was a handsome man before the stoop took him."

"So I'm told."

"He never minded showing his face, that one."

"The dead are careless that way." Dominic let the door swing another inch shut. "The damp. I mustn't stand in drafts. You understand."

Madame Perrin understood nothing and suspected everything, which was, Dominic had long ago decided, the correct arrangement between landlady and tenant. A landlady who understood would have asked different questions. One who merely suspected asked the same ones forever, and the same questions could be answered with the same cough.

"Well," said Madame Perrin, retreating down the stairs with the dignity of a woman denied, "tell your master the gutters want clearing before the frost."

"I'll tell him," said Dominic, "though he takes it ill, advice about his own house."

"It isn't his house."

"No," Dominic agreed pleasantly. "It's yours."

The door closed. She stood with her forehead against it for a moment, listening to the grumbling descent, then went back to the casting bench and began again.

* * *

By eight the shop smelled as it should: ink and oil and the cold-metal smell of the type cases, under it all the ghost of glue from the binding corner where the ledger lived. Customers wouldn't come till ten or after — Vellamont kept river hours, everything starting late and ending drunker — so she had three hours of the good quiet, the kind you could set type in.

She was printing a funeral sermon for the curé's cousin, forty pages of consolation, which meant setting the same pious phrases over and over until they wore smooth in her mouth. *Safe in the arms of the Savior.* She set it once and felt nothing, twice and believed it slightly, five times across the forme and it became simply shapes, brass and lead shapes, and that was the trick of the trade nobody warned the grieving about: print enough copies of heaven and it stops being a place and becomes a line of type. You could justify it. You could correct its errors. There was comfort in that, of a low kind.

Her father would have set it faster. He'd cut faces for half the printers between here and the mountains — punches, the true masters' work, cutting the steel original of a letter so that every cast ever struck from it carried his hand inside it invisibly. He had shown her once, when she was nine, how to hold the graver, how to read the grain of the steel.

"You don't cut a letter," he'd said. "You cut around it. The letter is already there. You're just the one patient enough to find it."

She had found the *e* for the funeral sermon in eleven minutes, which would have earned her a nod. She missed the nods more than she could reasonably explain to anyone.

At half past eight she allowed herself the other work. She washed her hands properly — ink fingers on ledger pages were a sin her father had numbered among the mortal ones — and carried the lamp to the binding corner, where the ledger of the house of Yarrow lay open under its weights.

It was not, strictly speaking, a shop ledger anymore. It had started as one, thirty years ago, when her father bought his press secondhand and wrote down what he owed and to whom. But ledgers grow like coral, layer on layer, each entry making a surface for the next, and by the time Dominic could read, the book held considerably more than sums. It held the town.

Not officially. Officially it held: *Mr. Aubry, flour, 14 sols. Widow Marchand, broadsheets, paid in bread, 6 mos. Tannery, handbills, quarterly.* The unofficial entries were written smaller, in her father's cramped private shorthand, and later in her own, which she had improved upon — his system had been built for one paranoid man and she had refined it for paranoia at scale.

She turned to October and began the mending. That was the word for it; her father had called it mending, as if the town were a garment and he kept its seams. Old Cabral the rope-walk foreman owed the tannery money he could not pay and could not be seen to owe, because Cabral had testified once in a matter the tanner's family still smarted over — so the entry read instead that the tannery owed *Cabral & Son* for cordage, a debt running the friendly direction, and everyone slept. The Vasseur brothers were entered as one man, *V. Vasseur*, because one of them was supposed to have died at sea seven years ago and had inconveniently not, and a dead man buying salt pork every week raised questions that ended at the magistrate's office. And there was a page — she checked it was turned to the wall, out of pure ritual, since no one living could read the cipher — with three names on it that were not names anyone had been born with, given to three people fleeing warrants from the old regime's courts, warrants grown dusty now but dust burns.

Forty-one names in all, if you counted the way her father had taught her to count — not heads, but lives that continued *because* the book said otherwise. Forty-one continuations, resting in a quarto volume with a cracked spine and a leather cover gone bald at the corners from handling.

She dipped her pen. She entered the morning's business: sermon, forty pages, paid half in advance by the curé, who was the only client in Vellamont who paid in advance and therefore beloved beyond his theology. Then, beneath it, in the small hand:

*Oct. 9th. V.V. (the elder) — passage arranged, Odile's ferry, Thursday last. Settled: 30 sols, per agreement. No further entry required.*

Which meant: the Vasseur brother who was dead had crossed the river to safety, the ferrywoman had been paid, and the whole affair was concluded. She blotted it, weighted the page, and sat back with the specific pleasure she took in a balanced line — the pleasure of a thing true, hidden, and finished, all three at once.

There was no word for what she was to the book. Clerk was too small. Keeper sounded like a zoo. Her father had called her, in his letters to nowhere — he wrote letters to his own dead master, unsent, filed in a drawer — *my son, who keeps the accounts.* So: son. Accounts. It fit loosely, the way most true things do.

Because that was the other entry, the founding one, the one at the very front of the book in her father's youngest handwriting, dated the year of her birth. She did not reread it often. It was a single line, and she had never fully parsed it — a payment, a name withheld, some arrangement made in the year she was born that her father had recorded and never explained, and he had died — apoplexy, mid-sentence, mid-*order*, the compositor said, halfway to asking for more sort — without explaining it. She kept the page stiff with pasteboard so her eye didn't fall on it by accident. Some mornings it fell on it anyway.

This was not one of those mornings. This was a good morning: rent paid, sermon justified, a dead man safely deader across the river, and the whole day ahead like a clean galley waiting for lockup.

* * *

At noon the town came to her, as it did. First Petit-Jean from the ferry stairs wanting handbills for a lost dog — *reward offered, animal answers to Caesar but answers to nearly anything if you're holding food* — and Dominic set it while he watched, open-mouthed, the way apprentices' cousins always watched, as though letters assembled themselves out of obedience rather than out of nineteen years of thumbs. Then the cooper, wanting his trade sign repainted and repainted cheap, and settling for a printed placard when Dominic observed that paint faded but print could be re-struck annually for less. Business sense was half the trade; her father had said the other half was remembering everybody's birthday, and Dominic had extended this to remembering everybody's grievances, which served better.

The afternoon brought Madame Foucault from the chandlery, which meant trouble dressed as gossip.

"They're saying on the north road there are soldiers," Madame Foucault announced, taking the shop's one customer chair without being asked, which was her right by long occupation of it. "A whole army, coming down out of the passes. Requisitioning, they call it. My sister's husband saw the dust from the hills, he says, like weather coming."

"Armies are always somewhere," Dominic said, distributing type into the case with her back half-turned. "Preferably elsewhere."

"That's what I told her. But they take the big warehouses, these people. Grain, horses, carts. My sister says they post papers on the church doors wherever they go. Orders."

"Vellamont has two churches and four doors between them," Dominic said. "We'd need a great many orders to cover us all."

Madame Foucault laughed the laugh of a woman who enjoyed doom in small doses, took a twist of tobacco Dominic sold her at a price that would have made her father wince, and left. But the word *orders* stayed behind in the shop like a draft, and Dominic found herself glancing, more than once, at the ledger in its corner.

Nothing would come of it. Nothing ever came of anything on the north road; the road's chief product was rumor, aged in casks and exported monthly. Six years ago the rumor had been plague. Four years ago, counter-revolutionaries in the mountains, which had at least been true, distantly, the way stars were distant fires. Rumor reached Vellamont the way light reached the bottom of the river — present in theory, harmless in practice.

Still. When the light went soft toward evening she doused the lamp early — lamplight in the window drew Madame Perrin like a moth with a grievance — and she sat with the ledger and read backward through it, the way she did when something itched. Debts and aliases, pardons and payments, forty-one threads holding a small town together over a gap it preferred not to look down. All of it in her hand now, every page of the last three years. Her father had built the coral reef; she was its polyps.

*If anything happens to me,* he had written, in the only instruction he ever gave her outright, tucked into the flyleaf like a man hiding a key under a mat, *the book goes to no one. Not to the magistrate, not to the guild, not to any officer wearing any color. Names in a book are only safe while the book stays shut.*

She had read that line a hundred times and thought she understood it the way you understand weather reports about far-off coastlines.

She locked the ledger in the press cabinet — the proofing press beside it, her father's last purchase, heavy as sin and used mostly as a table, since proofs were a luxury for shops with apprentices — banked the stove, hung the CLOSED IN ACCORDANCE WITH THE SABBATH sign even though it was Tuesday, because it discouraged late questions, and ate bread and hard cheese standing up, which her mother, whoever she had been, would presumably have objected to.

From the window above the binding corner she could see the river, going dark, and the ferry lights coming across, and beyond the north gate the last grey strip of the north road, empty as far as the bend.

It was not empty, she would learn soon enough. Something was coming down it with drums. But that evening it looked like any road looks at dusk: like it led away from here, and whatever traveled it belonged to elsewhere, and elsewhere had never once, in nineteen years, managed to find her door.

She trimmed the lamp, touched the cabinet lock once with two fingers — a superstition she'd deny under oath — and went down to sleep in the room behind the type cases, where the smell of lead rose faint and constant all night, patient in the dark, waiting to be made into letters.

* * *

The knocking began before dawn, and it was not Madame Perrin.

It came from below, at the street door — fists, many fists, and voices with the flat carrying tone of men trained to be heard over gunfire — and Dominic came awake with her heart already going and lay still in the dark, counting. Through the floorboards she could feel, rather than hear, boots. Boots in the street, boots in numbers, boots in a rhythm that had nothing casual in it.

Someone in the street began beating a drum, unhurried, like a heart that had made arrangements.

Dominic sat up in the dark with the taste of lead still in her mouth, and listened to the world she'd read about in other people's broadsheets arrive, at last, at hers.
