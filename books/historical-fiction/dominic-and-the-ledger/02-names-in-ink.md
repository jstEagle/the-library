## 2. Names in Ink

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width before Dominic Yarrow finished with the day's accounts, and by that hour the print shop smelled of tallow, lampblack, and the particular metallic sweetness of lead type that had been handled all day by warm hands. She liked the shop best at this hour. In daylight it belonged to customers, to the landlady, to the fiction of Master Yarrow; at night it belonged only to her and to the ledger, which lay open on the counting desk like a patient animal waiting to be fed.

She dipped her pen.

The entry was a small one, as these things went. *Baptiste Hurel, cooper, debt of eleven francs four sous, incurred Michelmass two years past — forgiven.* She hesitated over the word *forgiven*, as she always did, because forgiveness sounded like something a church did, or a saint, and she was neither. What she actually did was quieter than forgiveness. She ruled a line through the figure in such a way that the ruling looked accidental — a smudge, an ink blot, the kind of thing that happens to any book kept by human hands over years — and then she wrote *paid in goods, sawdust and barrel staves, value accepted* in the margin, which was true in the sense that she had once accepted a barrel of good nails from Baptiste's cousin, and true enough for any purpose that mattered.

Her father would have called it cooking the books. He'd taught her the phrase himself, laughing, his big cutter's hands dusted with lead. *A ledger is only honest if someone cooks it now and then, little one. Otherwise it's just cruelty with arithmetic.*

Dominic blew on the wet ink and turned the page.

The trouble with keeping Vellamont's secrets was that there were so many of them, and they were all kept in one place, and that one place was currently being guarded by a girl of nineteen who had eaten nothing since noon but bread ends and who could not tell a soul about any of it. Not the widow Marchand, whose name appeared in the book under three separate spellings, only one of which was real. Not old Père Anselme, who came every Thursday for his newspaper and left his debts behind like shed coats. Not even the moon, which was looking in through the frost-furred window with the particular attentiveness of a witness.

She turned to the aliases.

This was the part of the ledger no tax man, no magistrate, no curious landlady would ever be shown, though it took up nearly a quarter of the pages. It began years ago, when her father was alive, with a single entry in his hand: *René Fouchard of the ferry road shall henceforth be recorded as René Fabre,* followed by no explanation whatsoever, because her father had understood instinctively that explanations were the first thing to rot. The entries had multiplied since. Vellamont sat on a river, and rivers bring not only barges but histories, and some men arrive in a town carrying a warrant behind them the way a cart carries dust.

Tonight there were two new names to enter.

The first was simple enough. A young woman calling herself Madame Delacour had arrived in the autumn and taken rooms above the wool merchant's, teaching pianoforte to the daughters of anyone who could pay, and Dominic had discovered within a fortnight — because discovery was simply what she did, sitting behind the shop counter with her ledgers and her unremarkable, forgettable face — that Madame Delacour's true name was Anouk Vidal, and that there existed in Marseille a warrant describing a forger of exactly her height, her age, and her singular talent at the keyboard, since it is difficult to forge documents without excellent hands.

*Madame Delacour, music teacher,* Dominic wrote, *is Madame Delacour.* And beneath it, smaller: *rent paid quarterly through this shop, receipts issued in the name of A. Delacour, all correct.*

That last phrase — *all correct* — was her father's, and it did more work in the ledger than any other three words in it. It meant: I have checked. It meant: the story holds. It meant: whoever reads this page will find a tidy little fiction wearing the clothes of fact, buttoned up properly, and will never know the difference unless I tell them.

The second name was harder.

Tomasz Kroll had been the town's farrier for six years, longer than Dominic had been keeping the ledger alone. He was a broad, slow-spoken man who shod half the horses on the river road and never once, in six years, had asked Master Yarrow for credit — a restraint so unusual that it had made Dominic suspicious within a month of taking the book over, because in her experience the people who never asked for anything were the ones who couldn't afford to be looked at closely. She had found nothing, though. Nothing at all, for six years, until Tuesday, when a letter had come up from the south addressed in a hand that trembled, and Tomasz had read it standing in the street outside the shop, and then had come inside, very quietly, and stood before the counter with his hat in his fists like a boy at a funeral.

"I need," he had said, in his careful French, "for my name to be a different name. For a while. Perhaps for always."

He had not explained, and Dominic had not asked, and this mutual silence was itself a kind of currency in Vellamont, more stable than francs. But he had told her the new name — Anton Keller, a good solid German-French name, the sort of name you could set in type without anyone looking twice at it — and he had pressed two coins across the counter, silver, older than either of them.

"For the ink," he'd said. "I know there's a cost."

"There's no cost," Dominic had begun, because there wasn't, there never was, but Tomasz had fixed her with the look of a man who had spent six years paying for everything honestly precisely because he could not afford to owe anyone anything.

"There's a cost," he repeated. "There's always a cost. I'd rather pay you."

So now she entered him. *Anton Keller, farrier, is Anton Keller,* she wrote, and her pen slowed over the date, because dates in the alias section were dangerous things — they marked the moment a man became someone else, and moments like that should not be written down too precisely. She gave the entry the same date as a routine invoice for pamphlet paper two entries above, so that if the book were ever read by unfriendly eyes, the alias would sit buried among deliveries like a single grey fish in a school of them.

Then, because she could not help herself, because she was her father's daughter down to the marrow: *all correct.*

She sat back and flexed her writing hand and looked out at the dark window, where her own reflection waited politely, as it always did, for her to notice it. A thin young man with ink on his fingers and his hair tied back. That was the other entry, the one that appeared nowhere in the book because it could not. Somewhere — she had never learned where, her father had taken the arrangement with him into whatever country the dead inhabit — there were papers saying that Dominic Yarrow had died of the fever at age nine, and that his child, a son, Dominique, had survived. Her father had carried the fiction so carefully that even the parish priest believed it, or claimed to, and priests could be paid to believe things, which was its own entry in a manner of speaking.

Nineteen years old, and she had been Master Yarrow longer than she had been anyone else.

"It's not so strange," she said aloud to the shop, because talking to oneself after midnight was another of the perquisites of concealment. The type cases along the wall said nothing, ten thousand small letters asleep in their compartments, each one identical except for the mark on its face. Her father used to say the type was the most democratic thing ever made — that a lowercase i and a capital W weighed nearly nothing between them, that lead didn't care what name it printed. He had found this very moving. Dominic found it mostly cold at eleven o'clock at night, but she was tired.

She counted through the day's receipts again, out of habit rather than need. Three sales of the weekly gazette. One job of wedding invitations for the mayor's niece, forty-eight cards at six sous, paid in advance because mayors' families paid in advance and everyone else paid eventually. Two hundred handbills for the grain merchant advertising a price of flour that Dominic privately knew to be fictional, since she had seen the grain merchant's own figures in this very ledger and knew his flour cost more than he admitted and that he was quietly borrowing against next season to keep this season smiling. She had advanced him the loan herself, from the shop's reserve, at an interest rate of nothing, repayable in flour, at prices to be determined by hunger.

Vellamont ran through this book the way the river ran under the bridge: continuously, mostly invisibly, and with occasional flooding.

It was a thought that arrived some nights with warmth and some nights with vertigo, and tonight it arrived wearing both coats at once. Because here was the thing she almost never let herself think clearly, the thing that lived at the bottom of the ledger drawer under the spare nibs: every secret in this book was safe exactly as long as she was safe. Forty-one households — she had counted, more than once, the counting itself being a guilty pleasure she indulged in the way other people indulged in sweet wine — forty-one households in and around Vellamont owed their present peace, their unremarked lives, their *all correct*, to entries in a book kept by a person who did not legally exist.

If Master Yarrow were ever found out — and *found out* meant two discoveries braided together, both fatal — the book would not merely fall into the wrong hands. It would become, overnight, a confession written in forty-one different hands. Every alias an admission. Every forgiven debt an irregularity for some clerk to pick at. Every quiet kindness reinterpreted, in the flat light of an official reading, as evidence of conspiracy.

She had constructed, over nine years, a machine for protecting her neighbors. She had also constructed, whether she liked it or not, a machine for destroying them, and both machines were operated by the same key, and the key slept in a nightshirt in the loft above the shop with ink still under its fingernails.

"Stop it," she told herself firmly, and blew out the candle, and then relit it, because she hadn't finished.

One more entry. There was always one more entry.

This one she wrote slowly, and it was the closest thing the ledger contained to extravagance. *Widow Marchand — bread, two loaves weekly, winter term — paid.* The truth underneath, which the entry wore like a loose garment, was that the widow Marchand's bread had been free since the first hard frost, and would remain free, and that the widow Marchand would threaten Dominic with eternal damnation if she ever caught her doing it, and that therefore the fiction of payment must be maintained by both parties with the seriousness of liturgy. Marchand paid. Dominic received payment. What form the payment took varied — once it had been a fierce lecture on the proper storage of onions, delivered at length — but it was paid, oh yes, scrupulously.

Some debts in this book were settled in money. Some in silence. Some in onions.

Dominic sanded the page, closed the ledger — it was a heavy thing, bound in calf leather gone soft as a dog's ear, with brass corners worn to shine — and carried it to the back room, where it lived in a locked drawer inside a cupboard behind the stone sink, which was itself behind a door that stuck, all of this being security of a layered and mostly psychological kind. The real lock was the sticking door. No stranger had ever gotten through that door quickly enough to matter.

As she pushed the drawer shut, she heard, faintly, through two floors of old timber, the sound of Madame Delacour above the wool merchant's practicing her scales. Even at this distance, even muffled by snow-thick night, the playing had a quality Dominic had noticed before and could now put a word to: it was the playing of someone listening for footsteps. Every scale ended a half-beat early, as if apologizing for existing.

Dominic listened to it for a moment with an odd tightening in her chest — sympathy, she supposed, and recognition, and something colder underneath, the thought she had been walking around all evening finally catching up with her on the stairs.

Two of us in one small town now, hiding in plain hearing of each other. And how many others? How many names in this book were living within a mile of this shop, lying awake as she was, doing their own arithmetic of exposure?

She banked the fire, took up the candle, and climbed to the loft. Below her, through the floorboards, the shop held its breath around its ten thousand letters. Somewhere north, she knew — everyone knew, it was in the gazettes she printed herself — armies were moving. That was the great luxury of a river town: wars happened elsewhere, upstream, downstream, in cities whose names arrived in newsprint and stayed safely flat on the page.

*Ink outlives men,* her father used to say, hammering a letterform home, delighted with himself. He had meant it as comfort. Lying in the dark with the ledger's drawer three floors below her, Dominic turned the sentence over and found it had a second face, the way coins do.

Ink outlived men. All of it. The forgiving entries and the damning ones alike. Every *all correct* she had ever written was already outliving her decision-making, drying into permanence one stroke at a time, and there was no unmaking any of it, and she would not have unmade any of it, and this — pride and terror in equal measure, poured into the same mold — was simply what her life was now.

She fell asleep composing, as she often did, imaginary entries for imaginary troubles, and so she did not hear, and would not remember hearing, the distant sound of many boots on the ferry road, arriving with the dawn.

* * *

In the morning the landlady came.

Madame Gobin arrived, as she always did, at the stroke of nine, with the punctuality of a clock that suspected other clocks of slacking, and Dominic met her downstairs with the shop swept, the counter cleared, and the day's innocent face on — sleeves rolled, apron dusted with ink, the whole costume of a busy tradesman's son who had no thoughts in his head beyond type and invoices.

"You look pale, Dominique." Madame Gobin pronounced it the way she pronounced everything, as an accusation. "Pale and thin. You work too hard. Where is your father this morning?"

"Asleep, madame. He worked late on accounts."

"Accounts!" Madame Gobin's eyebrows performed their usual ascent, the ascent they made every morning, having never once in nine years reached the summit of actual suspicion. "A printer who does accounts at night. In my experience it is the accounts that keep a man up at night, and never in a good way." She peered past Dominic into the shop with the frank appetite of a woman who had been trying to see inside Master Yarrow's private affairs for nearly a decade and had succeeded only in seeing counters, chairs, and a great deal of type. "And his health?"

"Mended, thank God. He takes the waters."

"The waters. Yes." This was the ritual. The waters. Madame Gobin had never established where the waters were, exactly, and had apparently decided that asking directly would be vulgar, and so for nine years Master Yarrow had been taking unspecified waters at an undisclosed location with results that were always described as *mending*. "Well. Tell him the stove chimney draws badly when the wind is north. Tell him I said so."

"I'll tell him."

"And Dominique—" She paused in the doorway, and for one lurching instant Dominic felt the whole architecture of nine years sway like a shelf in an earthquake — "that boy from the mill asked after you again. I told him you're promised to the trade. A boy married to his letters, I told her— to him. To the trade."

"Entirely, madame," said Dominic gravely. "I don't have time to court anything but the alphabet."

Madame Gobin laughed, satisfied, and went off up the street to inspect someone else's life, and Dominic stood in the doorway breathing again, and noticed that the frost on the bridge railings glittered unusually bright, and that the light coming down the river road had a strange, hard, moving quality to it that she had never seen on an ordinary morning.

Then she heard the drums.

They were still a long way off — a sound like a slow heart beating under the hills — but they were on the ferry road, and they were getting closer, and behind them, catching the sun, moved a long line of steel that was most definitely not flour barges.

Dominic went back inside and locked the drawer.
