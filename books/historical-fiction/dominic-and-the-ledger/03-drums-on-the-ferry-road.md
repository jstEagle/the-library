## 3. Drums on the Ferry Road

The drumming began, as most troubles in Vellamont did, somewhere out past the tanneries.

Dominic heard it first as a nuisance — some journeyman practicing for a wedding, she thought, and badly. She was up to her elbows in the wash tub, scrubbing ink from the composing stone with a brush that had once, in a prouder life, been her father's best sable, and the last thing she wanted was a wedding. Weddings meant orders for invitations, which meant money, but they also meant the landlady Madame Perrin would come upstairs to ask why Master Yarrow never attended any of them, and Dominic would have to invent a fresh complaint — his stomach, his eyes, his profound disappointment in the bride's family — while her hands remembered how badly they wanted to shake.

The drumming grew closer. And underneath it there was another sound, one her body knew before her mind did: boots. Not two pairs or six, but dozens of pairs, striking the ferry road in time.

She dried her hands on her apron and went to the window.

They came along the river road in column, gray-green coats gone the color of wet slate in the drizzle, bayonets catching what little light October bothered to offer. An officer rode at the front on a horse too fine for the weather, sitting very straight, as though straightness alone could keep the rain off. Behind him came wagons, then more soldiers, then a second band of drummers who were, if anything, worse than the first.

"Well," said Dominic to the empty shop, "there goes the neighborhood."

* * *

By noon the town square had transformed itself the way a body transforms under fever — same shape, wrong temperature. Soldiers had requisitioned the salt warehouse by proclamation nailed to the church door, which struck Dominic as a poor choice of venue, since half of Vellamont couldn't read and the other half read slowly and took offense at being rushed. She stood at the edge of the crowd with her cap pulled low and listened while the cooper Fenn read it aloud in the booming voice he usually saved for disputing invoices.

"BY ORDER OF THE ARMY OF THE REPUBLIC—" Fenn paused to spit, considered his audience, and thought better of it, "—all warehouses, cellars, and covered structures are subject to requisition for the provisioning of troops. All householders shall furnish lodging according to their means. All commercial records — ledgers, accounts, books of debt, bills of sale — shall be surrendered to the commission appointed at the customhouse within three days, that the just dues of the Republic may be assessed."

He stopped. The crowd made the sound crowds make when a sentence ends and nobody wants to be the first to admit they understood it.

"Three days," said Widow Marchand, at Dominic's elbow. She said it the way other people say *murderer*.

"That's what he read," Dominic offered.

"I heard what he read. I have ears, boy." Marchand's eyes were fixed on the proclamation as though she could set it alight by disapproval alone. "Three days."

Dominic's heart had begun doing something complicated against her ribs, and she made her face do nothing at all, which was the first skill her father had ever taught her, before type-cutting, before anything. *A printer's face is a blank forme, Dom. You set what you like on it and nobody reads the wrong thing.*

"Come along," Marchand said, and gripped her sleeve with fingers like a bookbinder's clamp, and towed her out of the square.

* * *

"They want ledgers," Marchand said, once they were inside her kitchen, which smelled of yesterday's bread and forty years of stubbornness. "Why would an army want ledgers? Armies want grain and boots and young men. Ledgers are for priests and lawyers and other people nobody has ever shot on purpose."

"Perhaps things have changed," said Dominic, who knew perfectly well why an army wanted ledgers, and had known since the moment Fenn reached the word *surrendered*, and had spent the walk over trying to think about anything else.

An army wanted ledgers because a town's accounts were a town's confession. Every name in her father's book — every debt forgiven, every alias entered in her careful hand, every payment marked *received in full, no further inquiry* next to the names of men hiding from warrants ten and fifteen years old — was evidence now. Not gossip. Evidence, with her handwriting on it like a signature on a gallows ticket.

Forty-one names. She had never counted before. She counted now, standing in Marchand's kitchen not hearing whatever the widow was saying about the moral character of officers, and the number arrived in her head with the finality of a coin dropped down a well: forty-one, including the widow herself, including the alias *Marchand, V., otherwise Verchère*, entered eleven years ago in her father's hand, back when Dominic had still been allowed to be a girl in public.

"—and you'll tell your father," Marchand finished, in the tone of a woman who had just assigned him a chore.

"I'll tell him," said Dominic, which was true, in its way. She told him everything.

* * *

The walk back up Water Street gave her time to take inventory of the catastrophe, which she conducted with the methodical despair of a woman counting spoons after a burglary.

Item: the ledger sat in the shop's back room, where it had sat for nineteen years, fat as a Bible and twice as damning.

Item: Madame Perrin would be wanting to know whether Master Yarrow intended to comply with the proclamation, since Madame Perrin's own accounts — kept, in a fit of modernity, by Master Yarrow himself, on the understanding that a landlady's rents looked more regular when a professional hand wrote them — contained certain creative arithmetic regarding the number of persons residing in the house.

Item: the billets. Soldiers in the house. Soldiers in the *house*, with the press, and the type cases, and the loose sheet in the drawer of the desk that said, in Dominic's hand, *Perrin, M. — rent received to Michaelmas, actual figure withheld by agreement,* and beside it a small drawing of a cat that Dominic had done when she was bored.

Item: three days.

"You look like a man who has been asked to bury a horse," said Odile the ferryman, hosing down her deck as Dominic passed. Odile was seventy if she was a day and had arms like mooring rope and opinions about the Republic that she shared freely, at volume, having concluded years ago that at her age prosecution was a compliment.

"They've come for records," Dominic said.

"Hah. Records." Odile directed the hose at a spotless patch of deck. "I gave my records to the river twenty years ago. Best archivist there is. Never once lost a page, never once informed on anybody." She squinted at Dominic. "You keep books, don't you? For the shop?"

"For the shop," Dominic agreed, which was a sentence so technically true it deserved a medal.

"Mm," said Odile, who missed nothing and prosecuted less, and went back to her hose, and Dominic walked on feeling the peculiar gratitude one feels toward people who know things and decline to say them.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble in Vellamont generally did, on her own doorstep, where Madame Perrin was waiting with her shawl pinned and her mouth already loaded.

"Well?" said Madame Perrin.

"Well what?"

"Don't you *well what* me, Dominique." The name came out low and quick; Perrin only used it when no one living was within earshot, which was now, since Water Street had emptied itself toward the square. "Soldiers. In the square. Proclamation on the church. Three days to hand over accounts, and I have accounts written by a print shop that officially does not exist because its master is a recluse with weak eyes, and you will explain to me what we are to do."

"We are going to do nothing dramatic," Dominic said, unlocking the door. "We're going to be dull. Dullness is armor. Come inside before someone sees you talking to me like a person."

Madame Perrin followed her in, muttering. Inside, the shop smelled of ink and cold iron and the particular dry sweetness of paper that had lived near lead for two decades — the smell Dominic privately thought of as home, and which she supposed a wine merchant felt about his cellar. The press stood in the middle of the floor like a patient ox. The type cases lined the wall in their ranks. And through the doorway to the back room, on its shelf, the ledger sat with its brass clasps and its swollen spine, looking — Dominic was almost certain — smug.

"The billets," Perrin said. "If they put soldiers in here—"

"They won't put soldiers in a print shop. Too much ink, too many sharp tools, and nothing to steal that anyone can fence." Dominic hoped this was true. She made it sound true. Setting type and setting one's face: the same trade, her father used to say, only the letters had fewer opinions.

"And your father's accounts?"

"Will be surrendered," Dominic said, "like everyone else's. A copy, properly made, of ordinary figures. Rent in, wages out, paper purchased, nothing interesting."

"And the other book?"

"There is no other book," said Dominic, and went to fetch the good lamp, because dusk was coming, and because if her hands were busy trimming the wick they could not go anywhere near the back room, and if her hands were safe then perhaps her mind would follow.

It did not follow. Her mind stayed in the back room, doing sums: three days minus the day already spent, minus a day to copy honest figures into a decoy book without arousing suspicion by producing it too promptly, left — she did the sum twice — almost no time at all, and forty-one names, and one pair of hands.

* * *

That night she did not sleep so much as lie down near sleep and listen to it snore elsewhere.

Somewhere past midnight — that hour when the town belongs to cats and conscience — she got up, lit the stub of candle she kept for exactly these transactions with herself, and carried the ledger to the worktable. She did not open it. She sat with her hand flat on the cover instead, the way you'd steady a frightened animal, and felt through the leather the faint ridges of nineteen years of entries pressing back against her palm.

Her father had started the book the year the shop opened, in a hand so fine it looked printed. *Yarrow, A., Printer & Typefounder, Vellamont.* Below that, the first entry: two francs owed to a paper merchant, repaid in full within the month, boring as bread. The book had gotten interesting later, the way rivers get deep gradually, one tributary at a time. A widow's debt crossed out with the notation *forgiven — she paid enough before.* A name entered twice, once real and once not, with the real one scored through so thoroughly the paper had thinned. Payments received *in full settlement* from men whose full settlements coincided suspiciously with the departure of magistrates' clerks.

And in her own hand, seven years of it now, since the week after the funeral when she had buried her father and her girlhood in the same fortnight and picked up the pen because somebody had to: *received of T. Fenn, cooper, balance on coffin plates, he overpaid, say nothing.* She remembered writing it. She remembered smiling while she wrote it. Fenn had overpaid because Fenn could not bear the thought of owing a dead man money, and she had entered the excess as credit against future work, which was charity dressed as bookkeeping, which was, she suspected, the only kind of charity that survived winter.

Forty-one names. Some of them innocent as Sunday. Some of them guilty of nothing worse than being born with the wrong name in the wrong year. All of them hers now — her flock, her freight, her ballast — and a proclamation nailed to a church door offering three days to drown them.

The candle guttered. Outside, on the quay, a sentry coughed, and the sound of it — a young man's cough, ordinary, human, bored — frightened her more than the drums had. Drums were weather. A cough outside her window at two in the morning was a neighbor.

She closed the ledger and carried it back to the shelf, and stood a moment in the dark with her hand still on it, and heard her father's voice as clearly as if the grave had a speaking tube: *Ink outlives men, Dom. So does ink's opposite. Everything you write is either going to feed somebody or hang somebody, so choose before you dip the pen.*

"I chose," she whispered. "I chose years ago. It's only now everybody else is finding out."

Then she banked the fire, and set the decoy account book on the desk where a conscientious clerk would find it, and went at last to bed, where she slept like a stone with a warrant out on it.

* * *

Morning brought the billet after all.

He arrived at nine, apologetic even in silhouette — a boy of maybe eighteen, gray-green coat a size and a half too large, carrying his kit and his orders like a man transporting someone else's breakables. He read the order aloud in bad French to Madame Perrin, who received it with the expression of a duchess accepting a delivery of manure, and installed himself in the parlor, and turned out to be named Gaspard, and to be terrified of Madame Perrin, which Dominic filed away as a strategic asset.

By ten the requisitions had reached the shops. By eleven an officer with a clipboard and the weary eyes of a man who had counted too many other men's property stood in the doorway of the print shop itself, and Dominic's heart performed a maneuver she declined to acknowledge.

"Printer?" said the officer.

"Typefounder, mostly," Dominic said, bowing the small bow she'd practiced since she was nine. "Master Yarrow is unwell. I keep the books."

Something flickered behind the officer's tiredness — interest, or indigestion. "You keep the books."

"Every figure, monsieur. Rent, wages, metal, paper." She gestured at the decoy ledger, lying open on the desk precisely as arranged, all its numbers dull as ditchwater. "The shop's affairs are simple. We sell letters. Nobody needs ours to count anything worth counting."

The officer looked at the decoy ledger. He looked at it for a long moment, and Dominic stood in the doorway of her own life wearing her blank forme of a face and discovered that terror, fully matured, tastes like copper and patience.

"Records are to be surrendered at the customhouse within three days," he said finally. "All of them. The proclamation was specific."

"Ours will be there, monsieur."

"And Master Yarrow will certify them personally? The commission requires the principal."

There it was, the hook under the bait. Dominic bowed again, deeper. "My father will certify anything I tell him to, monsieur. His eyes are very bad and his trust in me is very good. If the commission requires a blind old man carried through the rain to sign his name, I'll bring him Tuesday and we can all regret it together."

A pause. Then, unexpectedly, the officer's mouth twitched — the twitch of a man who had once been a person and occasionally remembered it. "Tuesday," he said. "Very well. Wednesday, if the rain continues." He made his mark on the clipboard, turned to go, and paused at the threshold with his back to her.

"The commandant," he said, without turning around, "is a reasonable man. He believes burning is cleaner than hanging. But he will read what you surrender, clerk, and everything in it becomes true the moment he reads it. Whatever is in those books of yours — consider, before Wednesday, what you want to be true."

He went out into the drizzle. Dominic stood among her type cases with her heart going like a galley drum, and after a while she became aware that Madame Perrin was in the doorway to the stairs, pale as fresh paper.

"He knows," Perrin whispered. "Whoever that was. He knows there's something."

"Everyone in this town knows there's something," Dominic said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Nobody knows what. That's the whole architecture of the place, and I built half of it." She looked at the door the officer had left ajar, at the gray light and the distant sound of boots on stone, at the world she had always believed happened somewhere else, arriving at last at her threshold with its drums and its clipboards and its terrible, reasonable face.

Three days. Forty-one names. One book.

She went to the back room and took the ledger down off the shelf, and held it against her chest, and began — for the first time in her life — to think about how a person steals from an entire army.
