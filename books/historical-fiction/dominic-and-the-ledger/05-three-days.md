## 5. Three Days

The proclamation went up on the market cross at noon, and by half past one my shop smelled of fear.

Fear, as it happens, has a particular scent in a print shop: sweat over ink, breath over paper, and underneath it all the faint tallow-and-onion smell of men who had run to Vellamont without stopping to wash. They came through my door in a trickle that became a stream — the cooper first, then his brother-in-law, then three of the Perrin cousins, then a man I only knew as Anselm who had been calling himself Anselm for eleven years and whose real name I kept in the ledger under a different letter entirely.

"Master Yarrow," said the cooper, hat in hand, addressing the empty air behind me the way everyone did. It was an arrangement the town had made with itself long ago: Master Yarrow was ill, or traveling, or at prayer, or simply shy, and his daughter handled the business. Nobody believed it. Everybody agreed to it. That was Vellamont all over — a town that survived on fictions the way other towns survived on fish.

"He won't see anyone today," I said. "What do you want?"

"You've heard. The proclamation." He wiped his palms on his apron, which was already more rag than apron. "Three days. All commercial records surrendered to the requisition officers. All debts, all accounts, all—" he swallowed— "all names."

"All names," I repeated, and let it sit there between us like a coin nobody wanted to pick up.

The cooper had two names. Well — one name and one alias, entered in the ledger in my father's hand fourteen years ago when he'd come over the border ahead of a warrant from the old duke's courts, and my father, who believed that ink outlived men but that men should be given time to outlive their warrants, had recorded him under a name that belonged to no one at all. *Josse Ferrant, called Perrin the elder, debt of twelve livres, forgiven at Midwinter.* Twelve livres and a whole life, forgiven in one line.

"I only want my page," the cooper said. "Torn out. Clean. No one would know."

"No one would know," echoed his brother-in-law, who wanted the same thing, and then the Perrin cousins wanted it too, and Anselm stood by the door turning his cap like a man churning butter.

I held up my hands. "Gentlemen. Do you know what this book looks like?"

They looked at me blankly.

"It looks like a ledger," I said. "It does not look like a ledger with pages missing. A book missing pages announces itself. A sergeant opens it, sees torn stubs, and asks why. And what answer will you give him? That you tore them? He'll want to know what was on them, and then your clean name becomes the dirtiest one in Vellamont."

The cooper opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "Then what?"

"Then nothing," I said. "The army wants records of commerce. Debts. Sales. What they'll find is debts and sales. They have forty thousand soldiers to feed and no interest in a cooper's grandmother's maiden name."

This was, I confess, perhaps sixty percent true, and I delivered it with the confidence of the full hundred. My father taught me that. *When you sell a lie, Dominic, price it high enough that people are embarrassed to question it.*

They filed out somewhat comforted, and I turned the sign to *closed*, which I had never once done during business hours, and went upstairs to think.

* * *

Thinking lasted until the bread arrived.

Baker Huet did not deliver his own bread; he had boys for that. So when Huet himself appeared at my door at dusk carrying two loaves so white and fine they could only have been baked for someone he wanted something from, I knew exactly what he wanted.

"Master Yarrow's daughter," he said warmly, presenting the loaves like ambassadors. "From my own oven. Not the ordinary stock."

"Baker Huet. You haven't brought bread yourself since my father's funeral, and then you tried to bill us for the mourning loaves."

"A misunderstanding of the moment," he said grandly, sweeping past me into the shop as though invited. His eyes went immediately, hungrily, to the counter where the ledger sat. "Times are strange. Strange times make strange friends. I thought — neighbors ought to stand together."

"What flavor of together?"

He leaned close, breathing flour and brandy. "There is an entry," he said. "Dated some years back. Concerning a shipment that came upriver without paying its full duties, and certain names attached to it, and my name among those names not as a principal but as a — a facilitator."

"A smuggler," I said.

"*A merchant of flexible goods,*" he corrected. "And if that entry were to become illegible — water damage is common in river towns, ink fades, mice—" 

"How much?"

He blinked. "How much what?"

"How much are you offering? You didn't bring eighty-sous bread to haggle in circles."

He drew himself up, offended, then deflated, then produced a purse from inside his coat with the reluctance of a man pulling a tooth. "Twenty livres."

"The entry stays where it is."

"Thirty."

"Huet." I picked up one of his beautiful loaves and handed both back to him. "In three days the army takes every record in this town. Every account book, every invoice, every letter of credit. And every single one of those books will show the same shipment you're worried about, because you weren't clever enough to keep it out of your own ledgers either. Tearing one page from mine saves you nothing. It only saves me nothing, which is a worse trade."

He stared at me. "You're refusing money."

"I'm refusing *stupidity*, which is rarer and worth more." I opened the door for him. "Eat your bread. Pray the sergeants can't read."

He left in a huff, and I confess I watched him go with a certain satisfaction, because there is a pleasure in refusing a bribe that one cannot afford to refuse, and it is almost worth the poverty. Almost.

Then Widow Marchand came, and the satisfaction went out of me like heat from an open window.

* * *

She did not knock. Marchand never knocked; she arrived, the way weather arrives. She was seventy-one years old and walked like a woman who had buried a husband, two sons, and every illusion she'd ever owned, and had found the exercise clarifying.

She sat down uninvited at the counter and folded her hands. "Girl."

"Widow."

"You know what I've come about."

"The army. Yes."

"Not the army." Her eyes were pale and terrible. "The army is three days away from being everyone's problem. I've come about *my* problem, which lives on page thirty-one of your father's book."

Page thirty-one. I knew it the way you know your own hands. *Marie Marchand, born Marie Delacour of Saint-Aubin. Wife of the elder Marchand, deceased. Entered 1779 under warrant of the Bishop's court, charge of forgery. Name amended 1781 by private settlement. Debt: none recorded. Obligation: ongoing.*

My father had written "obligation: ongoing" because Marchand insisted on it. She liked her debts visible. She said invisible debts bred contempt.

"They'll hang me," she said flatly. "Or send me south, which is slower hanging. A forged name from the old church courts, in the year of our Lord whatever-it-was? To these new ones, that's not a crime, it's an *appetite*. They'll want to know who else changed names. They'll want the whole page. The whole book."

"So you want it torn out."

"I want it *gone*. Burned, soaked, eaten. I don't care." She leaned forward. "And if you tell me about torn stubs announcing themselves, girl, understand me: I have watched men die for less than what's written in that book, and I will watch you too, if it comes to it. Tear out the page or I will go to the requisition officer myself and tell him Master Yarrow's daughter keeps secrets in her cellar."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere outside, an army drum practiced a march, patient as rain.

"You would burn the whole town to save yourself," I said.

"I would burn the whole town to save myself," she agreed pleasantly. "That is what being seventy-one teaches you. Now. The page."

I looked at her a long moment. And here is the thing I could not explain to her, could not explain to anyone, the thing that had been growing in my chest since the drums came up the ferry road: the page could not be spared, because no page could be spared, because I had gone upstairs that afternoon and actually counted.

"There is no page thirty-one," I said.

Her face didn't move. "Don't lie to me, girl."

"I'm not. Sit still." I unlocked the drawer, took out the ledger, and laid it open on the counter between us, and for once I was glad to show it, glad to have a witness. "Look. Page after page. Forty-one people in this town living under names your priests and magistrates gave them warrants for. Forty-one. I counted this afternoon while you were all lining up at my door with your bribes and your threats. Cooper Perrin. Anselm — real name Bors. The ferryman Odile, whose debt was expunged in '84. Your own page, widow, sitting right there where my father wrote it. And do you know what happens if I tear out any one of them?"

She said nothing. Her jaw was set like a keel.

"The book stops matching itself. Cross-references break. My father numbered everything, cross-filed everything — he was a type cutter, precision was his religion. A missing page isn't a hole in the record. It's a wound, and wounds attract eyes. But more than that—" and here my voice did something I hadn't planned, cracked slightly, and I hated it and let it happen— "more than that, each page is the only copy. There are no duplicates. There was never time to make duplicates, there was never money for a second book, and now there is no time at all. If I tear out yours, you might survive the army and die of the bishop's old warrant anyway, someday, somewhere, because the proof that you were ever forgiven exists nowhere but here. If the book burns, all forty-one of you become forgers again. Forever."

Marchand stared down at the open ledger. At her own name, in my father's upright, careful hand. Something moved behind her face — not softness, exactly. Arithmetic.

"You could copy it," she said at last, grudging. "Three days. You copy fast."

"Two hundred and forty pages, four entries to most, marginal notes in my father's shorthand that took me two years to learn." I shook my head. "Even if I wrote day and night I'd finish half, and a half-copy is a death warrant with better handwriting. No. The original is the only thing that matters. The original has to survive."

"And how does a printer's daughter make a ledger survive three days and an army?" she asked. "Will you talk it to death? Bribe it with bread?"

"I don't know yet," I admitted.

Something almost like respect crossed her face, quickly smothered. She stood, straightened her shawl with two sharp tugs, and looked at me the way she looked at underweight loaves — assessing whether I was worth the trouble.

"Then you had better learn," she said. "Because I withdraw my threat, girl — temporarily. Not from kindness. From arithmetic. My forgiveness lives in that book, and so my life lives in that book, and so it seems I am reduced to trusting a half-grown boy who isn't a boy." She jabbed a finger at me. "But hear this: if you fail, if you lose that book or let it fall into their hands whole, I will not go down alone. I will swear before God and the requisition officers that you forged every name in it yourself, and I will do it convincingly, because I have had practice."

"Thank you, widow."

"Don't thank me. Survive." She swept toward the door, paused, and added, without turning around: "Your father would have made a better liar than you. He'd have promised me anything."

"He did promise you anything, once," I said. "It's in the margin of page thirty-one. *Obligation: ongoing.* He trusted you to collect honestly."

For a moment she was silent. Then she snorted — the closest thing to laughter Marchand permitted in public — and was gone into the dark.

* * *

I locked the door, shuttered the windows, and sat down at the counter with the ledger and a candle, and for a long time I did nothing but breathe.

Panic is a strange houseguest. It arrives politely, sits quietly for hours while you deal with bakers and widows, and then, the moment you're alone, it goes through all your cupboards and turns over all your furniture and whispers in your ear that you are going to die in this shop, that the book is going to burn or worse, be *read*, that forty-one names will become forty-one trials and one of the names read out in the square will be *Dominic Yarrow, called Dominique, daughter of Emile, who lived five years under her dead father's name and signed his signature to contracts the whole while*, which is forgery of a kind no tribunal will find quaint.

I let it rampage. My father said panic was like setting type: useless loose, but if you could get hold of the letters, order them, they'd carry weight.

So. Order the letters.

First: the army wanted records. In three days they would come for them, probably with carts and sergeants and lists, efficiently, the way armies do everything — including burning. Whatever I chose to do, it had to survive a search. Hiding it outside the shop meant moving it past soldiers. Hiding it inside the shop meant hiding it from men trained to find things, in a building the size of a barn with all the subtlety of a barn.

Second: I could not copy it. That road was closed. The original, whole, entire, two hundred and forty pages of other people's mercy, had to remain exactly itself.

Third — and this was the letter that refused to sit straight in the stick — third, whatever I did, I would likely have to do it knowing that Ilse Brandt existed.

I had seen her twice since the army came. Once on the ferry road, sketching, her greatcoat collar up against the wind. Once at the well, where she had watched me haul water with an expression I couldn't read — not the soldiers' way of looking at the Yarrow boy, which saw nothing at all, but something more attentive. She was a cartographer. Her entire profession was noticing where things were and writing it down. I thought of my father again: *ink outlives men*. Hers was the opposite trade and the same truth. Maps outlive towns.

If she noticed the shop properly, she would notice everything about it.

I put that thought in a box and sat on the box.

Then I got up, took the candle, and walked slowly around the print shop — past the frames of standing type my father had cut with his own hands, past the press with its iron screw and worn oak bed, past the drying lines and the paper cabinet and the stone floor that rang hollow in exactly one place, behind the press, where the foundation of the old chimney had been bricked shut and never finished.

I put my palm against the press. Cold iron, warm wood. My father had rebuilt this machine twice and repaired it a hundred times, and he had told me once, laughing, that a good press was just a strongbox that happened to print.

I stood there a long while, listening to the drum across the river practicing its patience, and felt the panic in my chest change its shape — the way ore changes in a furnace, same metal, new temper. Two days and some hours. One hollow behind one press. One book containing everything Vellamont had ever been forgiven for.

"Right," I said aloud to the empty shop, in my father's voice without meaning to. "Then we work."

And upstairs, the ledger waited on the counter, open to no page at all, holding its forty-one lives as patiently as a heart holds blood.
