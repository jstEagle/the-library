## 6. What Her Father Cut

The proofing press had come into the Yarrow shop before Dominic had, which in her father's accounting made it the elder sibling. It stood in the corner like a great patient ox — iron frame, oak bed, screw worn smooth by thirty years of hands — and it was to this press that Elias Yarrow now limped on a raw December morning, carrying a chisel, a mallet, and a look on his face that Dominic would later learn to call *the arithmetic face*, because he wore it whenever he was working out how much trouble a thing was worth.

She was eleven years old. Her mother had been dead two years and her father's cough had moved into the house like a relative nobody wanted to name, and on that particular morning the town of Vellamont was expecting soldiers of one flavor or another — she could no longer remember which revolution, there were so many queued up along that century like coaches at a station — and Elias Yarrow had looked at his press for a long while and said, mostly to himself:

"Well. You'll do."

"You'll do what?" Dominic asked.

"Be furniture," her father said, and knelt down at the base of the frame with his chisel, groaning in two registers, one from his knees and one from his lungs.

* * *

Sixteen years later, kneeling at the same base with the same chisel — though not the same mallet; that one had lost its head to an accident involving Widow Marchand's goose and a misunderstanding about territory — Dominic thought: *he taught me grief is just material you haven't found the shape of yet.*

But she was getting ahead of herself, and her father had always said that a printer who rushed the composition deserved the errors she got.

* * *

Flashbacks came to her, these days, the way impressions came off bad type: unevenly, ink pooling here, missing entirely there. She supposed it was the army drums still rattling around in her skull. Two days since the proclamation. Three days left until every commercial record in Vellamont was to be laid out on tables for the revolutionary authorities to paw through, and the ledger sat upstairs wrapped in oilcloth like something dead that refused to stop breathing.

So she worked. It was either work or sit in a corner and let the fear have its way with her, and Yarrows did not sit in corners. Yarrows cut, composed, inked, pulled, cleaned, and slept, in roughly that order and sometimes all before breakfast.

The proofing press needed to become furniture. Specifically, it needed to acquire a hollow inside its base large enough to hold a ledger measuring nine inches by fourteen by three — she had measured the book four times, because if you were going to gut your father's press you had better not do it twice — and it needed to acquire this hollow without looking hollowed. A press that had clearly been opened recently was a confession bound in iron. A press that looked as if it had always been slightly wrong somehow, in a way nobody could quite name — that was a door with dust on the latch.

Her father had understood doors with dust on the latch better than any man in Vellamont, and he had never once told her why.

* * *

*"Hold the graver like you're holding a bird," her father said. "Firm enough it can't fly. Gentle enough it doesn't know it's caught."*

*Dominic was eight, sitting on a crate turned on its side so she could reach the bench, and before her lay a blank punch of type-metal, soft as promise. Her father's hands closed over hers, big and nicked and black at the creases with forty years of ink, and guided the graver's point to the metal.*

*"We're cutting a lowercase e today. The humblest letter. Everyone starts with the e."*

*"Why?"*

*"Because the e forgives you your mistakes. Cut a capital G badly and it looks like a pig fell down stairs. Cut an e badly and it merely looks tired. Learn on letters that forgive."*

*He had a way of talking about letters as if they were people, or people as if they were letters — Dominic never did settle which. He'd tap the case wall and say, "That's old Master Perrin, right there, the long s — bent but reliable," and "This ampersand is Madame Clovis, holds everybody together and complains about it," and little Dominic had grown up believing the type case was simply the town, filed alphabetically.*

*"Now. Watch the counter."*

*"What's a counter?"*

*"The white inside the letter. The hole in the o, the gap in the e. Amateurs think type is marks on metal. Type is the space you leave. The mark is easy; the mark is just where you stopped being brave enough to remove more."* He turned her wrist a fraction, feather-light. *"There. See? Ink outlives men, Dominic. Men are the counters. We're the spaces we leave."*

*She hadn't understood it. She'd nodded anyway, the way children nod, and cut her first e, and it was terrible, and he laughed until he coughed and then framed it anyway — actually set it, printed it, pinned the impression above the bench where it hung for six years, crooked and tired-looking, next to the specimen sheets of his finest work.*

*It hung there still. She'd have to take it down before the soldiers came. Or leave it. Leave everything, maybe. She wasn't ready to decide what leaving everything meant.*

* * *

The problem with hollowing a press, it emerged over the next day and a half of intermittent, guilt-ridden carpentry, was that iron frames are honest and oak bases are nearly honest, and the difference between them is a man named Aubert who built the thing in 1761 and had opinions about wood.

Dominic discovered those opinions the hard way. The base was not solid oak. It was oak facing over a core of cheaper pine, and the pine had been fitted with a craftsmanship that approached devotion — joints so tight she couldn't find them, so tight that when she finally located the seam near the rear foot, hidden under a decorative chamfer that served no purpose except to hide exactly such a seam, she sat back on her heels and laughed out loud in the empty shop, and then the laugh turned into something else halfway through and she had to put the mallet down for a minute.

Because Aubert had built a hiding place. Or rather, Aubert had built the *possibility* of one — a seam placed where a hiding place would go, in a press destined for a print shop in a river town that had changed masters four times in Aubert's lifetime. Maybe it meant nothing. Maybe joiners always put a chamfer there. But her father had found it — she was certain now that he'd found it, sometime, quietly, the way he found everything — and had kept the knowledge the way he kept everything: in ink, in some ledger of his own that no one would ever read.

*Ink outlives men,* she thought, running her thumb along the chamfer. *Men are the counters.*

"All right, Papa," she said aloud, to the shop, to the press, to the smell of oil and metal and the ghost of pipe smoke that no amount of sweeping had ever fully evicted. "You'll do."

* * *

The work itself took the better part of two days, fitted into the gaps between customers, which mercifully were wide. Since the army's arrival the shop had acquired the atmosphere of a house with a sick relative: people still came, but softly, and bought things they didn't need (a handbill praising sobriety; a printed copy of the new proclamation, which they paid for and then held as if it might bite) and didn't linger, and didn't ask after Master Yarrow, because asking after Master Yarrow had begun to feel dangerous in a way none of them could have articulated and all of them felt anyway.

Which suited her. Grief needs privacy even when it hasn't officially happened yet.

She opened the seam with a fine saw, cutting only as deep as the chamfer allowed, and then went at the pine core with chisels — her father's chisels, each one ground to its particular purpose, and she used them in the order he'd taught her, roughing gouge first, then paring chisel, then the thin flexible one he called *the gossip* because it got into places nothing else could. Pine comes out of an oak box like a secret out of a drunk: messily at first, then all at once. She saved the shavings in a sack. She didn't know yet what she'd do with them — you couldn't burn fresh pine in the shop without announcing yourself to the whole street by smell — but throwing them away felt like throwing away pieces of the press, and she was already committing enough indignities against the poor beast without adding littering.

By the second evening the cavity existed: nine and a half inches by fourteen and a half by three and a quarter, with an eighth-inch everywhere for forgiveness, because her father had also taught her that exact measurements were for God and specimen books. She lowered the ledger in, wrapped in oilcloth, and it fit with a snugness that felt less like engineering and more like benediction.

Then came the hard part.

The lid — the oak facing she'd sawn free — had to go back so perfectly that the eye slid off it. She planed the cut edges until the joint was a hairline, glued it, and pegged it with dowels of matching oak driven in at angles that mimicked the grain, their ends trimmed flush and stained with a mixture of soot and linseed oil that she had tested three times on scrap before trusting it to the actual wood. When it was done, she stood back, wiped her hands, and looked at the press.

It looked like a press. It looked like a press that had been standing in that corner since 1761 doing nothing more remarkable than proving type, which, in fairness, it had. If you knew — if you knew exactly where to look and what you were looking for — you might notice that the chamfer near the rear foot was very slightly darker than its brothers, as if that spot got less light, which it did, being behind the ink slab's shadow. But you would have to be looking. And you would have to be the kind of person who measured print shops against tax records.

She thought of the cartographer woman then — Brandt, the quiet one with the German accent and the habit of standing in doorways writing in a notebook — and dismissed the thought the way you dismiss a draft: firmly, and while pulling your collar tighter.

* * *

*"Papa, why does ink outlive men?"*

*She was twelve, and it was Sunday, the one afternoon the press rested, and they were walking the river road with their collars up against a drizzle that couldn't be bothered to commit to rain. Behind them Vellamont smoked gently from a hundred chimneys. Ahead of them the ferry crossed and recrossed its gray ribbon of water, patient as a loom.*

*"Because men agree to die," her father said. He said it comfortably, the way other men discussed weather. "There's a whole ceremony to it, dying. Neighbors, priest, the lot. Very orderly. But ink has no ceremony. Nobody teaches ink how to end. You write 'Elias Yarrow owes Madame Bonnet twelve francs' and somewhere that sentence goes on existing whether Elias likes it or not. Long after Elias is a stain in the churchyard and Madame Bonnet is a story the baker tells, the sentence says what it says. Twelve francs. Owed. Ink doesn't grieve and it doesn't forgive and it doesn't forget. That's why I keep the ledger honest, Dominic. Not because honesty is nice. Because the ledger is the only one of us planning to live forever."*

*"Is that why you write down people's other names too? The ones they don't want written?"*

*He'd stopped walking. Just for a step, just a hitch, quickly smoothed over — but she'd seen it, the way she'd learned to see everything about him, because loving a secretive man makes you a reader of small signs. Then he'd laughed and rumpled her hair under the hood and said, "Someone has to remember who people really are, or the town's just a list of faces," and changed the subject to whether the ferryman's dog had forgiven the goose yet.*

*She had thought, at twelve, that he was dodging. She had thought so at fifteen, and nineteen, and on the morning she'd buried him and read his will — which named her, in ink, as* my son Dominic, *a sentence that went on existing whether she liked it or not — she had thought so most of all.*

*Standing at the press now with the ledger sleeping inside it, she wondered for the first time in her life whether he hadn't been dodging at all. Whether he'd been answering. Whether the aliases in the ledger, the debts forgiven off the books, the careful record of who Vellamont really was underneath its faces — whether all of it had been him, cutting the counters. Leaving the spaces. Making sure that when the men died, something remained that knew the shape of them.*

*"Twelve francs," she said to the empty shop. "Owed." And wiped her eyes on her inky cuff, which served her right.*

* * *

She spent the third morning rehearsing.

Not the hollowing — that was done, finished, the shavings sewn into a mattress ticking and carried out to the tannery midden in the dark, where pine shavings among oak leavings would excite comment from no one, tanneries being nature's own shredder of evidence. No; what she rehearsed was the press itself.

Because the press had to *work*. That was the whole trick of it. A sealed press was suspicious; a broken press was suspicious; a press that sat there being a press, inking and proving and getting cleaned like it had done every week of its life — that was furniture. So she locked the new lid down with the press's own weight and ran proofs. Real ones. She composed a page from whatever came to hand — half a psalm, half a price list, a stray line of the proclamation that read, alone and out of context, so poignantly: *"...shall be considered an act against the people"* — inked the ball, rolled the bed, pulled the bar, and printed.

The vibration worried her. The first dozen pulls she flinched at every impression, listening for creaks from the base, listening for the ledger shifting inside, and on pull number seven she was almost certain she heard a whisper of paper against wood, and stopped, heart hammering, and dismantled her own lock-down to check, and found the book exactly where she'd left it, smug and immovable, and swore at it with feeling.

After that she stopped listening for it. Either the cavity held or it didn't; either her father's press and Aubert's chamfer and her own two days of terror-fueled joinery were equal to the task, or they weren't, and no amount of flinching would improve the odds. By noon she could pull a proof of the psalm-price-list-proclamation and look at it afterwards without checking the base at all, and she counted this as progress, though a colder voice in her noted that it might simply be progress in not caring, which was a different commodity altogether.

Madame Clovis — the landlady, whom her father had immortalized in type as an ampersand and who had outlived the metaphor — came by at midday with bread and an interrogation disguised as concern.

"The soldiers on the corner," she said, setting the loaf down with the gravity of a woman laying down a trump card. "They asked me about this house."

"What did you tell them?"

"That the rent's been late twice this year." She sniffed. "Honesty confuses them. They expect everyone to lie. Now — the press. Why is your press making noise at night? Old Toinette across the lane says she hears knocking."

"It settles," Dominic said. "Iron and oak argue about the damp. Papa used to say the press was the only Catholic in the family, it complained so much about everything."

Madame Clovis's eyes stayed on her a moment longer than the joke earned. She was not a stupid woman, Madame Clovis; she was, in fact, precisely as sharp as an ampersand, holding things together and complaining about it, and Dominic watched her decide — visibly, the way you watch someone decide whether to mention a stain — that whatever she suspected, she suspected it on the side of the shop rather than the side of the soldiers.

"Hmph," she said, which in the local dialect of landladies meant approximately *I know more than I say and I say less than I know, and the bread is yesterday's.* She took her leave, pausing at the door to add, without turning around: "Whatever your father would have done, girl — do it neatly."

The bell over the door jingled her out. Dominic stood a moment with the loaf warm against her palm.

*Whatever your father would have done.* As if it were a known quantity. As if the whole town had privately concluded, years ago, that Elias Yarrow had been up to something beyond type and taxes, and had agreed, without ever saying so, to extend the same courtesy to his heir.

She looked around the shop. The type cases with their filed-and-labeled townsfolk. The framed crooked *e*, tired and forgiving, hanging above the bench. The press, squatting in its corner, digesting forty-one lives in its belly.

Tomorrow was surrender day. Tomorrow the army would come through Vellamont door by door, table by table, taking every record, every debt book, every scrap of inked truth the town possessed — and they would come here too, and find a shop that was exactly what it appeared to be, run by a son who was exactly what he appeared to be, keeping a press that was exactly what it appeared to be, and the whole fragile architecture of it would hold or collapse depending on whether a sergeant from wherever sergeants came from looked at a darkened chamfer and decided to care.

Dominic set the loaf down, took up the ink ball, and rolled the bed smooth for another proof, because the press had to be caught in the act of being ordinary, and because her hands needed work, and because — as a voice she was beginning to suspect would be with her for the rest of her life liked to observe — ink outlives men.

At dusk, boots rang on the cobbles outside, several pairs, in step, coming up the lane.
