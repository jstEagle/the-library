## 7. Surrender Day

The morning of the third day, Dominic woke before the drums and lay still in the dark, listening to the shop.

Every house has a voice at night — beams settling, pipes ticking, wind worrying the shutters — but a print shop speaks in lead. The type cases along the back wall ticked faintly as frost worked into the wood around them. The press stood in the middle of the room like something patient and enormous, and if you listened with your eyes shut you could almost believe it breathed.

She had hollowed it two nights ago, working by one candle with her father's smallest chisel, and she had wept the whole time, which she considered a reasonable allocation of grief: cry now, she told herself, so that later, when it matters, you will be dry-eyed and clever. The cavity was not elegant. Her father would have been ashamed of the joinery. But elegance was for men with time, and time had packed its bags three days ago when the drums came up the ferry road.

Now the ledger lay inside the press like a heart inside a chest, and the press sat in plain view of anyone who walked through the door, and this morning the army was coming to walk through every door in Vellamont.

Dominic dressed as Master Yarrow's son. Breeches, waistcoat, the stiff collar that scratched her jaw raw by noon. She bound herself, checked the mirror's angle the way other people checked the weather, and went down to open the shutters on a town gone strangely quiet. No carts. No fishwives calling prices across the canal. Just frost-smoke off the river and, somewhere eastward, the creak of many boots moving in unison, which is a sound no town forgets once it has heard it.

"Master Yarrow!" called Madame Perrine from the stairwell, in the voice she reserved for questions she already knew the answers to. "They'll be wanting entry today, I expect. Shall I tell them you're ill again?"

"You may tell them whatever keeps them out of my bedchamber, Madame," Dominic called back, "though I warn you, soldiers have very little respect for invalids."

"And less for liars," said Madame Perrine, appearing on the landing with her arms folded and her spectacles pushed up into gray hair. She looked at Dominic for a long moment — the look she had been giving her for six years, the look that weighed and measured and, mercifully, always concluded nothing aloud. "You've a face like a woman reading her own death warrant, boy. Eat something."

"I can't."

"That," said Madame Perrine, "is what I thought." And she went back down the stairs, which was as close to an embrace as Vellamont ever gave anyone.

* * *

They came at nine o'clock precisely, because revolutions love precision; it flatters their sense of destiny. A corporal with a list nailed a fresh proclamation to the doorpost of the cooperage across the lane, and then the collecting began, and Dominic watched it from her shop window the way one watches hail flatten a neighbor's field — with sympathy, and with one eye on one's own roof.

They started at the notary's. Two privates carried out ledgers, deed boxes, a cabinet of correspondence, while Maître Aubry stood in his doorway wringing his hat into a shape no hat had ever been intended to take. Then the wool merchant's. Then the salt warehouse office, where the clerks came out blinking like moles dragged into daylight, each clutching his little book of figures against his chest until a soldier pried it loose with the gentle impersonality of a man taking candy from a child he does not particularly dislike.

By ten the requisition cart at the corner of Ledger Row — the street had been named for the notary's family, generations back, and Dominic had always found the irony nourishing — was stacked higher than a hay wagon at harvest. Books she recognized. Books she had bound. A ledger with a water stain shaped like a swan that she knew belonged to the rope-walk, recording forty years of hemp purchases, none of which could possibly interest a revolutionary tribunal, and all of which were now evidence of something or other by decree.

A sergeant stopped in front of the print shop.

He was not the first soldier to pass her window that morning, but he was the first to stop, and Dominic felt her heartbeat relocate briefly to her throat and conduct its business there. He was perhaps thirty-five, broad, with the red-rimmed eyes of a man who had slept badly in a borrowed bed, and he read the painted sign above her door with the concentration of someone sounding words out.

*YARROW & FILS — TYPOGRAPHES.*

He pushed the door open without knocking. The bell — her father had loved that bell, bought it off a wrecked Portuguese carrack, swore it had crossed the ocean twice — gave its small bright complaint.

"Print shop," announced the sergeant, to no one in particular, in the manner of a man cataloguing rooms aloud to keep himself honest. His eyes went around the room: the type cases, the composing stone, the drying lines hung with proof sheets, and then, inevitably, centrally, unavoidably, to the press.

Dominic had spent two nights making the hollow invisible and one sleepless hour deciding where to stand, and she had concluded: near the press. Not far from it, hovering guiltily. Near it, leaning against it even, the way a man leans on his own furniture, because guilty men hover and innocent men lean.

"Messire," she said, bowing slightly. "You're collecting records."

"We're collecting records." He had the accent of the north counties, that flat vowel that turned every statement into mild accusation. "All commercial correspondence, accounts, registers of debt, letters of credit—"

"There's nothing here of that kind." Which was true, technically, and she watched him decide whether truth was a currency the revolution still accepted. "This shop prints. We don't keep accounts beyond what any tradesman keeps in a drawer for the tax man, and the tax man took those months ago."

The sergeant grunted. He moved toward the press.

Everything in Dominic's body advised her to move too — to intercept, to distract, to place herself between him and the thing — and everything she had learned from six years of being a lie told well advised her to do exactly nothing. She stayed leaning. She picked up a composing stick from the stone beside her, turned it over as though checking it for wear.

The sergeant walked around the press once. He put out a hand and laid it flat on the frame, and Dominic felt the touch in her own ribs, absurdly, as though he had rested his palm on her sternum.

"Big machine," he said.

"My father's. Cast in Lyon."

"Hm." He knocked on the frame with one knuckle. Once. Twice. The sound came back dense, wooden, solid, telling him nothing, because she had rebuilt the inner panel with a double skin and packed the gap with rosin dust so it would knock true. It was the sort of precaution her father would have called paranoid and then quietly copied. "And what does a print shop in a fish town find to print?"

"Bills of lading. Ferry schedules. Almanacs." She shrugged. "Last year we printed four hundred copies of an essay on the moral failings of the eel trade. The author paid in advance and then left town, which tells you something about authors."

It should not have worked. Humor never works on frightened mornings. But the sergeant's mouth did something at one corner that was not quite a smile and not quite its enemy, and he turned away from the press toward the composing stone, and Dominic allowed herself to breathe at roughly half capacity.

Then he saw the proof sheets hanging on the drying line, and frowned, and stepped closer to read them, and she understood with a lurch that the morning was not over with her yet.

* * *

The proof sheets were practice work — her father's old habit, teaching her letterforms by making her correct pages full of deliberate errors. He used to set them at night after supper: *Find the seven faults, little master.* Seven errors per sheet, planted like seeds. There were eleven sheets on the line, each one a small museum of her childhood, and the sergeant was now squinting at them with the expression of a man who suspected documents of intent.

"This one's wrong," he said, tapping a sheet. "'The *liberté* of the citizen consist in the *pouvoir* to—' that's misspelled."

"It is," Dominic agreed. "Deliberately."

"Deliberately wrong?" He looked at her sharply, and she realized how it sounded — deliberately printing errors, in an occupied town, under a regime that shot printers for worse.

"It's a training exercise," she said, and moved to stand beside him at the line, close enough to point. "My father taught me typesetting this way. You set a page, then you corrupt it, then the apprentice finds the faults. See — here, *consist* should be *consiste*. Missing the final e. And here, *citoyen* has been set as *citizein*, which is not even a plausible misprint, it's just spite." She tapped down the sheet, finding the planted faults one by one, and heard her own voice settle into the flat cheerful competence of a tradesman discussing trade, and marveled distantly at how steady it sounded, given that the actual center of her life was currently hidden eighteen inches from this man's hip.

The sergeant studied the sheet. "But *consiste*," he said slowly, "that's not right either. *La liberté consiste dans le pouvoir* — no. Wait." He rubbed his jaw. "*Consister à*? Or—"

"*Consister à*," Dominic said. "Or *consiste dans*, depending on your schoolmaster. My father held that French grammar was invented specifically to torment typesetters, and that the Académie should be tried for it."

"The *consiste*," persisted the sergeant, with the stubbornness of a man who had been corrected about something once in his youth and had never fully recovered, "it's *être*? One says *la liberté est le pouvoir*—"

"One may say anything in speech," said Dominic gravely, "and that is why God gave us compositors. On paper, messire, you want *consiste à* or *réside dans*. If you write *la liberté est le pouvoir de faire*, the sentence survives. If you write *consiste être*, the sentence dies, and so, professionally speaking, do I, because readers write angry letters."

That got the smile, the real one, brief and crooked. "Angry letters. To a printer."

"To me, eventually. Everything ends up on a printer's desk eventually, messire. That is our curse and our trade." She said it lightly. She felt it not at all lightly, because it was, in fact, the entire architecture of her life and the reason a hollowed press stood behind her sweating out its secret.

The sergeant read the sheet once more, lips moving. Then he straightened. "No accounts here, you say."

"No accounts here."

"Registers of debt?"

"A drawer," she said, and fetched the decoy.

She had prepared it on the second night: a modest daybook, genuinely old, containing three years of ordinary transactions — ink, paper, two hundred pamphlets for the ferry office, a disputed charge for gilt edges. Real entries, because fabricated books smell false to men who read books all day. Beside the genuine entries, in a hand indistinguishable from the rest, she had added a page of debts owed to the shop, all small, all dull, all invented. It was a confession designed to satisfy: here is our whole dishonest little commercial soul, take it, it's worth twelve livres.

The sergeant leafed through it without much hope. Debt registers were what the proclamations wanted; debt registers were where names lived; and every name in this drawer-book was either dead, fictional, or herself.

"This Yarrow," he said. "Your father."

"Dead two years."

"And you run the shop alone?"

"With the help of God, Madame Perrine upstairs, and a cat of no fixed principles."

The sergeant closed the book. He looked around the shop one more time — at the cases, the stone, the press standing there in the middle of the room with its patient wooden breathing and its stolen heart — and for one long moment his eyes came back to the press and rested on it, and Dominic stood very still inside her own skin, wearing her competence like a mask over a scream, and waited.

"Good machine," the sergeant said at last, apparently to the press itself. "Lyon casting." And he tucked the drawer-book under his arm, nodded once — a soldier's nod, curt, already halfway to the next door — and left.

The bell rang behind him. Small and bright and entirely inadequate.

Dominic made it four steps before her knees informed her they had opinions. She sat down on the composing stone, which was cold and hard and smelled of ink and her whole childhood, and laughed once, a strange bark with no joy in it, and pressed both hands over her face.

Under the floorboards, faint as a pulse, the press kept its silence. Somewhere in it, forty-one lives in careful ink, dry as bone, safe as anything was safe this winter — which was to say, provisionally, temporarily, until some better liar than a sergeant came asking.

* * *

By afternoon the requisition cart had grown into a wagon train, and Vellamont had the hollowed look of a town that had been patted down by strangers. Dominic forced herself through the motions: she set type for a ferry schedule nobody would pay for, swept a floor that was already clean, ate half the bread Madame Perrine shoved at her without comment.

Toward dusk, the light in the street turned the color of brass, and Dominic stood at the window watching the last of the wagons creak toward the garrison, every book in town aboard them, every debt and alias and forgiven sin of Vellamont riding under a tarpaulin toward whatever tribunals did with paper. She thought of Widow Marchand's forged name. Of old Tice and his smuggled salt. Of the forty-one entries that existed nowhere on earth but eighteen inches of hollow oak behind her.

Her father's voice, from years back, hands over hers on the graver: *Lead can be melted, girl. Wood burns. Men die. Ink outlives the lot of us — that's why you treat it like a sacrament.*

"Well," she said aloud to the empty shop, "then I'm the priest, and the reliquary is a press with a bad joinery seam."

The bell rang.

Dominic turned, and her stomach dropped through the floorboards, past the cellar, into the river — because standing in the doorway, cap in hand, unhurried, was not a soldier. It was a young officer in army blue with mud on her boots and a leather map-case slung at her hip, a woman, looking around the print shop with the slow, measuring, appallingly professional gaze of somebody counting windows.

Her eyes passed over the press. Passed over it. Stopped. Came back.

"Good evening," she said. Her French was fluent and foreign. "I'm told this is the largest building on the row. I'm making a survey of the town."

She unslung the map-case as she said it, and began, without asking, to unfold a drawing of Vellamont across Dominic's clean composing stone.
