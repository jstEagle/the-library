## 8. The Cartographer

Ilse Brandt had been drawing towns since she was eleven years old, and she had learned long ago that a town was never lying on purpose. It lied the way people did — out of habit, out of vanity, out of an old grief it didn't care to discuss. Rivers shifted their beds and left streets pointing at nothing. Wealthy men built their houses a floor lower than pride required. Church towers leaned. A good cartographer's job was not to record what a town said about itself but to find where its mouth and its meaning diverged, and then — this was the part no one thanked you for — to decide whether the discrepancy mattered.

Vellamont, she decided by her fourth day of surveying it, was a town that lied gently. It sat on its river like an old woman on a bench, watching everything and admitting nothing. The ferry road curved when it could have gone straight, because some ancestor with more sentiment than sense had refused to cut down an orchard. The market square was drawn too large in every official map Ilse had ever consulted; in person it was modest, cobbled, and smelled permanently of onions and wet wool. She corrected the square. She kept the orchard curve.

Her quarters were at the garrison, which she shared with three junior officers who believed maps were made by standing somewhere high and copying what God had already labeled. Ilse rose before them each morning, packed her board and her folding rule and her paper weighted with flat stones, and went out into the frost to measure Vellamont honestly.

It was on the sixth morning that the print shop lied to her.

* * *

The tax rolls were kept in the customhouse, in a room that smelled of tallow and mouse, under the supervision of a clerk named Perrin who had the mournful dignity of a man whose life's work had been interrupted first by revolution and now by soldiers wanting to see his shelves. He let Ilse at the property records with the air of someone lending his only coat to a stranger and expecting it back damp.

She copied the riverfront parcels into her notebook: dimensions, assessed values, ownerships. Most of it confirmed what her own chain and compass had told her. Warehouse Fourteen was forty feet deep, and her pacing agreed. The cooperage was irregular, as cooperages were, all elbows and additions. The chandlery matched. The bakery matched.

And then: *Premises of Yarrow, printer. Frontage eleven feet, depth twenty-two.*

Ilse stood very still in the cold room, pencil resting against her lip, and thought: *No.*

She had walked past the Yarrow shop twice already without stopping. It was a narrow building wedged between a haberdasher's and a house that listed like a drunk uncle, and its frontage was indeed eleven feet — she'd have staked her commission on it. But she had also, out of pure professional habit, glanced down the alley behind it, and the alley had told her a different story than twenty-two feet. The alley was short, pinched between the rear walls of the two neighboring properties, and those rear walls ran deep. Thirty-four feet, at her eye's estimate. Perhaps thirty-six.

Twelve feet of building that existed but paid no taxes. Twelve feet that appeared on no record, belonged to no one, admitted to nothing.

Vellamont lied gently. This lie was quiet as held breath.

"Something wrong, citizen?" Perrin asked, because she had been standing motionless for the better part of a minute, and clerks are sensitive to stillness the way horses are.

"The handwriting," Ilse said, which was even true — the entry was cramped and old. "When was the last survey of these premises?"

Perrin consulted the ceiling. "Before my time. The elder Yarrow bought the plot at auction, oh — thirty years ago? Built to the lot line and no further, I'd imagine. Printers are poor as churchmice." He sniffed. "Well. Was."

*Was.* The son had taken over, everyone said. Master Yarrow the younger, who kept the shop running and the broadsheets printing and who had never, in the memory of anyone Ilse had spoken to, been seen at the tavern, the market, the mill, or the bathhouse. A shy man, they said. A private man. A man who did his business through the landlady and through notes pinned to the door.

Ilse wrote *verify* in her notebook beside the entry, in small letters, and closed it.

* * *

She went the next day not to the shop but around it, the way you walk around a piece of furniture before deciding whether to move it.

The rear of the Yarrow premises faced a yard shared with the listing house, a clutter of firewood and rain barrels and one astonishingly fat goose that regarded Ilse with the flat hostility of a customs official. Beyond the yard wall she could see the back of the print shop: a second story, low-ceilinged, with two windows — and here was the thing, here was the thing that made her take out her pencil right there in the cold with the goose judging her — the second story ran the full depth of the building. Thirty-odd feet of upstairs. Over what?

Print shops needed space for paper, certainly. Paper was bulky. But twelve extra feet of unrecorded depth, roofed and windowed and paying nothing to anyone, was not storage. Storage got recorded; storage got taxed; storage was honest. Twelve secret feet was something else. In her years of army mapping Ilse had found hidden rooms used for contraband brandy, for counterfeiting, for hiding sons from conscription, and once — in a wine cellar in a border province whose name she still did not say aloud — for something that had ended with three arrests and a file she was not permitted to reread.

Or, said a quieter voice, it was twelve feet of nothing. An error in the tax roll. A lean-to built by a dead man who forgot to tell the assessor. Towns were full of arithmetic mistakes wearing roofs.

The goose honked once, decisively.

"Yes, thank you," Ilse told it, and went to find out which it was.

* * *

She told herself she was being thorough. Thoroughness was, after all, her profession and her alibi and nearly her whole personality; if thoroughness required her to change her boots, put on her civilian greatcoat instead of her uniform, and visit a print shop on the pretext of needing a proclamation printed, then thoroughness was welcome to the disguise. The requisition orders required commercial records surrendered within three days, and a printer would have records, and an officer confirming compliance was entirely proper. That she might equally have sent a sergeant was a fact she declined to examine closely, the way one declines to count how many glasses of wine one has had.

The bell over the door was cracked and gave half a chime, like a cough.

Inside, the shop was warm and dim and smelled of ink and oil and lamp-black, a smell so specific and so sudden that it put her briefly back in her father's study in her childhood — he had owned books he could not afford, and had let her smell them — and she had to stand a moment in the doorway recovering herself.

"You're wanting the notice posted?" said a voice from behind the press.

The press itself was a great wooden animal filling the middle of the room, iron-faced and ancient, and beside it stood — a boy, she thought first, and then revised, and then was annoyed at herself for revising. Young man. Slight, ink to the wrists, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with what appeared to be a shoelace. He had the particular stoop of someone who had spent ten thousand hours bent over a composing stick.

"Notices are being handled by the provost," Ilse said. "I'm told the shop has commercial records to surrender."

"They went in yesterday." He said it easily, wiping his hands on a rag that had been black so long it had achieved a kind of peace. "Sergeant took the delivery book and the accounts. There wasn't much. We're poor as churchmice."

So the widow Perrin's phrase traveled. "I don't doubt it. I'm actually here about the building."

That landed. Not dramatically — he didn't drop anything or go pale — but there was a small recalibration, the way a scale adjusts when you add a single coin. "The building?"

"I'm an engineer with the survey corps. We're updating the town plans for billeting purposes. Your neighbors' premises have been measured. Yours is next." She produced, from her coat, her folding rule, and held it up like credentials. "Eleven feet of frontage. I'll need the depth."

"Twenty-two," he said. Too fast. Then, slower, with the studied boredom of a liar discovering the craft late: "Twenty-two feet, give or take. It's a narrow shop. You've seen it from the street."

"Mm," said Ilse, who had seen thirty-four feet of it from the goose's yard. "May I?"

She stepped past him before permission arrived, because asking and waiting gave liars time to build, and looked around the shop with a surveyor's eyes while pretending to have a decorator's. Composing stone. Cases of type along the left wall, ranks of little brass-rimmed drawers, each labeled in a hand so fine and regular it looked typeset. Paper stock wrapped in oilcloth, stacked to chest height. The press. The stove, ticking gently. A ladder to the loft above.

Everything visible explained itself. That was the trouble. Rooms that hid things usually had one object slightly wrong — a shelf too clean, a wall too thick — and this room had nothing wrong in it at all, which meant either honesty or a very good tidier.

"The loft?" she said, nodding upward.

"Paper and sleeping," he said. "You're welcome to count the rafters."

"I may," said Ilse pleasantly, and did not climb, and watched his shoulders come down a quarter inch, and filed that away too.

She measured the frontage — eleven feet and a hand's width, exactly as promised — and paced the interior depth aloud, and stopped, deliberately, four feet short of the rear wall, as though satisfied. "Twenty-two," she announced, writing it. "Matches the roll."

"Does it?" he said. Something crossed his face that was almost — relief? No. Almost amusement. Faint, quickly folded away, like a card turned face-down. As if twenty-two feet matching the roll was a joke only he had heard told properly.

"It does now," Ilse said, and closed her notebook, and understood with a small internal lurch that she had just committed her first falsification of record in nine years of service, and that she had done it without deciding to, the way you step off a curb before noticing the light changed.

* * *

At the door she paused. It was not part of any plan. Plans, she suspected, had very little to do with the rest of this exchange either.

"The type cases," she said. "The labels on the drawers. Who cuts your letterforms? That hand is extraordinary."

He blinked, genuinely thrown — thrown, she noted, far harder by a compliment than by an inspection. "My father's," he said. "I maintain them. Cut replacements when a letter wears." And then, despite himself, despite whatever wariness kept this boy buttoned to the throat, pride leaked out of him like light under a door: "There's a lowercase g in the third case that he cut when he was nineteen. Look at the tail of it sometime. Nobody cuts a tail like that anymore."

"I'll look," said Ilse, meaning it, and wondered when she had last meant anything she said in a requisitioned building.

"What do you need letterforms for?" he asked. "Aren't engineers all numbers?"

"Numbers are letters that grew up," Ilse said, and was rewarded — she catalogued the reward, examined it, found it disproportionate — with a short, surprised laugh. He laughed the way his drawer labels were written: precisely, and as if he didn't do it often enough to be practiced.

"Master Yarrow," she said. "Thank you for your cooperation."

"Anytime," said the boy who was not Master Yarrow, in a voice pitched exactly halfway between *I am who I claim* and *please go now,* and closed the door behind her with the softness of someone who wanted very badly to slam it.

* * *

She walked back toward the garrison by the long way, along the river, which she justified as hydrographic verification and which was, rather more honestly, because she wanted the time to argue with herself.

The facts arranged themselves in her head with the tidy inevitability of a well-drawn legend. One: the premises exceeded the record by roughly twelve feet. Two: the occupant stated a false dimension with unpracticed speed. Three: the occupant was not the occupant. She had spent enough hours among men to know the architecture of a jaw under its binding, the set of shoulders pretending to be narrower than they were. She had been surprised at herself for the revision at the door and was not surprised anymore. *Master Yarrow's son* was a daughter wearing her father's name like one of his coats, and wearing it, Ilse thought, with considerably more skill than most lies of her acquaintance — the drawer labels, the easy talk of debts, the stoop. Years of it. Since childhood, probably. Since a deathbed and a signature and a girl who looked at a dying man's shop and decided it would not close.

Four: none of this was any of Ilse's business, and all of it was exactly the business the army had brought her here to attend.

The proclamation was clear enough. Records concealed from requisition were evidence of fraud; premises concealing them were subject to search; persons concealing them were subject to whatever Commandant Ravel's careful mind made of them. And Ravel, from what Ilse had seen in three weeks of his command, did not make careless use of persons. He read people the way she read terrain, patiently, for what could be built there. A print shop that hid twelve feet of itself, run by a girl hiding inside a dead man's name, keeping — what did printers keep? Ledgers. Accounts. Every debt in the neighborhood, every alias, every transaction that never met the light.

Names, Ilse thought, stopping at the rail where the river slid dark and certain under its skin of new ice. The town was full of names that weren't real. Half of Vellamont had drifted here ahead of warrants, or married under borrowed histories, or paid some clever hand to make an old debt disappear. And if even a tithe of that lived in the Yarrow books —

If even a tithe of it lived there, then the shop was not a shop. It was an archive of everyone the revolution had promised to forgive and never quite had. Burn it, and forty ghosts rested easy. Read it, and the tribunals would eat until spring.

None of which explained why, standing at the rail in the gray afternoon with her fingers going numb, Ilse Brandt's dominant thought was neither duty nor suspicion nor the considerable danger of the falsified measurement sitting in her notebook like a loaded pistol.

Her dominant thought was the lowercase *g*. The tail of it. The way his — her — face had opened for one unguarded instant over a dead man's craft, pride shining through the disguise like a coin through a worn pocket, gone as soon as seen.

*You're welcome to count the rafters.*

*I may,* she had said. And hadn't.

She had walked out of that shop holding the truth of it — the discrepancy, the name, the girl — and had written *twenty-two* in her book anyway, and she could not presently locate, anywhere in her professional conscience, the reason why. Curiosity, she told herself. A cartographer hates to close a map on a wrong number. She would go back. Of course she would go back; there were legitimate grounds; the survey was incomplete; she would go back with her rule and her questions and she would get to the bottom of those twelve feet if it took all winter.

The river ice creaked, settling. Downstream, a ferryman sang something tuneless and profane. Ilse pulled her collar up against the wind and noticed that she was smiling, and that the smile had nothing to do with hydrography, and that she had already, without consulting herself, chosen the pretext for tomorrow's visit.

There was, after all, a lowercase *g* she intended to look at.
