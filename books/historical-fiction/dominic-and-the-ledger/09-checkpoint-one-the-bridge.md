## 9. Checkpoint One: The Bridge

The bolt of sailcloth had been sitting in the back of the shop since before the army came, left there by a boatman who owed the shop eleven sous and had paid instead in canvas he swore was English and therefore priceless. Dominic had spent an afternoon holding it up to the window light, testing its weave against her thumb, and had concluded that the boatman was a liar but that the cloth was, at least, thick enough to hide a book.

Sewing it took most of the night.

She worked by tallow light at the composing stone, the ledger open on her lap like a patient on the table. The book was heavier than it looked — ledgers always were, her father used to say; paper remembers everything it's made to carry — and getting it flat against the bolt's cardboard stiffening required her to press it under the proofing press's screw for two hours first, until it lay obedient as a fresh-printed sheet. Then she stitched it in with waxed thread, in long careful passes, the way she'd watched her mother hem a skirt once, years ago, before everything.

It was strange, sewing. Her fingers knew type. They knew the weight of a bodkin and the angle of a graver and the exact pressure that coaxed a letter out of lead without breaking it. They did not know a needle. Three times she stabbed herself, and three times she said a word her father would have washed her mouth out with soap for, and continued.

By the fourth hour the ledger had vanished. What remained on the table was a bolt of sailcloth, unremarkable, worth perhaps thirty sous, the kind of thing a print shop might plausibly be sending to a chandler or a sailmaker across the river. She turned it over twice, looking for the book in it, and could not find the book in it. That, she decided, was the whole trick: not to hide a thing well, but to make it boring. Nobody searches boredom.

"You would have hated this," she told the dark shop, meaning her father, who had believed that anything worth doing was worth doing in broad daylight with your name signed at the bottom in twelve-point Roman. "You'd have walked it through the checkpoint yourself and dared them to open it."

Then again, her father was dead, which suggested his methods had limits.

She slept two hours on the cot behind the type cabinets, in her shirt and breeches with her boots half-laced, the way she'd slept since the drums came down the ferry road. Before dawn she ate the heel of a loaf Widow Marchand had left — thrust through the door without comment, still warm, which from Marchand was practically an embrace — and wrapped the bolt in sacking, tied it with cord, hoisted it onto her shoulder, and became, for the purposes of Vellamont's new masters, a boy running a delivery for Master Yarrow's print shop.

Master Yarrow, as ever, was indisposed.

* * *

The bridge over the Auronne was the only sensible way out of town on foot. The ford upstream was frozen too shallow to wade and too deep to jump; the ferry was commandeered; and the north gate opened onto roads the army wanted for itself. So everyone leaving Vellamont honestly went over the old stone bridge, past the tollhouse that had collected tolls for three dukes and now collected names for a republic, and Dominic joined the morning line of carters and fishwives and one nervous priest, all of them breathing steam, all of them pretending the soldiers at the far end were furniture.

The soldier at the near end was young enough that his beard was mostly ambition. He had a list. He was very proud of the list.

"Name," he said, when Dominic reached him.

"Yarrow. Apprentice to the printer." She pitched her voice down into the register she thought of as her father's floorboards — flat, unhurried, slightly bored. Boredom, again. The great camouflage.

"Business?"

"Sailcloth for Madame Gorse, the rigging supplier, across the water." This was even true, in the sense that Madame Gorse existed, supplied rigging, and lived across the water. Dominic had chosen her precisely because every part of the lie was load-bearing truth. "Master Yarrow sends his compliments and regrets he cannot come himself."

The soldier wrote *YAROW* on his list, spelled wrong, which somehow made her feel safer. A man who misspells you hasn't really seen you.

"Open it?"

"It's sewn," Dominic said, and let a note of long-suffering apprenticeship into her voice, the universal dialect of boys everywhere complaining about their masters' errands. "Bolt's sealed for the wet crossing. You want me to cut my master's stitching so you can look at cloth?"

She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed the shrug, the tilt of the bolt, the exact degree of teenage resentment. And it was working — she could see it working, see the young soldier's eyes already glazing, his pencil hovering over the list, already halfway to writing *CLOTH* and waving her through —

And then the bell began to ring.

Not the church bell. The garrison bell, up by the customhouse, a flat iron sound she'd only heard twice before, both times when something had gone wrong somewhere else. Along the bridge, heads turned. The soldier's head turned. Upstream, at the far end of the span, four men in blue greatcoats came out of the tollhouse at a trot and began dragging a timber barrier across the roadway, and an officer's voice carried over the water with the particular crispness of someone enjoying his own authority:

"The bridge is closed! All traffic halts! Search protocols — nothing crosses until it's lifted!"

The line groaned, collectively, the way lines do, a low animal sound of shared inconvenience. Somewhere behind Dominic a woman said, "Search? Search *what?*" and nobody answered, because nobody knew, and not knowing was rapidly becoming the town's chief industry.

Dominic stood very still with a book sewn into a bolt of sailcloth on her shoulder, and did the arithmetic.

A search protocol meant going through everything. Not spot-checks, not the bored wave-through — everything. Every cart unpacked. Every basket upended. Every bolt of cloth, she would have bet her father's remaining type, unrolled and run over with hands looking for exactly the sort of lump that a ledger makes when it is sewn into one.

She turned around and walked back into the crowd flowing the other way, and it says something about how frightened she was that she didn't remember any of the walk. Just the sacking against her neck, suddenly heavy as sin, and the certainty crawling up her spine that every eye at that checkpoint had already found her and was simply waiting politely for her to sweat.

Nobody stopped her. Of course nobody stopped her. She was a boy with a delivery, walking away from a closed bridge like any sensible person. But her heart kept up its own drumroll the whole length of the quay, past the net-sheds and the tar vats, until the bridge was well behind her and the bolt was on her shoulder and her lungs remembered their trade.

She sat down eventually on an overturned dinghy by the shipwright's yard, out of sight of the water, and let her hands shake, because they were going to regardless, and it seemed better to give them leave.

Humiliation, she discovered, is a specific flavor. It is not fear exactly, though it has fear in it, the way soup has salt. It is the taste of having believed yourself clever. She had spent a night sewing and an hour rehearsing and had walked up to that checkpoint feeling like the heroine of one of the smuggler tales she printed on Saturdays — and the universe had answered with a bell and a plank across the road. Not even caught. Worse than caught: *inconvenienced*. Turned away by bureaucracy rather than bested by intelligence.

"He didn't even look at it," she said aloud, to the dinghy. "I gave him my whole speech."

The dinghy, being a dinghy, offered no comfort.

* * *

The problem, once the shaking stopped, was where the book went next.

She could not take it home. Home was being watched — she'd felt the watching for two days now, the loose pattern of soldiers' rounds that happened to pass the print shop more often than geometry strictly required. Carrying a suspicious bolt of sailcloth back into the shop she'd just left with a suspicious bolt of sailcloth was the kind of move that got people hanged in the ballads, usually in the third verse.

She could not stash it in any of the obvious places, because she'd spent her whole life in this town and knew precisely how un-obvious places became obvious the moment anyone thought to look. Under the pier: first place searched. The tannery: Marchand's territory, and Marchand's favors ran at interest. The church: Father Aubert was a good man, which in 1792 was another way of saying a dangerous one, and she would not make him an accomplice by ambush.

Which left the burial ground.

It sat on the rise above the ferry road, the old one, older than the parish register — a scatter of leaning stones where the town had buried its dead back when the dead were buried close, before the new ground beyond the orchards. Half-forgotten, unvisited, on the army's maps surely marked as empty space, because armies do not trouble themselves over the settled affairs of the deceased.

There were niches in the retaining wall along its lower terrace. Her father had pointed them out once, years ago, on one of their walking-lecture days — *see there, Dominic, the old bone vaults, dry as a sermon, built to last longer than the names above them.* He'd meant it as a mason's observation. He'd had a habit of handing her facts that would turn out, decades later, to be tools.

The sun was low and the wind off the river had teeth in it when she climbed the terraced path with the bolt under her arm, doing her best impression of a boy cutting across to the orchard lane. The burial ground was empty, obviously. The living had better places to be cold.

The niche she chose was in the second course of the wall, behind a slab whose inscription the weather had eaten down to a word and a date — *...BEL... 174...* — someone whose name had already done what names do when nobody carries them. She worked the slab loose with her knife blade, wincing at the scrape of it, and slid the bolt into the cavity feet-first. It fit with room to spare, which felt faintly insulting after all that sewing. She seated the slab back in its groove, swept the dust of two centuries across the joint with her sleeve, and stepped back.

From three paces, the wall was just a wall. From ten, the terrace was just a terrace. The ledger of Vellamont — forty-one aliases, six forgiven debts, one forged widow, a hundred small mercies in her father's cramped hand and her own neater one — was spending the night in the company of somebody named Bel-something, born and died in the seventeen-forties, and would keep warmer company than she would.

Because she was not leaving either. That was the part she hadn't fully decided until her feet decided it for her, settling into the lee of the wall where the yew threw its blind shadow. If the search reached the burial ground — unlikely, but the army had surprised her once today already — she wanted to be within a dead sprint of that slab. So she pulled her coat tight, put her back against cold stone, and prepared to spend the night sitting vigil over a book, in a graveyard, in December, disguised as a boy who had no good explanation for any part of his circumstances.

Her father would have had opinions.

Above her, the first stars came out through the bare yew branches, hard and white as punch marks. Below, the town lit itself window by window, and she could pick out — she always could — the glow of the print shop's lamp, left burning because a shop that went dark looked like a shop hiding something. Somewhere down there, Commandant Ravel was presumably dining on requisitioned wine and drafting whatever came next. Somewhere down there, Ilse Brandt — the quiet cartographer who had visited the shop once and said almost nothing and looked at everything — was drawing Vellamont, and God only knew what her sharp German pencils had already committed to paper about the dimensions of one small printing house.

"I'm keeping them safe," Dominic told the stars, quietly, in case the stones objected to conversation. "All of them. Even the ones who don't deserve it. Especially those, probably, since they're the ones who'd talk."

The wind answered. The wind, she reflected, was not a believer.

Around midnight it began to snow — fine, dry snow, the kind that doesn't melt on contact but sits on you and slowly becomes you. She counted her misfortunes to stay awake, in the manner her father taught for staying calm while correcting proofs: bridge closed, plan ruined, feet freezing, dignity lost. Against which: book hidden, not caught, and tomorrow the search would lift and she could try again, smarter, some other door.

She was still tallying when the lantern light came up the ferry road below the wall — two lanterns, moving slow, and behind them the crunch of boots in the fresh snow, more boots than a patrol needs for a stroll. The lights stopped at the foot of the terrace. A voice drifted up, muffled by snowfall, a sergeant's voice, tired and official:

"...then we'll work the riverside in the morning. Start with the houses on the quay. And someone said the Yarrow boy was seen at the bridge today with a delivery. We'll want a word with him."

The lanterns moved on. Dominic sat perfectly still against the tomb wall with the snow gathering on her shoulders, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter, that tomorrow would begin not with her choosing the next move —

but with soldiers knocking on her own front door, asking her where she had been all night.
