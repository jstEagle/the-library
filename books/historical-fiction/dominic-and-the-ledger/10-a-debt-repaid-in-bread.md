## 10. A Debt Repaid in Bread

Widow Marchand had three opinions about winter, and she held all of them simultaneously: that it was good for killing beetles, bad for her knees, and excellent for making people honest. Cold stripped a town down to its true character the way frost stripped a windowpane. In summer everyone was generous and vague. In winter you found out who had wood, who had bread, and who had been lying about both since September.

She was thinking about this on the fourth morning of the occupation, standing in her kitchen in her dead husband's coat, kneading dough with the particular violence she reserved for occasions when thinking was required.

The occasion was this: somewhere down the river road, drums. Again. Not arrival-drums this time — those had been three days ago, a sound like the sky clearing its throat — but patrol-drums, the smaller, meaner kind that meant soldiers were walking door to door with a list.

And Marchand knew exactly what was on the list, because Dominic Yarrow had told her, in the flat careful voice of a person reciting scripture: *all commercial records, madame. Every ledger, every account book, every debt noted anywhere. Three days.*

Three days ended at noon today.

Marchand punched the dough. The dough, being dough, did not care, which made it the only creature in Vellamont she could currently abuse with impunity.

The trouble — her trouble, specifically, the trouble with her own name written in it — was this: twenty-two years ago, when she had still been Céline Marchand née Fournier, wife of the late and largely unlamented Grégoire Marchand, she had not existed. Not officially. There had been a warrant, an unpaid debt of her husband's, a name signed to it that was not quite hers — two letters transposed by a clerk in Ravel's own customs office back when Ravel's customs office had belonged to other people — and for twenty-two years the woman who paid taxes on this house, who baked half the town's bread, who had buried a husband and raised no children and outlived every gossip who remembered why, had done so under a name that was a small, careful lie.

She had told herself the lie was Grégoire's. That soothed her for years. Then Grégoire died, and the lie kept working without him, and she'd had to admit it was hers now. She had fed it. She had grown old inside it.

And then a quiet girl with ink-stained fingers had sat across from her last spring, opened a leather book, and said, *Madame, there is an entry here from before my father's time. I could correct it. Or I could leave it as it is, because as it is, it says you already paid.*

Marchand had stared at her. "Paid."

"Paid in full," Dominic had said. "In my father's hand. He forgave debts sometimes. Quietly. It looks better in the book if they're forgiven than if they never existed." A pause, those gray eyes lifting. "Forgiven debts are history. Nonexistent ones are forgeries waiting to be noticed."

*And what does that make me?* Marchand had almost asked. But she'd caught the girl's expression — that particular Yarrow stillness, the look of someone holding a great deal of other people's water behind a very thin dam — and understood that the child was offering her the only mercy on offer: complicity, dressed as bookkeeping.

"You're a strange girl," Marchand had said instead.

"I'm a ledger-keeper," said Dominic, "which is stranger."

So the entry stood. *Fournier, M. — settled, 1770, in kind.* And now the army wanted every record in town, and somewhere in that leather book, in beautiful copperplate, was proof that Céline Marchand had spent two decades being a person who did not exist.

The dough surrendered. Marchand let it be.

* * *

They came at half past ten. She heard them before she saw them — boots in the street doing that rhythm soldiers do, too fast for walking, too slow for marching, the gait of men who have been given a job they find beneath them and intend to finish anyway. Then knocking, next door, at the cooper's. Voices. The creak of the coopers' cart.

Marchand untied her apron, put it back on, took it off again, and finally draped it over the bread basket, which she positioned on the table like a centerpiece. If one was going to be interrogated, one might as well be interrogated amid evidence of hospitality. Hospitality disarmed. Her husband had taught her that by counterexample: Grégoire had met every official of his short crooked life with either bluster or flattery, and had died owing money to both kinds.

She opened the door before they knocked. Better to look like a woman interrupted mid-bread than one lying in wait.

There were two of them. A sergeant, weathered, fortyish, with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't trust; and a boy soldier whose chin hadn't committed to anything yet, carrying the requisition papers against his chest like a hymnal.

"Madame." The sergeant consulted his list. "Céline Marchand. Widow. Baker?"

"Baker," Marchand agreed. "Widow. In that order of seniority."

The boy soldier blinked. The sergeant did not.

"We're collecting commercial records, madame. Ledgers, accounts, receipts. By order of the commandant."

"I heard the proclamation read at the fountain." She stepped back from the doorway, which was as close to an invitation as she intended to extend. "Come in, then, if you're coming. It's cold enough out there to make honesty of anyone."

They came in. The sergeant's eyes went around the kitchen with professional patience — the hearth, the proving shelf, the basket under the apron — and Marchand watched him do it and felt something she would later describe to no one as *fear*, though at the time it registered only as an acute awareness of her own heartbeat, which seemed suddenly very loud for an organ so unremarkable.

"Where do you keep your records, madame?"

"In there." She nodded at the basket.

The sergeant looked at the basket. "That's bread."

"It's under the bread. Where else would one keep anything in a bakery? Under the beds?" She lifted the apron, moved two round loaves aside — she was pleased with how naturally her hands shook slightly, or perhaps they weren't performing — and drew out a slim, greasy notebook. "Forty years of customers and what they owe me. Half of Vellamont owes me for bread. The other half owes me for pretending not to know it."

The sergeant took it. He turned pages. His expression suggested the arithmetic was confirming itself.

"And that's everything? All your business?"

"All my business," said Marchand, "is bread."

* * *

It went wrong on the way out, as things did.

The sergeant paused in the doorway, that small hesitation of a man remembering a task, and turned back. "One more matter, madame. We're also asking about the print shop. The Yarrow shop." He glanced at his paper. "The proprietor is listed as a Master Yarrow. We've been unable to determine—" a faint tightening around the words, as though he'd already asked this question several times and received several unsatisfying answers "—his whereabouts."

"His whereabouts," repeated Marchand.

"He hasn't presented himself for registration. No one in town will say where he keeps himself."

"No one in town knows where he keeps himself," Marchand said. This was true. It was also, in the sense that mattered, a lie, and she felt the ledger's weight settle onto her shoulders like her husband's coat. "Master Yarrow is a recluse. Hasn't shown his face in years, not since his health failed. He does his business through the daughter."

"The daughter." The sergeant wrote something. "The one who keeps the shop."

"The one who keeps everything," said Marchand. "The man is a shadow and the girl is the shadow-caster. If you want the shop's affairs, ask her. Though I'll tell you now what she'll tell you: the records are the shop's, and the shop's records are being handled per the proclamation, and if you know another way to handle a print shop's ledgers than carefully, then you know something I don't, and I've baked bread for every regiment that's passed through this valley since before you had a rank."

The boy soldier made a small sound that might have been the beginning of a laugh, hastily swallowed. The sergeant looked at her for a long moment.

"You're very forthcoming, madame."

"I'm sixty-one years old and I've been questioned by men with lists since I was nineteen," said Marchand. "I've learned the trick of it. The trick is to talk until the questions get tired."

Something moved behind the sergeant's face — not amusement exactly, but a cousin of it. He tucked her bread ledger under his arm with the others'.

"There's a detail," he said, more quietly, "that the girl was seen, two nights ago, near the churchyard. After curfew. Carrying something heavy. You're her neighbor. You'd have seen her come and go."

And there it was. Marchand kept her face still, which after sixty-one years was less an effort than a habit so deep it had become architecture.

The grave niche. She didn't know it for certain — didn't know what Dominic had been carrying or where — but she knew the girl, and she knew that whatever had been carried had been carried because it must be, and she knew the churchyard because Marchand herself had walked it at all hours for twenty years and knew every stone by its lichen.

"After curfew," Marchand said slowly. "Two nights ago." She frowned, and let the frown do its work, and arrived at the truth as if discovering it. "Well, of course she was. That was the night old Père Aubert's marker was set. The stone-cutters came late from Saint-Roch, and someone had to see it right — the girl cut the letters herself, you know. They teach them lettering in a print shop, it's practically the same trade." She sniffed. "Carrying something heavy, yes. The bronze plate. It took two of us to lift it and I'm not ashamed to say the girl did most of the work. I only supervised. Supervision is heavy in its own way."

The sergeant wrote none of this down, which Marchand recognized as either a very good sign or a very bad one.

"The widow Marchand," he said, "attended a burial after curfew."

"I attended the setting of a stone, sergeant, which is a different thing entirely and considerably jollier. The deceased has been dead eleven years. He was in no condition to attend himself." She picked up a loaf from the basket and pushed it into the boy soldier's hands before either of them could construct a refusal. "There. For the road. And one for the sergeant, though he looks like a man who takes payment only in paperwork."

The sergeant accepted the loaf the way men accept things from grandmothers and artillery: without protest, because protest was futile.

At the door he stopped once more. He did not turn all the way around, only his head, and his voice dropped into a register meant for her alone.

"Madame. Your ledger. The handwriting in the early entries changes. Around 1770."

The kitchen was very warm and very quiet. Somewhere outside, the patrol-drums had stopped, which was worse than their continuing.

"Changes," said Marchand.

"Goes from a careless hand to a careful one. Around the same year, an entry marked *settled, in kind*." He looked at her with those patient, tired eyes. "I count ledgers for a living now. It isn't the trade I expected, but it turns out I have a gift. So I'll say this once, and then I'll forget I said it, and we'll both be grateful: the commandant wants this collection finished and burned by week's end. Burned. Understand? Not read. There are men above him who would read. Pray it stays burned."

Then he was gone, boots in the too-fast-too-slow rhythm, the boy soldier trotting after with his bread and his hymnal of papers.

Marchand stood in her doorway a long while, watching the street empty.

*Burned.* The word sat in her chest strangely. Twenty-two years ago a careful hand had written *settled, in kind*, and for twenty-two years that line of ink had been the wall between Céline Marchand and whatever cell awaited women whose names were small lies. And now the army proposed to burn the wall, and she discovered — this was the astonishing thing, this was the thing she would turn over at night for weeks — that she did not want it burned. Not because burning freed her, but because somewhere in Vellamont there was a girl carrying bronze plates through churchyards after dark to save a book full of names like hers, and if the book burned, the girl's risk would have bought nothing but ash.

"Hypocrite," Marchand said aloud, to herself, and found she didn't mind the accusation much. Hypocrisy, properly managed, was just solidarity that hadn't decided on its price yet.

Hers, it appeared, was bread.

* * *

She went to the print shop at dusk, when the patrols changed over and the streets belonged to cats and the occasional cold priest.

Dominic opened the door before she knocked — the girl always knew footsteps, some inheritance from the type-cutter's trade, listening to the weight of things — and regarded her visitor without surprise.

"You spoke to a sergeant today," Dominic said.

"I fed a sergeant today. It comes to the same." Marchand slipped inside, into the smell of ink and oil and lead that she had always found oddly comforting, like the inside of a clock. "He noticed your handwriting improves around 1770. He noticed my entry. He chose to tell me he noticed, which in my experience means he's decided not to notice, and wants a witness to his decency so he can find it again later if he needs to. Soldiers are sentimental creatures. No one warns you."

Dominic's hands had gone still on the counter. "You covered for me. The churchyard."

"The churchyard," Marchand confirmed. "Père Aubert's magnificent fictional monument. I hope you appreciate that I have now perjured myself before the revolutionary army on behalf of a gravestone."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Marchand lowered herself onto the shop's one decent chair, the one clients sat in, and fixed the girl with the look she used on apprentices and late payers. "Because here is what thanks costs, little ledger-keeper, and you'll hear it plainly. I lied for you today. Freely, mind — nobody twisted my arm, and I've been lying since before your father cut his first lowercase — but freely given favors are worth nothing in this world unless someone remembers to collect them, and I am a collector. One day, and it may be soon, and it may be at the worst possible hour, I will stand in your doorway and I will call that favor due, and you will pay it, whatever it is, without haggling. Do you understand the terms?"

For a moment the girl just looked at her, and Marchand saw her do the sum — saw the gray eyes move through it, interest accruing, risk weighed — and thought, not for the first time, that whoever ended up owning Vellamont, it would not be the men with the drums.

"I understand the terms," Dominic said. "But madame — if the book burns, your name burns with it. You'd be safer letting it go. Safer letting me go."

"Oh, undoubtedly." Marchand rose, straightened her husband's coat, and allowed herself, for exactly one second, the luxury of a smile that wasn't armor. "But I've been a fiction for twenty-two years, girl, and I find I've developed opinions about who gets to decide which fictions survive. Burn my lie and you burn the only proof I ever outlived my husband's debts. Besides—" she tapped the counter, once, where the hollow press waited under its dust sheet like a coffin with opinions "—you keep forty-one lives in that book of yours. Mine makes forty-two. I don't intend to be the only coward in the congregation."

She let herself out. The cold took her breath at the doorstep, clean and metallic, and down at the end of the street she could see the customhouse windows burning yellow against the early dark, and figures moving at the bridge — more figures than yesterday, and a lantern strung where no lantern had hung before.

They were tightening something in this town, knot by knot. Well. She had called her favor in; now she would go home and bake for whichever morning arrived first.

Behind her, faint through the closing door, she heard the click of type being sorted, quick and steady as a heart deciding.
