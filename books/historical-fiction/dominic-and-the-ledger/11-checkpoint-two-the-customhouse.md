## 11. Checkpoint Two: The Customhouse

The second attempt began, as most of my bad ideas do, with a hat.

Not my hat. My hats were few and honest and all of them sat on the peg by the door of a shop that was now, officially, a shop that had surrendered nothing worth noticing. No — this hat belonged to Perrin Dubosc, delivery clerk for the Dubosc & Sons hauling concern, which had exactly one son, and the son had a sprained ankle and a mother who owed her brother's alias to page forty-seven of my father's ledger.

"You'll ruin it," I said, turning the hat in my hands. It was a fine hat, felted wool, with a brim that meant business.

"Ruined already," Madame Dubosc said, arms folded, standing in the back room where I now slept because sleeping upstairs felt like advertising myself to the ceiling. "Perrin wore it into the canal when he was eleven and it never fit him right since. A hat with a history suits you." She looked me up and down with the frank appraisal of a woman who had dressed three sons and buried one husband and was not easily fooled about what she was looking at. "Stand straighter. Clerks slouch from sitting. You're slouching like someone hiding."

"I am someone hiding."

"Then hide taller."

I hid taller. The rest of the costume came together around the hat: Perrin's spare jacket, let out at the shoulders with thread pulled from the hem so no tailor could later testify to it; a canvas satchel stuffed with genuine delivery chits, because Madame Dubosc understood that a lie goes further when it carries legitimate papers in its pockets; and the ledger itself, sewn flat against my ribs under my shirt, wrapped in oilcloth, bound tight with a strip of linen so that when anyone looked at me they would see a clerk gone slightly soft around the middle from desk work, which was precisely the sort of detail people believed because they wanted an explanation for every body they saw.

"Third time's the truth," Madame Dubosc said at the door, which is not the proverb, but she was not a woman to waste luck on bridges. "The bridge failed you. So don't go over the bridge. Go in through the front of the customhouse itself, like you belong there, because as far as anyone in this town can prove—" she tapped the chits "—you do."

"And if they search the bags?"

"They won't search the bags. They search faces. Soldiers are boys, Dominic. Boys look at faces." She paused, then added, with terrible kindness, "You've got your father's face. That helps. Nobody ever suspected a man who looks tired and mildly disappointed."

* * *

The customhouse had been built in the last century by a man who believed that commerce deserved architecture, and so it stood at the river's edge like a stone wedding cake: arched windows, a grand stair nobody used, and the customs floor itself stretching wide and dim under a ceiling painted with allegories of Abundance that two hundred years of pipe smoke had turned into allegories of Lung Trouble.

I came in with the morning deliveries, third in a line of six, and discovered immediately that Madame Dubosc had been right about everything except the difficulty of impersonating Perrin, whose walk I had studied for perhaps four minutes and whose natural gait, it turned out, was a rolling, impatient stride that suggested he had personally been kept waiting by the entire building since birth. I rolled. I strode. I arrived at the intake desk radiating the specific grievance of a man with places to be.

"Perrin Dubosc," I said, and my voice sat down low in its chair where it had learned to sit these six years. "Dubosc & Sons. Three casks tallow, one crate glassware, manifest attached. And before you ask — yes, the seal on the tallow is broken, and no, it wasn't us, it was the ferryman's boy, and I've written it on the chit so nobody has to be curious about it."

The intake clerk, whose name tag said VERNIER and whose face said he had been nineteen years old for approximately thirty years, took the chit. Read it. Looked up at me with dawning gratitude, because here at last was a delivery man who answered questions before they needed asking.

"Dubosc's boy," Vernier said. "Thought you were taller."

"Growth comes later," I said gravely. "Mother says I'm saving it up."

There is a species of conversation that runs entirely on each party wanting it to be over, and I have never loved anything the way I love that kind of talk. Vernier stamped my chit, waved toward the far corridor — "Store room's left, inspection bench is right, don't go past the green line, army business beyond it" — and went back to his stamping, and just like that I was inside the customhouse with the ledger warm against my ribs and the whole day still unspent.

Because here was the thing I had worked out over three nights of lying awake listening to the river ice tick and settle: the checkpoints were not the danger. The bridge checkpoint existed to catch things coming *into* town, contraband and deserters and pamphlets, because armies think in one direction. The requisition order demanded records surrendered by the third day, and the third day had come and gone with my hollowed press sitting quiet and empty-looking under the sergeant's eye, and so far as the army knew, the matter was closed. What remained open was the postal route north — the dispatch barge that left from the customhouse wharf twice weekly, carrying sealed army correspondence to the division command in the city. Dispatches traveled with escorts. Escorts required manifests. Manifests were logged at the customhouse master's office, second floor, where civilians could stand at the counter and watch their own goods weighed.

A book sewn into an army dispatch parcel is invisible. An army parcel requires only a courier's token, and courier tokens were issued at the master's office to licensed haulers, and Dubosc & Sons held license number fourteen, and license number fourteen was currently hanging around my neck on a string, warm from Perrin's neck, smelling faintly of horse.

I did not intend to steal a dispatch parcel. Let me be clear about that, because later, in rooms where my testimony mattered, people assumed worse of me than I deserve. I intended to *add* to one — to slip the ledger, thin as it was after my father's rebinding, into the bottom of a legitimate courier packet bound for the guild archive in the city, addressed in perfectly genuine ink to the guild tribunal itself, requesting archival storage per the enclosed letter, which I had spent two nights composing in a hand I invented specifically for the purpose, neither mine nor my father's but some brisk, blameless stranger's.

All I needed was to reach the second floor, see the courier tokens issued, and come back tomorrow with a plan built on facts instead of hope. Reconnaissance. Ilse Brandt would have called it surveying, though I didn't know her name yet, and wouldn't for another week, and God help me, when I finally heard it I would be standing in a room with her measuring tape and my whole life unrolled on the floor between us.

But I'm ahead of the century. Back to the customhouse.

* * *

The stairs were marble and complained under every footstep, and the second floor smelled of sealing wax and cold coffee. The master's office was a cage of oak lattice at the corridor's end, and outside it stood the counter, and at the counter stood a problem in an army coat: a courier, mid-twenties, jaw like the prow of a boat, arguing with the clerk inside about tokens.

"—third time this month the haulers get priority and I wait," the courier was saying. "Division mail doesn't wait. Division mail is *why* there's a war schedule."

"And division mail waited Tuesday also," said the clerk inside, unhurried as syrup. "Because Master Fouché is at the weighing floor. Tokens issue at ten, or after he returns. There is a clock. It is on the wall. Even soldiers can read it, I am told."

I took my place behind the courier at a respectful distance and became furniture. Furniture with a plan, but furniture. Through the lattice I could see the token book, the wax pots, the rack of lead seals — and, more importantly, I could see the procedure, which took ninety seconds and consisted of: license shown, entry copied, token stamped, seal pressed, done. Ninety seconds. Tomorrow, ninety seconds, and then the wharf, and then the book was the river's problem and the city's problem and no longer the thing underneath my heartbeat.

That was the moment the doors at the far end of the corridor opened, and the morning stopped being mine.

They brought the smugglers in through the loading entrance below — I heard them before I saw them, boot-storm on the marble stair, and then six soldiers came up escorting three men in river clothes, and behind the soldiers, unhurried, drying his hands on a handkerchief as though the men themselves were damp and unpleasant, came Commandant Ravel.

I had seen him once before, at the proclamation reading, from behind a crowd and a chimney's worth of distance: a grey man, medium everything, with the face of a schoolmaster who has graded so many examinations that he has forgotten any of the answers could surprise him. Up close he was worse, because up close you could see he was tired. Tyrants in the pictures are always furious. Nobody warns you that the dangerous ones are mostly weary, and kind to their horses, and thorough.

"Search them," Ravel said, and the soldiers did, right there in the corridor, efficient and shameless, and out of the smugglers' coats came wax cylinders, silk samples, letters in cipher that were almost certainly recipes, and one live duck, which is a story I have promised myself I will someday understand.

"Body searches," Ravel said to his sergeant, in the tone of a man noting a change to the house rules. "Effective now. Every person leaving this building, and every person entering who is not in uniform or known to the staff. The river brings us one smuggling ring and I am expected to believe it brought only one." He looked down at the duck. The duck looked back at him with what I can only describe as solidarity. "Confiscate everything. Log the men. And Sergeant — the searches are to be *thorough*. If anyone objects, they may object in a cell while we are thorough somewhere else."

The sergeant saluted and began posting himself at the head of the stairs, and I performed what I believe was a convincing imitation of a clerk remembering an urgent errand downstairs, and walked — did not run, *walked*, Perrin's roll and stride intact, heart going like a drum major — back toward the corridor's other end, where a service door stood open onto the back gallery.

Ninety seconds. That was all I had needed. Now I couldn't even stand at my own counter without a soldier's hands on me, and a soldier's hands would find the ledger in one pass, because oilcloth and linen strips are excellent against rain and useless against curiosity.

The service door led along the back of the building, above the loading yard, open to the December air through a colonnade of arches. Below me: carts, crates, the six soldiers' comrades, and at the yard's far corner, connected to the customhouse by a covered passage, the cooperage — the barrel works, where the customs service kept its own cooper to open and re-hoop seized casks, because confiscated tallow must be stored in something, and empires run on staves and hoops as much as gunpowder.

And the cooperage, friends, smelled like the inside of a wine dream.

I want it recorded that what happened next was improvisation, not courage. Courage implies choosing the harder thing over the easier. There was no easier thing. Behind me the corridor was filling with soldiers; ahead of me the passage sloped down into the cooperage, and the ledger against my ribs had begun to feel less like a book and more like a second skeleton I was wearing badly.

I went down the passage. The cooper was at lunch — the cooperage was empty except for shavings, hoop iron, adzes on the wall, and half-finished barrels standing around the floor like patient wooden people. In the corner lay a stack of returned casks awaiting repair, and among them, tipped on its side, a tallow cask with a sprung stave, empty, lid off, interior scraped clean and chalk-white and frankly enormous.

Mathematics happened. The ledger is this thick. A grown woman curled around a satchel is thicker, but a cask is a cask, and shame is lighter than discovery. I climbed in feet-first, drew the lid over myself from within — a maneuver I do not recommend, involving as it does the practitioner folding at the waist like a carpenter's rule — and lay in the dark smelling of old fat and new wood, listening to my own pulse conduct its orchestra.

They searched the cooperage. Of course they searched the cooperage; Ravel struck me even then as a man who searches everywhere, including places he has already searched, on principle. Boots crossed the floor above the thunder of my blood. A hand tested the lid of my cask — actually rested on it, the weight of a whole human arm pressing down through the wood and onto my shoulder — and then lifted away, because a tallow cask in a barrel works is a tallow cask in a barrel works, and boys look at faces, and I had none showing.

He moved on. Somewhere a whistle blew. The boots withdrew.

I gave the darkness another hundred heartbeats, then another fifty for interest, and then pushed the lid up an inch, and the light that came in was late-afternoon gold and the cooperage was empty and I climbed out of that cask stiff, stinking of rendered fat, laughing — actually laughing, silently, helplessly, the way you laugh when the alternative is screaming — because I was alive, and the ledger was safe, and I had gotten *out*, hadn't I? Out of the customhouse, out of the search, out clean.

Out, yes. But not forward. The book was still under my shirt. The courier tokens were still locked behind a clerk and a clock and now a sergeant with newly thorough instructions. And Ravel — I understood this with the clarity that arrives only in the bottom of a barrel — Ravel had not been reacting to smugglers at all. One ring of tallow-runners, and within the hour he had rewritten the rules of every doorway in Vellamont? A man that quick about a duck was a man who was looking for something already, and tightening the town the way you tighten a drumhead to hear where it rattles.

I slipped out the cooperage yard door into the alley behind the fish market, and walked home through the blue end of the day, reeking gloriously, nodding to the sentry at the corner like a man whose every step was accounted for.

It was full dark when I reached the shop, and the light was wrong. A lantern hung outside my own door — army-issue, steady, indifferent — and beneath it, nailed to the frame where our brass plate had hung for thirty years, a fresh paper seal bearing a wax stamp and one word in a soldier's round hand:

*SEALED.*

Below the seal, chalked directly on the doorframe in a small, precise, unhurried hand, someone had made two short marks. Not a tally. Measurements.

Someone had measured my door.
