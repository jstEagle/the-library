## 12. Ilse Draws the Shop

Ilse Brandt had been drawing towns since she was eleven years old, when her father — a surveyor of modest ambition and immoderate opinions — had set a pencil in her fist and told her that maps were the only honest form of power. *A king can lie to a thousand men,* he said, *but a map cannot lie to itself.* She had believed him for twenty years, more or less, and she still found that a good map gave her a feeling very close to peace. Lines. Angles. The world reduced to what could be measured.

Which was why the Yarrow print shop annoyed her.

It was not a dramatic annoyance. It sat on the corner of Rue des Fontaines like any other building in Vellamont — two stories, slate roof, shutters the color of old mustard, a sign with faded letters announcing PRINTING & BOOKBINDING BY APPOINTMENT OF NOBODY IN PARTICULAR, which Ilse suspected was the shop's idea of a joke. But when she laid her field book open that morning, propped against a rain barrel across the street, and compared her measurements to the tax assessment she'd copied from the town archive, the numbers did not agree.

The tax record gave the shop's ground floor as eleven and a half meters deep.

Her chain had given her nine and three-quarters.

Ilse frowned at the figures, erased nothing, and wrote them again in her small, upright hand: *9 m 74 cm. Tax roll says 11 m 50. Discrepancy: 1 m 76.*

One meter seventy-six. Somewhere behind that wall of type cases and paper stock, the print shop was hiding a space roughly the size of a coffin standing up. Or two coffins lying down. Or a barrel of brandy. Or — and here her pencil paused over the page, because Ilse's mind was a cartographer's mind and did not stop at brandy — a cavity large enough to hold anything the army's requisition officers had been told to look for.

She closed the field book and stood in the cold for a while, watching the shop. Smoke rose from its chimney, ordinary as bread. A cat slept on the windowsill of the upstairs room with the air of an animal who knew exactly how much trouble the household beneath it contained and had decided it was none of his business.

"You are staring," she told herself quietly, in her own language, which she did sometimes when no one could hear. "Staring is not measuring."

But it was a beginning.

* * *

The problem with occupying a town, Ilse had learned in four weeks of occupation, was that everyone in it became either an obstacle or a resource, and the classification changed daily. The baker was a resource until the bread ran short; then he was an obstacle. The ferryman was an obstacle until someone needed to cross the river at night; then he was a resource. The commandant, Ravel, was both simultaneously, which Ilse found exhausting.

And the Yarrow boy — the print shop's keeper, who signed the ledger, ordered the ink, and had never once been seen by anyone in Vellamont at the same time as the late Master Yarrow — was currently classified, in Ilse's private notes, under a third heading she hadn't invented a name for yet.

She had met him properly only twice. Once at the requisition office, where he'd surrendered the shop's commercial records with the smooth compliance of a man handing over a decoy duck, and once by accident on the bridge, where they'd stood side by side for a full minute watching the river carry ice southward, and neither had said a word, and Ilse had noticed — because noticing was her whole profession — that his hands were stained black to the second knuckle, and that he held his shoulders the way people hold their shoulders when they are carrying something heavy that no one else can see.

Also, and this was irrelevant, purely observational, filed away under professional thoroughness: he had very fine eyes. Grey, steady, with a kind of dry humor living at the corners of them, as though the whole occupied town were a slightly badly drawn map and he alone could see the errors.

Irrelevant. Filed.

Now she crossed the street, rapped twice on the shop door with the flat of her compass case, and waited.

The bolt slid back slowly enough to suggest deliberation. Dominic Yarrow opened the door to the width of a hand. "Shop's not taking orders. Army took our paper allocation."

"I'm not here for orders." Ilse held up her field book and, after a moment's thought, added the truth, because lying to this particular face felt like smudging a clean line. "I'm here to measure your shop."

A pause. The grey eyes went from her face to the compass case to the field book and back, doing sums. Ilse could practically hear the abacus.

"Measure it?"

"Ground floor. Interior walls. It's routine." This was true. It was also, she was aware, a thing that could be checked, which made it the best kind of lie's cousin: the truth deployed strategically. "Every commercial building gets surveyed for billeting capacity. Yours has the deepest footprint on the street."

Something flickered there — quick, quickly mastered. "Deep foundations," the Yarrow boy said. "Old buildings lie."

"So do old tax rolls," Ilse agreed pleasantly. "May I come in?"

He stood aside. The door opened on warmth and the smell Ilse would remember for the rest of her life, though she didn't know it yet: ink and oil and hot metal, paper dust, the particular sharp sweetness of type metal cooling in a mold. It smelled, she thought, the way a library smells if a library were alive and working for its supper.

* * *

Dominic watched the soldier walk her shop and hated every minute of it, and hid the hatred under the same flat courtesy she used on bill collectors.

The woman — Brandt, the cartographer, the one from the bridge — moved through the workroom unlike any officer Dominic had seen. The requisition sergeants had swept through like weather, all noise and no attention. Brandt moved like a reader. Her eyes touched each object once, completely: the composing stone, the galley of sorted type, the imposing frame, the press.

The press.

Dominic kept her breathing even and her hands busy, sorting a case of brevier she had absolutely no need to sort. *Nine and three-quarters,* she thought. *She'll have measured outside too. She knows the numbers don't add up or she wouldn't be here with her little book.* Behind her, under the proofing press her father had bought secondhand and she herself had spent one long terrified August night dismantling with a joiner's tools, lay a cavity one meter seventy-six deep, and inside that cavity, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed linen and her father's old apron, lay forty-one lives.

"Your press," Brandt said.

"What about it."

"It's a proof press. Columbian pattern?" She ran one finger along the frame, and Dominic's heart performed a small gymnastic exhibition. "I didn't know Vellamont had a printer who did proofs. Proof work usually means a bigger house upstream."

"We do jobbing work for the mills." *Breathe. Count. Type case, ink slab, tympan.* "Labels. Bills of lading."

"Mm," said Brandt, and wrote something in her field book, and Dominic aged a year.

The inspection took forty minutes. Brandt measured everything — the front room, the stairs, the width of the workroom, the depth of it, pacing with a two-meter chain and calling numbers to herself in her own language under her breath, a habit she seemed unaware of, and which Dominic found, to her considerable irritation, rather charming. She asked questions about the chimney flue. She asked about the cellar. She stood in the middle of the workroom floor with her head tipped back, reading the ceiling joists like lines of text, and Dominic realized with a lurch that the woman was not surveying for billets at all, that billeting surveys did not require anyone to read ceiling joists, and that she had perhaps thirty seconds to decide what to do about it.

What she did was offer tea, because terror had apparently eaten the strategic part of her brain.

"No, thank you." Brandt snapped the field book shut. Then she looked at Dominic directly, and something in the look had changed — the professional flatness gone, replaced by an attention so total it was nearly physical. "Your shop measures nine and three-quarters meters deep inside. Your tax record says eleven and a half. That's a discrepancy of one meter and seventy-six centimeters, and it is behind your press."

The fire popped. On the windowsill, the cat opened one eye, assessed the situation, and went back to sleep.

"I don't know what you mean," Dominic said. "Old buildings—"

"Lie. Yes. You mentioned." Brandt tucked the pencil behind her ear. And then she did the last thing in the world Dominic expected: she walked to the door, shot the bolt, and drew the shutter closed over the front window, shutting out the street.

Then she turned around, unslung her satchel, set it on the composing stone, and folded her hands on top of it like a schoolmistress.

"I am going to say something once," she said. "I have not written it down. I will not write it down unless you make me. Do you understand me?"

Dominic understood several things at once, most of them contradictory: that she was alone in a locked room with an army officer; that the army officer had just confessed to withholding information from the army; that her heart was going hard enough to be audible from the street; and that the correct move in this game was impossible to calculate because she could not tell what game it was.

"Say it, then," she said, and was proud of how level it came out.

Brandt took a breath. For the first time since entering the shop, the cartographer looked uncertain — a small crack in the drafting, a line drawn freehand where everything else had been ruled.

"There is a cavity behind your press," she said. "I have known since Tuesday. I mapped your exterior on Tuesday, and the interior measurements yesterday confirmed it, and I have spent two days deciding whether to report it, and I have decided — provisionally, stupidly, against every regulation I swore to — to ask you one question first."

The fire popped again. Somewhere out on the river a barge horn sounded, long and low, like the town clearing its throat.

"Whose names?"

* * *

Later — years later, in fact, in rooms with different fires in different countries — Ilse would try to explain to Dominic what those six words cost her, and she would never quite manage it, because the explanation required describing the two days in between.

Tuesday night she had sat in the garrison mapping room with her finished plan of Vellamont spread before her and the discrepancy circled in red, and she had done what her father taught her: followed the line to its logical end. A hidden cavity in a print shop. A print shop whose records had been surrendered with suspicious promptness. A requisition proclamation demanding all commercial ledgers within three days. Therefore: the cavity likely contained the ledger, and the ledger, per Commandant Ravel's standing order, was to be surrendered or burned, and its contents — names, debts, aliases, whatever web of quiet criminal mercies small river towns wove to survive their governments — were evidence.

Therefore, duty said: report. Report tonight. A sergeant, a crowbar, ten minutes' work.

She had even picked up her pen. She had even dipped it.

And then she had thought — not reasoned, *thought*, the way one thinks of a person instead of a problem — of the man on the bridge with ink to his knuckles, holding his shoulders like a man carrying something heavy. Of the smooth compliance at the requisition office. Of the fact that Ravel wanted the ledger burned not because its contents were false but because they were *true*, and true names in a hungry revolution were kindling.

She had put the pen down. She had not reported it. She had spent Wednesday being aggressively normal — mapping the tannery row, eating the garrison soup, correcting a lieutenant's bearings — while inside her chest a argument raged between the girl her father raised and the officer the army had made, and both of them used her voice, and neither won.

Thursday morning she had lost the argument to a pair of grey eyes, knocked on a print shop door, and asked her one question.

* * *

In the locked and shuttered workroom, Dominic Yarrow stood very still.

*Whose names.* Not *what are you hiding* — that question had an answer ready, prepared weeks ago, rehearsed in the dark: spare type stock, nothing valuable, army can search if it likes. Not *where is the ledger*, which was the same question wearing a uniform. *Whose names* was a different animal entirely. It was a question asked by someone who already knew what the ledger was, and was asking instead what it meant — whether it was contraband or evidence, treasure or burden, whether the person holding it was a smuggler or a keeper.

There was, Dominic discovered, only one honest answer, and it frightened her more than any lie.

"Forty-one," she said. "Forty-one names. Widow Marchand, whose name in that book isn't Marchand and hasn't been for nineteen years. Old Perrin at the mill, who paid a debt twice and never complained. The Duval brothers, who aren't brothers. A wet-nurse, a ferryman, two deserters from the *last* war, and a child born on the wrong side of a bed the wrong church approved of." Her voice stayed low and even, but her hands had found the edge of the composing stone and gripped it. "Some of them owe money. Some of them are owed. All of them trusted my father, and now they trust me, and if that book burns or lands on a tribunal table in the wrong hands, half of them hang and the other half lose everything they've built hiding inside names I gave them."

Silence. The cartographer's face did something complicated and mostly invisible, the way ice does something complicated and mostly invisible just before the thaw.

"The army believes that ledger contains traitors," Ilse said quietly.

"The army believes every book contains traitors. It's cheaper than believing books contain people."

Another silence, longer. Outside, boots passed on the rue — a patrol, unhurried, four men, fading. Dominic counted them past out of pure habit and heard Brandt do the same, a half-beat behind her, soldier's habit answering printer's habit, and something about that near-simultaneous counting cracked the tension in the room like a knuckle.

"My brother," Ilse said abruptly, "was arrested in '89. Wrong street, wrong evening, wrong name on a list. He was in the Abbaye prison for seven months." She was looking at the wall of type cases as though she could read through them. "Our family paid. Quietly. Through a man who knew a man. I was sixteen and I was told never to ask how, and I never did, and I have always known anyway." She turned back. "If your ledger is what I think it is, then somewhere in it there is a record of how things like that happen. Who pays whom. Which pardons were bought and which were earned. Forty-one names in Vellamont — but the shape of the book, the *pattern* of it — that goes further than this town. Doesn't it."

It wasn't really a question, and Dominic didn't treat it as one. "Yes."

Ilse nodded slowly. She unslung her satchel, and Dominic's whole body tensed — but the woman only took out her field book and, holding Dominic's eyes, tore out the page with the interior measurements of the print shop. She struck a match from the tin on the mantel — *my mother's matches*, Dominic noted absurdly, *she's using my mother's matches* — and fed the page to the fire. It curled, flared, and was gone.

"Your shop," Ilse said, when the last corner went to ash, "measures eleven and a half meters deep, exactly as taxed. Old buildings lie. I've written that down now, so it must be true." A ghost of a smile crossed her face, there and gone, dry as the humor that lived at the corners of her eyes. "I will survey the tannery row again tomorrow. And the cooperage, and the gate quarter. I will be slow and thorough and visible, and if anyone asks, the print shop's billeting capacity is four men and a stove, and I recommend against it, the chimney draws badly."

"Why?" The word came out of Dominic raw, unplanned. "Why burn your own report? You don't know me. You know less than nothing about what's in that book. You're throwing away your—" she gestured, helplessly, at the ash, the uniform, the entire architecture of the woman's life "—everything. For what?"

Ilse Brandt picked up her satchel and slung it over her shoulder, and for a moment she looked, not like a cartographer or an occupier, but like a sixteen-year-old girl who had once been told never to ask how, and had finally been given permission to answer.

"For a look at the pattern," she said. "Someday. When you decide I've earned it. That's the price, Yarrow — not payment, just the truth eventually, and the choice of when it's safe to give it." She slid the bolt back and opened the door onto cold light and the smell of the river. Then she paused, half through the doorway, and added, in the tone of someone appending a footnote to a map:

"And fix your shutter hinge. The left one. It hangs crooked, and crooked hinges are how suspicions start."

The door closed behind her. Dominic stood in the warm dimness of her father's shop, listening to the boots fade up the rue, and did not move for a long time.

Behind the press, one meter and seventy-six centimeters of hidden Vellamont kept its silence, holding forty-one names and one new and terrible uncertainty: that the army's own mapmaker now knew where the ledger slept — and that tomorrow morning, Ilse Brandt would have to stand in the garrison mess and eat breakfast beside the men she had just betrayed.

Dominic banked the fire, touched the ash where a page had been — a page of her own measurements, freely given to the flames — and thought, with a feeling too tangled for any single name:

*I have either made an ally or handed a noose to the only person in this town who can draw it accurately.*

Upstairs, the clock in her father's old room struck four. Two days remained before the surrender deadline — and somewhere across town, in the garrison mapping room, Commandant Ravel was, at that very moment, asking his aide to bring him the survey reports.
