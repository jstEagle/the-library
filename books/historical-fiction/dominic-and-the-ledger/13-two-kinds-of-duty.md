## 13. Two Kinds of Duty

Ilse Brandt had been taught to draw straight lines before she had been taught to pray.

Her father, a surveyor of the northern forests, had put a rule in her hand at six years old and said, *The land doesn't lie, little bird. Only people do. So learn the land first, and you will always know where people have been.* It was the closest thing to a catechism her family kept, and she had carried it across two borders and one war into the army's cartographic corps, where a steady hand and an honest eye were worth more than piety.

An honest eye was the problem.

She sat in the requisitioned customs office with the shutters half-drawn against the afternoon glare, her map of Vellamont spread before her, and she could not stop thinking about a page.

Not a map page. A ledger page.

Three nights ago she had stood in the Yarrow print shop with her compass in her hand and asked one question — *whose names?* — and Dominic Yarrow, that narrow-shouldered, stubborn, impossible creature, had opened the book to answer her. Ilse had meant only to glance. A cartographer learns to take in a whole sheet at once; it is the trade. Coastline, river, contour — the eye sweeps and the mind keeps everything, whether the mind consents or not.

The eye had swept. The mind had kept.

*Emmerich Brandt.*

Her brother's name, written in a careful clerk's hand she now recognized as Dominic's own, dated four years back: *Received of E. Brandt, through the hand of Herr Vogel, the sum of sixty crowns, entered here as settlement of all claims, the original record being by agreement destroyed. Let this entry stand as the true one.*

Sixty crowns. Emmerich had told her he'd lost it at cards. He'd wept over it, actually — her big brother, who could carry a sack of grain under each arm, weeping at their mother's table because he'd been a fool and lost sixty crowns to some river smuggler whose name he would never speak aloud. She had comforted him. She had thought, privately and unkindly, *what an idiot.*

He hadn't lost it at cards. He had bought his way out of something — she still didn't know what; the entry didn't say, and the print shop smelled of ink and tallow and had gone very quiet around her — and the proof of the purchase lived in a hollowed-out press in a town he had sworn never to visit again.

And now she knew. And knowing was a kind of wound that did not bleed so much as seep.

She pressed her pencil flat against the map and drew a line along the mill race. Straight. True. The line did not care what she knew. That was the mercy of lines.

"Ilse." Her colleague Pellatier stood in the doorway, coat on, collar up. "Ravel wants the warehouse district surveyed again before the frost. You've the steadiest hand."

"Tell him I'll have it tomorrow."

"He wants it tonight."

"Then tell him the frost doesn't wait for me and neither should he."

Pellatier grinned, saluted ironically, and left. Ilse looked at her line along the mill race and found her hand had drifted three degrees off true without her noticing. She rubbed it out with the heel of her palm until the paper shone gray, and started again.

*The land doesn't lie. Only people do.*

She was lying to herself about how long she'd known. That was the humiliating part. She had noticed the shop's dimensions on her second survey — the interior wall sitting a forearm's width proud of the tax record — and she had come back a third time, and a fourth, telling herself each visit was professional rigor. Then she had stood inside the shop watching Dominic's hands move among the type cases, quick and certain, ink to the second knuckle, and had thought nothing rigorous whatsoever.

Duty, she had always believed, was simple. Not easy — simple. You held your oath the way her father held a chain: taut, both hands, eyes forward. The army fed her, paid her, gave her instruments better than any she could have afforded in civilian life. In exchange she drew honest maps and asked no questions about what the maps were for. That was the bargain every professional made, and most slept fine under it.

But the oath had not mentioned this. There was no clause for *you will report the woman whose ledger holds your brother's soul, who let you hold the book, who watched your face while you read your own family's name and then said, quietly, "I'm sorry — I can't tell you what he paid for," and meant it like an apology for the weather.*

Ilse set down the pencil.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not balance, and she was a woman who balanced things for a living. If she reported the hidden ledger — and it was certainly hidden, certainly contraband, certainly the exact object Commandant Ravel wanted burned — then Emmerich's entry went into the fire too. It sounded like nothing. A settled debt, an old transaction. But entries were evidence, and evidence was rope, and rope found necks. Ravel's men had already pulled two families apart on thinner paper. If the ledger burned clean, Emmerich's purchase became rumor forever, deniable, safe. If it survived into tribunal hands — which was clearly what Dominic intended, the madwoman, hauling forty-one lives around a occupied town sewn into sailcloth — then it might save thirty-nine neighbors and hang one Brandt.

Or it might not. Tribunals were weather. She had served under three commands in five years and watched verdicts turn on less than a bad dinner.

So: report the ledger, and possibly destroy her brother. Stay silent, and betray the uniform she had worn since she was nineteen, the oath she had taken with her father's rule in her pocket like a talisman. There were no straight lines between those two points. Every route she plotted bent somewhere she didn't want to look.

She laughed once, short and ugly, alone in the customs office, and a clerk two desks down glanced up. She arranged her face into bureaucratic mildness and began inking the warehouse footprints.

* * *

Dominic found her at dusk, or perhaps Ilse found Dominic — afterwards she could never reconstruct which of them had sought the other out, only that they ended up walking the levee together in the blue hour, breath smoking, the river running black beneath the ice shelves.

"You look like a woman doing sums," Dominic said.

"I'm a cartographer. Sums are my whole personality."

"What are they adding up to?"

"Nothing that divides evenly."

They walked. The army's cook fires glowed along the quay like a string of low orange beads. Somewhere a fife was practicing badly. Dominic had her collar up and her cap pulled low, and Ilse was struck — not for the first time — by how completely the disguise worked, how a person could stand beside another person for weeks and see only the shape they presented. She knew what was under the cap now. She had known since the night in the shop, when the question *whose names* had cracked something open in both of them and Dominic had answered with truth instead of lies, as though honesty were simply the cheaper option.

"You read the page," Dominic said. It wasn't a question either.

"Yes."

"And?"

"And I'm deciding whether to hate you for showing me."

Dominic stopped walking. The lamplight from the ferry road caught half her face. "I almost didn't. I turned past it twice first. But you asked me a fair question and I don't lie to people who ask fair questions. It's the only rule I have left."

"It's a good rule." Ilse kicked a chunk of ice into the water. "It's a terrible rule. Do you know what my brother told my mother? That he lost sixty crowns at faro. He cried at her table, Dominic. I brought him soup."

Something moved across Dominic's expression — guilt, maybe, or its cousin — and Ilse seized on it with more cruelty than she intended: "Did he cry when he paid you? Or does your trade not go in for that sort of thing?"

"That's unfair."

"It's entirely fair. That's the problem with it."

They stood facing each other on the frozen levee, and the practiced fife squeaked through some march across the water, and Ilse felt the strange double vision of anger: she was furious and she also wanted, quite badly, to take Dominic's cold hands in hers, and the two feelings sat in her chest like passengers who refused to acknowledge one another.

"My father took the money," Dominic said at last, quietly. "I only wrote the entry. I was fourteen. I remember because my hands shook and he made me write it twice — the first copy he burned himself, right there on the stove, so there'd be only one true record." She looked up. "Your brother came at night, hood up, shaking worse than I was. Whatever it was he'd done — and Papa never told me, and I never asked, because asking is how ledgers get you killed — it must have been bad enough that sixty crowns felt like salvation to him. Your father's rule about the land? Mine said ink outlives men. He believed if a thing was written truly, it couldn't be used falsely. I've spent four years believing him." A pause. "Last week I started doubting."

"The army has that effect."

"No." Dominic's voice didn't rise. "You have that effect. You stood in my shop and read your own brother's name and you didn't run to Ravel, and you didn't spit at me, and you didn't laugh. You just asked whose names. Nobody asks anymore, Ilse. They assume, or they accuse, but they don't ask." She pulled her cap lower. "I don't know what that means. I've been trying not to find out, because I'm carrying a book through three checkpoints and I can't afford —"

"Can't afford what?"

"Nothing. Forget it."

"Dominic."

"Don't say my name like that on a public levee."

"Whose names?" Ilse said again, softer this time, the same question, the same crack in the same place.

Dominic told her. Not all of it — not Marchand's forgery, not the aliases, not the debts forgiven in whispers — but enough. Forty-one entries that were not debts at all but lives folded flat, kept dry, kept safe, by a girl who had inherited her father's hand and his stubbornness and his conviction that a written truth was a shelter. Enough that Ilse understood, standing there with her ears going numb, that the ledger was not a record of Vellamont's sins. It was the only reason Vellamont still had so few.

"I should report you," Ilse said.

"I know."

"If I report you, my brother burns with the book."

"I know that too. I've done the sums. They're your sums, but I've done them."

"And if I don't report you, I'm finished if anyone ever finds out, and you're finished anyway, and probably me with you, and none of the routes divide evenly."

"No," Dominic agreed. "None of them do."

The awful thing — the thing Ilse would think about for years afterward, on roads she could not yet imagine — was that even now, mid-quarrel, frozen and furious and professionally compromised in every direction, some warm animal part of her was merely happy to be standing next to this person in the dark. Duty said one thing. The other thing, the unnamed thing, said nothing at all; it just leaned slightly toward Dominic Yarrow like a compass needle finding north.

Her father had warned her about needles, too. *A compass is honest,* he'd said, *but it will point at anything magnetic. Check what you're standing near before you trust the reading.*

She was standing near Dominic. The needle was useless. She laughed, and this time it came out human.

"What?" Dominic demanded.

"Nothing. My father would have liked you. He distrusted everyone, so you'd have gotten along splendidly." Ilse turned up the levee toward the garrison lights. "I haven't decided anything. Understand that. I haven't decided, and I won't decide tonight, and if Ravel asks me directly I will not perjure myself for you — I'll simply be elsewhere, being surveyed."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," Dominic said.

"It wasn't romantic. It was cowardice with a schedule."

"It was romantic," Dominic insisted, and there was that grin, the crooked one, the one that had no business working on someone carrying forty-one contraband souls. "Goodnight, Cartographer."

Ilse walked back to her quarters through the falling dark and did not look back, because looking back would have decided something, and she had promised herself she wouldn't.

* * *

She did not sleep. Instead, by candle stub, she did what she always did when her mind refused to divide: she drew.

Not Vellamont. She drew the northern forest of her childhood, the survey grid her father had laid out the summer she was nine, the blaze marks on the birches, the creek that flooded every spring and redrew its own banks so that the maps were perpetually, gloriously wrong. She drew Emmerich at fourteen, hauling the transit case, complaining. She drew her mother's kitchen. She drew the table where her brother had wept over sixty crowns and a bowl of soup, and she understood suddenly, with the pencil still moving, that she had never once asked him what the money was for. She had accepted his story because accepting was easier, because her brother's shame was not her business, because the family map had a blank space there and she had always, obediently, drawn around it.

Cartographers were taught that blank spaces were the enemy. *Terra incognita* bred monsters. Fill the space, measure it, name it — that was the whole discipline. And here she was, twenty-six years old, discovering a blank space in her own family the size of sixty crowns, and wanting desperately to leave it unmarked because marked, it might hurt him.

That was the moment the oath finally tore — not loudly, not dramatically, just the small sound of stitching giving way. Because she realized she could no longer say what her duty protected. The oath pointed at the army; the army pointed at Ravel; Ravel pointed at a fire. And somewhere behind all that pointing stood a woman with ink on her knuckles who had kept a town's secrets dry for four years and asked, when she could easily have lied, *whose names?*

Ilse blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the garrison clock strike eleven, twelve, one.

In the morning, she knew, there would be new orders. Ravel had the look of a man receiving letters he didn't like, and men like that made other men choose sides whether they wished to or not. Whatever came next — searches, sealings, burnings — she would need to know, before it arrived, which side of the line she stood on. She was a woman who drew lines for a living. It was past time she drew this one.

She got up, dressed in the dark, and took from her instrument case the one thing she had not yet dared to use: a sheet of official requisition paper, army watermarked, with the corps stamp she was authorized to apply to survey orders.

She laid it flat on the desk and stared at it for a long time.

Outside, the frost was coming down hard on Vellamont, and somewhere across the sleeping town, under a floor or behind a wall or sewn into a bolt of sailcloth, a ledger waited with her brother's name in it, kept safe by a liar with honest handwriting — and Ilse picked up her pen.
