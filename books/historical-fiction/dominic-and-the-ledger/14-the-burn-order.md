## 14. The Burn Order

Commandant Ravel had a theory about paperwork, which was that all the miseries of the French nation could be traced to the invention of the file folder. He had expressed this theory to his aide, Lieutenant Perrin, eleven times since October, and Perrin had developed the habit of nodding at the precise moment Ravel drew breath, which allowed the theory to proceed without interruption and permitted Perrin to think about lunch.

On the fourth of December, the post rider brought a packet that made even Ravel wish for the simplicity of folders. It was from the Bureau of Public Safety, western section, and it began — as their correspondence always did — with an assurance of fraternal regard that read like a man apologizing before hitting you.

"Read it aloud," Ravel said. "If I must be insulted, I would prefer it in someone else's voice."

Perrin cleared his throat. "'To the Commandant of the occupied district of Vellamont. The Committee notes with concern the absence of measurable progress in the identification of enemies of the Revolution within your jurisdiction.'"

"Measurable progress," Ravel repeated. "As though treason were wheat and I had reported a poor harvest."

"'You are reminded,'" Perrin continued, his voice taking on the careful flatness of a man walking across ice, "'that three suspected conspirators already fled Vellamont in the summer prior to occupation, that the town's commercial records have been seized and catalogued, and that the year's end approaches. The Committee expects that the new calendar will open upon a district cleansed. Fraternity. Equality. Signed, etcetera.'"

Perrin set the paper down on the desk between them, on top of the other papers, in the manner of a man laying down a card he hopes will not be called.

Ravel looked out the window. Snow had begun again, the fine dry sort that didn't accumulate so much as arrive and reconsider. Below, in the square, two soldiers were arguing with a woman over a cart of cabbages, and losing, which pleased him obscurely. He had been a soldier for twenty-six years and an administrator for four, and he had learned that the second occupation was harder, because at least an enemy soldier will admit he is your enemy.

"You know what will happen," Ravel said, "if I send them traitors."

"I imagine they'll be tried," Perrin said.

"Summarily. And if the traitors are invented — and they will have to be invented, because Vellamont's greatest criminal enterprise is a man who sells eels short of measure — then some months from now a different committee will ask why I manufactured evidence, and some months after that a different commandant will write to *them* complaining of *my* insufficient rigor, and the wheel will go around." He tapped the desk. "Names, Perrin. That is how revolutions eat their own tails. Not with knives. With lists. Someone denounces someone, who names two others to save himself, who name four each, and inside a year half the town has informed on the other half and the only people untouched are the ones too dull to have opinions."

"You could refuse," Perrin said, in the tone of a man suggesting, gently, that one could also refuse to breathe.

"I could. And they would send a younger man who reads Voltaire badly and believes what he reads in reports." Ravel pulled the packet toward him. "No. We do it my way or we do it their way, but either way we do it."

He rose and went to the cabinet where the requisitioned records were kept — three shelves of ledgers, day-books, debt rolls, the accounts of the salt warehouse and the tannery and both mills, all of it gathered in those first orderly weeks when the army still pretended it had come as librarians. He had read most of it. It was the reading that had convinced him to burn the Yarrow ledger.

Because the Yarrow ledger was different. Every other book in town recorded what people owed in money. That one recorded what people owed in fear.

He had gone through it twice in the first week, alone, after hours, and found its architecture beautiful and terrible. Debts crossed out with a notation — *forgiven, no cause to record* — which was mercy, but mercy with a memory. Names that did not match any name on any tax roll, entered beside dates and small sums, and once you understood the pattern you understood that these were the hidden people: the smuggler living under his dead brother's identity, the widow drawing her husband's pension under a name she'd bought fair, the boy from the south with a warrant on him who now swept a church and answered to Pierre. Forty-one entries of that character, give or take. Forty-one people whose entire legal existence lived in one book kept by a print shop that never seemed to have a printer in it.

That was why he had ordered it burned. Not destroyed — he was not a vandal — but committed to the fire formally, publicly, with a receipt. A book like that, in the wrong hands, was not evidence. It was ammunition. Burned, it became ash, and ash accuses no one. He had drafted the order in November and had felt, drafting it, something close to peace. It would be, he had told Perrin, the only genuinely kind act the Revolution would perform in Vellamont, and it would look exactly like tyranny, which was the great joke of administration.

Now he took the draft out of the drawer, unfolded it, and read it again. *The ledger of the establishment Yarrow, containing matter seditious and compromising to public trust, to be consumed by fire in the market square, witnesses to be drawn from the town council.*

"Perrin," he said. "When I gave this order, what did you think?"

Perrin considered. Perrin was twenty-four and honest in the specific way of young men who have not yet learned that honesty is a uniform accessory. "I thought it was strange," he said. "Burning a ledger instead of using it. Sir."

"You thought it was a waste."

"I thought—" Perrin stopped, recalculated, and chose truth. "Yes, sir."

"So does the Committee. Only they think everything unexploited is a waste, including people." Ravel held the draft over the stove. The paper began to brown at the corners, and he watched it the way he had once watched orders to advance, with the particular nausea of a man who understands precisely what he is doing and why it cannot be helped. "There is a window, Perrin. Between now and the new year, the Committee wants names, and they want them documented, cross-referenced, defensible in a tribunal. This book is the only document in this district that could survive a tribunal. Which means the kindest thing I ever planned to do has become the necessary thing, and I find I cannot tell the difference anymore from the inside. That is the part they don't warn you about. Not the cruelty. The confusion."

The corner caught. He let it burn halfway up the page and then dropped it into the grate, where it finished without ceremony.

"New order," Ravel said. "The Yarrow ledger is reclassified. Evidentiary material, priority one. It is not to leave the district, not to be copied, not to be read by anyone below the rank of captain without my signature." He sat and pulled fresh paper toward him. "And the shop is to be sealed."

"Sealed, sir? The print shop?" Perrin blinked. "What are we sealing it *for*? The press is still in there. Well." He consulted a memorandum. "A press. Sergeant Coignet reported it's old enough to print the Ten Commandments, and possibly did."

"The press stays. Everything stays, exactly where it is, under seal. If the ledger is to be evidence, then the place it was kept becomes part of the evidence, and I will not have it disturbed by some corporal with a taste for souvenirs." He was writing as he talked, in the small backward-sloping hand that his superiors mistook for efficiency. "Nail the doors. Wax the seals. A sentry outside, rotated daily, instructed to be bored. Do you know what makes a good sentry, Perrin?"

"Boredom, sir?"

"*Visible* boredom. A sentry who looks interested invites curiosity, and curiosity is how rumors start, and rumors are how forty-one quiet people in this town learn that their book still exists. While it sits sealed behind a nail, it protects no one and damns no one. It's only paper." He signed. "It's when books start moving that men start hanging."

Perrin collected the order, then hesitated at the door with the expression of a man who has one more card and no good reason to play it. "Sir — the Yarrow person. The son. The young cutter who keeps the shop. He came to surrender the shop's records on collection day like everyone else, but there's never been a follow-up interview. Most households got one."

"Did they."

"It may look irregular. If the Committee asks why the one suspicious establishment in town wasn't questioned—"

"Then the Committee may ask me, and I will explain that interviewing a frightened type-cutter produces nothing but a frightened type-cutter, whereas leaving him alone produces the illusion that we are not interested, which is worth more than any interview." Ravel almost believed it. There had been something about the Yarrow boy on collection day — he had seen him only briefly, across the square, a thin figure with ink-stained fingers answering the sergeant's questions with a fluency that had made the sergeant report, baffled, that the boy had corrected his French twice. "Besides. The seal will do our questioning for us. If the Yarrow boy is merely a boy, he will grumble about rent and carry on. And if he is anything else—"

He stopped. Outside, the cabbage negotiation had concluded; the woman was pushing her cart away at a pace suggesting total victory. Snow kept arriving and reconsidering.

"—then he is presently wondering," Ravel finished quietly, "what a sealed door means. And a man wondering is a man watching us, Perrin, which means for the first time in this whole dreary occupation, the advantage is ours. We know something. He only knows that we might."

* * *

The sealing of the Yarrow print shop took place at nine in the morning and attracted, by Ravel's deliberate design, almost no attention. No drums, no proclamation, no squad — just Corporal Vasseur with a hammer, a sack of nails, two strips of paper bearing wax seals, and instructions to behave as though he were closing a barn. Perrin supervised. Ravel watched from the garrison window through a spyglass, for the same reason he read his own intercepted mail: to see what people did when they believed no one important was looking.

What the town did was walk past. That was nearly all. The baker slowed, and the tanner's man slowed, and Widow Marchand did not slow at all but crossed to the far side of the street, which was more conspicuous than slowing and told Ravel that she at least understood what a seal meant before anyone explained it to her.

Then, near noon, the Yarrow boy himself came.

Through the glass Ravel watched him stop at the mouth of the lane and stand very still, the way a man stands when he has walked a familiar road and found it suddenly fenced. Small-built for a printer's son — apprentice's build, all the lifting done by the master, presumably — hat low, collar up against the snow. He stood perhaps two full minutes, which Ravel timed on his watch, and did not approach the sentry, did not ask questions, did not do any of the things an innocent tradesman does when his livelihood is nailed shut. He simply looked, the way a cartographer looks, and then turned and walked back toward the river with a basket on his arm that had, until that morning, presumably had some errand attached to it.

"He didn't come to the door," Perrin said, appearing at Ravel's shoulder with tea nobody had requested.

"No."

"If he'd asked, Vasseur had the explanation ready. Requisition pending inventory. Very dull."

"Yes. That is what troubles me." Ravel lowered the spyglass. In the lens's shrinking circle the lane emptied, the seals hung pale against dark wood, and the snow went on with its patient erasures. "An innocent man argues, Perrin. Arguing is the privilege of innocence — he has the town's whole sympathy to draw on, the landlady, the priest, anyone. A guilty man avoids. But this one didn't avoid either. He *studied*. Nails, seals, sentry's height, sight lines. I have seen that exact stillness twice in my life. Once on a scout assessing a bridge, and once on a man deciding whether to run."

"You think he'll run, sir?"

"I think," Ravel said, setting the glass down and reaching, without enthusiasm, for the Committee's next letter, "that I have just taken the only book in this town that can hang forty-one people, and locked it behind one nail and one bored corporal, and called it safety. And I think that somewhere across this square a young man with clean grammar is doing arithmetic — what the seal costs him, what the risk of moving it costs him, and which of us wants it more." He dipped his pen. "Mercy was simpler. Write that down somewhere, in case anyone ever asks. Mercy was a great deal simpler, and I gave it up for a list."

He began to draft his reply to the Committee: *progress is anticipated; evidentiary material of unusual quality has been secured; the new year will open upon a district prepared to account for itself.* All of it true. That was the devil of it. Every word true, and every word a step down a stair he could no longer see the bottom of — and below, faint through the floorboards of the world, he could already hear the sound of a door being pried, very quietly, at night.
