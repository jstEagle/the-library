## 15. Sealed Doors

They came at noon, which Dominic thought was a cruelty of scheduling more than anything else. Men who nail shut your life ought at least to have the decency to do it at dusk, when the light is forgiving and the neighbors are indoors. But no. Noon. Bright, hard, honest noon, the kind of light her father had always said was the enemy of bad type and worse lies, and under that light four soldiers of the revolutionary army stood in the street outside the Yarrow print shop with a hammer, a crate of nails, and enough pine boards to build a coffin for something larger than a man.

Dominic stood in the doorway of the apothecary's across the lane, where she had gone — she would later insist — merely to purchase a stick of licorice root, and not at all because she had woken that morning with a feeling in her chest like a door swinging on one hinge.

"Master Yarrow," the sergeant had told her an hour earlier, when the order was posted on the customs house door and half the town had gathered to read it in the particular silence of people reading their own funerals, "the premises are requisitioned pending inventory. No entry permitted."

*Pending inventory.* She had almost laughed. There was nothing left to inventory. The army had already taken the paper stock, the ink casks, two cases of type they'd mistaken for spare musket parts, and the good ladder. What remained behind the sealed doors was a broken proofing press with a hollow in its belly, and inside that hollow, wrapped in sailcloth and oilcloth and her own winter scarf, the ledger of Vellamont — forty-one names, forty-one lives, every debt forgiven and every alias forged and every secret kept by a girl who signed herself *son*.

And now they were going to nail it shut.

She sucked the licorice root and watched.

The soldiers worked without malice, which was somehow worse. If they had been cruel about it she could have hated them, and hatred is a kind of company. Instead the youngest of them — a boy with straw-colored hair who had bought bread from Madame Fenn three streets over and been overcharged like everyone else — held the first board against the doorframe with the earnest concentration of a boy doing chores, and the corporal drove the nails, and the sound went through the lane like gunshots that nobody flinched from because everybody understood: this was how things were done now. Not bullets. Boards.

The first board crossed the window. The second crossed the door.

"That's Master Yarrow's shop," said a voice beside her. Widow Marchand had materialized the way she did, without footstep or warning, smelling of vinegar and grievance. "They can't do that."

"They just did," Dominic said.

"I mean legally."

"So do I."

Marchand sniffed, which from her was a paragraph of commentary. She stayed, though. That was the thing about Marchand that Dominic could never reconcile with the woman who had once threatened to have her flogged: she stayed. Through the whole miserable ceremony she stood in the cold with her basket on her arm like a woman waiting for a late butcher, and when the last board went up and the corporal slapped a paper seal over the wood — wax the color of old blood, stamped with the republic's cockade — Marchand said, loudly enough for the whole lane, "Well. I hope they enjoy the smell of ink. It never comes out."

It was not solidarity, exactly. It was closer to heckling. But it made the youngest soldier look ashamed, and shame in an occupying soldier is a small coin worth hoarding.

* * *

Afterward Dominic walked the perimeter of her own building the way she had once, as a child, walked the fence line of a neighbor's orchard, calculating what was reachable and what was theft. The alley behind. The yard where the drying lines hung, empty now, the army having confiscated her printed broadsheets as seditious material — which they had been, technically, being almanacs. The little window above the press room, too narrow for her shoulders since she was fourteen, boarded as well. They had been thorough. Ravel's thoroughness was famous already; men in town spoke of it the way they spoke of weather coming in off the marsh, with resignation and a glance at the sky.

She counted the boards. Eleven across the front. Eight at the back. She counted twice, because counting was the only prayer she knew.

The trouble — and here her mind circled the fact the way a tongue circles a cracked tooth — the trouble was not the boards. Boards could be pried. Nails could be pulled; she owned a puller, if she could ever reach it again. The trouble was the paper seal, and beneath the paper seal the signature, and beneath the signature the meaning. To break the seal was a crime with a name now, and the name was read aloud at roll call every morning so that every soldier in Vellamont knew it before he knew his own breakfast. *Tampering with sealed property of the republic.* They had not needed to invent a punishment yet, because punishments were arriving weekly like parcels, each one more imaginative than the last.

And the ledger sat inside, eleven feet from the front door, in a hollow her own hands had cut, listening — she could not help imagining — to everything.

Her father had believed books had ears. He'd said a book was the most patient creature alive, that it would wait two hundred years in a cellar for the right pair of eyes, and that printers were merely midwives to things that refused to die. She had found this sentimental nonsense at twelve and found it true at twenty-six. Now the ledger waited in the dark of the press with all its patience, holding forty-one lives in its pages like a hen on eggs, and she had nailed — well, the army had nailed, but she felt the hammer in her own palm regardless — she had let it be nailed into a coffin with no airhole.

"You're muttering," said the landlady.

Madame Fenn stood in her doorway across the yard, arms folded in her shawl, wearing the expression of a woman who charged rent for gossip and was currently assessing the market.

"I am not," Dominic said.

"You were. You've been muttering at that wall since the soldiers left. The whole yard can hear you." Fenn came down her step, moving with the deliberate slowness of a woman who considered haste vulgar. "So. They've sealed it."

"Yes."

"And your father's equipment is inside."

"Some of it."

Fenn looked at her. It was the oldest danger in Dominic's life, that look — the look of a woman doing arithmetic. Three years Dominic had paid her rent in coin that arrived punctually and answered questions that arrived politely, and in three years Madame Fenn had never once seen Master Yarrow enter or leave the building, only his child, and had never once asked why, and Dominic had understood perfectly that the silence was not free. Silence in Vellamont ran on credit like everything else.

"What's in there that they want," Fenn said, "if the presses are dead and the paper's gone?"

"Old stock. Broken furniture. A press that doesn't work."

"Mm." Fenn examined her fingernails, which were formidable. "My late husband kept accounts too. Fish trade. Every debt in the harbor quarter in one green book. When he died I burned it in the kitchen stove, page by page, and I'll tell you what I learned." She looked up. "You cannot burn a book fast enough. Not alone. It takes hours, and every hour someone might knock."

Dominic's mouth went dry. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you've gone white as flour and I like knowing why things happen in my own yard." Fenn turned back toward her door, then paused on the step. "I also like my tenants alive. Rent's unreliable from dead ones. Good evening, Dominic."

It was the first time in three years she had used the name.

* * *

That night Dominic did not sleep. She took a candle down to the yard and sat on the chopping block where she had split kindling for nine winters, and she looked at the back of her own house — eight boards, gray in the candlelight, the seal's wax glinting dull red where it caught the flame — and she let the despair come, because she had been holding it off all day at arm's length like a drunk uncle at a wedding, and it deserved its hour.

This was the arithmetic she did, sitting in the cold:

Inside that building was everything her father had been. His type, cast by his own hands, each letterform carrying the small signature nick he cut into the shoulder of every piece so that any printer in any city could identify Yarrow work at a glance — pride, he'd called it; forgery-deterrence, she'd called it; immortality, they'd both privately known it was. Inside was the proofing press he'd bought secondhand from a drowned man's estate and repaired so lovingly it had outlived him. Inside were the walls he'd whitewashed himself, the shelf he'd built crooked and refused to straighten because, he said, a shop needs one honest flaw. Inside was the smell — oh, the smell — of lead and lampblack and linseed that was, to her, simply the smell of being loved.

And inside, in the belly of the press, wrapped in her scarf, was the only copy of forty-one names. Widow Marchand's forged marriage lines. Old Tessier's pardoned smuggling run. The cooper's son hiding under his mother's maiden name. Ilse Brandt's brother, cleared by a bribe recorded in Dominic's careful hand. Forty-one threads, and she was the needle, and the needle was locked in a box.

If the army opened the press — and they would, eventually; *pending inventory* meant some clerk with a list would eventually poke a bayonet into every cavity in the building — then the ledger went to Ravel, and Ravel, merciful Ravel, reasonable Ravel, would burn it. Or worse. Ravel had wanted to burn it as mercy. But Ravel answered to superiors, and superiors had a habit, she was learning, of curdling other men's mercies into necessity. A ledger full of pardons-for-sale was not evidence of treason against the republic. It was evidence of treason against certain persons in the republic, and those persons had names, and those names had prices.

Forty-one lives, and hers among them — because her own secret was in the book too, wasn't it? Her father had entered it, years ago, in whatever cipher of honesty he'd allowed himself. *Payment received. Matter settled. Let no further record be made.* She had never dared decipher the whole entry until recently, and now she wished she never had, because her secret and the forty-one others were bound in the same calfskin, and if the book burned, her past burned with it, and she would be free —

— and if the book burned, the forty-one burned with her, and freedom bought at that price was just a larger sort of sealing.

A cat crossed the yard and regarded her with the flat judgment of its kind. "Go away," she told it. It sat down. Cats and armies, she thought; neither respects a closed door.

She thought about prying the boards that night. Actually composed the plan: the alley, the third nail, ten minutes, in and out, ledger under her coat, gone before the patrol's next pass. And then she stood at the back wall with her knife against the seam and imagined the drumbeat of discovery — because there would be drums, there was always drums — and saw, with terrible clarity, the tribunal reading out her sentence to a crowd that had known her as the Yarrow boy for nine years and would learn otherwise in the space between one word and the next. *She.* Just that. One syllable, and nine years of careful carpentry collapsed, and her father's last protection with it.

She put the knife away. The despair, having had its hour, did not entirely leave; it did what despair does in stubborn people. It packed its bags and moved into the back rooms of her chest and began arranging itself for a long stay.

* * *

In the small hours she went back inside Madame Fenn's — she rented a room there now, since sleeping in the shop was impossible and sleeping in the yard was idiotic — and lay on the narrow bed listening to the wind work at the loose shutter. And it was there, in the dark, that the thought arrived. Not a plan yet. Not even a shape. More like the first hairline crack in ice that tells you the river underneath is still moving.

The inventory clerk. *Pending inventory.* Someone would have to go in. Someone official, with papers, with a list, with the authority to open the seal lawfully — and whatever they carried out would be carried out lawfully, in daylight, past checkpoints, in a cart, under everyone's approving gaze.

She had no papers. She had no authority. She had a knife, a scarf, and a face the army knew as a boy's.

But somewhere in this town was a woman who drew maps, whose measurements of the print shop had not matched the tax rolls, who had stood in the press room and looked at the hollow behind the press and asked one quiet question — *whose names?* — and then walked away without reporting anything to anyone. Ilse Brandt. Cartographer. Soldier of the republic. Owner of a handwriting that appeared, Dominic now knew, on garrison requisitions.

Dominic stared at the ceiling and did not let herself finish the thought, because finishing it would make it real, and real things could be hanged for.

Outside, the wind found the shutter properly and set it rattling like a printer's frame. Somewhere north of the river, dogs were barking at nothing. In the sealed shop across the yard, patient as her father had promised, the ledger waited in the dark with its forty-one names, keeping its own counsel, keeping its own count.

Eleven days to the new year. Two hundred pages of evidence. One woman with no plan except the beginning of one, and the beginning was a name, and the name was Ilse.

She got up, dressed, and sat by the cold hearth to wait for morning — because cartographers, she reasoned, surely slept sometime, and even treason deserved a civilized hour.
