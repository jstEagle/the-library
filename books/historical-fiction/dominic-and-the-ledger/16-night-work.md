## 16. Night Work

The garrison's administrative office smelled of tallow, damp stone, and other people's bad penmanship. Ilse Brandt had come to think of it as the truest portrait of the revolution: grand intentions, poor materials, everyone copying everyone else's spelling errors with total conviction.

She sat at her corner table by the window — she had claimed it the first week, and no one had contested it, because cartographers were considered a slightly unhinged species and best left alone — and she drew. Or appeared to draw. The map of Vellamont spread before her was finished. It had been finished for four days. What lay under it now, weighted at the corners with ink pots, was a different kind of drafting altogether.

A requisition order.

She had been studying the commandant's paperwork the way she studied riverbanks: not for beauty but for structure. Where did the water run? Where were the weak points? Ravel's office produced three kinds of document in steady supply — requisitions, movement passes, and confiscation inventories — and each had its own grammar, its own habits, its own little tells. The requisitions were typed up by Corporal Fenner, who wrote a *p* like a man falling down a staircase and always, *always* forgot the accent on *réquisition* until someone corrected him, and sometimes even after.

Ilse had filled two pages of practice sheets and burned them in the stove. Now she set a fresh sheet before her and dipped her pen.

*By order of Commandant Ravel,* she began, and then stopped, because the next word mattered more than any line she had ever surveyed.

Fenner would have written *autorisation*. He would have written it slightly wrong. She wrote it slightly wrong too — that was the trick of forgery nobody told you: perfection was the loudest tell in the world. A document too correct announced itself like a peacock in a henhouse. She gave the *p* its little tumble, missed the accent, hesitated half a heartbeat over the date, and then committed.

*Autorisation is hereby granted to remove printing equipment from the premises of the Yarrow printshop, Rue des Tanneurs, for transport to the garrison, there to be inventoried.*

There. That was the whole architecture of the thing, and it rested on a single word: *equipment*. Not records. Not documents. Equipment. Nobody guarded equipment. Equipment was heavy, dull, and belonged to the state now, which amounted to the same thing as belonging to nobody. A sergeant could read the order three times and find nothing in it, because there was nothing in it — except that the print shop's press, if one knew where to look, was currently serving as a bookshelf.

Ilse read the sentence back and felt something turn over in her chest, slow and heavy, like a barge changing course.

She was aware, distantly, that she ought to be having a crisis. This was, after all, the moment — she could feel historians circling it like gulls — the first open act of betrayal against her own side. In the stories, this was where the candle guttered dramatically and the traitor clutched a locket and wept. Her candle burned quite steadily. She had no locket. What she had was an ache behind her sternum and a very clear memory of an open ledger page, and a name on it written in a careful, upright hand: *Brandt, Matthias — remitted in full, per arrangement, 1789.*

Her brother, alive because somebody's father had been paid to keep a secret, and honest enough — or kind enough — to write the bribe down.

Well. If her family owed its survival to meticulous record-keeping, it was fitting that she repay the debt in the same currency.

* * *

The trouble with forging documents, she discovered, was that it was mostly waiting. Ink had opinions about drying. Seals had opinions about wax. And the garrison's seal — that was the real problem, the river crossing of this particular map.

Ravel's seal sat on his desk during the day and in a locked drawer at night, and the drawer key lived on a ring that lived on the belt of Sergeant Ochs, a man who slept like a dropped sack of flour but woke like a sprung trap. Ilse had spent two evenings observing the night rotation of the outer corridor from the window of her quarters, which overlooked the courtyard, and had concluded that between the second watch change and the kitchen fires being lit, there existed a gap of roughly forty minutes in which the commandant's office stood empty and the corridor stood unwatched, provided one avoided the third stair, which creaked in E-flat.

Every building has a note it sings when betrayed. She'd made a career of listening for them.

Tonight was the night. She told herself this with the flat competence she used for river crossings and bad weather, and then she sat with her finished forgery for another hour anyway, reading it over so many times that the words stopped meaning anything. *Printing equipment.* She began to worry about it. Suppose they asked why the equipment couldn't wait. Suppose Ravel himself signed off on transports personally — he did, sometimes, the man was tireless in the specific way of people who couldn't sleep — suppose—

"You've read that map four hundred times," said Lieutenant Voss from the doorway, around a bread heel. "If there's a bridge on it I haven't seen, I'll eat my hat."

"There is no bridge," Ilse said. "Eat your hat."

He grinned and wandered off. She let out a breath she had been holding since approximately the armistice discussions. This was the other thing nobody warned you about betrayal: how much of it was just pretending to be bored in front of your colleagues.

* * *

At half past eleven she went down.

The corridor held the particular silence of buildings full of sleeping soldiers — not quiet, exactly, but muffled, breathing quiet. She had mapped this route in her head the way she mapped everything: left at the notice board, past the quartermaster's door (open; snoring within, rhythmic as surf), down six steps, past the third stair — she took it wide, foot hugging the wall, and the stair kept its E-flat to itself — and then the commandant's office door, unlocked, because Ravel trusted his own men, which was either touching or tragic depending on how the next ten minutes went.

Inside, moonlight came through the frost-furred window in a pale square, and the seal sat on the desk like a small brass animal asleep.

Ilse lit no candle. She worked by feel and by the window-light, the way she'd drafted coastal features in fog. Wax — there was a stub of sealing wax in the desk's upper tray, because of course there was; armies traveled on their stomachs and sealed things with whatever was nearest. She heated the spoon-end over the banked embers of the office stove, counted seconds instead of watching the wax, because watching made you rush, and poured.

Then the seal, pressed firm, held — one, two, three — and lifted.

She looked at the impression in the moonlight and felt her stomach drop through the floorboards.

It was wrong. The eagle's wing — she hadn't noticed before, how had she not noticed — the wing overlapped the border ring on genuine documents, and on hers it sat inside it, neat as a coin. Because she had practiced from a requisition that had been stamped by a tired clerk at a bad angle, and the angle had hidden the overlap, and now her forgery wore a seal that no document in this army had worn in its life.

She stood in the cold room with her heart going like a drum corps and thought, with surprising calm: *so this is what drowning looks like on land.*

And then — because she was a cartographer, and cartographers do not panic at wrong coastlines, they resurvey — she picked up the seal, tilted it, and looked at it properly for the first time. Brass wears. Brass takes on the habits of the hands that use it. And Ravel, pressing hard at the top edge and light at the bottom — she'd felt it in the impression without understanding what she felt — had worn his own seal into asymmetry, the way a stair wears hollow.

She re-heated the wax. Poured again. Pressed harder at the top than felt natural, trusting the tool rather than her instincts, the way her old instructor had drummed into her: *the instrument knows more than you do, Fräulein Brandt, stop arguing with it.*

Lifted.

The wing broke the border ring. Imperfect. Tired-looking. Utterly ordinary.

She blotted the order, folded it once, and stood a moment in the moonlight letting her pulse come down to something survivable. On the desk beside the seal lay a sheet of Ravel's own correspondence, half-drafted, his hand cramped and relentless even in a private letter. She did not read it. She found, examining herself, that she still drew one line, and it surprised her: she would forge his seal, steal his prisoner-of-sorts, and gut his investigation, but she would not read his letters. Some borders you keep even while committing treason across all the others. It made no sense. She filed it under *later*, a country she was visiting more and more often these days.

Then out, past the snoring quartermaster, wide on the third stair, and up to her room, where she put the forged order inside the tube with her rolled maps — the one place in the garrison no soldier ever touched, because maps were heavy, dull, and belonged to the state, which amounted to the same thing as belonging to nobody.

The symmetry of that almost made her laugh. Almost. What it actually made her do was sit on the edge of her cot in the dark and shake for a quarter hour, quietly, like a horse after a thunderstorm. Betrayal, it turned out, was less a decision than a procession of decisions, each individually reasonable, none of them feeling like the famous one. The famous one had happened somewhere back around the moment she'd asked Dominic Yarrow *whose names?* and already known she wouldn't like the answer and asked anyway.

Everything since was just cartography.

* * *

Morning brought complications, because mornings in Vellamont considered themselves obliged to.

She presented the order to Sergeant Ochs at nine, with the transport request timed for that evening — night removals looked suspicious, day removals merely looked tedious, and tedious was the armor of bureaucracy. Ochs read it twice. Then he did the thing she had dreaded most in her catalogue of dreads: he squinted at the seal.

"New wax," Ilse said, before he could speak. "The good stock ran out Tuesday. Half the requisitions this week are sealed in the soft batch — ask Corporal Fenner, he complained about it at breakfast."

Had Fenner complained about it at breakfast? Almost certainly he had complained about *something* at breakfast; the man treated grievances as a food group. Ochs grunted, which in his language meant a full audit had been conducted and found wanting only in enthusiasm, and stamped the order with the transport chit.

"Equipment, eh?" he said. "What's a print shop got worth hauling?"

"Presses are lead and iron," Ilse said. "Lead's worth hauling anywhere."

This had the advantage of being true, and Ochs nodded the nod of a man who had carried enough lead in his life to have opinions about it. He waved her off. She walked out across the courtyard at a pace calibrated to convey *a woman with errands*, neither hurrying nor strolling, and only allowed herself to breathe once she reached the street, where the cold hit her lungs like a debt collector.

Rue des Tanneurs. She had business there anyway — official business, even. The survey of the north quarter needed checking, and there was no better cover in the world than looking like you were supposed to be somewhere.

Dominic was sweeping the step when she came up the street, and Ilse watched her the way she always did, which was too long. There was a particular quality to the girl's stillness — a stillness like a held breath, like water that has decided to be ice — and today it was worse than usual, because the shop's doors had fresh paper pasted across them downstairs at the entrance to the lane: the army's inventory notice, the word *SCELLÉ* in letters tall as a child.

They had sealed the shop with everything inside it. Ilse had known this, had planned around it, had drawn the fact into her mental map of the town the way one draws a marsh: passable, given the right season. But knowing a thing and seeing Dominic sweep a step that led to a nailed-shut door were different countries entirely.

"Master Yarrow," Ilse said, formal, because the street had ears and some of them reported weekly.

"Cartographer." Dominic didn't look up. The broom moved. "Come to measure my ruin?"

"Come to confirm your measurements. Ruins hold their dimensions better than most things."

That earned her a flicker — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its tools. God, the girl was proud. Proud the way frozen rivers were proud: you could walk on it, if you respected the depth beneath.

"I need the interior dimensions for the archive record," Ilse went on, pitching it correctly — dull, procedural, the voice of paperwork itself. "Sealed property still gets recorded. Tonight would suit. The light is better after dark for the plumb lines." This was nonsense; plumb lines didn't care about light. But nonsense delivered confidently is indistinguishable from procedure, and Dominic — clever, clever Dominic — caught the second layer under it in the space of one breath.

"Tonight," Dominic repeated. The broom stilled. "After dark."

"After eight. The sentries change at eight, and again at midnight, and the ones between are young and warm-blooded and hate standing still in January." Ilse opened her map case and drew out the tube, and with it — angled so only Dominic could see, for less than a second, no longer — the corner of a paper bearing a familiar brass eagle breaking its border ring.

Dominic went very still indeed. Stillness like the middle of winter. When she spoke, her voice was level, but her knuckles had gone white around the broom handle, and her eyes — her eyes were doing something Ilse wasn't sure she'd survive, some mixture of hope and terror and gratitude aimed straight at her chest like a surveying instrument.

"You understand," Dominic said quietly, "that if this goes wrong, they don't flog you for a forged stamp. They hang you next to me."

"I've considered the geography of the situation," Ilse said. "Eight o'clock. Bring nothing. Wear your clerk's coat — the brown one. Sentries remember uniforms, not faces, and a coat they've seen at checkpoints is worth more than a face they haven't."

She turned to go, professional to the last, and had taken three steps when Dominic spoke again, low, almost not at all.

"Ilse."

She stopped. She did not turn around. Turning around would have been fatal; she knew it the way she knew contour lines.

"Why?" Dominic asked. The word held everything underneath it: *why betray them, why risk this, why me.* A whole atlas of questions in a single syllable, and no safe answer among any of them. Ilse looked down the street toward the river, gray and patient under its rim of ice, and gave the only answer that was both true and survivable.

"You have a delivery coming," she said. "Heavy freight. Lead and iron."

She heard the breath Dominic let out — half laugh, half something that in a less disciplined person would have been tears — and then she was walking, briskly, a woman with errands, while behind her a sealed door kept its secret and somewhere above it a girl stood holding a broom like a weapon she had decided, at last, not to use.

Eight o'clock. One forged order, one crowbar, forty minutes between sentry changes, and a book that weighed less than a press and more than the world.

Ilse checked her watch, adjusted nothing, and went to spend the afternoon doing sums that refused to stay done.
