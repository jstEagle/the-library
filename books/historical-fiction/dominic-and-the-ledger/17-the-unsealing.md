## 17. The Unsealing

Ilse had written the requisition order three times before she got it right.

The first attempt she burned in the stove of her billet, watching the paper curl like a startled animal. The second she soaked in the washbasin until the ink ran into grey ghosts. The third — the third said, in her careful administrative hand, that *by order of the Commandant's office, one press and associated printing equipment are to be removed from the Yarrow premises to the garrison for purposes of producing official notices*, and it was signed *Capt. Ferenc, by direction*, which was a lie stacked on a forgery stacked on a theft, because Captain Ferenc had gone to bed at nine o'clock with a bottle of the good brandy and would remember signing nothing.

She looked at it a long while. Then she folded it inside her coat, next to her heart, where traitors presumably kept all their documents, and went out into the snow.

* * *

Dominic had not slept. This was not remarkable — she had not properly slept since Surrender Day — but tonight the sleeplessness had a different texture. Tonight it was not dread but its cousin, anticipation, and she found she could not tell them apart in the dark. Both felt like standing at the top of stairs.

She heard the knock she was waiting for: three raps, a pause, two more. Not any soldier's rhythm. A cartographer's rhythm — measured, deliberate, the knock of someone who measured everything, including her own courage.

Dominic opened the door of the shop's back room, where she had been living like a mouse in a wall since the sealing, and let Ilse Brandt into the cold.

"You came," Dominic whispered, which was foolish, because of course Ilse had come; she had said she would come; they had planned it down to the quarter-hour over three meetings conducted in whispers behind the tannery and once, absurdly, in the open air of the market, disguised as a conversation about turnips.

"I said I would," Ilse said, as if this settled the matter of whether forgeries, betrayals, and midnight burglaries were involved. She unwound her scarf. Her breath hung between them. "You look terrible."

"I've been sleeping on a floor."

"Your floor is sealed. You've been sleeping on Marchand's floor."

"My floor *spiritually*." Dominic almost smiled. It hurt her face; she'd forgotten the motion. "Do you have it?"

Ilse produced the order. Dominic held it up to the shuttered lamp and read it twice, slowly, the way she read everything now — the way you read anything that might cost lives.

"*Associated printing equipment*," she murmured. "That's me, I suppose. Associated."

"It's the ledger, and you, and possibly a few drawers of type if we're greedy." Ilse took the paper back. "Ferenc signs everything by direction. He hasn't read his own orders in six weeks. If anyone challenges us, act bored. Boredom is the great disguise. Nobody interrogates a bored person."

"Where did you learn that?"

"The army. It's entirely composed of bored people pretending otherwise."

* * *

The seals on the front door were wax and ribbon, official-looking, and Ilse broke them with a penknife as gently as a surgeon. The ribbon she wound around her finger and pocketed; Dominic did not ask why. There was a whole category of things she no longer asked Ilse why about, and it grew daily, like a well-tended garden of small madnesses.

The door opened onto the shop, and the shop opened onto grief.

It had been nine days since Dominic had stood inside it, and nine days of a print shop left cold do things to a place. The ink had stiffened in its pots. The type trays gaped half-drawn, exactly as they'd been abandoned mid-task, her father's letters standing in their cases like a congregation interrupted mid-hymn. Frost had climbed the inside of the windows and made lace of them. And there in the corner sat the proofing press — her father's proofing press, forty years old, walnut and iron, hollowed out with chisels over four sleepless nights to make a coffin big enough for a ledger — sitting in plain sight where the soldiers had seen it a hundred times and never once thought to look inside, because who looks inside a press?

"It's smaller than I imagined," Ilse said quietly.

"Everything's smaller than you imagine from the outside. Shops. Towns. People's courage." Dominic crossed the room, laid both hands flat on the press frame, and allowed herself three seconds — she counted them — of simply touching it. Then she began work.

The hollow was accessed through the base plate, which her father's old tools made quick work of, because she had built the concealment to be opened fast, in the dark, by hands that knew it blind. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and then in sailcloth scraps, lay the ledger — fat as a well-fed cat, smelling faintly of machine oil and lies.

Forty-one names. Forty-one neighbors. One book.

"Here." She held it out, and Ilse took it, and for a moment neither of them moved, because there is a weight to a thing like that which has nothing to do with pounds.

"We should go," Ilse said.

"Yes," said Dominic, and neither of them went.

* * *

The plan was simple, which Ilse distrusted on principle, all good plans being complicated. Ilse, in uniform, would carry the requisition order and a handcart. Dominic, bundled in her dead father's coat with her hair bound up under a soldier's cap, would be the *associated equipment* — an assistant conscripted for the labor, sullen, unremarkable, male. They would wheel the ledger up Maren Street to the garrison annex, where Ilse had arranged a locked storeroom and a sympathetic or at least incurious corporal, and from there, in daylight, under genuine orders Ilse would spend another night forging, the book would leave town in a map chest marked *SURVEY INSTRUMENTS*, because nobody in the history of armies had ever opened a case marked survey instruments.

They got eleven steps.

It was the cough that did it. Dominic's chest, after days on cold floors, produced a small dry cough at the exact wrong moment, and Sergeant Blaise — Blaise, of all people, Blaise who had lingered at the press on Surrender Day and been corrected on his French grammar so thoroughly he'd probably rehearsed revenge in his sleep — turned around at the corner of Maren Street with a lantern already rising.

"Hallo," said Blaise. "Who goes there?"

"Corporal Brandt," Ilse said, with the magnificent boredom she had promised. "Requisition detail. Garrison equipment." She held up the order without being asked, which was either the boldest thing Dominic had ever seen or the most reckless; the lamplight caught the paper and the forged signature and Ilse's face, which was doing something heroic: absolutely nothing.

Blaise read it. Dominic stood very still beside the handcart, head down, cap low, breathing through her nose in slow controlled draughts like a woman trying not to sneeze in church. Under the sailcloth in the cart, the ledger lay with its forty-one names and its terrible weight, close enough that she could have touched it through the cloth.

"This is dated tomorrow," Blaise said.

* * *

Later, Ilse would maintain she had known instantly what to say. Later, Dominic would maintain that what actually happened was a silence of perhaps four seconds, during which the entire winter held its breath, and that salvation arrived not through cunning but through Blaise's own exhaustion.

"No it isn't," Ilse said finally, evenly. "It's dated the ninth."

Blaise squinted at the paper, then at the sky, grey-black and snowing, then at the paper again. "What's the date, then?"

"The eighth. Late." Ilse let a fraction of weariness into her voice, a soldier's weariness, the universal currency. "We've been at this since the eleventh hour, Sergeant. The commandant wants his printing equipment secured before the new year and nobody told the requisitions clerk the calendar doesn't care what the commandant wants."

A pause. Snow ticked on the lantern's tin hood.

"Eleventh hour," Blaise muttered, with the deep grievance of a man who had also been working past his own eleventh hour. "Nobody tells anybody anything in this army."

"No," Ilse agreed. "They don't."

"Do you need a hand with the cart? There's ice on Maren Street, my wife slipped there twice last week—"

"That's kind. We'll manage."

"Take the middle of the road, then, not the gutter side—" And Blaise gave them directions on ice safety for the better part of a minute, raised his lantern in something adjacent to a salute, and trudged off toward whatever warm post was waiting for him, leaving Dominic frozen at the handcart handles with her heart going like a drum corps.

When his footsteps faded, Ilse exhaled for approximately the length of the Rue des Forgerons.

"Dated tomorrow," she whispered. "I dated it *tomorrow*. I am a professional forger and I dated it *tomorrow*."

"Perhaps you were thinking ahead."

"Perhaps I was thinking ahead," Ilse repeated, giddy with relief and terror in equal parts, and then — Dominic would replay this moment for years — she laughed. A real laugh, brief and helpless, muffled immediately in her glove, and Dominic laughed too, silently, shaking, both of them bent over the handcart in the falling snow like a pair of conspirators overcome by church giggles, guarding a book worth both their necks.

"He called you Corporal," Dominic managed. "You're a lieutenant."

"He didn't look at my collar. Nobody looks at collars. Collars are how I'll conquer this entire army someday." Ilse straightened, wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist. "Middle of the road. Apparently the gutter is trying to kill wives."

They crossed Vellamont that way, laughing in fragments, quiet again at each corner, and somewhere along Maren Street — Dominic could never afterward say precisely where — the fear changed shape. It stopped being two separate fears walking beside one cart and became one fear, shared, which is either the definition of partnership or of doom, and she decided, gripping the handles, watching Ilse's steady back and the cart's precious burden, that she would take either.

* * *

The storeroom received them. The corporal on duty accepted Ilse's second set of papers without reading them, having discovered a jug of something earlier in the evening and made a thorough study of it. By half past one the ledger was inside a map chest, and the map chest was inside a locked room, and the lock key was inside Ilse's glove, and everything had worked.

Which was when the bells started.

Not the church bells — the garrison bells. Two strokes, then three: the alarm pattern. Lamplight bloomed in the barracks yard. Somewhere a whistle shrilled, and boots hit frozen cobblestones in numbers, and Ilse was at the storeroom window with her hand flat against the glass, listening the way other people pray.

"They're not coming for us," she said at last, slowly. "Listen. They're forming at the customhouse. That's the smuggling investigation — Ravel's been pushing it for days. It's nothing to do with—"

A hammering on the outer door of the annex.

"—unless," Ilse added, "they decide to search every building in the quarter, which they will, because that is what armies do when they are frightened, they check their own pockets."

Another hammering. A voice — Ferenc's voice, thick with brandy and indignation — demanding entry. Of course. The annex held the requisition records, and Ferenc had been hauled out of his bed to account for every order signed in his name, including, quite possibly, one dated the ninth that he did not remember signing.

Ilse turned from the window. In the lamplight her face was pale and completely calm, which Dominic had learned was her most dangerous expression, and her most beloved.

"The chest stays here," Ilse said. "If we carry it out now we're two people fleeing a searched building with a heavy box, which is a confession with handles. We walk out the front, we're a lieutenant and her assistant reporting for the emergency muster like everyone else. The lock holds. The corporal is asleep and will perjure himself by morning out of pure embarrassment."

"And the ledger?"

"Is a box of survey instruments in a locked room belonging to the mapping section, and if anyone opens it, I will explain it is a new gridding technique, and they will believe me, because I am very boring." She paused at the door. "Dominic. The press. We can't go back for it tonight, and after tonight the shop will be watched harder than ever. You know that."

Dominic knew it. She had known it since the first chisel stroke: the press was a sacrifice from the moment she hollowed it. Her father's tools, her father's bench, the whole shop — all of it was already lost, had been lost the day the drums came up the ferry road, and she had only been pretending otherwise because pretending otherwise was how she got out of bed.

"Leave it," she said, and her voice cracked on the word, and she let it crack, because Ilse was the first person in nine years she had let hear anything crack. "It's wood and iron. My father used to say the press was just the body. Ink's the soul, and the soul's in this chest."

Ilse looked at her for a long moment. Then she did something she had never done: she reached out and straightened Dominic's crooked soldier's cap, tugging it down over her ears against the cold, an unhurried, ordinary gesture, the gesture of a person who intended to keep performing it for a long time.

"Together, then," Ilse said. "Out the front. Bored faces."

"Bored faces," Dominic agreed.

They stepped out into the ringing, snow-blind chaos of the muster yard, two small figures among fifty, indistinguishable, unremarkable, and utterly — irreversibly — on the same side of the door.

Behind them, in the annex, the map chest waited. Ahead of them, across town, Commandant Ravel was already awake, dressing by candlelight, and thinking — as he dressed, methodically, button by button — about signatures.
