## 18. What the Book Costs

The tannery loft smelled like a lie about a forest.

That was the trouble with tanneries — they took something honest, the smell of oak bark and river water, and boiled it down into something that clung to your clothes for weeks and announced you to every dog within half a mile. Dominic had been in the loft above Marchand's cousin's drying sheds for two days now, and she had begun to dream in the smell, which she suspected meant it had colonized her entirely. She was a creature of the tannery now. Perhaps when this was over she could simply walk into the garrison and no one would question it; they'd assume the smell was carrying her.

She had the ledger with her, of course. It went where she went, wrapped in oilcloth inside a flour sack that was itself inside an old grain crate marked *OATS — SPOILED*, on the theory that no soldier alive would open a crate that promised disappointment. The theory had not yet been tested, and she prayed nightly that it never would be.

There was nothing to do in the loft but wait, and waiting, Dominic had discovered, was a kind of work — heavy, repetitive labor performed by the whole body at once. Her ankle still throbbed faintly from the frozen shallows below the gate, a souvenir of the River Road that she wore like a medal she hadn't asked for. The dogs had lost her in the reeds. Odile's hut had kept her one night. And then, before dawn, the old ferryman had rowed her upriver under a pile of eel traps and put her ashore below the drying sheds with a grunt that might have been farewell or might have been heartburn.

So: waiting. And there was only so much counting of roof beams a person could do.

On the second evening, with the light going copper through the gaps in the boards and the vats below exhaling their slow chemical sighs, Dominic did the thing she had been circling since the night Ilse pried the press open and slid the ledger into her arms.

She unwrapped the book, set it on an upturned barrel, and began to read it from the beginning.

* * *

She knew the ledger the way a sailor knows a particular rope — by its working end. She knew the last four years of entries because she had written them, in her father's hand, which was to say in her own hand disguised as his, a forgery so long-practiced that sometimes she forgot whose letters they actually were. She knew the debts current, the aliases active, the little marginal codes: a dash for forgiven-and-never-to-be-mentioned, a circle for paid-in-goods, a small careful *f.* beside names that existed on no warrant anywhere because she had invented them herself out of mercy and printer's ink.

But she had never read it front to back. Nobody reads a ledger front to back. A ledger is a tool, and tools are known by their handles.

Except that this particular tool was now the most dangerous object in Vellamont, wanted dead by the commandant and alive by forty-one households, and it seemed suddenly obscene that she should carry a thing she did not fully know. You do not smuggle a stranger.

So she turned to the first page, dated eleven years before she was born, in her grandfather's hand — broader than her father's, with a flourish on the descenders that suggested a man who enjoyed being owed money — and she began.

The early pages were dull in the way that only deep water is dull: flat, dark, and full. Grain accounts. Paper stock. A dispute over a broken forme of type, settled in cider. Page after page of Vellamont being Vellamont, the town's whole economy conducted in ink because nobody trusted anybody's memory farther than the churchyard wall.

Then her father's hand began, and Dominic slowed without meaning to.

He had beautiful lettering. She had always known this in the abstract, the way you know your own face is unremarkable, but seeing it here, page after page, decade after decade, she felt it fresh: Aldous Yarrow wrote numbers the way other men carved headstones, as if each figure were expected to stand at attention for a century. And between the columns, in the margins, he had left notes — dry, tiny, occasionally funny in a way she had never once caught him being aloud.

*Boulanger claims the widow Renard owes for 40 sheets. Widow Renard claims otherwise. Both are lying. Split the difference and tell neither.*

Dominic laughed — an actual laugh, alone in a tannery loft, startling a pigeon somewhere above her — and then pressed her hand to her mouth as if the sound could be recalled.

* * *

The hours passed. Below her, the sheds cooled and ticked. She read.

It was strange company, the dead. Her father had been a quiet man at the best of times and a closed one at the worst, and his worst had lasted the last nine years of his life, ever since her mother died and he looked at his daughter across the funeral bread and said, in the tone he used for discussing paper stock, *"Someone will have to be the son."* No explanation. No apology. Just inventory: what the shop needed, what the town would permit, what was to be done.

She had thought, all these years, that she understood the arrangement. It was practical. It was necessary. It was the sort of cold arithmetic a printer applies to everything, including family. She had lived inside it so long it had become less a disguise than a skin, and she had stopped asking whether the man beneath the arrangement had felt anything about it.

Now she read his margins, and the man beneath the arrangement kept making jokes.

*Yarrow fils has grown two inches this year. Must adjust the boy's apron again. The landlady is suspicious of boys who grow.*

She had forgotten that apron. He'd let the hem down twice in one spring and grumbled about it for a month, and she had assumed the grumbling was about cloth.

*The boy cut his first 'g' today. Better than mine at that age, though I shall deny this under oath.*

Her eyes stung. She wiped them with the heel of her hand, impatient with herself, and kept reading, because stopping felt more dangerous than continuing.

* * *

It was well past midnight, by the feel of it rather than any clock, when she found the entry.

It was in the middle years' section, wedged between a paper order for the curé and a routine note about a delivery to Rouen, and at first glance it looked like nothing: a payment made out to a name she didn't recognize, in an amount that made her sit up straight on the barrel.

Sixty livres. Sixty livres was not a rounding error. Sixty livres was the shop's profit for half a season.

The recipient's name meant nothing to her — *Mme. Geneviève Aubert, care of the Convent of Sainte-Rustique* — and there was no goods column, no explanation, none of his usual tidy accounting. Just the payment, entered plainly, and beside it in the margin, in letters smaller than any he had yet used, as if he hoped the page itself might not quite hear:

*For silence. Renewed annually. God forgive me, worth every sou.*

Dominic read it four times.

The loft was very quiet. Below, a vat burped. Somewhere a rat conducted its small affairs.

*For silence.*

Silence about what?

And then she knew, of course, and knowing arrived not as a thought but as a physical event, a slow flood rising through her from the feet up, filling her ears last so that even her own heartbeat sounded distant. There was only one secret in Aldous Yarrow's life large enough to cost sixty livres a year and worth every sou. There was only one person in the shop who needed buying out of other people's sightlines, year after year, from the age of eight.

He had started paying when she was eight. The year her mother died. The year he first dressed her in the apron and walked her out front and introduced her to the town as his son.

He had not merely decided. He had *purchased the decision*, annually, from someone — this Mme. Aubert, whoever she had been, some busybody with a view of the schoolyard, some midwife, some aunt by marriage, some woman who had delivered him and knew exactly what had come out of his wife and could have ended their lives with a sentence. And every year, quietly, without a word to the child growing up under his roof believing her father was simply a cold, practical man, Aldous Yarrow had gone out with sixty livres and bought his daughter another year of being safe.

Gratitude hit her first, and it was enormous, and it was unbearable, because gratitude of that size is indistinguishable from grief. He had loved her. All those years she had catalogued his silences as indifference, he had been paying blackmail in the next room, and never once — not once in nineteen years — had he let the cost show in his face, because if the cost showed, she would have known she was a burden, and apparently Aldous Yarrow would sooner have set fire to his own type than let his child believe she was a burden.

And underneath the gratitude, arriving slower and settling deeper, like silt: rage.

Because he hadn't told her. Because he had built her whole life on a transaction and let her think it was arithmetic. Because she had spent nineteen years being grateful for a father's cold pragmatism when she could have spent them knowing — *knowing* — that she was the thing he valued above the shop, above the type, above his own neck, and how differently might she have carried herself, how much less lonely might the apron have been, if she had once, just once, seen the truth of it in ink instead of having to dig it out of a ledger like a debt collector?

He had recorded it. That was the bitterest part. He told no living soul, but he told the book, because he was a printer and printers believe, down to the marrow, that anything not written down didn't happen — and so his one great act of love sat filed between a paper order and a delivery note for nineteen years, waiting for his child to find it, coded in his own cramped margin like everything else he couldn't say aloud.

*God forgive me, worth every sou.*

"He wasn't asking forgiveness from God," Dominic said out loud, to the rafters, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "The old fool was leaving me a receipt."

* * *

She sat with the book until the sky behind the board gaps went from black to iron to the color of a fresh-cut onion, and she did not sleep, and she was not sure at any point whether she was weeping, the air in the loft being thick enough that a person's eyes watered on general principle.

What she felt, finally, when she could name anything at all, was rearranged. Not shattered — she had expected shattering, and braced for it — but *rearranged*, the way a compositor lifts a line of type and sets it back down in a different order and the same letters spell something else entire. She was still Dominic Yarrow. Still the cutter's daughter, still the son, still the keeper of the book. But the story underneath the story had changed, and every fact of her life now had to be picked up and reset.

Forty-one names in this ledger. Forty-one people whose secrets she was hauling toward the guild tribunal on her back, through checkpoints, past dogs, at the price of a burned shop. She had thought of herself as the custodian. The servant of the book.

But her own name — her true one, her birth-one, the girl's name her mother had chosen and her father had paid to protect — was in these pages too. Had been in them all along. She wasn't just carrying the ledger's people to safety.

She was one of them. She always had been. Entry number forty-two, hidden in plain type, the first name the book had ever saved.

Dominic closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on someone sleeping, and wrapped it back in its oilcloth, and set it in the flour sack in the oat crate, and then she stood for a while in the grey light with her hand flat on the crate lid, breathing the tannery's terrible honesty.

"Thank you," she said to her father, because rage and gratitude were entwined now past untangling, like the two strands of a thread, and she supposed they would stay that way. "And I'm furious with you. Both things. Get used to it."

The pigeon above her shifted and said nothing, being a pigeon.

* * *

It was full morning, and she was chewing the heel of bread Marchand's cousin left on the stairs — rye, dense as a hymnal, but bread is bread — when she heard boots on the lower landing. Two sets, not one. Then Marchand's cousin's voice, pitched low and urgent, and another voice answering that stopped the bread in Dominic's throat.

Not soldiers. Worse, in its way. A town voice. A voice that belonged to a face she knew, wearing the particular bright politeness of someone who has come to look at something rather than to buy it.

"—just need to ask the boy a few questions, that's all, official business, won't take a minute—"

And beneath the words, faint but unmistakable, the sound of paper being unfolded. A printed notice, being smoothed out against the doorframe.

Dominic rose without a sound and crossed to the gap in the boards, and looked down through the crack, and saw the man holding the notice turn it toward the light — and even upside down, even at this distance, she recognized the layout. Army stock. Broad headline. And she knew, with the sick certainty of a compositor who spots a wrong letter in a locked forme, exactly what was printed there, because Ravel was a methodical man and methodical men do not hunt blind:

A description. Height, age, occupation.

*The Yarrow boy.*
