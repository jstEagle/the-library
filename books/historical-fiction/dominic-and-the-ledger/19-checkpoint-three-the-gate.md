## 19. Checkpoint Three: The Gate

The tannery loft smelled, as always, like a hundred years of dead animals apologizing for it, and Dominic had come to find the smell almost companionable. It was honest, at least. Nothing in Vellamont was honest anymore, but the tannery stink never pretended to be anything but what it was.

She sat cross-legged by the loft's small window with Ilse's map spread across her knees — the second route, drawn in that precise cartographer's hand she had come to know better than her own heartbeat — and studied the north gate the way her father had taught her to study a line of type before locking it into the chase. Look for the flaw. Every piece of lead has one. Every wall, every argument, every plan.

The north gate had two flaws, and both of them were guards.

Not that the guards themselves were flawed men. From this distance they looked perfectly sound: two soldiers at the wicket door beside the great timber gates, one checking passes, one checking faces, a brazier between them throwing enough heat to keep their hands moving. The flaw was simply that they existed at all, and that there was no way around them. The wall ran east until it became cliff, west until it became river, and the river — well. The river was a problem for a colder day, and every day lately got colder.

"Third time you've looked at that map," said Odile from the ladder, without looking up from the net she was mending. The old ferrywoman came and went through the tannery yard as if she owned it, which in some sense nobody wrote down, she did. "Third time I've watched you look at it. A body could develop superstitions."

"I'm being careful."

"You're being frightened," Odile said comfortably, biting off a thread. "Nothing wrong with frightened. Frightened people live long lives. It's the brave ones fill the churchyards." She squinted up at the loft window, at the grey wash of afternoon beyond it. "You mean to go today."

Dominic folded the map. "Before the new year. Before they stop asking for papers and start asking questions."

"Mm." Odile tied off her knot with unnecessary violence. "Gate's the wrong door, girl. You know that."

"I know it's the obvious door. That's different."

"It's not different when the man on the other side of it is clever."

*It's not the man I'm worried about,* Dominic almost said, and didn't, because saying Ravel's name aloud in the tannery felt like setting type with wet hands — nothing would hold. Instead she said, "I need to see it close. Maps are lies told with straight lines. I need to stand in front of it once before I commit to the river."

Odile considered this, chewing the inside of her cheek, a habit Dominic had watched her perform over three days of hiding and which seemed less like thought than like digestion of the world's stupidity. Finally she grunted.

"Then go as a clerk. Not as yourself."

"I'm always myself. That's rather the whole difficulty."

"You know what I mean." Odile jabbed the mending needle toward her. "Cap, coat, clipboard, an air of being owed money. Nobody looks twice at a clerk. Clerks are the wallpaper of the world. But don't—" and here the old woman's voice dropped its comfortable rasp and found something underneath it, something with gravel in it, "—don't let them put a lantern near your face. There are men in this town who've bought bread from 'the Yarrow boy' for eleven years. Faces are debts too, and yours is known all over the market square."

Dominic reached up and touched the brim of the cap she wasn't wearing yet, an unconscious gesture, eleven years deep. The wool cap she *was* wearing — borrowed, shapeless, smelling of tallow — sat differently. Everything about her current self sat differently: the bound chest under three shirts, the smudged jaw, the shoulders she'd learned at fourteen to square just so, neither broad nor narrow, deliberately forgettable.

Her father had cut her first cap out of good felt the winter he decided she would be his son. *A name is only a letterform, Dom,* he'd said, tugging it down over her ears. *Same letter, cast in a different matrix. Nobody reads the type. They read the words.* She had believed him. She had lived eleven years proving him right.

Now somewhere below, pinned to a board beside the north gate, was a piece of paper describing exactly that letterform, and every eye in Vellamont's garrison had been taught to read it.

* * *

She went down through the tannery yard at dusk, when the light does its best work for liars — long shadows, no color, everyone squinting. The clerk's costume was Odile's late husband's: a coat of respectable drab, a muffler up to the chin, a leather folio under one arm stuffed with genuine-looking nonsense Odile had provided from a drawer of documents she declined to explain. Dominic had added the finishing touches herself, because if there was one thing a printer's child knew, it was how paper wanted to be held. She carried the folio the way her father had carried invoices: clamped, impatient, a man with too much to do to be interesting.

Vellamont at dusk in the last week of December was a town holding its breath so hard it had turned blue. Smoke stood straight up from the chimneys, too cold for wind. Soldiers moved in pairs along the frozen ruts of the high street. Shutters closed early now, not from darkness but from policy; the whole town had adopted the sleeping habits of prey animals, and Dominic moved through it feeling the terrible kinship of it, one more frightened creature among hundreds, indistinguishable unless you knew what to look for.

She made herself walk past the garrison square instead of around it. This was discipline, and she paid for every step of it. The proclamation boards stood there under an awning, lit by a lamp some conscientious corporal trimmed every evening, and she allowed herself one slow glance as she passed, the glance of a bored clerk with nowhere better to look.

The top sheet was a tariff notice. The second was a curfew amendment. The third—

The third was drawn, not printed. Someone had sketched a face in charcoal over the text, quick and accurate and deeply unfair: the sharp jaw, the ink-stained fingers rendered in hatching, the cap pulled low. And beneath it, in a fair secretary hand:

*Wanted for theft of official records: the person known as YARROW, sometime of the printing premises by the market, presenting as a MALE of some nineteen years, slight build, dark hair, habitual stoop. Answers readily when spoken to about books or trade. Was raised in this town and is KNOWN TO MANY OF YOU. Any person providing information leading to recovery of the records will be compensated. Any person sheltering same will be treated as accomplice.*

*Known to many of you.*

They had written her whole life into four lines and made it a warrant. Eleven years of buying flour with a lowered voice, of mending the widow Aubert's shutters, of being *Master Yarrow's son, quiet lad, keeps to the shop* — all of it, every careful day, gathered up and turned against her. Her disguise had not been stripped away. It had been *published*. Ravel hadn't unmasked the Yarrow boy; he had issued an edition of him, one hundred copies pasted to every wall in the district, and now the whole town was a proof sheet and every reader a potential corrector.

And the worst of it — the part that made her teeth ache behind the muffler — was that it was *good*. Accurate. The habitual stoop, God help her. *Answers readily when spoken to about books or trade.* Whoever had written that had watched her for longer than an afternoon. She thought of Ilse saying, weeks ago in the shop, *you talk about typefaces the way other people talk about their mothers,* and had to swallow something that was half laugh and half something else entirely.

Keep walking. She kept walking. The clerk with the folio turned up the rue des Tanneurs at a pace suggesting a warm stove and an unpaid invoice at the end of it, and did not run, because running was the one thing the Yarrow boy would never do, and she had to be someone else now entirely.

* * *

The trouble — and she would have ample cold hours afterward to examine exactly where it began — was that the north gate stood at the end of the rue des Tanneurs, and the road beyond it was the only road she had ever imagined out of this town, and she was already halfway there, and it seemed a shame to have come so far not to look.

So she looked.

The wicket door glowed orange at its edges where the brazier burned on the far side. One soldier took passes; one soldier took faces; and beside them, propped on an easel like a painting at a fair, angled to catch the lamplight, stood a copy of the notice. Of course there was a copy at the gate. Where else would it do its work?

Dominic stopped twenty paces off, at the corner of the cooperage, and became a clerk consulting his folio. This much was even true; the folio contained a requisition for tallow, and she checked it with genuine attention while her eyes did their real work over its edge.

Flaw one: the passes were being read slowly. Very slowly. The soldier with them was young and careful and turning each sheet as though the answers might be hiding on the reverse. That meant delay, and delay meant queues, and queues meant standing in lamplight, and lamplight meant the charcoal sketch on the easel getting a long, leisurely look at everyone who passed.

Flaw two was worse. The second soldier wasn't checking faces against the notice at all. He was checking them against *nothing*, against memory, against the general principle of suspicion — and every few men he would say a pleasant word, and lean in, and look down at the pass and then up at the mouth above it, and Dominic understood with a lurch that he was listening to voices. Reading them like type. Looking for the one that matched a description that said *answers readily when spoken to about books or trade.*

"He's good," said a voice at her shoulder, and Dominic's heart performed a maneuver that would have killed a horse.

Odile stood beside her in the cooperage shadow with a basket of oysters she could not possibly have legitimately acquired, looking at the gate with the frank professional interest of a woman appraising someone else's rigging.

"You followed me," Dominic whispered.

"I was going my own way, which happens to be your way, which happens to be away from that gate." Odile did not look at her. "That's Corporal Fenner with the passes. He's nobody. The tall one talking — that's Sergeant Brissac, came in with the requisition, used to run deserters down along the coast roads. He doesn't read papers. He reads *people.* You can't be a clerk past Brissac, girl, because clerks don't exist to him. He'll want to know what's in the folio, and then he'll want to know why your hands shake, and then he'll offer you a drink to watch you take it."

"My hands don't shake."

"They're shaking now."

Dominic looked down. They were. She flattened them against the folio and breathed the way her father had taught her to breathe over a graver, slow through the nose, the stroke happening between heartbeats.

"Go home, Odile."

"In a moment. First, look there." With her chin, the old woman indicated the wicket. "Watch who comes through."

A cart was passing inward — firewood, a driver Dominic half-recognized from the market — and the procedure went like this: Fenner took the driver's pass, read it, handed it back; Brissac asked the driver something; the driver answered; Brissac laughed, clapped the driver's shoulder, and waved him through. All of it warm. All of it easy. All of it, Dominic saw now, a performance of ease laid precisely over the machinery of a trap, because at the edge of the lamplight stood two more soldiers who did nothing at all except look at the faces of everyone admitted, and compare them, openly and patiently, with the charcoal drawing on the easel.

"They're not hunting the book at the gate," Dominic said slowly. "They're hunting *me.*"

"They're hunting you everywhere. Gate's just where they've got the light." Odile shifted her basket. "Ravel's not stupid. He knows the book won't come through the front door. He's put your face there because he thinks *you'll* come through it, sooner or later, because you're young and young people believe in doors." She paused. "Or because he wants you to think this door's the trap, so you'll pick the one that isn't, and he'll have guessed that too."

"That's a very pessimistic theory of a very clever man."

"It's not a theory. It's a biography. Clever men always show you one door and stand behind the other." Odile started to turn away, then stopped, and her voice came out lower, rougher, almost gentle, which from Odile was more alarming than any threat. "Eleven years, girl. Eleven years you walked through this town as somebody else, and it worked, and you got proud of it — don't glare, it's not a sin to be proud of good work. But understand what that paper means. He hasn't caught you. He's done something worse. He's made it so that *being seen at all* is the danger. The Yarrow boy can't buy bread now. Can't show his face at a gate, a market, a church. Your father built you a name that hid you, and Ravel has hung it on a board with a price under it."

Dominic looked at the notice one last time. The charcoal face looked back, jaw set, cap low, eleven years of careful living compressed into a likeness that was somehow both exact and a stranger's. *Answers readily when spoken to about books or trade.* She wanted to laugh. Some part of her did laugh, quietly, into the muffler, a small mad sound that steamed in the freezing air — because it was, when you stepped back from it, the finest compliment anyone had ever paid her. Somewhere in that garrison was an officer who had studied her the way she studied type, and found her worth describing accurately.

Then the laughing part finished, and the rest of it arrived.

Because there was no way through the gate. Not tonight, not as anyone she knew how to be. The clerk was spent; Brissac would unwrap him in a minute flat. The Yarrow boy was posted on an easel. And underneath both disguises, in the dark between them, was a person who had never once, in nineteen years, walked through the world with nothing between her skin and the eyes of strangers — and could not start tonight, at a checkpoint, in winter, carrying the only copy of forty-one lives sewn into a sailcloth bolt hidden back at the tannery.

"Come along," Odile said. "You've seen your door. Now come and help me carry these oysters past it like an honest woman, and we'll both enjoy the irony."

* * *

They walked back through Vellamont side by side, the smuggler and the clerk, and the soldiers at the gate did not spare them a glance, which proved either Odile's theory of wallpaper or the completeness of their distraction. Halfway up the high street, Dominic realized her legs were trembling — not fear now, exactly, but the aftershock of it, the body settling its accounts — and she was glad of the older woman's pace, which permitted slowness.

At the mouth of the tannery lane, Odile stopped.

"Now you know," she said. "Gate's no door. Wall's no door. Which leaves the river."

"The river's ice and current and a mile of open ground with dogs."

"Aye. Terrible door." Odile hefted her basket. "But it's the only one he hasn't drawn a picture of you on."

She went on into the yard. Dominic stood a moment longer in the lane, looking back the way they'd come, toward the distant orange glow of the gate and the town lying dark around it, every shuttered window a held breath, forty-one names asleep behind forty-one doors, each of them depending on a book that couldn't pass a checkpoint in any shape she owned.

Her father had said a name was only a letterform. Fine. Then she needed a new casting — a new matrix, a new body of type entirely, something Ravel's careful eye had never read. And there was exactly one person in Vellamont who drew things for a living, who measured walls and copied hands and knew, God help her, *both* of Dominic's names.

Which meant the next door was not a gate at all. It was a question, and it had Ilse Brandt's face on it, and Dominic had no idea whether knocking on it would save her or end everything.

She pulled the muffler higher and went in out of the cold.
