## 20. Ravel Suspects

Commandant Ravel had a theory about paper, which he shared with no one, because his theories about paper were the only private property he still owned. The theory was this: every lie a man tells eventually takes the form of a document. A spoken lie drifts like smoke and is gone by supper. A written lie sits down. It makes itself comfortable. It dates itself, signs itself, and waits — patiently, smugly — for someone who knows how handwriting works.

Ravel knew how handwriting worked.

So it was that on the ninth of December, in the requisitioned customhouse that served as garrison headquarters, he sat alone at a table lit by two candles and one bad lamp, with a mug of wine gone to vinegar, and read the same page for the fourth time.

*Requisition order 114-C. Authorizing removal of printing equipment from the Yarrow premises to the garrison stores, for inventory purposes.*

On its face — and Ravel was a man who made his living on faces, both the human kind and the kind you find at the bottom of a stack of forms — it was unremarkable. Bureaucracies bred such orders the way stagnant water bred mosquitoes: constantly, seasonally, without anyone's quite intending it. Printing equipment was heavy, army inventory was perpetually wrong, and some clerk somewhere had plausibly grown tired of the discrepancy.

Except that no clerk had grown tired of anything, because no clerk had written it.

He turned the page toward the lamp. The ink was good ink — garrison ink, the iron-gall mixture that ate into paper like remorse into a conscience. The wording was correct. The form number was correct; he had checked it against the registry himself, twice, and found the corresponding blank torn out of the book, which showed either remarkable thoroughness or remarkable luck. The signature of Captain Ferrand, who was real and currently stationed at the ferry depot and would swear until his dying day that he had never seen this document, was a competent forgery. Slightly too competent, actually. Ferrand's true signature slouched. This one stood up straight like a soldier at inspection. Forgers almost always overcorrected. They gave their lies better posture than the truth ever had.

But none of that was what held him. Forms could be stolen, blanks could be torn, signatures could be practiced against a stolen exemplar. All of that was craft, and craft was common.

What held him was the *2*.

Specifically, the numeral 2 in "114-C," and again in the date, and again in the quantity column where someone had entered *two presses, one cabinet, sundry type.* Three little twos, and every one of them made in the same peculiar way: the base curve finished with a small flat horizontal stroke, as though the writer had lost patience with the number halfway through and decided to end it like an envelope flap.

Ravel leaned back. His chair, which had been built for a customs officer considerably wider than himself, creaked its disapproval.

He had seen that envelope-flap stroke before. He had seen it forty times a day for three months, in fact, because it lived on every map that came out of the survey office. Cartography required thousands of numerals — elevations, distances, bearings, soundings in the river — and Ilse Brandt, the army's cartographer, drew her twos exactly that way. Every time. With the small flat finish. As consistent as a heartbeat and, until this moment, as invisible to anyone not looking for it.

Ravel looked for things. That was substantially the whole of his profession. Generals looked at maps of fronts; ministers looked at reports; Ravel looked at the space between what a town said about itself and what its paperwork said about itself, and nine times out of ten, that space was where the bodies were buried. Metaphorically. Usually metaphorically.

He picked up the forged order again and held it beside a map of Vellamont's riverfront that Brandt had drafted in October — a lovely piece of work, precise without being fussy, the wharf pilings rendered with something approaching affection. He compared numeral to numeral under the lamp.

The same hand.

He sat with that for a long while.

Here was the difficulty, and it was a difficulty Ravel appreciated the way one appreciates a well-made trap one has stepped in oneself: there were innocent explanations. There nearly always were, and they nearly always turned out to be lies wearing innocence like a borrowed coat, but one had to let them try the coat on first, that was simple fairness. Perhaps Brandt had drafted the order at Ferrand's request, a routine chore, never knowing the blank was torn rather than legitimately removed. Clerks did borrow each other's hands; half the paperwork in any army was written by whoever could write, signed by whoever outranked them. Perhaps the similarity of the numerals was coincidence — some writing masters taught that flourish, though Ravel privately doubted it, having examined enough hands in enough provincial archives to know that everyone believed their quirks were common and nobody's were.

And perhaps — here his mind went, because it was his mind and it went where it liked — perhaps the cartographer had walked past the sealed Yarrow print shop more often than her duties strictly required. Perhaps she had been the one person in Vellamont who noticed that the shop's exterior promised more interior than the tax rolls allowed. Ravel had read her survey notes. She was not a woman who overlooked dimensions.

He thought about the ledger.

It had become fashionable, in the dispatches from headquarters, to refer to the Yarrow ledger as a matter already resolved. Surrendered or destroyed; either way, closed. Ravel had ordered it burned himself, back when burning it had seemed like mercy — a whole town's worth of old debts, aliases, and accommodations swept clean in one fire, no denunciations necessary, no neighbors fed to the tribunal to feed the tribunal's appetite. He had even felt briefly virtuous about it, which for Ravel was an unfamiliar sensation, like wearing new boots: correct in principle, blistering in practice.

Then the year had begun running out. The new year demanded traitors the way the old year had demanded taxes, and headquarters' appetite had not diminished with distance. Hence his reversal, hence the sealing of the print shop, hence the quiet instruction that the premises be searched properly — thoroughly, methodically, by men who understood that hidden things were usually hidden in boring places.

And now a forged requisition, in a cartographer's hand, authorizing removal of printing equipment that the sealing party had sworn — he checked his notes — *sworn did not exist*. No press found. No cabinet. Sundry nothing. Sergeant Boileau's report had been very clear and slightly defensive about it, in the way reports are defensive when the writer suspects the reader will eventually ask questions.

So. Someone believed there was equipment worth stealing from an empty shop. Someone had gone to considerable trouble — a torn blank, a practiced captain, good ink — to authorize removing it legally rather than simply taking it. Which meant the removal needed to look legal. Which meant someone might eventually audit it.

Which meant the equipment was not equipment.

Ravel drank the vinegar wine without tasting it. It deserved better and he had stopped deserving things around 1789.

He considered, briefly and professionally, the alternative reading: that Brandt was smuggling. Officers smuggled constantly — brandy, linen, silverware pried from requisitioned houses. It was practically a pension plan. But nobody forged a requisition for brandy. Brandy forgave everything. You only needed paper armor for cargo that somebody would hang you for.

A book, then. The book. Still in the shop after all, hidden inside whatever that shop was hiding — and Ravel had always believed it was hiding something; buildings that paid taxes on air always were — and now out, or being taken out, moved through his checkpoints under a document written by the last person in Vellamont anyone would think to suspect. A cartographer. A professional observer of other people's geography. If you wished to move a thing through a town, you could do far worse than recruit the woman whose job was knowing where everything was.

He should arrest her tonight. He drafted the thought, examined it, and set it aside the way one sets aside undercooked bread.

Because — and here was the part he would have said aloud only to the vinegar, if the vinegar could be trusted — he was not certain. And Ravel had learned, at cost, the difference between suspicion and knowledge, and what tribunals did to men who brought them the former labeled as the latter. Arrest Brandt on handwriting, and her counsel — the army would provide one, the army provided everything grudgingly — would produce ten legitimate documents in her hand and dare him to explain why a woman's twos convicted her. Meanwhile the actual book, if there was an actual book, would travel wherever it was going, unwatched, because he would have shown his hand for nothing. Spent the accusation. Accusations were ammunition, and Ravel hoarded ammunition.

No. There was a better way, slower, less satisfying, the way he preferred most good things: wait. Watch. Let the machinery of suspicion assemble itself from evidence rather than inference. If Brandt had forged an order to remove printing equipment, then sooner or later — very much sooner, if she was competent, which she was — someone would arrive at the garrison stores with a cart and the order and expect to sign for a press that did not exist. All he had to do was make sure that when she came, the receiving clerk was a man Ravel had chosen, and that the man said yes. Said yes to everything. Helped load. Waved the cart through.

Let the rope pay out. A noose only worked if it was drawn slowly. Drawn fast, it was just a collar, and collars could be slipped.

* * *

In the morning he was a different man, or rather the same man performing a different man, which amounted to the same thing and had kept him employed through three regimes.

He took his breakfast in the survey office.

This required no explanation, or rather it required the explanation everyone wanted to believe, which was that the Commandant was fussy about maps. He was fussy about maps. He was also fussy about not being poisoned, about the quality of his candles, and about the correct boiling of eggs, and no one had ever deduced espionage from any of these preferences. A commandant who wandered the offices with his cup was simply a commandant who distrusted sitting still — and every officer in the garrison had privately concluded that Ravel's restlessness was the residue of an unhappy marriage, or digestion, or ambition, and each theory protected the truth nicely.

"Brandt," he said, pausing at her table, where she was working on the contour survey of the northern hills with the concentration of a surgeon. "Your river soundings. The October series."

She looked up. She had a face that gave away very little and gave it away honestly, if that paradox could be managed; Ravel had spent three months failing to manage it. "October series, sir. They were filed."

"They were. I want them re-verified. The channel shifts in winter; the ferry landing depths will matter if we move artillery." True, all of it. Truth was so obliging about lending itself out. "That means resurveying the waterfront. Both banks. Take what time it needs."

Something crossed her face — not guilt, he thought, or not only guilt; possibly the ordinary annoyance of a craftsman told to redo work she knew was sound. Or possibly a woman recalculating routes. He genuinely could not tell, and told himself that the inability was itself instructive. Innocent people were hard to read because they were thinking about the work. Guilty ones were hard to read because they were thinking about you. The result was identical and only the follow-through distinguished them.

"Both banks," she repeated. "Including the north gate approaches?"

"Especially those. Headquarters keeps asking questions about the gate." Also true. Headquarters asked questions about everything, the way a toddler asks why, ceaselessly and without wanting answers. "You'll file weekly. Directly to me."

"Directly to you," she said, and wrote it down, and her twos — he watched her write the date — finished with the little flat stroke, neat as a signature on a confession no court would accept.

"Good," he said, and moved on, and complimented Lieutenant Aubry on his penmanship, which was terrible, and took another egg from the mess tray, which was adequate, and by eight o'clock had arranged three things that would never appear in any order book.

First: the garrison stores receiving desk would be manned henceforth by Corporal Vasseur, transferred from the bridge checkpoint for reasons documented as a disciplinary rotation and known to exactly two people, one of whom was Ravel and the other of whom was now suspicious of his own luck. Vasseur's instructions were delivered verbally, in the yard, while both men watched a farrier work, so that the words would have witnesses of a sort but no paper existence. *Whatever comes with a requisition order — accept it, log it loosely, help carry it, lose it in the general stores. Ask nothing. Report everything to me within the hour.* Vasseur had nodded with the particular solemnity of a corporal who suspected he was in a story larger than his rank and had decided, sensibly, to survive it.

Second: watchers. Not on Brandt — watching a cartographer was nearly impossible; she looked at things for a living and noticed being looked at the way other people noticed weather. No, he set them on the opposite end of the thread. Two men on the Yarrow shop, relieved every six hours, instructed to see nothing and remember everything. Two more rotating among the checkpoints with orders to note, without stopping, any cart, barrow, sledge, or burdened person passing northward — especially at dusk, when the light excused mistakes and the dogs were hungry. And one man — Duval, slow, patient, unimaginative, therefore perfect — simply to walk the town at irregular hours and notice whether any familiar face appeared in places where it had no business. Duval had once identified a smuggled printing press by noticing that a widow had bought twice her usual quantity of lamp oil. Ravel treasured him the way other men treasured racehorses.

Third: he wrote a letter to headquarters. Not about Brandt — nothing about Brandt, not yet, not a syllable. About procedure. He requested confirmation of all requisition orders above serial 110 issued since the first of December, citing — truthfully, damn it, truthfully — irregularities in the winter inventory. When the reply came back, it would state plainly that no such order as 114-C existed. And then the document would be dead paper, deniable by no one, and whoever presented it at the stores would present their own evidence, willingly, into Ravel's waiting hands.

He sealed it and set it with the outgoing pouch and felt the small cold satisfaction that was as close to joy as his temperament allowed.

There remained only the matter of sitting still, which was the hardest duty in any army. He addressed it in his own fashion: he resumed the rounds he had let lapse, drank coffee in the cooperage, inspected latrines with genuine attention (a neglected garrison rots from below, in every sense), chatted with the ferry depot's sergeant about eels. He made himself so visible, so ordinary, so relentlessly *present* that his absence of action became invisible. The town saw the Commandant doing commandant things and relaxed, and relaxation, Ravel knew, was when towns talked.

And talk it did, in its sideways manner. The baker mentioned that young Marchand — the Marchand widow's boy, not the widow herself, the widow required a category of her own — had been asking soldiers odd questions about patrol schedules. A laundress noted that the tannery had taken delivery of more food than tanners required, tanners smelling as they did, though the foreman explained it as charity to a sick cousin, and explanations were like hats, Ravel thought; you could tell a great deal from whether they fit. None of it was proof. All of it was texture. He collected texture the way Brandt collected elevations, patiently, one measurement at a time, until the shape of the land declared itself.

* * *

On the third evening he permitted himself one indulgence: he walked past the Yarrow print shop.

It was not sentiment. He told himself it was not sentiment, and the telling was mostly successful. The shop sat behind its nailed boards the way a face sits behind a shuttered expression, and the seals he had ordered were intact — wax unbroken, nails unmoved, snow on the sill undisturbed except by wind. Whatever had been inside was outside now, or soon would be. He stood in the falling dark a moment longer than inspection required, feeling the cold work its way through his coat, and admitted to the night what he would admit to no aide:

he hoped, insofar as a man in his position was permitted hopes, that the book had gotten out.

Not for the town's sake — the town had survived worse than exposure; towns were durable, it was individuals who broke. And certainly not for the Yarrow creature's sake, whoever and whatever the Yarrow creature truly was; Ravel had posted the description himself, "the Yarrow boy," and had known when he wrote it that he was describing a costume, and had chosen to describe the costume anyway because the costume was what the paperwork knew. No — he hoped the book had gotten out for the same reason he had ordered it burned in the first place, back when mercy was still administratively convenient. Because it contained forty-one lives reduced to entries, and because he had spent twenty years learning exactly what armies did with lists of names, and the arithmetic never changed: the list was always shorter afterward.

But hoping a thing and permitting a thing were different jurisdictions entirely, and Ravel had never once confused them.

He turned up his collar and walked back toward the lights of the customhouse. Behind him the watchers settled into the shadows across the lane; ahead of him Corporal Vasseur was taking up his post at the stores desk, rehearsing his best helpful face; and somewhere in the town, in a warm room he could not picture, a hand that drew its twos like envelope flaps was surely at work on whatever came next.

The rope was out. Ravel had tied his knot with care, tested the anchor, and chosen his tree.

Now he would wait, and watch it draw tight — and pray, in the perfunctory way of practical men, that when it caught, it caught the right throat.
