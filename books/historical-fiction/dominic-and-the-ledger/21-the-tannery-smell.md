## 21. The Tannery Smell

Widow Marchand had three rules for surviving an occupation, and she recited them most mornings the way other women said their prayers, because prayers required believing someone was listening and rules only required stubbornness.

One: never be the first to know anything.

Two: never be the last to sell anything.

Three: never, under any circumstances, owe anyone a kindness you cannot pay back with interest.

She was seventy-one years old, widowed twice — once to a fever and once, more expensively, to a man who gambled — and she had outlived every person in Vellamont who could testify against her, with two exceptions, and one of them kept a book.

She thought about the book more than she liked to admit. Not constantly. But in the way a tongue returns to a loose tooth: *Héloïse Marchand, née Vassal.* A name she had not used in thirty years, written in that careful printer's hand, recorded on a page in a shop on the tanner's row, along with the fact that the warrant for her arrest — issued under the old regime for forging customs seals, which was unkind of them since she had only ever forged *good* ones — had been quietly made to disappear. Paid for. Erased. Forgiven, if you wanted to use the sentimental word, which Héloïse Marchand did not.

The word she preferred was *entered*. It had been entered into the ledger, and therefore it existed, and therefore it could be read by anyone holding the book, including, at present, a revolutionary army with a taste for old warrants.

So when Marchand set out on the fourth morning of the freeze to collect her tallow from Renard the chandler and cut down through the alley behind the tannery, and caught a smell that did not belong —

Well. Rule two had never failed her yet.

* * *

The smell, to be precise about it, was wrongness itself. Tannery row smelled the way tannery row smelled: lime and dung and wet hide, a stink so committed to itself that the women of that street stopped apologizing for it sometime around the Revolution of the previous century. Everyone in Vellamont knew the smell. What everyone in Vellamont did *not* know was what it should *not* contain, and Héloïse Marchand, who had spent her girlhood forging customs seals in a tanner's attic in Marseille, knew exactly one thing better than anyone else in this town:

What lye did not smell like when it came from the pits versus when it came fresh from a barrel.

And there was fresh lye on the air. Someone had opened a new cask behind the tannery within the last day. And nobody on tanner's row had money for a new cask of anything, because the army had bought their entire winter output for boot leather and paid in assignats worth rather less than the paper they were printed on.

Marchand stood in the alley with her basket on her arm and did arithmetic.

A new cask of lye meant someone was working the pits off-hours or hiding something in them. Hiding something in the pits meant the something did not want to be found, and things that did not want to be found in Vellamont in the winter of 1792 were, these days, generally books.

She did not go straight to the loft. That would have been amateurish. She went and collected her tallow, argued with Renard about the weight of it — he was shorting her by a full half-pound and they both knew it and both enjoyed the argument — and then walked home the long way, past the print shop with its sealed doors and the pale nails driven in fresh, and past the garrison, where she noted without appearing to note the comings and goings at the side entrance where the sergeant with the kind face took his tobacco.

Then she went home, fed her cat — a fat gray monster named Citoyen, whom she had named ironically and who had grown into the name with alarming enthusiasm — and sat down at her table to think.

* * *

Here is what the widow Marchand knew, laid out on the table alongside her mending like so many socks.

She knew the Yarrow child — the Yarrow *boy*, as the town had always politely agreed to call her, a fiction maintained with the collective discipline of people who all owed the same man money — had been seen nowhere since the shop was sealed. She knew this because Marchand noticed things; it was her profession before it was her hobby. She knew the cartographer woman, the serious one with ink-stained cuffs, had been asking after ferry schedules she had no professional reason to ask after. She knew Odile at the ferry crossing had suddenly come into money enough to buy brandy that wasn't his usual paint thinner. And she knew that a certain loft above the tannery, belonging to old Perrin, had stood empty since Perrin went to his reward two years ago, and that its ladder was accessible from the yard behind the lime pits, and that the lye cask was new.

She did not *know* the Yarrow child was in the loft. But knowing, in Marchand's experience, was merely the price you paid before you could afford certainty, and certainty was going cheap this winter.

Now. What does one do with such information?

Marchand poured herself a finger of the cooking brandy — the real bottle stayed hidden inside the false bottom of her flour bin, because rule one applied to liquor above all — and considered.

The commandant wanted the ledger. This was no secret; he had announced its burning from the church steps in the voice of a man reading a laundry list. Then, apparently changing his mind the way governments do, he had sealed the print shop and posted soldiers, and there were now watchers at the north gate examining every soul who left with unusual attention. Something had shifted. The book had gone from rubbish to treasure without passing through usefulness, which told Marchand the army's masters upstairs wanted names, and wanted them before the new year, and Ravel was a reasonable man being made unreasonable by deadline.

A reasonable man made unreasonable pays well. That was practically the founding principle of her entire adult life.

She imagined the transaction. She had done it often enough in Marseille: walk into the office, set the information on the desk like a dead mouse a cat brings in, and wait for the counting. The garrison sergeant first, perhaps — no, go over his head, sergeants take cuts. The aide. Or the commandant himself; she had seen him at Mass, such as Mass still was, and he had the hollow-cheeked patience of a man who listened to everything and forgave nothing. He would listen. He would pay. And in exchange for her information, the widow Marchand would purchase — what?

Safety. A guarantee. Perhaps a little paper with a seal on it saying that whatever names surfaced from whatever book, *Héloïse Marchand* was not among them. Immunity. The one commodity Vellamont had none of left.

Her own name sat in that ledger like a splinter under the skin. Thirty years healed over and still she could feel it when the weather changed. If the army got the book and read it, some clerk with good handwriting and bad intentions would find *Vassal* eventually, cross-reference the old registry — they kept registries now with German thoroughness, the cartographer woman saw to that — and then the widow Marchand, respected pillar of the rue Saint-Aubin, purchaser of candles and hater of sentiment, would become once more Héloïse Vassal, seal-forger, and would die in some depot city with her name spelled wrong on the warrant.

Sell the information, buy immunity. It was clean. It was even, in its way, self-defense.

She finished the brandy and put on her good shawl.

* * *

The garrison occupied the old customs house, which Vellamont's elders had surrendered with the speed of men who had never liked the building anyway. Its stone steps were worn into shallow smiles by a century of clerks, and now soldiers stood on them, and their boots were undoing a hundred years of polite erosion.

Marchand climbed those steps slowly, because old women who climb slowly are invisible, and invisibility was her second-best asset after memory.

At the door, the sergeant with the kind face — Groleau, she'd heard him called — stopped her with a hand that managed somehow to be apologetic. "Madame. The commandant isn't receiving petitions today."

"Petitions," Marchand said, tasting the word and finding it beneath her. "Young man, I am seventy-one years old. I do not petition. I *inform*."

"Then I'll inform you that the commandant is busy."

"With what? He has been busy for a month and the town has been quiet for a month, and in my experience those two facts are related in ways that end badly for somebody." She looked up at him — she was a small woman and getting smaller, the way of it, compacting year by year toward her essential self — and softened by exactly one degree, which for Marchand was a great deal. "Sergeant. What I have to say concerns a book. And I think you know which book I mean, and I think your commandant has been lying awake wondering where it went. I can tell him. I can tell him before the new year."

Groleau's face did something complicated. The kind face was, she suspected, the real one, which made him ill-suited to his work and interesting to her. "Wait here," he said, and went inside, and Marchand waited, arranging on her tongue exactly how she would say it: the lye cask, the empty loft, the ladder, the child. Four facts, laid out like cards. Simple. Clean. Priced high.

The morning was very cold and very bright, snow coming down in that fine dry powder that doesn't melt on wool. Through the open door she could see the corridor of the old customs house, and the lamps lit against the dark, though it was barely noon — they kept lamps burning at all hours now, the whole garrison living in permanent dusk of its own manufacture.

And that was when the trouble started, because waiting is dangerous for a woman of her age, and by that I mean: waiting lets you think.

* * *

She thought about the night, eleven years ago now, when she had come to the print shop after closing.

It had not been a dramatic errand. That was the thing she always returned to, the detail that refused to dramatize itself no matter how many times she turned it over. She had come to settle an account — a printing bill, forty sous, genuinely owed — and while she stood at the counter counting out coins, she had seen the ledger lying open on the press stone, and the child had seen her see it, and there had passed between them one of those silences that contain an entire negotiation.

Because there, under *M*, in that patient hand, was *Héloïse Marchand, née Vassal,* and beside it, in the column for debts: *paid in full.*

Not the forty sous. The forty sous were separate. *Paid in full* referred to the other entry, the older one, in faded ink, dated before the child was born: a sum paid by a typeface cutter named Yarrow to a magistrate in Lyon, for the purpose of losing a certain document. Her father had never told her. Marchand had assumed for thirty years that her escape from that warrant had been her own cleverness — her forged seals, her new name, her nerve. And here it was in black and white: Yarrow had paid. Quietly. Without telling her. Without ever, in twenty years of trading across the same counter, mentioning it, hinting at it, or so much as letting his eyes rest on her a moment too long.

He had simply crossed it out. In the margin, in his neat hand: *forgiven.*

The child had watched her read it. Marchand had watched the child watch. And then the child — fourteen, fifteen, with ink on her fingers and her father's grave eyes — had closed the book gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping person, and said, "Forty sous, madame. Thank you," and sold her the funeral broadsheet she'd come in for, and never spoke of it again.

Marchand had walked home in the dark and sat in her kitchen and cried, briefly and furiously, the way one cries at a kindness one has done nothing to deserve and cannot imagine how to repay.

That was eleven years ago. The father was dead. The child lived in a boy's name like a hermit crab in a borrowed shell. And the book that held the only generous act anyone had ever performed for Héloïse Marchand was hidden somewhere above a tannery, hunted by an army, and she was standing on the garrison steps preparing to sell it.

"Sergeant's fetching the aide," Groleau reported, reappearing. "Won't be long. You may come in out of the cold."

"In a moment," said Marchand. "My knees."

Her knees were, in fact, fine. Her knees were the finest part of her. It was the rest of her that had begun to tremble, very slightly, in a way she recognized from long ago and disliked intensely.

* * *

Here was the flaw in the plan, the flaw she had walked past in the alley without seeing, because greed makes a poor lantern: selling the location of the ledger meant selling Dominic Yarrow.

Not the book alone. Books don't hang; people do. A girl disguised as a dead man's son, carrying a stolen ledger past three checkpoints — the army wouldn't need her testimony once they had her body. And the tribunal, wherever it was convening, whatever printers' court she was staggering toward, would sit without its evidence and without its witness, and the thirty-nine — Marchand didn't know the number, but she knew it was *many*, many neighbors, many names she'd traded with across decades — would stand exposed with nothing between them and the new regime's appetite except a burned shop and a girl in a ferryman's hut.

Versus: one old woman's name, safe in nobody's book at last.

It was a good trade. By any honest accounting it was a *reasonable* trade, and Marchand had spent a lifetime teaching herself that reasonable was the highest compliment available to a survivor.

But.

But the child had closed the book gently.

But the father had paid for a stranger's life and told no one, not even her, and had entered it not as credit — Yarrow had never once, in all those pages, entered a kindness as credit — but as *forgiven*, as if the debt ran the wrong direction entirely, as if he owed *her* something, as if forgiveness were just another transaction in a ledger where the currency was different.

Citoyen wound around her ankles, back home, probably. Cats have no politics. Lucky creatures.

Inside the corridor, she heard footsteps — the aide, young and brisk, coming to receive her. Coming to receive four simple facts and pay for them in paper, and everything would proceed from there with the smooth awful efficiency of institutions, and it would all be perfectly reasonable.

"Madame?" Groleau again, holding the door wide. Warm lamplight spilled out onto the steps, onto the snow, onto her good shawl.

And Héloïse Marchand stood at the threshold of the garrison of Vellamont with her mouth open to speak, and what came out instead was:

"I appear to have forgotten why I came."

The sergeant blinked. "You said it concerned a book—"

"Did I?" She frowned, magnificently, the frown of a woman whose memory was failing in a manner both tragic and inconvenient for everyone around her. "I forget everything these days. My doctor says it is the cold. It was something about my tallow order. Renard is shorting me half a pound, you understand, and I came to—" she waved a hand vaguely at the interior of a military garrison, "—register a complaint about tallow."

"About... tallow."

"The army requisitions everything else. One assumes grievances fall under requisition as well." She drew her shawl tighter and descended one step, then another, moving with the unhurried dignity of a ship leaving harbor. "You've been very kind, Sergeant. Tell your commandant the tallow matter will keep."

Behind her, in the warm doorway, Groleau said nothing, because there was nothing a kind-faced man could say, and Marchand walked down the worn steps into the snow with her heart hammering like a journeyman's press and absolutely no idea what she had just done.

Except that she knew. She knew exactly what she had done. She had chosen the ledger's silence over her own safety, and it hadn't even been difficult, which was the humiliating part — thirty years of rules, three of them iron, and one gentle page in a printer's book had knocked them all flat in a doorway on a Tuesday.

Rule three was broken. She owed a kindness now, a great one, and it could not be repaid with interest.

Unless.

Unless she repaid it the other way — the Yarrow way. Entered in the wrong column. Given, not lent.

Marchand turned up the rue Saint-Aubin through the falling snow, past the chandler's, past the shuttered shops, and did not slow her step until she reached the corner where the alley began that led, past the lime pits and the new-smelling lye, toward a certain ladder and a certain loft, where a certain fugitive was presumably hungry, cold, and unaware that the most dangerous woman in Vellamont was coming to call.

There would need to be bread, Marchand decided. And the good brandy, from the false bottom of the flour bin. And a story about why she had come.

The truth would do, she supposed. There was a first time for everything.

She turned into the alley.
