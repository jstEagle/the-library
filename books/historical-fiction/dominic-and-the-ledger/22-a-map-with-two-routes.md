## 22. A Map With Two Routes

Ilse Brandt had been taught, from her first week in the corps of cartographers, that a map was an argument. Every line was a claim about the world; every omission was a lie you had chosen deliberately rather than accidentally. Her instructor in the academy — a half-blind old Saxon named Herr Vogel who drew coastlines from memory and dared the sea to disagree — used to say that the honest mapmaker's sin was not error but flattery. *Do not draw the country as it wishes to be seen*, he said. *Draw it as it trips you.*

She thought of Vogel now, at midnight, in the tannery loft, working by the light of a shuttered lamp with its flame turned down to a thumbnail, drawing two routes on one sheet of paper.

The tannery smell was everywhere. It got into her hair, her cuffs, the ink. In a day or two she would stop noticing it, the way Dominic plainly had; already Dominic slept up here among the hanging hides like a sailor sleeping among sails, breathing the reek of lime and animal ruin as though it were home. Ilse could not decide whether this adaptability was courage or simply what happened to a person who had spent eleven years being someone she was not. Perhaps they were the same thing.

Below her, on the floorboards between the vats' shadow and the stack of salt barrels, Dominic Yarrow slept under Ilse's army greatcoat, one hand curled near the ledger even in sleep, the way other people sleep near a lover or a weapon. The book was wrapped in oilcloth now, tied with sailmaker's twine. It weighed perhaps four pounds. Ilse knew this because she had weighed it, because weighing things was what she did, because if she reduced the situation to weights and distances then maybe it would hold still long enough to be solved.

It did not hold still.

Here was the problem, drawn in her own careful hand across two days of observation: the north gate, the only gate through which a person could leave Vellamont carrying anything heavier than a prayer, had two exits from the town side. The road went through the checkpoint. But the river — frozen hard along its edges, broken and grinding in its channel — ran past the gate within thirty yards of the walls, and in the shallows below the old ferry landing there was a stretch where a person might wade, thigh-deep, along the ice shelf, past the sentries, out beyond the picket line, if the person did not mind the cold, the current, the dogs, and the very good chance of drowning.

Two routes.

Route one: the river shallows. Fast for the book — a wrapped bundle carried high against the chest passes dry, mostly, and even wet, oilcloth is oilcloth — and slow and lethal for a woman with a limp in her right ankle, which Ilse had noticed three days ago and which Dominic insisted was nothing, in the particular tone of voice that meant it was something. The sentries watched the road, not the water. Nobody expected anyone to be mad enough for the water. The book would live.

Route two: the road, in a cart, behind a face that no one had ever described correctly. Ravel had posted the description — Ilse had read it pinned inside the guardhouse, and had stood very still while reading it so that no one would wonder why she cared. *A youth of about nineteen, calling himself Yarrow's son, slight build, ink-stained fingers, speaks with the soft vowels of the river quarter.* They were hunting a boy. Dominic had not been a boy for years, and had never been a boy at all, but the description hunted the disguise, and the disguise was all the town knew how to see. On the road, in motion, in the crowd of market carts — possible. Possible for a person. Impossible for a book, because carts were searched, every one, twice, since the smuggling bust, and a search found bundles, and bundles found questions, and questions found forty-one names on a fire.

So. One route safe for the book. One route safe for Dominic. Not both. Never both. The map said so. The map was an argument and Ilse had lost it twice.

She sat back and looked at what she had drawn, and hated it, and drew it again anyway, cleaner, because if this was going to be a lie then it was going to be an accurate lie, and Vogel could rest in whatever grave held him.

* * *

The trouble with loving a printer's daughter — a type-cutter's daughter, she corrected herself; Dominic always corrected her, *my father cut letters, not pages, there is a difference, Ilse, letters are carved before they are born* — was that such a person could read intent the way Ilse read contour lines. You could not hand Dominic a map with a hesitation in it. She would feel it under her fingertips the way Ilse felt a false bearing.

So Ilse spent the whole of the next morning rehearsing honesty, which is to say she spent the morning learning precisely how much truth a person can carry before it becomes a weight that makes them put the thing down.

She tried out versions of it while ostensibly doing nothing. While mending the lamp wick. While dividing a heel of bread into unequal halves and giving Dominic the larger, which Dominic noticed, because Dominic noticed everything except when Ilse looked at her, which was the single reliable blind spot in an otherwise flawless instrument.

"You're drafting," Dominic said, around the bread. "You get a line between your eyes. Here." She touched the place between her own brows. "Like a plough went through."

"I'm thinking about drainage," Ilse said, which was true, in the sense that everything she thought about eventually drained somewhere.

"Mm," said Dominic, who trusted her, which was the whole catastrophe.

Ilse had considered, seriously and in order, the following alternatives:

One: tell her nothing, choose for her. Rejected because Dominic would find out, and because finding out afterward would break something that finding out beforehand might not. Also because it was cowardice dressed up as protection, and Ilse had worn that uniform before, in her family, for years, on her brother's behalf, and it had never once fit.

Two: tell her everything — *one of these routes kills you and one kills the book, choose* — rejected because Ilse had watched Dominic for nine days now, and knew exactly what Dominic would choose, and knew that choosing it would be a form of suicide performed so calmly and so reasonably that no one could call it that, least of all Dominic, who would call it *the only sensible option*, and smile, and Ilse would have to watch.

Three: the third way, the terrible one. Give her the choice without letting her see it was a choice. Let her hands do the deciding where her heart could not overrule them.

Vogel again, from somewhere: *the honest mapmaker's sin is flattery.* And its dark twin, which he had never said aloud but which Ilse had understood at seventeen: *and sometimes the kindest map lies by one degree, because one degree is the difference between the harbor and the rocks, and the sailor steers more truly toward a lie he trusts.*

She was not proud of the thought. She noted it anyway. A mapmaker records.

* * *

She waited until dusk, when the tannery lamps were out and the workers gone and the loft dim except for their one candle stub. She laid the paper on the floor between them and weighted its corners with four stones from the windowsill, because Dominic respected a well-weighted document the way other people respected scripture.

"Two ways out of Vellamont," Ilse said. "I've been over both. Listen before you look."

Dominic sat cross-legged, hands folded, ink ghosts on her fingers catching the light. "Listening."

"The road." Ilse put her finger on the western line. "Market carts go out at dawn, Thursday, for the grain exchange at Sainte-Colombe. You'd ride as a clerk for the tannery — I can draw the papers, I've drawn worse. The checkpoint searches cargo but clerks travel light. Once past the picket line you change roads at the chapel fork. Three hours to safety."

"And the book?"

"The book doesn't go on the road. Carts are turned out since the bust. Every crate, every bale." She let that sit. "The book goes the other way. The river."

Dominic looked at the second line, the blue one, hugging the wall, threading the shallows below the ferry landing. Ilse watched her read it the way she read type — fast, sure, missing nothing — and saw the moment she understood the water, the cold, the distance, the dogs.

"I could carry it," Dominic said. "Wading. High against—"

"No." Ilse kept her voice flat, professional, a briefing, nothing else. "The channel ice is moving. You go in to your waist, the current takes the pack or takes you, and either way the book is done and so possibly are you. Your ankle won't take the footing — don't argue, I've watched you walk for a week, you list to starboard like a loaded barge."

That earned her half a laugh, which hurt more than anger would have.

"So," Dominic said slowly. "Road for me. River for the book." Her finger moved between the lines. "Separately."

"It has to be separately."

"Then who carries the book?" And here was the hinge, the terrible little pivot on which the whole night turned, and Ilse heard her own voice say, in the tone of a report delivered to a superior, the sentence she had built her entire conscience around for two days:

"A courier. There's a ferryman at the old landing — Odile. Smuggler from way back, half-lame, mean as a boot. She owes nothing to anybody, which makes her expensive, but she'll run the river for pay. The book travels with her tonight, before the moon. You travel Thursday dawn. The guild tribunal sits in the city at the century's turn; the book arrives ahead of you and waits under seal with the guild archivist until you come to speak for it."

Every word true. Every word arranged like furniture in a room whose door Ilse had quietly locked behind her. Because the truth she was not saying was this: Odile would not carry the book. Odile would laugh in Ilse's face, and had, in fact, been dead for two years according to the parish roll, which Ilse had checked, because Ilse checked everything, and the woman living at the ferry hut was somebody else entirely wearing Odile's name the way Dominic wore her father's — and that woman would demand payment no one could make, or refuse, or betray, and the river route was a coin flip with the book as the stake.

No. That was the lie Ilse had told herself first, on the first night, and it had lasted an hour. The real arrangement, settled at three in the morning with the candle drowned and her face pressed against the cold glass of the window, was simpler and worse: Ilse herself would carry the book down the river route, tonight, after Dominic left believing otherwise — no.

Stop.

Ilse made herself look at the map instead of at Dominic, because maps didn't have eyes.

The actual plan, the one written nowhere: both of them, the water, together, tomorrow night, and hang the ankle and hang the odds — was impossible, and she knew it, and had known it since she'd measured the shallows herself with a sounding pole in the dark like a madwoman, and felt the current try to take the pole out of her hands. One strong swimmer's chance in three. Two people, none.

So the routes stayed split on paper. And the paper had to go to Dominic now, and Dominic had to choose a line to believe in, and whichever line she chose, Ilse had already decided — this was the part she could not examine too closely, the part she handled only sideways, the way you handle a hot pan — Ilse had already decided that whatever Dominic chose, Ilse would be standing at the other end of it. If Dominic took the road, Ilse took the river with the book and met her at Sainte-Colombe or didn't meet her at all. If Dominic refused and demanded the river — and she would, Ilse knew she would, that was the flaw in the whole beautiful scheme, Dominic was going to see through this, Dominic saw through everything —

But that was later. That was the next hour's problem. Right now there was only the map, and the candle, and the question.

"Choose," Ilse said quietly. "Not for me. For the forty-one. Whichever way keeps most of them breathing."

And there it was — the flattery, the one-degree lie — because *for the forty-one* was the lever that moved Dominic Yarrow more surely than any rope ever tied, and Ilse pulled it knowing it was a lever, knowing Vogel would have called it what it was, knowing that love expressed as terrible honesty is still, some nights, just dishonesty with good posture.

Dominic was quiet a long time. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, a hide creaked on its frame like a ship's rigging, and neither of them jumped anymore, which Ilse supposed meant the tannery had become home, or close enough.

"You've already chosen for yourself," Dominic said at last. Not accusingly. Worse — gently. "I know that look. It's my look. It's the look I wear when I've written an entry in the ledger and I'm waiting to see if anyone asks what it cost."

Ilse said nothing. Cartographers record.

"Thursday dawn," Dominic said finally, and put her finger on the western line, the road, the safe one, and Ilse's heart did something complicated and painful, relief and grief in one motion, because it had worked, God forgive her, it had worked. "I take the road. The book goes with the ferryman. And you—" Dominic looked up, and her eyes in the candlelight were the exact brown of iron-gall ink left to oxidize, and she smiled the small crooked smile she wore when she had decided something and intended to survive it. "You'll be where?"

"I have duties," Ilse said. "Maps don't draw themselves."

"Mm," said Dominic again, and reached across the map, and took her hand, smudging ink across Ilse's knuckles, marking her, and said, "One degree, Ilse. Whatever you're not telling me — I hope you've allowed for it."

They blew out the candle together, four hands on one small flame, and in the dark Ilse lay awake listening to Dominic's breathing slow toward sleep, and counted the hours until moonset, and told herself the harbor was that way.

Outside, far off toward the north gate, a dog began to bark, and then another, and then the whole cold town was talking in its sleep.
