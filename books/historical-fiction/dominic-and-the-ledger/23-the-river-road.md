## 23. The River Road

The map Ilse had drawn was a beautiful lie, in the way that all maps are lies and the best ones are useful ones. Two routes, inked in her precise cartographer's hand: the eastern lane marked with a small, careful *X* at every checkpoint, and the river road — if it could be called a road, being mostly water, ice, and prayer — marked with nothing at all.

"Nothing means no soldiers," she had said, when she'd pressed the paper into Dominic's hands two nights ago in the tannery loft, her fingers cold as a compass needle. "Soldiers don't stand in rivers in December."

"No," Dominic had agreed. "Neither does anything else."

She thought of that exchange now, standing on the frozen bank a half mile downstream from the north gate, with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest and the Velle running black and broken under its scum of ice. The river was not deep here — that was the whole point, that was why Ilse had chosen it. Chest-deep at the worst of it, she'd said. Waist-deep for most. The shallows braided and shifted every season, which is why no ferry crossed here and no bridge spanned it and no soldier bothered to guard it, because only a madwoman or a smuggler would try.

Dominic counted herself, that evening, as both.

* * *

She had left the tannery an hour before dusk, when the light went the color of weak tea and the garrison's evening drum sent its flat rattle across the rooftops. Marchand had stood in the loft's doorway below, arms folded, watching Dominic check and recheck the oilcloth wrapping.

"You'll freeze," Marchand said. It was not sympathy. It was inventory. A frozen courier could not carry a book.

"I'll move fast."

"You'll be wet."

"I'll still move fast."

Marchand's mouth did something complicated, somewhere between a sneeze and a blessing. "My husband tried to cross the Velle in November, the year of the bad ice. Took the fever. Died stupid." She thrust a bundle at Dominic: bread, hard cheese, a flask that sloshed with something that smelled less like medicine than like courage. "If you drown, I want the bread back."

It was, Dominic understood, the closest thing to love that Marchand permitted herself before sundown. She took the bundle and said thank you, and Marchand said don't thank me, hate me properly when this is done, we'll have earned it between us — and then turned away before anything softer could be forced out of either of them.

Now, on the bank, Dominic ate half the bread and stowed the rest inside her coat where her body heat would keep it from turning to flint. She left her boots on. Ilse had argued for removing them — *wet boots are worse than bare feet, you'll lose the toes* — but Dominic had overruled her on the grounds that she would sooner lose the toes than walk the far bank barefoot over frozen shale, and besides, she had reasoned with the grim logic of someone who has spent nine days being hunted by her own description, the boots were part of the disguise. The Yarrow boy wore boots. Everyone in Vellamont knew it. The Yarrow boy had worn the same boots for eleven years, resoled twice, and if some sharp-eyed sergeant ever saw those boots on a woman's feet he would deserve his commission.

Not that there were sergeants on the bank. That was the point.

She eased down the mud — mud with frost in it, mud that crackled like thin biscuits — and put one foot into the river.

The cold was not pain. Pain would have been honest. It was a white absence, a subtraction, as though her leg had been quietly removed from the ledger of her body without her consent. She gasped once, bit down on the sound, and kept walking.

*Chest-deep at the worst of it.* Ilse's map showed the channel bending left around a gravel bar, then right, then splitting into three threads that rejoined downstream of the gate. The town's walls ended at the waterline; the gate's lanterns threw long smears of orange across the ice skin near the banks, and Dominic could hear — absurdly, horribly domestic — the sound of the guard changing, boots stamping, a man laughing about his supper. The revolution, she reflected, wading up to her knees and trying not to think about her knees, apparently ran on sausage.

Halfway across, the ice got serious.

It came in plates near the gravel bar, sheets as thick as her thumb, and where the current slid beneath them they ground together with a sound like teeth. She had planned to break through with her elbows, quietly. What she had not planned was the plate that tilted underfoot as she stepped onto it, pitching her sideways into three inches of water above four inches of ice, so that she landed full-length with a crack that echoed off the far bank like a musket salute.

Everything stopped. The laughter at the gate stopped. Somewhere upstream, a dog began to bark.

Dominic lay on her stomach on the ice plate, soaked to the collarbone, the ledger digging into her ribs, and performed the fastest arithmetic of her life. If she moved, she made noise. If she stayed, the cold began its real work — the work that had killed Marchand's husband — and in this cold the work went quickly. Behind her, upstream, the dog was still barking, and now there was a second dog, and a man's voice shouting something that contained the word *river* and the word *fool*, though whether the fool was the dog, the man, or some imagined smuggler, she couldn't tell.

She moved.

She moved the way her father had taught her to move type — one piece at a time, each placement exact, no wasted motion, because wasted motion in a composing stick meant spilled letters and spilled letters meant starting over. Elbow. Knee. Elbow. The plates took her weight or didn't; where they didn't she went through to the thigh and hauled out again, and the river peeled another degree of heat from her with every immersion. Twice she heard the gravel crunch under boots on the near bank and froze flat among the ice shards, gray coat on gray water, thinking with unnatural clarity: *I am thirty yards from the gate that wants my head, lying in a frozen river, and what worries me most is that the cheese is getting crushed.*

Her father would have laughed at that. Her father had laughed at everything that couldn't be set in type, and at several things that could.

She reached the far bank at full dark, dragged herself up the shale on hands that no longer reported to her, and lay there steaming faintly in the starlight while her heart banged like a loose chase in the press. Behind her, across the black ribbon of the river, a torch bobbed along the near bank, paused opposite her, and moved on. Dogs whined. Men argued. The river, which forgave everyone because it forgot everyone, carried the argument downstream.

She had done it. She was past the gate. Forty-one names were past the gate.

Then she stood up, and her left ankle informed her, via a white bolt of pure editorial comment, exactly what it thought of the ice plates.

* * *

She walked anyway. There is a stage beyond stubbornness that is not courage but simple animal continuation, and Dominic found it somewhere in the first mile of the north road, hopping when she thought no one could see and limping with tremendous dignity when she feared they might. The ankle swelled inside the boot until the boot became a splint, which was the single practical mercy of the night, and she was grimly grateful to her father for having bought such good leather.

The plan — Ilse's plan, the safe-for-the-book route — said: *north road two miles, then west into the osier beds, then the ferryman's hut at the old crossing. Odile. Say nothing, show nothing, ask for nothing. Odile will know.*

*I don't trust Odile,* Ilse had added, in the margin, in pencil. *But Odile doesn't trust anybody either, and that's almost the same thing.*

Dominic reached the osier beds after midnight. The willow shoots stood in dense gray thickets, and pushing through them on a bad ankle in the dark was like being personally thrashed by the landscape. Twice she fell. The second time she stayed down a moment longer than necessary, cheek against frozen leaf mold, and let herself consider, seriously, the luxury of simply stopping. The ledger was heavy. Not physically — physically it weighed perhaps four pounds, paper and board and thread — but four pounds multiplied by forty-one names multiplied by nine days of fear came to something her shoulders understood better than her scales would have.

Her father's voice, memory-voice, dry as a well-set page: *A line of type doesn't care how tired you are, Dom. It either stands or it spills. Choose.*

"Standing," she told the osiers, and got up.

The hut appeared all at once, the way buildings do in the dark — one moment thicket, next moment a low black shape with a roofline, close enough to touch. It sat on stilts above the old landing, where the ferry had run before the new bridge made it redundant and the war made the bridge worth guarding. A single window glowed dull orange. Smoke, thin and honest, rose from a stove pipe into the stars.

Dominic stood in the willows and rehearsed. She was a clerk. No — a peddler. No — peddlers had carts. She was a courier with a bad leg and no explanation anyone would believe, and Odile, according to Ilse, believed nobody, which meant explanations were beside the point. She knocked the way one knocks on the door of someone who has been expecting trouble all their life: firmly, apologetically, once.

Footsteps. The bar scraped. The door opened the width of a hand, and an eye in a face like a walnut regarded her.

"Well," said the eye.

"I need—" Dominic began, and discovered that her voice, which had held steady through rivers and checkpoints and the entire catalog of her fears, chose this moment to shake. "I need to get to Corvel, and I can't walk another mile, and I was told—" *Say nothing, show nothing, ask for nothing.* Too late for all three. "I was told you were the person who helps people who weren't here."

There was a silence in which the stove ticked and the river muttered under the landing and Dominic's ankle throbbed in time with both.

"That," said Odile, opening the door, "is the most honest thing anyone's said on this landing in thirty years. Come in before the dogs do."

* * *

Inside, the hut was warm in the specific way of rooms inhabited by one person who has arranged every object within arm's reach: kettle, cup, coil of rope, a musket above the door that looked ornamental and wasn't. Odile — a woman somewhere past sixty, built like the landing pilings she'd spent her life among — looked Dominic up and down with the unhurried thoroughness of someone appraising salvage.

"Soaked. Limping. Alone. Northbound." Odile poured something from a brown bottle into two cups without asking anyone's opinion. "Sit before you fall. Boots off."

"The boot's holding the ankle—"

"Boots. Off." Odile crouched, cut the laces with a knife that appeared from nowhere, and drew the boot off with a care that contradicted entirely the ferocity of the order. The ankle beneath had swollen to the size of a pear and turned a color Dominic associated with storm skies. Odile prodded it once; Dominic saw actual stars. "Not broke. Sprained proper. You'll not walk on it tomorrow, and tomorrow you'll tell me why I shouldn't sell you."

The words landed in the warm room like a dropped awl.

"Sell me?"

"This is a ferry crossing, girl. Everything that comes down the north road comes past my window eventually. Soldiers today, smugglers tomorrow, and now a soaked courier carrying—" the old eyes flicked, brief and accurate, to the oilcloth bundle that Dominic had never once set down, not in the river, not in the osiers, not here, "—something square and dear to her chest, who crossed the shallows rather than use the gate. I've poached enough years to know what that costs. And I know what the garrison pays for information these days. It pays well. My roof leaks."

Dominic's hands closed on the bundle. It was not a decision; it was a reflex older than thought, the same motion that had pulled the ledger from the press hollow on surrender day, the same animal that had walked her up out of the river. "Then you know what's in it isn't mine to give."

"I know it's a book." Odile handed her a cup. The liquor in it tasted like a barn fire and worked like a promise. "I also know books are heavier than they look. So." She settled onto her stool, folded her arms, and fixed Dominic with the gaze of a woman who had haggled with death itself and gotten change back. "Tell me why I shouldn't sell you. And mind — I've heard every reason there is. Money. Mercy. Threats. Try to be original."

Dominic looked at her — at the leak-stained ceiling, the mended nets, the knuckles like river stones — and felt exhaustion reach down through her body and pull up something that had been waiting all night for its turn. Not wit, exactly. Not quite honesty. Something between.

"Because in 1784," Dominic said slowly, "a woman named Odile Ferrez paid a fine she owed to the harbor court — paid it twice, actually, because the first payment went into a customs man's pocket instead of the town's — and the second payment was recorded in a print shop ledger by a boy who kept his father's accounts. And when that ledger is read — and it will be read, Madame, I have given my blood to see that it will be read — your name is in it. Paid in full. Witnessed. Which makes you the only soul on this stretch of river whose debts exist anywhere but in other people's memories."

The stove ticked. The river muttered. Odile's face did not change, and then changed completely, the way ice shifts on a thawing pond — one moment solid, the next a map of cracks with water moving underneath.

"You've read my name," she said.

"I keep the ledger."

"You're the Yarrow boy."

Dominic said, very carefully, "I'm the Yarrow child," and watched the word land — *child*, neither son nor daughter, the truth stripped to its load-bearing beam — and watched Odile, veteran of thirty years of smuggling, decide what to do with it.

The old woman stood. Went to the window. Stood there a long while, looking out at the black river, and Dominic sat with her ruined ankle and her cup and the ledger against her heart and understood that everything now depended on the contents of another person's memory of 1784 — on whether a fine paid twice, recorded honestly in a dead man's shop, meant anything at all to anyone still living.

Odile turned around.

"My roof," she announced, "does leak. But it leaks over my bed, and I sleep in the boat shed anyway since my hip went." She jabbed a finger at Dominic. "Tomorrow, when you can't walk, I'm taking you downriver in the boat as far as the mill race, past the picket that watches the ford — because the picket watches the ford, girl, nobody watches the water, nobody ever has. And it'll cost you."

Dominic's stomach dropped. She thought of Marchand's bread. Of favors with teeth. "Cost me what?"

"A page," said Odile. "When your book is read in whatever grand room they mean to read it in — and I believe you, it will be read — I want my entry copied out fair, signed by whoever keeps the record, and sent to me here. Not the debt. The debt's paid. The *proof* of it. Thirty years I've been a woman of bad reputation on a good river. I should like, before I die, to be a woman of documented character."

Dominic blinked. Then, despite everything — the ankle, the river, the dogs somewhere behind her still working the scent — she laughed. It came out cracked and steam-breathed and helpless, and Odile glowered at it, and that made it worse.

"You want a receipt."

"I want a *certified* receipt."

"Madame Ferrez, you are the most respectable criminal I have ever met."

"And you," Odile said, fetching blankets and a splint and, unasked, a second measure of the barn-fire liquor, "are the strangest thing the river's brought me since the flood of '88. Sleep. We go at first light, before the pickets eat — hungry soldiers watch the road, fed ones watch their plates." At the curtain she paused, and her voice dropped its bark for just a moment, showing the pilings underneath. "You crossed the shallows in December with that thing strapped to your chest. Whatever's in it — whoever's in it — they'd best be worth it."

"They are," Dominic said. It was not complicated to answer. It was the only answer she had, and it had carried her through ice and willows and the whole long dark.

She slept as the animals sleep, in snatches, one hand on the oilcloth, while outside the river ran on toward Corvel, patient, forgetting everyone, forgiving no one — and behind her, miles south in Vellamont, she would learn later, a lamp was burning late in the garrison, and Commandant Ravel was looking at a requisition order that had been written in a hand he knew better than his own.
