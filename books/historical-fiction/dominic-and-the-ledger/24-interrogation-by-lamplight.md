## 24. Interrogation by Lamplight

They came for her at the mapping table, which Ilse supposed was fitting. She had always imagined arrest as something that happened in doorways and dark alleys, sudden and rough, a hand on the collar. Instead two gendarmes simply appeared at the tent flap while she was shading the contour lines above the tannery, and the older one said, "The commandant would like a word, Citizen Brandt," in the tone of a man inviting her to dinner, and the younger one collected her compasses from the table with surprising delicacy, wrapping them in oilcloth as though they were wounded birds.

"They'll be returned," the young one said. "He said to tell you. Your instruments will be returned."

*Your instruments.* Not *your effects*, which could be confiscated, not *your tools*, which could be broken. He had chosen the word carefully, and Ilse understood that she was meant to notice. That was the first thing to understand about Commandant Ravel, she had learned in four months of occupation: he did nothing carelessly, including his gentleness.

She walked between them through the evening streets of Vellamont without being held by either arm, which somehow made it worse. The town smelled of woodsmoke and the river's iron breath. At the bakery on the corner, Marchand's window glowed yellow, and Ilse saw the old woman's silhouette pause inside it, watching the little procession pass. Ilse kept her eyes forward and her hands loose at her sides and thought, absurdly, of her brother Emil teaching her to whistle through a blade of grass when she was seven, because thinking of anything true and warm seemed like the only way to keep her face from doing something treacherous.

The garrison had been a cloth merchant's warehouse before the war found Vellamont, and it still smelled faintly of dye. They took her down a corridor to a small room with one table, two chairs, and a lamp with a brass shade that threw its light in a neat circle, like a stage awaiting an actor.

Ravel sat at the table already. Of course he did. He was a compact man somewhere past fifty, with the gray, unhurried face of a clerk who had seen every kind of ledger and trusted none of them. Before him lay a single sheet of paper, face down.

"Sit down, Brandt." He indicated the second chair with two fingers. "Thank you for coming."

"I wasn't aware I'd be given a choice."

"You weren't." He said it mildly, as if confirming the weather. "But courtesy costs nothing, and I find people sit differently when they've been thanked. More honestly. Would you like tea? There is tea. It's terrible, but there is some."

"I'd like my compasses back."

"After." He poured the tea himself, into two tin cups, and pushed one across the table. Steam rose between them. The lamplight caught the gray at his temples. "You're not drinking."

"I'm told prisoners shouldn't accept gifts from their captors. It creates obligation."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but a recognition of one. "You've been briefed on interrogation. Good. That saves me some time." He turned his cup slowly, not drinking either. "Then you know how these conversations usually go. Shouting. Threats. A man slamming his fist on the table until the ink bottle jumps. I've watched it done. Do you know what it produces?"

"Lies," Ilse said. "Fast ones."

"Precisely. Fast lies. And then I spend three weeks discovering they are lies, and the prisoner has spent three weeks hardening, and in the end we are both tired and nobody has told anybody anything useful." He leaned back. "I don't have three weeks. So we will try it the other way. Slowly. Kindly, if you can stand it. You may leave whenever you tell me the truth, and not before. Tea?"

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. Then she picked up the cup and drank, because refusing kindness was also a kind of performance, and she was suddenly too tired to perform. It was terrible tea. He hadn't lied about that.

* * *

He began, as she might have guessed he would, with maps.

"Your work is good," he said. "I'll say that plainly, since I intend to ask you to believe other things I say plainly, and it helps if the first things are true. The survey of the north approach — the way you handled the marsh ground, the hatching for the wet season levels. That's Paris Academy work. Better. Where did you train?"

"Saxony," she said. "Before the annexation."

"Ah." He nodded slowly, and let the word *annexation* sit on the table between them like a coin neither would pick up. "A border woman. That explains the German in your marginal notes. My aide thinks they're errors. I told him they were habits." He paused. "You have family across the border, then."

And there it was. Not slammed onto the table. Set down gently, the way you set down a key you know fits the lock.

"My mother," Ilse said. "In Lindau."

"And your father?"

"Dead."

"And your brother?"

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere above them a soldier's boots crossed the floor, unhurried, going nowhere in particular.

"Emil," Ilse said, because lying about a name Ravel clearly already knew would cost her more than it bought. "In Lindau. A cooper."

"A cooper." Ravel opened a drawer — unhurried, no theater in it — and drew out a folder, and did not open it, only rested his hand flat upon it. "Emil Brandt. Yes. You see how slow and kindly this goes? I know your brother's trade. I know that he was arrested last spring under the old warrant — sedition, they called it, though from what I read of the file it was mostly a loud mouth in a tavern. I know that he was released." He tilted his head. "Released remarkably quickly. Released, in fact, in a manner that has been queried twice by the tribunal clerks in the provincial office, because releases like that require money, and coopers are not famous for having it."

"The ledger," Ilse said quietly.

"The ledger," Ravel agreed. "Or rather, a page of it. A payment recorded — discreetly recorded, in a very fine hand — against the name E. Brandt. Pardon purchased, debt forgiven, entry marked closed." He folded his hands. "Your brother walks free because a typeface cutter's daughter once wrote his name in a book with forty-one others. And now I am asked to burn that book, and instead it has vanished, and someone with access to my requisition forms, my seals, my garrison schedule, and my signature — someone who could reproduce my signature well enough to fool a sergeant of eleven years' service — walked into a sealed print shop three nights ago and removed printing equipment that, upon inspection, turns out never to have contained any equipment worth removing."

He turned the paper over at last. It was the requisition order. Her own forgery looked back up at her, drawn in Ravel's careful block capitals where hers imitated his flowing script, annotated around the margins in his small red notes: *the loop on the R — compare the map legend, north approach, the R in Roche.* He had caught her by her letterforms. She had signed a lie with the same hand that drew rivers, and rivers do not change their handwriting.

"Do you know," Ravel said, almost conversationally, "in thirty years of service I have executed four men for forgery and pardoned two, and the difference between them was never the quality of the work. The quality was excellent in all six cases. The difference was always whether they gave me a reason to look for mercy." He slid the paper aside. "I am looking now, Brandt. Help me find it."

* * *

She had prepared, in the sleepless nights since the unsealing, a set of answers. She had rehearsed them the way she rehearsed terrain: here a firm line, here a deflection, here a marsh to lose pursuit in. She had answers about the requisition being authorized verbally by an officer whose name she couldn't recall; answers about misfiled paperwork; answers that scattered blame like seed corn.

Sitting in the lamplight with terrible tea cooling in her hands, she discovered she didn't want to use any of them. That was his method, she understood — not to break her but to make breaking unnecessary, to make truth feel like rest. And the frightening thing, the thing she would examine later on the long road north, was how much she wanted to rest.

"Why does it matter?" she asked. "If you were going to burn it anyway. You ordered it burned. I've read the early correspondence. You wanted it gone — you called it, what was it—"

"'A book of old grievances that will do nothing but raise ghosts we cannot feed,'" Ravel quoted himself without embarrassment. "Yes. I did. And I was right." For the first time something like weariness came through the clerk's calm, a hairline crack. "Do you know what happens to men like me in revolutions, Brandt? We are useful in the taking of towns and expendable afterward, and the ledger — that stupid, beautiful ledger — contains the debts of half the officials above me. Debts forgiven. Favors recorded. Names written down by an honest hand, which is the most dangerous kind of evidence in the world, because it cannot be dismissed as malice. I wanted it burned because burning it was mercy — for the town, for the names inside it, and, I will admit, for myself." He spread his hands. "But mercy is a luxury that requires permission. And this week the permission ran out. The tribunal in the capital wants traitors delivered before the new year, quotas being what they are, and so the book must be found, and the finding of it will be blamed on somebody, and I would very much prefer that somebody not be the woman sitting in front of me drinking my terrible tea."

"You could report it missing. Claim the shop fire took it."

"The shop fire," Ravel said, "which I did not order, and which happened after the seal was cut, and which the arsonists will eventually mention was meant to destroy evidence they believed still inside." He watched her take that in. "Yes. You see how the circle closes. Whoever holds that book now is the only person alive who can prove the fire was reprisal rather than accident — and the only person alive who knows forty-one names that powerful men paid to have forgotten."

"And Emil."

"And Emil." He opened the folder at last, and turned it toward her, and she made herself look. Her brother's name. His arrest record, his release, the queries, the annotations — and beneath it all, in fresh ink, a draft order: *detain and hold pending review of prior release.* Undated. Waiting for a date.

"I can lose this," Ravel said. "Papers go astray in wartime. Clerks make errors. Emil Brandt of Lindau can grow old pulling hoops onto barrels and die in his bed at seventy, and no one will ever trouble him again. That is what I can offer. In exchange—" he tapped the requisition, gently, once, "—the ledger comes back to this table. Unharmed. Every page. And you tell me it was recovered, and I choose to believe you, and the story ends with everyone alive."

The lamp hissed. Ilse found that her hands were perfectly steady on the tin cup, which surprised her; she had expected them to shake. Perhaps steadiness, like everything else, was a habit of training. Ten thousand hours of drawing lines that must not waver.

"And Dominic Yarrow?" she asked.

Ravel's eyebrows rose a fraction — at the name itself, or at her saying it aloud. "Would be questioned. Naturally. As the shop's owner and the ledger's keeper."

"Naturally," Ilse repeated. The word tasted like the tea. "Questioned kindly, I suppose."

"As kindly as the situation permits," Ravel said, and she believed him, and believing him was the worst part, because it would have been so much easier to refuse a cruel man. Cruelty gives you a wall to push against. He had built her a door instead, warm-lit, with her brother standing on the far side of it, and all he asked was that she walk through and close it behind her on a girl who had once, in a cramped print shop, written *E. Brandt — closed* in a hand so careful it had saved a life.

She thought of Dominic's hands. Ink-stained at the cuticle always, no matter how she scrubbed. The particular way she held the ledger when she thought no one was looking — not like property. Like a child.

"May I ask you something, Commandant?"

"You may ask."

"If I give you the book — if I walk out of this room tonight and Emil keeps his freedom and I keep my commission — what happens to the forty-one?"

Ravel was quiet for a moment. To his credit, he did not pretend not to understand the question.

"Some will be fine," he said. "The ones whose debts were small and whose patrons still breathe. Some will be arrested, questioned, perhaps released, perhaps not. Two or three—" he paused, weighing honesty against comfort, and chose honesty, because he had promised her plain speech and plain speech was his weapon, "—two or three will hang. Not because I will it, Brandt. Because the book exists, and the book is true, and truth in this century has become a thing that kills people regardless of which side carries it." He folded his hands again. "That is why I wanted to burn it. I have been wrong about many things in this campaign, but I was not wrong about that."

"No," Ilse said softly. "You weren't wrong about that. You were only wrong about who would get to decide."

She stood. Her legs held. Outside, snow had begun — she could hear it in the quality of the silence beyond the door, the muffled softness, the town holding its breath under white.

"The tea really was terrible," she said. "Commandant."

"It gets worse the longer it steeps. Like everything else in this army." He remained seated, patient as furniture, waiting for the answer he was owed. "Brandt. The location."

Ilse stood in the lamplight with her brother's life in one hand and a girl named after a saint's candle in the other, and understood at last that there was no answer that was not a betrayal — there was only the choice of whom to betray, and how much, and whether she could live in the house she built with whatever she traded away.

"The ledger is at the ferry crossing," she said. "Odile's hut. Under the floor planks, beneath the net chest. She doesn't know it's there."

It was a lie, and it was measured to the inch: plausible enough to spend soldiers a day searching, distant enough from the tannery loft to buy Dominic a night's running, and built, like all her best cartography, out of real features arranged along a false road. Ravel studied her face for a long, cold moment — studying her letterforms, her contours, the marshy ground of her — and Ilse smiled at him with the pleasant blankness of a woman reading elevation marks.

"That's very helpful," Ravel said at last, in a voice that suggested he knew precisely how helpful it was, and rang the small bell on the table for the guards. "Take Citizen Brandt to the east room. See that she's comfortable. She'll want her rest."

At the door Ilse paused.

"Commandant. When your men come back from the ferry crossing empty-handed — what happens then?"

Ravel poured himself the last of the cold tea and drank it without flinching.

"Then," he said, "we stop asking kindly."
