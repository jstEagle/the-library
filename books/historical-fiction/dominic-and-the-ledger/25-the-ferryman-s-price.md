## 25. The Ferryman's Price

Odile Fenn had been ferrying people, goods, and things that were neither across the Vell for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three rules worth dying by.

One: never take a passenger who won't meet your eye. Two: never carry anything that smells of government. Three: when the ice comes, retire to the hut, drink the good brandy, and let the river sort out its own affairs.

She was deep into rule three — two fingers into the good brandy, boots on the stove, cat on her lap pretending he wasn't a cat but some manner of small, entitled landlord — when someone knocked at her door at dusk in the third week of December, which no one with any sense did.

"Go away," she said comfortably. "River's closed. Ice. Everything's closed. The whole world is closed."

Another knock. Softer this time. And under it, the sound of a person trying very hard not to make any sound at all, which is the loudest kind of person there is.

Odile sighed, moved the cat — who protested with the full theatrical grievance of his species — and opened the door on a lantern-lit sliver of a figure wrapped in a fishmonger's coat three sizes too big, hood up, one ankle visibly wrong, leaning against her doorframe like the last plank of a wrecked ship.

"Well," Odile said. "You're not the tax man."

"No."

"Pity. He knocks softer." She looked the figure up and down, taking in the frozen mud on the hem, the raw hands, the way the stranger stood angled as though protecting something under the coat — a stance Odile knew the way a shepherd knows a wolf's walk. "And you're not a fishmonger, either, because no fishmonger alive holds their left elbow like they're carrying a christening. Come in before you die on my step. Corpses are terrible for business, and worse for the cat's nerves."

* * *

The stranger came in, sat where told, and refused brandy, which Odile considered a moral failing but not a disqualifying one.

Up close, under the lamplight, the fishmonger's coat resolved into what it was: disguise, and recent, and hastily chosen. Young face. Sharp in the cheekbones. Eyes that kept going to the door even while the mouth said thank you. A limping girl dressed as a boy dressed as nothing much at all — Odile had carried forty years of those across her river and buried half of them in shallow water, God forgive her, and she knew the architecture of a secret when it sat dripping on her bench.

"I need to cross," the stranger said. "Tonight. Below the weir, where the shallows freeze hard enough to walk — I'm told you know the safe line better than anyone."

"Told by whom?"

"A friend."

"That's what everyone says, and it's true about a third of the time." Odile poured herself more brandy, unhurried. "Below the weir there's no crossing. That's why the army put dogs at the gate instead of below the weir. If there were a crossing below the weir, believe me, someone would have taxed it by now."

"There's a line," the stranger said quietly. "You know it because you cut it yourself, eleven years ago, for the salt runners. Two stakes driven into the far bank, a white stone between them, and the ice holds everywhere except a span the width of a cart where the current turns under. You call it the Seam. Anyone who steps outside the stakes goes into the water and the water doesn't give them back until spring."

The hut went very quiet. The cat, sensing leverage shifting, relocated to the warm spot the stranger had left on the bench, traitorously.

"Now that," Odile said slowly, "is a story I've told exactly four people in my life, and two of them are dead and one of them is me." She set down her cup. "So either you're a ghost, or you've been reading somebody's letters, or — " and here her eyes narrowed, old and bright and quick as a pike's — "you're carrying something worth knowing that story for. Something small enough to hold. Something made of paper."

The stranger did not answer. But her left elbow tightened against her side, the way a mother's arm tightens around a sleeping child when a dog barks, and Odile had her answer, and it sat in her chest with a weight entirely out of proportion to paper.

"The ledger," Odile said. Not a question.

The girl's face did something complicated — fear, mostly, and then a strange, exhausted dignity rising up through it like a swimmer surfacing. "If it is," she said, "then you also know it isn't mine to trade. And if you mean to sell me, sell me. I can't run on this ankle anyway. But the book crosses tonight, and it crosses north."

"Hm," said Odile, who meant by this to convey an entire philosophy of life, none of which could be spoken aloud before breakfast.

Because here was the thing, the thing the girl couldn't know, the thing that had been sitting in Odile Fenn's chest for nine years like a swallowed hook: Odile knew the ledger. Not its contents — nobody knew all of its contents but the Yarrow ghost and whatever dead man had taught him the trade — but she knew its power the way a hanged man knows rope. There was a page in that book with her name on it. There had been, once. A debt, twenty-two years old by then, from her running days: a cargo of untaxed tobacco, a customs officer bought, a name signed in a fair, careful hand — the father's hand, the cutter's hand — as witness and guarantor both. When the amnesty came through Vellamont the way amnesties do, sloppy and selective and purchased, her name had vanished from the record. Expunged. Paid off quietly, through channels, by a hand she never learned and never stopped being grateful to and never once mentioned at church.

But a woman like Odile didn't need to read a page to know it existed. She'd spent nine years waiting for that page to surface somewhere, in some soldier's satchel, some informer's purse, and every year it hadn't come had been a year of borrowed air.

And now here sat the book itself, under a fishmonger's coat on her own bench, breathing.

* * *

"One page," Odile said.

The girl looked up.

"My page. My entry. Whatever's written about Odile Fenn, twenty-two years back — tobacco, a customs man named Perrin, God rest his greedy soul. I want it torn out. Fair payment. Cheaper than gold, and worth more." She leaned forward, and let thirty-one years of the river come into her voice. "Think on it a moment, boy, before you refuse. That book gets read in front of tribunals, doesn't it? Read aloud, name by name, in some marble hall where nobody's ever smelled honest work? My name comes out of your mouth in that room and I'm finished — old, yes, but finished, dragged out of my hut in winter, questioned about things I did when your father was buying his first inkpot. One page. It's already paid. It was forgiven once. I only want the forgiveness to be real, in ink, where no clerk can dig it up again."

She watched the girl's face while she said it, and what she saw there surprised her. Not calculation. Not the flicker of a trader weighing goods. Something more like grief — the specific, tired grief of a person who has been asked to break one more small holy thing and has finally run out of hands to hold the cracks together.

"No," Dominic Yarrow said.

Just that. No speech. Odile almost admired it.

"You don't even hesitate."

"I hesitated all winter," the girl said. Her voice was rough and low and terribly tired. "I hesitated over every page. I've had nine days to learn what happens when you start deciding which names deserve to survive. Commandant Ravel taught me — he wanted to burn the whole book and called it mercy, and he was nearly right, and that frightened me worse than the fire did. Either all of it stands or none of it does. Including the ugly pages." She looked up, and her eyes were clear, and young, and absolutely immovable, and Odile felt something in her old chest turn over. "Including yours. Especially yours. Your page is evidence, mistress, same as every other page — evidence that debts were forgiven, that men could be ransomed out of ruin, that this town ran on mercy before the army ever arrived to teach us justice. Tear yours out and you tear the thread, and then the whole cloth unravels, and then I've carried a bundle of paper north for nothing."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind walked across the ice with soft, enormous feet.

"That's a pretty argument," Odile said at last.

"It's not an argument. It's just what's true." The girl shifted, and winced at her ankle, and for a moment she looked about fourteen years old and ready to cry from exhaustion, and then she didn't, and that was the remarkable thing about her, Odile thought. The not-crying. The way she folded the weakness up small and put it away like sewing.

"And if I take the page anyway?" Odile asked, gently, testing the last lock on the door. "You can't stop me. You can't walk. You're in my hut, on my river, in my country now, such as it is."

"Then you'd be a thief," Dominic Yarrow said simply. "And I'd still have read every name aloud before you got it open, because I've memorized the book. All forty-one. All the debts and all the aliases and all the payments, including yours, including Perrin's widow in Chastel who got an anonymous parcel of money every Michaelmas for six years and never knew from whom — that was you, wasn't it. Guilt money. Or love. Probably both; they usually travel together."

Odile stared at her.

Then she began to laugh — a low, rusty, wheezing laugh, the laugh of a hinge unused to joy, laughing until she had to wipe her eyes on the cat, who forgave nothing.

"Oh, you wretched creature," she said. "Oh, you magnificent, impossible little clerk. Yes. Yes, it was me. Sixteen years of Michaelmas parcels and she never once wrote to ask, because asking would have made it real, and we were both happier with the mystery. And now you sit there and tell me you know it, because of course you know it, you know everything, you and that book between you know every sin this town ever committed and half its kindnesses too, and the kindnesses are only ever recorded by accident, aren't they. In margins. In the spaces between the debts."

"Your name is in a margin too," the girl said softly. "Under another name. A delivery, marked 'paid in bread.' Widow Marchand's entry. Someone covered a debt for her once, in kind, without signing."

Odile stopped laughing.

"Bread," she repeated.

"Two sacks a month, for a year, delivered to the miller's back door, marked as spoilage. Ninety-four. It's in the margin in a different hand. Smaller. Older."

The stove ticked. Odile looked at her own hands, broad and scarred and rope-calloused, hands that had pulled a hundred liars across a hundred dark waters, and found that they were not entirely steady.

"Yannick Marchand owed the shop money," she said, mostly to herself. "Before he drowned. His widow swore she'd sooner starve. Stubborn as a mule with a grudge, that woman. Proud as Lucifer's bootmaker." She shook her head slowly. "Bread. For a year. And she never knew."

"Someone knew her," Dominic said. "That's all any of it ever was, in the end. Somebody knowing somebody, and writing it down so the knowledge wouldn't drown."

Odile sat with that for a long moment. Then she stood, joints cracking like rigging in a squall, and went to the corner where the gear hung, and took down the long pole with the iron shoe, and the oilskin bundle, and a coil of the good rope — not the display rope, the real rope — and began packing.

"What are you doing?" the girl asked.

"Rule three says I retire when the ice comes," Odile said. "Rules are for people with nothing better to do. Get your boot off, let me look at that ankle, and eat something. You'll want the strength. The Seam's a quarter mile of walking on a knife's whisper in the dark, and if you go outside my stakes you'll spend spring being politely returned to your family by the river, and from what I hear your family is a press and a pile of lead, and neither of them floats."

"I can't pay you," Dominic said. "I have nothing. That was going to be — I came hoping you'd help for old debts' sake, and instead I offered you the one thing I have, and you refused it, and now I owe a stranger a quarter mile of my life and can't—"

"You paid," Odile said shortly.

"I what?"

"You told me about the bread." She cinched the bundle tight, not looking at her. "Sixteen years, girl, I've carried that alone, and never told a living soul, because telling would have made it real. Now one other person in the world knows, and she's sitting on my bench with a busted ankle eating my cheese, and somehow that makes it — " she hunted for the word, and gave up, and settled for — "lighter. That's payment enough. That's more than enough. That's the only currency I've ever truly traded in, and I've rarely seen it so debased as this year, so don't you dare offer me coin."

* * *

They went out at moonrise, because the moon would set by the hour the Seam needed crossing and darkness was the second-best ally a smuggler ever had, after greed.

Odile led. Behind her came the girl, hopping on the good foot for the first stretch to the stakes, then walking — stubbornly, jaw set, sweat freezing in her eyebrows — along the line Odile called out stake by stake, white stone to white stone, the pole's iron shoe tapping the ice ahead of every step like a blind man's cane, reading the river's mood in the sound. Solid sang low. Hollow answered high. Over the invisible turning of the Seam the pole spoke with a thin, wet, dangerous note, like a finger on a wineglass, and Odile heard the girl's breath catch the first time, and heard her master it, and thought: Yarrow's blood. Ink outlives men, the old cutter used to say across his counter, and here was the proof of it, walking on water in the shape of his daughter, whom the whole town believed was his son, carrying a book of forty-one lives toward a tribunal that might hang her for the courtesy.

Halfway across, Odile stopped.

"Here," she said. "This is where I always stop. Superstition. Twenty-two years ago, coming back over this exact span with the tobacco, I promised the river a tithe if it held, and it held, and I never paid, because I'm a thief and always was. So now I pay late, with interest." She turned, and held out her hand. In it lay a single sheet of paper, folded small — not torn from the ledger, which she wouldn't have taken now for all the tobacco in the province, but copied in her own blunt hand that morning's evening, from the girl's dictation, every word of her own old entry transcribed fair and true. "My page. A copy, not the original. The original stays in the book where it belongs, standing witness with the rest. This one's for me — to burn if I like, or keep, or leave for whoever comes after. It's mine either way. That's all I ever wanted. Not to erase it. Just to hold it in my own two hands for once, instead of having it held over me."

Dominic took the folded paper as though it were a communion wafer.

"Mistress Fenn," she began, "I—"

"Don't. Sentiment freezes on the ice. Walk."

They walked. The stakes rose out of the dark on the far bank like the masts of a sunk fleet, and beyond them the north road ran pale through the trees toward the city, toward the guild, toward whatever tribunals waited to hear a tired girl read names aloud until a courtroom remembered it had a soul. Odile planted the pole, hauled the girl the last yard up the bank, and stood a moment with her boots on solid ground and her heart going like a drum.

"North road, half a mile, then west through the birches before the gate picket sees you," she said. "There's a hay barn with a loose board, sleep till the milk cart passes, tell Marta Fenn her aunt sent you, and don't — " she paused, and grinned into the dark, all her teeth and none of her shame — "don't tell her about the tobacco."

Dominic laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, exhausted, genuine sound, the first one Odile suspected she'd managed in nine days — and gripped her hand hard, and turned north.

Odile watched until the dark took her. Then she stood alone on the bank with a copy of her own sins warming in her pocket, feeling lighter than any honest woman had a right to, and turned back toward the ice.

Behind her, far downstream toward Vellamont, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then, faint but unmistakable, carried flat and cold across the frozen river, the sound she had been listening for all night without admitting it: voices, lantern-light blooming along the bank road like a string of feverish stars, and among them — she'd have known it anywhere, that particular unhurried cadence, the voice of a man who reads maps the way priests read scripture — a voice giving orders in French with a northern accent, patient as falling snow.

They had found the tannery empty.

Odile Fenn spat thoughtfully into the dark, shouldered her pole, and started home the long way, whistling — because the girl had a half-mile's grace and a barn and a milk cart, and because thirty-one years on a river had taught her exactly how fast a lie travels when the army tells it, and how slow the truth walks when it's limping.

"Hold the ice for her," she told the river. "Just till morning. You and me, we're square after that."

The river said nothing, in the way of rivers. But the Seam held, and the night held, and somewhere north, a light in a hay barn burned small and stubborn until dawn.
