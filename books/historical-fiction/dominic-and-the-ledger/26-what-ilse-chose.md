## 26. What Ilse Chose

They took her boots first.

Ilse noted this the way she noted everything — as a matter of measurement. The sergeant who carried them out of the cell was a large man with small feet, and he walked oddly in them, like a heron in another heron's nest. She almost told him so. She had discovered, somewhere between the arrest and this cold stone room, that humor was the last supply she carried and she meant to ration it carefully.

The lamplight interrogation room was not what novels would have made of it. There were no irons on the walls, no brazier of coals. There was a table, two chairs, a jug of watered wine, and Commandant Ravel, who looked — there was no kinder way to put it — tired. He sat with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, a man interrupted at his own supper.

"Sit, please," he said. "I've been standing all day at maps that lie to me. I'd like to sit while someone lies to me face to face instead."

"That's very nearly honest," Ilse said, sitting.

"I find honesty is cheaper than theater. It produces the same results with less candle." He poured wine into both cups, pushed one across. "You understand I have no authority to torture you. You understand also that if I did, it would be pointless, because you are the sort of person who has already decided what she will say, and decided it before we arrested you. Am I wrong?"

"You are not wrong." She drank. The wine was thin and slightly vinegary and it was the best thing she had tasted in weeks, because it was freely given by an enemy, which is a category of gift that sharpens the palate. "Though I'll point out I haven't lied to anyone yet. Not officially. The record should show that."

"The record," Ravel said, "shows a requisition order for the removal of printing equipment to the garrison, signed with a stamp that does not exist, dated on a night when every soldier at the north gate was watching a dog fight over a stolen sausage." He turned a paper toward her. Her own hand looked back at her from it, neat and sure. "I know this hand. I have spent three months knowing this hand. You draw your sevens with a crossbar like a Swiss, your capitals lean forward as though they're in a hurry to be done with themselves, and you make your ampersands—" he paused, and something almost fond crossed his face— "you make your ampersands like nobody else in this army. It took me four days to find the forgery because for four days I believed it, and believing it made me doubt everyone else."

"I'm sorry about the ampersand," Ilse said gravely. "It's a habit from my brother. He taught me letters."

"Your brother." Ravel said the word without weight, without cruelty, and somehow that was worse. "Emil Brandt. Yes. I've been reading about your brother."

* * *

Here is what Ilse had learned about fear, having mapped it the way she mapped rivers: it has tributaries, but only one main channel. People feared pain, feared death, feared shame — but all of those fed into the single wide water of losing something. The trick of surviving any interrogation was therefore cartographic. Know where your river runs. Refuse to be redrawn.

Her river ran through a name written in a careful hand on a page of a stolen ledger: *Emil Brandt, pardoned, 1789, payment recorded.*

She had read it once, by lantern light in Dominic Yarrow's shop, standing in the hidden cavity behind the proofing press with dust on her cuffs and her heart going like a drum corps. Her brother's pardon — the pardon their family had called luck, called mercy, called the wisdom of the tribunal — had been bought. Her mother's savings, passed through hands she could not see, entered in a ledger by a typeface cutter's daughter who recorded bribes the way other clerks recorded rainfall: accurately, without comment, because accuracy was its own kind of mercy.

Dominic had never once held it over her. That was the thing. In three months of complicity — of forged orders and shared bread and one terrible, tender hour in the tannery loft where the ledger lay open between them like a confession neither had planned — Dominic had never used Emil's name as a lever. She had left it lying where Ilse could find it, and let Ilse decide what it weighed.

Ravel, now, was weighing it aloud.

"He's in the garrison hospital at Marlanne," the commandant said. "Fever, I'm told. The winter fevers are bad this year. Of course, men recover from fevers. Men also fail to recover from them, particularly when questions are being asked about their sisters, and paperwork is such a slow business. So much can happen while paperwork is slow." He folded his hands. "Tell me where the ledger is, and I will write to Marlanne tonight. My letter travels faster than fever."

Ilse set down her cup with great care, because her hand wanted to shake and she refused to let it.

"Commandant," she said, "do you know what I do all day? I measure things. Coastlines, roads, the width of the Velle at low water. And I have learned one law above all others: a map that tells you what you want to hear kills you. Men march into marshes on hopeful maps. They starve following rivers that aren't there." She met his eyes. "If I gave you the ledger now, quickly, gratefully — you should distrust it more, not less. A confession extracted by threat is a map drawn under duress. It shows the marsh where the draughtsman wishes the marsh."

"And yet," Ravel said, "I notice you haven't denied there is a marsh."

* * *

They gave her a cell with a window, which she understood to be a courtesy, or a message, or both. Through it she could see the tannery district, chimneys smoking against the gray sky, and beyond it the north gate, and beyond that the frozen ribbon of the river where — she was quite sure — nothing was happening at all, because Dominic Yarrow was two days gone by then and getting further with each hour.

Two days. That was the arithmetic Ilse had performed in the first hour of her arrest, before the wine, before Ravel's tired eyes. Dominic had left on the river road with the book sewn into her coat the way a mother carries an infant: against the body, where warmth is. Odile the ferryman would help — Odile owed half the town and paid her debts strangely, but she paid them. If Dominic reached the guild tribunal in the city, the book became evidence, public, argued over, printed. A book in a courtroom cannot simply vanish. That was its whole defense.

But a book needs time. Tribunals deliberate; commandants do not. Every day Ravel lacked the ledger was a day the pages traveled further from his reach. So the question was not *where is it* — she would never tell him that truth anyway — but *how much time can I buy, and at what price.*

She sat on the cell's narrow bunk and priced it honestly, the way she'd been taught to price everything.

The currency Ravel offered was Emil. Fever at Marlanne, and a letter that could travel faster than fever — toward help or harm, depending on how you read a man like Ravel. And here was the trap's true teeth: she could not verify anything. The fever might be real or invented; the letter might heal or hang her brother. Threats built on unverifiable facts were the strongest kind, because doubt did the work that cruelty otherwise had to.

And yet.

And yet she had watched Dominic Yarrow carry forty-one names through checkpoints and frozen shallows for people who mostly didn't know her name, for a widow who had threatened her, for a town that called her *Master Yarrow's boy* without ever wondering why the master never appeared. Dominic had taught her — not in words, Dominic was terrible at words, she expressed love chiefly by correcting other people's kerning — that a secret kept for someone is worth more than a secret kept from someone. That the ledger was not a list of sins but a promise: *your name is safe here. I will carry it myself.*

Ilse lay back on the bunk and laughed, briefly, alone, at the ceiling.

Because the answer had been sitting in her own saddlebag the whole time. Maps. She was going to buy time with a map.

* * *

Ravel returned after dark with the lamp and the wine and, this time, a second paper — blank, and a pen.

"No tricks," he said. "No riddles. Write the location. I will send riders within the hour. If they find what you describe, your brother gets my letter tonight and my protection thereafter, as long as it costs me nothing, which I will be honest with you, is not forever."

"Commandant," Ilse said, "may I ask you a professional question first?"

He looked wary, but he was a man who liked being competent in public. "Ask."

"When your engineers rebuilt the Marlanne road last autumn, whose survey did they use?"

"...Mine. Ours. Yours, partly." His eyes narrowed.

"Yes. Which means you know that surveys disagree with each other. That two competent mapmakers looking at the same country will produce different countries." She took up the pen. "So before I write, you must accept that I am giving you my country, not yours. If your riders go expecting *your* Vellamont, they will be angry and lost. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Ravel said slowly, and she saw that he believed he was winning, which was the most important condition of all.

She wrote for some time. She was thorough; thoroughness was credibility. She described a hiding place with loving technical detail — the old salt warehouse below the cooperage, its double wall, the cavity accessible only from the river side at low water, the specific rotting pallet that concealed it. Every word of it true. That was the beauty, the wicked, glorious beauty: it was entirely true. There *was* a cavity in the salt warehouse wall. She had found it in October while correcting the army's survey of the riverfront, and had noted it in her field book with the small private satisfaction of a cartographer who finds a door in a wall everyone else has drawn as solid.

It was empty, of course. Had been empty since the first week of December. But finding it empty would take Ravel's riders a full day — the warehouse, the cooperage, the low-water timing, the breaking of the pallet — and then the *searching* of it, because Ravel was Ravel, and a man who believes a map does not stop at the marked X; he searches the whole marsh around it. Two days, if the weather held. Three, if it snowed, and snow was coming; she had smelled it in her cell all morning, that iron-and-paper smell of a sky deciding.

She signed the page. Ravel read it twice, and she watched him do the mathematics too — watched him arrive at the same conclusion she had planted: *this is precise enough to be true and detailed enough to be trusted.* He was not a stupid man. Later — perhaps in a day, perhaps in three — he would stand in a rotten-smelling cavity full of river muck and understand exactly what she had done, and how, and why. She would rather be elsewhere for that moment. Preferably in another century.

"The letter to Marlanne," she said, as he tucked the page away.

"Goes tonight. You have my word."

"Then I have something of yours, and you have something of mine, and we're even until one of us breaks." She stood. "Commandant. One more thing, off the record."

He paused at the door. "Off the record."

"Dominic Yarrow is not the traitor you're hunting. She's the archivist of everyone else's. There's a difference, and someday, when this is all paper in an archive, I think you'll be able to read it."

Ravel considered her for a long moment. "You know what's strange, Brandt? I believe you. And it changes nothing, because I am not hunting her. I'm hunted *by* her — or by what she carries. There's a difference too." He almost smiled. "For whatever it's worth, I hope your brother's fever breaks."

"For whatever it's worth," she said, "I hope your riders dress warmly."

* * *

The penal column formed up on the parade ground three mornings later, in snow.

Ilse had expected chains and had received instead a rope, knotted at intervals, which was somehow more insulting and more practical. She stood in line with smugglers, deserters, a man who had sold flour to both armies simultaneously and with evident pride, and a boy of perhaps sixteen who kept crying quietly and apologizing to no one in particular. The column would walk north to the labor camps at Torvane — road cutting, she'd heard, and drainage, work that killed you with weather rather than cruelty, which in this army counted as moderation.

She inventoried herself, as she always did before a journey. Boots: confiscated, replaced with army issue a size large, which she had partially corrected with spare cord. Coat: adequate. Papers: transferred, stamped, describing her as *Brandt, I., cartographer's assistant, remanded.* Sins: one forgery, one false map, one act of love disguised as logistics. Assets: none declared.

She was surprised to find she wasn't afraid, and took a moment to examine this absence the way she'd examine a gap in a coastline, suspicious of anything too smooth. Part of it was temperament — she had never been able to sustain terror longer than a good map could distract her, and she was already composing in her head a proper survey of the north road, because no one had ever surveyed it well and the drainage problem at kilometer twelve was obvious even from the parade ground. Part of it was calculation: penal columns discharged. Men served their terms and came back thinner and stranger but *back*. Two years, perhaps three. Emil's fever would break or it wouldn't regardless of where she stood. Ravel had his letter and his word, and whatever else he was, he was a man who kept accounts.

And part of it — the largest, least mappable part — was this: somewhere south of here, past the checkpoints, along a frozen river road, a woman with ink-stained fingers and a stubborn chin was carrying forty-one lives toward daylight, and every step of her journey had been purchased with these days Ilse was now spending. The debt ledger of her own heart showed a balance squarely in Dominic's favor, and Ilse Brandt paid her debts. It was, when you thought about it, the family trade. Her mother had paid for Emil's pardon. Ilse paid in miles.

The sergeant called them to order. The rope tightened. Somewhere a drum began, indifferent as weather.

As the column lurched forward through the gate, Ilse looked back once — just once, she allowed herself, a cartographer's privilege, fixing the bearing. The town lay white-roofed under the coming century. The print shop's chimney stood cold among the others. Snow was starting again, fat slow flakes, the kind that filled tracks within the hour, and she thought: *two days' head start, plus this snow. Go slowly, my dear. Take the long route. Argue with every ferryman.*

Then the road bent north, and Vellamont closed behind her like a page, and she began, professionally, to count the kilometers.

She was still counting at dusk, four leagues out, feet numb, humor ration down to its final store — when a rider overtook the column from the south, moving fast, and pulled up alongside the escort officer with a dispatch case and snow to his eyebrows. Ilse caught only fragments on the wind, the escort's voice rising:

"—warehouse empty, sir, cavity's flooded, nothing but river muck and one dead cat—"

And then, sharper, angrier, carrying down the whole length of the rope-tied column so that even the flour smuggler stopped smiling:

"—and the Yarrow woman's been seen. North road. Two days ahead of us."
