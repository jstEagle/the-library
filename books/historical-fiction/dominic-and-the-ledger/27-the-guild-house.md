## 27. The Guild House

The city of Corvenne announced itself to Dominic the way a wealthy aunt announces herself at a funeral: loudly, and with an air of having expected better accommodations.

She had come down from the hills on the last leg of a journey that had taken eleven days instead of the five Ilse's map had promised — the ankle she'd twisted at the gate saw to that — and every mile of road had traded river mud for cart ruts, cart ruts for cobbles, cobbles for the wide, swept boulevards of a capital that had never once heard of Vellamont. She was stiff, filthy, smelling faintly but unmistakably of tannery, and carrying the most dangerous book in three provinces wrapped in sailcloth against her chest, where a woman might carry a child or a stolen ham.

The guild house stood on Inkworkers' Square, which turned out to be neither square nor particularly given to inkwork anymore. A coffeehouse had taken the ground floor corner; a milliner's occupied the opposite side; and between them rose the guild house itself, four stories of pale stone with tall windows and a carved lintel on which someone had chiseled *THE WORD ENDURES* and then, some decades later, someone else had added underneath, in smaller letters, *PROVIDED THE RATES ARE AGREED IN ADVANCE*.

Dominic stood in front of it for a long moment, letting the cold seep through her boots and doing sums she didn't like. It was the twenty-ninth of December. The tribunal would convene — if it convened at all — before the new year, because after the new year the army's writ would renew itself with fresh signatures and fresh enthusiasm, and whatever the guild meant to do, it would have to do it while the old year's paperwork still bound its hands.

She had walked nine winter days past three checkpoints for this. She adjusted the bundle under her coat, straightened her borrowed clerk's coat as best a borrowed coat can be straightened, and climbed the steps.

* * *

The door was answered by a boy of perhaps fourteen with ink on his chin, his collar, and — impressively — one ear.

"Guild business," Dominic said, in the voice her father had taught her for speaking to strangers behind counters. It was a good voice. It said, *I am mildly inconvenienced and you are the cause.* "Master Yarrow, of Vellamont. I'm expected."

This was a lie so brazen that it worked immediately, which told her something about how the guild house was run.

The boy — he gave his name as Perrin, mostly with his eyebrows — led her up two flights of stairs lined with framed specimens of typefaces, each labeled in a careful hand. Dominic read them as she passed the way other people read scripture: *Garamond, double-pica. Caslon, great primer. Bodoni — do not use for broadsheets, the thin strokes fill in on cheap paper.* Her fingers itched. It had been weeks since she'd touched type, and her hands missed it the way a sailor misses the roll of a deck.

At the third landing Perrin knocked on a tall door, announced her with the confidence of a boy who had not thought to ask any questions, and ushered her into a room where six old men sat around a table that could have seated thirty, arranged at intervals like chess pieces abandoned mid-game.

"Gentlemen," Dominic said, and bowed exactly as far as a master printer bows and no farther. "Thank you for seeing me."

There followed the particular silence of six men trying to remember whether they had agreed to see anyone.

The eldest of them — a vast, slow-moving man with white side-whiskers and the complexion of a man who had spent forty years indoors arguing — leaned forward eventually and said, "And you are?"

"Yarrow," Dominic said. "Of the Yarrow press, Vellamont. Cutter and keeper of the shop." All true. Every word of it true, if you knew which words to leave standing next to each other. "I've come regarding the ledger of my father's house, which I believe your clerk received notice of—"

"We received," said another elder, a thin one with spectacles like the bottom of two bottles, "*a letter*. Some weeks ago. Unsigned."

"I signed it."

"You signed it 'Yarrow,'" said the thin man, with the air of a prosecutor producing the murder weapon. "Which is precisely the difficulty, young man, because I have it on record that Master Aldous Yarrow died — let me see—" he produced a sheet of paper and peered at it upside down first, then righted it — "four years ago."

The room waited. Dominic felt the familiar lurch, the trapdoor sensation of a name being pulled out from under her feet, and steadied herself on the oldest trick she knew: she did not deny anything.

"My father is dead," she said. "His ledger is not. And it is his ledger I've carried two hundred miles through a occupied town to place before this tribunal, which I understood existed for precisely that purpose. If I've been misinformed about the guild's purpose, gentlemen, say so now and I'll trouble you no further — though I'll want my deposit back for the room I took on the Rue Saint-Aubin on the strength of your summons."

Another silence. The fat elder — his name, she would learn within the hour, was Master Quenlin — hid what might have been a smile in his whiskers.

"The summons," said a fourth voice, dry as paper dust, from the end of the table, "was sent by me." The speaker was small, neat, and ancient, with hands folded on the table like two sleeping birds. Dominic recognized him at once from engravings in her father's trade journals: Master Aurel Foss, compositor emeritus, once famous for setting twelve hundred ems an hour, now famous mostly for being alive when everyone who'd disagreed with him wasn't. "Because a tribunal has been called, and tribunals require evidence, and evidence requires someone mad enough to carry it here. Sit down, Yarrow. You look like a woman who has been walking."

Dominic sat.

* * *

They examined the ledger for two hours, and it was the strangest experience of her life to watch it happen.

She had expected reverence, or greed, or fear. What she got was craftsmanship. The thin elder — Master Delacroix — held pages up to the window light to examine the watermark. Another tested the iron-gall ink with a damp fingertip and sniffed it. Foss himself went through the book column by column with a loupe, checking entries against dates against the slant of the hand, and twice asked her questions of a precision that made her want to laugh with relief: *Was this entry made the same day as this one? The nib differs — do you account for it?*

"A repaired quill," Dominic said. "My father cut his own nibs and mended them badly. After his death I used bought pens. Everything after the ninth of March, year before last, is mine."

"And everything before is his."

"Every line."

Foss closed the ledger at last and laid both hands flat upon it, and looked down the table at his colleagues, and Dominic watched six faces perform six varieties of the same calculation.

"It is authentic," Foss said. "That isn't in question. What's in question, gentlemen, is whether we wish to be the ones who say so *aloud*."

* * *

They adjourned — "to deliberate," Quenlin said grandly, though everyone present understood it to mean *to eat lunch and argue privately* — and Dominic was deposited in a waiting room furnished with three chairs, a dying fire, and a wall of framed guild citations dating back sixty years. She read all of them twice. Then she opened the window shutters and looked down at Inkworkers' Square, where a lamplighter was working his way along the row, and tried very hard not to think about the fact that the tribunal had not actually promised her anything.

A boy brought her bread and cold sausage and no information whatsoever, despite her friendliest interrogation. It was Perrin, the ink-eared apprentice, and he turned out to have a natural gift for the guild arts in one respect only, which was keeping his mouth shut while looking pleased about something.

"They're frightened," he offered at last, when she'd given up asking directly and simply stared at him.

"Frightened of the army?"

"Petrified." He said it with relish, chewing. "Old Man Hurel — Master Hurel, he's got the bindery contract for the ministry — says if the guild rules against the commandant, they'll revoke the contracts, and half the houses here live quarter to quarter on ministry work. Bibles. Forms. Tax schedules." He wrinkled his nose at the word, as befitted a printer's apprentice. "Master Foss doesn't care. Master Foss says he's been thrown out of better rooms than this one."

"Has he?"

"Oh, twice. Once by the king's men and once by the last revolution's men. He keeps the notices framed." Perrin pointed at the wall, and Dominic found, low in the corner, two faded documents of expulsion, hung side by side like medals.

She was still smiling at them when the door opened and Foss came in alone, small and neat and older-looking than he had at the table, and shut the door behind him with great care, as if shutting out eavesdroppers of unusual determination.

"Well," Dominic said. "Tell me."

"Sit," said Foss, and sat himself, and warmed his bird-bone hands at the dying fire. "You should understand, Yarrow, what we are. We are not a court. We are a trade association with delusions of dignity. Our charter gives us the right to arbitrate disputes over printing property, records, and authorship — a power nobody has cared about for a generation, until suddenly everybody cares about it, at which point it becomes the most dangerous sentence in our charter." He poked the fire. "Four of my colleagues wish to rule that the ledger is a commercial document properly surrendered to requisition, and wash their hands of it in time for supper. One wishes to rule for you but abstain from signing. Quenlin wishes to rule for you and sign, but Quenlin is fat and brave in roughly equal measure and they cancel out."

"And you?"

Foss looked at her over the fire for a long moment. His eyes were quick, unold eyes, set in a ruin of a face.

"I cut type for the crown for thirty years," he said. "Set proclamations praising men I'd seen hang people. When the revolution came, I set proclamations hanging the men I'd praised. Do you know what I learned?" He didn't wait. "Ink doesn't take sides, girl. Ink takes orders. Unless somebody picks the thing up and makes it testify. That's what you did — you picked up a book of debts and made it a witness. There are perhaps five people in this country with the nerve, and here you are dripping tannery smell on our carpet."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a yes with conditions, and the conditions are the interesting part." He held up one finger. "First: we can admit the ledger as evidence of record accuracy. We cannot pardon anyone. Pardon is not ours to give — we are printers, not God, whatever the ministry thinks of us. What we can do is certify that the records are genuine and untampered, which means the amnesty provisions recorded inside them become legally awkward for anyone who wishes to pretend otherwise. It is a narrow door. But it is a door." A second finger. "Second: the ruling must be read publicly, entered into the guild register, and copies struck and distributed before anyone can suppress it. That takes compositors. It takes a press. It takes time."

"How much time?"

"The tribunal convenes tomorrow night. New Year's Eve." Foss smiled thinly. "The army's writ renews at midnight, and everyone knows it, including the commandant, whoever he is, who will by then have decided whether we are worth the trouble of a garrison detachment. So the real question—" he leaned forward, and the firelight made his face briefly young — "is not whether we rule for you. I will make sure of that, even if I must vote twice, and at my age no one checks. The question is whether you can stand in front of six frightened tradesmen and thirty years of ministry contracts and make them believe, for one hour, that a book matters more than their livelihoods. Because the vote, Yarrow, will follow whoever speaks last and best. That is the whole history of committees."

Dominic sat with that.

"I've read names aloud before," she said finally. "Under worse circumstances than a warm room."

"Somehow," said Foss, rising, "I don't doubt it. Get some sleep. Perrin will show you the compositor's dormitory — mind the third bunk, it sags, and its previous occupant was a poet."

At the door he paused, hand on the latch.

"One more thing. The letter you sent — unsigned, Delacroix says, though I see he can't read his own correspondence. You wrote that the ledger contains the names of forty-one persons whose legal standing depends on its authenticity." His voice had gone quiet and careful. "Are you one of the forty-one?"

The question landed in the middle of the room like a dropped composing stick. Dominic thought of her father's entry — the payment, the careful hand, the secret bought and kept — and of the answer she had prepared for tribunals, magistrates, sergeants, and priests, which was smooth and ready and false.

"No," she said. "I'm the one who carries it."

Foss studied her face for a moment longer than comfort allowed, and then, being a man who had survived two regimes by knowing which questions not to press, nodded slowly.

"Tomorrow, then," he said. "Sleep. You'll want your voice."

* * *

The dormitory was cold and smelled gloriously of lead shavings and oil, and Dominic lay in the second bunk — declining the third on principle — listening to the building settle and the city murmur beyond the glass. Somewhere out there was the north road, the river, Vellamont, the sealed black shell of her father's shop, and Ilse marching in a penal column toward a winter she might not — no. She stopped that thought the way you stop a leaking pen: cap it, shelve it, deal with it in daylight.

Instead she counted the forty-one names, as she did every night now, touching each one in her head like beads on a string. Marchand's forged name. Odile the ferryman's expunged debt. The cooper, the schoolmistress, the two brothers who'd fled the old warrants. Thirty-nine, forty, forty-one. She fell asleep somewhere in the thirties with the syllables still moving under her breath like type being set by a hand too tired to lift.

She woke before dawn to shouting in the square.

She was at the window with the shutter cracked before she was fully awake, heart going hard, one hand already finding the place beneath her coat where the sailcloth bundle rode — and there was nothing there. No soldiers. Only a milk cart, two arguing fishwives, and a squad of army runners moving up the square at a jog, posting papers to the doorways with the brisk cheerfulness of men delivering good news.

One of them slapped a sheet onto the guild house door itself, not ten feet below her window.

Dominic read it upside down in the grey light. It was written in the clean, official hand she had last seen on a proclamation nailed outside her own shop, and it said:

*By order of the Commandancy of the Eastern District: all records certified by the Printers' Guild tribunal sitting at Corvenne shall be subject to immediate military review. The tribunal is reminded that adjudication of matters touching state security exceeds its charter.*

And beneath it, the signature, and beneath the signature, the seal — and beneath the seal, in smaller type, the part that mattered, the part that made her go cold in a way the December morning couldn't claim credit for:

*The tribunal will convene as scheduled. Attendance of the document-bearer is required.*

They knew she was here. Of course they knew. Somewhere behind her, a requisition forgery had crossed Commandant Ravel's desk, and a pair of quick grey eyes had read a familiar hand in it, and watchers had been set, and the net had traveled up the road ahead of her and settled over the guild house like snowfall — quiet, patient, covering everything.

Down in the square, the runners jogged away. The fishwives went on arguing about eels. The lamplighter snuffed his last lamp of the night and yawned.

Dominic let the shutter close, and stood a moment in the dark with her hand flat against the ledger through the cloth, feeling its weight the way you feel a sleeping animal that trusts you.

Tomorrow night, then. Midnight. Six frightened tradesmen, one narrow door, and whatever she could build out of forty-one names and a voice gone rough from the road.

She began, quietly, to rehearse.
