## 28. Century's Eve

### Ravel

Commandant Étienne Ravel had spent thirty-one New Year's Eves in uniform, and he could not remember one of them that had ended the way the almanacs promised — with renewal, with hope, with the year lying ahead like fresh snow. Years did not arrive clean. They arrived the way requisitioned warehouses arrived: smelling of someone else's life, full of furniture he had to account for.

Tonight he sat in the garrison office with a decanter of wine he had not touched and a stack of paper he had touched too often. The charges needed drafting before midnight. That was the instruction from above, delivered in the particular language the Directory used when it wished to be obeyed without wishing to be implicated: *the town should see justice done as the century turns.* Justice done. As though justice were a performance scheduled between the fireworks and the feasting.

He dipped his pen and wrote the heading. *In the matter of the Yarrow ledger.*

The words sat there on the page, patient as flies.

It had been, he reflected, a reasonable plan once. Burn the book. He had meant it sincerely — there were men in Paris who would have laughed at him for the mercy of it, who would have wanted the ledger read aloud in the square with drums rolling between the names. Burning it was kindness. A fire erases; it does not accuse. He had imagined himself, in his more self-flattering hours, as a man sweeping broken glass off a road so children would not cut their feet.

Then the orders changed, because orders always change, and now the book was to be found, and the finding was to produce traitors, and the traitors were to be tried before spring, and none of it — none of it — was going to bring back the sergeant who'd died at the customhouse, or un-smuggle the wine, or make Vellamont anything other than what small river towns become when armies pass through them: a purse to be emptied and a list to be shortened.

And now there was the other matter. The requisition order. He had it in front of him, beneath the charges, and he looked at it again the way a man looks at a tooth that has begun to ache only when he bites down.

*Authorizing removal of printing equipment to the garrison.*

The hand was confident. The seal was good — very good, good enough that he'd had to hold it to the lamp and look for the seam. But the letterforms. He had spent eleven weeks reading Cartographer Brandt's maps, and a map is nothing but handwriting pretending to be geography. She crossed her sevens the way she drew her bridges: twice, decisively, as if daring the river to argue.

He had said nothing. He had posted two watchers on the print shop instead, then three when the shop turned out to be already empty of everything except a ruined press with its guts scooped out like a fruit.

So the charges. He wrote the first line of them and stopped.

Somewhere across the square, the clock began striking eleven. Ravel listened to it the way he had once listened, years ago, to a chaplain reading names of the dead — not hearing each one but counting them, feeling the weight accumulate.

There was a third document on his desk, and it was the smallest, and it troubled him most. A tribunal summons. The printers' guild, in the city, had convened an inquiry into the provenance and admissibility of certain commercial records, and they had done it under the antique guild charters which even the Directory had not yet dared to burn, and they had — this was the part he kept rereading — requested the presence of the commanding officer *as a party with relevant knowledge.*

They had asked him to testify. Him. About a book.

His aide, Devereau, put his head around the door at half past eleven, scarf up to his ears. "The men are asking about leave for the celebrations, sir. And there's a woman at the gate — a widow, Marchand, she calls herself — insisting she has information regarding—"

"Tomorrow," Ravel said.

"Sir, she says it concerns the ledger."

Ravel set down his pen. Outside, someone was already setting off firecrackers early, the little cowardly bangs of a town that wanted the year over with. He thought of the widow Marchand the way he thought of most citizens of Vellamont: as a person whose file he had read and whose life he had not. Forged papers, old regime, a debt forgiven by somebody's careful hand. Everybody in this town was owed something by somebody, and the book knew all of it, and that was precisely why it had to burn, and precisely why it wouldn't be allowed to.

"Show her in at eight o'clock tomorrow," he said. "If the world is still here."

Devereau hesitated. "And the charges, sir? They want them filed by midnight."

Ravel looked at the blank space where the first name ought to go. Forty-one names, give or take. He knew most of them by now. The baker. The ferryman. The type-cutter's child who had talked circles around a sergeant's French grammar and walked out of her own sealed life with a book under her coat.

"Midnight is the end of the year, Devereau," he said. "Not the end of the world. File them at one."

It was not mercy. He told himself it was procedure. At his age a man learns the difference is mostly a question of which lie sleeps better.

### Marchand

Widow Marguerite Marchand lit her candles at dusk, as she did every night of the year including nights when lighting a candle felt like an act of insolence, and tonight it felt exactly like that, and so she lit six instead of five.

The last day of the century. Her mother had been born in the last one; her husband had died in this one believing the next would be better, which was the sort of thing dying men say to avoid discussing their finances. Marguerite had outlived two governments, one husband, several reputations, and every scrap of patience she'd ever had for young people who thought history happened somewhere else.

She stood at her window with a candle in each hand and surveyed the street like a general. Snow coming down soft and businesslike. Lamplighters gone home. Down the lane, the print shop stood dark behind its army seals, the wax stamped with an eagle that had no business being on a printer's door.

She had been to the garrison tonight. That was the fact sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She had walked there in her best shawl with the information folded up inside it — *the tannery loft, the girl in the loft, the book* — rehearsing how she'd say it, how she'd demand terms, how a woman of property negotiates. She had gotten as far as the door. She had actually put her hand on it.

And then she had stood there remembering a page.

Not the page with her own forged name on it, though she remembered that one too, with the complicated gratitude of a woman who had never in her life been given anything free and still didn't know what to do about it. No — the page she remembered was smaller than that. An entry in that meticulous, upright hand: *M. Marchand, bread, on credit, winter of '89, paid in mending.* Somebody's boots. Somebody's hem. Little debts, entered and struck through with a single clean line, forty-one lives kept solvent in ink by a girl who was legally nobody at all.

Marguerite had taken her hand off the garrison door. She had told the sentry she'd come about a noise in her chimney. The sentry, being nineteen, believed her, possibly because he was afraid of her, which was the correct amount.

Now she set the candles on her table and allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to feel ashamed. It had been close. Closer than she would ever admit. Leverage was leverage, and she was seventy-one years old and had buried a husband on credit, and no one — no one — got to call her a saint.

But she hadn't done it. That was the entry in her own private ledger, and she meant to keep it.

She took down her good brandy, the bottle she saved for deaths and weddings, and poured two fingers, and drank to the century, which had been hard on everyone and worse on women, and to the next one, which promised to be interesting, God help them all.

Then, because she was practical before she was sentimental, she packed a basket. Bread — her own, the dark loaf, the kind that travels. Cheese. A flask of broth. If the girl was hiding in a tannery loft through a Vellamont January, she was eating tannery food, which was to say nothing, and hunger made people careless, and careless people got caught, and Marguerite Marchand had decided — sometime between the window and the second brandy — that this particular debt was going to stay paid.

At half past eleven she wrapped up, took the basket, and let herself out the back way, into the snow, moving through the lanes she had known since they were mud, invisible as only a stubborn old woman can be.

The basket had no note in it. Notes were evidence. But she tucked in the good knife too, just in case, because grace was grace and prudence was prudence, and Marguerite had never confused the two.

### Ilse

The penal column marched north on the last night of the century, because columns do not keep holidays.

Ilse Brandt had expected chains, and there were none — only the loose formation, the flanking escort, the mud frozen into ruts hard enough to turn an ankle. Someone had decided that cartographers were not dangerous in the way wolves were dangerous, more in the way unlocked doors were dangerous, and so she marched at the rear of forty-odd souls, carrying her kit like anyone else, her wrists free, her pride a small cold coal in her chest that she fed carefully and did not let flare.

Two days. That was what her false location had bought. Two days in which Ravel's men had turned over a mill upstream that contained, at last count, some flour, one angry miller, and no ledger whatsoever. Two days' head start for a girl with a twisted ankle and a book worth forty-one lives.

It was, Ilse judged, the best trade she had ever made, and she had made it without haggling, which for a cartographer was practically religious ecstasy.

The road ran along the canal, and the ice on the water made that sound ice makes — a low musical groaning, like the world clearing its throat before speaking. The column's breath rose in a single ragged cloud. Ahead of her, a man began, quietly, to sing something old, something about rivers, and nobody told him to stop, and by the second verse most of the column was humming it, badly, in four different keys.

Ilse hummed too, and let herself think, which was dangerous, and therefore necessary.

She thought about maps. All day she had been mapping in her head, involuntarily, the way she did — the road's bearing, the canal locks, the distance to the border, the places a small party might leave the column and not be missed until roll call. She was not going to escape. That was not the plan, and plans made of hope were how cartographers ended up in ditches. But knowing the ground was not the same as hoping, and she had never in her life been able to stop knowing the ground.

She thought about her brother, whose name sat in a book somewhere south of here, written in a hand like neat fencing, pardoned by a bribe she had never known about and vindicated now by a tribunal she would probably never see. She hoped the guild elders understood what they held. Elders rarely did. She hoped the girl did. The girl did. The girl corrected a sergeant's grammar under threat of search; she understood things.

And she thought — briefly, severely, the way you touch a hot pan on purpose to prove you've learned — about a print shop that smelled of lead and lamp oil, and a voice saying *whose names* like it was the only question in the world, and a kiss that had tasted of cold air and ink.

"Brandt." The escort sergeant had drifted back to her, a heavy, decent man named Courtois who had shared his tobacco with her twice and pretended it meant nothing. "You're smiling. Nobody smiles on this road. What do you know that we don't?"

"That the century ends tonight," Ilse said, "and the next one has better surveying in it. I've seen the preliminary figures."

Courtois snorted. Somewhere ahead the song reached its chorus and even the escort joined in, tunelessly, gloriously. The canal ice groaned and sang underneath them, and Ilse marched north into the new year with her hands free and her heart doing the one thing she had strictly forbidden it to do, which was hope.

### Dominic

Outside the guild hall, snow fell on Dominic Yarrow like a verdict postponed.

She had been standing in the little arcaded court for three hours now, in the lee of a pillar, with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest where it had lived so long it had worn a smooth place in her coat. Inside, behind tall lit windows, silhouettes moved — the guild elders, arriving, arguing, taking their seats at the long table where, at midnight, the inquiry would convene. She could hear the murmur of them through the glass, that particular sound of important men disagreeing in warm rooms.

A porter had brought her tea at nine o'clock, unprompted, and refused payment. A journeyman printer had come out at ten, looked her up and down, and said, "You're the Yarrow one, then," and gone back inside before she could answer, which she suspected meant the answer was already known.

Her ankle ached where the frozen shallows had punished it. The tannery smell had followed her for two days before the river finally argued it out of her hair. In her pocket was a letterform wrapped in a scrap of sailcloth — the lowercase *d*, salvaged from nothing yet, from a shop still standing as far as she knew, carried anyway, the way soldiers carry a saint's medal. Her father's cutting. The curve of it fit her thumb exactly, because her thumb had been trained on it before her memory started.

Snow gathered on her shoulders and she did not brush it off. Brushing it off admitted she was cold; admitting she was cold admitted she had been standing here long enough to get cold; and she preferred to maintain the fiction that she had simply arrived, briskly, moments ago, a person with business rather than a person with nowhere else to be.

The truth was she had nowhere else. The truth was that everything she owned was either nailed shut in a sealed shop, marching north in a penal column, or pressed against her sternum in oilcloth, and that of the three, only the oilcloth was doing any good.

Through the window she watched an elder stand and pace. Another shook his head slowly, the universal gesture of a man explaining why courage is imprudent. She had counted seven elders. Four looked sympathetic, two looked frightened, and one — the fat one with the chain of office, Master Quenlin, whom she'd placed on sight from her father's stories — looked like a man doing arithmetic with his reputation.

They were going to hesitate. Of course they were. Ruling for the ledger meant ruling against the army; ruling against the army meant the army might rule against the guild; and guilds survived centuries by the ancient science of ruling whichever way the room was already leaning. She had walked nine winter days on a bad ankle not because she'd expected bravery from committees but because the book had to be *here*, in the room, on the table, undeniable, when the moment came. Courage, her father used to say, is mostly logistics.

Still. Standing outside the lit glass while reasonable men weighed whether forty-one lives were admissible — that was its own species of trial, and no tribunal had thought to summon her to it early.

Across the square, the cathedral clock showed quarter to twelve. Bells would ring at midnight, and the century would die quietly in the snow, and the inquiry would convene, and she would walk in through those doors with the oilcloth bundle and whatever remained of her voice, and she would read the names. Not law. Not argument. Names. Her father had taught her that ink outlived men; tonight she intended to introduce the guild elders to forty-one men and women it had outlived already, and see whether their nerve held.

A carriage rattled into the square — army green, lamps swinging. Dominic's stomach dropped before her mind caught up: not soldiers, a single officer descending, older, gray at the temples, stamping snow off his boots with the weary economy of a man who has been cold for decades. Commandant Ravel. Summoned, as she was summoned. A party with relevant knowledge.

Their eyes met across the square, and held, for one long heartbeat, over the falling snow.

Then the cathedral bells began, and the century ended.
