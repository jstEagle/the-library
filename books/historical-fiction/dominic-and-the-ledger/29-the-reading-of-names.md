## 29. The Reading of Names

The tribunal hall had been a granary before it was anything important. You could still smell the rye in the rafters, a sweet dusty ghost of harvest that no amount of official bunting could drown. Dominic knew this because she had been standing outside its doors since nine o'clock in the evening, and by eleven she had catalogued every smell the building owned: rye, tallow, wet wool from the guards' cloaks, and underneath it all, faintly, ink — though that last one might have been her own cuffs.

Snow fell in the courtyard with the patience of something that intended to outlast everyone present. It gathered on her shoulders and she did not brush it off. It seemed important, somehow, to arrive at midnight carrying the weather on her, so that when they looked at her they would see what she had crossed to reach them.

The ledger hung against her chest inside her coat, bound there with the sailcloth strap she'd used at the bridge, at the customhouse, in the frozen shallows past the north gate. Nine days it had taken her to carry a book the length of a town. Her ankle still sang about it whenever she shifted her weight.

At half past eleven, a clerk with a candle-stub face opened the doors a hand's width and peered out.

"The guild elders will receive the evidence-bearer now," he said, in the tone of a man announcing the arrival of a shipment of tallow. "You will speak when spoken to. The tribunal sits until the matter is resolved."

"How long does a matter take to resolve?" Dominic asked.

"That depends," said the clerk, "entirely on how much trouble you are."

Inside, the granary had been dressed for authority: a long table on a low dais, five elders behind it, their chain-of-office links catching candlelight like fish scales. Four were guild men, old and upholstered. The fifth was a woman with spectacles and ink-stained fingers — Madam Corvez, whom Dominic recognized from the specimen books her father had kept, the master whose foundry stamps appeared in the colophon of half the almanacs in the province. Dominic's heart did something complicated at the sight of her, the way a child's heart does upon meeting a storybook giant who turns out to be short.

A soldier stood at each wall. At the room's far end, apart from everyone, sat a single officer in a gray coat, hat on his knee, watching with an expression Dominic couldn't read at that distance — though she would come to know it better before dawn.

"You are the person called Yarrow," said the eldest elder, whose name was Fontenay and whose voice sounded like a drawer of cutlery being opened carefully. "The print shop of Vellamont."

"I keep my father's shop," Dominic said. "Yes."

"Your father is Master Alcide Yarrow, cutter of type, member of this guild for thirty-one years."

"My father is dead," Dominic said. "Seven years."

"Then you hold his book without standing or license. You are not a guild printer. You are not, so far as our records show—" here Fontenay consulted a paper, and his cutlery-voice acquired a slight hesitation, "—anyone at all."

The word *anyone* landed in the room and sat there. Dominic felt the old reflex rise in her throat, the practiced baritone, the shrug that made her shoulders into a boy's shoulders. She let it pass. There was no more use for it tonight; whatever happened here, it would happen to her actual self.

"No," she agreed. "I'm not anyone in your records. But I'm the only person who can tell you what's in mine."

She unstrapped the ledger and set it on the table before the dais. The sound it made — a heavy book settling onto government wood — was the loudest thing that had happened in Vellamont all winter.

* * *

They examined it the way officials examine everything, which is to say badly. Fontenay leafed to random pages and read entries aloud in a puzzled monotone: sums owed, sums forgiven, dates. Another elder pronounced it a mere account book. A third worried aloud about precedent, jurisdiction, whether admitting an unsanctioned private record constituted an insult to the army's commission, whether it constituted an insult to *his* commission, which apparently was a different and more delicate thing.

Dominic stood and let them talk. She had expected this. In the tannery loft, reading the ledger whole for the first time, she had understood that the book's power was not legal at all. No court in the world had rules for it. If she argued law, she would lose in an hour.

Madam Corvez turned pages slowly, left to right, the way printers do, and once looked up at Dominic over her spectacles with an expression that said, as clearly as speech: *I know whose hands made these letters.* Dominic held her breath through an entire page-turn.

It was past one in the morning when Elder Brissac — upholstered, prosperous, owner of two presses and a vineyard — delivered the sentence she had been waiting for.

"It may all be perfectly accurate," he said. "That is precisely the difficulty. Accuracy is not admissibility. This book names men now sitting in this province's assembly. It names pardons bought and sold. If we certify it, we certify accusations against persons who are not present to answer, on evidence gathered by—" he gestured vaguely at her entire existence, "—an unlicensed person, during a military occupation, under circumstances I am told involved forgery, theft, and flight from soldiers of the revolutionary army. Elders, we would be ruling against the state."

"We would be ruling about a book," said Madam Corvez.

"A book is never only a book," said Brissac, "that's the trouble with them."

Dominic heard her own voice come out quieter than she'd planned, so she stopped planning and simply spoke.

"May I say what it is? Before you rule on it?"

Fontenay looked down the table. Nobody objected, which in committee is consent.

"It isn't evidence," Dominic said. "I know that's what you've been asked to decide — is it evidence, is it admissible, does it meet some standard. Sirs. Madam. I've lived inside this book since I was eleven years old. My father taught me to mend its spine before he taught me to cut a lowercase. And I can tell you that it will fail any test you apply to it, because it wasn't made to pass tests. It was made because people came to our shop at night and said, *write this down so it stays true*, and my father wrote it down, and then I did."

She put her hand flat on the ledger's cover. Under her palm she could feel the swell of the hollowed-press splinter that had scarred its back board, the whole journey written into the binding.

"You want me to defend it as law. I can't. I'll defend it as memory. That's all it is — forty-one people's memory, kept where nobody thought to burn it. And I'll tell you the difference between my book and your records." She nodded at the paper still lying in front of Fontenay. "Yours says I'm no one. Mine says who everyone is. One of those documents has kept a town alive through seven winters, and it wasn't yours."

Brissac's mouth opened, but Corvez got there first.

"Then read it," she said.

The room turned to her.

"Read it," she repeated, to Dominic. "Not the sums. Not the dates. The names. All of them, if you know them. We have sat in this hall for four hours discussing whether a list of human beings meets procedural standards, and I find I cannot recall the last time anyone in this province simply *said the names out loud.*"

* * *

There is a craft to setting type that no one who hasn't done it can quite believe: the way ten thousand small decisions of spacing become, in the end, one seamless sentence. Reading aloud, Dominic discovered, was the same labor in reverse. You began with one name and had to trust the rest would follow at the proper measure.

She stood beside the dais rather than before it — she wanted no misunderstanding about who was addressing whom. She opened the ledger to the first page, which was her father's hand, upright and patient, dated the year Dominic was born.

"Ambrose Fett," she read. "Cooper. Owed fourteen livres for broadsheet printing, 1784. Forgiven 1789, on condition he never again calls my father 'the heretic of Bridge Street' within earshot of my mother. Condition met."

Somewhere near the guards' door, someone snorted. It broke something open in the room — the first breath anyone had laughed in hours — and Dominic went on.

"Perrine Odile, ferrywoman. Debt of six livres, 1787. Settled in full, 1791, in smuggled tobacco of surprisingly good quality, entered here so that no future authority may claim otherwise."

She read them the way her father had taught her to proof a page: steadily, without hurry, correcting nothing, embellishing nothing. Some entries were dry as dust. Some were small comedies — the baker Luçon's alias recorded as "Widow Thibault's nephew from Lyon," which fooled absolutely no one including the widow Thibault, who was his aunt and proud of him anyway. Some were terrible. When she reached the entry recording a payment made to register a dead infant as having died baptized, after the priest had refused, the hall went so quiet she could hear the snow touching the windows.

She did not perform. That was the whole secret, she realized somewhere around the twentieth name. She had watched Ravel's officers command rooms with their voices, and Brissac fill silences with his, and none of it worked tonight. What worked was the flat, faithful delivery of fact — a cooper, a ferrywoman, a baker, a widow, a child — each name given exactly the weight it had on the page, no more. Ink outlives men, her father used to say, and she understood at last that he hadn't meant it as comfort. He'd meant it as instruction. *Say it true and let it outlive you too.*

An hour passed. Two. The candles were replaced twice. The soldier by the east wall stopped standing at attention somewhere around name twenty-six and simply listened, his chin up, like a man hearing a letter from home. Brissac, who had opened proceedings demanding procedure, sat with his vineyard-keeper's hands folded and did not interrupt once.

When she reached the name of Ilse Brandt's brother — *Emil Brandt, clerk, pardon obtained 1788 through payment recorded herein; the debt assigned to the undersigned, who accepts it gladly* — she heard, very distinctly, a chair shift at the far end of the room. The officer in gray. She didn't look up. Later she would learn why that chair moved, but the book came first, and the book always came first.

Forty-one names. It took three hours, and it took no time at all, both at once, the way the nine days from the shop to this hall had been both the longest and shortest of her life.

When she read the last one — Marchand's forged name, entered in her own careful hand, forgiven without condition because, as the ledger itself put it, *some debts are best kept as friendships* — she closed the cover and the sound of it echoed in the granary rafters like a door shutting gently.

Nobody spoke.

Then Elder Brissac blew his nose, loudly, into an enormous handkerchief, and said in a wrecked voice, "I withdraw my objection regarding insults to my commission."

Laughter moved through the hall — tired, cracked, real. Madam Corvez removed her spectacles and pressed her ink-stained fingers to her eyes for a moment. And Dominic understood that the room had changed while she read. It was not that the tribunal had been persuaded. It was that the tribunal had stopped being a tribunal. Five elders and a dozen soldiers and a hostile officer had spent three hours being, simply, a room full of people listening to the truth about their neighbors, and there is no procedure for undoing that.

Fontenay cleared his cutlery-drawer throat. "The hour is late," he said, "and the matter is—" he paused, glanced at the ledger, and seemed to revise several rulings at once, "—no longer merely a matter of evidence."

But the decision was not made that night, or not all of it. Because it was then that the doors opened, letting in a blade of cold and lamplight, and a young lieutenant crossed the hall with snow on his shoulders and a paper in his fist, and announced in a voice pitched carefully between apology and terror:

"Elders. Commandant Ravel's compliments. The garrison has received orders from above. If this tribunal certifies the document known as the Yarrow ledger, the hall is to be surrounded at dawn and the record seized by force."

He set the order on the dais and stood there, miserable, dripping.

Every eye went to the far corner — to the officer in the gray coat, who had listened to forty-one names without moving, and who now rose, unhurried, and put on his hat.

Commandant Ravel walked toward the dais through the silence, and Dominic, holding a book worth more than the building around it, waited to hear what a man who burns ledgers out of mercy does when mercy stops being allowed.
