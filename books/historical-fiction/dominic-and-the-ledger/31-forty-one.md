## 31. Forty-One

The guild hall smelled of cold stone and older ink, and Dominic had never loved a smell so much in her life.

She had been outside the doors for the better part of two hours while the elders deliberated — she could hear them through the oak, a low grumble like a river under ice — and when a clerk finally appeared and beckoned her in, her legs nearly declined the invitation altogether. She made them work anyway. A Yarrow did not limp unless bleeding.

The tribunal chamber was smaller than its reputation. Seven elders sat behind a crescent of tables stacked with ledgers of their own — the guild's records went back a hundred and forty years, and it showed in their faces, which had the particular pallor of men who lived mostly by lamplight among paper. At the center sat Elder Voss, whose beard had once, Dominic was fairly sure, been white on purpose.

"The book," Voss said, without preamble, laying his hand flat upon the ledger as though taking its pulse. "You understand what you are asking us to do with it."

"I understand exactly," Dominic said. "That's why I'm asking."

There was a rustle down the table — half disapproval, half, she thought, something closer to appetite. Printers loved a good line. They simply preferred it set in someone else's type.

"You bring us a record kept by an unlicensed shop," Voss said, ticking items off on thick fingers, "maintained without guild sanction, containing entries of dubious legality — forged names, suppressed debts, payments we will charitably call *irregular* — and you ask this tribunal to admit it as evidence against the army's own commission, thereby declaring that a girl with a burn scar and no credentials kept better books than the Republic."

"Yes," Dominic said.

Another rustle. Someone at the far end coughed in a way that might have been hiding a laugh.

"Master Yarrow," Voss began —

"Dominic Yarrow," she said. "If we're going to be accurate about names, Elder, let's start with mine."

The silence that followed had a different texture than the ones before it. She watched it move down the table, elder by elder, and understood that she had just done the thing they would talk about later: not the reading of the names, though that would become the story everyone told, but this small stubborn correction first, before witnesses, before the book itself was even opened.

Voss studied her for a long moment. Then he opened the ledger.

* * *

They read for three hours.

That was how it went — not speeches, not drama, just reading. Voss read; the elder to his left checked entries against the army's requisition lists, which a captain had delivered that morning under seal and visible resentment; the others asked questions in dry voices about dates and hands and ink. Was this entry contemporaneous? Was this signature witnessed? Could she account for the gap in the fourth month?

"Apprentice sick," Dominic said. "I was the apprentice. I nearly wasn't anything for a week."

"And this page, with the corner torn?"

"My father's dog," said Dominic, whose father had never owned a dog, and who watched the lie land gently among men who had certainly told better ones themselves.

But it was the accuracy that undid them. She could see it happening, hour by hour — the moment when each elder stopped listening as a judge and started listening as a bookkeeper. Every debt matched. Every alias cross-referenced against a warrant or a tax roll or a marriage register. There was an entry from 1787 recording that the cooper Grondin had paid his arrears in barrel staves at a rate the shop had *negotiated upward on his behalf*, with a note in her father's hand explaining why. There was a margin note beside Widow Marchand's second name — the real one, the dangerous one — that read only: *good for it, always has been.*

"Elder Corvez," Voss said finally, to the woman at the end of the table who had not yet spoken, "you wished to say something."

Corvez was small and ancient and had ink stains on her knuckles of a shade Dominic recognized from her own fingers — the particular violet of a certain expensive red-brown ink that came out of Lyon. She had used her father's last stick of it mending the ledger in the winter of '92, and seeing it now on another living hand nearly undid her.

"I wish to say," Corvez said, "that whoever cut the type in the colophon of this book learned from a master, and that I have spent forty years wishing I could keep accounts like these." She looked at Dominic. "And that the guild has spent forty years pretending shops like hers don't exist, which is a different kind of fiction, and worse-written."

"Motion," said Voss heavily, "to admit the ledger of the Yarrow print shop as evidence before this tribunal, and to enter its contents into the record of amnesty proceedings."

"Aye," said Corvez.

The ayes came down the table like dominoes. On the last one, the ledger itself seemed to sit up straighter on the table, if a book can be said to do such a thing. Dominic felt it anyway. Nine days of winter, a hollowed press, a frozen river, a twisted ankle, a ferryman's price, Ilse marching north in a penal column with her maps confiscated — all of it balanced on this table now, and holding.

"The amnesty question," Voss said. "Forty-one names appear in this book under assumed or expunged identities. The commission demands prosecution of all persons whose entries indicate evasion of lawful warrant." He paused. "This tribunal rules otherwise. Where the ledger demonstrates settlement of debt or reconciliation of grievance, the amnesty holds. Thirty-nine names stand cleared."

Thirty-nine. Dominic heard the number and felt it go through her like a stitch pulled tight — joy first, enormous and stupid and warm, and then, arriving half a heartbeat behind it, arithmetic.

"Which two?" she asked.

Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. Voss looked at her with something that was almost kindness and entirely useless.

"That is not—"

"Which two, Elder. You've had my whole winter. Give me that."

Corvez slid a slip of paper down the table to her. Dominic looked at it and the room went slightly distant, the way a street looks when you're falling.

The cooper Grondin was saved. The ferryman Odile was saved — her own page of the ledger, bought fair, honored here. Old Tavane the smuggler, saved. The schoolmaster with three names, saved. Down the list, saved, saved, saved — and then two lines that were not.

One belonged to a man named Ferrant, whom she barely remembered, a carter who had paid the shop in 1790 to bury a warrant under a false death notice. She had never met him. She suspected he had never met her father either; the transaction had probably been conducted through the letter slot. It hurt less than she expected, and she hated herself a little for how much less.

The other name was Marchand's.

* * *

She walked back across the city through snow that had turned fine and mean, the kind that doesn't fall so much as hang in the air waiting for you to walk into it. The slip of paper was in her pocket. She had read the two names perhaps six times between the guild hall and the river bridge, as though repetition might rearrange them. It didn't. Paper is very loyal to what's written on it; her father had taught her that too.

She had imagined, in the tannery loft, in the ferryman's hut, in the doorway of the burned shop, what winning would feel like. She had imagined it as a door opening. Nobody had warned her that doors open both ways, or that a verdict which saves thirty-nine people must be carried, personally, on foot, to the two it does not.

Ferrant first, then. Or maybe not at all — the tribunal would send its own notice, and Ferrant might have moved, might be anyone anywhere by now. She could tell herself that. She was already telling herself that.

Marchand was not hard to find. Marchand was never hard to find; she arranged her life like a shop window so that finding her was easy and leaving her was the difficult part. Dominic found her at the candle stall near the church steps, where the widow had established, over the course of the occupation, a small empire of tallow and gossip. Business was good lately. Fear, it turned out, sold candles.

"Well," said Marchand, looking her over. "You're upright. That's more than I expected from the tannery crowd." She was already wrapping two candles in brown paper, because Marchand expressed affection exclusively through merchandise. "Take these. You look like a woman who hasn't eaten since a verdict, and those are the worst kind."

"They ruled," Dominic said.

"I know they ruled. Half the town knows they ruled. Old Tavane wept in the square and then bought a hat, which tells you everything about Tavane." She held out the candles. "Thirty-nine, they're saying. Take your candles, Dominic."

Dominic took the candles. She did not take the exit being offered.

"Madame. Widow Marchand. Marguerite." The Christian name landed between them like a dropped plate. Marchand's face did its work — closed, polished, immovable — but her hands stopped moving on the counter. "There were two names the amnesty couldn't reach. One is a carter named Ferrant. The other—"

"Don't," said Marchand.

"—is yours," Dominic finished, because her father had taught her that a lie of omission was still set in type, it just didn't have your name on it, and she had spent a winter learning what unnamed print cost.

The market noise went on around them — somebody arguing about the price of onions, a cart, a child — with the terrible indifference markets have to the people standing still inside them. Marchand set down the wrapping paper very precisely, squared its corners, and looked at the middle distance over Dominic's shoulder.

"I paid that debt," she said quietly. "It's in the book. Your own book says I'm good for it. *Always has been.* You wrote that yourself, girl, I've seen your hand do it."

"It's in the book," Dominic agreed. "The book won. But the commission dug up the original warrant from the Rouen archive, and there's no settlement recorded on their side — whatever your husband paid, it never reached the court. The guild can prove the debt was honest. They can't prove the pardon was. Their ruling clears what's in the ledger. Yours—" She stopped. Started again, because cruelty delayed is cruelty doubled. "Yours is the wrong shape for the amnesty. I'm sorry. I argued until Corvez herself told me to stop, and Corvez does not tell people to stop."

For a long moment Marchand said nothing at all, and Dominic braced herself for the storm, the threats, the full Marchand artillery, and part of her — the honest part — wanted it, because fury would have been easier to carry than what actually happened, which was this:

The widow laughed. Short, bitter, and real, a laugh like a knot coming loose.

"Of course," she said. "Of *course*. Twenty years I keep my nose clean, I pay every soul in this town, I sell candles to the occupying army at a markup that would embarrass a bishop, and the one account that comes due is the one settled by a dead man's bribe going astray." She pressed the backs of her fingers to her eyes, briefly, briskly, the way women of her generation did grief — housekeeping it. "My Henri. God rest him. He'd have haggled with the executioner himself and talked him down to a discount."

"Madame—"

"Marguerite, if you're going to stand there looking like a drowned puppy, you'll use my name while you do it." She straightened. And here was the thing Dominic would remember for the rest of her life, the thing she would set down later in a different ledger under a different sky: Marchand reached over the counter and took her chin in a flour-dusted hand, firm as a mother, and made her be looked at. "Now. You listen to me. You carried that book past three checkpoints for people who mostly never said thank you. You do not get to stand in my market and apologize for the mathematics of governments. Do you hear me? I have been threatened by better than you, and I have been *thanked* by worse."

"He's still in prison," Dominic said, because it needed saying. "Ravel's commission holds pending appeal. There may be—"

"There is always *may be*." Marchand released her chin and picked up the wrapping paper again, and her hands resumed their work, and that was how Dominic knew the cost was going to be paid privately, later, in a room with the door shut, the way that generation paid everything. "Go home, Dominic. Whatever home is now that they've burned it. Go home, and when some official offers you a comfortable arrangement — and one will, mark me, tribunals always have one more favor in their pocket — you think very carefully before you trade away anything else for anybody." She paused. "Even me."

"I wouldn't—"

"You would consider it," Marchand said, not unkindly. "That's not an accusation. That's just arithmetic. Now take your candles and get out of my light, I have customers."

Dominic got three steps before Marchand spoke again, softer, to the counter rather than to her:

"The boy who ran your errands last winter. Ilse's brother, the one whose pardon was in the book." Her hands kept folding. "Tell them to clear his papers properly. All of it, in triplicate. If nothing else in my account comes right, let that one come right *boringly*. Boring is the safest thing a document can be."

* * *

She stood afterward on the bridge over the canal with two candles in her coat and the slip of paper in her pocket, watching the water carry pieces of the day downstream, and let herself feel all of it at once, which was a mistake she intended to make anyway. Thirty-nine. Her father's book had gone into a hostile room and come out with thirty-nine lives attached to it, and somewhere north a penal column was marching a cartographer who would, if she ever heard the number, want to know about the other two, and Dominic was going to have to tell her, and Ilse would do her arithmetic the way Dominic had done hers — joy first, then the wound arriving half a beat behind.

A clerk found her there. Of course a clerk found her there; clerks always know where people go to be miserable in peace. He was young, apologetic, and wore the new authority's gray sash, and he waited at a respectful distance until she turned.

"Mistress Yarrow. The tribunal's secretary asks me to convey—" he consulted a card, "—its formal compliments, and to inform you that the ledger will remain in the guild archive pending publication of the record."

"It stays with the guild," Dominic said. "In writing?"

"In writing, yes." He folded his card away. "Also — and forgive me, this is slightly outside my errand — there is a gentleman from the commission who has been asking after you since the verdict. He's waiting at the guild hall. He would not give his business to the clerks." The young man hesitated, lowered his voice as though the bridge might inform on him. "He said to tell you he comes with an offer, not a summons. He said you'd know what kind. He said—" the clerk squinted, remembering, "—he said, *everything has a settlement price, even mercy.*"

Dominic looked at the water a moment longer. Then she put the candles deeper in her coat pocket, where they wouldn't break, and turned toward the guild hall.

Everything had a settlement price. She had spent her whole life in a shop that proved it daily, in ink, in her father's hand. The only question — and she walked toward it slowly, through the hanging snow — was what currency they thought she had left.
