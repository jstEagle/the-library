## 32. The Pardon Offered

The summons came on the ninth morning after the tribunal, delivered by a boy so young his uniform had clearly been issued in hope rather than measurement.

"Dominic Yarrow," he read off the paper, then looked up at her with frank curiosity, because everyone in the guild house did, sooner or later. Everyone wanted to see what the Yarrow boy looked like up close, now that the Yarrow boy was the most discussed person between Vellamont and the city.

"That's me," Dominic said, which was true in more ways than the boy could count.

He handed over the paper, touched his cap in the direction of someone who wasn't there — perhaps Master Yarrow, perhaps the Republic — and left. Dominic read the summons twice. It said *the Provisional Commission for the Restoration of Order* wished to speak with her regarding *matters of record*. It did not say which matters, or which order, or whose restoration. Official language, her father had taught her, was type set too loose: lots of space where the meaning should be.

She had been staying at the guild house since the tribunal, in a narrow room above the composing floor that smelled gloriously of ink and oil and old paper — the smell of her whole childhood, concentrated like a tincture. She should have slept well there. She did not. She lay awake listening to the presses below and thinking about the two names she could not save, and about Marchand's face when Dominic had told her one of them was hers.

Marchand had not wept. Marchand had stood very straight in the guild house doorway, adjusted her shawl as though preparing to be insulted by royalty, and said, "Well. I always knew my name would be the death of me. I only hoped it would be a better sentence." Then she had asked whether the tribunal's record would spell her name with the double *l*, because her mother had spelled it that way and she would not be remembered with less.

Dominic had promised the double *l*. It was the only thing left to give her.

Now she dressed, pinned her collar high, and went down to meet the Commission.

* * *

They had given themselves the best room in the guild house, which was not hard, since the guild house had exactly one good room — the hall where tribunals met — and the Commission had simply declined to leave it after their last session. Three men sat behind the long table where the elders had sat during the Reading of Names. The middle chair belonged to a Citizen Auditor named Perrin, who had a lawyer's soft hands and a soldier's impatience with them; he kept folding and unfolding a document as though it were a bird he was trying to decide whether to release.

The other two were clerks. They had the stillness of men paid to listen.

"Citizen Yarrow," Perrin said. "Or is it Mistress Yarrow? The tribunal records are admirably thorough but inconsistent on this point."

"I answer to both. I answer fastest to Dominic."

"Then Dominic it is." He gestured to a chair. She sat. "You'll forgive the informality. After the past month, I imagine you've had your fill of formality."

"I've had my fill of rooms like this one," Dominic said. "But I'm told the coffee here is decent, so the visit isn't wasted."

Something flickered across Perrin's face — surprise, or possibly appreciation. He poured her a cup himself. It was terrible coffee. She drank it anyway, because a man who pours his own coffee wants something, and she had learned in the print shop that you let people get to the wanting at their own pace.

"You performed a remarkable service to the Revolution," Perrin began. "The ledger has already been cited in three proceedings. Two more open next week. There are men in this city who will sleep easier tonight, and men in Vellamont who will not sleep at all, because of what you carried through the snow."

"I know," Dominic said. "I carried it. My ankle remembers."

"Yes." He unfolded the bird of paper at last and laid it flat. "And here is where I must tell you something uncomfortable. The service you performed was illegal."

"So was the requisition," Dominic said. "So were the pardons-for-sale the ledger exposed. So, if we're making a list, was the burn order. I could set it for you in six-point type by tomorrow morning, if you'd like it documented properly. I do miss the work."

One of the clerks made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Perrin silenced him with a look and turned back to Dominic with the expression of a man who had rehearsed this conversation in the bath and found it going off-script in the first line.

"The new authority," he said carefully, "is in the business of drawing lines. Behind us, disorder. Ahead of us, order. The ledger sits precisely on the line. It damns the old corruption — good. It also documents how much of this town survived by forgery, alias, quiet debt-forgiveness, and other irregularities — less good. There are forty-one names in that book, and thirty-nine of them are safe under amnesty. But amnesties are fragile things, Dominic. They hold exactly as long as no one gives ambitious men an excuse to reopen them."

"And you're here to tell me not to give them an excuse."

"I'm here to offer you a full pardon."

The word sat in the air between them. *Pardon.* She turned it over the way she'd turn a piece of type toward the light, checking for flaws in the casting.

"For what crime?" she asked. "Name it. I've never been charged."

"For carrying restricted records out of a sealed premises. For impersonation — the army spent a week hunting 'the Yarrow boy' who did not, strictly speaking, exist. For conspiracy, if we include the cartographer—"

"No," Dominic said. "We don't include the cartographer."

Perrin paused. The pause was very slightly too long, and Dominic understood, with a coldness that started in her fingertips and moved inward, that the cartographer had been included from the beginning — that this entire interview had been built around her, the way a page is built around its largest letter.

"There it is," she said quietly. "There's the price. Go on. Say it all. I'd rather hear the whole bill than be surprised by the interest."

Perrin folded his hands. "Full pardon. All charges real or imagined, yours forever, unimpeachable. The guild will be directed to restore you to standing; there will be a press for you, compensation considered. And the Commission requires three things."

He laid them out with the tidiness of a man reading a manifest.

"First: the ledger passes permanently into the Commission's custody. Not the archive — the Commission. It will not be printed further. It will not be cited in the remaining proceedings. Those trials will be resolved administratively. Quietly."

"Quietly," Dominic repeated. "Meaning the guilty men go free."

"Meaning the town stops bleeding," Perrin said. "You read the book better than anyone, apparently. Tell me — how many of those forty-one names would survive a truly thorough inquiry? Not the amnesty. An inquiry. You know what's written between the entries. You kept it."

She did know. She knew every marginal note, every debt forgiven at midnight, every alias that had saved a life and would look, in a courtroom, exactly like fraud.

"Second," Perrin went on, not waiting, "you affirm publicly that you acted alone. That the requisition order you used was found, not forged. That no officer of the army assisted you."

"And third?"

He hesitated for the first time. "Third, you state — for the record, once, briefly — that the entry concerning Lieutenant Brandt's brother was misread at the tribunal. That it records a legitimate settlement, not a bribe."

The room was very warm and very quiet. Somewhere below, the guild presses ran on, indifferent, printing somebody's almanac.

"My father taught me typesetting," Dominic said at last, "and one rule above all the rest: never set a line you know to be false, because ink outlives men, and someday the lie will be the only thing left of you." She set the cup down. "You're asking me to make three false lines. And the second one — the one about acting alone — isn't even false in the way the others are. It's false in the worst way. It takes a woman who gave up everything to help me and makes her into nothing. Not a traitor, not a hero. Nothing. Unwritten."

"She faces the penal column regardless," Perrin said. "Your statement wouldn't change her sentence. It would only change what gets written near her name."

"That's exactly why I won't say it." Dominic stood. Her ankle ached, and she let herself lean on the table, deliberately, so he would watch her do it. "Citizen Perrin, I spent nine days learning what that book costs. Every page of it is somebody's survival bought with somebody's compromise. I can live with what's already written there — barely, most nights. What I cannot live with is adding clean lies on top of it to make the book prettier for men who want history tidy."

"It's not history we're managing," Perrin said, rising too, weary now, the rehearsal fully abandoned. "It's winter. It's bread prices. It's four hundred soldiers who need to believe the war they walked away from meant something. A pardon is not a punishment, Dominic. It's mercy wearing a coat. Try it on."

"I tried on a coat once," Dominic said. "Wore it for eleven years. I know exactly how heavy they get, and exactly what happens when someone finally asks you to take it off in public."

She walked to the door. Behind her, one of the clerks had begun writing, faithfully recording everything, including — she noted with some satisfaction — her refusal, in whatever words he chose, because ink outlives men and let it outlive her refusal too.

"Dominic," Perrin said, when her hand was on the latch. "Think on it three days. The offer stands until the twelfth. After that, the Commission's protection withdraws from Vellamont entirely, and the town learns what it looks like when ambitious men have no excuse needed."

"I don't need three days."

"Take them anyway." He almost smiled. "Everyone thinks they won't change their mind right up until they do. It's the one thing my whole profession agrees on."

* * *

She did not go back to her room. She went down to the composing floor instead, where old Master Quennel — the eldest of the guild elders, half-deaf and wholly unbothered by it — was setting type with the slow precision of a man who believed haste was a form of lying.

"They offered you the pardon," he said without looking up. News traveled fast in a building made of talkers.

"They offered me the pardon."

"And?"

"I told them ink outlives men."

Quennel grunted. He finished his line, slid it into the stick, and only then turned his head. "My master used to say that too. He died poor, in debt to half this city, and every press in the province runs on letters cut by his hands." He tapped the case. "Poverty isn't the price of honesty, girl. It's just the receipt."

"I know the receipt," Dominic said. "I've been reading it all month. I lost the shop before the pardon was even offered, Quennel. Did you hear? They burned it in reprisal the same night as the tribunal. My father's type is in the ashes. So when Perrin offers me compensation and a press of my own in exchange for unwriting Ilse Brandt—" She stopped, surprised by how quickly her voice had gone rough, and finished more quietly: "He's offering to sell me back things that were already stolen. At a discount. If I pay in someone else's name."

Quennel considered this for a long moment, the way he'd consider a badly kerned line.

"Go back to Vellamont," he said. "Whatever's left there, go stand in it. Decisions look different from inside a ruin than from inside a warm guild house. Trust an old compositor: never accept or refuse anything important while you can still smell someone else's ink."

* * *

She left the next morning, before the second day of her three had properly begun. The Commission had not posted watchers — or if they had, they were very bad at it, or possibly very good, and she had simply stopped caring, which amounted to nearly the same thing.

The road to Vellamont was shorter than she remembered, which she suspected was memory flattering itself; roads, like debts, only shrink when you stop being afraid of them. She rode part of the way on a grain cart whose driver talked for two hours about the price of flour without once asking her business, which made him the finest conversationalist she had met all winter.

Vellamort came up out of the river fog the way it always did — the bridge first, then the customhouse roof, then the crooked bell tower, then everything else spilling downhill toward the water as though the town had been poured rather than built. The army presence had thinned. The checkpoints stood empty, their barriers knocked sideways by some citizen with opinions. On the bridge, someone had chalked, in a schoolroom hand: *FORTY-ONE*. Below it, someone else had added: *MINUS TWO*, and below that, in a third hand, angrier: *WE REMEMBER THE DOUBLE L*.

Dominic stopped and read all three lines twice.

Then she walked up the ferry road, past the tannery, past Marchand's shuttered window — a candle burned there, she noticed, in broad daylight, which was either defiance or grief or, with Marchand, indistinguishable — and turned the corner onto her own street.

She had prepared herself. She had told herself the story on the road: *it burned; you knew it burned; ruins look smaller than houses.*

Nothing prepared her for the smell. Wet char and old smoke, weeks stale, and underneath it — impossibly, heartbreakingly — the ghost of ink, as if the shop had gone on being a print shop for several minutes after the fire took it.

The roof was gone. The walls stood hip-high along two sides, one full side, and a broken shoulder of the fourth. The press cavity was open to the sky like a wound finally allowed air. Scattered through the rubble, fused into strange gray sculptures, lay the type — her father's type, twenty years of cutting, every letterform he had ever carved, run together into nameless metal by heat that cared nothing for legibility.

She knelt in the ash. It seemed right to kneel; it also seemed absurd, and she thought, with a wild little laugh that escaped before she could stop it, that her father would have made exactly the same sound she was making, which was not crying exactly and not laughing exactly, but the noise a person makes when the world sets a line wrong and refuses to correct it.

That was when she saw it, half-buried near where the composing stone had been: a single letterform, thrown clear of the melt, black with soot but whole. She picked it up, rubbed it clean against her sleeve, and held it to the gray light.

A lowercase *d*. Her father's cutting — she knew his serifs the way she knew her own hands. The first letter of the name she had chosen for herself, back when choosing it had been the bravest thing she'd ever done, in a life that had since gotten considerably more competitive.

She closed her fist around it and stayed there, kneeling in the ruin of everything, while somewhere down the hill the river kept moving under the ice like a debt being quietly paid.

Behind her, boots crunched on ash. She turned, the letter tight in her hand — and stopped.

Ilse Brandt stood at the edge of the rubble, gaunt, footsore, in a coat that had marched a long way north and then somehow marched all the way back, holding a folded paper out toward her like an apology she hadn't yet found words for.
