## 33. Ashes of the Shop

She smelled it before she saw it.

That was the cruelty of it, Dominic thought as the coach crested the last hill above Vellamont. Ten miles out, while the town was still a gray suggestion between the river and the sky, the smell had found her — wet char and old smoke, the smell of a thousand hearths banked for the night, except wrong, too thick, too sweet underneath, like something living had been in the fire. She had spent her whole life breathing ink and lamp oil and the hot lead smell of the casting pot. She knew what a print shop smelled like when it was alive. Now she was going to learn what it smelled like dead.

The coachman — a chatty man who had spent four hours narrating every turnip field between the city and the river — glanced back at her. "Fire in Vellamont, seems like. Recent."

"So it would seem," said Dominic.

"Hope nobody was hurt."

"Yes," said Dominic, and did not trust herself to say more, because the honest answer was *nobody you'd count*, which was a lie, and she had sworn off lies on the road north, or at least sworn to ration them.

The coach came down into Vellamont by the ferry road, past the first checkpoint, which was no longer a checkpoint — just a barrier lying in the road like a felled tree, its sentry box tipped over, a goat tethered to it eating something out of a discarded cap. The army had moved on or dissolved or been eaten by the century; it hardly mattered which. What mattered was that no one stopped her. After nine days of winter in which every step of hers had been watched, weighed, hunted, she now walked through the town's throat entirely unnoticed, a travel-stained person with a limp and a satchel, and not one soul gave her a second look.

There was a kind of freedom in that. It tasted like ash.

She saw the shop before she was ready to. That was the other cruelty — Vellamont was small, and Yarrow's stood at the bend of Cooper's Lane where it could not be avoided, only approached. She came around the corner by the cooperage, where she had once escaped through a hole in a barrel stack with soldiers' boots behind her, and then there was no more approaching to do. There it was.

Or rather, there it wasn't.

The front wall still stood, mostly, one story of blackened stone with the window frames burnt out of it, so that it looked like a skull that had kept its jaw but lost its teeth. The roof was gone. The upper floor — her father's room, the room where he had died, where the bed still held the shape of him for three weeks after because she could not bring herself to strip it — that floor was simply absent, open to the sky, and the sky had been raining into it for what looked like weeks. Everything else was rubble and cinder, heaped and slumped and fused, the whole shop collapsed into itself like a thing that had knelt down to die.

Dominic stood in the lane with her satchel strap digging into her shoulder and discovered that her legs had opinions of their own. They declined, politely but firmly, to carry her closer.

"Master Yarrow," said a voice behind her, and she nearly jumped out of her skin.

Madame Toussaint, the landlady, stood at her gate with her arms folded and her chin up, wearing her best shawl, which is to say she had dressed for an occasion and the occasion was being right. She had never once believed Dominic's explanations about why her father never showed his face. She had asked questions for eleven years with the patient relentlessness of water on stone. And now she looked at Dominic — at the bandage still peeking from under her collar, at the way she was standing, at everything — and said, "So. You're the daughter after all."

"I'm—" Dominic began.

"Don't." Madame Toussaint came down her steps with surprising speed for a woman whose principal exercise for a decade had been disapproving from windows. "Don't start building me a boy. I buried my husband in '78 and I have not had the energy to be surprised since. I want to know two things. Are you hurt?"

"Healed," Dominic said. "Mostly."

"Good. Second thing: who lit it?"

"Soldiers. Reprisal. For the tribunal." Dominic heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "For me, if we're being exact."

"Then they're fools twice over," said Madame Toussaint, "because the tribunal made them look like criminals and this makes them look like cowards, and burning a print shop in January without checking which way the wind blows—" she jerked her chin, and Dominic followed it, and felt something in her chest crack sideways.

The wind blew inland, off the river, straight across Cooper's Lane. The scorch fan spread up the opposite side of the street in a great black tongue. The cooperage's far wall was charred. But between the two, standing alone in the gap like a man who has walked through a battle unscratched, was Widow Marchand's bakery — untouched, whitewashed, smoke curling cheerfully from its chimney, and propped against its door, of all things, a hand-lettered sign: **OPEN. BREAD. NO CREDIT FOR SOLDIERS.**

"The fire took the roof beams east," Madame Toussaint said, following her gaze with grim satisfaction. "Marchand stood on her own doorstep with a broom and told a lieutenant exactly what she'd do to him if a single spark crossed her property line. He believed her. Everyone believes her."

"Everyone always has," Dominic murmured.

"Come inside first," said the landlady, and for the first time in eleven years there was no question hidden under it. "You'll want tea before you go picking through that. Nobody should grieve cold."

* * *

Nobody should grieve cold turned out to be a philosophy with teeth in it. Madame Toussaint fed her soup, bread, half a preserved pear she claimed not to remember buying and then watched Dominic eat with unconcealed satisfaction, and interrogated her gently the entire time — not about the ledger, not about the tribunal, but about whether she was sleeping, whether the ankle still swelled, whether she was eating enough in the city, questions so ordinary they were almost unbearable. When Dominic finally rose to leave, the landlady pressed a bundle into her hands: bread, cheese, a jar of goose fat for the ankle.

"You'll want your rent deposit back," she said. "I've had it wrapped in oilcloth since the army came. Under the loose brick in my kitchen, where I keep things I don't intend to lose."

Dominic stared at her. "You knew there'd be trouble."

"I keep a boarding house in a river town, girl. I know weather." She folded her arms. "Take it. And take this too." She produced, from her apron pocket, a small flat parcel, and put it in Dominic's hands with unexpected care. Inside the paper was a typecase drawer, one long composing stick, and a burnishing stone, all of them clean and oiled.

"These were—"

"In your cellar. Where you kept the spare stock. I carried them out the night it burned, while the whole street stood around saying *what a shame* in voices loud enough to be overheard by anyone who might report insufficient enthusiasm." Madame Toussaint sniffed. "A shame. Eleven years I collected that rent from Master Yarrow, who had the finest handwriting of any man who never existed. You'll forgive an old woman for keeping a few of his tools."

Dominic held the composing stick and could not speak. Madame Toussaint patted her once on the arm — hard, the way she did everything — and shut her door.

* * *

The rubble let her down gently at first, which she distrusted.

The threshold stone was intact. She stepped over it into the ruin of the front room, where the counter had been, where she had mended the ledger by lamplight on ordinary evenings, and found the floor beneath her feet solid — the old oak joists had fallen inward and made a bridge of themselves, charred but holding. She crossed into the heart of the shop and stood where the press had stood.

The press was gone. Not broken — gone. In its place was a puddle of iron shapes, slagged and rusting, the frame melted into a dark lake with bones in it. The hollowed cavity where she had hidden the ledger for those first desperate weeks was open to the sky now, empty as a socket. Someone had taken the screw and the platen — scavengers, probably, iron fetching good money — and left the rest.

Her father had bought that press before she was born. He had taught her to wipe the rollers when she was seven, standing her on a stool, telling her the press was a stubborn animal that loved work and hated hurry. She had hidden forty-one lives inside its ribs and it had kept them. Now it was a puddle.

"All right," she said aloud, to it, because there was no one else to say anything to. "All right."

The type was worse.

The type was everywhere and nowhere. Lead melts at a temperature that fire reaches without effort, and her father's life's work — twenty years of cutting punches, striking matrices, casting letter by letter the characters that had set down every broadside, every bill of sale, every name in Vellamont — lay in the corner of the ruin in a single fused mass the size of a loaf of bread, silver-gray streaked with oxide bloom, dripping shapes frozen mid-run like candle wax. She knelt beside it. She touched it. It was cold, and it was beautiful, and it was dead, and she understood suddenly why people put coins on the eyes of the departed: you want to give the metal something to do in the dark.

She sat down in the rubble — she meant to kneel, but her legs resigned again — and she wept, not gracefully, with her sleeve over her face and the smell of char all around her, for the press and the type and the roof her father raised and the bed she hadn't stripped and the eleven years of concealment that had ended here, all of it burned or stolen or melted into a lump she couldn't lift.

When she was done, the light had gone amber, and her stomach reminded her of Madame Toussaint's provisions, and she wiped her face on her sleeve and got back to work. Grief, she had learned, was a job you did in shifts.

She was looking for survivors.

Lead melts. Steel doesn't, not at any fire a mob can build. Her father had cut his punches in steel — *ink outlives men, little D,* he used to say, *because I make it stubborn on purpose* — and the punches, the actual engraved letterforms, had lived in the cabinet by the south wall. The cabinet was a black skeleton. But drawers scatter, and steel is stubborn on purpose.

She worked methodically, turning scorched boards, sifting ash with her fingers through ruined gloves, finding the archaeology of her own life in strata: a buckle she recognized, the leg of the stool she'd stood on at seven, the brass weights from the casting pot fused into a cluster like a strange fruit. Twice her fingers found type — sorts, cast letters — and both times they came up crumbled or half-melted, faces blurred, unreadable. A language dissolving in her hands.

The light failed. She built a small fire in the doorway out of broken lath, ate bread and cheese, and slept in her coat with her back against the standing wall, and if anyone in Vellamont thought it strange that a stranger was camping in the Yarrow ruin, no one said so, because Madame Toussaint had spent the evening on her doorstep telling everyone exactly what she'd do to anyone who bothered the poor girl sorting through the ashes, and everyone believed her.

* * *

The third morning, she found the cabinet's bottom drawer.

It had been thrown clear, somehow — flung into the corner by the old privy wall, half-buried, its lid sprung. She dug it out with a shard of slate and sat back on her heels and lifted the tray into the light, and her breath went out of her.

Punches. Dozens of them. Steel letterforms, each one a tiny mirrored masterpiece, each one cut by her father's graver in the years before she could hold a tool. The steel had blackened in the fire but not yielded. She tipped them into her palm one at a time, reading them like braille: an **m** with a hairline flaw in the left shoulder that he'd refused to recut, calling it *character*. A **7**. An ampersand, his pride, so ornate it was nearly illegible — *a letter should earn its keep but it's allowed to enjoy itself.* A lowercase **e**, worn bright at the face from twenty years of use, because e is in everything.

She wrapped them in the oilcloth from her satchel, all of them, every survivor, and she was closing the drawer when she saw it: one punch lying apart from the rest, at the very lip of the tray, as if it had been set down last. As if it had been waiting.

A lowercase **d**.

Not the d from her father's standard alphabet — she knew that d the way she knew her own hands. This was older. Smaller. The serifs were slightly unsure of themselves, the bowl a fraction too round, the countersink a shade deep in one corner. She recognized the hesitations in it because she had made those same hesitations herself, with a graver, at fourteen, sitting across the bench from him while he pretended not to watch.

Her first letterform. The first punch she ever cut, the one he'd teased her about — *a d for Dominic, and don't tell me it's for anything else, I can see you blushing in the steel* — and which she had assumed lost or melted decades ago, because he'd certainly never displayed it. He had kept it. All these years, in the bottom of the tray, at the edge, where he'd find it whenever he reached in for the others. Her father, who told her ink outlives men, had kept the proof that she outlived every name he'd ever given her.

She sat in the ruins of the shop with the little steel d in her cupped palms, and the morning came up pale gold through the empty window frames, and mourning became something she could hold.

"Well," she said finally, to the puddle that had been the press, to the walls, to him, wherever he was — probably at her elbow, pretending not to watch. "It's a good letter. Bowl's a bit proud of itself. But it'll do."

She cleaned it with goose fat and her thumb until the steel showed bright, and put it in the breast pocket of her coat, where it sat over her heart like a coin placed where coins belong.

* * *

She was raking the last corner of the ruin — not looking for anything anymore, just unable to leave it disorderly, because order was the only inheritance she had left to tend — when the bell over Marchand's door rang across the lane, and the widow herself came out into the street carrying a loaf like a diplomatic dispatch.

Marchand crossed to the ruin's edge, planted herself, and looked Dominic up and down with the expression of a woman appraising a horse she intended to buy regardless of price.

"So you're alive," she said.

"Reported correctly."

"And the book?"

"Safe. Admissible. Thirty-nine names cleared." Dominic set down the rake. "Thirty-nine."

Something moved behind Marchand's eyes — quick, then sealed. She knew, then. Of course she knew; the tribunal's rulings had traveled faster than any coach. Dominic watched her decide, in real time, not to talk about it. It was the bravest arithmetic Dominic had ever seen performed at close range.

"Then you'll eat dinner at my table tonight," Marchand said, "because I have decided to be insufferable about it, and I need a witness." She thrust the loaf at Dominic's chest. Dominic took it. Neither of them mentioned the missing names, and neither of them needed to, and somehow that was the kindest conversation they had ever had.

Marchand turned to go, then paused. "There's a letter came for you yesterday. To this address, if you can believe the postal service." She pulled it from her apron — dirty, stamped with a garrison mark half-struck through, forwarded twice, the paper soft from handling. "Fellow who brought it said to tell you it was written under supervision, whatever that means, and that the handwriting is not the point."

Dominic turned it over. The direction on the face was in a hand she knew better than her father's alphabet — the precise cartographer's italic, the letters ranked like troops on a well-drafted map.

Ilse.

She broke the seal right there in the ashes, with flour on her fingers and Marchand watching with no pretense of discretion whatsoever, and read:

> *D —*
>
> *They are releasing me in exchange for testimony, which I am supplying in abundance, having discovered I enjoy it. My brother is safe. Do not thank Ravel; he will only explain, at length, that he acted out of procedure.*
>
> *I have requisitioned, forged, or otherwise acquired three passes north. The third has your name on it. Not your father's name. Yours. It seemed time someone wrote it down.*
>
> *I will wait at the north gate on the first of the month. If you are late I will assume you are being heroic, and I will come find you, and we will both regret it.*
>
> *Bring the maps nothing. Bring yourself everything.*
>
> *— I.S.*

Dominic read it twice. Then she folded it along Ilse's immaculate creases and put it in her pocket next to the steel d, where the two of them fit together as if the pocket had been designed for exactly that purpose.

Across the lane, Madame Toussaint appeared at her window with the unhurried inevitability of sunrise, saw the letter, saw Dominic's face, and withdrew with the satisfied air of a landlady whose ledger had finally balanced.

"Well?" said Marchand.

"The first of the month," Dominic said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, and warm, which surprised her more. "At the north gate."

Marchand sniffed. "Then you'll need a coat that isn't full of holes, and bread for the road, and someone to tell you what to pack, because you have clearly never traveled in your life." She was already walking back toward her bakery, issuing instructions over her shoulder. "And you'll dine tonight, and you will not argue, and you will tell me everything about the tribunal twice."

Dominic looked down at the ruin one last time — the standing wall, the melted press, the whole burned anatomy of her old life — and found that it hurt less than it had that morning, the way a wound hurts less once it's been properly cleaned and dressed. The shop was gone. The type was gone. But her father's steel was in her satchel, and her first letter was over her heart, and somewhere north of her a woman was forging passes in her true name.

She picked up the rake, finished the corner, and went to wash the ash off her hands.

Ink outlives men, her father used to say. He never mentioned what else does. But she was beginning to draw up the list.
