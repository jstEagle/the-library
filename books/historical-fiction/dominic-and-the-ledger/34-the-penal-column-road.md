## 34. The Penal Column Road

The road north from Vellamont was, in Ilse Brandt's professional opinion, badly surveyed.

She had been making this assessment for eleven days now, silently, because a woman in the penal column did not offer cartographic criticism to her guards no matter how much the meander of the river offended her. The column hugged the bank when it should have crossed, climbed a ridge that any competent engineer would have skirted by the valley floor, and twice forded a stream that, judging by the erosion patterns, had been fording *them* since before the revolution. Ilse catalogued all of it. It was either that or think about everything else.

There were thirty-one prisoners in the column, and Ilse had spent the first three days deciding what kind of prisoner to be. She had seen columns before, from the comfortable side of a map table — stragglers shot at the roadside, troublemakers bound wrist to wrist. She was not made for martyrdom; she suspected she wasn't even made for dramatic suffering. She was made for long walks and careful measurements and noticing things other people missed. So she noticed.

She noticed that Sergeant Pommier, who walked the left flank, had a habit of humming when he was bored and stopping mid-phrase whenever an officer looked his way, which meant he had been corrected for it before, which meant he could be managed. She noticed that Corporal Vasse, the youngest of them, counted the prisoners aloud every morning and always came up one short on the first attempt and blushed about it. She noticed that the column's lieutenant, a pale boy named Duval who had probably never been north of a wine cellar, navigated entirely by asking farmers, which meant that as far as the army knew, the road north was whatever old men in fields said it was.

Ilse filed all of this away in the place where she kept maps she hadn't drawn yet.

Not that there was anything to draw *toward*. That was the arithmetic she performed each night, lying on straw in whatever barn or chapel or abandoned presbytery they were locked into, doing sums until sleep came: two days bought by the false location she'd given Ravel. Two days, perhaps three if the winter roads lied convincingly, and the ledger would need every hour of them. Beyond that, the future resolved into a fog no surveyor could fix. Penal service was not a sentence so much as a slow subtraction. She expected to survive it. She had told herself she expected to survive it often enough that some mornings the telling almost worked.

What she had not expected was the cold in her chest that had nothing to do with January — the specific ache of having done something irreversible. She had forged an army requisition with the same steady hand that had charted the Vellamont waterfront, and somewhere behind her, in a town she could no longer see even from the high ground, that forgery was either a small treason soon forgotten or a large one with her name on it. Ravel knew her hand. Of course he knew her hand. He had studied her maps the way she studied coastlines.

He had arrested her gently. That was the part she kept turning over, like a coin of uncertain currency. No manacles in the office, tea she was fairly certain had been actual tea, and that terrible soft voice saying, "You understand I have to write this down, Mademoiselle Brandt." As if the paperwork were the tragedy and not the thing the paperwork described.

She thought about Dominic more than was practical. It interfered with counting fence posts, which was her other occupation. She thought about ink-stained fingers and the particular set of Dominic's mouth when she was pretending not to be afraid — a stubbornness like a knot tied by someone who intended it never to come loose. She thought about the ledger itself, which she had held open in her hands exactly once, in the hidden cavity behind the proofing press, and the page she had turned to without looking for it: her brother Emil's name, entered in a careful clerk's hand, *pardoned — consideration rendered — let this stand.* Her brother, alive in a farmer's field in Friesland, alive because Dominic's father had written him back into the world for a fee and then, apparently, had chosen never to collect again.

A book full of mercy, disguised as a book of debts. Ilse had marched past a great many monuments in her life. None of them had weighed as much as that page.

* * *

On the twelfth day the column stopped at noon instead of dusk, which everyone understood immediately to mean bad news, because armies only rested early when something had gone wrong somewhere else.

The halt was a crossroads hamlet called Saint-Aubin-des-Chênes, which consisted of a church, a smithy, and an inn with pretensions — a painted sign of a stag that some optimistic hand had given eyelashes. The prisoners were penned in the churchyard while the escort argued over billets, and Ilse, sitting with her back against a leaning headstone, permitted herself the luxury of reading the graves. Local stone, poorly cut, names worn soft by weather rather than by grief. A family plot where four children shared one marker and one date. She had learned, mapping towns, that churchyards told you the truth about a place faster than any mayor.

It was Corporal Vasse who brought the news, though he didn't know he was bringing it. He came through the lychgate at a half-run, skidded on the frozen mud, caught himself against the gatepost, and stood there vibrating with the specific agony of a young soldier carrying information he is not senior enough to deliver to anyone.

"You'll burst," Ilse advised him, in her flat accent. "Like an overripe plum. Very undignified."

Vasse went red. He glanced at Sergeant Pommier, twenty paces off, arguing with the innkeeper about hay. Then he sat down on the headstone next to hers as if his legs had made the decision independently.

"They're saying," he said, too low, "that there's news from the city. From the guild tribunal."

Ilse kept her eyes on the graves. Her heart was performing in a manner she declined to acknowledge. "News," she repeated, in the tone of a woman discussing drainage.

"The printers' guild. They convened a tribunal — the *guild*, a civilian body, imagine — and they ruled on some record from Vellamont. Some ledger." He dropped his voice further, thrilled and frightened at once, a boy standing too close to a fire he liked. "They say the army's own commandant testified. Ravel. That the records were accurate. That half the pardons his superiors sold years ago are sitting in this book in black and white." Vasse shook his head, savoring it. "They say officers are being recalled to the capital to explain themselves. They say—" he faltered, remembering whom he was talking to, and finished miserably, "well. They say a lot of things."

"Yes," Ilse said. "They usually do."

But her hands, folded in her lap, had gone still in the way that hands go still when their owner does not trust them to do anything sensible. The ledger had survived. The ledger had survived and reached the tribunal and been read aloud, which meant the two days had held, which meant the river road and the tannery and whatever Dominic had done with her stubborn knotted heart — it had all held. Somewhere south of here, in the direction she was forbidden to look, a book full of forty-one lives was now a matter of public record, and history was chewing on it with great appetite.

And she, Ilse Brandt, cartographer second class, was walking north away from all of it at the pace of the slowest ox.

Vasse was still watching her sidelong, waiting to see whether he'd committed a crime. Ilse considered him. Twenty, perhaps. A farm face. Counted prisoners wrong every morning and blushed about it.

"Corporal," she said. "When your sergeant finishes haggling, you might mention that the stag on that inn sign has been repainted at least twice — see how the antlers don't match the body's perspective? A town that repaints its sign has money passing through it. Ask the innkeeper what he charges the carter traffic, not the officers. You'll get better rooms."

"Why would I—"

"Because you're the only one in this column who will still be an honest man in five years, and honest men deserve better rooms." She said it mildly, as one remarks on the weather, and Vasse stared at her the way soldiers stare at prisoners who have suddenly become people.

He got up, hesitated, and then — clearly against the entire manual of conduct — said: "There was something else. In the dispatches."

"Was there."

"The tribunal petitioned. For the release of certain persons connected to the case. Persons whose testimony—" he stumbled over the official phrasing, "—whose testimony was material. There's a list. Names being sent back down the road for verification." He swallowed. "I shouldn't have said. But you looked—" he gestured helplessly at her whole situation, "—you looked like someone waiting for a letter."

"I am always waiting for a letter," Ilse said. "It is my principal occupation. Go on, before Pommier sees you fraternizing with the inventory."

He went. Ilse remained against the headstone, and let herself, for exactly the length of time it took the church bell to toll noon, imagine the rest.

*Certain persons.* A list, traveling south to north at the speed of bureaucracy, which meant at the speed of nothing. But lists required names, and petitions required petitioners, and a petitioner with enough standing to move the guild tribunal to write to an army column — that was not a stranger. That was someone who had stood in a courtroom at midnight and read forty-one names aloud until the room forgot to be a courtroom. That was someone who had, by every account the road was carrying, refused something enormous in order to keep refusing smaller things.

Dominic Yarrow was petitioning for her release.

Or — Ilse was a professional skeptic; skepticism was most of what surveying was — Dominic had included her name among others, a matter of legal completeness, one line among forty-one. Or the petition was a formality that would die in a quartermaster's drawer. Or it would arrive weeks late, routed through three offices, forwarded to a column that had already moved, chasing her across a province the way her forged requisition had once chased the truth out of Vellamont.

Hope, she reminded herself, arriving as rumor was still only rumor. Rumor was the cheapest commodity on any road. She had drawn maps of places she later learned did not exist; she had believed in coastlines that turned out to be cloud banks. She knew precisely how unreliable human testimony was, having recently perjured herself with a requisition form.

And yet.

She sat in the churchyard of Saint-Aubin-des-Chênes with frost coming up out of the ground between the flagstones, and she allowed herself the smallest possible version of hope — a hope measured in millimeters, drafted lightly, subject to revision. Someone was writing her name on a list. Someone with ink-stained fingers had sat down at a table in the city and put *Ilse Brandt* on paper in her defense, after everything, after Ilse had cost her the shop and the press and her father's type and nearly the road home. The arithmetic of it refused to balance. Ilse kept trying anyway, the way she kept trying to make the northern road make sense.

Sergeant Pommier's voice cut across the churchyard: column forming, ten minutes, and anyone lagging could ride in the supply cart, which nobody ever did because the supply cart contained the turnips.

* * *

That evening they were billeted in a tithe barn belonging to a farm whose owner had departed abruptly sometime in the past year, judging by the yard — a gate hanging by one hinge, a well with its bucket rope cut clean. Another entry, Ilse thought, for the vast unwritten ledger of the times: property abandoned, debts uncollected, names gone quiet. She wondered idly whether the Yarrow ledger would someday hold this farm too, and then remembered that it couldn't, that the book was closed now, sealed in an archive, become history instead of heart. Strange, how a thing could be saved by being taken out of the world.

The barn was cold and smelled of old grain and mice with opinions. The prisoners were locked in, the lantern hung, and the escort settled in the doorway to play cards by the light. Ilse found her usual corner, and found also that Vasse had contrived, by some miracle of logistics, that her blanket ration was one more than the regulations strictly required. She did not thank him aloud. She nodded at the stag-sign inn's distant roofline, visible through a gap in the boards, and he blushed as if complimented.

Sleep did not come quickly. Around her the column breathed and coughed and muttered into dreams. Somewhere near the door, Pommier was humming very quietly, having decided no officer could see him.

Ilse lay on her back and took inventory, as she did each night, of what she still owned. Her hands — intact, calloused in the specific pattern a drafting pen builds. Her memory, which held the coastline of half a province. One stolen pencil stub, taken from the tithe barn's ledge in a moment of reflexive larceny she chose to find charming. And now, newly added to the inventory, weightless but somehow displacing everything else: a rumor. *Certain persons whose testimony was material.* A list moving down the roads toward her at the speed of ink drying.

In her mind's eye she drew it: the route a petition would take. Guild house to ministry office; ministry to army postal; postal to the column's parent battalion; battalion to column — each stop a desk, each desk a chance to die. She gave the route three weeks if fortune was drunk, ten days if fortune was merely careless. And the column, meanwhile, marching north at the pace of the slowest ox, would be—

Her thoughts stopped. Sat up, mentally, the way she sat up physically whenever a survey produced a number that was impossible and therefore interesting.

The column was marching north. The petition was coming north. These were not two lines on a map. Unless the roads diverged, they were going to meet — or miss each other by a village, by a day, by a single wrong ford of a badly surveyed stream. She had spent her career watching routes converge and diverge on paper, feeling nothing, because paper was patient. Now she lay in a freezing barn calculating the intersection of two moving points, one of which was her life, and understood for the first time why sailors prayed.

And beneath the calculation, quieter, another thought — one she examined the way one examines a strange coin found in the road, checking its metal, its minting, its honesty.

If the petition succeeded, she would be released. Released into what? A country eating its own officers. A tribunal that had ruled for memory against the army. And somewhere in it, a type cutter's daughter with no shop, no type, no name she could safely use, waiting — or not waiting; Dominic did not wait well; Dominic would be working, cutting letters under some new alias, keeping some new ledger with honest entries. Ilse had seen the blank pages once, in the cavity behind the press, stacked and ready. *A fresh book,* Dominic had said, and hadn't explained, and hadn't needed to.

Ilse fell asleep doing sums, and for the first time in twelve days, the sums came out even.

* * *

Morning brought frost on the boards and Vasse counting prisoners — "twenty-nine... thirty... thirty-one, all present" — correct on the first try this time, which felt like an omen of some modest kind. The column formed. The oxen complained. Somewhere ahead, the road bent north around a hill that Ilse privately rated as geologically pointless.

They had been marching perhaps an hour when the rider came up from behind.

She heard him before she saw him — hoofbeats at the canter on frozen mud, which on these roads meant either disaster or dispatches. The column's lieutenant, Duval, waved the rider down at the head of the file. There was the usual theater: salutes, a leather satchel, papers read squinting against the wind. Ilse watched with the mild attention of a woman watching weather. Then Duval's posture changed — shoulders dropping, then straightening too fast, the whole grammar of a young man who has just been handed something he must now be important about.

He turned and walked down the column, checking faces against a folded paper, and Ilse understood with a lurch that had nothing to do with the ruts in the road that he was checking *her* face, that her name was on that paper, that the two moving points on her mental map had just intersected in the middle of a frozen road between a dead man's farm and a hill with no purpose.

Duval stopped in front of her. Behind him, the whole column slowed to watch, because a prisoner singled out is the finest entertainment an escort ever gets.

"Ilse Brandt?" He pronounced it carefully wrong, as everyone did.

"Close enough," she said.

"You are to be detached from the column." He consulted the paper, visibly proud of his own formality. "By order transmitted through the army postal, countersigned at the sector bureau. Your case is under review by the civil tribunal of the printers' guild, which claims—" he frowned at the phrasing, "—claims jurisdiction over matters of testimony pertaining to the Vellamont records. You are to be conveyed to the nearest garrison for holding pending..." He trailed off, because the sentence had no good ending, and finished lamely: "Pending."

"Pending," Ilse agreed gravely.

Pommier, at the flank, had stopped humming. Vasse had gone the color of the frost and was grinning anyway, helplessly, like a boy whose team has scored in a match he wasn't supposed to care about.

Detached from the column. Held pending. It was not freedom — it was a cage with better paperwork, and both the cage and the paperwork could close on her at any desk along the way. Ilse knew exactly how much a word like *pending* could bear before it broke. She was a surveyor; she tested loads for a living.

But she also knew what that folded paper was, underneath its stamps and countersignatures. It was a rumor that had survived the roads. It was forty-one names and one stubborn hand reaching north faster than an ox, faster than winter, faster than the machinery built to outrun it. Somewhere behind that rider, at the end of a chain of desks, someone had picked up a pen and written *Ilse Brandt* in the column marked *material* — and she would have known whose hand it was if every signature in the province had been forged to disguise it.

"Well?" said Duval, already bored, already turning back toward the head of the column. "You'll ride in the supply cart. Try not to converse with the turnips."

Ilse climbed aboard the cart among the sacks, and as the column jolted into motion she permitted herself, at last, the full-sized version of the thing — not the millimeter draft but the whole coastline. Pending meant moving. Moving meant southward, or near enough. Held for testimony meant a room, sooner or later, where a tribunal might ask what she knew about the ledger, and she had a great deal of testimony prepared, and none of it was going to hurt anybody who didn't deserve it.

And beyond the garrison, beyond the pending — a city, a tribunal, a printers' guild, a border town where a new press hummed under a new name, and a pair of ink-stained hands that had once measured a hollow space behind a proofing press and chosen, instead of reporting it, to ask only *whose names?*

The cart hit a rut. The turnips shifted. Ilse braced herself with her hands — her hands, still hers, still fit for a pen — and looked back down the road they had come, south, toward Vellamont, toward the archive, toward everything the century was busily printing.

Then she looked forward, and began, silently, with the stolen pencil stub she should not have had, to sketch the road ahead on the back of her ration ticket — because whatever came next, someone was going to need a map of it, and she intended to be the one who drew true.
