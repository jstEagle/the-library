## 36. Terms of Exile

The guild house kept its ink in silver pots.

Dominic noticed this on her fourth visit, because it was the sort of thing her father would have noticed and commented upon at length — *silver pots, Dominic, for ink that costs four sous an ounce; somebody in this building has never worried about a bill* — and because noticing it gave her something to do besides look at the document lying on the table between herself and Elder Corvin.

Three pages. Fair hand. Guild seal at the foot of each one, wax the color of dried blood.

"Read it through," Corvin said. He was a soft-spoken man with spectacles too large for his face and the patient air of someone who had spent forty years talking printers out of duels. "There is no trick in it. What we promised, it does."

"I know there's no trick in it." Dominic did not touch the papers. Her hands had been behaving strangely all morning — steady when she wanted them to shake, and now, when steadiness would have been useful, trembling like type loose in a stick. "I helped draft stronger documents than this with my own hands. That's rather the point."

Corvin took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and put them back on. It was, she understood, his version of pacing.

"The tribunal ruled in your favor," he said. "Thirty-nine names under amnesty. The record printed, distributed, entered into evidence at three separate proceedings. You did that. Whatever else happens—"

"Whatever else happens," Dominic said, "happens to Ilse Brandt."

She had rehearsed this conversation on the walk over. She had rehearsed it in the loft above the chandler's shop where she was lodging, standing at the narrow window with her breath fogging the glass, saying the words aloud to the street below: *I want the pardon traded. I want her papers.* It had sounded reasonable out loud. Clean. A transaction, and transactions were what she knew best — she had kept the ledger of Vellamont since she was fourteen years old, every debt and credit laid out in fair columns, and here at last was one of her own: one pardon against one set of release papers, signed and sealed and delivered before the column marching north reached the first river crossing.

But sitting across from Corvin with the actual papers on the table, the transaction felt less like arithmetic and more like holding her father's cold hands in the winter of '86, knowing that whatever she said next would be the last thing either of them ever said to anyone about anything.

"You understand," Corvin said gently, "what you are declining."

"A full pardon."

"Not merely a paper. A *position*. The new authorities do not hand those out to girls who steal books from commandants. They are offering to make you respectable, Miss Yarrow. Protected. There are men in this city who were hanged last spring for half of what you did, and they are offering you a certificate saying none of it happened." He tapped the table. "And in exchange you give up the ledger — which the tribunal has already secured, so it costs you nothing but pride — and you deny that the cartographer aided you. One sentence. *She acted without my knowledge.* Nobody would even blame you. She was army; she deceived you as easily as she deceived her colonel."

"She didn't deceive me."

"No," Corvin agreed. "I suspected not. But it would be believed, which is nearly as good, and considerably safer."

Dominic looked past him, through the tall window, at the courtyard below where an apprentice was sweeping snow off the stone steps with the grim thoroughness of someone who would rather be doing literally anything else. Beyond the wall, the city went about its business. Carts. Bells. A fishmonger shouting about the price of eels as though the century had not just turned over in its sleep.

"They told me the terms on Century's Eve," she said. "Before the reading. They thought I'd refuse the whole bargain if I knew the pardon came with strings, so they saved the strings for after. And I sat outside that hall all night in the snow, and I told myself: whatever they ask, I'll pay it. For thirty-nine names, I'll pay anything." She smiled, and felt it come out crooked. "That's the funny part, Elder Corvin. I meant it. I truly meant it. Anything except this."

"Love," Corvin said, without judgment.

"Love," Dominic agreed. "And something better than love, if you want the truth of it. Duty. She broke her oath for me. Do you know what an oath costs a woman like Ilse? It isn't sentiment with her. It's the frame she built her whole self inside. And she took it apart, plank by plank, with her own hands, because she read one page in my ledger and decided that the truth in it outweighed everything she'd sworn to a government that sells pardons by the pound." She leaned forward. "You printed the proof of that yourself. Her brother's entry. The bribe her own superiors took. If I stand in this room and say she deceived me — if I let them hang their lies on her back so I can walk free — then I am doing exactly what they did. I'm entering a false name into the record. And I have spent my entire life making sure the record stays true."

Corvin was quiet for a long moment. In the courtyard below, the apprentice gave up on the snow and went inside, leaving the steps half-done, which Dominic found oddly encouraging. There were battles not worth finishing.

"The release papers require an exchange," Corvin said at last. "That is the mechanism. The authorities will not simply *give* you Brandt. Pardons are granted; prisoners are exchanged. If you refuse yours, it reverts to the state, and the state has been persuaded — I did some persuading myself, you should know, along with Elder Fenn and two others — that releasing Brandt into your custody, under bond, pending the northern review, serves everyone. The army saves face. The tribunal keeps its precedent. And Brandt walks out of the penal administration with papers bearing your name as guarantor."

"My name."

"Which means," Corvin said, with the first dry humor he had shown all morning, "that should you both abscond northward within the week — purely hypothetically — the authorities will be very cross with you indeed. Fortunately, you will be beyond the reach of their crossness, and they will quietly discover that being angry at two vanished women is bad for morale."

"And the pardon?"

"Dies unclaimed. Which means the denial of your actions stands in the record as fact. You'll be a fugitive in your own country, Miss Yarrow. Vellamont itself may be closed to you. Your father's grave. Your shop." He paused. "What remains of it."

She had not gone back. She knew what was there — word had traveled ahead of her, the way bad news always walked faster than good — the shop burned in reprisal three days after the tribunal, the army claiming it was accidental, nobody believing it, the melted type found fused in lumps by neighbors who had stood in the street and wept. Marchand had written to her. An actual letter, four lines long, which for Marchand was practically an epic poem:

*Your shop is ash. Your enemies are embarrassed, which is a kind of payment. Come see me before you go anywhere. Bring nothing. I have bread.*

She had gone. Marchand had fed her brown bread and hard cheese and said almost nothing, and at the door had gripped Dominic's wrist hard enough to leave marks and said, "You couldn't save mine. You saved thirty-nine others. In Vellamont we know how to do sums, girl, and that sum comes out in your favor. Now get on the road before they think better of all this mercy."

So she knew what she was refusing. Not an abstraction — a country. A town. A grave she could no longer visit. Every road home, closed and signed and locked, forever, or at least until regimes changed again, and regimes changed the way weather changed, eventually, but never soon enough for anyone actually standing in the rain.

"I'll sign," Dominic said.

Corvin slid the papers across. She read them properly, because her father had taught her that documents were like typefaces: the danger was never in the large letters, it was in the small ones, the ones cut carelessly, the ones you only saw when the impression was already made. But the small letters were honest. Guarantor of conduct. Bond of two hundred livres, forfeit on violation. Release of Ilse Margarethe Brandt, cartographer, into the custody of the undersigned, pending review by the northern commission.

Two hundred livres. She almost laughed. She owned one letterform, a lowercase d, fished out of the ashes of everything her family had built over sixty years, and it wasn't worth two sous. The bond would be forfeit within the week and everyone at this table knew it, including, apparently, the authorities, who had priced their own humiliation in advance and found it acceptable.

Her hand hovered over the signature line.

*Ink outlives men,* her father used to say, guiding her graver through the curve of a letter. *Wood rots, iron rusts, people die and are forgotten by Tuesday. But ink — ink is stubborn, Dominic. Ink remembers. So whatever you set down, set it down true.*

She set down her name. Then, beneath it, in the space reserved for remarks, she added a line in her careful ledger-hand, because if she was going to make ink remember, it was going to remember correctly:

*Signed freely. The undersigned denies nothing.*

Corvin read it, and read it again, and then took off his spectacles and polished them for quite a long time.

"That sentence," he said finally, "will annoy approximately forty officials."

"Good."

"It may also, someday, save someone. Statements like that have a way of surfacing, decades on, in exactly the places they're needed." He blotted the page with the tenderness of a man handling scripture. "You are your father's daughter, Miss Yarrow. The guild records say otherwise, but the guild records have been wrong about many things, and I am told they are about to be wronger still."

* * *

They brought Ilse to the guild house at dusk.

Not in chains — the papers forbade chains, Dominic had checked twice — but flanked by two clerks with the weary faces of men who had processed too many humans that winter and would process too many more, and who regarded this particular transfer as one more line item in an account that never balanced.

Ilse looked thinner. Penal rations, ten days of them, and the particular thinness that comes from sleeping badly in rooms where nobody speaks. Her coat was the army gray she'd worn the night they met over the discrepancy in the shop's tax measurements, and there was ink on her fingers still — they hadn't taken her drawing implements away until the third day, she'd tell her later, and by then she'd memorized enough to redraw half the garrison from memory, *which showed them,* she'd add, with the small fierce satisfaction of a woman who considered memory a weapon.

The clerks presented the papers. Dominic countersigned. One clerk read her appended line, raised his eyebrows, and said nothing, which she chose to interpret as professional respect.

Then the clerks left, and the doors closed, and they were alone in the guild house vestibule among the silver ink-pots and the smell of beeswax and old paper.

"So," Ilse said.

"So," Dominic said.

Neither of them moved. Ten days. It had been ten days since the tannery, since the gate, since Ilse had walked into Ravel's office carrying a lie shaped like a map and bought two days with it and known, calmly, precisely, what it would cost — and Dominic had spent those ten days telling herself that reunion, when it came, would be simple, that it would be all at once, arms and breath and the whole debt of it discharged in a single embrace.

Instead they stood six feet apart like strangers at a customhouse, because ten days was long enough to learn that the person you loved might be marching north forever, and fear leaves residue the way ink leaves ghosting on cheap paper.

"You're staring," Ilse said.

"You're real."

"I'm frequently told I'm too real. Very exacting, cartographers." But her voice cracked on the last word, and then she crossed the space in four strides and took Dominic's face in her inked fingers and kissed her, thoroughly, inexpertly, with the desperation of someone confirming coordinates, and Dominic held onto her coat and thought, distantly, *the vestibule, Elder Corvin's vestibule, there is going to be gossip*, and found that she did not care even slightly.

When they broke apart, Ilse rested her forehead against Dominic's and laughed shakily.

"I had a speech," she said. "For if you'd signed the other thing. Very dignified. 'I release you from any obligation,' all that. I practiced it in the cell. The rat seemed convinced."

"There was never another thing."

"I know. Corvin sent word. He's braver than he looks, that man — did you know he threatened the Ministry of Records with a typesetting error?" She pulled back, held Dominic at arm's length, and examined her the way she examined terrain: systematically, looking for what had changed. "Your ankle."

"Healed. Mostly. Odile's granddaughter splinted it properly; the ferryman's family considers medicine a branch of smuggling."

"And you?"

The question underneath the question. *And you, who watched soldiers nail your life shut, and carried a book through frozen water with dogs behind you, and read your father's secret in his own handwriting, and gave away your country — how are you, Dominic Yarrow, actually?*

"Ask me on the road," Dominic said. "It's a long road."

Ilse studied her, and then nodded, because Ilse understood instruments, and knew the ones that needed calibrating could not be rushed.

"The passes," she said, briskly now, unrolling a sketch she'd drawn from memory on guild paper — the border country, rivers and ridges in her clean sure hand. "We go north tomorrow, before anyone reviews these papers too closely. Three days to the crossing at Hesse Ford. I have a route—"

"We have a route," Dominic corrected gently. "You've been drawing maps alone in a cell for ten days. You can share the pencil."

Ilse looked at her. Something loosened in her face, some rigidity she'd been holding since the arrest, and she handed over the sketch without a word, and Dominic smoothed it on the table and bent over it beside her, shoulder to shoulder, the way they had once bent over the shop's false dimensions in a print shop that was now smoke.

Outside, snow was beginning again. Somewhere north of the city, on a road neither of them could see yet, the rest of their lives was waiting — poor, hunted, unpardoned, and, Dominic thought with a ferocity that surprised her by feeling exactly like joy, entirely theirs.

"One condition," she said, tracing the route with one finger.

"Name it."

"When we stop somewhere permanent — wherever it is, however long it takes — I'm starting a new ledger. First entry gets to be yours."

Ilse smiled, slow and warm, and pulled her closer against the cold coming through the vestibule window.

"What will it say?"

Dominic thought about it. Debts owed. Debts forgiven. Names kept safe. Everything her father had taught her, and everything she'd learned since, and one line more, the most important entry in any book she would ever keep.

"*Paid in full,*" she said. "*Both columns.*"
