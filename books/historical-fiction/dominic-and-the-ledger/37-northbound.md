## 37. Northbound

The city gate opened at seven, and Ilse Brandt was there at half past six, standing in the trampled snow like a woman waiting for a coach that had already left.

She had been early to everything her whole life — early to her cartography examinations, early to her own arrest, early to the penal column's muster so that no one could accuse her of lingering. Punctuality was a form of armor. But this morning she was early for a different reason entirely: she did not trust the world to hold still while she counted the minutes.

The release papers weighed almost nothing. Two sheets of paper, signed by a guild clerk whose hand shook with either cold or conscience, stamped with a seal that had been invented eleven days ago and would probably be declared illegal by spring. As far as the army knew, Ilse Brandt had been transferred out of the penal column on a technicality — *misattributed rank*, the papers said, which was an absurdity so elegant she had laughed aloud in the clerk's office, alone, and startled a pigeon off the windowsill.

Misattributed rank. Eight years of service, two hundred maps, one act of treason committed out of love, and the empire's entire accounting of it came down to a clerical error in her favor.

She looked down the north road. Snow. Rutted ice. A line of carts waiting to be inspected. And somewhere among them —

There.

Ilse would have known her anywhere. Not from the way she walked — Dominic Yarrow walked differently now, favoring the ankle she had twisted in the frozen shallows, listing slightly to the left like a ship taking on water — but from the set of her shoulders. She carried herself the way she had stood over the proofing press: braced, always, against the possibility that the world would ask her to account for herself. She wore a man's coat two sizes too large and a cap pulled low, and she carried a canvas satchel hugged to her chest as though it contained a sleeping infant.

It contained, Ilse knew, exactly three things: a letterform the size of a thumb, a bundle of maps, and two forged passes of Ilse's own manufacture.

*Nothing,* Ilse thought. *We are two women leaving a city with nothing at all.*

Then Dominic saw her, and stopped walking, and the whole gray morning changed weather.

---

Dominic had rehearsed this moment for nine days, which was eight more than she had rehearsed anything else in her life. In the tannery loft, in the ferryman's hut, on the long jolting ride in a wool merchant's cart, she had composed sentences. Something about how Ilse looked. Something dignified about what they owed each other. Something, perhaps, clever — she was a printer's daughter; she could be clever when it mattered.

What came out was: "You're shorter than I remembered."

"And you're limping."

"It's a very dignified limp."

They stood three feet apart in the trampled snow, two people who had conspired to commit crimes against a revolution, who had traded a book back and forth across a town like a coal passed hand to hand in winter, and neither of them moved. A cart rattled past. A soldier yawned at his post. Somewhere behind the walls, Vellamont's bells were ringing the hour wrong, as they always had, because the sexton refused to climb the tower twice.

"You came," Dominic said finally, and heard how foolish it sounded — as if there had ever been a version of the world where Ilse Brandt, who drew roads for a living, would fail to appear at one.

"I was released," Ilse said. "Technically, I was never imprisoned. There was a misattribution of—"

"Rank. I heard. I've been telling everyone at the guild house that my co-conspirator escaped the penal column through sheer bureaucratic incompetence." Dominic's voice cracked in the middle of it, which ruined the joke, which was fine, because neither of them had wanted the joke anyway.

Ilse crossed the three feet. The embrace was clumsy — the satchel between them, Ilse's coat stiff with road frost, Dominic's bad ankle betraying her so that she tipped sideways and had to grip Ilse's arm — and it was, both of them would later agree, the single most graceless reunion in the history of reunions, and neither of them let go for a long time.

"You have snow in your collar," Dominic said into her shoulder.

"You have the entire garrison of Vellamont looking for your satchel."

"Yes. Well. One of those things can be fixed."

---

The inspection at the gate took four minutes, which Ilse spent performing boredom so convincingly that Dominic began to worry she had actually died of tedium and was being propped up by sheer discipline.

"Purpose of travel?" the sergeant asked, not looking up.

"Cartographer," Ilse said, "surveying the northern toll roads for the Roads Commission," which was true in every particular except the Commission, the survey, and the roads.

"And her?"

"My assistant. Carries my instruments."

Dominic, who was carrying a stolen letterform, forty-one lives' worth of vindication, and an ankle full of ice, nodded solemnly. She had, in her day, talked her way past a sergeant by correcting his French grammar; a small lie about instruments hardly raised her pulse.

The sergeant waved them through. Neither woman spoke until the walls of the city were a smudge behind them and the road had narrowed to a track between bare fields.

"Instruments," Dominic said. "You called me instruments."

"It was either that or luggage."

"Luggage has more dignity."

"Luggage doesn't get an opinion," Ilse said, and Dominic laughed — really laughed, the first time in weeks, a sound that startled crows out of a hedgerow — and then had to stop walking and sit on a fence post until her ankle forgave her.

---

They made eight miles the first day, which was six fewer than Ilse's maps recommended and exactly as many as Dominic's ankle permitted. They lodged at a posting inn where the innkeeper asked no questions because Ilse paid in coin and coin, in this winter of the century's turning, answered all questions before they were asked.

The room had one bed, which neither of them remarked upon, because remarking on things had never been their method. Their method, developed over months of glances across a print shop counter, was to say almost nothing and understand almost everything, and it had served them through checkpoints and burn orders and a tribunal, and it served them now: Ilse hung the maps to dry by the fire while Dominic laid out their assets on the table with the gravity of a general reviewing troops.

One lowercase d, wrapped in cloth. Eleven maps, folded. Two forged passes, valid for the border districts until March. Three coins remaining, plus whatever Ilse had sewn into her coat lining, which turned out to be seven coins and a button.

"We're rich," Dominic said, regarding the hoard.

"We are not destitute," Ilse corrected. "Destitute is a state of mind. We are merely liquidating our expectations."

"When did you become funny?"

"I've been funny for years. You were always too busy hiding from soldiers to notice."

Dominic unwrapped the cloth. The letterform sat in the candlelight — a d, her father's cut, its bowl still round despite the fire that had taken the shop, the stem nicked where the heat had begun to work at it before someone, Ravel or a soldier or simple chance, had raked it clear of the worst. It was the only piece of Yarrow type left in the world.

Ilse watched her look at it. She had watched Dominic read the ledger, watch the tribunal, watch the verdict; she knew the face Dominic made when grief and pride arrived together and refused to decide which would speak first.

"He'd have hated this," Dominic said at last. "All that lead in the world, melted for spite, and one letter survives. He'd have said it wasn't enough to print so much as an apology."

"He'd have been wrong," Ilse said. "You only need one letter to start."

Dominic looked up. "That's sentimental nonsense for a cartographer."

"I'm off duty."

And then, because the candle was low and the fire was warm and the passes could wait until morning, Dominic reached across the table and closed Ilse's hand around the little d, and said nothing, and was understood completely.

---

On the third day the road climbed, and conversation got easier the way it does when walking does the talking.

Ilse told her about the penal column in the flat tone she reserved for terrain features — mud, lice, a lieutenant who quoted philosophy badly — and skipped lightly over the parts that would have made Dominic stop walking again. She told the parts about hope as rumor instead: a guard who mentioned a trial in the capital, a name spoken with something like respect. *The Ledger Trials*, they were calling them already. The word traveled faster than any map Ilse had ever drawn.

Dominic told her about the tribunal in exchange — the elders' faces at midnight, the way the room changed when she stopped arguing law and started reading names, forty-one of them, aloud, until the courtroom became something older than a courtroom: a town remembering itself. She told about the thirty-nine saved. She did not pretend the other two didn't exist. Ilse listened and did not pretend either, and that was the whole of the comfort either of them could offer, and it was enough.

At the top of the hill they stopped, and Ilse unfolded her northern route across a milestone, weighting the corners with stones.

"There's a fork ahead," she said. "The east road goes through Lisseau — longer, but there's a printing house there. Guild-affiliated. If you wanted work—"

"If *we* wanted work."

"If we wanted work," Ilse agreed, without looking up from the map, "the pay is poor and the master printer is rumored to be difficult."

"What's the west road?"

"The west road is shorter, warmer, and has better inns, and none of it matters, because you've already decided on the printing house."

Dominic grinned. "I decided on the printing house before we left the gate."

"I know. You get a look. Like a dog hearing a bell."

"A *cartographer* gets a look too. When she finds an unmapped river."

"That's different. That's professional satisfaction."

"That's exactly the same look."

They argued about it companionably for another mile, which was, both of them understood, the truest form of tenderness either of them knew: the luxury of quarreling about nothing, in the open air, with no checkpoints behind them and no descriptions nailed to gates. *The Yarrow boy*, Ravel had written, and the disguise that had saved her life had nearly cost it — but here on the empty road Dominic wore her cap low out of habit rather than terror, and once, daringly, pushed it back altogether, and the wind on her bare hair felt like a door opening onto a room she'd never been allowed to enter.

---

The news caught them at the end of the week, at a crossroads tavern where the coach from the south stopped to change horses.

They were eating bread and cheese in a corner when the coach passengers came in talking all at once — a merchant, two clerks, a woman in fur who clearly enjoyed being the bearer of tidings. Fragments drifted across the room: *the old ministry dissolved... arrests in the capital... the trials widening...*

"They're saying the ledgers are being printed," the woman in fur announced to the room at large, in the tone of someone who had personally arranged it all. "The evidence from the Vellamont affair. Printed in full, thousands of copies, being read aloud in towns from here to the coast. Every debt, every pardon sold, every false name — all of it in ink now, public, permanent."

The clerks murmured. The merchant ordered wine. Dominic sat very still with her mug halfway to her mouth.

"Printed," she said quietly. "In full."

"Thousands of copies," Ilse repeated. She was doing arithmetic Dominic could see — the same arithmetic, run in the opposite direction. One book, copied by hand over twenty years. Now multiplied past counting, past recall, past any commandant's bonfire. Unburnable now. Everywhere.

Dominic started laughing. It was only partly laughter; Ilse recognized the other part and put her hand over hers on the table.

"All that work," Dominic managed, "to save *one copy*. Forty-one names, one book, three checkpoints, a press hollowed out like a loaf — and the punchline is that history wanted it *typeset*." She wiped her eyes. "My father used to say ink outlives men. He meant it as a warning. He never imagined it as a battle plan."

"Neither did I," Ilse said. "And I'm the one who drew the routes."

They sat with it while the coach passengers argued happily about politics they barely understood. Outside, the light was going blue over the hills, and somewhere north of those hills was a border, and beyond the border a town, and in that town — if the guild letters were honest — a print house with a vacancy for a type cutter, and a master printer rumored to be difficult.

Dominic finished her bread. She took the little d out of the satchel, set it upright on the table, and looked at it for a long moment — one letter, survivor of a fire, ancestor of ten thousand pages that did not yet exist.

"Well," she said. "We'd better go and earn it a few thousand colleagues."

She picked it up, pocketed it, and shouldered the satchel — and froze, because through the tavern window, on the far side of the crossroads, a rider had drawn rein under the signpost. Army cloak. Satchel of dispatches. He was studying the posted notices, and he was studying, at some length, the fresh sheet nailed beneath the travel regulations — a sheet Dominic could not read from where she sat, but whose shape, whose layout, whose dense block of printed description she knew the way she knew her own hands.

A description. Another one. Posted this far north, this fast.

The rider turned his horse toward the tavern door.
