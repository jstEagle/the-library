## 38. The Century Turns

The road north taught Dominic things she had never learned in a print shop: how to sleep sitting upright against a fence post with one hand still curled around her satchel; how to tell the ripeness of a hedgerow apple by smell alone; how Ilse took her tea (barely warmed, mostly milk) and how she took her silences (long at dusk, shorter by morning, never once reproachful).

They had been on the road eleven days. The passes behind them had closed with the first real snow — which meant no pursuit could follow easily, which meant Ravel either had given up or had decided they were not worth frostbite. Dominic preferred not to guess which.

"You're doing it again," Ilse said that morning, without looking up from the map she was mending with a strip of paste and a flat stone.

"Doing what?"

"Counting your fingers."

Dominic looked down. Her left hand did indeed have all ten, resting atop the satchel where the ledger rode against her hip like a sleeping cat — heavy, warm-ish, occasionally inclined to scratch. "Habit," she said. "A person carries something that important for nine winter days, she develops opinions about its continued presence."

"A person carries it for nine winter days," Ilse said, "she should also develop opinions about eating breakfast." She broke the last of the black bread in two and handed over the larger half, which was its own small argument about who loved whom more.

They ate in the ditch beside the road, because the inn ahead flew a flag Dominic didn't recognize — a new flag, or an old flag returned, nobody at the ferry crossing yesterday had been able to say which, only that it had gone up on Tuesday and that the innkeeper had wept, though whether from joy or terror he had declined to specify.

"That's the third new flag in a week," Dominic said, mouth full.

"It's not a new flag. It's a restored one." Ilse squinted at it. "Though the border towns change them the way other people change tablecloths."

* * *

The news caught them at Maren's Ferry, two days later, in the way that news catches travelers: sideways, secondhand, wearing somebody else's coat.

The ferry house was low and smoky and smelled of wet wool and fish oil, and it was crowded, because the river had begun to groan at its edges and everyone wanted to cross before it made up its mind about ice. Dominic and Ilse wedged themselves onto a bench between a tallow merchant and a woman with six geese in baskets, and it was the goose woman who supplied the revolution.

"There's been fighting in the capital," she announced, to no one and therefore to everyone. "Real fighting. The palace is taken. They're saying the old ministers have run, the ones that weren't arrested, and the tribunals are sitting day and night."

"Tribunals always sit day and night," the tallow merchant grumbled. "It's the sitting that makes them tribunals."

"Not like this. This is the new order. They're trying the old men — the ones who sold pardons, the ones who took bribes for names." She lowered her voice with the delicacy of someone who very much wanted to be overheard. "And do you know what they're using as evidence? Documents. Ledgers. Records kept honest by little people while the great men were busy stealing."

Dominic's hand found the satchel strap and held it.

"They read out names in open court," the goose woman went on, warming now, geese shifting indignantly in their baskets as if they too felt history move. "Names that were bought and names that were forgiven and names that were hidden away in some provincial town nobody's ever heard of—"

"Vellamont," Dominic said.

It came out of her before she could stop it — quiet, but the room heard it, the way rooms hear anything said with a cracked voice. The goose woman turned. The tallow merchant turned. Even the ferryman paused with his pole half-lifted.

"What's that?" the goose woman said.

"Vellamont," Dominic said again, steadier. "The provincial town. It's called Vellamont. River town, south of here. I've — heard of it."

"Well, of course you have, everyone has now." The goose woman settled back, triumphant, a woman whose gossip had been confirmed by a stranger and could therefore be entered into the permanent record of the world. "That's the one. A printer's book, they're saying. Forty-one names, every one of them true. They're printing extracts in the capital already. Broadside sheets, posted on the church doors. My cousin saw one. He can't read, but he stood in front of it anyway for an hour, out of respect."

Beside her, Ilse had gone very still — the particular stillness of a cartographer sighting a landmark she had drawn herself from the wrong side.

* * *

They crossed the river in grey light, standing in the ferry among sheep and crates and a man who would not stop singing, badly, a song about liberty that everyone else seemed to know. Ilse held the rail. Dominic held the ledger.

Neither spoke until the far bank.

Then Dominic sat down on a mooring stone and laughed — a strange, unsteady sound she didn't recognize coming out of her own chest, laughter with water at the bottom of it.

"Dominic?"

"She's being *printed*," Dominic said. "The ledger. My father's book — my father's careful, fussy, bad-tempered book with its crooked rules and its ink blots — is being set in type somewhere by strangers who don't even know that the W's are cut too narrow because he always rushed the W's, and it's going up on *church doors*, Ilse. Church doors. Where proclamations go. Where the burn orders went."

"Yes."

"I spent nine days carrying it past three checkpoints thinking I was saving forty-one people from being *erased*. And the whole time—" She stopped. Started again. "Ilse, the whole time it wasn't a hiding place waiting to be discovered. It was evidence waiting to be needed. It was always going to matter. We just got there first."

Ilse sat down beside her on the cold stone, shoulder to shoulder, and watched the river shove its ice southward in slow white plates.

"Do you know what I thought when the goose woman said it?" she said at last. "I thought about the map room at the garrison. All those beautiful maps, and every single one of them is already obsolete. Every route, every border, every colored province — some clerk is drawing lines through them right now with a straightedge." She smiled, slow. "But your book isn't obsolete. Your book got *more* true."

"My book has terrible W's."

"Your book has true things in it. That survives bad type." Ilse bumped her shoulder. "Believe me. I've read enough false things in beautiful ones."

Dominic looked at her then — really looked, the way she had been learning to on the road, when there were no checkpoints and no forged orders and no duty left to hide behind except the ordinary kind. There was frost in Ilse's eyebrows. There always was, by morning. Somewhere north of them was a penal column and a brother and a war rearranging itself like furniture, and here on this mooring stone Ilse was warm at the shoulder and smiling at a joke about typography, and Dominic understood with a vertiginous lurch that this was what people meant by *history*. Not drums. Not flags. Just this: sitting next to someone while the world quietly changes underneath you both.

"I keep waiting to feel different," Dominic admitted. "Bigger, somehow. Like I should stand up and look at the horizon. Forty-one names, read aloud in the capital. I did that. Me. And instead I feel about four inches tall and mostly cold."

"That's awe," Ilse said. "I've surveyed mountains. They always look smaller than the maps claim. The feeling comes later, usually at night, when you can't sleep." She stood, and pulled Dominic up with her, easy as lifting a proof off the press. "Come on. There's a town ahead with an inn, and inns have stoves, and stoves have evenings. You'll get your vertigo after dark like a civilized person."

* * *

The town was called Brandenmere, and the inn was called the Sign of the Two Compositors, which Dominic took at first for a good omen and later for a personal message from the universe, depending on the hour.

The broadsheet was on the door of the church across the square, exactly as promised, nailed up at eye level with four nails and one obvious sense of ceremony. A knot of townsfolk stood before it in the falling snow, and Dominic joined them anonymously, hood up, heart going like a galley proof under a heavy roller.

There it was. Set in double column, tight leading, cheap ink:

*Extracts from the ledger of Vellamont, admitted into evidence before the Tribunal of the Second Section, being a true record of debts, pardons, and names concealed or redeemed...*

Below, in smaller type, the names began. She knew every letterform of them even though these were not her letters, not her setting — she knew them the way you know your own handwriting in someone else's hand. Widow Marchand, née an alias long buried, entry dated, debt recorded, disposition noted. The cooper's boy. The ferryman Odile, whose expunged page was — Dominic checked twice — marked *withheld at the request of the holder*, which meant the tribunal had respected it, which meant somewhere a clerk had argued for a stranger's privacy and won.

Thirty-nine names on the sheet. Thirty-nine saved. Two absent, and their absence sat in Dominic's chest like a stone she had agreed to carry.

She read it through three times. On the third pass, a man beside her — old, muffled to the eyes — said, "My sister's name is on there. Fourth from the bottom. Pardoned twelve years ago by a bribe, and none of us ever knew how she managed it." He sniffed. "Now everybody knows, and she's furious. Says it was private."

"It was," Dominic said.

"And now it's justice," the old man said, with a shrug that contained an entire century. "Those aren't the same thing. But they're cousins." He shuffled off into the snow.

At the bottom of the broadsheet, beneath the final name, the compositor had added a line in italics, probably on his own initiative, the way compositors do:

*The original ledger remains in the custody of the printers' guild, pending further proceedings. Its keeper is unnamed.*

Unnamed. Dominic read that line four times. Unnamed, and yet the whole capital was talking about a keeper; unnamed, and yet the goose woman on the ferry had known the town's name; unnamed, and yet any officer of the old order with a memory and a grudge — and Ravel, whatever he had chosen at the tribunal, was neither blind nor forgetful — could put a face to the blank space on that sheet without much trouble.

Famous and anonymous at once. Protected and hunted at once. She was, she realized, the ledger now: admissible, vindicated, and not entirely safe.

* * *

In the inn, by the stove, Ilse spread her maps on the floor and did arithmetic with a stub of pencil.

"The border posts are in disarray," she said. "Half the garrisons don't know whose flag to fly, so they're flying none, which means nobody's checking papers, which means the next week is the safest crossing window we will ever see. After the new order consolidates, the checks come back — new flags, new questions, worse."

"So we cross now."

"We cross now." She drew a line with the pencil, north by northwest, through country she clearly knew well. Then she paused. "One complication. The line goes through Larchen. My brother's column was routed through Larchen in the autumn."

Dominic set down her cup. "Through your brother."

"Through the town, not the man. He'd be moved on by now — penal columns don't winter anywhere, that's rather the point of them." But her pencil had stopped, and she was looking at the dot that was Larchen the way cartographers look at places: measuring, weighing, pretending not to feel.

"Ilse."

"If we go around, it adds four days and a river ford. If we go through—" She exhaled. "If we go through, I might learn something. A rumor. A scrap. Whether the column went east or west. Whether anyone petitioned for releases, the way the tribunal said someone was doing." She looked up, and the firelight did something unguarded to her face. "You should know that I want to. You should also know that I won't ask us to."

"You just did ask."

"I asked honestly. Those are different." She tapped the map. "You choose. It's your ledger that got us this far, and your road we're on. I only draw the lines."

Dominic looked at the map — at the neat pencil route, the small defiant dot of Larchen, the vast unmarked whiteness beyond it that was, presumably, the rest of their lives. Eleven days ago she had been a girl with a secret and a shop. Now she was a nameless keeper on a church door, an exhibit, a footnote to trials she would never attend, traveling under forged passes with a deserter-cartographer whom she loved with the specific ferocity of someone who had been carried past three checkpoints and intended to repay the favor.

History, happening to them all along. Not elsewhere. Here. In the ink and the ice and the pencil line.

"Through Larchen," she said. "We'll take four days' risk to buy you one rumor. That's a fair rate. I learned bargaining from the best — my entire town owes each other money."

Ilse smiled and bent over the map, and the pencil moved, and outside the snow fell on the new flag over the church, and somewhere far south a compositor was setting the second extract sheet, rushing his W's, never knowing whose words they were.

It was nearly midnight when the innkeeper knocked — apologetic, stamping snow off his boots, holding a folded paper. "Beg pardon. Came up with the mail coach. No name on it, but the driver said the party asking had army manners." He handed it over and fled the particular silence that fell.

Ilse opened it. Read it once. Read it again. Then she passed it to Dominic without a word, and her hand was not quite steady.

The paper was a copy — a clerk's fair copy, official seal half-pressed, the ink of the new government still smelling of the press itself. Dominic read the heading twice before the letters would hold still:

*Notice of Petition for Release: penal transfer, name entered below...*

And below, in the entry line, written plainly where anyone could see it, was Ilse's brother's name — and beside it, in the column for petitioner, a phrase that made the stove seem suddenly very far away:

*Petitioner: the Keeper of the Vellamont Ledger, by proxy.*

"But I didn't—" Dominic began, and then stopped, because she remembered: the guild archivist, the last morning, the old woman with sharp eyes saying *leave the rest to us, Keeper; books make arguments better when their authors have left the room.* "Someone at the guild is filing petitions. Using the ledger's authority. Using *me*."

"Using your name," Ilse said softly, "without your permission. To free my brother." She looked at the fire, then at Dominic, and there was something in her face that had no business coexisting with exhaustion — something dangerously close to hope. "Dominic. The petition routes through the northern review board. Which sits at Larchen."

Dominic looked at the map. At the dot. At the impossible, converging, history-drunk geometry of it.

"Then we'd better hurry," she said. "Before the century turns without us."
