## 39. A New Hand

The town was called Grauhof, which everyone pronounced as though they were clearing their throat, and it sat close enough to the border that the church bells rang in two countries' time and were wrong in both. Dominic had been there eleven weeks when she first touched a piece of type metal that belonged to her.

She had told herself she would not do it. She had told herself this on the road north, telling herself being, by that point, something of a profession: *I will not cut type again. I will keep books for a wool merchant, or tend a garden, or learn to make candles.* Something honest and dull. Something with no history in it at all. A person could be born entirely out of a trade, she reasoned, the way a snail abandons a cracked shell, and go soft and nameless through the world.

She lasted until the second week of November, when Herr Fessler, whose print shop smelled of size glue and boiled linseed, dropped his case of eight-point brevier down the back stairs.

The sound alone nearly did it. Anyone who has heard a full case of type meet a stone stair knows the sound — a kind of metallic weeping, every letter crying out at once, the small characters and the capitals, the thin spaces and the quads, all of it mingled into one long syllable of grief. Dominic was passing the open door with a basket of bread. She stood very still. Her hands, entirely without her permission, began to itch.

"Young man," Fessler said from the doorway, rubbing his hip, "if you laugh, I will sack you before I hire you."

"I wasn't going to laugh."

"You were going to do worse. You were going to look at it the way my wife looks at a sick lamb." He sighed. "Can you sort?"

"Sort," said Dominic, "is an insult to what I can do."

Which was how she came to be kneeling in Fessler's back room at nine in the morning, sorting four hundred pounds of scrambled type back into its compartments, humming, before she remembered to be someone else.

* * *

Being someone else, she had discovered, required less art than she'd feared and more discipline. Her papers named her **Domenic Yarrow**, which had been Ilse's suggestion — *change only one letter, change nothing else, the best forgery is the truth wearing a hat* — and so she remained, officially, the Yarrow boy grown to a quiet manhood, late of Vellamont, lately of nowhere in particular. The town of Grauhof accepted this without interest. Border towns are connoisseurs of men with incomplete pasts; they collect them the way other towns collect saints' relics, and ask fewer questions.

What was harder was the body. In Vellamont she had spent eleven years folding herself small, dropping her voice, learning to walk like a man the way other people learn to pray — mechanically, out of duty, with occasional sincerity. Here nobody watched. And so she kept doing it anyway, out of habit, and then caught herself doing it, and then one gray afternoon in the market deliberately walked the length of the fish stalls with her shoulders loose and her stride her own, feeling like a criminal getting away with something, and bought a herring she didn't even want just to have a reason to keep moving.

"Your accent," said the fishwife, wrapping the unwanted herring. "South. River country?"

"A while ago."

"They say the river towns are burning."

"They say a lot of things," Dominic said, and paid, and left, and did not eat the herring.

* * *

Fessler's shop turned out to be the kind of place Dominic's father would have called *honest but deaf* — meaning the work was good and the proprietor had never once listened to the press. Fessler printed handbills, almanacs, funeral notices, and a weekly sheet called the *Grauhof Advertiser*, which contained, in descending order of reliability: grain prices, weather, and opinions.

"You sort well," Fessler admitted on her fourth day, watching her from the doorway with his arms crossed like a man conceding a chess game. "Suspiciously well. Where did you learn?"

"My father," Dominic said, which was true, which was dangerous, which she was going to have to get better at not saying. "He was in the trade."

"In the trade." Fessler chewed on this. "A printer."

"A cutter," said Dominic, and saw the word land on him like a cold drop of water.

"There are no cutters anymore," Fessler said. "Not really. Not since the foundries. It's a dead craft, young man. Like saddling unicorns."

"Someone has to saddle them," said Dominic, "or they stop existing," and Fessler looked at her for a long moment, and then, in the manner of printers everywhere who have just realized they are standing next to something rare, went and put the kettle on.

* * *

It happened gradually, the way rust happens, and then all at once, the way floods happen. First Fessler asked her opinion on a worn matrix. Then he asked whether she could repair a chipped lowercase g that had been annoying him since the previous administration. Then, on a Tuesday, he set before her a bar of type metal, a graver, and a look of suppressed greed, and said, "The almanac needs a title piece. Something with flourishes. My hands shake. My eyes are sixty-two years old. Yours, presumably, are not."

Dominic picked up the graver.

Her father's hands had been enormous, spatulate, scarred at every knuckle, and they moved over steel with the delicacy of a woman threading a needle in a dark church. Hers were smaller, quicker, less patient. But the memory lived in them — the angle of the cut, the way you had to think three strokes ahead because type metal does not forgive, the little click and curl of the chip coming away, the smell of it, tallow and mineral and something faintly sweet underneath, the smell she had known since before she had language for it.

She cut for two hours without speaking. When she finished and inked the proof and pulled it, the flourish came off the paper like handwriting, like breath, and Fessler held it up to the window light and said nothing at all for a very long time.

"Well," he said finally. "Unicorns."

"Don't tell anyone," said Dominic. "I have a reputation as a bookkeeper to maintain."

"Why?" said Fessler, with the bluntness of old men and children. "You're the worst bookkeeper I've ever met. You balance the column and then you stare out the window looking like someone buried you in it. But you cut like a seraph." He folded the proof with unexpected care. "Whatever name you're hiding under, son, the graver doesn't know about it."

Dominic looked at her hands. They were steady.

"No," she said. "It doesn't."

* * *

The ledger began, as most catastrophes and several redemptions do, with an argument about firewood.

Fessler's apprentice, a mournful boy called Ottokar whose entire personality was a cowlick, had been buying wood for the shop from his uncle at prices that would have embarrassed a bishop. Dominic found it in the accounts — she still did the books, because Fessler refused to learn arithmetic on principle and Ottokar refused to learn it out of temperament. She laid the figures before Fessler at lunch.

"That's theft," Fessler said.

"It's incompetence wrapped around theft. Which is more common and slightly sadder."

"What do you propose?"

And Dominic, without thinking, without any thought at all, said: "Enter it. Properly. Date, amount, restitution schedule. If he repays by spring we say nothing further. If he doesn't—" she shrugged "—then there's a record, and records do the arguing for you."

"Enter it where?"

That was the problem. Fessler kept his accounts in three exercise books, a biscuit tin, and his head, and the head portion was unreliable after the second glass. So that evening Dominic walked to the stationer's and bought, with a strange thudding heart, a ledger. New. Blank. Bound in gray boards, unremarkable, unhaunted. Two hundred pages with nothing written on them at all.

She carried it home through the first snow of the season and sat with it on her knees by the stove for an hour before opening it.

She had expected — she admitted this to herself honestly, because dishonesty with oneself was a debt that collected interest — she had expected the other book to rise up in her hands. The weight of it. Forty-one names. Her father's entry. The whole crowded, secret, indispensable history of a town that had mostly burned behind her. She had expected every blank page to feel like an accusation: *you had one book, and it cost you everything, and here you are buying another.*

But the pages were only pages. Clean, faintly ruled, smelling of sizing and patience. A ledger is not a conscience, her father used to say. It's a mirror. It shows whatever stands in front of it.

She dipped her pen. On the first page, in her best round hand — her father's hand, really, inherited along with the graver and the bad knee — she wrote:

*Ledger of Domenic Yarrow, printer's cutter, Grauhof. Begun the 4th of December, in the year 1800.*

Underneath, after a moment's thought:

*All entries true. All debts real. No aliases.*

And then, because it was her ledger and she could, and because some things need saying even to a gray board book:

*Ink outlives men. Let us see what it makes of me.*

* * *

The entries came slowly, and they were small, and they were glorious.

*12 December. Ottokar Fessler — firewood overpayment, 14 gulden, 3 kreuzer. Restitution: 2 gulden monthly, beginning January. Note: the boy cried. Note to self: be kinder when presenting columns.*

*19 December. Frau Hirt, widow, — one mourning broadsheet, price deferred "until times improve." Times will not improve for Frau Hirt. Marked forgiven. Do not tell her. Let her think she owes me; pride is also bread.*

*27 December. Herr Fessler — one bar English type metal, borrowed for cutting experiments. Repaid in labor. Also: he has begun calling me "his cutter" to visitors, in the tone of a man describing a racehorse. Charge accordingly.*

She laughed aloud writing that last one, alone in her rented room with the snow ticking at the shutters, and realized she had been laughing more generally these weeks — small laughs, startled out of her by Grauhof's small absurdities, by Fessler's feud with the bell-ringer, by Ottokar's ongoing war with the apostrophe. Grief had not left her. It had simply agreed to sit quietly in another chair.

There was one entry she delayed. She wrote it on the last night of the year, by candle, after the town's bells had done their confused dual-country ringing.

*31 December. Account current with myself, Domenic Yarrow, sometime of Vellamont. Assets: one graver, one lowercase d (melted edge, legible), a knowledge of forty-one names that is now public property, and a pair of hands that remember their father. Liabilities: one pardon, declined; one shop, burned; one heart, encumbered — pledged northward, to a cartographer, terms of repayment unspecified. Entered honestly. To be settled, God willing, in the new century.*

She blotted it, closed the book, and set it on the shelf above the stove, where it looked — she had to smile — exactly like what it was: the most boring object in the room. Nobody torturing a town would ever burn it. Nobody reading it would find a single secret worth keeping.

Except the last one. Except always the last one.

* * *

On the second of January, Fessler met her at the door of the shop with a newspaper in his fist and an expression she hadn't seen on him before — not anger, not fear. Recognition. The look of a man who has just seen a stranger wearing a friend's coat.

"You'd better sit," he said.

"I'll stand."

"The *Gazette*, from the city." He thrust it at her. "They're printing excerpts. Some tribunal record. A ledger entered as evidence — treason trials, pardons-for-sale, the whole rotten business read into the record. Half the chancelleries are quoting it, apparently. It's the cause of the season." His eyes narrowed. "They're calling it the Ledger of Vellamont."

Dominic took the paper. Her own hand stared back at her from the printed excerpt — typeset now, multiplied, immortal, her careful figures marching across three columns under a headline she couldn't finish reading because her eyes had gone hot and useless.

"Yarrow," Fessler said slowly, watching her face the way he watched a press pull badly, noting every small failure of alignment. "That's your name."

"It's my name," Dominic agreed. Her voice was remarkably level, considering.

"And the hand in those excerpts—"

"Is my father's," she said. "And mine."

Outside, the snow had stopped, and the bells were ringing noon in both countries, wrong in both, and somewhere far to the north, on a penal road she tried very hard not to imagine, a woman with ink-stained fingers and a cartographer's memory might be hearing the same rumor: that the book had survived, that the names had held, that the ink had outlived everyone who tried to bury it.

Dominic folded the newspaper, set it on the counter, and reached for her coat.

"Where are you going?" said Fessler.

"To buy every copy in Grauhof," she said, "before Ottokar uses one to wrap herring."
