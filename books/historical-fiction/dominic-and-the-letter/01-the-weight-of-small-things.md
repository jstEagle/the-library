## 1. The Weight of Small Things

The summons came at half past six in the morning, delivered by a boy who had run the whole way from the observatory hill and arrived at Mr. Hedley's shop front wheezing like a broken bellows. Rosalind heard him through the partition wall while she trued a hairspring, her tweezers steady even as her attention drifted to his ragged breathing and the muffled urgency of his message: *the master clock has stopped, and Master Alcott wants the Pemberton apprentice, the girl, the one who does the fine work.*

Not Rosalind Pemberton by name. The Pemberton apprentice. Even her labor was spoken of as belonging to someone else.

She finished the hairspring first. That was discipline, not coldness — a movement half-assembled was a movement ruined, and she would not leave a ruin behind to go chase a larger one. She set the tweezers in their groove, wiped her fingers on the apron that had been hers for three years and would likely be hers until the seams gave out, and only then untied it.

"I'm wanted at the observatory," she told Mr. Hedley, who grunted from behind his loupe without looking up. He was not unkind, exactly. He simply had never learned to see apprentices as anything other than hands attached to benches. She had stopped expecting more. Expectation, she had found, was the heaviest thing a person could own and the least useful.

The observatory stood on its knoll above the river town like a man craning his neck to watch the shipping lanes — white dome, black shutters, and below them the long room where the master clock governed everything: the astronomers' transit observations, the harbor chronometers, the church bells, the trains of ships' captains who came with hats in hand to set their watches by it. It was, in a very real sense, the town's heartbeat. And it had stopped.

* * *

The clock room smelled of oil, dust, cold stone, and beneath all of it something faintly sweet — old varnish, perhaps, or the ghost of whatever had been stored in the deep cases decades ago. Morning light came through the east windows in long gray bars and lay across the flagstones like planks she might walk on if she were lighter, or braver.

Master Alcott was waiting, a stooped astronomer with ink-stained cuffs and the particular helplessness of brilliant men confronted with machinery. "It stopped in the night," he said. "Between two and three, by the stars. No warning. I wound it as always." He said this last with an edge of wounded dignity, as though the clock had betrayed him personally.

"May I?" Rosalind asked, and opened the case without waiting for permission, because permission was a formality that slowed good work.

The master clock was a regulator, long-case, taller than she was, built before her birth by hands whose names were lost even to the observatory's ledgers. Its case was oak gone the color of dark honey, its brass movement heavy enough that opening the trunk door required both arms. She let the door swing wide and looked into the works the way a physician looks at a patient — whole first, then parts.

The pendulum hung still. That was the strangest part. A stopped clock has a silence different from other silences; it is a silence with a shape, the negative space where a tick ought to be. Rosalind had lived inside such silences for most of her nineteen years, and she felt this one settle over her shoulders like a coat.

"Nobody else is to touch it," she said. "I'll need the room."

"You'll need lunch," Alcott said, "and possibly dinner. It took three men a fortnight to install it."

"It will take me a day," said Rosalind, who did not know yet whether this was true, but knew that saying so made it likelier.

He left her alone. Most people, given the chance, left her alone. She had long ago decided to treat this as a professional courtesy rather than a verdict on her character.

* * *

She began, as she always began, by cataloguing what she owned.

This was a habit from the orphanage years and she had never managed to break it, though she no longer said the items aloud. In those days the counting had been survival: when they moved you — and they moved you, dormitory to infirmary to the punishment room with its whitewashed window, whenever the mood took the matrons — you needed to know what could be carried in ten minutes or less. Everything above that number was not yours in any way that mattered. Ownership, Rosalind had concluded at nine years old, was not a matter of deed or claim. It was a matter of weight.

So while her fingers found the click-spring on the trunk door and freed the crutch, her mind ran its familiar ledger:

One apprentice's apron, worn at the right hip where the file rubbed. One dress, brown, plus the better grey one kept in Hedley's storeroom against funerals and interviews. One comb, tortoiseshell, cracked, inherited from a mother she could not remember and therefore worth more than everything else combined and worth nothing to anyone else alive. One book — *Rudimentary Treatise on Clock and Watch Making*, purchased secondhand with three months of saved wages, its margins now crowded with her own hand. One small tin of tools, her own by purchase if not by law, containing broaches, tweezers, two screwdrivers ground down to fit her grip, a pegwood stick, and a loupe. Fifty-one shillings and fourpence sewn into the hem of the spare dress, because banks required signatures and references and histories, and she had none of these.

That was the sum. Apron, dresses, comb, book, tin, coins. If the world asked her to leave in ten minutes, she could do it in five, and no one who watched her go would ever guess she was carrying her entire life.

She wondered — not for the first time, but with the mild detachment of long practice — what it would be like to own something too heavy to carry. A bedstead. A chest of drawers. A chair that fit your back. People spoke of possessions as burdens, but Rosalind suspected the truth ran the other way: that the truly poor were the ones with nothing so permanent it could anchor them, and that she drifted through the world like a hull with no keel, quick and light and unable to hold a course against any wind at all.

Then she put the ledger away and turned to the clock, because the clock deserved her whole attention, and because work was the one conversation in which she had never once been at a loss.

* * *

The fault announced itself within the hour, though finding it and proving it took until mid-afternoon.

The suspension spring — a thin ribbon of steel no thicker than paper, holding the pendulum's whole rhythm — had fractured at the top fixing block. Not snapped clean; worse, it had developed a slow crack that let the pendulum's beat drift for weeks, losing fractions of a second per swing until the error compounded and the clock's own locking plate tripped the strike train wrong and jammed the whole mechanism in self-defense. The clock, in other words, had not failed suddenly. It had been failing quietly for a very long time, compensating, absorbing its own damage, until the damage outgrew it.

Rosalind sat back on her heels and looked at the broken spring lying in her palm like a dead moth's wing.

She understood this condition intimately.

There was steel in the shop that would serve. She chose a blank, annealed it in the little spirit flame until it glowed a soft dusk-red, and rolled it flat between polished plates, checking thickness with her micrometer at four points, then eight. She marked the drilling centers with a scriber, drilled the fixing holes under the slowest, most careful turns of the bow drill, and filed the slot that would receive the pendulum rod — filing, resting, filing again, testing the fit against the block until it slid home with the whisper of metal accepting metal. Then she hardened the spring in the flame and quenched it, and tempered it to a pale straw blue, watching the oxide colors crawl across the steel like weather seen from a great height: pale gold, deeper gold, and — *there* — straw. She quenched it and held it up to the window light.

A new part should not be beautiful, exactly. But she permitted herself the thought anyway. It was the color of early evening over water.

Fitting it took another hour: the crutch aligned, the beat set by ear. This was the part outsiders never believed — that a clock could be tuned like an instrument, that you listened for the *tick-tock* to space itself evenly, evenly, evenly, and adjusted the crutch a hair's breadth left or right until the intervals matched. An uneven beat wore a clock to death slowly. An even one could outlive its maker.

By lamplight — for the day had gone gray and the room had gone dim without her noticing — she set the pendulum swinging and counted the first sixty beats against her pocket watch. Then a hundred and twenty. Then she sat in the borrowed chair Alcott had left and listened to the master clock fill the room with its own weather.

Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock.

The silence with a shape was gone. In its place: motion, order, a heart beating in the house again. Rosalind discovered, somewhat to her embarrassment, that her eyes had gone hot. She blamed the lamp smoke, which was thin evidence, since the lamp burned clean.

*It is only,* she thought, *that I have been in this town three years and this is the first time I have fixed something that everyone will notice.* At Hedley's shop her work went out under his name, into watches owned by strangers, repaired so well they forgot the repair had happened. Invisible competence. A life of small perfect joints that showed nowhere.

She ate the bread and cheese in her satchel without tasting it, and stayed late, cleaning the movement tooth by tooth with pegwood, because the clock was warm now and she was not ready to go back to her cold room above the tannery office, where the landlady's television of family life — no, that was not the word, her *tableau*, her theater of family life — played through the floorboards every night: a father's laugh, a child's running feet, a spoon against a bowl. Sounds she catalogued too, though she never entered them in the morning ledger. Some weights you carried whether or not you could lift them.

* * *

It was near ten o'clock when she saw the panel.

She had been wiping down the inside of the case with a rag — final ritual, leave the works cleaner than you found them — and the lamplight caught it wrong. Or right. Along the left interior wall of the trunk, below the counterweights, the shadow fell at an angle that the wood grain did not explain. She moved the lamp closer.

There. A seam, hairline-fine, describing a rectangle perhaps the length of her hand. She pressed one fingertip to it and felt the faintest give — a loose piece in a case that was otherwise tight as a drumhead everywhere else, every joint dovetailed and glued by some dead craftsman who had taken pride in permanence. Someone had made this panel to move. Someone had made it to hide.

Her pulse did something inconvenient.

Old clocks hid things; she knew this from the trade. Watch cases with false backs, snuffbox movements concealing letters — the eighteenth century had loved its secrets, and horologists had been their accomplices, building concealment as elegantly as escapements. Whatever lay behind this panel had lain there through decades of winding, of oiling, of men like Alcott leaning close with their loupes, and none of them had ever seen it, because none of them had been looking at shadows at ten o'clock at night, alone, in a room full of ticking.

She could open it in ten seconds. She was alone; no one would know.

And that was precisely why she would not. Not tonight, not here, not on a summons and a stranger's trust. She had been lent this room, this clock, this confidence, on the strength of her reputation — the one possession she owned that weighed nothing and carried everything. A girl who pried open hidden panels in her employer's client's property after hours was a girl who deserved her locked doors and her suspicious silences and her hem-sewn shillings, because she had made herself the kind of person people locked things away from.

Besides — and this was the true reason, the one she examined only briefly — she was tired, and lonely, and curious, and that combination of conditions had gotten better people than her into trouble.

Rosalind straightened the rag, closed the trunk door, and latched it. The clock ticked on behind the oak, steady, patient, keeping its secrets the way it kept its time.

She banked the lamp, gathered her tin of tools — apron, dress, comb, book, tin, coins; she would come back tomorrow to finish regulating the strike train, and the panel would keep, panels always kept — and let herself out into the cold.

Behind her, in the dark observatory, the master clock counted the seconds toward something none of them could yet measure: three days until the ice began to groan on the river, and a letter older than her apprenticeship waited in the dark, sealed and patient, for the only pair of hands in the town fine enough — and honest enough, or desperate enough — to find it.

But that night Rosalind walked home light, carrying everything she owned, and heard nothing behind her but the ticking.
