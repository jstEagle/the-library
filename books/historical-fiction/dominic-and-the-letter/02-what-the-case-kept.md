## 2. What the Case Kept

The observatory at night was a different creature entirely.

By day it belonged to the astronomers — all shuffling slippers and ink-stained cuffs, brass instruments catching light like small suns, voices pitched to the register of lecture halls. But at half past ten, when Rosalind let herself in through the tradesman's door with the key Mr. Hargrove had pressed into her palm that afternoon, she entered a kingdom of shadow and starlight. The great dome overhead breathed. Somewhere above her, wind moved across the copper skin of it with a low, oceanic moan, and the whole structure seemed to settle on its foundations like a sleeping animal drawing its feet beneath it.

She lit no lamp beyond the small shuttered lantern she carried, its glow narrowed to a coin of gold on the flagstones. There was no need for more. She knew the way to the master clock as she knew the way to her own bed — better, if she was honest, since her bed changed depending on which landlady had failed to pay the rent and which garret had opened up on which street. The clock stood where she had left it that morning: against the north wall, in its tall case of blackened walnut, patient as a monk at prayer.

"I'm back," she said to it softly, and heard the absurd comfort of her own voice in the empty room.

She had told Mr. Hargrove she would need two evenings. This was true in the way that all professional estimates were true — generous enough to protect her reputation, honest enough not to insult anyone's intelligence. In fact the escapement needed perhaps three hours of careful work: the anchor had worn unevenly against the escape wheel, and each tooth was releasing a fraction late, so that the clock lost four seconds a day. Four seconds. To an astronomer it might as well have been an hour. An observatory clock that could not be trusted to the second was a broken thing wearing the costume of a working one, and Rosalind had been raised — such as she was raised — by men who believed that a clockmaker's first duty was to time itself.

So she spread her tools on the cloth she'd brought, unwound her muffler, and set to work by lantern-light while the wind sang over the dome and the stars wheeled behind stone walls she could not see.

It was good work. It was the kind of work that made the rest of thinking unnecessary.

She removed the pendulum first, cradling it in the crook of her arm like an infant — a habit from her apprenticeship, when old Mr. Fennick had rapped her knuckles for letting one swing loose against a case and chip the lacquer. *A pendulum remembers every indignity,* he used to say. *Treat it like something with feelings and it will keep faith with you.* Then the hands came off, then the dial, then the movement itself, lifted free on its seatboard and set before her so she could see the escape wheel turning its slow silver circle under her lens.

And because her hands were occupied but her mind was free, she did what she always did. She counted.

Not teeth tonight — the counting of teeth was for daytime, for diagnosis. Tonight she counted what she owned, the way she did whenever the world grew quiet enough to hear herself living in it. It was a habit so old she could not remember forming it, the way some people could not remember learning to walk. Everything she possessed, catalogued and weighed, held in the ledger of her skull where no fire or flood or landlord's temper could lose the entries.

One set of fine tools, inherited, thirty-one pieces, the gravers rehandled twice. One loupe, cracked at the rim but sound at the center. One coat, good wool; one coat, everyday. Two shirts, three shifts, one pair of boots resoled in October. Her mother's ring, too small for any of her fingers, worn on a cord at her throat. The tin box with her papers: her indenture papers, her mother's death record, the letter from the Pembertons' solicitor that had summoned her here, folded soft as cloth from handling. Eleven pounds, six shillings, threepence — though the threepence was currently in the pocket of her other coat, so call it eleven pounds, six shillings.

Everything she owned would fit in one trunk and one satchel. She had proved this twice, once fleeing a fire in Cheapside and once fleeing nothing more than a look on a landlady's face, and there was a strange liberty in it. Other people accumulated. They bought sideboards and pictures and chairs with carved legs that gathered dust in corners, and then those things owned them right back, rooting them to houses and streets and obligations. Rosalind carried her whole life in two loads and could be gone by morning. That was safety. That was freedom, of a kind.

That was loneliness, of a kind, too, though she had never once called it that aloud, even alone, even to the clocks, which kept her secrets better than any confessor.

*Eleven pounds, six shillings, minus the rent owed at Mrs. Quill's through Saturday,* she thought, filing the last burr from the pallet face, *which brings us to eleven pounds, one shilling, and the threepence still wandering.*

The escapement went back together like a sentence completing itself. She oiled it — one drop, no more; oil was cheaper than reputation but only just — and remounted the movement, and set the pendulum swinging, and put her ear close to the case to listen.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

Even. Steady. Four seconds a day lost no longer. She felt the small fierce satisfaction that good work always gave her, purer than any other pleasure she knew, and began to replace the dial.

That was when her lantern caught it.

She had noticed the panel that morning, of course — she noticed everything about a clock case; noticing was the trade entire. A seam in the walnut below the movement, hairline-fine, running vertical then hooking left. She had taken it for a joint, aged and sprung. But now, dial in hand, angling the light, she saw what she had missed in the flat daylight: the faint ghost of a finger-worn edge, a place where the wood was polished smoother than the wood around it. Not sprung. Made. A door, cut flush and cleverly, by someone who understood cases as well as she did — better, maybe, because they had hidden their understanding inside another man's clockwork.

Rosalind crouched there a long moment, dial forgotten in her hands, and listened to the clock tick out its corrected, faithful seconds.

Curiosity is a professional hazard in her line. Every apprentice learned early that the itch to open things — cases, drawers, other people's correspondence boxes — must be mastered like a fear of heights, because a clockmaker who pried where she ought not to pry soon found her name whispered out of workshops and her bench empty. She had never once given in to it. She was proud of that. Thirteen years old and orphaned and hungry in her first year out, and she had repaired a magistrate's traveling chronometer without opening the secret drawer in its base, though she'd known it was there, though she'd lain awake wondering.

But this was different, she told herself. This was her own repair. If a panel in a case she had been hired to restore was loose — and it *was* loose, she could feel that much, the seam breathing slightly when she pressed near it — then leaving it was negligence, not discretion. A sprung panel would warp with the damp. It would gape by summer. Any competent successor would find it and wonder why she hadn't.

The reasons arranged themselves neatly, the way they always did when a person wanted them to. She knew this about herself even as she reached for the thin blade of the case knife. She knew it, and she did it anyway.

The panel gave with a sigh of old wax and slid into her hand.

Behind it, the case was hollow — a shallow cavity lined with felt gone grey with age, and resting in the felt, square and pale as a bone, a letter. Sealed with red wax, cracked but unbroken, stamped with a signet she did not recognize: a wheel, or a compass rose, or both. On its face, in a clerk's hand — she knew a clerk's hand at a glance, that disciplined, economical slope — three lines:

*To whoever finds this. Read it, act on it, and God forgive us all.*

*Before the ice breaks.*

*D.V.*

Her first thought, absurdly, was arithmetic: how long had this been here? The felt was faded evenly, no brighter rectangle where the letter had shielded it — so years, then, not months. Years of astronomers adjusting dials and winding weights a hand's width away from it. Years of it waiting behind walnut thinner than a hymn book.

Her second thought was that she should put it back exactly as she'd found it and go home and sleep and never think of it again.

She did not do that. Her hands — traitors, always, to whatever her head decided — turned the letter over. And in turning it, the cracked seal broke fully along its fault line, quietly, with a sound like a stepped-on twig, far too loud in the dark of the dome.

She should stop. She should stop now.

She opened it.

The paper inside was thin, written close on both sides, the ink browned at the folds. The hand was beautiful and it was hurried, and the hurry frightened her more than anything else, because a man writes calmly about weather and taxes, and like this only about things that are hunting him.

*I, Dominic Vasseur, chief clerk to the River Customs Commission, being in my right mind and short of time—*

She read the first page standing. She read the second sitting down on the floor of the observatory with her back against the clock case, because her legs had made their own decision without consulting her. The lantern sat between her knees and threw her shadow enormous against the movement, so that a giantess crouched in the corner of the room, listening.

Smuggling. A ring moving untaxed goods upriver under commission papers — real commissions, stolen authority, the kind of protection that turned contraband into cargo simply by writing it down. Names followed, names with titles attached, and then, halfway down the third page, a name she knew only as everyone in the city knew it, the way you know the shape of a spire on the skyline:

*and because I could not refuse them and could not join them, I did the next cowardly thing: I lent them the Pemberton name. Their documents bear it. Their ledgers carry it as partner and guarantor. The Pembertons are honest people, and I have signed them into a conspiracy they have never heard of, because they have what I lack — a name worth stealing, and money worth seizing, and enemies already positioned to destroy them when the ring is found. When it collapses, it will collapse onto them, and I will be dead and safe from the wreckage, and they will not be. So I have hidden the truth — the true ledger, the sworn statements, the proof — where only I know, and I seal this confession here, in the one place in this city where time is kept honestly, and I beg whoever finds it: deliver it, and deliver them, before the ice breaks. After the ice breaks the river opens, and when the river opens the *Meridian* sails, and everything I have hidden goes to the bottom of the sea with the men who paid me to drown.*

There was more — instructions, half-legible, a reference to checkpoints and to a granddaughter named Marguerite who was not to be trusted, or perhaps was to be trusted above all others; the sentence contradicted itself, struck through, rewritten in a shakier hand, as if he had composed it across weeks or months while his body failed him by inches.

Rosalind read it twice. Then she became aware that the cold of the flagstones had climbed through her skirts into her spine, and that her breath was visible in the lantern light, and that somewhere above her the wind had stopped, and the silence was worse.

Dread is a specific sensation, she thought distantly. It has weight. It settles in the body like ballast, low and central, and it does not care what you own or whether it fits in one trunk. She had felt it perhaps three times in her life: the night of the Cheapside fire; the day the indenture papers were signed and she understood what she was signing away; and now, on the floor of an observatory, holding a dead or dying man's confession with her own fingerprints settling into the wax.

Because she saw it clearly, the way she could see the workings of any mechanism laid open before her: this letter was not information. It was contagion. Whoever held it became part of its story — finder, accomplice, heir. The Pembertons' name was forged onto it, and hers was now written nowhere and everywhere, in the only ledger that mattered. Put it back? The seal was broken. Her hands had touched it. There was no version of the world in which she climbed into her narrow bed at Mrs. Quill's and woke tomorrow as the woman who had found nothing behind a panel in a clock.

*Deliver it, and deliver them, before the ice breaks.*

She looked up at the clock she had just healed. Its pendulum swung in the dark case, steady as conscience, keeping perfect time for a city full of people who would never know how many small failures had been mended to make their minutes trustworthy.

The ice on the river, she thought, had already begun to groan this year. The ferrymen said it was the earliest break in a decade.

She folded the letter along its original creases, wrapped it in the felt, and slid it inside her shirt, against her skin, where it lay cold as a coin for perhaps a minute before it warmed to her and became, irreversibly, hers to carry.

Then she rose, took up her lantern, and went to find the house of Pemberton — hoping, with a fervency that surprised her, that villains would answer the door, because villains she could manage.

They were not villains. That was the trouble. That, and everything after it.
