## 3. A Name She Never Chose

Rosalind had always believed that a name was a thing you could weigh. Hers, Pemberton, had come to her secondhand — pinned to her at the foundling home because a woman had left nothing else, no locket, no letter, only the word scrawled on a scrap of paper that smelled of lamp oil and river water. She had carried it for nineteen years like a coin of uncertain currency, spending it carefully, never quite sure what it would buy.

Now it might buy her a hanging.

She spent two days telling herself she would not go to Harrow Lane at all. The letter sat where she had hidden it — inside the hollow brass weight of her own practice clock, wrapped in oilcloth — and every night she lay in her narrow room above Mr. Fennick's shop and listened to it tick, or thought she did, though weights do not tick and neither do secrets. They simply wait.

But curiosity was a mainspring in her, wound too tight to rest, and so on the third morning she put on her good coat, the one with the mended cuff hidden under her sleeve, and told Mr. Fennick she had an errand for a private client. He grunted without looking up. Lies came easily when you were nobody; nobody checked the errands of nobodies.

Harrow Lane climbed away from the harbor in a long curve, and the Pemberton house stood at its crest like something that had decided, against all fashion, to remain beautiful. It was brick gone soft rose with age, its windows tall and many-paned, ivy trained along one side in careful rows as if even the garden had been taught its manners. A brass knocker shaped like an acorn. A door painted the deep green of bottle glass.

She had expected, somehow, something furtive. Shutters closed. A house that knew it was guilty. Instead there was a girl of perhaps ten chasing a cat across the front steps, laughing, and a woman in a gray apron beating a rug with rhythmic, unembarrassed thumps, and from an open window came the smell of bread and the sound of someone — someone weeping quietly, the way people weep when grief has become furniture in the house.

Rosalind stood at the gate with her heart going like an escapement out of beat.

*Smugglers,* she reminded herself. *Forgers of their own name onto bills of lading, bribers of customs men.* But the woman beating the rug had kind eyes and called the girl back from the wall with such gentleness that Rosalind's certainty frayed at the edges like old thread.

She made herself walk up the path. "I'm looking for Mr. Edmund Pemberton," she said to the woman, and heard her own voice come out smaller than intended. "On — family business."

The woman looked her over, taking in the coat, the mended cuff, the calluses on her fingers that no glove quite hid. Whatever she concluded, she kept it behind a polite face. "Wait here, miss."

* * *

Edmund Pemberton received her in a study lined with books that had actually been read — spines cracked, pages dog-eared, a telescope by the window with the lacquer worn off its focusing knob from use. He was younger than she expected, maybe forty, with his sister's tired eyes and ink stains on his cuffs that matched her own in shape if not in cause. When he saw her hands he said, "You're a tradeswoman," not as a question but as a greeting, and something in Rosalind's chest turned over, because no one ever *saw* her hands first.

"Apprentice clockmaker," she said. "Rosalind Pemberton."

She watched the name land. Watched it land badly.

He went very still. His hand, reaching for the teapot, stopped mid-air, and for three full seconds the only sound in the room was the mantel clock — a good bracket clock, she noted automatically, Verge escapement, slightly over-wound — marking the silence in small deliberate strokes.

"Say that again," he said quietly.

"Rosalind Pemberton." Her mouth was dry. "I know how that sounds. I know what you must think—"

"What I must think," he repeated, and now his voice had a strange, strained quality, half laugh and half grief, "is that I have spent eleven years and most of my late wife's fortune trying to prove my family innocent of a crime I don't fully understand, and God has sent me a stranger wearing my own name like a key." He sat down heavily. "Who are you? Truly."

So she told him. The foundling home. The scrap of paper. The mother who had never come back. She had never spoken the whole of it aloud in order before — it had always come out in pieces, apologetic, trimmed to fit other people's patience — but something about this room, these read books, this man who looked at her hands, made her give it whole.

When she finished, he did not pity her. That was the first miracle. He simply nodded slowly, the way a man nods when a mechanism finally explains its own fault.

"And you came here," he said, "because of the Vasseur letter."

Her blood went cold. "You know about it?"

"I know Dominic Vasseur wrote something before he got sick. I've known Dominic thirty years — he was my father's head clerk, he kept our books straighter than scripture. And I know that six months ago men started following me. That my shipments began arriving inspected. That my name — *our* name — appears on documents I have never signed, for cargo I have never seen." He leaned forward. "I do not know what's in that letter. But you do. Don't you."

Rosalind realized then that she had already decided. Somewhere between the acorn knocker and the weeping behind the window and this man's ink-stained cuffs, she had decided. She reached into her satchel and drew out the oilcloth packet and laid it on the desk between them like a surgeon laying out an instrument.

"I found it in the observatory clock," she said. "Dominic Vasseur sealed it himself. It names the men who forged your name — and it says where the proof is."

Edmund Pemberton did not grab for it. He looked at it the way a starving man looks at bread he doesn't yet believe is real. Then he stood, took up the packet, and said, "Come. If you've read it, then you know he's dying. And if you know he's dying—" his voice caught, steadied, "—then you know there isn't much time."

* * *

They went upstairs together, past the woman in gray — *Mrs. Row, the housekeeper*, Edmund said, *she raised me after my mother died* — and past the window where the quiet weeping had come from, and into a back bedroom where the curtains were half drawn against the light and an old man lay beneath quilts like a man already half-finished, like a sketch the artist had abandoned.

Dominic Vasseur was small now. Illness had pared him down to essentials: bright eyes, working hands, a mind that still moved fast behind failing lungs. His breath came in a rhythm Rosalind recognized instantly and hated herself for recognizing — the wet, irregular tick of a mechanism near its end.

Beside the bed sat a young woman Rosalind's own age, dark-haired, straight-backed, with her grandfather's eyes set in a harder face. She was reading aloud from a ledger — actually reading ledger entries to a dying man, which told Rosinald more about this family in one stroke than an hour of questions could — and she rose when they entered, positioning herself between them and the bed like a drawn bolt.

"Papa. Who is this?"

"A friend," Edmund said. "Of a complicated sort."

"Marguerite," the old man said, and the word cost him air, and the young woman was beside him instantly, adjusting pillows, furious with tenderness. His eyes found Rosalind's over his granddaughter's shoulder. "You found it. In the clock."

"Yes," Rosalind said.

"You opened a panel that wasn't yours to open."

"I repaired what was mine to repair. The panel was loose. I noticed it. I came back for it." She lifted her chin. "Curiosity is part of the trade, sir. A clock that keeps a secret usually keeps a fault."

Something flickered across the ruined face — she would swear later it was approval — but Marguerite turned sharply.

"She's been reading it? Does everyone in this city know my grandfather's business while he lies dying?" Her voice was low and hard. "Papa, she's a stranger. An apprentice off the street carrying our name around like a — like a forged coin herself, if you'll forgive me. How do we know she wasn't sent?"

"By whom?" Edmund asked gently.

"By *them*. To see how much we know. To find the letter." Marguerite's gaze raked Rosalind. "You want honesty, apprentice? Here it is. Three weeks ago someone searched our shipping office. Last month a man asked questions about Grandfather at the ferry toll. We are being watched, and you appear at our gate the same week the letter disappears from the observatory. Coincidence is a luxury we can't afford."

"Margo," Dominic whispered.

"No. She reads your confession, she knows everything, and we're simply to trust her because she has the right name? Names can be *made*, Grandfather. You of all people should know."

The words hung there, cruel and true, and Rosalind felt them land in the old bruised place where every orphan keeps their tenderest accounting. But she had been measured and found wanting before; she knew how to stand still under the gauge.

"You're right not to trust me," she said quietly. "Trust takes time, and time is the one thing your grandfather hasn't got. So don't trust me. Verify me. Send someone to Mr. Fennick's shop on Candle Street; ask about the apprentice who re-bushed the observatory's master clock last week. Ask the observatory keeper why he paid twice what the job was worth. And then ask yourself—" she held Marguerite's gaze, "—if I'd been sent by conspirators, would I have come through your front door and announced my name to the whole household? Would I have handed the letter to your father in your own study? People sent to hunt don't knock."

Silence. The mantel below struck the quarter hour, faint through the floors.

Marguerite didn't yield — Rosalind could see she was not a woman who yielded — but something in her stance recalculated, a gear engaging rather than releasing.

Then Dominic Vasseur reached out his hand, and Rosalind took it. His grip was astonishingly strong for all its frailty, a clerk's grip, made by decades of pens and ledgers.

"They forged our name onto their dirty papers," he rasped, each word bought with breath, "because ours was clean. Clean names are worth money, girl — did you know that? A respectable name opens customs doors, buys silence, launders cargo. They used *Pemberton* the way a thief uses a stolen coat." His eyes shone. "And here's a girl grown up wearing the true coat, honest, with clever hands and no family to protect her. God has a wicked sense of humor and a perfect sense of justice."

"There's evidence," Rosalind said. "Your letter says where. The originals — the real ledgers, the real signatures — they're being shipped out on the *Meridian* before the ice breaks. Once they're at sea, there's no case. Your name stays dirty forever."

"Then it must not reach the sea." He pulled her closer, and she bent to hear. "Three checkpoints on the river road, child. Customs house. Ferry toll. Garrison bridge. Each one has a man on their payroll, though only the bridge knows the full truth. The evidence must reach Magistrate Corven at Thornmouth before the *Meridian* passes the last bend — and I—" the cough took him then, terrible and long, and Marguerite was there with a cloth and medicine and murder in her eyes for whoever had caused this, "—I cannot go. I cannot even sit up. So it must be you. Or it must be no one, and my name dies with me, and this family drowns in another family's crime."

He let go of her hand and lay back, exhausted, his eyes moving from Rosalind to his granddaughter and back, as if watching two gears approach mesh.

"I didn't choose this name," Rosalind said softly. "Any of it. I never chose to carry it."

"No," Dominic agreed, closing his eyes. "That's what makes you the one to save it. Thieves protect what they stole. You'll protect what was *given*."

Mrs. Row appeared with tea — real tea, fragrant, in cups with gilt rims that had chips lovingly left unthrown-away — and pressed a warm cup into Rosalind's hands as though she'd lived in this house for years. The little girl appeared in the doorway with the cat and was shooed out, laughing. Below, somewhere, the mantel clock ticked on, steady, keeping faith.

Kindness, Rosalind thought, holding the cup too tightly. She had braced herself all her life against cruelty and knew all its calibrations. No one had ever taught her what to do with kindness from strangers — strangers bound to her by a forged name and a dying man's desperate arithmetic.

Marguerite returned from the bedside, arms folded, and looked at her for a long moment.

"My grandfather trusts you," she said at last, flatly. "That is not the same as my trusting you. Come downstairs, apprentice. If we're truly going to do this mad thing, you and I need to talk terms — and you should know that I intend to be the one setting them."
