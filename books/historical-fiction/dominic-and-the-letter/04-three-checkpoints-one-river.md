## 4. Three Checkpoints, One River

I have spent forty years among ledgers, and I know this: a river is only arithmetic moving slowly. Water finds its level the way debt finds its debtor, and in the spring of 1847, the level was rising against me faster than the ice could melt.

They carried me to the window chair at two o'clock, because I insisted, because a man who cannot walk will still insist on the small tyrannies left to him. From that chair I could see the slate roof of the Pemberton house next door and, beyond it, the grey seam of the river between the warehouses. The girl sat across from me with her hands folded like instruments waiting to be used — Rosalind Pemberton, though she was no more a Pemberton than I was a bishop, an apprentice with ink on her knuckles and a letter in her apron pocket that had already begun to weigh on her the way iron weighs.

Marguerite had let her in grudgingly, standing at the door of my sickroom like a sentry who had not been consulted about the war. Good. Let her stand there. A sentry who doubts is twice as watchful as one who trusts.

"Tell me what you saw," I said to Rosalind, "when you read my letter. Not what it said. What you saw."

She considered the question longer than most people would have. That was promising. Foolish people answer before they understand; careful people understand before they answer.

"I saw a dying man's hand," she said at last. "The writing started even and got worse toward the bottom, like the pen was heavier each line."

"The pen *was* heavier each line." I laughed, and the laughing cost me something I did not have to spare. "Then you will do. Now listen, girl, and listen as though your life depends on it, because it does — and so does a name bigger than both our lives put together."

I made Marguerite bring the atlas, the old one with the frayed corners, and I opened it across my knees though I could not lift it properly, and I began, as I had begun so many explanations in my life as a clerk, with the geography.

"The evidence — the true ledger, the originals with the forgeries beside them — sits in a strongbox at Harrow & Finch, on the far bank. It must reach Magistrate Corliss, who has chambers on this side and no love for the men I am about to name. Between here and there lie three crossings, three doors, and behind each door stands a man whose salary is paid twice: once by the Crown, once by the ring."

I touched the first mark with my finger, which trembled; I pretended it did not.

"*The customs house.* Quay Street. Inspector Aldous Crane. A fat, careful man who weighs everything twice — cargo, coin, and suspicion. He is not cruel, which makes him worse, because cruelty can be predicted and greed cannot. He opens cases at his own pace. He cannot be hurried, but he *can* be bored. Remember that."

Rosalind's eyes moved as I spoke, and I realized she was not watching the map but my finger — reading the tremor the way she would read the wobble in a wheelwork. Clockmakers and clerks, we are the same trade at heart. We both live and die by the small motions.

"*The ferry toll.* Old Marn's crossing, mid-river. The collector is Josiah Fenwick. Younger than Crane, hungrier. He knows faces, especially the faces of families he has helped to ruin. If either of you carries a name anyone recognizes, it is the Pemberton name and mine, and Fenwick holds a list of both." I looked at Marguerite when I said it, and she lifted her chin, and I loved her so fiercely in that moment that I had to look away, back to the map, back to the work. Love, at my age and in my condition, is an unaffordable luxury. But I paid it anyway. I have always been careless with what little I have left.

"*The garrison bridge.* The last door, and the strongest. Sergeant Hale commands the checkpoint, or appears to; in truth he is commanded by the same men who command Crane and Fenwick. Soldiers at his shoulder, orders in his pocket, and a warrant — I promise you — already drawn up, waiting only for a description of whoever comes carrying what we are about to carry."

Silence, then. The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the house breathed — a grieving house, half-empty, full of the ghosts of Mrs. Pemberton's music and Mr. Pemberton's laughter, both of them eleven years drowned and the children still setting places for them at table. I had done that to them. My signature, my cowardice, my silence of nine years. Every tick of that fire was a year I owed.

"So," Rosalind said quietly. "Three men bought and paid for, standing between us and the magistrate. And you are asking two women — an apprentice and—" she glanced at Marguerite, corrected herself, "—and a lady — to walk past all three."

"I am not asking," I said. "I am teaching. Whether you walk is yours to decide. But if you walk, you will walk as clerks walk, and I will show you how."

I had Marguerite fetch the instrument case from the shelf — the brass barometer awaiting repair, and the clockmaker's own case with its velvet-lined compartments. And then, because my hands were failing but my memory was not, I taught her the oldest trick in the clerk's poor arsenal: how paper hides inside honesty.

"A searcher expects falsehood to be hidden," I said. "So he searches the dark places — the false bottoms, the hollow handles, the lining torn back. He does not search the truth, because the truth requires him to *know things*, and lazy men do not know things. So." I lifted the barometer's faceplate with a fingernail, showing the shallow channel behind the dial where the mechanism sat. "A barometer is delivered to be repaired. Its owner expects it opened. An inspector may open it — but what does he see? Brass, glass, a needle. He does not know what a barometer's interior ought to look like, any more than I know what the inside of a cathedral looks like. Paper, folded thin and laid flat against the case wall beneath the movement plate, becomes invisible precisely because it sits in plain sight among honest machinery."

Rosalind leaned forward. Her fingers itched, I could see — the fingers of someone who takes things apart to learn their griefs. "And the seal?" she asked. "Your letter is sealed. Sealed letters invite opening."

"Ah." I smiled, and I felt the old clerk's pleasure rise in me like warmth into cold hands. "Sealed letters invite opening when they look important. Wax the colour of blood, ribbon, a crest — those are theatre, and theatre gets searched. But a tradeswoman's delivery note? Folded paper, dirty thumb-mark, addressed in a plain hand to a shop that everyone knows is expecting a delivery? No one opens that. Boredom, girl. Boredom is the best guard ever invented. We will weaponize dullness."

For an hour I taught them, and they learned differently, the two of them, and I watched the difference the way I had once watched the difference between two entries in a ledger that should have matched. Marguerite learned like a duelist — angles, exits, what to say and when to say nothing. But Rosalind learned like a mechanic. She took the barometer apart without being asked and put it back together with the folded paper seated behind the dial, and then took it apart again to do it better, faster, her hands sure as water finding a channel.

"You've done this before," Marguerite said, suspicious as ever.

"I've repaired clocks," Rosalind answered. "It's the same problem. Something valuable in a case too small for it, and everything depending on the fit."

I closed my eyes briefly. Nine years I had carried this guilt, and in one sentence an orphan apprentice had described my whole crime better than any judge could have. Something valuable — the Pembertons' good name — forced into a case too small for it — forged papers, false accounts — and everything depending on the fit. Yes. That was the smuggling ring entire. Men who built lies the way she built escapements, with tolerances fine enough to run undetected for years.

But every mechanism fails eventually. Every escapement slips a tooth. Mine had slipped, at the end, and the letter was the tooth falling.

"It will be spring soon," I said, when the light through the window had gone the colour of pewter. "When the ice breaks, the ring's ship goes downriver with the season's cargo — and with it, every document that could convict them, every copy they hold. After the thaw, proof dies. Before the thaw, proof lives. So you have days, not weeks. You must go within—"

A cough took me then, the bad kind, the kind that feels like a hand wringing out a cloth, and I felt Marguerite's arms around my shoulders and heard her voice going thin with fear, calling for the nurse, and through the roaring in my ears I gripped Rosalind's wrist — hard, harder than I meant to, hard enough that I saw her flinch and hold still anyway, hold still like a good instrument under a rough hand.

"Days," I rasped, when I could. "Not weeks. And girl — if I die before you return, do not stop. Do not turn back for a funeral. Promise me the ledger reaches Corliss if it costs you everything you own. Count what you're carrying and tell me it is worth more than what I am."

Something moved behind her eyes — that inventory-taking look, the orphan's arithmetic, the habit of measuring life by what can be carried. I had seen her do it when she thought no one was watching: lips barely moving, cataloguing. Now she did it openly, and I watched her count, and I watched the count come out in favour of the dead man's name and the living family next door.

"It's worth more," she said. "I'll carry it."

"And I'm coming with you," said Marguerite, in a voice that closed the subject like a door.

I lay back then, emptied, lighter than I had been in nine years. Outside, far off, deep under the river's grey skin, something groaned — long and low, the sound of winter losing its grip a fortnight early. Too early. Far too early.

The ice was breaking sooner than any of us had reckoned.
