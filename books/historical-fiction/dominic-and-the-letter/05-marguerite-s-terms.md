## 5. Marguerite's Terms

Marguerite had watched the clockmaker's hands for three days before she trusted them, and even then only in the way one trusts a blade left on a table — carefully, and with an eye to the door.

The girl worked at the long table beneath the south window, where the light fell cleanest, disassembling Marguerite's grandfather's marine chronometer into a constellation of small bright parts laid out on white cloth. She handled each piece as though it were a word she was learning by heart. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow; her wrists were thin as the fusee chains she mended. She did not hum. She did not sigh. She worked the way some people pray.

*She could be anyone,* Marguerite thought, standing in the doorway with a lamp she had no reason to carry at midday. *A spy sent by Halloran. A girl bought for six pounds and a promise. Orphan apprentices are cheaper than secrets.*

"You've been watching me since Tuesday," Rosalind said, without looking up. Her voice was flat as a file's face, but not unkind. "You watch like my old master did when he suspected me of stealing. If it helps, I have never stolen anything but sleep."

"I wasn't aware I required your assessment of my character," Marguerite said.

"No. But you require an assessment of mine." Rosalind set down her tweezers and looked up at last. Her eyes were grey as pewter, patient as pendulums. "So test me. You've been circling it for days. Ask what you actually want to ask."

Marguerite set the lamp down harder than she intended. The flame guttered and righted itself, and she thought, absurdly, of her grandfather upstairs, his breath going in and out like something wound too tight and running down.

"Very well." She pulled out a chair and sat opposite, arms folded. "Why you? Of all the clockmakers in this city, why does my grandfather's letter come to *you*? He hid that paper for two years. He chose that clock, that panel, that observatory. And then he tells his nurse to summon a seventeen-year-old apprentice nobody has ever heard of, to repair a mechanism that any journeyman could have fixed. Explain that to me as if I were simple."

Rosalind was quiet for a moment. Outside, a cart went by, and the windowpanes shivered faintly in their leads.

"I can't explain it," she said. "I don't know your grandfather. I'd never heard his name before the summons came." She hesitated, and Marguerite watched her decide to say the rest, watched the decision happen like a key turning. "But I'll tell you what I know about hiding things in clocks. There are only so many places. Behind the dial. Under the base. In the weight. A man who knows clocks picks the place a clockmaker would think to look last — and he leaves signs that only a tradesman reads. There was a scratch inside the case, cut deliberately, in the shape of a half-hunter escapement with the pallets reversed. No accident makes that mark. It says: *the mechanism is false, look closer.* Any clockmaker who opened that case honestly would have found the letter. Your grandfather didn't need to know my name. He needed someone whose hands would do what hands like ours always do."

"And if Halloran sends a clockmaker?"

"Halloran's men don't repair master clocks," Rosalind said. "They break them. There's a difference in the hands. You'd know it if you'd watched as many hands as I have."

Marguerite wanted to despise the answer — its coolness, its certainty — but there was no arrogance in it. Only the terrible composure of someone who had been alone so long that competence was the only warmth available to her. Marguerite knew that temperature. She had lived in it herself since her mother died, keeping her grandfather's house with a fury of efficiency that everyone mistook for contentment.

"One more question," she said. "If I told you to take the letter back tomorrow — put it in the clock, seal the panel, walk away, forget you ever saw it — would you go?"

Rosalind met her eyes without flinching.

"Yes," she said. "If you asked me truly. It's your grandfather's letter and your family's name. Not mine." Then, after a breath: "But you won't ask me. Because you've already read it. And because you know what happens to the Pembertons when that ship reaches port with the forged manifests aboard."

Marguerite stood abruptly, because sitting across from the girl felt suddenly like being read by her, and she had endured quite enough of that. She went to the window. Below, the river lay under its grey lid of ice, seam-less and patient, and she hated it with a new hatred — the hatred of a thing that looks like a road.

"He's asking for you by name now," she said quietly. "Grandfather. When the fever lifts, he asks if 'the girl who keeps time' has come. He won't tell us the rest of it. He says the route is only safe spoken once, to the one carrying it. Which means either he trusts you absolutely—" she turned— "or he has lost the judgment to trust anyone, and I cannot tell which frightens me more."

* * *

That evening she tested her properly.

Not with questions — questions were cheap, and Marguerite had learned from her grandfather, clerk to the bone, that paper and procedure reveal what conversation conceals. She waited until the house had gone dark except for the sickroom candle, and she came down to the kitchen where Rosalind sat cleaning tools by lamplight, and she put three things on the table between them.

A letter, sealed, addressed to the Pemberton solicitor. A second letter, identical in size, seal, and hand — which she had copied herself that afternoon from a specimen of her own forgery, containing nothing but blank pages and, folded within them, a single sentence: *You are being measured.*

And a third envelope, empty, sealed over nothing at all.

"The post goes out at seven," Marguerite said. "One of these is true, two are traps, and I will not tell you which. Choose the one that goes. Take an hour if you need it."

Rosalind looked at the envelopes for a long time. Then she picked up each in turn, weighing it in her palm, tilting it against the lamp. With the first she did nothing at all. With the second she held it close to the flame's edge, warming the wax until the impression softened slightly, and read the ghost of pressure beneath — the way a heavier fold shows through a seal — and Marguerite saw her lips move faintly as she found the hidden sentence and understood.

With the third she pressed her thumbnail gently against the wax and felt the hollowness underneath.

"The solicitor's letter," Rosalind said, setting down the first envelope. "The second is bait — sealed over a fold, and no clerk folds a true letter that way. The third is sealed over air; whoever made it wanted to see if I'd shake it." She paused. "This isn't a test of honesty. It's a test of method. You wanted to know if I think the way a smuggler thinks."

"I wanted to know if you think the way a *clerk* thinks," Marguerite said. "My grandfather taught me that trick with the seals. You passed faster than I did at fourteen." She sat down slowly, and something in her chest ached with the effort of what came next, like a joint being set. "All right, Rosalind Pemberton. Here are your terms."

"Pemberton?" Rosalind frowned. "My name is Rosalind—"

"I know what your name is. Grandfather wrote it that way in his notes — *the Pemberton girl, ironically* — and I've been chewing on it for days. Listen." Marguerite flattened her hands on the table. "In four days, perhaps five, the river breaks. When it does, the *Cormorant* sails with papers aboard that name my family as smugglers — forged documents, real enough to hang us in reputation if not in law, and reputation in this city is a longer rope than any judge's. My grandfather hid the true evidence and then hid himself dying, waiting for a courier he couldn't name and couldn't hire, because everyone he might have hired is either bought or watched. Then you walked through his door with oil on your hands."

She took a breath.

"So here it is, plainly. Help me carry that evidence downriver past three checkpoints — the customs house, the ferry toll, the garrison bridge — every one of them held by a man on Halloran's payroll. Share the risk exactly as I share it. If we're caught, we were both deceived; you'll say I coerced you, and I will confirm it, whatever it costs me. Or refuse, take your tools, and leave tonight — and understand that I cannot let you walk out of this house knowing what you know and unwatched. I would report you myself, to whom and for what I haven't entirely decided, but I would do it, because my family has lost everything but its name and I will not lose that too."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, and both women glanced at the ceiling as one — a small synchronized motion that Marguerite would remember afterward as the moment it stopped being two strangers at a table.

Rosalind turned one of her tweezers over beneath the light, considering it as though it might advise her.

"If I refuse," she said, "I go back to the shop attic, and a bed I rent by the week, and a life that fits in one valise. That's what staying free costs me. That's the whole inventory." She looked up. "Do you know what my terms are? Nobody has ever offered me a share of anything before — not a risk, not a purpose, not even a blame. You've just handed me a portion of your family's ruin to carry, and called it an alliance." Her voice stayed level, but Marguerite saw her throat move. "Yes. I'll help you. On one condition."

"Name it."

"When this is done — however it ends — your grandfather teaches me the rest of the clerk's tricks. The seals, the folds, the hiding places. Everything. I've spent my life learning how machines keep faith with time. I want to learn how paper does."

Something loosened in Marguerite's chest that she refused to call relief.

"He'd enjoy that more than any medicine," she said. "He'll make you copy manifests until your eyes bleed. He's a tyrant with a ruler."

"I've served worse masters."

"He's not a master," Marguerite said, sharper than she meant. "He's—" and the word arrived before she could stop it, carried on three weeks of fear and midnight vigils: "—he's *family*. You'll be dealing with family now, and family is worse than any checkpoint on that river."

Rosalind regarded her across the lamplight, and slowly, for the first time in Marguerite's memory of her, the clockmaker smiled — a small, careful expression, like a mechanism trying its first tick.

"Then I'll take the terms as offered," she said. "Family and all."

They shook on it, ink-stained hand to oil-marked one, and Marguerite went to the dresser and brought out the brandy she had been saving against bad news, and they drank to the enterprise in tea cups because neither could find the good glasses in the dark. It tasted of smoke and currants and, faintly, of decision.

Later, climbing the stairs past her grandfather's door, Marguerite heard him stir and murmur, and she paused. His voice came thin through the wood:

"Has the girl who keeps time agreed?"

"She's agreed, Grandfather. We leave when the route is ready."

A long silence. Then, softly, with something in it that was almost peace:

"Then wind the regulators, child. All of them. We shall want the hours counted truly, from here to the sea."

Marguerite stood a moment in the dark hallway, listening to the river muttering under its ice, and thought of the customs house waiting at the water's head — its green doors, its ledgers, its inspector Voss with his soft voice and searching hands.

Three days, perhaps. Four.

She went to bed and did not sleep, and in the morning there was ice on the inside of the windows, early and wrong, like a signature appearing where none should be.
