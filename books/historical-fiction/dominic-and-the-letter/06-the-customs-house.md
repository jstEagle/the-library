## 6. The Customs House

The customs house at Verrin's Landing had been built by a man who wanted the river to know it was watched. Its stone face rose from the bank like a judge's brow, and the flag above it hung stiff with frost, snapping once each gust like a finger tapping on a desk. Rosalind saw it first through the carriage window, then through her own fear, which made everything sharper — the black water sliding beneath grey ice, the gulls arguing over the shallows, the long queue of travelers shuffling toward the arched doors.

In her lap sat the barometer.

It was a fine instrument, an aneroid with a brass case she had spent three evenings polishing, and its papers were genuine: Mr. Halloran of the shipping office upriver had indeed commissioned its repair, and she was indeed the tradeswoman engaged to return it. That was the beauty of Dominic's plan, if it could be called his plan and not simply the accumulated cunning of forty years spent among ledgers and locked drawers. *No one suspects a delivery that is true*, he had told her, propped against his pillows, his voice thin as paper but his eyes still quick. *The forger's art is not in making lies. It is in choosing the lie that is already half-true.*

So the barometer was real. Only the letter inside it was not where any inspector would think to look — not in the packing straw, not taped beneath the lid, but coiled into the hollow chamber of the instrument itself, wrapped in oilcloth no thicker than a prayer book's cover, wedged behind the vacuum capsule where the mechanism's own shadow concealed it. To find it, a man would have to take the barometer apart down to its bones, and to justify taking it apart he would need suspicion, and suspicion required something more than a tired apprentice carrying a repaired instrument on a cold morning.

Rosalind repeated this to herself as the queue advanced. *You are exactly what you appear to be.* She wore her work apron rolled into her satchel and her second-best coat, the one with the leather patches at the cuffs that said *hands that labor* rather than *hands that hide*. Her tools were in their proper roll. Her chapped knuckles were honest. Even her breath, ghosting white in the cold, seemed to her the breath of someone with nothing to confess.

Marguerite walked three paces behind her, bundled in a grey cloak, playing the part of a merchant's daughter escorting the family's instrument to Halloran because her father could not spare the time. They had agreed on the platform at dawn not to travel together — two strangers, sharing nothing but the road — and Marguerite had kept to the arrangement so faithfully that Rosalind sometimes forgot she was there. Then Marguerite would cough, once, softly, the way she did when checking that Rosalind still breathed, and Rosalind would remember.

The line moved. A soldier at the door turned each traveler sideways, patted coats, looked into baskets. Inside, the hall opened upward into a vaulted ceiling streaked with pipe smoke, and the noise of it closed over them — boots on stone, a clerk calling names, somewhere a dog whining at the end of a rope. Along the far wall, iron gates gave onto the quay, where the *Coriander* — no, not the *Coriander*, that ship was weeks away; only barges today, low and heavy with winter goods — where barges waited to take travelers the next leg of the river.

*Three checkpoints*, Dominic had said. *This is the first, and it is the worst, because it is the one where they are still bored.*

The inspector at the third table had a face like a closed ledger. He was younger than Rosalind expected, with a fair mustache trying to be authoritative and ink stains on his fingers that no soldier's pay had put there. He waved forward the farmer ahead of her, glanced at his manifest, waved him through, and then his eyes came up and settled on Rosalind and did not move.

"Name."

"Pemberton. Rosalind Pemberton, apprentice to—" She caught herself, too late, the name already out and hanging in the smoky air between them. "Apprentice clockmaker. I'm delivering a repaired barometer to Mr. Halloran, shipping office at Greyfell. Papers here."

She laid the manifest on the table. The inspector did not look at it at once. He was looking at her face when she said *Pemberton*, and she watched something pass behind his eyes — not recognition exactly, but a note being taken, a coin dropped into a purse for later counting.

"Pemberton," he said again, tasting it. He picked up the manifest at last, read it slowly as though it were written in a language he distrusted. "Clockmaker, are you. And what does a clockmaker want on the river road in March?"

"The same thing everyone wants, sir. To get where she's going before the ice decides otherwise."

Someone behind her laughed shortly. The inspector did not. He set the manifest down and squared it with the table's edge, twice, precisely, and Rosalind understood with a cold settling in her stomach that he was a man who liked things squared, who would sleep badly tonight if anything on his table remained unsquared, and that the barometer in her arms was the most unsquared object in the room.

"Open the case."

Her heart performed a trick she had never felt it do — a full stop, a held silence, like a pendulum arrested mid-swing. In her chest, where a lifetime of small mechanisms had taught her every rhythm, there was now only a terrible stillness, and then the frantic effort of restarting.

"Sir, it's a sealed aneroid. Opening the case disturbs the calibration—"

"Then you'll recalibrate it." He extended one hand, palm up, patient as winter. "Or I'll send for a man who can. Your choice, mistress Pemberton. But nothing crosses my checkpoint in a box I haven't seen inside."

Her hands were moving before her mind finished arguing — lifting the barometer, setting it on the scarred oak table, reaching for the small screwdriver in her apron pocket because that was what her hands knew how to do, because work was the only prayer she had ever trusted. Somewhere very far away, Marguerite coughed once.

The oilcloth wrapping was wound tight around the vacuum capsule, tucked behind the spring bar. To reach it, the inspector would have to watch her lift out the movement entire, and the movement was heavy, unmistakable, and the letter would come with it like a wick pulled from a candle. There was no way. There had never been a way. She had known it since dawn and had let the sunrise talk her out of knowing it.

The first screw came free. The brass back plate lifted. The inspector leaned in, and his shadow fell across the works, and Rosalind thought, absurdly, of the master clock at the observatory, of all the years that clock had kept its secret in the dark, and how secrets were heavier than gears and yet weighed nothing at all until someone asked to see them.

"I'll need the light," the inspector said, and turned toward the window, turning the barometer with him—

—and that was when the dog went mad.

Not the customs house dog, tied by the gate, but a terrier that had slipped in with a fishwife, and it shot across the stone floor like a spark along a fuse, straight at the inspector's table, and leapt. A wheel of cheese went over the edge and burst its rind on the floor. The fishwife shrieked apologies in three languages, none of them fluent. The soldier at the door lunged, caught the terrier by the scruff, and was dragged two stumbling steps as it twisted. A basket of herring overturned, and the smell of it bloomed through the hall like a scandal.

And through the middle of it all came Marguerite.

She had shed timidity the way other women shed a shawl. "Oh — oh, sir, your hand—" she was crying, kneeling at the inspector's elbow, and Rosalind saw the smear of herring oil silvering the back of his wrist where the terrier's chain had caught him. "That beast has fouled your sleeve, sir, and it's gone onto the papers — look, the ink will run — here, let me—"

"I don't require—" the inspector began, and then the fishwife arrived demanding compensation for her cheese, and the soldier arrived dragging the dog, and another traveler began loudly insisting he had been waiting since morning, and the whole hall tilted toward the little storm like iron filings toward a magnet.

Rosalind's hands moved alone.

Screwdriver. Plate lifted a finger's width — not off, never off, just enough. Two fingers into the gap, finding the oilcloth edge by feel, drawing it out flat against the underside of the plate, sliding it into her apron pocket in the same motion that her thumb pressed the plate home and the screwdriver found its thread. Four turns. Five. The sixth turn she made slower than the others, so that when the inspector finally shook free of Marguerite and the herring and turned back to the table, red-faced, he saw exactly what he expected to see: a tradeswoman bent over her work, tightening the last screw of a sealed instrument.

"Well?" he snapped.

Rosalind set the barometer upright on the table. Her hands were steady. Later she would understand that they had been steadier in those thirty seconds than they had ever been in her life, and that terror and mastery were made, somehow, of the same wire.

"Sealed and calibrated," she said. "You're welcome to test the needle yourself, sir. If it reads true, I've done my work; if it reads false, you may keep the instrument and tell Mr. Halloran his money was wasted."

He stared at her a long moment. The mustache twitched. He picked up the barometer, turned it over once, ran his thumbnail along the seam of the case — a slow, deliberate line, a man marking territory — and set it back down.

"Greyfell," he said, and stamped the manifest, and stamped it hard enough that the table jumped. "Mind the ice, mistress Pemberton. It's breaking early this year. Rivers don't wait on anyone's business."

"No," Rosalind said, gathering the instrument to her chest, feeling through the brass the faint warmth of it, hers again, empty again, safe. "They don't."

She walked toward the quay gates on legs that felt borrowed. Behind her, Marguerite was apologizing her way free of the fishwife, folding coins into the woman's hand, murmuring about clumsiness and dogs and the shocking state of public buildings. At the gates the soldier checked the stamp and waved them through, and then the river wind hit them, raw and enormous, and the quay stretched out ahead with its waiting barge and its grey water, and Rosalind discovered she was laughing — a small, cracked, disbelieving sound that steamed in the cold.

"You're laughing," Marguerite said, catching up, breathless, her hood thrown back. "We nearly died and you're laughing."

"We didn't die." Rosalind drew the oilcloth packet from her pocket and held it a moment between them, this folded nothing that had outweighed the whole customs house. "It held. You were—" She stopped, recalculated, and said honestly: "You were magnificent."

"I threw myself at a man's sleeve smelling of herring." Marguerite's mouth twitched despite itself. "My grandfather will hear I defended the family honor with fish oil. He'll be insufferable." She took the packet and, with quick practiced fingers — clerk's fingers, Rosalind realized, the granddaughter had them too — slid it deep into the lining of her cloak, where it vanished entirely. "Two checkpoints left. Don't celebrate yet."

But as they boarded the barge, as the mooring lines came free and the current took them out past the customs house walls, past the windows where the inspector stood watching them go with his squared papers and his unsquared thoughts, Rosalind looked upriver and saw what the morning light had been showing her all along and she had refused to read.

The ice was moving.

Great plates of it, grinding downstream in the channel, groaning like a struck bell, a full month early — and on the far bank, riders. Three of them, spaced along the towpath like beads on a wire, keeping pace with the barge.

Marguerite saw them too. Her hand found Rosalind's arm, and her voice, when it came, was very quiet.

"They're not waiting for the ferry toll," she said. "Someone told them we're already on the water."
