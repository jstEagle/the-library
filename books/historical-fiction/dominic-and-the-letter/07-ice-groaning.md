## 7. Ice Groaning

The storm found them an hour past the second checkpoint, as if it had been lying in wait along the river road all day, patient as a creditor.

It came first as a change in the light — the grey of the afternoon thickening to the colour of pewter — and then as a sound, a long low exhale rolling down the valley from the north, and then it was upon them entirely: snow driving sideways, fine as sifted flour, filling the wheel ruts behind them in minutes so that their own passage was erased as they made it. Marguerite pulled her collar up until only her eyes showed above the wool. Rosalind could no longer feel the fingers of her right hand, the one that had gripped the handle of the instrument case for two days without letting go, even in sleep.

"We can't go on," Marguerite shouted over the wind. "The road bends with the river ahead. If we miss the turn we'll walk straight onto the ice in this white."

Rosalind nodded rather than shout back. Her voice felt like something she had left behind at the ferry toll that morning, along with the coin Marguerite had paid and the dignity she had spent with it. They had passed the collector's inspection by an hour, perhaps two, and Rosalind had not yet stopped hearing his voice — *Vasseur, is it? Grandfather to the girl, then* — repeating in her head like a clock with a faulty escapement, catching, catching, catching on the same tooth.

The ferrymaster's house appeared out of the storm all at once, the way things do in whiteouts: a dark shape resolving into a door, a lamp behind horn panes, woodsmoke bending flat in the wind. A man answered Marguerite's knocking before she finished it, as though he had been standing there listening for footsteps.

He was sixty perhaps, built like the pilings of his own dock, with a beard gone to rust and frost melting on it in beads. His eyes went from Marguerite to Rosalind to the case in Rosalind's hand, and something moved across his face — recognition, then calculation, then a decision made quickly and without pleasure.

"Off the river," he said. "In."

Inside was warmth of the blunt, animal kind: a stove, two chairs, nets hung from beams, the smell of fish and tar and wet wool. He shut the door against the howl and put his back to it, and for a moment none of them spoke, all three of them listening to the storm press its whole weight against the house.

"You'll be wanting crossing at the garrison bridge tomorrow," the man said at last. It was not a question. "Everyone does, this time of year. Everyone in such a hurry."

"We are in a hurry," Marguerite said.

"Then you're fools, or you're carrying something worth hurrying for." He crossed to the stove and lifted the kettle onto it, unhurried now, deliberately unhurried, the way men are when they want you to know they hold the clock. "Sit. I'm Halloran. I ran the ferry at Mill Reach twenty years before the bridge took my trade, and before that I knew every soul who worked the customs ledgers on this water. Including," he said, setting down the kettle with a click, "a clerk named Vasseur."

Rosalind's heart struck once, hard, like a hammer on a case wall.

"Dominic Vasseur," Halloran repeated, turning. "So you're not just travelers."

* * *

They told him nothing at first. That was Marguerite's instinct and Rosalind's discipline both, honed over three days of roads: trust no one fully, trust someone necessarily. But Halloran did not press. He fed them instead — bread, smoked fish, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in — and while they ate he talked, in the low rambling way of rivermen, about ice.

"Early this year," he said. "That's what I'd tell you if you asked, and you should ask. Ice usually holds till the first week of May. Last night I heard it groan twice from my bed. Twice, before the storm even came. The river's talking early." He looked at Rosalind over his cup. "You're a tradeswoman. You'll understand me when I say a thing that starts talking before its time is a thing already coming apart."

"I understand," Rosalind said quietly.

"And you'll understand this too." Halloran set the cup down. "Two days ago a revenue cutter tied up below the reach, and men came through asking questions. Not revenue men — they wore the coats, but they asked wrong questions. Not about cargo. About papers. About a letter." He let the word sit in the warm room like a stone dropped in still water. "About whether anyone had carried letters upriver from the town. They searched every traveler at the reach. Turned out hampers, opened flour sacks, ran knives through mattresses. And yesterday morning they posted a man at each landing with a description."

Marguerite's hand had gone still on the table. "A description of whom?"

"Two women," Halloran said. "One young, dark-haired, sharp-featured. One younger, carrying an instrument case, walking like her feet were clocks." His eyes came to Rosalind's case where it sat between her boots, and stayed there. "I've ferried smugglers, excise men, eloping couples, one bishop, and a bear once. I know cargo when I see it sitting on my floor. So. Are you going to tell me what's in the case, or am I going to have to decide what I think is in it?"

The stove ticked as the iron expanded. Outside, beneath the wind, there came another sound — longer, lower, older — a sound Rosalind felt in her sternum before she heard it with her ears. The ice on the river, speaking.

Halloran heard it too. Something in his shoulders changed.

"That's Vasseur's granddaughter sitting at my table," he said slowly, "and that's Vasseur's letter in the case, or I'm a landed fish. He used to take the ferry twice a year, always in the dead season, always alone, always carrying that same leather satchel and looking like a man going to his own funeral. I asked him once, thirty years ago, what he carried that was so heavy. He laughed and said, 'Nothing, Halloran. Nothing is very heavy when you carry it long enough.'" The old man rubbed his jaw. "Last autumn he rode with me and he didn't laugh. He looked at the water and said, 'Everything comes loose in spring, ferryman. Everything breaks open.' I thought he meant the ice."

"He meant himself," Marguerite said, barely above a whisper.

"Aye." Halloran leaned back. "So tell me where it has to go, and I'll tell you what I know about getting it there. Not because I love your grandfather's memory overmuch — though I do — but because those men at the landings burned my cousin's boat license eleven years ago on a charge nobody ever explained, and I've been waiting since to be of inconvenience to somebody."

* * *

Rosalind laid it out the way she laid out a repair: parts in order, no part skipped. The magistrate at Ashcombe, beyond the garrison bridge. The evidence sewn where no searcher would think to look. Three checkpoints between here and there, two behind them, one ahead, and the ship that would carry the conspirators' last hope out with the spring tide.

Halloran listened with his eyes closed, which unnerved her until she realized he was navigating — she could see it in the small movements of his head, reading the river from memory, running the route the way she would run a train of gears.

"The bridge is your problem," he said at last. "Customs house, you've beaten. Toll, you've beaten — and don't think I didn't hear how. The bridge is different. There's one road onto it, one garrison, and by now they'll know two women slipped the toll. They won't be searching bags at the bridge, girl. They'll be searching faces."

"Then faces aren't what crosses," Rosalind said.

She opened the case.

She had not shown it to anyone, not even Marguerite — not truly shown it, with the panel swung wide and the works exposed to another person's eyes. Now she lifted out the barometer casing, the false papers, the folded decoys, and set them aside, and there at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the thing she had made in Dominic's attic over two sleepless nights: a pendulum bob, cast in brass to the exact weight and figure of the observatory master clock's own, hollowed by her own drills and resealed with soft solder, the seam invisible unless you knew where light fell. Inside it, rolled tight as a wick, the true evidence.

Halloran looked at it a long time. "You hid papers in a pendulum."

"I hid them in time," Rosalind said. "In the steadiest, most lawful, most ignored object a town owns. Nobody searches the thing that keeps everyone honest. They only check that it swings."

The old man began, very quietly, to laugh — a rusty sound, like a gate after winter. "Oh, Vasseur knew what he was doing, sending for a clockmaker."

Outside, the storm suddenly dropped, as storms on rivers do, all at once, like a held breath released — and in the silence that followed, the whole valley heard the river clearly.

It was not one sound but many: a deep tonal groan rising from downstream and traveling under the ground beneath the house; a crack like a pistol, sharp and near; then a long shivering series of reports, overlapping, echoing bank to bank, the ice field shifting, settling, breaking its own seams in the dark. Through the window, in the sudden moonlight between clouds, Rosalind could see the river: a vast white plain gone grey at its edges, and down the middle channel a line of black water opening and closing like a slow mouth.

"It's going," Halloran said, standing. "Three weeks early, maybe four. That changes everything. The barge traffic moves when the channel opens — and so does your conspirators' ship, and so do the soldiers' orders, because garrisons get restless when the ice goes and every smuggler on the water wakes up." He turned to Marguerite. "How fast can you be on the road at first light?"

"As fast as needs be."

"Then listen. The bridge garrison changes watch at dawn, and dawn is when the ice runs loudest. No sentry hears a footstep over that sound. But they'll be watching the road, not the shore." He tapped the table. "There's a ferry landing half a mile below the bridge — my old landing. The ice will be running loose in the channel by morning, but the near third may still hold if you cross before sun-up. Cross the ice, come up under the bridge itself, and walk on past the garrison gate as what you are: a tradeswoman taking a repaired clock home. Let them search the case. Let them empty it. The bob travels in your hand, plain as daylight, because plain things are invisible."

Marguerite stared at him. "Walk onto the ice. In the dark. While it's breaking."

"People have crossed at break-up for centuries," Halloran said. "Most of them lived. And none of them," he added, glancing at Rosalind, "were carrying anything half so heavy as nothing."

Rosalind did not sleep. She sat by the banked stove with the pendulum bob in her lap, feeling its weight — three pounds of brass, eight ounces of paper, one family's name — and listened to the river talk all night. Groan, report, settle. Groan, report, settle. It was a clock, she realized somewhere past midnight. The largest she had ever stood inside. Every tick was a seam giving way, every tock a piece of the season dying, and somewhere far upstream a dying man was counting the same strokes without knowing whether his message would outrun them.

Dominic had taught her that a good escapement does not stop the fall of the wheel — nothing stops it — it only decides when the fall happens, one tooth at a time.

Very well. She would choose the teeth.

At the grey edge of dawn, Halloran shook them awake, pressed bread into their hands, and opened the door onto a world scoured white and silent. The river lay black-veined down its middle, the ice groaning in long, tired notes, and on the wind from the bridge came another sound beneath it all, thin and human: a bugle, sounding the garrison's morning watch.

"Go," Halloran said. "And when you pass the sentry — swing the bob a little. Let him see it's real."
