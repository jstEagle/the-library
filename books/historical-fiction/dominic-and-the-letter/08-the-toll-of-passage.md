## 8. The Toll of Passage

The river smelled of thaw and old wood that morning — a smell like something loosening its grip. I felt it in my teeth before I heard it: a low groan beneath the fog, the ice on the Maranne shifting in its sleep, dreaming of the break to come. Grandfather had told me once that a river never breaks all at once. It breaks the way a heart does, quietly at first, in places no one thinks to watch.

I was watching now.

Rosalind walked half a step ahead of me along the towpath, her instrument case strapped to her back, her posture that of a tradeswoman with an ordinary delivery and an ordinary morning ahead of her. She was very good at looking ordinary. I had not expected that. When she first came to our door with mud on her hem and my grandfather's letter in her satchel, I had taken her for exactly what she appeared — a fatherless apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a habit of counting things under her breath. I had not taken her for brave. That was my error, and I had been correcting it ever since, one mile at a time.

"Two hours to the crossing," she said, not turning her head. "The ferry runs when the mist lifts."

"Then we have two hours to become boring," I said. "Boring people pass through tolls. Interesting ones get searched."

The corner of her mouth moved. It was almost a smile, and it cost us both something to allow it, because allowing ourselves small comforts felt like inviting disaster to sit down at our table. Still, we had learned each other's silences by now. Hers came from years of having no one worth speaking to. Mine came from a house where grief sat in every chair, wearing my grandfather's face.

* * *

The ferry landing emerged from the fog the way a wrong answer emerges from a page — gradually, then all at once. A low stone quay, slick with spray. A flat-bottomed ferry moored to an iron ring, its boards pale with frost. A queue of perhaps a dozen travelers: two drovers with sheep bleating in their crates, a seed merchant with his cart, a woman in gray leading a mule loaded with salt fish. And at the little toll shed beside the gangplank, a window glowing lamplight yellow through the mist, sat the collector.

I knew his face before I knew his name, which is a particular kind of dread. Bram Cossart. I had seen him twice at my grandfather's ledger desk years ago, hat in hands, asking for extensions on debts my grandfather had long since forgiven and he had long since resented. He had a face like a closed ledger too — ruled lines of disappointment, columns of grievance, a red-ink mouth. And I knew what Rosalind knew, what the letter had made plain: Cossart was on the payroll. His window was not a toll window. It was a net.

"He'll be looking for us," Rosalind murmured. We had stopped at the edge of the quay, pretending to study the ferry schedule nailed to a post, which was weathered past reading. "Travelers carrying instrument cases. A clockmaker and her sister."

"Cousins," I said.

"Cousins," she agreed.

"And if he knows my name—" I stopped. There it was, the thing I had been chewing since dawn. "He will know my name, Rosalind. Vasseur. This stretch of river remembers every family that ever owed it money, and mine owed *him*." My grandfather had bought Cossart's patience twice over the years, quietly, the way one pays to keep a dog from barking. If Cossart connected the girl at his window to the dying clerk whose papers had gone missing from a locked drawer—

"Then we don't give him time to connect anything," Rosalind said. She turned to me fully, and behind the fog on her lashes her eyes were very steady. "You said once you wanted to be useful. Not protected. Useful."

"I said that before I knew how much it would cost."

"Everything costs," she said. "That's what makes it worth anything."

* * *

We joined the queue behind the salt-fish woman, close enough to smell the brine, far enough not to invite conversation. The line moved with the maddening slowness of all official things. Cossart examined papers, took coins, licked his thumb to count them, and waved each traveler across the gangplank with the weary contempt of a man performing a job far beneath his imagined importance. As each party passed, I watched his eyes do their arithmetic — faces, luggage, hands. Looking for something. Looking for *us*.

My hands were cold. I told myself it was only the mist off the water. Beneath my cloak, against my ribs, I carried the second copy of the deposition — not the evidence itself, which rode hidden in Rosalind's keeping, but the testimony my grandfather had dictated with his failing voice, naming names, dates, sums. If the original was lost, this remained. I had insisted on carrying it. Rosalind had argued; I had won; neither of us had slept well since.

Three parties ahead of us, Cossart stood up from his stool.

He did it slowly, unfolding himself like a rule being enforced, and walked down the queue to the seed merchant's cart. "Open it," he said, just loud enough for all of us to hear. The merchant protested. Cossart's men — there were two of them, loafing by the shed with the bored menace of hired muscle — began pulling sacks apart, stabbing into seed corn with a knife, letting it spill gold onto the stones while the merchant's face went the color of his own millet. Searching. For a letter. For papers. For whatever had gone missing from a sick man's house.

"They're turning over everything," Rosalind breathed. Her hand found my sleeve, not for comfort but for balance, the way a climber tests a hold. "Marguerite. If they open the case—"

"They won't," I said, and did not know until I spoke it whether I was lying.

We shuffled forward. Two parties. One. The sheep crate passed, bleating; Cossart waved it through without a glance. Then the salt-fish woman, and then the mist shifted, and then there was nothing between us and his window except cold air and my own heartbeat, which had picked up grandfather's old metronome rhythm — tick, tick, tick — as though my body had learned clockwork from spending my childhood in his shop.

Cossart looked up. And I watched recognition move across his face like weather.

"You," he said.

I stepped up to the window. Beside me, Rosalind became a piece of furniture — still, plain, waiting her turn. I had never loved a stranger's stillness more.

"Good morning to you, Master Cossart," I said. My voice came out level. Somewhere below us the ice groaned again, long and low, like the river clearing its throat. "Passage for two. To the north bank."

"Marguerite Vasseur." He pronounced it carefully, tasting it, setting each syllable in its column. He leaned back on his stool, and I saw the thought complete itself behind his eyes — the missing papers, the reward being whispered about, the name Vasseur arriving voluntarily at his window like a parcel he hadn't even had to fetch. "Well. Well. Your grandfather kept his books late, I hear. Kept them locked, too." His smile showed more gum than tooth. "And now here's his blood, crossing *my* river, carrying — what is that? Instruments?"

"My cousin is a clockmaker," I said. "She has been engaged to repair the observatory clock at Bellemont. You may verify the commission with the observatory steward; here is the work order, signed." Rosalind produced the paper with hands that did not tremble, and I marveled at her, I could have wept at her, this orphan girl holding out a forged-by-necessity document to a corrupt official as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

Cossart read it. He read it twice. Behind him one of his men drifted closer to the shed, and I saw the shape of the next hour if we let it happen: the case opened on the quay, the knife in the seed corn, everything spilling out gold and ruined.

So I did the thing I had rehearsed against the ferry rail while Rosalind pretended to sleep. I traded my dignity for our passage.

"Master Cossart," I said, and let my voice break — not much, just enough, a hairline crack in good porcelain. "Before you summon your man. Before you make this difficult for a grieving family." I leaned toward the window and lowered my eyes, the picture of shame. "You know who we are. Then you know what we've come to settle. My grandfather's debts. All of them." I drew out the purse Rosalind had prepared — not our whole reserve, but enough, weighted with coins to make a sound like a promise when I set it on his sill. It rang against the wood, and his eyes went to it the way a starving man's go to bread. "He was proud, you understand. Too proud to beg extensions. He always meant to make it right. He's dying now, Master Cossart — the doctors give him till the ice breaks — and his last wish was that the Vasseur name cross this river clean. No debts. No stains. We are nobody interesting. We are only a family paying what it owes."

For a moment the only sound was the sheep and the water talking under the ice.

Cossart looked at the purse. He looked at me — searching my face for the forgery in it, for the flicker of a conspirator's guilt — and what he found instead, because grief had made me fluent in it, was a granddaughter ashamed and hollowed and wanting only to be done with the whole sad business. Grief, I discovered that morning, is a language every crook believes he can read. None of them ever imagines it might be spoken in a second tongue.

"He should have paid when he was able," Cossart said at last, sweeping the purse off the sill with one practiced motion. It vanished somewhere into his coat. "Sentimental nonsense, deathbed settlements. But the office recognizes honest obligation." He stamped the work order, stamped our passage chit, and jerked his chin at the gangplank. "Go on, then. And tell the observatory their clock had better keep better time than their stewards pay their tolls."

"Thank you, Master Cossart," I said, humble as ash. "Truly."

We crossed the gangplank. The ferryman cast off. The quay slid backward into the fog with its spilled seed corn and its lamplit window and its two hired men who never even glanced at the instrument case, because the collector had decided we were boring, and boring was the best disguise in the world.

It was not until the north bank rose out of the mist that I let my knees remember what they'd felt. I sat down hard on a mooring bollard, and Rosalind crouched in front of me, and for a moment neither of us said anything at all.

"You gave him your grandfather's debts," she said finally. "A lie."

"A true lie." I wiped my face with the back of my wrist. "He died owing nothing but the truth. Cossart can spend the coins. He can't spend that."

Rosalind looked at me then with an expression I couldn't name — something between astonishment and recognition, as if she were meeting me properly for the first time, three checkpoints into knowing her. "Marguerite," she said slowly. "That was—"

Whatever it was going to be, we never learned. Because behind us, across the river, the toll shed door banged open, and Cossart's voice carried thin and sharp over the water:

"*Stop that boat!*"

And beneath it, rising from the depths of the Maranne like the answer to a question no one had dared ask — the ice began to sing.
