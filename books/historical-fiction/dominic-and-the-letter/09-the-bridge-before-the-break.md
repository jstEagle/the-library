## 9. The Bridge Before the Break

The river spoke first.

We heard it from half a mile off — a low, grinding groan beneath the morning fog, as though the water had grown impatient with its own imprisonment and was testing the locks. Marguerite stopped on the frozen ruts of the road and tilted her head, listening the way I listened to a clock that had begun, faintly, to lose time.

"It's early," she said.

"So is everything else about this week." I shifted the pendulum bob inside its sacking wrap. It hung from my shoulder by a leather strap, and though it weighed no more than nine pounds, it pressed against my hip like a sleeping child — like something alive, something entrusted. "A clockmaker knows when a mechanism is running ahead of itself. The question is whether we can outrun it."

Ahead of us, through the thinning mist, the garrison bridge emerged the way bones emerge from shallow soil: pale, arched, inevitable. Three spans of grey stone crossing the brown-black water, and at its near end a squat customs post flying the garrison colours, and before it — my stomach tightened before my eyes confirmed it — soldiers. Two at the barricade, red coats dulled by fog, muskets slung. And behind them, apart from them, a third figure who wore no uniform at all: a tall man in a black coat and a beaver hat, standing very still, his hands folded at his waist like a man waiting for a coach he knew would come.

Marguerite saw him too. Her breath went out of her slowly.

"That's Corvane," she whispered. "Grandfather's old superior. He signed the ledgers. He—" She swallowed. "He visited the house last autumn. He asked after Grandfather's health with such tenderness. I brought him tea."

"Then he knows your face."

"He knows mine." She turned to me, and in the flat white light her face looked carved from something colder than flesh. "But not yours. Not yet."

I understood what she was doing even before she said it. My hands understood first; they had already begun tightening the strap.

"No," I said.

"Yes. He'll be watching for us both — two women travelling together, that's what they'll have been told to find. But one tradeswoman, alone, delivering a clock part—"

"You don't know what they'll do if they catch you."

"I know exactly what they'll do," she said, "because I've spent three weeks learning what they did to my grandfather." She reached out and took my wrist, and her grip was stronger than her frame suggested. "Rosalind. The evidence has to cross. It doesn't matter which of us carries it across that bridge — only that it crosses. You're the clockmaker. You're the one who thought of hiding it in the bob. You're the one they'll wave through because you look exactly like every other tired tradeswoman on this road. I'm the one whose name is in their ledger." She almost smiled. "Let me be useful for once. Let me carry nothing but myself across that bridge, and let them waste their suspicion on me while you walk past with the truth under your arm."

For a moment I could not speak. I am not, by nature or upbringing, a person accustomed to being argued out of sacrifice; I had never had anyone willing enough to argue. Orphans learn early that love is measured in what others will risk for you, and until this winter my ledger had been nearly blank. Now here stood Marguerite Vasseur — who had distrusted me, tested me, bargained with me — offering herself up as the decoy so that I might pass.

"The decoy case," I managed. "It's still in the cart."

"Then give me the cart, and the case, and five minutes' head start." She was already moving. "And Rosalind — whatever you hear behind you, whatever you see — do not stop. Do not turn around. Grandfather's letter says the magistrate's man waits at the Harrow Inn on the far bank, the one with the green door. Deliver it there. Promise me."

"I promise."

She squeezed my wrist once, hard, then turned away down the slope toward the bridge, pulling the little handcart behind her, and I watched her go with my heart hammering like an escapement gone wild, and I did not call after her, because calling after her would have undone everything.

* * *

What remains strange to me, looking back, is how ordinary the last quarter-mile felt. My boots on the frozen mud. The sacking rough against my palm where I steadied the pendulum bob at my hip. The fog lifting in slow rags off the water. A crow somewhere upstream, complaining. All around me the world went about its small business while underneath it the ice groaned and split by fractions, and I thought — absurdly, professionally — that the river and I were keeping the same hour now, both of us counting down to a moment already fixed in the mechanism, unavoidable as noon.

There were wagons on the bridge. That saved me, I think. Two farm carts ahead, a drover with sheep, the ordinary traffic of a market morning, and the soldiers at the checkpoint were bored rather than alert, waving vehicles through with the weary economy of men who had stood in the cold since before dawn. Only Corvane stood alert. Only Corvane watched faces instead of carts.

I approached the barrier as Bram Almeida taught me to approach any inspection: slowly, openly, with the expression of someone carrying nothing more interesting than a bill of sale. I had dressed the part before dawn — my work apron over my coat, tools visible in the belt, the guild badge of my master's shop pinned where an inspector could read it. In my free hand I carried the paper Marguerite had forged two nights earlier, a delivery docket for a compensated pendulum, consigned to a watchmaker in the far town, marked *urgent — instrument repair*.

"Halt." The nearer soldier raised a hand. His breath steamed. "Business?"

"Delivery." I held out the docket. "Compensated pendulum, brass and lead, for the Halloran workshop. There's a regulation clock in the county court losing four minutes a day, and the assizes open next week, and everyone concerned is very anxious about it."

That earned me half a smile from the younger soldier. Bored men want to be done with you; give them a reason to be bored. He glanced at the docket, at the sacking-wrapped weight at my hip, at my apron and tools.

"Open it."

My blood went cold and hot at once. This was the moment I had rehearsed and dreaded in equal measure, and now that it had arrived I found my body acting without consultation of my fear, the way a trained hand finds the escapement in the dark. I shifted the bob around and unwound the sacking from its top, exposing the polished dome of brass, the suspension hook, the rating nut beneath. I held it up.

"Nine pounds," I said. "You're welcome to lift it. Mind your back — it's awkwardly balanced."

The soldier took it, staggered slightly, laughed at himself, and turned it in his hands. And I stood there watching another man's fingers travel over the thing that contained everything — Dominic Vasseur's confession rolled tight and waxed against damp, seated in the hollowed core of the bob beneath a threaded brass cap I had made with my own hands, invisible, watertight, indistinguishable from solid metal unless one unscrewed the base with the proper key — which I carried in my tool belt, disguised among a dozen similar keys, and which no clerk or soldier would ever think to ask for, because who searches inside a pendulum?

"Solid as a church bell," the soldier said, handing it back. "Move along."

I was rewrapping the sacking when Corvane's voice came from behind the barricade, mild and precise as a razor drawn from its sheath.

"One moment."

* * *

He came around the barrier unhurried, hat beaded with fog, and up close he had the sort of face that ages well because it was never warm to begin with — smooth, grey-eyed, courteous. I had seen his kind in my master's shop: gentlemen who examined a movement and found fault not because fault existed but because finding it established ownership of the room.

"A pendulum," he said. "For the assizes clock. How fitting." His gaze moved over me without hurry. "Your name, tradeswoman?"

"Pemberton," I said.

It was the gamble we had argued over until dawn. Not Vasseur — that name would stop his heart cold. Pemberton: a name on forged documents, a name the conspirators had spent years ruining, a name attached in their minds to a family safely ruined and scattered and watched, yes — but watched at the house, at the wharf, at the places a Pemberton might flee from. Not expected on a bridge, dressed as a clockmaker, handing over dockets. The best lies are true things worn inside out.

Something flickered in his eyes. "Pemberton."

"The firm is Ashworth and Sons, sir, Cheapside. I'm apprenticed there." I let my accent sit where it sat — not gentle, not grand, the careful neutral speech of a shop floor. "Is there a problem with the delivery?"

"There is a problem with the morning," Corvane said pleasantly. "We are looking for certain papers. Letters, perhaps. Perhaps instruments containing letters. One hears such fanciful things." He smiled. "You came from the east road? From town?"

"From the ferry crossing, sir. We lodged overnight at the Ferryman's Rest — the storm closed the road yesterday, as I expect it delayed you too."

Behind him, at the far end of the span, I heard it begin: a soldier's shout, then Marguerite's voice, high and clear and furious, performing outrage the way she had promised she could — *"Take your hands off that cart, I am a Vasseur of Harrow Lane, my grandfather is dying and you will answer for this—"* — and every head at the checkpoint turned toward the sound, including the young soldier's, including, for one blessed second, Corvane's.

I walked onto the bridge.

Not running. Running is a confession. I walked, a tradeswoman late for an urgent repair, the pendulum swinging gently against my hip with each stride, and behind me the shouting swelled — Marguerite's voice cracking beautifully over words I couldn't distinguish — and Corvane's boots struck the stones as he started toward the commotion, and then stopped, and then, horribly, started again in my direction.

"Stop there," he called. "You. Tradeswoman—"

And the river answered for me.

The sound I will hear for the rest of my life came up through the bridge stones first — a crack like the world's largest clock spring failing all at once — and then the whole ice field below us moved. Not broke; *moved*, a groaning, heaving mile-wide shift, plates grinding and riding up, the brown water showing through in black seams that widened as we watched, as I watched, halfway across the span with the fog tearing off the surface and the whole frozen river shuddering like a beast rising. The sheep bolted. A cart horse screamed. The soldiers at the barrier forgot me entirely, forgot Marguerite, forgot their posts, stumbling back from the parapet as a slab the size of a barn door tilted up downstream and sheared apart with a roar.

Corvane was shouting orders no one obeyed. I ran now — running was no longer a confession, merely prudence — across the remaining arches with the bridge trembling under my feet, and I did not stop at the far side, and I did not turn around, because I had promised.

Only at the bend in the road, where the bridge fell out of sight behind me, did I allow myself one glance back. Figures on the span, small in the mist: soldiers clustering at the rails, a cart abandoned, and one slight figure in a grey cloak standing upright amid the chaos with her hands still bound in the cart reins they'd seized, her face turned toward the far bank. Toward me.

Toward the Harrow Inn, and the green door, and the magistrate's man, and whatever terrible price the truth had chosen to charge for passage — paid, this morning, in full.

I tightened the strap across my shoulder, felt the pendulum bob knock once against my heart like a pulse, and ran.
