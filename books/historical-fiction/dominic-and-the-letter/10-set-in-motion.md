## 10. Set in Motion

The magistrate's clerk read the verdict aloud at half past ten on a morning so bright the courthouse windows threw diamonds across the floor, and Rosalind counted them without meaning to — a habit of a life spent measuring everything, in case measuring it was all she ever got to do.

She stood at the back of the courtroom between Marguerite and old Mrs. Pemberton, and she listened to words that had been three weeks arriving: *the documents are declared forgeries; the name Pemberton is struck from the indictment; the ring is broken; arrests have been made at all three checkpoints.*

The customs inspector first, taken at his own desk with a drawer full of sealed confessions he had never expected anyone to survive long enough to testify against. Then the ferry collector, who had wept, they said, when they came for him — not from remorse, Marguerite observed, but because his hands were too fat for the irons. And last the agent from the garrison bridge, the man who had held out his palm for their decoy case while the true evidence crossed the river inside a pendulum bob carried openly by a tradeswoman in a patched grey coat.

Rosalind had not slept properly since that night. Her body still woke at every groan of settling timber, every crack of late frost, listening for ice that was already gone downstream. But this morning, standing in the light, she felt something in her chest finally stop ticking against its case — the escapement of her fear releasing, tooth by tooth, into ordinary time.

Mrs. Pemberton's hand found hers and gripped it. The old woman did not weep. She had done her weeping in the nights after Dominic's death, and what remained now was something harder and quieter, like the polished brass of a clock face after years of winding.

"Sixty years," Mrs. Pemberton said, to no one in particular. "My husband's grandfather built ships, honest ones, and this town believed for six months that we built lies." She squeezed Rosalind's fingers until they ached pleasantly. "You gave us back our name, child."

"You gave me a place to carry it," Rosalind said, and immediately wished she had said something less foolish, but Mrs. Pemberton only nodded as if Rosalind had recited scripture correctly, and turned toward the door.

Outside, the thaw had done its work. The river ran high and brown and loud, carrying the last rotten plates of ice seaward, and the gulls screamed over the wharves where the conspirators' ship sat impounded under customs seal, its cargo rotting in the hold, its crew scattered or arrested or fled upcountry. Spring had come the way spring does in river towns — all at once, rudely, drowning the winter's careful silences under water-sound.

Marguerite walked beside her with her chin up and her jaw set, which was how Marguerite did nearly everything, and said nothing until they reached the corner where their roads would have divided, if Rosalind had still had a road of her own.

"He never knew," Marguerite said. It was not quite a question. They had circled it for three weeks the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

"No," Rosalind said gently. "He knew the night before. He asked me if the letter had gone. I told him yes."

"And the rest?"

"I told him the ice was breaking early. I told him we'd made it past the bridge." She looked down at her hands. "I didn't tell him about the decoy case. Or the soldiers. Or how close—" She stopped. "He died believing it was finished. I thought he'd earned that."

Marguerite studied her for a long moment, wind pulling loose strands of dark hair across her face. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, wet-edged laugh, the first Rosalind had heard from her since the ferry crossing.

"He'd have seen through you in a heartbeat, you know. Grandfather could smell a softened truth at forty paces. He kept ledgers for thirty years; he knew exactly how much things cost." Marguerite wiped her eyes briskly with the heel of her glove. "But he'd have let you tell it your way anyway. That's the thing about him. He understood mercy was also a kind of bookkeeping."

They stood together on the corner while carts rattled past, and neither of them moved to go, and eventually it became clear that neither of them had anywhere else to be at all.

* * *

The Vasseur house smelled of beeswax and dried lavender and, beneath both, faintly of medicine — the scent of a sickroom kept scrupulously clean after the sick had left it. They had buried Dominic on a hillside above the river, in a plot the family had owned since before the smuggling charges, back when names were simple things, and the stonecutter was still carving the dates.

Rosalind had been given the room at the top of the house, the small one under the eaves where the roof sloped so low she could touch the plaster from her bed. The first night, lying awake, she had catalogued her possessions out of habit: one valise, two spare collars, the good bodice, a set of pin-vice drills, her father's loupe worn to a whisper of glass, four shillings and threepence. Then she had looked around the room — the washstand with its china bowl, the quilted coverlet, the candlestick someone had filled fresh without being asked — and realized none of it fit in the valise. None of it was hers to carry. All of it was hers to keep.

She did not know yet what to do with an inheritance that weighed nothing.

On the evening after the verdict, Mrs. Pemberton summoned her to the drawing room. Rosalind climbed the stairs with the particular dread of a person who has been summoned to drawing rooms perhaps six times in her life, and never once for good news.

The fire was lit. Marguerite sat by it, pretending to read. On the table between the chairs lay something covered with a linen cloth, and beside the cloth, a document in fine rag paper, folded but unsealed.

"Sit," said Mrs. Pemberton.

Rosalind sat. The chair was deeper than chairs were meant to be; she perched on its edge like a bird on a wire.

"You've been with us nine weeks," Mrs. Pemberton began, in the tone of a woman reading terms aloud. "You arrived to repair a clock. You remained to save this family. In between you crossed a frozen river three times, lied to a soldier, lost a barometer, and taught my granddaughter to load a pistol, which I have chosen not to think about." Marguerite, behind her book, mouthed *you begged me to.* "During that time you also, I am told by every servant in this house, wound the hall clock, regulated the kitchen clock, repaired my late husband's carriage watch without being asked, and complained — loudly — about the state of the barometer in the front hall."

"The mercury column was separated. Anyone would have—"

"Hush." Mrs. Pemberton's eyes were bright and terrible. "I am seventy-one years old. I have buried a husband, a son, and as of eleven days ago the oldest friend of my marriage, who died owing us nothing and paying us everything. My remaining son lives in Calcutta and writes twice a year. My granddaughter intends to marry a surveyor and move to Canada, and don't think I don't know it, Marguerite, we will discuss it later." Marguerite lowered her book with an expression of pure alarm. "Which leaves this house, this name, and this business of living, requiring someone with steady hands and an inconvenient conscience to attend them."

"I don't understand," Rosalind said, though a wild, dangerous hope was already rising in her chest like water in a flood channel.

Mrs. Pemberton drew the linen cloth off the table.

Beneath it lay the master clock's pendulum bob — the very one, its seam re-soldered where Rosalind had cut it open on the far bank with shaking hands and a jeweler's saw, the letter's oilcloth wrapping still ghosting a dark line around its waist. Beside it lay a second object: a small travelling clock in a walnut case, its brass face engraved with the Pemberton crest, and beneath the engraving, freshly cut, spaces where new letters were waiting to be added.

"Your indenture papers expired last month," Mrs. Pemberton said. "Your master is dead, his shop is closed, and your tools are in the cellar where Marguerite put them, because I told her to. This house requires a clockmaker. This family requires—" she paused, and for a moment her voice failed her, and Rosalind saw the grief underneath the iron, the shape of a woman who had outlived everyone she measured her life against, "—this family requires a daughter. We are asking, formally, whether you will consent to be one. Not a servant. Not an apprentice. A Pemberton, if you'll have the name. It has been polished recently and comes with excellent references."

Rosalind discovered she could not speak.

She had imagined, in the long sleepless weeks, many versions of the future: the road out of town, another workshop, another city where nobody knew her. She had imagined gratitude, and repayment, and even — once, shamefully, at three in the morning — being asked to stay as staff, wages and a uniform and a place below stairs. She had built her defenses against every version of charity she could foresee.

She had not defended against this.

"The clock," Mrs. Pemberton continued, merciless in her kindness, "is your grandfather-in-law's, if we're to do this properly. It stopped the night Dominic died — the servants swear it simply wound down, though Marguerite says that's nonsense, and I say that in this family we are permitted a little nonsense. It wants cleaning. Regulating. A maker's touch." She pushed the paper across the table: the deed of adoption, drawn fair by the same magistrate's clerk who had read the verdict, the blank where a signature belonged open as a held breath. "And the bob is yours. Grandfather Vasseur left instructions. He said the girl who carries a pendulum across a frozen river should keep it, so she always knows exactly what her courage weighs."

Rosalind picked it up. It was heavier than she remembered — or her arms had grown stronger; she couldn't tell anymore. Cold brass warming slowly against her palms, the way everything in this house was warming, slowly, against her.

"I'm an orphan," she said at last, stupidly, because someone had to say it. "I come with nothing. I don't know how to be anybody's—"

"Neither do we," said Marguerite, dropping her book entirely and crossing the rug in three strides to kneel at Rosalind's side, taking her cold hands in her warm ones. "Grandfather used to say a family isn't a thing you're born into. It's a mechanism. Everyone a gear of a different size, meshing wrong at first, grinding, until you adjust the teeth." Her grip tightened. "We've been grinding beautifully for nine weeks, Rosalind. Say yes. Stop measuring everything. Some things aren't for carrying. They're for keeping."

Through the window, far below, the river ran loud and free, hauling the winter away. Somewhere in the house a clock struck eight — the hall clock, which Rosalind had wound herself that morning, its strike true to the second.

She picked up the pen.

Her hand did not shake. She noticed that it did not shake, and almost laughed, and signed — *Rosalind Pemberton* — in ink that gleamed wet and black in the lamplight, letters she had never written before and would write ten thousand times.

Mrs. Pemberton took the paper, examined the signature with a magistrate's gravity, and pronounced it, "In perfect order," which was, Rosalind would learn over the following years, the closest the old woman ever came to weeping.

Then she lifted the linen cloth fully away and gestured at the stopped clock with its waiting face.

"It's been silent eleven days," she said. "That's long enough. Whenever you're ready, daughter — set it in motion."
