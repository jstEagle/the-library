## 1. The Weight of Lead

The first thing Jude Ravensworth learned about lead was that it remembered everything you did to it and forgave nothing.

Their father had said it on the very first morning, when Jude was seven years old and their hands were still soft as candle wax, sitting them down at the cutting bench in the back room of Ravensworth & Son, Printers, and pressing a graver into their small fingers as though placing a sword in the hand of a knight. *Lead keeps your mistakes*, Ambrose Ravensworth had said, closing Jude's fingers around the tool. *Ink can be washed out. Paper can be burned. But a letter cut wrong is cut wrong forever, and every book it prints will carry your carelessness into houses you will never enter.* Then he had turned back to his own work, because Ambrose Ravensworth was not a man who said things twice, and left Jude alone with the metal and the terrible weight of being permanent.

Ten years later, Jude still felt that weight every time the graver touched the matrix.

The shop sat on Cordwainer Lane, three doors up from the fish market and half a street from the river, in that part of Vellmouth where the buildings leaned together like old conspirators and the cobbles were worn smooth by four centuries of feet. It was a narrow building, tall for its width, with a bowfront window on the ground floor that displayed their finest work — a wedding invitation for the Harrowgate family done in copperplate, a funeral card with a border of engraved lilies, sample pages of the almanac they printed every autumn for the whole eastern valley — and above it two floors of living space where the family had lived since before Jude was born. The sign hanging outside was older than Jude's father, its gilt gone the color of old honey, and the letters RAVENSWORTH had been re-cut twice, once by Jude's grandfather and once by Ambrose himself, so that the name over the door contained, in its very strokes, the handwriting of three generations.

Inside, the air always smelled the same: lead and oil, ink and paper-dust, the faint animal sweetness of the hide roller, and underneath it all the cold mineral breath of the river that came up through the cellar in damp weather. Jude could not remember a time when the smell had not been the smell of home. Other children had grown up on bread and milk; Jude had grown up on type-metal fumes, and suspected, without much distress, that it had permanently shaped their character.

It was the second of the winter months, and the light through the workshop's deep-set windows came in thin and blue, falling across the cutting bench in long slats. Jude worked with their sleeves rolled despite the cold, because precision required bare hands — gloves dulled the sense of the graver's bite — and because their father considered complaint a form of bad manners. Before Jude, clamped in the holding stick, sat a steel punch they had been finishing for three days: a capital Q, commissioned by none other than the Vellmouth Guildhall, which had decided after sixty years to have its record books reprinted in a proper proprietary face rather than the generic types ordered downriver like everyone else's. It was the largest commission the shop had received in two years, and Ambrose had treated it less like good fortune than like a test he intended to pass contemptuously.

"The tail is wrong," Ambrose said, from across the room, without looking up.

Jude looked down at the punch. The tail of the Q curled beneath the bowl in an elegant sweep they had spent the better part of a day refining. "The tail is correct. I checked it against the drawing nine times."

"You checked it against the drawing nine times, so you stopped seeing it." Ambrose set down his own graver at last and crossed the room, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who never wasted a motion because he had never in his life been in a hurry to be anywhere but where he was. He bent over the punch, held it to the window light, and turned it once between finger and thumb. "Look at where it crosses the baseline. It hesitates. A letter should never hesitate. The man who reads it won't know why the page feels uneasy, but he'll feel it, and he'll trust the book a little less, and he'll never know it was your fault."

Jude bit down on the retort rising in their throat, because it was true. They could see it now that he pointed — the infinitesimal thickening where the tail met the baseline, the stroke's confidence faltering for the width of a hair. Ten years at the bench, and their father could still find the flaw in anything inside thirty seconds.

"I'll take it down again," Jude said.

"Take it down a full degree and recut. You've been nursing it. Metal doesn't want nursing, it wants decisions." Ambrose straightened and returned to his own station, where a jobbing order waited — five hundred handbills for Ostler & Daughters, Fishmongers, announcing the winter prices for smoked herring, a job so routine it could have been set asleep. But even handbills got his full attention, because Ambrose Ravensworth held the belief, quiet and absolute as bedrock, that there was no such thing as unimportant printing. Somewhere in the world, someone would read those herring prices and make a decision based on them, and the decision would be made easier or harder depending on whether the figures stood clear on the page.

That was the creed of the house, unstated but practiced daily: print as if it mattered, because it did.

Outside, Vellmouth went about the business of the last fine days before winter closed its hand. Jude could hear the fish market through the window — Maren Ostler's voice carried over everyone's, as it had carried since she was fourteen and first took over her mother's stall — and beneath it the deeper sound of the river, low and brown and patient, sliding past the town on its way to the sea. From the upper floor came the intermittent thump of Old Hetty moving furniture overhead; she rented the second floor from them and paid partly in coin and partly in opinions, delivered at volume.

"Guildhall's nervous," Ambrose remarked, some while later, when Jude had recut the tail twice and was beginning to hate the letter personally. "Clerk who placed the order wanted delivery before the bridge closes. Wouldn't say why the hurry. Paid half upfront, which the Guildhall has never once done in my lifetime."

"They're always nervous in autumn," Jude said. "Accounts to close, sins to balance."

Ambrose made a noncommittal sound, the kind he used instead of disagreement, and the workshop settled back into its rhythm: the tick of gravers, the whisper of the burnisher, the slow breathing of the presses under their canvas covers in the next room. There were two presses. The great one, the workhorse, built by Weyland of Thornbury and older than the town hall; and the second, smaller one, retired these eight years with a cracked cheek and no successor ordered, standing shrouded in the cellar among the flood-stains and the stored paper. Jude had barely thought about it in years. Dead machines, like dead languages, simply became part of the furniture of a life.

At midday, Wren Corliss arrived.

Wren was the apprentice, sixteen to Jude's seventeen-and-a-half, sharp-boned, dark-eyed, perpetually inkstained to the elbows, and possessed of the firm conviction that silence was a resource to be spent as rarely as possible. They came up the lane at a run, burst through the door with cold air and river smell trailing behind, and hung their coat on the hook with the single fluid gesture of someone who had been scolded about it enough times that it had become reflex.

"Am I late? Don't answer. I'm late. Master Ravensworth, I'm late, I know, the ferry queue—"

"The ferry queue is not an excuse, it is a consequence," Ambrose said mildly, not turning around. "You knew the timetable. The distribution of blame begins at home."

"Yes, master." Wren grinned at Jude over their father's shoulder, entirely unchastened, and tied on their apron. "Have you heard? Half the town's heard. There were men at the ferry steps this morning asking questions. Strangers. Downriver accents, good coats, taking names of anyone who'd give them. Tam Hollis says they asked him how many men worked the mills and whether the workers had been paid since the dispute began."

"What dispute?" Jude asked.

Wren stared at them. "The mill dispute. The strike. In the mill district — the eastern quarter, by the sluice gates? Where three hundred people we have met are currently refusing to work?"

"I know where the mill district is." Jude fitted the Q back into the holding stick and drew the first cut of the recutting, watching the curl of bright metal peel away from the punch face. "I don't know that anyone struck about anything. That's tavern talk."

"It started because wages were cut in the same month the flour price rose, which is not talk, it's arithmetic," Wren said. "And last week a foreman had his windows put in, and yesterday — this is from Doss, who heard it from the carter — soldiers came to the mill district. Not town watch. Soldiers. In grey. Asking after ringleaders."

"Then the millers should have thought of that before breaking windows." Jude heard their own voice come out in their father's cadence and was obscurely pleased.

Ambrose, however, did not reinforce the position. He had paused in his work, graver resting against the handbill form, and his eyes — pale, exacting eyes that missed nothing at any distance — were fixed on the middle distance in the way he looked when calculating something. "Grey uniforms," he said. "Not garrison colours."

"No, sir. New issue, Doss said. Some kind of provisional arrangement, since the provincial government—" Wren shrugged elaborately. "Since whatever it is that's happened downriver has happened. Nobody in Vellmouth knows exactly what it is. That's rather the point of what I'm saying. We get our news the way we get our type — secondhand, downriver, and six weeks late."

"The news will keep," Ambrose said, and returned to his work, and that was the end of the conversation, because with Ambrose it always was.

But Jude found, through the afternoon, that the tail of the Q would not come right, and that their attention kept sliding off the punch and out the window, down toward the river. History, Jude had decided long ago, happened elsewhere. It happened in the capital, whose name appeared in their almanacs in boldface; in the provincial seats where magistrates were dismissed and appointed; in the great cities downriver where, according to the newspapers they reprinted for local customers, crowds assembled and ministers resigned and constitutions were written and torn up. Vellmouth printed other people's history. It did not generate its own. The town's contribution to the age consisted of herring prices, marriage banns, and the annual almanac, and Jude had never once felt this as a wound. It felt like safety. History was a flood that happened downstream; Vellmouth stood on high ground and cut its letters straight.

Evening came early that time of year, blue shadows pooling in the lane before the lamps were lit. Ambrose sent Wren home at dusk with instructions to be on time tomorrow that bordered on prophecy, and then went upstairs himself, leaving Jude to close the shop — a ritual Jude privately treasured and would have denied under torture. Closing meant putting the shop to bed: washing the rollers, covering the forme, banking the stove, and finally standing a moment in the darkened workshop listening to the building tick and settle.

Tonight, the ritual felt different, though Jude could not have said why. Perhaps it was only the season. Every winter the same thing happened: the ice came down from the upstream shallows, the great bridge — the only crossing for forty miles in either direction — was closed by ancient ordinance until the thaw, and Vellmouth sealed itself like a jar of preserves. No carts crossed. No post came but what the hardy ferried at their own risk. For five months the town talked only to itself, and by spring everyone was sick of everyone else's face and desperate for the first barge.

Usually Jude minded the closing the way one minds the end of summer: mildly, ceremonially, without real grief. Tonight, sweeping the floor by lamplight, they found themselves thinking about the men at the ferry steps. Good coats. Downriver accents. Taking names. Names were Jude's trade; they understood, better than most, what it meant to collect them. A name in a ledger was a small leaden thing, but ledgers had weight in aggregate, and somewhere downriver someone was building a list of Vellmouth, and nobody in the town had thought to ask what the list was for.

They were still holding the broom when the knock came — not at the shop door but at the side entrance, the household door, which strangers almost never used. Three knocks, measured, unhurried, the knock of a person confident of being admitted.

Through the window, in the light of the new gas lamp at the corner, Jude could see figures in the lane. Four of them, perhaps five, standing with the stillness of men who had nowhere else to be and all the time in the world to be there. Their coats were grey — a grey Jude had never seen on the streets of Vellmouth before — and the tallest of them stood facing the door directly, hands clasped behind his back, studying the house with the attentive expression of a man reading a page.

Upstairs, faintly, Old Hetty's furniture had gone silent.

Jude set the broom against the wall, wiped their hands on their apron out of pure habit — as if lead dust were the thing to fear tonight — and went to open the door.

* * *

Later, Jude would try to remember exactly what they had expected to find on the other side of that door, and would fail, because the truth was they had expected nothing. Complacency is not a thought; it is the absence of thinking, and it leaves nothing behind for memory to hold.

The tall man removed his hat before speaking, which Jude noticed, because courtesy from power is more frightening than rudeness. Rudeness tells you what you are dealing with. Courtesy makes you wait.

"Good evening," he said. His voice was pleasant, cultured, and carried in the cold air the way a blade carries light. "Ravensworth & Son? Printers?"

"Printers," Jude said. Behind the man, the others waited, and beyond them the river ran dark and swift in the direction of the sea, and somewhere far downstream, in a city whose name filled a column of the almanac in boldface, history was happening — or so Jude had believed, every contented day of their life, right up until this moment.

"We'd like to discuss the presses," the man said, smiling.
