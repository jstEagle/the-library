## 2. The Bridge Closes

The crier came at first light, when the frost still held the windows in its teeth and the town smelled of banked coal and cold river stone.

Jude heard him before they understood him — a bell struck irregularly, the way a man strikes it who has walked the same circuit a hundred winters and no longer cares to make it musical. The voice followed, flattened by fog into something without a source, drifting up Quay Lane and through the shutters of the print shop like smoke finding its way under a door.

*"By order of the Bridge Commission! The Vellmouth span is closed for the season as of this morning's tide! No passage until the ice breaks! God keep you all until spring!"*

Jude stood in the doorway with a candle guttering in their hand and watched the man pass. He wore the Commission's grey sash over a coat too thin for the work, and his breath came out in white plumes that the fog swallowed almost before they existed. Behind him, at intervals, two other criers took up the same words, so that the announcement seemed to move through the town of its own accord, a rumour given official flesh.

*Closed.* Jude said the word to themselves the way one tests a coin against the teeth. It was not news, precisely. The bridge closed every winter; it had closed every winter of their life. Somewhere in the shop's ledger room there were jobbing bills dating back forty years announcing the same closure, set in the same tired faces of type, *BRIDGE CLOSED UNTIL THE ICE BREAKS*, and every year the town had shrugged itself deeper into its coats and gotten on with things.

But this year the crier's voice had a different weight. Or perhaps it was only that Jude was older now, and had learned to hear what lived underneath official sentences.

"You'll catch your death standing in the draft," their father said from behind, not unkindly. Gideon Ravensworth did not look up from the composing stone where he was graining an ink roller, his big hands moving with the unhurried certainty of a man whose fingers knew more than his eyes did. "Come away. There's a jobbing order for the chandler and it wants doing before he changes his mind about the ornament."

"In a moment."

Their father grunted — the particular grunt that meant *suit yourself, but I told you* — and returned to his roller. The shop was warm around them in the way print shops are warm: not from any great heat, but from the closeness of lead and paper and lamp-oil, the dense animal warmth of a room full of worked matter. Jude loved that warmth. They had been born into it, more or less, delivered upstairs while a press below thumped out funeral invitations, or so their mother had liked to tell it, back when there was a mother to tell things.

Jude pulled their coat tighter and stepped out onto the doorstep.

Quay Lane ran downhill toward the water, and though the fog stood thick on the river, Jude could see enough: lamplighters finishing their rounds, a cart of turnips abandoned by a sleeping driver, and down at the ferry stairs a knot of people, small and dark as starlings, gathered in the grey.

The last ferry. Of course. There would be a last ferry this morning, the way there was every year — the final crossing before the ice made the river unnavigable and the bridge gates were chained. Every year some handful of people cut it fine, leaving on the final boat rather than be sealed in until thaw.

Every year Jude had watched it go with nothing more than idle curiosity, the mild entertainment of watching other people's departures.

This year they found themselves descending the lane before they had decided to, candle snuffed between finger and thumb, boots ringing on stones glazed with rime.

* * *

The crowd at the ferry stairs was larger than usual, and quieter than usual, and it was the quietness that Jude noticed first, and would remember longest.

Perhaps thirty souls stood on the frost-slick steps and the cobbles above them, baggage at their feet, breath rising in a collective pale haze. A merchant family with brass-bound trunks and the pinched, determined faces of people fleeing debts rather than pursuing fortune. Two young men in the rough clothes of mill hands, saying nothing, looking at the water as if it owed them something. An old woman with a canary in a wicker cage, which sang absurdly, sweetly, into the cold. And scattered among them others — single travellers, a clerk, a woman in mourning, a boy of perhaps twelve quite alone, holding a canvas sack with both arms as though it might bolt.

The ferryman, a barrel-chested woman named Ostler — Maren Ostler, Jude knew her distantly, she sold fish at the Wednesday market and had done since before Jude could read — moved along the gunwale taking last coins and counting heads with the flat efficiency of someone who had closed this crossing twenty times and intended to do it right.

"No more after this," she was saying, not unkindly, to anyone who would listen and several who would not. "You hear me? Ice comes early this year, I can smell it. Whatever you've forgotten, you'd best learn to live without till the thaw."

A man near the top of the stairs laughed, a short hard laugh with nothing in it. "Live without. That's the whole of Vellmouth in a sentence, isn't it."

Nobody answered him. That was the quietness. In other years, Jude remembered now, the last ferry had been an occasion almost festive — shouts and jokes and women waving handkerchiefs at relatives who'd only cross as far as Harsen's ferry landing on the far bank, nine miles downstream, close enough to shout across on a windless day. This year nobody waved. This year the people on the stairs looked at the water the way the two mill hands were looking at it, as though it were not a river but a wall being laid course by course.

Jude found themselves standing beside the old woman with the canary. Up close her face was a net of fine lines, and the bird's cage swung from one knotted hand.

"Going somewhere?" Jude asked, because silence had become unbearable.

"Going nowhere." She nodded at the ferry. "My daughter's on that boat. Going to her aunt in Corleigh, past the mill district, says there's no work and less food and the soldiers take what they like." She lifted her chin toward the far shore, invisible in the fog. "I'm staying. Somebody ought to know where she went, in case she writes."

"The post still runs," Jude said. "Over the ice, when it thickens. They walk the mail across every year."

"Do they." The old woman looked at Jude with eyes the colour of shallow water, and something in that look made Jude feel suddenly, obscurely foolish — a child reciting facts to a room of adults. "And do you think, young Ravensworth — you are Ravensworth's child, aren't you, I know your father's shop — do you think the post will run this year, with things the way they are?"

"With things the way they are how?" Jude asked. "I've heard talk, but my father says it's tavern noise."

The old woman smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, but it was the smile of someone setting down a burden. "Your father cuts letters, doesn't he. Pretty letters, on good paper, for people who pay." She shifted the cage to her other hand; the canary hopped, indignant. "Letters don't tell you much. Come spring, ask me again whether the post ran."

She turned away toward the gangplank, conversation finished, and left Jude standing in the frost with her words stuck to them like burrs.

*Tavern noise.* That was their father's phrase, deployed whenever talk of the capital reached Vellmouth — the Provisional Directorate, the dissolved Assembly, the purges in the mill districts two counties over, the newspapers that arrived thinner every month and printed duller every week. *Tavern noise,* Gideon would say, inking his roller. *Men always shout loudest when they've nowt to eat. Feed them and they'll go quiet.* And Jude had always accepted it, because their father was old and had seen governments come and go like weather, and because accepting it was easy, and because the alternative — believing that history was not something that happened elsewhere, in capitals, to other people — was a heavy thing to carry, and Jude's shoulders were already full of type cases.

The ferry loaded. The gangplank came up. Maren Ostler cast off the lines with two practiced pulls, and the flat boat slid out into the fog, and the whole crowd on the stairs watched it go — all except the daughter of the old woman, who could not be picked out, and the canary, which had gone silent at last, as if it understood better than any of them.

The fog took the boat in pieces: first the passengers, then the hull, then finally the sound of the oars, a slow wooden knock that faded and was gone, and then there was only the river, wide and white-breathed and indifferent, and the town behind them, and the bridge.

* * *

Jude walked out onto it on the way back, because the gate at the town end was still open — would be open until the Commission men came with their chains at midday — and they wanted, without fully admitting why, to stand once in the middle of the span before it became forbidden ground.

The Vellmouth bridge was old, stone-piered, half-timbered along its length, lined with the little shops and stalls that bridges accumulate over centuries like barnacles. Most were shuttered for winter already. A knife-grinder was packing his wheel. A woman selling roasted chestnuts from a brazier gave Jude one, free, because it was the last of the basket and she'd have thrown it out anyway. At the far end, visible now as the sun made a weak attempt at the fog, stood the Commission's barrier: new timber, new paint, and — Jude slowed — new iron. Fittings for chains heavier than any Jude had seen used before, bolted deep into the masonry, and above them a board already nailed up bearing fresh-lettered words:

**CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL DIRECTORATE. PASSAGE BY WRIT ONLY.**

Jude stopped and read it twice. The lettering was competent — machine-cut stencils, mass-produced, the same face Jude had seen on requisition notices in the market square these past weeks. But it was the words that snagged, the way a splinter snags a stocking.

*Passage by writ only.* Last year the board had said simply **CLOSED FOR THE SEASON**, and the year before, and the year before that, going back through the ledgers. There had never been writs. There had never been anything to need a writ *for*. You closed a bridge against ice, not against people. Ice did not apply for permission to cross.

"New this year," said the chestnut woman, following Jude's gaze. She spoke around a mouthful of her own wares, comfortably. "Ironwork came up from the coast on the last barge. Commission men put it up Tuesday, working double-handed, like it couldn't wait."

"Like it couldn't wait," Jude repeated.

"Well." She shrugged, wiped her hands on her apron. "They say there's trouble upriver. Trouble downriver too, if you believe the bargemen, and bargemen will say anything for a drink. Soldiers at Renford Crossing already. My sister's boy saw them. Says they're not the old garrison kind neither, that swagger about like peacocks. These ones are quiet." She said *quiet* the way one says *poisonous*, and then laughed at herself, briskly, brushing crumbs from her front. "Listen to me. Tavern noise, eh? My old man'd box my ears. Here—" she pressed another chestnut into Jude's hand, "—for the printer's boy. Tell your father I want those handbills for the Chestnut Queen done by Martinmas, and I'll not pay more than last year."

"I'm not the printer's boy," Jude said, out of long habit. "I cut the matrices. I'm his equal in the trade, or near enough."

"Are you, now." She looked at Jude with frank appraisal, the way she might assess a fish at market, weighing it for quality. "Well then. Tell the printer's *equal* I want those handbills by Martinmas."

Jude walked on toward the middle of the bridge, eating the chestnut, which was burnt on one side and raw on the other and somehow exactly right.

From the centre of the span, where the timbers hummed faintly underfoot with the river's push, Jude could see the whole of Vellmouth laid out along the north bank: the huddled roofs, the mill chimneys standing dead and smokeless — the mills had gone to half-shifts since autumn, another piece of tavern noise — the church spire, the market square with its early stalls, and above it all the slow fog lifting off the water in ropes and sheets. A town of four thousand souls, give or take the dead and the departed. A town you could cross end to end in half an hour, if the mud wasn't bad.

It had never looked small to Jude before. Now, leaning on the rail with the cold coming up through the planks and into their palms, they saw it plainly: a bowl. A dish with one road out, and the road closing today, and the river running black and fast beneath them toward whatever lay downriver — toward Renford Crossing with its quiet soldiers, toward the capital with its Directorate, toward everything that happened elsewhere.

*Somebody ought to know where she went.*

The old woman's voice, arriving unbidden. Jude shook it off and made themselves look at the practical matters, the comfortable ones. Closed bridge meant closed trade, meant lean months for the shop — fewer jobbing orders, no coastal commissions, the chandler and the Chestnut Queen and the chapel calendar carrying them till thaw as they always had. It meant short rations and long evenings and their father teaching Wren Corliss, the apprentice, to set type blindfolded as a party trick. It meant, in short, inconvenience. Nothing more. Winter came; winter went; the ice broke; the world resumed. Their father had lived through sixty of these cycles and had a theory about all of them.

Jude straightened up from the rail and turned back toward the town end, and that was when they saw the Commission men coming with the chains.

There were six of them, and they were not the usual Commission men — the usual were local, stooped, apologetic, known by name to everyone. These six wore grey coats of a cut Jude didn't recognise and moved in step with each other, and they carried the chains not draped over their shoulders the way the old men did, coiled loose as fishing nets, but stacked and strapped like ordnance. Behind them walked an officer in a better coat, gloved, unhurried, looking about the bridge with the mild proprietary interest of a man inspecting property recently acquired.

The townsfolk on the bridge melted off it ahead of the soldiers, quietly, without being asked — the chestnut woman dousing her brazier, the knife-grinder wheeling his grindstone away with small urgent squeaks, a stallholder gathering her ribbons and buttons into a cloth with hands that moved quickly and did not tremble, quite.

Jude stood their ground by simple stubbornness, and so was close when the officer reached the barrier and paused, and looked at the board with its stencilled letters, and then looked at Jude, and smiled.

"You'll want to be off the bridge," the officer said. His voice was pleasant. It was the pleasantness that lodged under Jude's skin like a cold splinter. "We're closing it now."

"It closes every winter," Jude said. "I've watched it close sixteen times."

"Has it." The officer considered this as if it were information of genuine value. "And in all those times, I wonder, did you ever think about who does the closing?" He did not wait for an answer. He turned to his men and gestured, and the chains began to come off the cart, link by iron link, each one landing on the stones with a sound like a struck bell, and Jude walked off the bridge at an ordinary pace with their back very straight and their heart beating oddly fast, and did not let themselves hurry until they reached the foot of Quay Lane.

Behind them the first chain-loop settled around the barrier post, and the sound followed them up the hill: iron on stone, iron on stone, patient as a clock.

* * *

The shop was warm. The shop was always warm. Their father had the second press running — the old decommissioned one kept for show and storage, its platen stacked with reams of dampened paper — and Wren Corliss perched on the composing stool, all elbows and ink-stained cuffs, fitting type with the fierce concentration of a cat watching a birdhole.

"There you are." Gideon Ravensworth glanced up, read his child's face in one sweep the way he read galley proofs, and frowned. "What's happened?"

"The bridge is closed."

"I heard the crier at seven. I mean, what else."

"Nothing else." Jude hung their coat, sat down at their cutting bench, took up a graver, put it down again. The brass blank sat in its vice where they'd left it, waiting to become a matrix, waiting to be struck and struck and struck until it gave a perfect negative of a perfect letter. Their father's craft. Letters that made letters. The deepest, quietest level of the trade, and Jude was good at it, and had always supposed that was enough. "There were soldiers at the barrier. New ones. Grey coats. They've fitted ironwork for chains, and there's a sign now — *passage by writ only.*"

Gideon's roller stopped moving. For three full seconds the shop held only the tick of the press and the river's distant murmur through the floorboards. Then it resumed, steady as ever.

"Writs," he said. "Well. Bureaucracy grows everywhere, even here." But he said it a shade too evenly, the way a man walks carefully on a floor he suspects is rotten. "Wren, that 'm' is wrong. Look at it. You've set it backwards."

"It's not backwards, master, it's— oh." A pause, a chuckle, the soft clatter of type being redistributed. The small crisis passed. The press ticked. Outside, far off, the bell of the church rang ten, and somewhere below it, faintly, Jude could still hear — or imagined they could hear — the chains going on, link by link, all the length of the bridge.

That night Jude did not sleep well. They lay in their loft above the shop listening to the river, which had begun, very softly, to carry a new note underneath its murmur — the first whisperings of ice forming far upriver, grinding its slow way down toward them — and they thought about the old woman's canary, and the officer's smile, and *somebody ought to know where she went.*

Sixteen closures witnessed. Sixteen springs of breaking ice. It had always been a rhythm, as natural as breathing.

Lying in the dark, Jude understood — dimly, unwillingly, the way one understands a tooth is bad before it ever aches — that this year something had been locked in along with the town. Not the bridge. Something behind the bridge. And that when the ice broke in spring, it would not carry away whatever had been sealed inside.

Downstairs, the presses stood silent in the dark, full of sleeping lead, full of everything that had ever been printed there and everything that soon would be.
