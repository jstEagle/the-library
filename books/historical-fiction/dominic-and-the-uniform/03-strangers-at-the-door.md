## 3. Strangers at the Door

The knock came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour after the morning's first proofs had been pulled but before the stove had warmed the shop enough to take the chill from the type metal. Jude heard it as a sound without meaning — three raps, measured and unhurried, the kind of knocking that did not expect to be kept waiting and yet was willing to wait all the same.

Their father did not hear it at all. Ambrose Ravensworth was bent over his graver, cutting a new matrix for a client in Harrowgate — a shipping manifest head, nothing finer than commerce — and when he worked he inhabited a country of his own, population one. Jude set down the burnisher, wiped their palms on the leather apron, and crossed the floor between the two presses, past the racks of standing type that smelled of oil and old winters.

Through the leaded glass of the door, figures stood in the street like a sentence not yet justified: four soldiers in the slate-grey of the Provisional Directorate, and before them a man in a long coat the colour of wet ash, hands folded neatly at his waist.

Jude opened the door.

"Ravensworth's," the man said. It was not quite a question. He read the name from a paper he carried, then read the shopfront above the door, then read Jude — the apron, the ink-stained fingers, the face that could not decide whether to be nineteen or sixty depending on the light. "Established 1811 by Josiah Ravensworth. Passed to Ambrose Ravensworth upon the founder's death. Currently employing—" he glanced at the paper again, though Jude suspected he had already memorised it "—two. A master and an apprentice."

"My father employs me," Jude said. "Wren Corliss is apprenticed. And you have the advantage of me, sir."

"Aldous Verrek. Captain, Office of Public Information, Provisional Directorate of Vellmouth District." He said it the way a man recites his own name at a wedding — with the mild pleasure of someone who expects the title to do work for him. Behind him the soldiers shifted, snow creaking under their boots. One of them yawned. "We've come about your premises."

"The shop isn't for lease."

"No," Verrek said pleasantly. "It's requisitioned."

* * *

Ambrose came to the door wiping graver swarf from his hands, and Jude watched something pass over his father's face — not fear exactly, but the particular stillness of a man recalculating. Ambrose Ravensworth had survived three governments without ever once, so far as Jude knew, having an opinion about any of them. He had printed proclamations for the old magistrate, bills of lading through the Interregnum, and last spring a commemorative card for the mill owners' banquet. His politics were typographic: whatever the age set in metal, he would print it cleanly.

"Captain," his father said now, with a small bow that cost him nothing and meant less. "You'll want to come in out of the cold."

"We will," said Verrek, and they came in.

They filled the shop badly. Soldiers were not built for the close aisles of a printing house; one caught his rifle sling on the composing stone and swore, and another knocked a galley of fourteen-point Baskerville into pied type with his hip and did not apologise. Jude gathered the scattered letters afterwards on their knees while Verrek walked the floor with his hands behind his back, and felt each ruined em-quad like a small personal wound.

What Verrek wanted, it emerged, was everything and nothing. The Directorate did not intend to garrison the shop. They intended, he explained, standing beneath the great iron frame of the Albion press as though inspecting a cathedral organ, "to bring order to the flow of information." Vellmouth was entering a delicate season. There were persons — he did not specify whom; he waved a hand toward the river, toward the mill district, toward the whole grey world beyond the glass — who profited from disorder, who spread rumours, who printed sedition under cover of night. The Directorate required that all matter originating from this establishment be submitted for approval prior to publication. All matter. Ballot papers. Funeral cards. Invoices.

"You want to approve my invoices," Ambrose said.

"I want to approve everything printed in this town." Verrek smiled. He had a good smile, well-made, even white, and Jude would remember later how much damage a good smile can do. "Think of it as proofing, Mr. Ravensworth. You are a craftsman; you understand proofing. We merely wish to see the page before the public does."

"And if we decline?"

Verrek turned from the press and looked at him with genuine, almost tender curiosity, the way one looks at a man who has asked what happens if one declines to breathe. "Then the shop ceases to hold a licence, and some other printer — there is always another printer, Mr. Ravensworth; there are men in Harrowgate who would swim the river for this equipment — prints what the town needs instead. You'd be compensated for the premises. Fairly. The Directorate is not a robber." He touched the cheek of the Albion's frame with one gloved finger, delicately, the way a man touches a horse he intends to buy. "But you won't decline. Printers are practical people. Ink runs toward the lowest resistance, like water."

He was wrong about ink, and Jude, kneeling among the spilled letters with a *g* biting into their knee, knew he was wrong, and said nothing, and hated themselves for the silence the way one hates a first lie.

The inspection took an hour. Verrek examined everything: the stone where the formes were locked, the cabinet of matrices, the drawer of ornaments and fleurons which he flipped through with something approaching real interest. He asked about output — how many sheets a day, what classes of work, for whom. Ambrose answered in the flat, complete, unhelpful way of a man giving exact truth to an imprecise question, and Jude watched Verrek understand that this would be the shape of their dealings and found, disquietingly, that the captain seemed to *enjoy* it.

At the end he produced two printed forms, already signed, already stamped with an eagle-and-bar device Jude had not seen before, and laid them on the composing stone.

"One copy for the premises. One for yourself. Read them; they're short; we tried to make them humane." He buttoned his coat. At the door he paused, and turned back, and looked directly at Jude for the first time since entering.

"You cut type?" he asked. "Yourself? On the punch?"

"When it's wanted."

"Then you know," Verrek said, "better than anyone in this room, that a letter is only lead until it's given a word. And a word is only ink until it's given authority." He smiled his good smile again. "We're here to provide the authority. Do keep cutting the letters."

The door closed. The cold came in with the last soldier and stayed a moment, then left. The shop was very quiet. From the street came the sound of boots receding toward the square, four men and one captain walking in step, and then only the river, far off, doing what rivers do.

* * *

Ambrose did not speak for a long while. He read the requisition form twice, holding it at arm's length, then set it down on the stone precisely in its centre as though it might bruise.

"Well," he said. "It's paperwork. I've survived worse paperwork."

"Father—"

"They'll want proclamations. Commemoratives. Whatever the new men print, we print it clean, we print it fast, we take their money, and in two years there'll be different men and different paper. This town has changed flags four times since I was your age. Flags don't pay debts. Work does." He said it in the voice he used for lecturing apprentices, steady, reasonable, finished, and went back to his graver as though the morning were over.

But his hand was not steady. Jude saw it — the tiny tremor as the graver found its line, a hesitation that had not been there at dawn. Their father, who had cut ten thousand letters without a wobble, wobbled.

Jude did not go back to the burnisher. They stood by the window instead, watching the street. Snow had begun again, fine as sifted flour, dusting the wheel-ruts. Across the lane the fishwives were setting up their stalls despite the weather, Maren Ostler among them, her voice carrying even through glass as she argued the price of cod with a man whose hat was too big for him. Ordinary life, going on. Except that it wasn't ordinary anymore; except that every bill she chalked and every handbill the chandler posted and every funeral card in every window of Vellmouth now passed first across the desk of Captain Aldous Verrek, who liked fleurons and had a good smile and thought ink ran downhill.

*There are no other towns,* their father liked to say. *History happens elsewhere, child, and we get its leavings.* Jude had believed it the way one believes the floor. Now the floor had been requisitioned.

They looked down at the galley of pied type still spread on the sheet before them, the day's ruined setting — a bill for the chandler, of all things, SALT, TAR, AND CORDAGE in sturdy Roman capitals, harmless as bread. Harmless. Verrek's word for it. And underneath the harmlessness, faint, the thing the captain had actually said with his finger on the press: *a letter is only lead until it's given a word.*

Jude picked up the letters one at a time and sorted them back into the case. S. A. L. T. Each one cold, each one heavier than it looked, the way lead always is. Somewhere below the floorboards, in the dark of the old cellars where the river seeped in during spring, water was moving — the thaw was months off, but water never entirely stopped. They thought of the decommissioned press their father never spoke of, the second Albion, sold-on-paper and never collected, too heavy to shift and too stubborn to scrap. Everyone in the trade assumed it had gone to rust down there in the dark.

Everyone assumed.

"Jude." Their father, not looking up. "That look on your face. Put it away."

"What look?"

"The one your mother wore." The graver whispered along its line. "Put it away, and hand me the counterpunch."

Jude handed him the counterpunch. But the look — the flicker, the first spark struck off the flint of that morning's humiliation — did not go away. It sat behind their ribs all afternoon while they composed the Directorate's first approved job, a notice of curfew hours in fourteen-point, set perfectly because their hands were proud even when the rest of them was not, pulled twelve clean copies, and laid one aside for Captain Verrek's desk.

And it was still burning at dusk, when Wren Corliss came up the cellar stairs with lamp-black on their knuckles and a question they had clearly been carrying all day, and asked, too casually, whether Jude had ever been all the way to the back wall of the lower cellar, where the flood marks reached.

Jude looked at their apprentice — at the smudged hands, the too-innocent face — and understood that they were not the only one in Vellmouth who had heard the captain's words and taken them for something other than a warning.

"Show me," they said.
