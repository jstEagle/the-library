## 4. What the Type Remembers

The requisition notice had been nailed to the doorframe of Ravensworth & Son with four brass tacks, and it was the brass that offended Jude most. Anyone could pound a nail through paper with an iron tack; brass said *we intend to stay*. It said: this is not temporary occupation but permanent arrangement, the way a coffin lid is a permanent arrangement.

Jude read it each morning on the way in, as one reads a wound to check whether it has begun to close. *By order of the Provisional Directorate, all printed matter produced at this establishment shall be subject to prior review by the Office of Public Information. Contravention constitutes sedition.* Below the text, in a hand too elegant for its meaning, the signature: *A. Verrek, Capt.*

Sedition. The word had a taste to it now, like copper coins.

Inside, the shop smelled the way it always had — ink and oil and the faint animal sweetness of the hide rollers — but the soldiers' presence had changed its acoustics. Boots on floorboards where slippered feet used to shuffle. The two men Verrek had left behind sat by the window doing nothing with tremendous attention, which Jude had learned was more dangerous than soldiers who did something. Men doing nothing had time to look.

So Jude worked. There was nothing else to do and no better place to hide. The Directorate wanted pamphlets — *The Restoration of Order in Vellmouth*, first in a promised series — and until the father finished cutting the display capitals they'd demanded (fat, triumphant letters, letters like medals pinned to a chest), there was only the ordinary work to get through. Invoices for Harrow & Locke, the corn factors. A funeral card for old Mrs. Tam, seventy-one years, *at peace*. Price lists for the fish market, which Maren Ostler would pin up among the gutted pike without reading twice.

It was while clearing the long drawer of jobbing type — the miscellaneous catch-all where odd sorts migrated like sediment — that Jude found it.

Not found it, exactly. Found *them*. A bundle, wedged flat against the bottom of the drawer beneath a false lining of oiled paper that had been glued down so long ago the glue had gone amber and brittle. The lining lifted at one corner when Jude's bodkin slipped while chasing a stray em quad, and the corner lifted, and the bodkin went still in their hand, because beneath it there was paper that was not type.

Jude glanced toward the window. The soldiers were arguing over dice. The father was at his cutting bench in the back room, the little rhythmic *tk-tk-tk* of the graver coming through the doorway like a second heartbeat.

Jude drew the bundle out.

There were eleven sheets, folded once, printed on both sides in a crabbed, hurried setting — someone had set this fast, set it frightened, you could see it in the doubled letters and the bad justification, lines packed tight as refugees. The paper itself told the first part of the story: it was thirty years old if it was a day, foxed at the edges, browned along the folds, carrying the smell of a drawer that had been shut since before Jude could walk. The watermark, when Jude held it to the light, belonged to a mill that had burned down in Jude's childhood.

Then Jude read it, and stopped noticing the paper altogether.

*THE TRUTH OF THE MILL LANE,* ran the header, in woodcut capitals rough enough to splinter the eye. And below, in dense columns: names. Dates. A count — *one hundred and forty souls shot or drowned in the night of the twelfth* — followed by testimony set in quotation marks, the words of a widow, a ferryman, a boy of nine who had hidden in a culvert. And at the foot, in smaller type, as though the setter had run out of courage gradually: *Printed in Vellmouth. He who reads, remember. He who remembers, speak.*

Jude knew — not from any lesson but from the marrow-level knowing of a town's child — that there had been trouble at Mill Lane before the current troubles. A strike, a lockout, soldiers. Everyone's grandfather had a version. Nobody's grandfather told it twice. It had gone into the ground of local memory like something buried without rites, unmarked, un-mourned in public, and the town had walked over the spot for three decades pretending the grass grew naturally that way.

But this — this was the thing itself. This was the broadsheet that must have been printed in some cellar by some hand and then hunted, gathered, burned, until the only surviving copies hid in a type drawer in a shop whose owner had chosen, apparently, to glue them into darkness rather than feed them to a fire.

Jude looked again at the justification, the doubled letters, the hurried setting. And then at the types themselves — because a compositor reads a page the way a mother reads a face — and felt the floor tilt very slightly underfoot.

These were their types. Their shop's types. The old pica, retired these twenty years, with its characteristic nick in the *e* that the father complained about all his life and never replaced because, he said, a flaw you know is worth ten perfections you don't. Someone in this room, standing roughly where Jude stood, had set this truth in Ravensworth type. Had locked it in a chase, pulled proofs on a press that might have been this press, and then — what? Distributed? Hidden? Both?

And the father had kept them. Not one copy as a curiosity. Eleven. A printer keeps one proof out of pride. A printer keeps eleven out of intention.

*He who reads, remember.*

"Ravensworth."

One of the soldiers at the window. Jude slid the drawer shut with their hip in the same motion, the bundle pressed flat against their stomach beneath their apron, feeling the paper's age like a small cold animal against their ribs.

"The captain wants your answer on the commemorative order today. The fat capitals."

"They're being cut," Jude said. "Type is cut by hand. It takes the time it takes."

The soldier chewed on that, found nothing to spit at it, and returned to his dice.

Jude carried the sheets through the back room — past the father's bench, past his lowered eyes — and into the water closet at the rear of the building, where they unfolded them under the tiny window and read every word twice more while their heart performed strange arithmetic.

Because here was the thing that turned cold in Jude's stomach and stayed cold. The broadsheet described Mill Lane: the lockout, the massing of strikers at the gates, the order given in the dark, the bodies in the canal, the official account — *three deaths, regrettable, provoked by armed agitators* — versus the true count, *one hundred and forty*, attested name by name. And Jude, reading it, heard the sentences of the present coming back off the page as if shouted down a well. The mill district again. The strikes again. The disappeared again — Wren Corliss's cousin among them, the names Jude had been collecting in secret ever since the Directorate came, writing them in pencil on the backs of invoice copies because there was nowhere else safe to put grief. The same official grammar. The same regrettable arithmetic. Thirty years between the two events like the space between two impressions from the same plate.

The Directorate had not come to Vellmouth with new crimes. It had come with reprints.

And whoever had set this sheet, thirty years ago, had known — had *known*, had written it into the very last line in type that still trembled in Jude's hands — that this was how it went. That the crime recurred. That the record was the only thing that did not.

Jude thought of their own penciled list. Thought: I am not beginning anything. I am resuming.

The father was alone at the bench when Jude came back through. The soldiers had gone out — the door stood open to the street's grey afternoon, letting in the smell of snow. Jude waited until it closed behind them, then laid the topmost sheet on the bench beside the half-cut capital *O*, right on top of the oiled rag.

The graver stopped.

For a long moment the father didn't look at it. He looked at his hands, which were steady in the way of hands that have decided to be steady, and then he looked at the sheet, and Jude watched thirty years arrive in his face all at once, like weather.

"Where," he said.

"Bottom of the jobbing drawer. Under the lining." Jude kept their voice low. "You glued it down yourself?"

"I paid the glazier's boy to do it. My hands shook too much." He picked up the sheet by its edge, delicately, as though it might go off. "I was twenty-six."

"You set this."

"I set it, I printed it on the old press in Tam Olliver's boatyard, and inside a week two of the men who helped me carry the formes to the river were taken and one came back wrong and one didn't come back." His thumb moved across the header, not quite touching the ink. "The Directorate that was then — different name, same coats. They burned every copy they could find. They burned the boatyard. They made a list of everyone who could set type, and they visited us one at a time, alone, in rooms with the windows covered, and they explained the future to us." He set the sheet down with terrible gentleness. "Your grandfather bought my silence without knowing he was buying it. He owned the shop. If I fell, it fell. So I glued the rest into the dark and I put the flaw in my own memory where the flaw in the *e* is, and I let it be, and it worked. Thirty years, it worked."

"And now they're doing it again." Jude heard their own voice come out harder than they'd meant. "Mill Lane again. The same sentences. Father, I've been writing the names — the ones taken from the mill district since the bridge closed — I have nineteen already—"

"Nineteen." He closed his eyes as if the number had struck him across the mouth. When he opened them they were wet and furious and afraid in unequal parts. "And what will you do with nineteen names? Set them? Print them? And when they take you, what will I have traded? My son for my silence, then my daughter for my son—" He stopped. Corrected himself, or couldn't. "My child. My shop. Everything this family is, which is its hands."

"It isn't only about us."

"No," he agreed, softly. "That is precisely what makes it unforgivable. If it were only about us, cowardice would be cheap." He gathered the sheets together, squared their edges with the automatic precision of forty years at the case, and held them out — not to return to the drawer, Jude saw, but toward the stove, whose iron side ticked with heat. "Burn them. Now, tonight, before the thaw of your conscience finishes. Paper survives everything except the man who owns it deciding it's worth less than the people in his house. Burn them and set your nineteen names free the only way that's safe — forget them. Grief is not a genre, Jude. It does not need to be published."

Jude did not take the sheets. Nor did the father insist; his arm simply stayed extended over the bench, holding thirty years of fear in the shape of eleven folded pages, and the stove ticked, and outside a patrol's boots went by in step, four boots, then eight, the sound arriving and departing like a tide.

"Who were they?" Jude asked quietly. "The two who helped you carry the formes."

The father's jaw moved. "Tam Olliver's sons."

"And what does anyone in this town know about them? What's left? One sentence each, and half of that invented. That's what burning costs, Father. Not safety. Safety I understand, God help me, I want it more than you know. But what it costs *others* is that boy in the culvert, ninety years old now or dead, who told the truth to a press and got silence for an answer. You kept eleven copies for thirty years. A man certain he did right burns them. A man hoping somebody braver would find them glues them in a drawer."

The sheets trembled — barely, a vibration in the father's forearm that Jude might have imagined. He withdrew his arm. He set the bundle on the shelf above the bench, between the ledger and the lamp oil, in plain view, which was either surrender or accusation, and turned back to the half-cut *O*, and after a moment the graver began again: *tk-tk-tk*, slower than before.

Jude took the bundle upstairs to the room over the shop where the bed was and the window faced the river, and sat a long time with the pages in their lap while the light died. Below, faintly, the father still worked. Somewhere beyond the frozen bridge, in a town sealed like a jar, other people were also lying awake with truths they had nowhere to put. Nineteen names in pencil. Eleven pages in foxed paper. One press upstairs that the Directorate watched, and counted, and approved.

Which meant the work, if there was to be work, could not happen upstairs at all.

Jude was still turning that thought like a piece of type examined against the light — feeling for the nick, the flaw, the tell — when the trapdoor to the cellar stairs shifted in their memory. The old cellar. The flooded one, that nobody entered since the river had begun coming up through the sump every spring. The one where, according to the inventory ledger the father kept and never let anyone read, the shop's second press had been carried down thirty years ago and listed, in the father's own cramped hand, as *decommissioned — below waterline.*

Decommissioned. Or hidden.

Jude rose, took a candle from the sconce, and went looking for the ledger.
