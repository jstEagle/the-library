## 5. The Cellar Press

Wren Corliss had been in the cellars of Ravensworth & Son perhaps a dozen times since apprenticing there, and each time had felt like trespassing on the shop's own memory. The stairs went down farther than anyone expected — fourteen steps, then a landing, then six more — and at the bottom the air changed entirely. It was colder, denser, tasting of river-stone and old ink and something faintly sweet, like paper that had given up on ever being read again.

The spring floods came every year to Vellmouth; everyone knew that. The river swelled behind the bridge pilings, backed up through the storm channels, and found its way into every low place in the town as if it were taking inventory of what it owned. In the Ravensworth cellars it always left a few inches of brown water over the flagstones before retreating, and Master Ravensworth's standing instruction was simple: let it drain, sweep the silt, touch nothing.

Wren had obeyed that instruction for two years without ever once being tempted to break it. There was nothing down there but broken furniture, crates of ruined stock, and the smell of drowned seasons.

Then came the night after the broadsheet.

Wren could not stop thinking about it. Jude had shown them — carefully, with the lamp shaded and the door bolted — the old jobbing work pulled from a drawer no one opened: a suppressed broadsheet from the purge of the mill district, twenty years gone, printed right here in this very shop by hands long dead. Names set against names. The Directorate's own crimes, repeated like a bad habit of state. Wren had held the brittle paper and felt something shift in their chest, some load-bearing beam of the world quietly cracking.

Jude wanted to do something about it. Wren could see it in the way they spoke — faster than usual, quieter than usual, glancing at the ceiling as though the words might leak upward through the floorboards into the soldiers' requisitioned front room. But wanting was one thing. The big press upstairs sat under Captain Verrek's seal now, its output logged and pre-approved, its every impression an act of permission.

Which meant, if anything was going to be done, it would have to be done somewhere Verrek's eyes did not reach.

It was Wren who remembered, or half-remembered, and it was remembering that brought them down those twenty steps on a raw grey evening with a storm-lantern in one hand and their heart going like a treadle.

* * *

"Master Ravensworth said there was never a second press," Wren said, holding the lantern high so its light swung across the cellar's far wall. "He told me my first week. 'We are one press and one family, Corliss, and both are quite enough trouble.'"

"He told you that while you were carrying his dinner tray," Jude said, wading ahead through six inches of icy water with their boots tied around their neck on a string. "My father lies the way he sets type — beautifully, and only when necessary."

The water was bitter. It had come up early this year, fed by a wet autumn, and it lay flat and black between the stone piers, swallowing the lantern light rather than returning it. Wren followed in Jude's wake, trousers rolled to the knee, feet going numb in stages. Somewhere overhead a board creaked — the father, moving through the shop above, closing shutters. They both froze until the sound passed.

"It's this way," Jude said. "There's a bricked-up arch at the back, under the yard. My grandfather used it for storage when the shop did jobbing work for the tanneries. Father had it sealed after the last bad flood. He never talks about what's back there."

"And how do you know what's back there?"

"I don't. Not exactly." Jude stopped at a wall of mortared brick, dark with damp, and put their palm against it. "But I know my grandfather kept things. And I know my father seals things he doesn't want to think about. Put those together and you get a wall nobody's asked questions about for thirty years."

The mortar was old and the flood had been eating at it for decades; the lowest courses had gone soft as old bread. They worked with a cold chisel and a wooden mallet, taking turns, prying out bricks one at a time with a patience neither of them felt. Wren kept expecting the whole thing to be nothing — a cavity full of mud, a rat's nest, the ordinary disappointments of cellars. They kept their mouth shut about it. That was another thing they'd learned in two years at Ravensworth & Son: want a thing too loudly and the world hears you and takes it away on principle.

The fourth course came out easier than the third, and the fifth easier still, and then a gap opened wide enough for a lantern, and Jude reached through with the light, and Wren saw their face change.

"Well?" Wren demanded. "Well?"

"Corliss," Jude said, and their voice had gone strange and reverent, "come look at what my grandfather buried."

* * *

The arch beyond the wall opened into a low vaulted room, deeper than Wren had guessed — deep enough that the lantern seemed feeble, its circle of light swallowed by darkness on all sides. The flood had gotten in here too. Water stood shin-deep on a floor of packed earth, and everything in the room wore a skin of it: crates stacked and rotting, a hand-truck furred white with mold, coils of rope gone stiff as wire.

And in the center, on a raised stone platform built precisely so the water would pass beneath it and not over it, stood a press.

Even drowned in shadow, even furred with silt along its every ledge, Wren knew it at once for what it was, because they had spent two years oiling and feeding and cleaning its sibling upstairs. It was smaller — a proof-and-jobbing press, half-size, the kind used for cards and bills and small-run pamphlets — but it was unmistakably kin. Same iron spine. Same screw and platen. Same great counterweighted lever arm folded against its frame like a sleeping bird's wing.

"Somebody built it a platform," Wren breathed. "Somebody *planned* this."

"My grandfather planned it." Jude climbed up onto the platform, careless now of the water, running their fingers along the frame where the maker's plate should be. Silt filled the stamped letters; they scraped at it with a thumbnail. "Look. Look at the date on the plate."

Wren leaned close. Under the grime, in worn serif capitals: **VOLLMAR & SONS, IRONWORKS — VELLMOUTH**. And below that, a year. Wren did the arithmetic twice because the first answer refused to make sense.

"That's the year of the mill purge," Wren said slowly. "That's the year of the broadsheet."

"Now you're beginning to see it." Jude sat back on their heels, and in the lantern light their face looked older and younger at once. "Twenty years ago this shop printed the truth once, got away with it by a thread, and my grandfather bought a second press, hid it behind a wall, and told everyone — including, eventually, I think, himself — that it had been sold south years before. He wasn't hiding a press, Corliss. He was hiding a *capability*. An argument you can't unmake."

Wren stared at the machine. Something about it — the patient iron of it, sitting in the dark all those years waiting under the flood line with more faith in the future than any person Wren knew — made their throat tighten absurdly. They were glad of the dimness, because they did not particularly want Jude Ravensworth to see their eyes go hot over a piece of machinery.

"He never told your father?"

"If he told my father, my father has spent thirty years pretending a whole archway of his own cellar isn't there. Which, knowing my father, is entirely possible." Jude wiped their hands on their already-ruined trousers. "Either way he'll call this madness. He'll say the risk outweighs—"

"He'll say whatever keeps the shop open and you alive," Wren said. "With respect to the master."

"With respect to the master," Jude agreed. Then, quieter: "So we don't tell him. Not yet. Not until it turns and prints. A press that doesn't run is just a rumor made of iron. Let him disbelieve something that actually exists."

They shook hands on it there in the flooded vault, two cold wet hands gripping across a dead man's secret, and Wren felt the grip travel all the way up their arm and settle somewhere behind their ribs. It was the first conspiracy of Wren Corliss's life, and it fitted better than any coat they'd ever owned.

* * *

Restoration began the next evening and consumed every evening after.

The work had to happen in daylight hours too — the press needed cleaning, and cleaning meant rags and solvent and noise and time — but the cellar could not be visited by daylight without explanation. So they stole their hours in slivers. Wren learned to move through the shop above like a cat working out a route across a busy street: the moment Master Ravensworth bent to his composing stick, Wren was down the fourteen steps and the landing and the six more; the sound of his cough or his stool creaking was the signal to be elsewhere, innocent, holding a broom.

They carried supplies down in installments, disguised. Oil for the ways went down in a fish-oil jug among the household stores. Clean rags went down in a basket of laundry. A file, a whetstone, spare screws lifted from the repair drawer one at a time so the gaps would never be noticed together. Wren discovered they had a talent for this — for the small logistics of secrecy, the choreography of the unremarkable. It surprised them. They had always thought of themselves as the timid one, the apprentice, the extra pair of hands. But timidity, it turned out, had simply never been given anything worth being brave about.

The press itself was a lesson in what water does and does not destroy. Iron, it turned out, mostly waits. The frame was sound; the screw turned, groaning, after three days of penetrating oil applied drop by drop from a can with a spout fine as a pen nib. The platen's face was pitted with light rust that they took off with fine emery and endless patience, Jude doing the final passes with a cloth and crocus powder until the steel came up mirror-bright enough to hold the lantern flame. What the flood had murdered was the wooden parts — the frisket, the tympan, parts of the lever linkage — and these they rebuilt from seasoned stock scavenged bit by bit: a plank from the loft, a broken batten, one shameless theft of a chair leg from the shop's own scrap bin that Master Ravensworth noticed and mourned aloud for a full day while Wren studied the ceiling.

The type was another matter, and here Jude grew quiet in a new way.

"There's a case of type back there," they reported on the fifth night, hunched over the rebuilt press, testing the lever's fall with slow reverence. "Pica. Some brevier. Frozen solid with corrosion, but lead doesn't rust — it's only filth. It will clean." They paused. "And there are boxes. Paper. Sealed in wax-cloth, stacked high and dry on the top shelf of a cupboard my grandfather must have carried down here on his own back. It's yellowed at the edges but the centers—" They stopped, and Wren watched them master their voice. "The centers are still white, Corliss. He bought paper. Good paper. Enough for hundreds of impressions. Twenty years ago he bought paper for a newspaper that didn't exist yet."

"Then he knew it would be needed someday," Wren said.

"Or he hoped so hard it amounted to the same thing." Jude looked up. In the lantern light their eyes had the shine Wren had learned to recognize as the look Jude got when an idea was setting itself, letter by letter, somewhere behind them. "A chronicle. That's what it should be. Not a leaflet, not a rant — a chronicle. Numbered issues. Dates. Names. Facts, set properly, justified clean, with margins a reader can rest in. Something that looks like it deserves to be believed. Because people believe what looks like it cost effort."

"Names," Wren said, and thought of the disappeared from the mill district, of the silence that had come down over the town since the soldiers came, thick as the river fog. "Real names. Of real people."

"Every one we can verify." Jude's jaw set. "The Directorate makes people vanish and then makes the vanishing official. We make them reappear and make *that* official. Ink against ink, Corliss. That's the whole war, fought on our own ground, where we outnumber them."

Wren turned back to the press and laid their palm flat on the cold platen, and for a moment they let themselves imagine it hot — warm from work, ink-smelling, striking sheet after sheet into the lamplit dark while the river rose unknowing overhead. Their hands, which had been apprentice hands for two years, learning to hold a bodkin and a composing stick under a master's eye, suddenly felt like different hands. Hands attached to a person who was becoming someone. It was the strangest sensation of their life, and the best.

"We'll need a name for it," Wren said. "The chronicle. Papers need mastheads. People trust what has a name."

"Then we'll choose it together," said Jude. "Carefully. It may be the most-set line either of us ever composes."

* * *

On the eighth night, the press turned under its own power for the first time in thirty years.

They had agreed on a signal system days before — a specific double scrape of Wren's boot on the landing, answered by Jude's knock on the stair rail — because speaking in the cellar carried strangely, the vaults amplifying voices up through the old flues. But tonight there was no signal needed. Tonight they were both down there, side by side, and the vault smelled differently than it had a week ago: under the eternal river-damp there was now oil, and solvent, and the ghost of ink waking in the screw threads.

"Ready?" Jude asked.

Wren nodded, not trusting their voice, and took hold of the lever with both hands while Jude fed a blank salvage sheet onto the tympan — a test pull, nothing more, ink barely charged, just enough to prove the friction and the register.

The lever came over heavy as a falling tree. The screw sighed. The platen met the paper with a sound like a single slow heartbeat, deep in the stone, and Wren felt it arrive through their arms, their shoulders, their boots, through the flooded floor itself — a pulse in the bones of the earth under Vellmouth.

They pulled the sheet. One grey, imperfect, slightly crooked impression of a trial ornament and the letters **PROOF** — set backwards on the stone, of course, so it read true on the page.

Proof.

They stood looking at it in the lantern light. Neither of them laughed, though Wren felt laughter somewhere in them, mixed up with something closer to fear and something closer still to joy. It was a wretched little print. Crooked, grey, under-inked at one corner. It was also the most honest thing either of them had seen since the bridge closed.

"Tomorrow," Jude said at last, folding the sheet with exaggerated care, as though it were the first copy of something, "we charge the forme properly. And tomorrow night, Corliss, we start setting names."

They doused the lantern and climbed toward the cold ordinary world, and Wren was halfway up the fourteen steps when they heard it — footsteps above, crossing the shop floor with the measured, unhurried weight of someone who owns the building and intends to keep owning it, and then a voice through the boards, casual as weather:

"Jude. Wren. Come up. There's a gentleman here from the Directorate who wants to pay us a friendly visit — Captain Verrek, home from the barracks, and asking after the health of the whole household."

Wren felt Jude go still beside them in the dark. Above, faint and pleasant through the floorboards, came the sound of a second set of boots, and a voice smooth as fresh-set lead:

"No need to hurry on my account. Though I confess I've always wondered what a printer keeps in his cellar."
