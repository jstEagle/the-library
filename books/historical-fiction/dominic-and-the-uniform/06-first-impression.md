## 6. First Impression

The cellar had learned the river's voice.

Jude noticed it first that winter night as they descended the stairs with a candle in one hand and a bundle of type wrapped in flannel against their chest: the way the water beneath the floor spoke in low syllables, patient and endless, seeping up through the old stone as it always did when the frost deepened. In childhood the sound had frightened them. Now it was a kind of company. It covered what needed covering.

Below, by the light of three candles set in saucers of tallow, Wren Corliss was already at the second press, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hair tied back with a length of twine, their young face gilded gold from brow to chin. The press itself rose out of the gloom like something raised rather than found — which, in a sense, it had been. Three weeks of evenings had gone into it: bailing the flooded bed with a lading can, drying the iron with rags and lamp oil, scraping rust in curls from the platen, cutting new tympan paper from the backs of old jobbing work no one would miss. Jude's hands still smelled of the vinegar they'd used on the screws. Their shoulders carried an ache they had stopped remarking upon.

"You're late," Wren said, not looking up. They were setting quoins into the chase, tightening them with the key in short, careful quarter-turns, the way Father had taught them both — though Father did not know this press lived again, and never would if Jude had anything to say about it.

"The soldiers count the candles we burn upstairs now," Jude said. "I had to wait for the patrol to pass the cooper's lane. And I brought this." They set the flannel bundle on the stone beside the forme and unwrapped it with the deliberateness of a surgeon. Inside lay the type they had spent four nights cutting and sorting in stolen minutes: fresh-poured sorts, crisp-edged, still carrying the faint silver sheen of newly faced lead.

Wren leaned close. "The names?"

Jude nodded, and for a moment could not speak.

There were thirty-one of them. Thirty-one names from the mill district, taken between the ninth of Frostmonth and the twentieth, gathered the way all true things are gathered in an occupied town — slowly, sideways, in whispers. Maren Ostler's sister-in-law kept a boarding house near the sluice gates and remembered who had stopped coming down to breakfast. The widow Brack had counted doors. A boy who swept cuttings at the mill had memorized the roll called at shift-change and noticed which names were never answered again. Jude had sat with each account separately, cross-checking spelling against the parish registers they'd once printed for the sexton, correcting a doubled letter here, a transposition there — because a name wrongly spelled was a name half-betrayed, and if this thing they were doing was worth doing at all, it was worth doing with a craftsman's conscience.

*Dominic would have understood,* Jude thought, in the private language they kept for the novel that would never be written. Dominic, who forged documents in another man's war; Dominic, who knew that ink was only ever a promise, and promises were only ever as good as the hand that kept them.

"We print tonight?" Wren asked. There was eagerness in their voice, but under it something else — a tremor Jude recognized because they felt it too, humming in the marrow like the river under the floor.

"We print tonight."

* * *

It took two hours to lock the forme, and they were the purest two hours Jude had spent in their life.

Upstairs the shop slept. Above that, the apartment where Father snored with the window cracked against the stuffiness, his breathing the metronome of Jude's entire childhood. Beyond the shutters, Vellmouth slept too — or pretended to, which amounted to the same thing under the Directorate. Patrol boots rang along the quay at intervals of eleven minutes; Jude had timed them. Eleven minutes was enough. Eleven minutes was a currency now, and they spent it carefully.

They worked without speaking much. There was no need. Wren inked the plate with the hand-brayer while Jude fed each sheet by hand — cheap stock, bought in odd lots from three different stationers so no single purchase would draw notice, grey as porridge and thin enough to read a candle through. The first impression came off the press crooked and over-inked, the letters bleeding into small black stars at every curve, and Jude held it up anyway and felt their throat tighten.

Because there, in the third line, plain as a bell: **ELIAS BRUNT, MILLWRIGHT, TAKEN 11 FROSTMONTH.**

Elias Brunt, whose brother drank with Jude's father at the Anchor and had not drunk there in five weeks. Elias Brunt, whom the official ledger recorded as *departed for work in the eastern provinces, wages settled.* Elias Brunt, dead or worse, and here he was now, in lead, in ink, in duplicate — thirty copies before the night was done — resurrected onto paper that would travel in fish baskets and flour sacks through every lane of a town that had been told to forget him.

"It's real," Wren whispered. They said it the way people say *it's snowing* — as if reporting a fact of weather, something vast and impersonal arriving over the world. "It's actually real."

"Mind your fingers on the platen," Jude said, because terror has to be put somewhere, and craft is as good a place as any.

But it was true. That was the thing that astonished and appalled them in equal measure. For years Jude had believed history happened elsewhere — in capitals, in parliaments, in the mouths of men whose portraits got engraved. Type was set in Vellmouth; history was made elsewhere. They had accepted this the way one accepts weather. And now here they stood in a freezing cellar with wet ink on their thumbs, and the truth of it rearranged itself: history was not made elsewhere. History was made wherever someone decided what would be written down. The Directorate understood this. That was why they'd requisitioned the shop, why Captain Verrek walked the composing room with his gloved hand trailing across the cases like a man stroking a horse he intended to buy. Whoever sets the type sets the truth. Jude had learned that at seven years old, standing on a stool to reach the upper cases. They had simply never understood it until now.

The press clattered. The river muttered underneath. Sheet after sheet came off the stack, and with each pull Jude's fear changed shape. It did not lessen — they were honest enough with themselves to know it wouldn't, that when dawn came and the pages went out into the town, every copy was a rope waiting to be tied — but it *transmuted*. Fear alone is a helpless thing. Fear joined to purpose becomes fuel. By the fortieth copy their hands had stopped shaking, not because the danger had passed but because the work demanded steadiness, and the work had become larger than the fear that served it.

Near the bottom of the stack, Wren paused with the brayer halfway to the plate.

"Jude. If they find us — if Verrek finds this —"

"He won't."

"But if he does." Wren's voice was very quiet. Up close, in candlelight, Jude could see how young they were — seventeen, all sharp angles and chapped hands, an orphan of the mill district themselves, which was why the list mattered to them more than Jude could ever fully reckon. "My mother's name isn't on that sheet. She wasn't taken. She just — went. Before any of this. But if they find me printing it, she might as well be. Everything she was gets rewritten. *Mother of a traitor.*"

Jude set down the sheet they were holding. They thought of Father upstairs, snoring above a treason he knew nothing of, and felt the full weight of what they had done without asking his leave — had turned his shop, his life's work, into the belly of a conspiracy. Guilt rose in them like floodwater. They let it rise, examined it, and set it down again deliberately, the way one sets a heavy form into the press bed: squared, locked, ready to bear pressure.

"Then we don't get found," Jude said. "That's the whole discipline of it, Wren. Not courage. Courage is for the square, for the ones with rifles. Ours is smaller and harder: we are careful, every hour, forever, and we never once believe ourselves safe. The moment you feel safe, you're caught."

Wren looked at them for a long moment. Then they nodded and pulled the next impression, and the press clattered on into the small hours, and the river said nothing it hadn't said for ten thousand years.

By the time the candles guttered, ninety-eight copies lay stacked between two boards, weighted with the press stones, indistinguishable from scrap unless unfolded. Ninety-eight names multiplied by thirty-one. Jude blew out the last candle and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the water, feeling the ache in their arms and the terrible clean lightness in their chest, and understood that whatever happened now — discovery, arrest, the rope itself — the first issue existed. It was loose in the world's future. Ink dries. Lead endures. Some things, once printed, cannot be unset.

* * *

The fishwives' market opened at gray dawn along the seaward quay, under a sky the color of unprinted paper.

Jude went down with a basket on their arm, playing the errand they had played a thousand times: herring for the household, a twist of salt, change counted twice. Maren Ostler had given instructions two nights prior in the back of her sister's smokehouse, her voice pitched under the crackle of the fire. *Don't hand it to anyone. Don't look at anyone. You buy fish, you pay, you receive your wrapping. That's all you've done. A dozen women do the same. None of us knows more than one link of the chain, and none of us will.* She had a way of speaking that made secrecy sound like housework — tedious, necessary, done properly or not at all.

The market smelled of brine and ice and woodsmoke. Fish slid from hand to hand; scales flashed silver in the early light; the fishwives called their prices in the ancient half-singing cadence that had not changed under kingdom, republic, or Directorate, and would not change under whatever came next. It struck Jude, moving through the stalls, that this whole market was already a press of sorts — a machine for distributing things the authorities couldn't control, running for centuries, needing only a different cargo today.

At Maren's own stall, a woman Jude half-recognized from the mill district stood buying cod. Maren wrapped it in brown paper, folded the parcel twice — the signal fold — and passed it across the plank with exact and ordinary courtesy. The woman took it, tucked it in her basket among turnips, and walked away. Nothing happened. The world did not tremble. Somewhere in the mill district, within the hour, that parcel would be unfolded in some kitchen or loft, and thirty-one names would begin their slow circulation through the bloodstream of the town.

Jude bought herring from the stall at the corner. The fishwife — old Sorrel, who had known Jude since they were small enough to ride in a market basket — wrapped them without ceremony, folded twice, and added, in a voice fit for complaining about the price of salt: "Cold morning. Mind how you carry that home, dear. Paper tears so easy when folk handle it rough."

"I'll be gentle with it," Jude said, and heard their own voice come out steady, and marveled at the steadiness.

They were three stalls away, heart hammering a rhythm no louder than footsteps, when they saw the second parcel change hands, and the third. A boy of perhaps twelve took a folded sheet inside a sack of offal and trotted toward the tannery lane. A carter tucked one under the rope-lashing of his load as casually as tucking in a corner of canvas. The chronicle was leaving them, spreading outward through the market's capillaries into every quarter of Vellmouth, and no one — no soldier, no clerk, no informer — could yet see it moving, because it moved disguised as commerce, as gossip, as the thousand harmless transactions of an ordinary market day.

Jude stopped at the quay's edge on the way back and looked out at the frozen river. Ice stretched bank to bank, white and sealed, and beyond it the closed bridge stood in its winter scaffolding like a sentence left unfinished. No one in. No one out. Until the thaw, Vellmouth belonged to whoever controlled its words — and this morning, for the first time since the soldiers came, that was no longer entirely the Directorate.

Terror was still there. Jude could locate it precisely, under the breastbone, cold as river water. But it had changed employment. It no longer said *run.* It said *be exact. Be careful. Print again tomorrow.* Purpose had hired fear as its servant, and fear, it turned out, was excellent at its job.

They were nearly home, basket on arm, when the sound reached them: boots in step, and the creak of a military carriage turning into Cooper's Lane ahead. Jude flattened into a doorway, pulse leaping — but the patrol passed without a glance, escorting a sleek black carriage drawn by two grey horses, and painted on the carriage door, in gilt letters that caught the pale sun, was the crest of the Provisional Directorate.

The carriage slowed. It drew up directly before Ravensworth & Son, Printers, and from within it, unhurried, immaculate even at this hour, stepped Captain Aldous Verrek — who straightened his gloves, looked up at the shop's sign with the expression of a man confirming an appointment, and waited at the door as though he expected to be let in.

Jude stood forty paces away with ninety-eight copies' worth of treason in their basket and watched the Captain raise his hand and knock.
