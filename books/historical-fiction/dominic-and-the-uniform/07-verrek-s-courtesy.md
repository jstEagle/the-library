## 7. Verrek's Courtesy

The knock came at an hour when knocks meant nothing good.

Jude was at the stone sink in the back room, washing the last of the day's ink from their fingers — the black never fully left; it lived in the creases now, in the whorls of the fingerprints, so that every door handle in Vellmouth they touched carried a small confession of trade. They heard Wren go still over the type case in the corner, heard the almost imperceptible click of a hand-lie being set down flat among the sorts so it would not be seen. Then the knock again: three raps, measured, unhurried, the knock of a man who had never once in his life needed to hurry for anyone.

"Ravensworth." The voice through the door was pleasant. Pleasant the way a well-made trap is pleasant — oiled hinges, clean geometry. "Captain Verrek. I hope I find the household awake."

Jude dried their hands on the rag and did not look at Wren. *Do not look at anyone. Looking is answering.* They crossed the shop floor, past the Directorate press that no longer belonged to them, past the shelf of approved jobbing work with its censor's slips fluttering like pale leaves, and opened the door.

Verrek stood on the step with snow gathering on the shoulders of his grey coat, holding a bottle of plum brandy by the neck like a lantern. Behind him, at the mouth of the alley, two soldiers stood with the studied idleness of men pretending to be furniture. He smiled, and the smile reached all the way up to his eyes, which was the unsettling part. Most powerful men forgot to move their eyes when they smiled. Verrek remembered.

"Master Ravensworth. Or is it mistress? Forgive me — your father calls you *my child*, which has always struck me as admirably economical." He lifted the bottle. "I've come without ceremony. May I?"

Every answer available to Jude was wrong. They stepped aside and let him in.

* * *

He moved through the shop the way he had moved through it the first day of the requisition, but slower now, more gently, a man revisiting a house he had decided to buy. He set the bottle on the composing stone — directly on it, on the polished slate where matrices were dressed, and Jude felt the small sacrilege of it in their teeth. He read spines. He tilted his head at the framed specimen sheet above the hearth, the one showing their grandfather's hand-cut italic, each letter balanced like a dancer mid-step.

"This is beautiful work." He said it as though beauty were a category he had personally invented. "You know, before all this" — he gestured with two fingers, taking in the press, the town, presumably the entire reorganization of civilization — "I was a reader. A great reader. My mother wanted me in the church. She said I had the temperament for it." He laughed softly. "She meant the certainty."

"My father isn't home," Jude said. "He's at the guildhall. There's a — consultation. About the commemorative commission."

"I know," Verrek said pleasantly. "That is rather why I came."

Wren, in the corner, had become a piece of the furniture themselves, head down over the case, sorting type with a steadiness Jude doubted entirely.

Verrek wandered. He let his gloved fingers trail along the frisket of the requisitioned press, and Jude watched the glove and thought about everything that had been printed under this roof since the ice closed — none of it here, all of it below, wrapped in oilcloth in the dark beside the drowned second press, breathing river-smell into every page. Names. The disappeared of the mill district, thirty-one names in the first issue, forty-six in the second, each one verified twice by Maren Ostler's fishwives before it was allowed near a stick of type. *The Chronicle of the Sealed Town*, though no issue bore that name; the pages called themselves only *What Is Known*, which Maren had pronounced too modest and Wren had pronounced exactly right.

Three issues out now. Three issues folded into herring barrels and bread loaves and the false bottoms of market baskets. And here stood the man whose business it was to know everything, admiring their grandfather's italics.

"You cut type yourself, I'm told," Verrek said. It was not quite a question.

"Some."

"Then you understand something most people don't. That language is a technology." He turned from the specimen sheet, and his eyes were warm and terrible. "A rifle is a technology for delivering lead. Language is a technology for delivering belief. Whoever controls the foundry—" he touched the composing stone, beside the ring where his bottle sat, "—controls the caliber."

"That's poetry, Captain," Jude said. "My trade is less grand. We print receipts for tallow."

"We have all been reduced to humbler trades," Verrek agreed. "It builds character. That is what the Directorate keeps telling me, in any case." He smiled again, sharing the joke with them, folding them into it, and Jude understood suddenly how it must feel to be recruited by a spider: flattered, even, at the quality of the silk.

He uncorked the brandy himself, found two clay cups on the shelf, poured with the ease of a man in his own kitchen, and handed one to Jude. He did not offer any to Wren. That too was information — who counted, who merely existed.

"To craft," he said, and drank.

Jude drank. The brandy was excellent, which made it worse.

* * *

For a while he talked, and he was genuinely good company, which Jude resented the way one resents a fever that lets you sleep. He told a story about the Director-General's secretary mispronouncing *Vellmouth* at a briefing in the capital — *Vel-mouth*, with a hard emphasis, as though the town were some kind of dental condition — and imitated it so precisely, with such light malice, that Jude nearly laughed and hated themselves for the nearly. He asked after the health of the trade. He observed that the winter seemed long. He remarked that the river sounded louder lately, groaning under its lid of ice, and that in his boyhood in the valley towns the thaw had come early some years and late others, and no one had ever thought to make an omen of it either way.

Then, setting down his cup with a small click against the composing stone:

"I've been reading something lately," he said. "Anonymous. Circulating. You know the sort of thing — seditious drivel, badly argued, but occasionally…" He paused, selecting the word the way Jude selected a sort from the case, testing it in the tweezers before committing. "Occasionally well put."

The room contracted. Jude heard the fire tick in the grate. Heard Wren stop sorting — a silence inside a silence, one sort held forever between finger and tweezers.

"There was a phrase," Verrek went on, gazing mildly at the ceiling beams as though the sentence were written there. "*Ink does not forgive. It waits.*" His eyes came down and settled on Jude with perfect, patient kindness. "Now, that is not a soldier's sentence. That is not even, properly speaking, a politician's sentence. That is a printer's sentence. Someone who knows what ink does — how it sets, how it dries into permanence, how a page cannot take back what it says. Only someone who has handled wet type would think to say that ink *waits*. Waiting implies expectation. Anticipation. A future in which someone will have to answer for what was printed." He picked up the brandy bottle and turned it slowly in his hands, reading the label that wasn't there. "It struck me as very fine writing, Master Ravensworth. Too fine for the mill district. The mill district writes with bricks."

"The phrase means nothing to me," Jude said. Their voice came out level, and they were grateful to it beyond measure.

"No?" Verrek sounded honestly curious. "It's just that I've read perhaps four hundred pages of this town's output since the bridge closed — proclamations, price schedules, my own correspondence — and yours is the only house where the prose has *bones*. Whoever writes the official notices we run through your press writes like a clerk, God rest him. But the anonymous material — the chronicle, if you like —" he used the word delicately, laying it on the table between them like a card played face up, "— whoever writes that understands composition. Not merely sentences. *Composition.* White space. Weight. The eye's journey down the page. That is a craft education, Master Ravensworth. That is someone who grew up with a case of type under their hands the way farmers' children grow up with soil under their nails."

"Half the town learned letters off printers' sheets," Jude said. "Your own requisition orders are posted on every corner. People read."

"People read," Verrek agreed. "Few people *compose*."

He let it sit there. Outside, the soldiers shifted; snow squeaked under a boot. In the grate, a knot in the wood popped and collapsed into orange. Jude became aware of their own heartbeat with an anatomist's detachment — fast, regular, betraying nothing to the eye — and thought, with strange calm: *this is the moment. If he knows, this is the moment he stops being courteous.*

And then Verrek laughed — warmly, shaking his head, refilling both cups — and the moment passed through the room like a cold draft under a door, rattling everything on its way, settling nothing.

"Forgive me," he said. "Occupational disease. I see conspiracies the way you see kerning errors — everywhere, mostly imagined, professionally maddening. No, I came for two simple things. First, the brandy, which my quartermaster distills from confiscated orchards and which I consider my duty to share with the town's artisans." He pushed the cup back toward Jude. "And second — news. Good news, or at least the Directorate's version of good. The ice is rotten above the ferry landing. The engineers say the break will come early this year. Perhaps three weeks. Perhaps less."

Something turned over in Jude's chest — hope and horror wearing the same coat.

"When the bridge opens," Verrek continued, conversationally, examining his own gloved hand as though checking it for ink, "the historians arrive. Actual historians, from the capital, with warrants and archives and very tidy minds. Their task will be to compile the official record of the Vellmouth disturbances — what caused them, who fed them, what was done to restore order, and what regrettable excesses accompanied that restoration. Every loose page in this town will become evidence, Master Ravensworth. Every page. Evidence for, or evidence to be burned — and I confess the distinction has historically depended less on content than on custody." He drained his cup and stood, buttoning his coat with brisk good humor. "So. Print nothing unapproved. Report anything unusual. And if that anonymous chronicler should ever wish to negotiate — amnesty is a word the Directorate uses more often than people believe — well. Doors open both ways. Even this one."

At the threshold he paused, snowflakes settling again onto his shoulders, and looked back with that smile that troubled Jude precisely because it was genuine.

"*Ink does not forgive,*" he repeated softly, tasting it. "Whoever wrote that should be careful. Ink is patient, yes. But paper burns, and rivers give up their dead when the ice goes out. Everything surfaces in the thaw, Ravensworth. Everything."

The door closed behind him. Through the window Jude watched him cross the alley, exchange a word with the soldiers, walk away unhurried into the falling snow, a grey figure diminishing into a grey evening, entirely at his ease.

* * *

For a long time neither of them moved. Then Wren set down the sort they'd been holding — Jude heard the small click of it returning to its compartment, a sound like a period at the end of a sentence — and said, barely above breath: "Does he know?"

Jude went to the window. Down the lane, the soldiers were following their captain at a distance, and the snow was already filling their footprints, which struck Jude as the single most hopeful fact of the evening: even boots could be unsaid, given weather enough.

"He doesn't know," they said slowly. "Or he suspects and wants us to think he only suspects, or he knows and wants us to think he only suspects, or—" They stopped. Their hands had begun to shake now that the shaking could do no harm, and they pressed them flat against the cold glass. "Wren, I wrote that line. *Ink does not forgive. It waits.* I set it myself, in the cellar, by candlelight, on a press that's supposed to be dead. Nobody has seen those pages but us, the fishwives, and the people who pass them hand to hand in the dark."

"Then he got it from a page."

"Or he's fishing. Or someone talked. Or—" Jude turned from the window. The fear was still there, huge and quiet in the room like the river under the ice, but beneath it, unwelcome as a splinter, something else had lodged: the memory of Verrek's face when he'd recited the line. The pleasure in it. Real pleasure. A reader's pleasure. He had liked the sentence. He had admired it. And somehow that was worse than hatred; hatred you could aim at, admiration circled you like a wolf learning your gait.

"Three weeks," Wren said. "Maybe less. He told us the thaw is early because he wanted to watch our faces."

"I know."

"He wants us to run. Rush. Get careless. That's why the courtesy — it's bait on the same hook."

"I know," Jude said again, and heard how thin the words sounded, how much they failed to be a plan.

Below them, faint through the floorboards, the cellar breathed its cold river smell up through the seams, where the old press waited in the dark with the plates of three issues wrapped in oilcloth — three weeks' work, forty-six names, one unforgivable phrase — and above the town the ice groaned along its whole length, a long shifting sigh, like a vast ledger being opened.

Jude blew out the candles and stood in the dark listening to it.

Whatever Verrek knew, they had until the ice broke to finish the fourth issue — and someone, somewhere in Vellmouth, might already have sold them.
