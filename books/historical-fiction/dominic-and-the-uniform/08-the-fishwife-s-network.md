## 8. The Fishwife's Network

Maren Ostler had carried herring guts under her fingernails for thirty years, and she knew the weight of things the way a mason knows stone. A basket heavy with fish sat differently on the hip than one heavy with paper. Paper was lighter, but it burned hotter — that was the truth of it. You could drop a basket of brisling down the steps of the market and lose your morning's profit. You dropped a bundle of the *Vellmouth Chronicle* and you lost your hands, or your neck, or worse — and worse, in Vellmouth that winter, had a great many varieties.

She rose before the lamps went out, as she always did, in the low room behind the smokehouse where the ceiling was close enough to touch and the smell of brine had worked itself into the very grain of the boards. Her daughter Katrine slept in the box bed against the wall, breath whistling faintly through a chapped nose, and Maren dressed in the dark with the particular silence of women who have spent their lives not waking anyone.

The pages came to her folded inside salted fish. This had been her own suggestion, offered at the second meeting in the back of the cooperage, when the printer's apprentice — Wren, a sharp-boned girl with ink permanently shadowing the creases of her knuckles — had asked, in a voice that tried very hard not to shake, how they could possibly move paper through a town where every soldier watched every doorway.

"Nobody searches a fishwife," Maren had said. "They wrinkle their noses and let us pass. The Directorate thinks our trade is beneath notice." She had let that sit a moment. "Let them go on thinking it."

So now, in the grey before dawn, she sat at the table with a boning knife and forty brisling, and she slit each one along the belly and laid two folds of paper flat against the cold flesh and closed the fish over it, and packed them in the straw, and salted the top layer generously so that the whole business stank convincingly of honest commerce. The first copies went to Old Sef at the chapel steps, who read aloud to the widows while they netted. The second batch went up the hill in Katrine's school satchel, tucked beneath slate and spellbook, to be passed at midday to the baker's boy, who took them onward to the tannery. By evening, a single sheet printed in the cellar of Ravensworth's shop would have been read by two hundred pairs of eyes, and read aloud to perhaps a hundred more, and by the next night it would be recited from memory in taverns where no copy dared appear.

The names were what mattered. That was what Maren understood, and what she had told them at the cooperage when the men argued about strategy and the girl Wren argued about type sizes. The Directorate could burn a press, could close a bridge, could put a bullet in a mill striker's back and call it *the restoration of order* in their official broadsheet. But they could not un-name the dead. Once the town knew that Anders Vik, sixteen years old, who limped from a childhood fall, had been shot standing in the snow with his hands already raised — once that was known, it could not be unknown. It lived in people now, like a hook set deep in the flesh. The official broadsheet said *three agitators killed in an exchange*. The Chronicle said their names, their ages, their mothers' names, and where exactly the bullets had gone in. And every fishwife in Vellmouth had looked at those names and found someone she'd sold herring to, or someone whose mother she'd known since they were girls gutting together on the same quay.

That was the arithmetic the Captain had never learned. Aldous Verrek counted papers seized and presses controlled. He did not count the space between a woman reading a name and deciding, quietly, without telling anyone, that she would remember it forever.

Maren wrapped the last bundle, tied it with tarred twine, and touched Katrine's hair once — lightly, so as not to wake her — before going out into the dark.

* * *

The market opened under a sky the color of unwashed wool. Snow lay grey and trodden between the stalls, and the wind came off the river carrying that sound that had become part of the town's breathing now: the ice groaning, a long animal complaint from the middle of the water, as if something enormous were turning over in its sleep and could not settle.

The bridge had been shut for seven weeks. Seven weeks of no post, no barges, no letters out, no letters in. Seven weeks of the town sealed like a jar, and everyone pretending this was ordinary, this happened every winter, though every soul old enough to remember knew the closure had never come this early nor bitten this hard. Soldiers stood at both ends of the frozen crossing with muskets and braziers, and beyond them the river ran black in its channel, fringed with ice plates that ground together like teeth.

Maren set out her stall: silver-bellied herring, dark strips of dried cod, jars of pickled sprats. Around her the market assembled itself in the usual order — Griet with her root vegetables, the Halvorsen brothers with their rope and pitch, old Tante Rusk selling candles made of whatever fat she could beg. And woven through the ordinary commerce, invisible as thread through cloth, the other market moved.

A nod from Griet meant the vegetable crates had papers under the carrots. A certain way of stacking rope meant the Halvorsens had received and passed on. Nothing was written down; nothing was ever said above a murmur. They had learned — quickly, brutally — the discipline of silence.

Because four days ago, they had learned otherwise. Four days ago, Lise Brant had been taken.

Lise. Twenty-two, quick-eyed, a shellfish girl who worked the mussel beds below the tide mills and carried copies in her basket among the oysters, wrapped in damp sacking. She'd been careless once — just once, just a moment at the quay when she'd pressed a folded page into a deckhand's hand in plain sight of a patrolling corporal — and the corporal had been young and bored and looking for something to justify his morning, and he had taken her by the wrist and said, "What's that, then?"

By evening she was in the guardhouse by the customs stairs. By the next noon, the whole network knew, because the deckhand had come shaking to Old Sef, and Old Sef had sent word down the chain faster than any soldier could follow: *Lise taken. Papers on her. One copy only. Say nothing. Burn everything.*

Maren had spent four days burning everything. Four days of carrying her own fear like a stone in her belly alongside the ordinary stones of herring bones and salt. And today, the whole market moved in the shadow of what might be happening in the guardhouse at that very moment.

"She'll not talk," Griet said, low, arranging turnips that needed no arranging. "She's not the sort."

"Everyone's the sort," Maren said. "Everyone's the sort, given time and the right hands."

Griet's jaw tightened. She had a son at the mill, Maren remembered. Everyone carried their own reasons now, their own private arithmetic of risk. "The Ostler girl carries for you."

"And I'd carry for her," Maren said. "Which is why we do it properly or not at all." She looked up, scanning the market edge where the patrol crossed — four soldiers, red-and-grey uniforms stiff with frost, breath steaming, boots leaving dark prints in the trampled snow. "No more quayside handoffs. No more baskets left on steps. From today it goes mouth to ear and hand to hand only, and if anyone is ever unsure of a face, they walk away. Walk away, Griet. A lost copy is a lost copy. A caught courier is a caught town."

Griet nodded slowly, and something passed across her face that was not quite agreement but was older than agreement — the look of women who had survived other winters by closing ranks, by tightening the weave, by trusting less and holding more.

* * *

The soldiers came to the market at midmorning, which was unusual. Patrols passed through, but patrols passed through everywhere; they had the gait of men doing a duty they resented, eyes on the middle distance. These came differently. These came with purpose, and in front of them, held by the arm, walked Lise Brant.

The market went quiet in the way markets do — not silent, but hushed, the haggard haggling dropping to murmurs, every pair of hands finding something to do that wasn't looking. Maren kept her eyes on her herring and her heart in her throat and made herself watch anyway, because someone had to watch, because watching was a kind of witness, and witness was all they had.

Lise's face was wrong. Not bruised — not visibly — but emptied, scoured, the way a face looks after long weeping or long waking. Her hands were bare and blue with cold; they had taken her gloves, or she had lost them, or worse things had been done to her fingers than cold could account for. The corporal beside her — the young one, the bored one, Maren noted his face deliberately, filing it away the way she filed faces of men who bought stolen fish, a professional habit repurposed — the corporal was saying something to her in a low voice, almost gentle, and Lise was nodding at the stalls.

They were walking the market. Showing her the stalls. Making her point, or not point, or look.

Maren understood it with a clarity that felt like ice water: they didn't have enough to hold her, or they wanted more. They were parading her through her own world to see what flinched. Every stall she paused at would be noted. Every face she lingered on. It was crude, and it was clever, and it was working, because Maren could feel the whole market wanting to look at Lise, wanting to ask after her, wanting — God help them — to comfort her, because that is what communities do with their wounded, and comfort was exactly what the Directorate wanted them to offer, so they could follow it home.

"Move along there," Maren called out suddenly, loud and coarse, to no soldier at all — to a boy dawdling by her stall. "You've no coin, no herring for you. Out of it." She flapped her apron at him, shrill, common, drawing the eye of the patrol toward nothing but ordinary market ill-temper. Around her, taking the cue or acting on the same instinct, the market hardened its voice: Griet snapped at a customer over the price of onions; Tante Rusk declared loudly that candle prices were candle prices and sentiment wouldn't melt wax. The whole square turned itself into a wall of petty commerce, impenetrable, boring, offering the soldiers nothing human to hold on to.

Lise's eyes found Maren's for half a second. In that half-second Maren saw everything in them — the terror, the shame, the desperate plea not for rescue but for *recognition*, for someone to know what she was enduring and not despise her for whatever she might be forced to say. And Maren gave her the only thing she safely could: the flat, indifferent stare of a fishwife looking at a stranger. Nothing to see here. Nobody home. Then she bent to her fish, and her hands shook among the silver bodies, and she gutted and gutted and did not stop.

The patrol moved on. Lise walked between the soldiers past the end of the row, small and straight-backed, and disappeared toward the customs stairs, and the market exhaled all at once, a long communal breath that fogged the freezing air.

Old Sef appeared at her stall within the hour, ostensibly to complain about the price of sprats.

"They kept her all night standing," he murmured, head bent over the barrel as if inspecting the catch. "My cousin's wife's brother is a turnkey there. Sleep deprivation, cold, the questions asked round and round in circles till a body loses the shape of what she said yesterday. They're not beating her yet. Yet." He straightened. "And here's the poison of it, Maren. They're asking her not who printed it. They know the printing is beyond her. They're asking who gave it to her. Names, Maren. They want the web, not the spider."

"The web doesn't exist," Maren said. "That's what she has to make them believe. There's no web. There's only fish and luck."

"That's what she must believe under the third sleepless night," Sef said grimly, "with a man being kind to her, being *so* kind, promising her mother won't be turned out of her rooms, promising her it will all stop tomorrow if she'll only say one name. One name, he says. Just one."

Maren looked down the row of stalls, at these people she had known her whole life — Griet, whose husband had drowned off the point; the Halvorsen boys, who'd fought each other bloody at twelve and defended each other to the death at twenty; Tante Rusk, who had buried three children and still laughed at bad jokes. One name. Any one of them would buy the rest.

"We tighten further," she said. "No one knows more than two links of the chain. I don't need to know where the pages are printed, and by God I don't want to. Sef, you don't tell me who brings yours and I don't tell you who takes mine. If any of us is taken, the answer is true: *I bought my fish, I sold my fish, I don't know anything about papers.* And the beautiful thing—" she heard her own voice harden, "—is that it will be true. That's the discipline. Make the lie unnecessary by making ignorance real."

Sef studied her a moment, this woman he'd known since she was a girl hauling baskets bigger than herself, and something like sorrow moved through the weather of his face. "You've a head for war, Maren Ostler."

"I've a head for fish," she said. "It's the same arithmetic. Protect the school, sacrifice nothing you can't afford, and never let them see which fish is turning." She paused. "But hear me, Sef. If Lise breaks — and she may, she's twenty-two and they are patient — we do not curse her name. Whatever she gives them, she gives them broken. The ones who break under the knife are not traitors. The traitor is the knife."

* * *

Word came at dusk, through the deckhand who had started it all, white-faced and whispering: Lise had been released.

Released at sundown, walked to her mother's door, left there. No charges. The corporal had even returned her gloves.

The network received the news the way a body receives news of a wound that may or may not fester: with a dread that outlasted relief. Released was good. Released could also mean turned. Released could mean bait, followed from door to door until every contact was mapped. Old Sef sent word within the hour: *no one approaches Lise. No one speaks to her. If she comes to you, listen, touch nothing, agree to nothing.* Cruel orders, and necessary, and Maren hated every word of them even as she repeated them down the chain in her flat fish-market voice.

But late that night, when the smokehouse fires were banked and Katrine slept, there came a knock at Maren's door — three soft raps, then one, the new signal — and outside in the freezing dark stood the girl Wren Corliss, ink-stained, hood up, snow in her lashes, with a bundle under her arm wrapped in oilcloth.

"You shouldn't be here," Maren said, pulling her in out of the cold. "This street is watched since the Brant girl—"

"I know." The girl's voice was steady, but her hands, when she unwound her scarf, were not. "That's why I came myself instead of sending it down the chain. It's the next issue. And there's a change." She drew a breath. "There's a piece in it about couriers. About what the risk really is, and who bears it. Ravensworth wrote it — the younger one. It says the chronicle isn't made in the cellar. It says it's made in the market, on the quays, in the baskets. It names no one." Wren's eyes came up. "But it says the town owes a debt it doesn't know how to count, to the people who carry the words. Maren — it says the paper is the least dangerous thing in Vellmouth, because the paper is only lead and rag. The dangerous thing is the person who decides it's worth carrying."

Maren stood a long moment with the oilcloth bundle in her hands, heavier than any basket of herring she'd ever shouldered, and lighter than air.

"Tell them at the shop," she said finally, "that the debt runs both ways. Tell them a fishwife said so." Then, because the girl was young and the night was cold and the fear in the town had grown teeth, Maren fed her hot barley broth and would not let her leave until she'd finished it — and while Wren ate, Maren told her about Lise, all of it, the parade through the market and the empty eyes and the returned gloves, because the people who made the paper needed to know what the paper cost when it walked.

Wren listened with her spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. When Maren finished, the girl set the spoon down and said, quietly, "Then the next issue has to be worth it."

"It always has to be worth it," Maren said. "That's the whole discipline. Every page you hand me, you'd better be able to say: this one was worth Lise Brant's sleepless night. Worth the deckhand's terror. Worth whatever's coming." She took the oilcloth bundle and slid it into the bottom of tomorrow's fish pannier, among the straw, under the salt. "Now go home the long way, child. And mind the corner by the customs house — there's a young corporal stands there evenings, and he's learning to look at baskets."

After Wren had gone, Maren sat alone by the banked fire a long while, listening to the river ice groan in the dark — closer now, more restless, the thaw muttering somewhere far upstream ahead of schedule. She thought of Lise asleep or not sleeping in her mother's house. She thought of the young corporal's patient, bored eyes. And she thought, with a cold certainty that settled in her like a swallowed stone: whatever they had done to Lise, whatever game they were playing with released prisoners and returned gloves — the Directorate was not finished with the market. It had only just learned where to look.
