## 9. Father's Hands

Ambrose Ravensworth had been cutting letters for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had never once thought about his hands as separate from himself. They were simply the far end of his attention, the place where judgment became steel. His father had taught him at this same bench, in this same corner of the shop where the morning light came slantwise through the north window and fell across the graver's path without shadow. *A letter is a promise*, old Josiah had said, guiding the boy's grip with his own cracked fingers. *You cut it true, and it will tell the truth long after you're gone.* Ambrose had been eleven years old and had not understood, and had nodded anyway, and had spent the rest of his life learning what the old man meant.

He understood it now. He understood it in the worst possible way, sitting at his bench before dawn with a Directorate clerk's order weighted down at his elbow by a brass inkstand that was not heavy enough for the paper beneath it, though no amount of brass could have made that paper weightless.

*By requisition of the Provisional Directorate of Public Information: one commemorative heading, to be cut in display roman, for deployment on official broadsheets celebrating the restoration of order in Vellmouth and district.*

Restoration of order. He read the phrase the way he read all type now, which was differently than he had read anything before the soldiers came. Before, a phrase had been a phrase — sound shaped into sense, sense shaped into form. Now every phrase arrived already bleeding.

The order specified the wording. That was the thing about the Directorate; they were not content to command the press, they wanted to command the very alphabet, and so somewhere in the customs house a man who had never held a graver had written out, in a fair clerk's hand, exactly what the town would be made to see:

*GLORIOUS RESTORATION OF ORDER UNDER THE PROVISIONAL DIRECTORATE — VELLMOUTH GRATEFUL — PEACE RETURNS TO THE MILL DISTRICT.*

Peace returns to the mill district. Ambrose set the order down and looked at his hands.

They were good hands. He did not think this out of vanity, or not mostly. A man who worked steel into letters forty hours a week came to know his own hands the way a sailor knows weather, intimately and without flattery. The right hand still carried a white scar across the knuckle from a slip in his twentieth year; the index finger had a permanent flattening at the pad where the graver's handle rested; the thumbnail was thickened and yellowed from decades of tapping type into composing sticks, testing the fit by feel, because a compositor who could not feel a bad fit by touch was a compositor who shipped bad work. Jude had those same calluses coming in now. He had watched them form over the years with more pride than he had ever allowed himself to speak aloud.

And these hands, these honest hands, were going to cut letters that said Vellmouth was grateful.

He rose and went to the window. Below, the street lay under its usual winter hush, but the hush had changed quality since the bridge closed, since the soldiers came. It was no longer the hush of snow settling on a sleeping town. It was the hush of held breath. Across the way, Ostler's fish stall stood shuttered, and he thought of Maren Ostler's sharp eyes and wondered — not for the first time, and with a fear he kept folded small and hidden even from himself — how much his own children were doing that he did not know about. There were nights when Jude came up from the shop smelling of candle smoke at hours no lawful work required, and there was Wren Corliss with his scraped knuckles and his too-innocent face, and there were the wet footprints, once, on the cellar stairs that no one had explained.

Ambrose had decided not to ask. This decision had felt, at the time, like cowardice. Lately he had begun to suspect it might be the only wise thing he had done since autumn.

But the order on his bench was not a question about his children. It was a question about him, and it would be answered either way.

* * *

Verrek came at mid-morning, because Verrek liked to come at hours that demonstrated he could.

Captain Aldous Verrek did not enter a room the way soldiers entered rooms, with boots and noise. He entered the way an editor entered a manuscript: reading as he came, taking in the state of the floor, the angle of the chairs, whose eyes went where. Today he wore his grey coat brushed to a soft shine and carried his gloves rather than wearing them, which Ambrose had learned meant good humor, or something dressed as good humor.

"Master Ravensworth." Verrek inclined his head toward the bench. "You received our request."

"Request" — the word chosen, Ambrose noted, over *order*, the way a man might say *may I trouble you* while standing on your throat. "I received it."

"And you've begun?"

"I've begun." Ambrose stepped aside so the captain could see the punch block clamped in the vise, the first letter roughed out. He had chosen to begin with the R of *RESTORATION*. It seemed only fitting that the word should be born before the others.

Verrek bent over the block with what appeared to be genuine interest. This was the most dangerous thing about him, Ambrose had long ago concluded — the genuine interest. A man who merely performed curiosity was tiresome. Verrek actually looked. He looked at the steel the way Ambrose looked at a page of someone else's setting, hunting the flaw.

"Beautiful work," Verrek said. "Truly. You know, when we requisitioned this shop, some in the Directorate argued we should consolidate all printing in the customs house under military compositors. I argued against it." He straightened, smiling faintly. "I said: the town trusts its own craftsmen. Let them see familiar work on official pages, and they will trust what the work says. Type has authority, Master Ravensworth. Your family built that authority over three generations. We are merely borrowing it."

*Borrowing.* Ambrose held his face still. Thirty-one years at a bench teaches a man stillness.

"You'll want my best display roman," he said. "For a commemorative heading, nothing less will do."

"Nothing less," Verrek agreed warmly. He moved along the wall, trailing one gloved finger above the type cases without touching them — he was careful never to touch, Ambrose had noticed, as though the lead might accuse him. At the case marked with the worn brass plate his finger paused, hovering. "How long?"

"Two days for the full heading. Three to be proud of it."

"We are at war with confusion, Captain Ravensworth—" Verrek caught himself using the rank, smiled at his own joke, and did not correct it. "We are at war with confusion. Confusion does not wait three days."

"Two days, then."

"Two days." Verrek put his gloves on, working each finger home with small precise tugs, watching Ambrose do it. At the door he paused. "My clerks will inspect the finished matrices. They are thorough men. Not craftsmen — they wouldn't know a serif from a spur — but thorough." A beat, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Every letter must say exactly what the order specifies. Exactly. You understand me, Master Ravensworth. I believe you do."

The door closed behind him with the softness of money changing hands.

Ambrose stood a long moment in the empty shop. Then he went back to the bench, took up the graver, and looked at the half-born R until his eyes stung.

* * *

His father's voice came back to him that afternoon, unbidden, in the old workshop cadence. *Watch the counter, boy. The counter is the soul of the letter. The strokes are just the body.* Ambrose had cut tens of thousands of counters in his life — the enclosed spaces inside O and R and P and B, the negative shapes that gave letters their breath. And he knew, the way only a cutter knows, that a counter could lie.

That was the heresy that came to him slowly through the afternoon, while the light moved across the bench and his tea went cold at his elbow, while downstairs — or was it below, below the shop, in the dark? — he imagined he heard the faint sounds of his children doing whatever it was they did. A counter could lie. Not grossly; a clerk's inspection would catch a gross lie. But there were tolerances in every design, and within tolerances lived infinite small treacheries.

An R whose leg was cut a hair too fine at the join would print clean on the first hundred pulls and begin to fail on the thousandth, the thin metal wearing, the stroke opening into a hairline crack that no one would notice and everyone would feel — a letter that looked wrong without being legibly wrong, that unsettled the eye the way a portrait unsettles when the eyes don't quite agree. An O minutely eccentric, its axis tilted two degrees off true, would make an entire line of text lean like grass in wind. A serif clipped short by less than the width of a human hair would leave words that read as slightly wounded, slightly maimed, and readers would blame their own tiredness, their own eyes, and would never know why the glorious heading on the official broadsheet made their stomach turn a little, why they found themselves doubting the sentence without being able to say which word betrayed them.

Type spoke to people below the level of reading. That was the whole secret of the craft, the thing Josiah had tried to give him at eleven years old. And if type could carry truth below the level of reading, then type could carry accusation there too.

Ambrose sat with the idea the way a man sits with a live coal in his cupped hands, feeling first the heat and then the burn and refusing, stubbornly, to drop it.

Because here was the other side of the coal, the side that seared: it was still complicity. He knew that. Cut the letters crooked or cut them true, the broadsheet would print, the broadsheet would lie, the name of Ravensworth would sit in the imprint of the Directorate's triumph for as long as anyone preserved imprints — and someone always preserved imprints; that was the whole faith of his life, that type outlived its masters. He could sabotage the beauty of the lie, but he could not stop the lie, not from this bench, not with Verrek's clerks coming to inspect, not with the shop watched and Jude and Wren — whatever they were doing, God keep them — depending on the shop remaining quiet and obedient and boring.

Unless. Unless the crooked letters were not merely ugliness. Unless they were signature.

He thought of the suppressed broadsheet he had burned — or nearly burned; his hand had stopped over the stove and he had fed the fire other papers instead and told himself the old sheet was ash — the jobbing work from the purge of '19, when his own father had printed for the men who then ruled and fell. Had Josiah known? Had his father's hands, too, been conscripted? Ambrose remembered suddenly, with the peculiar vividness of things remembered once and never understood until now, that the commemorative headings of '19 — *ORDER RESTORED IN THE WEAVING QUARTER, ALL QUIET* — had always looked slightly wrong to him. Even as a boy he had felt it, a wrongness in the counters, in the lean of the lines, and had assumed his father had grown careless near the end.

Careless. Josiah Ravensworth, careless. A man who had once rejected an entire casting of type because a single e in four hundred was a quarter-point shallow.

His father had signed the lie with a flaw. And the flaw had waited thirty years in Ambrose's memory to be read as what it was: a confession, filed in the only court that never closes, the eye of every reader who ever felt a sentence go sour on the page.

Ambrose picked up the graver. His hands, the good hands, the honest hands, had stopped shaking sometime in the last hour without his noticing. He clamped the punch block back in the vise and turned the half-cut R to the light.

* * *

He worked through the evening, and Jude worked at the far bench, and the shop filled with the small music of their trade — the tap of the planer, the whisper of steel, the sigh of the stove — and neither of them spoke much, and the silence between them had the quality of two people keeping the same secret without agreeing to keep it.

Twice Jude glanced over. Once their eyes met, and Ambrose saw in his child's face a question forming and then deliberately, gently set aside, and he understood that Jude was extending to him the same mercy he had extended to Jude: not asking. Trusting the other to be about the right work in the dark.

At the second hour past midnight, alone now, stove banked low, Ambrose finished the R.

He held it to the lamp and examined it with the cold, total honesty he brought to every finished piece, and it was beautiful — it was, he could admit it, some of the finest cutting of his life, the bowl deep and round as a bell, the leg falling with the natural grace of a running man, the serifs crisp as frost on a rail. Any clerk would pass it. Any printer in three provinces would envy it.

And the counter — the soul of the letter — leaned. Two degrees off true, tilted against the direction of reading, so that the R stood like a figure walking into wind, resisting, refusing to arrive. Invisible to analysis, unmistakable to instinct. Every reader in Vellmouth who looked at *RESTORATION* would feel, without knowing why, that the word itself was leaning away from them, denying its own statement, trying to walk backward off the page.

Ambrose set the punch down among his tools and pressed both palms flat against the cold bench and let out a breath he felt he had been holding since autumn.

It was not enough. It was a hair's breadth against a mountain. But his father had left him a hair's breadth too, and hair's breadths accumulated, in type as in rivers, until the whole face of the stone changed.

He began the E of RESTORATION, thinking, with something that was almost peace and almost grief: *Forgive me, Josiah. I finally understand.*

From below the floor — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable to a man who had listened to presses his whole life — came the soft, steady breathing of a press running in the cellars, and Ambrose froze with the graver in the cut, listening, and understood at last what his children were doing in the dark, and smiled for the first time in weeks, and began, very carefully and very crookedly, to cut the next letter.
