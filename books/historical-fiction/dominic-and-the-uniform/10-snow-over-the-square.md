## 10. Snow Over the Square

Wren Corliss had never seen a hanging before, and would spend the rest of her life unable to say which detail she had expected and which she had actually witnessed. In the stories apprentices told in the type room, executions were distant things — a drumroll, a hood, a crowd holding its breath. What she saw instead was snow falling into the open mouths of six hundred people who had come to the square because they could not bear not to.

The Provisional Directorate had announced it by crier on Tuesday: *seven persons, condemned under emergency statute for sedition and destruction of property, to be dealt with publicly on Friday morning.* Dealt with. As though they were parcels. The word had gone through Vellmouth like a splinter working toward the skin.

By dawn on Friday the snow was coming down in fat, unhurried flakes, the kind that meant the cold had settled in for days. Wren stood at the edge of the square with Maren Ostler's shawl pulled over her head, close enough to the fishwives' stalls that she might be mistaken for one of them, far enough from the scaffold that no soldier would mark her face. She had argued with herself all night about coming. Jude had argued against it harder. *If they take you,* he'd said, *they take the press.* But someone had to see it truly. Someone had to stand there and not look away, because the chronicle could not print what no one had the courage to witness. That was the whole point of what they did below the flood line: the Directorate would tell its story whether or not anyone watched. The truth needed eyes.

She counted seven posts on the scaffold. Seven ropes. She made herself count them twice.

The mill strikers were brought out on foot from the old customs lockup, no wagon, made to walk the last three streets so the town could see them diminished. Wren knew two of their names already — Halven Brice, the loom-fixers' delegate, and a boy called Tam Ostler, no relation to Maren that anyone had ever proven but everyone had long since stopped questioning, because Maren fed him at midwinter and shouted at him like a nephew and that was as good as blood in Vellmouth. Tam was nineteen. He had gone on strike in the first week of November for a wage the mill owners had promised and withdrawn, and now here he came through the falling snow in his shirt sleeves, hands bound, and he was — this was the part that would not leave her — he was looking up. At the windows. At the rooftops. Turning his head slowly, side to side, reading the town the way you'd read a page, as if somewhere in all those shuttered faces was the word that would save him.

Nobody said anything. Six hundred people and nobody said anything, and the snow kept falling into their open mouths.

An officer read the sentence aloud. His voice carried badly; the words arrived in pieces. *Condemned... agents of foreign agitation... enemies of the restoration of order...* Wren wrote the phrases in her head the way she'd learned to set type, letter by letter, each one slotted into its place, because writing them down later meant remembering them now with precision. *Agents of foreign agitation.* Tam Ostler, who could not have found a foreign country on a map if you'd paid him.

Halven Brice was given the chance to speak a final statement. He looked at the officer for a long moment, and then past him, at the crowd, and he said only: "We asked for the wage we were promised."

That was all. It took four seconds. The officer gestured, impatiently, the way a man gestures at a door that won't shut, and the guards moved in, and Wren discovered that she had closed her eyes without deciding to, and opened them again just as quickly, because closing them was the same as lying, and she had come here to be the opposite of a lie.

Afterward — after, when the sound was over and the square was full of that particular silence that follows a held breath released all at once — the Directorate's men went along the front rank of the crowd taking names. Not arresting, just taking names. Writing them in a ledger. So the lesson was not only the hanging. The lesson was also: *we know who watched.*

Wren slipped away among the fishwives, small and gray-shawled and invisible, and did not stop walking until she reached the alley door of Ravensworth & Son, and then she stood in the dark of the stairwell with her hand on the wet stone and shook until her teeth chattered, and made herself stop, and went down to the cellars to set type.

* * *

Jude was already below, trimming the second press's new platen screw with a file, the candlelight jumping off the brass. He looked up when her boots hit the last stair, and whatever was in her face told him everything before her mouth could.

"How many?" he said quietly.

"Seven. All seven." She peeled off the shawl. Her fingers wouldn't quite work; she flexed them over the candle flame. "Tam Ostler was one."

Jude set down the file. For a moment neither of them said anything, and the cellar spoke instead — the drip of the river through the old stonework, the groan of water under the floor, the small ticking sounds of lead cooling in the melting pot.

"Names," Jude said finally. "All seven names if we can get them. Ages. Trades. What they were struck for. What the sentence said, word for word."

"Word for word," Wren repeated, and began pulling sorts.

They worked through the afternoon while the shop above clattered with requisitioned labor — Directorate clerks setting Verrek's official broadsheet two floors up, and every thump of their press coming down through the joists like a heartbeat overhead. That was the cruelty of it, Wren thought as she composed stick by stick: the same building, the same trade, two versions of the morning assembling themselves at once. Upstairs, the truth was being manufactured. Downstairs, it was being remembered.

Maren came at dusk, bringing the list. She had it from the lockup turnkey's wife, who had copied the condemnation roll before it was sealed. Seven names, seven trades, seven ages. Tam Ostler, loom-boy, nineteen. Wren set his name first and her hands were steady, and that steadiness frightened her more than shaking would have, because it meant some part of her had already turned grief into material, had already decided that the correct answer to a rope was a column of justified text.

"What did Brice say?" Jude asked. "At the end."

"We asked for the wage we were promised." She gave it exactly, and watched him compose it, letter by letter, into the widest measure of the issue, alone, set large, nothing else beneath it.

* * *

The official broadsheet hit the streets the next morning, and Wren bought one at the corner of the market with coin she didn't need, so she could hold it and read it and feel how lies were weighted.

It was a masterwork of the craft, she had to admit that. Whoever had set it upstairs — she suspected her own master's hands, and hated whoever had forced them — had produced a clean, handsome quarto, the Director's seal centered, the text dense and orderly. And it said, in smooth official prose:

*On Friday last, justice was executed upon seven criminals of the worst sort, agents of foreign gold, who by violence and intimidation sought to plunge Vellmouth into anarchy. These persons fired upon the forces of order. They burned the mill owner's warehouses. Armed resistance was offered and regretfully suppressed. The town may now sleep soundly. The restoration of order continues.*

Every line of it false. Wren had stood in that square. Nobody had fired upon anyone. Half the condemned had been too poor to own a pistol and the other half too sensible. There had been no warehouse fire — the scorch marks Maren described on the granary wall had been there since the spring flood, caused by an overturned lamp, investigated and forgotten months before any striker drew breath in anger. The broadsheet even named the wrong crime for two of the seven, accusing men of sabotage at the dye-works when they had been arrested at the mill gates. It lied lazily, carelessly, the way power lies when it has stopped believing anyone is checking.

But someone was checking now.

By noon the cellar press was running. They printed two hundred copies of the fourth issue of the chronicle — no title anyone dared print aloud anymore, just the little printer's device Jude had cut, a wren on a compositor's stick, absurd and anonymous — and the issue said what the square had seen. It named all seven. It gave Tam Ostler's age and his mother's street and the fact that he had been looking up at the windows as he walked. It printed Brice's four seconds of speech in full, and beside each claim of the official broadsheet it set, in a neat two-column comparison, what the Directorate said and what had actually happened, item by item, lie by lie. Jude had drawn the layout on the back of a jobbing proof: *their word / our word*, and where the columns diverged, the divergence itself became the accusation. No rhetoric required. Just the record, set square, printed black.

"Their sheet says armed resistance," Jude had said, inking the forme by candlelight. "Ours says: seven hundred people stood in the snow and none of them saw a single weapon but the state's. Which account do you suppose matches what six hundred witnesses carry home?"

Distribution was Maren's work now, refined since the fishwife's network had learned its hard lessons. No courier carried more than twelve copies. Each bundle traveled wrapped in ordinary goods — mackerel packed in straw, a bolt of dyed wool, a crate of candles — and each carrier knew only the next hand in the chain, no further. By evening prayer, copies were behind loose bricks in the tannery, under salt barrels at the wharf, folded inside hymnals in the chapel vestry. By the following noon they were being passed hand to hand openly, in daylight, in the market, because something had changed in Vellmouth and everyone could feel it.

What had changed was belief.

Wren heard it first at the bakery queue, standing behind two mill women who didn't know her face. *"Did you read the little bird sheet?"* one said, not lowering her voice nearly enough. *"My cousin stood ten feet from the scaffold. Says the bird sheet tells it true, every word. Says the official one's a fairy story for children."* And the other woman, older, cautious, glancing around — and then not glancing around — saying simply: *"Well. We know which one's lying, don't we. We were there."*

We were there. Wren carried those four words home through the snow like a coal in her cupped hands. That was the whole war, she understood suddenly. Not soldiers, not bridges, not even presses. Only the question of whose account the town believed when it lay in bed at night replaying the morning. For years the Directorate's version had won by default, because it arrived first and official and stamped, and doubting it cost more than swallowing it. Now, within hours of any event, a second account appeared — accurate, specific, unafraid — and people compared them, and comparing was the one act the Directorate could not hang them all for.

A town that checks is a town that has already refused you. Captain Aldous Verrek must have felt it too, wherever he sat; the clerks upstairs grew louder, faster, the requisitioned press running double shifts, flooding the streets with reprints and rebuttals and commemorative sheets nobody wanted. It didn't matter. Wren watched a Directorate notice nailed to the grain-office door get defaced within a day — not torn down, which was dangerous, but quietly annotated, in pencil, in a careful schoolroom hand: *not true*. Someone had checked. Someone always checked now.

* * *

On the eighth night after the execution, Maren came down the cellar stairs carrying a covered basket and wearing the expression of a woman deciding how much weight a girl can bear.

She set the basket down, uncovered it. Inside, wrapped in linen, were seven small things: buttons mostly, a carved loom-toggle, a clay pipe, a strip of embroidered collar. Relics, gathered by the families. And a paper.

"They're asking," Maren said. "The families. Whether the chronicle will remember them properly. Not the strike, not the politics — *them*. What Tam liked. What Brice kept in his pockets. Whether there'll ever be a stone anywhere in this town with their true names on it, or whether the Directorate gets to decide what their lives amounted to." She paused, and her voice, always so sure at the wharf, went uncertain in the candlelight. "I told them the little bird doesn't do stones. I told them ink isn't granite. But I said I'd ask."

Wren looked at the pipe, at the toggle, at the strip of embroidery stitched by a mother's hands for a boy who had walked to a scaffold looking up at windows. Ink wasn't granite. No, it wasn't. Granite could be toppled, though; she had seen the old monument in the mill district chiseled blank by the first purge, twenty years before she was born. Names erased from stone survived nowhere. But names set in type — type was a mother-mold; from one forme a hundred sheets, from a hundred sheets a thousand memories, and the forme could be buried in a flooded cellar and dug out twenty years on by someone who had never met you, and your name would come out of the ground still whole.

"I'll write to the families myself," Wren heard herself say. "Each house. I'll ask what they want remembered, and I'll set it exactly, and we'll print a memorial issue — portraits if Jude can cut them, trades, ages, the words they actually said. And I'll keep the forme safe under the waterline until there's a town that deserves to cast it in bronze." She picked up the loom-toggle and it was lighter than she expected, the way the dead are always lighter than you expect. "Ink isn't granite. But it can wait longer than granite can."

Maren studied her for a long moment — the girl she'd recruited out of the type room, the apprentice who had once flinched at smuggling twelve copies in a fish crate — and whatever she saw seemed to satisfy some tally of her own. "You've hardened," she said. It wasn't quite praise and it wasn't quite warning.

"Not hardened," Wren said, wrapping the relics again, laying them beside the formes where they would wait for the memorial issue. "Set. Like lead. You pour us soft, and then—" she touched the cold metal of the press frame "—and then something happens that fixes the shape, and after that you don't bend, you only break or hold."

Maren snorted, almost a laugh, and gathered her basket, and climbed the stairs, and her footsteps faded into the snow-muffled street.

Wren banked the candle, checked the forme one last time — seven names, locked tight in the chase, true and square and ready — and as she turned to follow, she heard it. Not the river's usual murmur. Something else, faint, rhythmic, wrong: boots. Many boots, moving up the alley above, stopping at the street door of Ravensworth & Son, followed by the flat official knock of men who intend to come in regardless of the answer.

Then, cutting through the floorboards from the shop above, smooth and unhurried and terribly familiar, the voice of Captain Aldous Verrek, saying: "Good evening, Master Ravensworth. May we talk about the little bird?"
