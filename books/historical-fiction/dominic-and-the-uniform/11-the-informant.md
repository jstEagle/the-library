## 11. The Informant

Maren Ostler had carried fish through every kind of weather Vellmouth could devise — sleet that froze in her braids, fog so thick the wharf vanished and she navigated by smell alone, summer heat that turned her baskets into a stench she wore like a second skin. She knew how to move through a crowd without being seen. She knew which of the town watch took bribes and which took pride, and she priced her goods accordingly for each. For eleven years the market had been her country, its customs her laws.

So when Tam Rooker did not appear at his stall on the Thursday before Candlemas, she knew before anyone told her that something had gone wrong. Tam was never late. Tam had sold eels through a fever that nearly killed him, through his mother's funeral, through the birth of his third child. A man like Tam did not miss a Thursday unless a man like Tam could not stand up to attend it.

By midmorning the story had come down the fishwives' line the way all stories did, passed mouth to mouth under the cover of haggling: soldiers had come to the Rope Walk before dawn. Not to the street — to number nine, the back house behind the cooper's yard, where Tam's cousin kept a room. They had taken four people. Two of them Maren knew by their false names only; one of them, a young woman from the mill district with ink-stained fingers and a stutter she conquered whenever she recited casualty lists aloud, Maren knew by her real one.

Her name had been Sela Brandt. Now it would be a name in a ledger, or worse, an absence in one.

Maren gutted whitefish with hands that did not shake, because shaking hands were noticed, and laid the fillets in rows as neat as type in a case, and inside herself she was screaming.

* * *

The network had rules, and Maren had written half of them herself, in the beginning, when it had been nothing but three fishwives, a lame ferryman, and a stack of pages that smelled of lamp-oil and cellar damp. Never carry more than five copies. Never take the same route twice in a week. Never let a page and a name meet in the same place — you might know the chronicle, or you might know a person, but no one should ever be able to prove you knew both.

And above all: *new blood moves slowly.* That rule they had broken.

It had seemed like mercy at the time. After the execution in the square — after Snow Over the Square, though nobody called it that aloud, the day having earned its name in silence — people had come to them. Not in ones and twos but in dozens, weavers and carters and two defected clerks, all of them asking to help, all of them burning with the particular fever of the newly converted. Wren Corliss had wanted to sign them all up. *The town believes us,* the apprentice had said, eyes bright with it. *We'd be mad to turn away hands now.*

Maren had argued. She remembered arguing. She remembered saying that belief was cheap and discipline was dear, that a movement made overnight could be unmade the same way. But the work was heavy and growing heavier — the chronicle came out now every six days instead of every ten, and every issue meant folding, wrapping, carrying, hiding — and so she had yielded, as she had not yielded to anyone in eleven years of selling fish to men who thought her slow because she was quiet.

One of the new hands had been Pell.

She tried now, walking home along the frozen canal with an empty basket and a mind full of knives, to remember exactly what she had seen in him. He had come to her first, which should have made her wary — volunteers who sought out a specific link in the chain were either earnest or hunting, and she had told herself he was earnest. He was maybe forty, soft-spoken, with the calloused hands of a warehouse man and a story about a brother lost in the mill district raids. His story had details. Too many details? She couldn't remember anymore. Grief made liars of everyone, including grief itself.

He had carried for her twice. Both times clean. Both times he had asked good questions afterward — where the pages went next, how many hands touched them between the market and the mill district. Good questions. The kind a careful man asked.

The kind a careful man asked, Maren corrected herself grimly, whether he was building the network or mapping it.

* * *

She went to the Rope Walk at dusk, taking the long way, doubling back twice, watching windows. Number nine was sealed with Directorate paper, the wax stamps glinting dully like pus under a skin. The cooper's yard stood empty. A neighbor woman drew water at the pump and did not look at Maren, which was itself a message: word had spread, and fear had spread with it, and both were moving faster than any chronicle.

Inside the back house — she did not go in, but she knew its shape from the one time she'd used it — there had been fourteen copies of the sixth issue hidden in a false-bottomed flour chest, and there had been the list. Not the distribution list; they were too careful for that. But Sela had kept a tally, names against addresses in a cipher that had seemed clever enough in October, and if the soldiers had found the chest and the tally together, then the cipher was not clever anymore. It was a map with the town's blood already dotted on it.

Maren stood in the falling dark until her feet went numb, and then she walked home by the canal path where no windows overlooked, and made her decision the way she did everything: slowly, thoroughly, and without flinching.

Pell would have to be tested.

* * *

The test was simple and cruel, and she hated it even as she built it.

On Saturday she gave him a delivery — five copies, wrapped in oilcloth, bound for the tannery lane. But she changed the route on him at the last moment, told him the old woman by the salt shed would receive them instead of the tanner's boy, and watched his face while she said it. Nothing moved in his face. That was the trouble with faces; the trained ones didn't move at all. So she added the hook: she told him the old woman was new to the chain, unreliable, possibly watched — *if anything feels wrong, drop the bundle in the culvert by the salt shed and walk on.* And then she posted Doss Harrow, the lame ferryman, in the loft above the salt shed with orders to watch the culvert until dark.

Pell delivered the bundle to the old woman cleanly. He thanked her for the work. He said his brother would have been proud.

And at noon on Sunday, the culvert was empty anyway.

Doss swore he'd seen nothing but a dog. But the bundle was gone, and the old woman swore she'd received hers, and when Maren went to check — walking past the salt shed with a basket of sprats, eyes down, heart hammering — the oilcloth lay folded neatly in the snow-melt at the culvert's mouth, and the pages inside were still there, dry, untouched.

A dog, then. Or a test of her own test. Or a man clever enough to leave the evidence and take the meaning: *I know this was a trap, Maren Ostler, and I passed it, and now you owe me trust.*

Or perhaps — and this was the thought that kept her awake through Sunday night, staring at the rafters while her husband slept beside her — perhaps he had simply done exactly as instructed, and she was poisoning herself with suspicion, and every hour she spent imagining Pell's guilt was an hour the true informer spent safe and warm somewhere else in her chain.

That was the worst of it. That was the lesson the Directorate taught without meaning to, the lesson that was perhaps its sharpest weapon: once betrayal entered a thing, it never left. You could not unlearn suspicion. You could distribute all the truth in the world and the lie of distrust would still find shelf space in every heart that read it.

* * *

Monday evening, the cellar beneath the print shop, candlelight, the smell of ink and river water. Maren laid it out before them because they deserved to hear it from her and not from the rumor line: the raid, the four taken, the tally likely compromised. Jude sat very still, hands flat on the composing stone as though steadying the whole shop through their palms. Wren paced. Old Ravensworth had withdrawn upstairs — these meetings were not for fathers whose craftsmanship the Directorate already owned.

"Fourteen copies," Maren said. "Four people. And if they broke Sela — and she was brave, but she was twenty, and nobody is brave forever under the right kind of questions — then they have addresses in the mill district, the Rope Walk, possibly the tannery lane."

"Then we warn them." Wren stopped pacing. "Tonight. Every address on—"

"There are no lists, Wren. That's the point. There's nothing to burn because there's nothing written." She said it gently, because the apprentice was young and this was the first time the movement had drawn blood out of them. "The list is in heads. Mine mostly."

"Then tell me the addresses and I'll walk them tonight."

"And whoever follows you walks them too, and finds a frightened family with fresh ink still on their doorstep, and follows *them* home." Maren shook her head. "No. We go quiet. No distribution this cycle. The seventh issue doesn't leave the cellar."

Jude looked up sharply. "The town will notice. Six days, like clockwork — people wait for it now. If it just stops—"

"It stopping is a message too. It says: we know they struck us, and we're still alive, and you won't catch us by waiting." Maren met Jude's eyes. In the candlelight the typesetter looked older than their years, and tired, and stubborn — a face she had come to trust the way she trusted her own hands, which was to say completely and with full knowledge that hands aged and failed. "Silence is a weapon, Jude. Your press taught the town that words matter. Let it learn what absence means."

Wren had gone pale, and Maren realized the apprentice had understood something else entirely. "Sela," Wren said. "Is she — do we know if she's—"

"I know where they take people," Maren said. "I don't know what comes out."

The silence that followed had weight, like floodwater pressing at the walls.

Then Jude said, quietly: "Who?"

Maren had prepared for the question and found, sitting with it in front of them, that she could not give the answer she had prepared. "Someone talked. Four arrests before dawn means someone gave a door and a name, or enough of a name to pull a thread." She turned her hands over, looking at them — fish-scaled, rope-scarred, honest hands. "It wasn't anyone old. The old links I'd stake my life on, and I have. It was new blood."

"Pell," Wren said at once.

Everyone looked at the apprentice.

"He asks too many questions," Wren said. "About routing. About how many hands touch a page. I noticed it weeks ago and thought — I thought he was just careful."

"So did I," Maren said, and hated how much the admission cost. "Careful men worry me now."

But Jude was frowning, slow and methodical, turning the thing over the way they turned a letter in their fingers to check the nick. "Careful cuts both ways. If Pell were Directorate, why volunteer at all? He could carry fish and listen. Why put himself in the chain, risk the searches, the patrols—"

"Because a chain needs a link cut from the inside," Maren said. "Because Verrek is patient. You've met him. Does that man grab, or does he cultivate?" She thought of Verrek's courtesy, his unsettling charm, the way he quoted things back; she had heard enough from Jude to fill in the shape of him. Men like that did not break networks. They grew them, like grafted stock, and harvested in their own season.

"We can't accuse him," Jude said. "Not on suspicion. Not on a missing bundle that came back dry. What do we even say — that he's too attentive? Half our best people are too attentive."

"Then we don't accuse," Maren said. "We isolate. From tonight, Pell carries nothing that matters. Wrappers, blank paper, whatever we want him to see. Everything real routes around him, and nobody tells him the routes changed. And we change every route, every drop, every signal — everything he learned, we retire."

"That could take weeks to rebuild," Wren protested.

"Better weeks than graves."

Jude was silent a long moment, thumb worrying the edge of the composing stone. When they spoke again, their voice had dropped, and Maren heard in it something she had not heard before — not the typesetter's certainty, not the apprentice's fire. Doubt, worn openly, like a tool laid on the table between honest workers.

"When this started," Jude said, "I thought the hard part would be the printing. The risk of the press, the patrols, the thaw. I never thought—" They stopped. Started again. "If we're wrong about Pell, we've cut a loyal man out of his own cause and left him standing in the cold wondering why. If we're right, we've learned it too late for Sela Brandt either way. There's no version of this where we don't lose something."

"No," Maren agreed. "That's what it costs. All of it. Even doing the right thing costs, because you can never be sure what the right thing was." She rose, knees cracking, and took her coat from the nail. "Verrek wants us afraid of each other. That's what informants are *for* — not the information, mostly worthless, but the rot they leave behind. So here's my answer to him: we get careful, and we stay human. We suspect Pell, and we don't destroy him. We grieve Sela, and we don't stop."

At the cellar door she paused, hand on the cold iron latch, and looked back at the two of them in the candlelight — the novelist's child and the orphan apprentice, guardians of a drowned press and a dangerous truth.

"One more thing," she said. "Nobody says Pell's name again. Not here, not anywhere. Not even to deny him. Names are what they arrest."

* * *

Walking home along the canal, Maren saw the ice had begun to whisper. Not the deep groan of the true thaw — that was weeks off yet, whatever the strange mild days suggested — but the small restless ticking of a river remembering that it was a river. Sound carries strangely over ice at night; a footstep a quarter mile off arrives at your ear like a knock at your own door. Twice she stopped, heart in her throat, and twice it was only the water talking to itself beneath the frost.

She thought of Sela Brandt, twenty years old, ink on her fingers, reciting the dead in a clear voice with no stutter at all. She thought of Pell's soft hands and detailed grief. She thought of Captain Aldous Verrek sitting somewhere warm with a ledger open, writing the town the way a man writes fiction — and she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that somewhere in his columns there was now a line item for her. *Fishwife, market. Distributor suspected. Watch.*

Let him write, then. She would make him earn every word.

At her door she stopped once more and looked back toward the river, where the bridge stood black against the faint grey of the sky, sealed and patient, holding Vellmouth shut like a fist around everything the Directorate hoped to keep.

Three streets away, a lantern bobbed along the towpath, moving unhurried, pausing at intervals — the pace, she noted, of a man checking doors.

It was headed toward Pell's lodging.

Maren stood in the snow with her key in her hand and understood that whatever happened before morning, the network would wake up changed — smaller, harder, and no longer able to pretend that printing truth was the most dangerous thing it did.
