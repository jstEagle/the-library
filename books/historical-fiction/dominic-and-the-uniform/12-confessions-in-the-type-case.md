## 12. Confessions in the Type Case

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width when Wren set the galley down too hard.

Jude heard the rattle of type from across the cellar — the sound a compositor makes when their hands are angry — and looked up from the stone where they had been cleaning the press rollers. The second issue of the chronicle was meant to be finished by dawn. Instead, Wren stood at the case with a stick of type half-set and shaking, and on the proof sheet before them, freshly pulled, were four names in capital letters, black as wet tar:

*OSTLER REPORTS: PELL OF THE MILL ROW SUSPECTED. THREE OTHERS UNDER WATCH.*

"You weren't going to show me this first," Jude said. It was not a question. They climbed the two steps out of the sump where they'd been working, water slopping over the tops of their boots, and crossed to the type case. The cellar smelled of river and oil and candle smoke, and beneath it all the sweet rot of old paper that had drowned years before they were born.

"It wasn't ready to show." Wren's jaw was set in the way it went set when they were frightened — Jude had learned this in three months of conspiracy, the way you learn the grain of a new piece of wood. "Maren brought the names tonight. Pell was seen at the customs house twice last week. Asking after cargo manifests. Talking to the patrol sergeant like an old friend."

"Seen by whom?"

"By people who know what they saw."

"That's not an answer, Wren."

Wren pulled the proof sheet off the stone and held it up between them, so close that Jude could smell the ink. "Neither is *innocent until we're certain*. We're not magistrates, Jude. There are no magistrates anymore. Verrek saw to that. There's just us, and the truth as fast as we can set it, and every day we wait is a day someone else walks into a room full of soldiers because Pell told them where the door was."

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, through three floors of joist and plank, they could hear their father moving about the shop — the deliberate, heavy tread he used now, always, so that any listener would know exactly where he was. He had not spoken more than fifty words at a stretch since he'd begun cutting the Directorate's commemorative type. The silence had gotten into everything, like damp.

Jude took the proof sheet from Wren's hands. Their own name for the trade rose up unbidden — *whoever sets the type sets the truth* — and for the first time since they'd hauled the cellar press out of the floodwater, the phrase tasted like iron filings.

"Sit down," they said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down anyway. This is going to take a while."

* * *

They sat on the upturned crate beside the paper stock, which was wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and Jude laid the proof sheet flat between them like something dissected on a table.

"Tell me how Maren got these names. Exactly. Word for word if you can remember it."

Wren frowned. "She said the fishwives watch the customs house because they have to — landing fees, quota inspections. And Pell was there twice, and both times he left by the sergeant's door, and once he carried a paper. She asked around. Two others confirmed it. And there's a woman from Tanner's Lane — she says Pell came to her asking where the Mill Row families slept, said he was collecting for the relief fund. Three days later the soldiers raided the Kessler house on Mill Row."

"The Kesslers. That's the family from the first issue. The names we printed."

"The same."

Jude nodded slowly and turned the proof sheet toward the candlelight. The four names stood up out of the page with terrible confidence. That was the thing about print that no one who hadn't worked metal understood: type did not hedge. A hand-copied rumor could trail off, could soften, could carry its own doubt along with it like sediment in a stream. But set type declared. Set type had been *locked*, chased, made into a single body of lead that could not take back a syllable. Every word on this sheet had already survived the only test type knew — the decision to compose it.

"Do you trust Maren?" Jude asked.

"With my life. Literally, at this point."

"And does Maren trust the women who gave her Pell's name?"

Wren opened their mouth, then closed it.

"Because I'll tell you what I see when I look at this page," Jude went on, keeping their voice low and even, though their pulse was not even at all. "I see a boy of nineteen who came to us in October with ink under his nails and a bad cough, and who has carried presses through flooded stairwells and stood watch in the snow. And I see four names that will be read by every person in Vellmouth who gets this issue, and I cannot tell you — *cannot*, Wren, not won't, cannot — which of those names is a traitor and which of them is unlucky."

"Pell carried a paper out of the sergeant's door."

"So does half the town. Indentures. Debt rolls. Confiscation notices. The Directorate hands out papers the way the plague handed out spots. If carrying a paper is evidence, then I'm guilty, and so are you, and so is your cousin who works the ferry toll."

"This isn't a game, Jude." Wren's voice cracked and they hated it — Jude could see them hating it. "The Kesslers are dead. Old man Kessler, the daughters, the baby. They're dead because somebody sold them, and you want to sit here and split evidentiary hairs while the next family packs their bags for whatever the Directorate does instead of prison."

"I don't want anything," Jude said. "That's the point. Wanting is what this page is for. Look at it." They tapped the proof. "You've already decided. This isn't a question being asked. It's a sentence being served. We'd be printing a verdict with no trial, no chance to answer, no way back. And everyone who reads it will believe it, because *we printed it*, because the chronicle doesn't lie — that's the whole reason they line the fish market at five in the morning to get it. We built a thing that tells the truth. And the first time we use it to settle a suspicion, we break it, and we can't ever fix it again. Type remembers, Wren. You taught me that, or I taught you, one of us. Once this page goes out, it's out forever. There's no pulling an impression back out of the world."

"Then what do we do? Nothing?"

"No. Something harder than nothing."

* * *

They argued for another hour. It was, looking back on it later, the strangest kind of argument — the kind conducted entirely in whispers, in a flooded cellar, over a galley of type worth hanging, and yet somehow also the most honest conversation Jude had ever had with another living soul. Wren cycled through fury and contempt and, near the end, a bleak exhausted honesty that Jude trusted far more than the fury.

"My father was a striker," Wren said at last, staring at the candle flame. "Before the mill closed. Before he drank himself into the churchyard. He used to say the worst thing the owners ever did wasn't the wage cuts. It was turning the men on each other. Piece rates so nobody knew who was slowing the line down. Foremen paying informers out of their own pockets so the men would never be sure whose handshake was clean." They looked up. "And here I am doing the Directorate's work for them. Turning us on each other."

"Maybe," Jude said carefully, "there's a version of naming names that isn't that. Maybe someday there has to be. Trials. Evidence. People answering publicly for what they did — Pell or anyone. I'm not saying the guilty get to hide behind our politeness forever. I'm saying we don't get to be the court. Not because we're not smart enough or good enough. Because a secret press can't hear a defense. Nobody accused can stand up in this cellar and say *you've got the wrong man*. And a verdict you can't argue with isn't justice. It's just weight. Like a press screw. It comes down the same on the innocent and the guilty."

Wren was quiet for a long time. Water dripped somewhere in the dark beyond the candle's reach, patient as a clock.

"If Pell's guilty," they said finally, "and we say nothing, more people die. That's the cost of your restraint. You should at least carry it honestly."

"I do carry it." Jude's throat was tight. "I'll carry it the rest of my life if it turns out that way, and I'd rather carry that than the other thing. But we don't do nothing. We do what a press is actually *for*."

They pulled the offending galley apart, distribution by distribution — Wren insisted on doing it themselves, letter by letter back into the case, and Jude let them; some penances should be done by hand. Then Jude sat down at the composing stick and began again.

Not names. Never names. Instead:

*A NOTE ON INFORMERS AND ON KEEPING EACH OTHER ALIVE.*

*The Directorate pays for betrayal and calls it loyalty. We will not print accusations, because an accusation without trial is the Directorate's own method wearing borrowed clothes. But we will tell you how to be safe, because safety is a craft like any other, and it can be learned.*

Then, in the plainest language they could manage, the procedures. No courier carries more than one copy. No courier knows the destination of any but their own. Change houses for meetings; never meet twice in the same place within a month. Ask nothing of a comrade you would not volunteer freely yourself. Trust the network's discipline, not any single person's virtue — a chain is judged by its weakest link, so make every link small. If you suspect someone, do not accuse, do not confront, do not warn — simply route around them, quietly, and pass your suspicion upward to one person only, never sideways through the market where rumor breeds.

*Paranoia is a solvent,* Jude wrote. *It dissolves trust faster than any informer can. The Directorate knows this. Half their army is the fear of their army. Deny them your fear and you have beaten half their strength already.*

It took them until the candle was a puddle and the second candle was half gone. Wren checked the proofs in silence, corrected two words, locked the forme, and began to ink the platen. Only when the press was running — the muffled, felt-wrapped thud that had become as familiar as a heartbeat — did either of them speak again.

"Maren's not going to like it," Wren said.

"Maren's seen what happens when a market crowd turns on a suspected name. She'll understand better than we do."

"And if Pell really is selling us? Restraint won't stop him."

"No," Jude agreed. "But it means that when we finally do name him — if we ever do, with real proof, with the chance for him to stand somewhere and answer — the chronicle will still mean something when we do. That's all restraint buys us, Wren. It buys us the right to be believed later."

Wren pulled the first clean copy of the new issue and held it up to dry, the ink gleaming wet and black in the candlelight. "You've changed," they said, and it wasn't quite accusation. "In October you thought history happened in other towns."

"In October I thought a lot of things." Jude looked at their own hands — stained, cold, nicked with burins, steady. "I think I thought power was something other people had, so it didn't matter what I did with it. Now we've got a press that half the town believes like scripture, and I'm finding out it matters enormously what we do with it. That's the trick of the thing, isn't it. Power doesn't corrupt because bad people find it. It corrupts because good people find it and discover it fits their hands so comfortably."

The last copies came off the press as the river below them began, faintly, to murmur louder — the winter swell, weeks early. Upstairs, the shop bell chimed once. Someone at the street door, at this hour.

Wren killed the candle. In the sudden dark, listening to boots cross the floorboards above, Jude found themselves thinking not of Pell at all, but of Captain Verrek's soft voice at their father's table, weeks ago: *I admire a well-made thing, Ravensworth. A well-made thing tells you who made it.*

The boots stopped directly overhead.
