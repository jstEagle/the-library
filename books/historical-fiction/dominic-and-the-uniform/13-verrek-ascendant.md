## 13. Verrek Ascendant

Captain Aldous Verrek had learned long ago that fire was the most honest of all instruments. A rifle lied — it could miss, could wound where it meant to kill, could leave a man alive to tell a version of events. A rope lied too, in its way, turning a death into a grievance that outlived the body. But a fire told the truth with perfect clarity: this existed, and now it does not. There was no ambiguity in ash.

He stood at the window of the requisitioned customs house — his customs house now, though the paperwork had not caught up with the fact — and watched the square below as soldiers stacked the morning's confiscations into a pyramid. Books came up the steps from the cellar carts in armfuls, passed hand to hand like buckets at a blaze run backward. Hymnals of the Reformed Congregation, unlicensed. Almanacs printed across the river two winters past, their calendars bearing the wrong government's seal. School primers with maps that showed Vellmouth's province under an older flag. Poetry, some of it — poetry was always the worst offender, because no one could say what sedition slept inside a rhyme until it woke.

Verrek dressed slowly, with ceremony. He had brought three uniforms north when the Directorate assigned him to Vellmouth, and he chose today the least worn of them: dark wool, brass buttons polished with vinegar and a scrap of flannel, the collar stiffened so that it held his jaw at the angle he preferred — slightly lifted, so that men addressing him had the sensation of speaking uphill. He was not a tall man. He had understood by his twentieth year that height was a currency one could counterfeit through posture, and he counterfeited it flawlessly.

In the glass, briefly, he saw not himself but his father — a notary in the provincial capital who had spent thirty years recording other men's decisions in a small careful hand and died owing money on his own burial plot. Aldous had been eleven at that funeral. He remembered thinking, with a child's merciless precision, that his father had spent his whole life writing down what mattered to other people and nothing that mattered to anyone at all. The thought had never left him. It had simply matured, the way a splinter of steel works deeper into flesh each year until the body grows around it.

He would not record other men's decisions. He would make them, and then he would write them down.

* * *

The square was full by ten o'clock, which pleased him more than he allowed his face to show. He had given instructions that attendance should be voluntary, and voluntary attendance — achieved through rumor, curtailment of market permits for the morning, and the simple gravitational pull of spectacle — was worth ten times what compulsion bought. Compelled crowds sulked. Voluntary ones participated, and participation was the thing. He did not merely want the town to watch its past burn. He wanted hands in it. He wanted every citizen of Vellmouth to be able to say, afterward, *I was there, I saw it, I stood in that crowd* — because a witness is already half an accomplice, and an accomplice does not write inconvenient chronicles.

Sergeant Holm had the pyre arranged according to Verrek's specifications: not a heap but a structure, tiered, with the largest volumes at the base and the thin pamphlets latticed above so the flame would climb rather than gutter. Verrek had corrected the first arrangement personally, moving three books with his own gloved hands, and he noted without surprise how the soldiers' posture changed once they'd seen an officer treat kindling as a matter of doctrine. Men followed detail. Detail was where conviction lived or didn't.

At half past ten he mounted the customs house steps, unfolded a single sheet — he wrote his own speeches; delegating prose was delegating power — and let the crowd quiet itself. It took longer than it once would have. Good. A crowd that takes time to hush is a crowd that has begun talking among itself, and talk among themselves was precisely what the last month had taught these people to do. The fishwives whispered at their stalls. The mill district, still smoldering in its grief from the executions, had gone strangely silent in a way he trusted less than wailing. And somewhere in the town there moved, he was nearly certain, a printed thing that bore his name in its accusations — a thing produced on equipment he had catalogued and sealed and believed himself master of.

He began to read.

"Citizens of Vellmouth," he said, and his voice carried well; he had trained it against the acoustics of parade grounds. "A town is not its buildings. A town is its memory. And memory, gentlemen, madams, neighbors — memory can be poisoned."

He gestured, and Sergeant Holm dragged the first cart forward, tipping its cargo down the steps with a sound like felled timber.

"For years you have been fed lies by men who profited from your confusion. The almanacs of a foreign press. The pamphlets of agitators. The sermons of men who preached obedience to God and practiced obedience to creditors." He paused, let the sentence settle, watched faces. "The Provisional Directorate does not ask you to forget. Forgetting is passive, and passivity is what made you vulnerable. The Directorate asks you to remember *correctly*."

There it was — the phrase he had been polishing for a week, turned over in his mind the way the type-cutter Ravensworth must turn a graver over copper. *Remember correctly.* It had a ring that ordinary brutality lacked. Anyone could order a burning. Only an author could name it.

The torch went into the pyramid at a quarter past eleven, and the smoke rose thick and white and then gray, carrying off the paper smell he had come to find almost pleasant — sweetish, funereal. The crowd pressed nearer than he expected. That was the way of it everywhere: people came to condemn and stayed to look, and looking became a kind of appetite. He watched a woman in a fish-market apron cross herself and then, shamefully, lean closer to see which book the flames were eating first. He watched a boy of perhaps twelve pick a scorched leaf of paper from the cobbles, read it, and drop it as though it had bitten him.

And he watched, near the fountain, a young person with ink-stained fingers and the build of an apprentice — Corliss, the Ravensworth shop boy, the one whose name appeared in his requisition ledgers beside a notation about a decommissioned press — stand entirely still through the whole of it, not watching the fire but watching *him*, Verrek, with an expression of such flat, careful hatred that the captain felt something adjacent to respect. Good. Hatred was legible. Hatred could be tracked. It was indifference that defeated an administrator, and there was very little indifference left in Vellmouth.

When the fire had burned down to a red skeleton, Verrek spoke the closing lines he had drafted in bed two nights ago, waking with them complete:

"History is not what happened. History is what is written by those with the discipline to write it. I intend that Vellmouth have such a historian."

He stepped back from the rail before applause could become necessary. Applause that has to be waited for is worse than none.

* * *

In the afternoon he received the delegation from the Provincial Commission on Public Information — three clerks and a commissioner's aide named Duvane, who arrived dusty from the coast road and full of capital manners that Verrek found simultaneously contemptible and useful.

"The Directorate is pleased with the situation report," Duvane said, in the customs house's confiscated parlor, over tea that Verrek had ordered served hot and strong because weak tea was the beverage of men who hedged. "The mill district is pacified. The bridge closure holds. You've requisitioned both print shops in the town?"

"One print shop and one jobbing office," Verrek said. "Ravensworth's establishment produces the official broadsheet now, under pre-approval. The Hessel office was smaller; I converted it to a records annex."

"And the second press reported in the Ravensworth inventory? The old cylinder machine, decommissioned—"

"Accounted for," Verrek said, in a tone that closed the ledger on it. In truth the inventory entry read *present, inoperable, cellar storage*, and he had sent a clerk down to confirm it twice. Both times the clerk had returned reporting floodwater in the cellars, standing black water to a depth that made inspection impractical, and both times Verrek had signed the report and filed it and thought, in the small cold room of himself where he kept things he did not say aloud: *impractical inspections are how empires die.* But the water was real; he had smelled it from the stairhead, river-smelling, mineral and old. And a press beneath four feet of river was a press for the spring, not for the winter. He would deal with it at the thaw, when the historians came and the town was opened like an oyster.

Duvane produced papers. "The Commission wishes to formalize the historical program. When the ice breaks, a team of historians will arrive on the first barge — Archivist Lenz heading it, you may know the name — to compile the definitive account of the unrest. The executions, the strike, the restoration of order. They'll want documents. Testimony. Your casualty ledgers."

"They'll have everything," Verrek said smoothly.

This was true, in the sense that he intended them to have exactly what remained after his editing. The casualty ledgers sat in his safe, and they were beautiful — he had spent two evenings on them himself, adjusting figures in a clerk's hand he could imitate better than most clerks. Nineteen dead at the mill gate had become eleven, then seven, then four, the numbers descending across three drafts like a staircase built for descending reputations. The final figure would match the official broadsheet, which matched the testimony he had coached from two constables and a foreman, which matched the commemorative plaque text currently being cut — he had inspected proofs — in type so fine and reverent that no one reading it would think to count gravestones.

Lenz and his historians would arrive, and they would find a town whose paperwork sang in one voice. And if any dissonant page survived anywhere — a smuggled pamphlet, a misfiled letter, some underground chronicle printed by candlelight on a press he could not yet prove existed — then it would surface against him later, and he knew it, and the knowing sat in him like a swallowed coin.

Which was why, he reminded himself, the burns were only half the work. Fire destroyed the past. Writing secured the future. He had requested, in his latest dispatch to the capital, the appointment of *commissioner of public information and provincial historiography* — a title he had invented, there being no such office, which was precisely the point. Invent the office, occupy the office, and the office becomes eternal. His father had recorded history. Aldous Verrek intended to constitute it.

"Commissioner Duvane," he said, setting down his cup, "when Archivist Lenz arrives, I want him received properly. Dinner here. The best table. And I want the first week of his research conducted in this building, under my roof, with my clerks assisting." He smiled — the smile he had practiced against mirrors until it stopped being practice. "History is collaborative work. It would be a pity if the archivist labored alone."

Duvane, who was not stupid, looked at him for a moment a moment too long, and then wrote the request into his notebook anyway. Everyone, Verrek had found, could be brought to write things down. It was simply a question of what fear or hope you offered in exchange.

* * *

Evening brought the report he had been waiting for, delivered by Corporal Enniskerry, a narrow young man with a talent for shadowing people unnoticed.

"The apprentice, sir. Corliss. Left the burning early, went to the Ravensworth shop, came out after dark with a covered basket. Made deliveries to two houses on Tanner's Row and one behind the fish market. Didn't knock loud. Left quick."

"Contents?"

"Couldn't see, sir. Flat parcels. Paper-weight."

Flat parcels, paper-weight, delivered quietly to houses near the fishwives' stalls. Verrek stood at the window again, watching the square where the pyre's remains still smoked faintly under new snow, and permitted himself the luxury of assembling the picture: a hidden press, working under the river's noise; a distribution net strung through the market women; a chronicle, anonymous, unsigned, accurate enough to frighten. Somewhere in his chest, beneath the operational cold, something that was nearly admiration stirred — and beneath the admiration, something hotter that he declined to name, because naming it would mean admitting that somewhere in his occupied town an enemy was matching him craft for craft, edition for edition, and that the war for Vellmouth had narrowed at last to the only battlefield he had ever truly feared: the printed page.

"Double the patrols along the waterfront," he said. "And find me the names of every fishwife who sold at yesterday's market. Every one. If a page can swim, I want to know the water it swims in."

Enniskerry saluted and withdrew. Verrek remained at the window a while longer, and in the glass his reflection wore the expression of a man drafting a chapter — because that, in the end, was what he was doing. The fire had been his preface. The historians would be his collaborators. And the underground press, whatever cellar it hid in, whoever's hands worked it — that would be his central antagonist, his necessary villain, the resistance his narrative required.

Every history, he thought, turning down the lamp, needs a monster.

By spring, he intended to have written one.
