## 14. The Raid That Missed

The river told them first.

Jude heard it in the small hours — a change in the cellar's voice, the water beneath the floorboards rising from a murmur to a low continuous roar, the sound of the Vellmouth swelling with upstream melt weeks ahead of schedule. It should have frightened them. Instead it had felt like a blessing: the louder the river, the quieter the press.

They were on the third page of the night's run when the knocking came.

Not the polite knock of a customer. Not even the hard knock of a patrol passing through. This was fists on wood in a rhythm meant to break thought — three strikes, a pause, three more — and beneath it the sound of boots, many boots, spreading along the front of the shop like water finding the low places in a floor.

Wren's head came up from the tympan, eyes enormous in the candlelight. The brayer was still in their hand, ink-black to the wrist.

"Finish the sheet," Jude whispered. It was instinct before it was strategy — the forme was inked, the paper laid, and a half-pulled impression left drying on the stone was a confession no amount of stalling could explain. "Pull it clean, then damp the fire. Everything under the canvas."

Below them, the press kept its rhythm. Maren's cousin Tam was at the crank tonight, big slow Tam who moved like a man hauling nets, and Jude prayed to no one in particular that he had heard the door, that he had the sense to stop—

He hadn't stopped. The platen fell, fell again, steady as a heart, and the river roared over it, and Jude could do nothing about any of it but climb the stairs and open the door.

* * *

Captain Aldous Verrek stood on the threshold with snow in the collar of his grey coat and six soldiers fanned out behind him across the yard, their lanterns making small orange worlds of the falling flakes. He looked, as always, as though he had been invited. He looked, as always, as though he had done the inviting himself.

"Mistress Ravensworth." He removed his glove to shake her hand, which he had never done before, and the courtesy of it was worse than a rifle butt. "Forgive the hour. We're conducting a survey."

"A survey." Jude kept their voice level, kept their hands where he could see them, ink-stained to the knuckles. They had thought, in the four seconds between the knock and the stairs, about washing. Then decided the ink was better armor than any lie. A printer at night is printing. A printer with clean hands at midnight is hiding something. "Of what, Captain? The shop hasn't moved since you last counted it."

"Of print capacity." Verrek stepped inside without being asked, and the soldiers followed, and the warmth of the shop went out of the air like a snuffed candle. "The Directorate has received new instructions regarding the documentation of the restoration. There will be — materials required. Commemorative work. Historical work." He said *historical* the way other men said *heretical*, with the small satisfaction of a word well chosen. "We must know exactly what every press in Vellmouth can produce, and for whom."

"And you couldn't count presses by daylight?"

"Daylight is for things that don't change." He smiled. He had a good smile; that had always been the obscenity of him. "May we look?"

Jude wanted to say no just to watch what his face did. Instead they spread their hands — *the shop is yours, the shop was always yours* — and Verrek turned to his sergeant and said, "Begin below."

* * *

The word *below* went through Jude like a nail through paper.

They watched the sergeant take two men and a lantern toward the cellar stair, and every calculation they owned ran at once and arrived nowhere. The press was running. Tam was at the crank with forty sheets still wet in the rack, and the cellar door at the back of the stairwell — Wren had oiled it Tuesday — opened onto the coal chute and the flood passage, but Tam was not Wren, Tam did not know the passage, Tam would stand there blinking like an ox while the lantern came down the steps, and then there would be forms and questions and a name read aloud into a ledger, and after the ledger came the mill district lists, Jude knew the order of things now as well as they knew the order of the case.

"Actually—" Jude heard their own voice, too loud, and moderated it with an effort that cost them something physical. "Actually, Captain, if it's capacity you want, the cellar is the wrong place to start. The old press down there is scrap. My father decommissioned it nine years ago. Rust in the screw, cracked bed. You'd learn more from the ledgers upstairs, and I'll confess the ledgers are my pride. If your clerks are going to write reports—"

"My clerks," Verrek said pleasantly, "can count rusted iron without my help."

He gestured, unhurried, and the sergeant went down the stairs, and Jude stood in the middle of their own shop listening to boots descend into the dark toward the sound of the river and the sound of the press, and found they had stopped breathing.

* * *

It was their father who saved it, though no one in the room understood until later that it had been saving.

Elias Ravensworth came out of the back rooms in his nightshirt and dressing gown, hair white and wild, spectacles pushed up on his forehead, moving with the peculiar authority of a man who has been woken and considers this an offense against a higher order than the Directorate. He was seventy-one years old and had cut type since he was seven, and his hands had begun, in the last months, to carry a tremor he pretended not to notice — but there was no tremor in his voice at all.

"Whoever is tramping about my shop," he announced to the lanterns, "will please keep off the stone floor by the imposing table. The slabs are Vermont marble and they chip, and I have chipped enough of them in fifty years to know that the Directorate will not be paying for replacements."

"Sergeant Dray is going to the cellars, Master Ravensworth," Verrek said, with a bow that managed to be an insult. "A routine inventory."

"The cellars." Elias's mouth turned down with genuine, magnificent distaste. "Then he'll ruin his boots and find nothing worth the walk, and he'll do it on marble if he cuts across by the bank of stone, so someone had better call down to him. Captain, if you've come about the commemorative commission, sit, sit — no, not there, that's a composing stool, you'll put your back out — and let me tell you what your clerks' last instructions have already made impossible."

Jude watched their father lower himself into the chair by the type cases like a king taking a throne built of small lead kingdoms, and understood, with a lurch, exactly what he was doing. He could not stop the search. So he would make himself more interesting than it. He had spent fifty years making letters that no eye could refuse to follow, and now he would be the letter.

Verrek hesitated — one heartbeat, visible only to someone watching for it — between the cellar door and the offered chair. Curiosity won. It was the flaw in him that Jude had learned to pray to: Verrek could not bear a thing he had not measured.

"Impossible," Verrek repeated, sitting. "Your clerks' instructions?"

"The commemorative text." Elias pulled a proof sheet from the rack beside him and spread it on his knees like a patriarch with a map. "Restoration of Order, dedication set in twenty-four-point roman, your office's own draft, returned twice for corrections. Captain, I am going to educate you, because apparently no one in the Provisional Directorate has ever set a line of type in their lives, and someone must say these things."

"Father," Jude said softly.

"No, they should hear it." And then Elias was off — into the difference between cast and cut sorts, into the villainy of the clerk who'd specified a ligature that hadn't been cast since the old king's mint closed, into a lecture on counters and serifs delivered with such pedantic, such inexhaustible relish that even the soldiers by the door began to shift their weight, and Verrek himself sat trapped in the courtesy he wore like armor, unable to interrupt a craftsman explaining his craft without conceding that craft mattered less than power, which was precisely the concession he could never make in front of witnesses.

And underneath the lecture — under Elias's rolling voice, under the tick of the stove, under everything — Jude listened to the cellar.

The press had stopped. When, they couldn't say; somewhere in the first minutes of the lecture, Tam had killed the light or the crank or both, and now there was nothing beneath the floor but the river's long throat-sound, rising, rising, the Vellmouth climbing its own banks in the dark. Boots moved down there. A lantern beam swung once across the underside of the floorboards, a pale seam of light between two boards that traveled, traveled — and stopped directly beneath Jude's feet, and stayed.

Someone down there was looking at something.

* * *

"—and that," Elias was saying, "is why your casualty tables are illegible, Captain, if you'll forgive the term. Figures cut by a man who learned on jobbing work. The threes are criminal. Look—" he was up, astonishingly fast, dragging Verrek by nothing but momentum toward the jobbing case against the wall, "look at this three. Open counter, flat spine, no ball terminal worth the name. Now here—" his trembling finger found a compartment, lifted a sort, held it to the lamp between thumb and forefinger, "here is a three. Cut by my own hand in the year of the ferry disaster. Feel the weight of it. Balance. A number is a promise, Captain. Your Directorate prints numbers, and every schoolboy in Vellmouth can feel that they're lying, because the *threes are wrong*."

Jude's blood went cold and then, strangely, warm.

Because their father knew. Of course he knew. He had cut the chronicle's headline figures himself, three nights ago, silently, without being asked, the sabotaged letters of the official commissions hidden in the same case — and now he stood in front of the Captain of the Provisional Directorate holding up a numeral and saying, in effect, *I can hear your lies in the shape of your letters,* and dressing the whole accusation in vanity where it could not be legally touched.

Verrek took the three. Turned it in his gloved fingers. For a long moment his face did the thing it almost never did: it showed thought.

"You're telling me," he said slowly, "that typography carries truth."

"I'm telling you typography carries *craft*, Captain, and lies travel badly in good type. It's why regimes always end up printing on cheap presses. The cheap presses are all they deserve." Elias held out his palm for the sort back, patient as a schoolmaster, and got it.

Under the floor, the seam of lantern light moved on. Swept once more across the boards. Climbed away toward the stairs.

Jude breathed for the first time in a minute.

* * *

Sergeant Dray surfaced looking like a drowned cat that had expected mice and found rust. He reported in a flat soldier's mumble: old press, seized screw, standing in six inches of river water, no workspace fit for use, nothing current, nothing dry, nothing printed. He said the word *nothing* with the slight resentment of a man who had been promised something.

"Six inches of water," Verrek repeated. His eyes came around the room — past Jude, past the drying rack, past the covered forme on the stone — and rested on Elias. "The river is high this year."

"The river is always high in thaw, Captain. It's rather the definition." Elias resettled his spectacles. "You'll want the inventory ledgers, I imagine, so your clerks can copy my errors into their columns. Second shelf, blue spines. Mistress Ravensworth will fetch them; my knees have opinions about ladders tonight."

Verrek did not move for a moment. Jude felt the whole night balance on him — on whether he would cross to the cellar door again, whether he would ask why a decommissioned press sat in a foot of floodwater instead of sold for scrap, whether he would look at the damp patch on the canvas over the stone and lift its corner and find forty sheets of names still smelling faintly of ink and candle smoke.

What he did was smile.

"Master Ravensworth. You are a remarkable old man." He drew on his second glove. "I hope the Directorate deserves its threes."

"It doesn't," Elias said mildly. "No one does. That's rather the point of cutting them well."

Verrek laughed — a short, real laugh, immediately regretted — and collected his soldiers with a tilt of his head, and the lanterns went out into the snow one by one, and the door closed, and the boots faded up the lane toward the square, and the shop stood silent except for the river, roaring now like something with a purpose.

Jude waited until the last footfall died. Then they crossed to the window and watched the patrol's lights shrink between the falling flakes, and counted to a hundred, and then to another hundred, because Wren had taught them that paranoia done properly is arithmetic.

Down the cellar stair came small quick footsteps — Wren, black to the elbows, white in the face, with Tam behind them carrying the drying rack like a man carrying a coffin.

"We stopped the moment the knocking started," Wren whispered. "Forty-one sheets. All of them finished. Jude — he was *in* the cellar. He stood next to the press. I was behind the cistern with the candle pinched out and I could have touched his coat."

"I know." Jude's voice came out strange, thin. They looked at their father, who had sunk back into his chair and was cleaning his spectacles with hands that shook now, properly shook, the performance spent and the toll come due.

"Your threes," Jude said quietly. "He'll think about them. He'll go home and pull every proof his office ever printed and look at the numerals, because you planted it in him, Father, and Verrek isn't a man who leaves a thought unplanted."

"I know that too." Elias set the spectacles on his nose and looked at his child across the lamplit shop, and behind his eyes was something Jude had never seen there before: not fear, and not defiance, but the cold clarity of a man doing arithmetic of his own. "Which is why you have days, perhaps. He'll come back not for the cellar but for the *case*. He'll requisition the type itself — every sort of mine he can identify, to be recut by his own people. A craftsman's sabotage survives inspection; it doesn't survive confiscation."

Outside, far off toward the bridge, the ice spoke — a deep cracking boom that rolled up the valley like cannon fire, and both of them turned their heads toward the sound.

"Sooner than we thought," Jude said. "The thaw is early. And now he's coming for the letters themselves."
