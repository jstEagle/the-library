## 15. Pell's Choice

Tobias Pell had not slept properly in nineteen days. He knew the count precisely because he kept it the way a debtor keeps sums — chalked on the inside of his own skull where no clerk could find it and read it back to him. Nineteen days since the night the millwright's son, Ansel Kroy, had been carried out of the interrogation room at the customs house feet first, and Pell had stood aside to let the bearers pass, and had seen that the boy's hands were wrong. Not broken. Wrong. Folded one over the other with a care that no living man's hands would have tolerated, arranged by someone who understood that they were arranging a corpse and wished it to look like sleep.

Pell had helped arrange them.

That was the sentence he returned to, over and over, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. He had not struck the boy. He had not even spoken his name into the little room with its drain in the floor and its chair bolted crooked. All Pell had done — all, only, merely — was give Captain Verrek's clerks the address on Tanner's Row where Ansel Kroy had been sleeping, and the name of the woman whose kitchen he ate in, and the hour the fishwives' pages changed hands. He had done it for coin, in the beginning. Then he had done it because refusing, after taking the coin, seemed more dangerous than continuing. And by the end he had done it because it was easier than not doing it, which was the most shameful arithmetic of all.

He was a small man with a clerk's stoop and a face built for forgetting — the kind of face that never made it into a witness's mouth whole. For years this had been his fortune. He sold thread and buttons from a stall near the corn exchange, and nobody remembered him, and so when the Directorate came looking for ears in Vellmouth, they had found his ears convenient: attached to a man no one guarded himself against. He had told himself, in those first weeks, that information was a soft trade. That names passed in a tavern were not the same as stones thrown. He had told himself many things, and each one had cost him something, and now the ledger of those costs had come due in the shape of a dead boy's folded hands.

On the twentieth morning he did not open his stall. He pinned a card to the awning — *ill, back tomorrow* — which was true in a way, and walked through the falling grey snow toward Cooper's Lane with his collar up and his heart going like a trapped bird against his ribs.

He was going to confess. He had decided this at three in the morning, sitting upright in bed with the candle long dead, and the decision had felt less like courage than like gravity: a thing his body simply did now, having been filled past holding. But a confession required a confessor, and he had thought about this with a clerk's thoroughness too. He could not go to Father Ivo — the church kept no secrets from men who paid for secrets. He could not go to Maren Ostler; she would put a gutting knife in him before he finished the second sentence, and he would deserve it, but a dead informer told Verrek nothing useful and the chronicle everything. No. It had to be Ravensworth. The type-cutter's child. Jude. Because Jude had written the pages — Pell knew that much now, knew it with the sick certainty of a man who has finally looked at what is in his own pockets — and because the pages were the only altar left in Vellmouth where a confession might mean anything.

And because he had something to lay on that altar besides guilt.

* * *

The print shop's windows were papered over now, he noticed — great sheets of brown paper pasted inside the glass, so that from the street the place had the blind look of a face with its eyes shut. He stood across the lane for a long while, letting carts pass, letting a squad of Directorate soldiers tramp by toward the square with their breath smoking, telling himself three times to turn around and three times refusing to hear himself. The bell above the door had been muffled with cloth. When he pushed the door it opened without sound, which somehow frightened him more than any noise would have.

The smell hit him first: ink and oil and lamp-black and, beneath it, the cold river smell of the cellars that rose up through the floorboards like a memory the building couldn't keep down. The shop was dim. The big press stood under a canvas sheet like furniture in a dead relative's house. At the back, bent over the type case in the light of a single shaded lamp, was Jude Ravensworth, sleeves rolled, a composing stick in hand, setting letters with the quick sure economy of someone doing the one thing in the world they were made for.

Jude looked up. Pell watched the recognition move across their face — not warmth, not welcome, but the flat alertness of an animal that has caught a scent on the wind.

"We're closed," Jude said. "Commissions go through the Directorate now. You'll want the customs house."

"I don't want the customs house." Pell's voice came out thinner than he intended. He worked his cap in his hands. "I need—" He had rehearsed this. Standing before the door he had rehearsed it, walking the lane he had rehearsed it, and now every word had evaporated and left only the raw thing underneath. "I need to talk to you where there's no wall with ears."

"Whatever you're selling—"

"I'm the informant."

The words dropped into the room like a type case turned over on a stone floor. Pell heard them land and saw Jude go very still — stillness of a particular kind, the stillness of a hand hovering over a press lever, deciding. Behind the lamp the shadows held their breath. Somewhere below the floor, faint as a pulse under skin, Pell thought he heard the river working at the cellar walls.

"You'd better," Jude said at last, quietly, "come away from the window."

* * *

They talked in the back room, among the reams of paper stacked like fallen snow, with the lamp turned low and Jude standing the whole time, never sitting, arms folded, positioned — Pell noted with a distant professional admiration — between Pell and the cellar door. Wren Corliss was sent up to the Ravensworths' rooms above, over his loud objection, and came down the stairs backwards glaring at Pell with a hatred so pure and so young that it nearly undid him. *Good*, Pell thought. *Hate me. It's cleaner than what I feel.*

So it was the two of them, and the lamp, and the paper.

Pell laid it out the way he laid out stock: methodically, in order, without ornament, because ornament was a form of lying and he had sworn to himself on the walk over that tonight he would not lie. His name — though they likely knew it already, or would guess it. The recruitment, eighteen months ago, a courteous man from the provincial capital buying buttons and staying to buy opinions. The first name he had given, a cooper with a loud mouth and no politics, who got a fine and a warning. The second, and the tenth, and then the addresses of the fishwives' drop points, the schedule of Maren Ostler's market rounds, the safehouse on Tanner's Row. He named the people detained because of him. He made himself say each name aloud, complete, watching Jude's face harden line by line like cooling metal. He did not soften anything. He owed the truth its full weight; measured short, it was worthless.

"And Ansel Kroy," he said, and here his voice failed him for the first time, and he had to begin again. "Ansel Kroy I gave them on the ninth of the ice month. Address, hours, the woman whose kitchen he used. They took him that same night." He stared at his own hands, folded in his lap, and hated how neatly they folded. "I didn't know they'd — I told myself they wanted him for questioning. Everyone says that. *Questioning.* As if questions were the worst thing in that room." He looked up. "His hands were wrong. They'd folded them. Like you'd fold a shirt. I stood in the corridor and watched, and I did nothing, and two days later I gave them another name anyway, because by then stopping was harder than continuing, and I want you to understand that about me. I'm not telling you I was brave once and broke. I'm telling you I was a coward the whole way down, and the bottom of cowardice looks exactly like ordinary life. You get up. You open your stall. You sell thread. You go home. And somewhere in the town a boy your own son's age is being folded into a shape that fits a coffin, and you eat your supper."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the wind dragged snow along the gutter with a sound like sand poured on paper.

Jude had not moved through any of it. Now they spoke, and their voice was low and level and worse than shouting. "Why are you telling me this? Verrek finds out, you hang. Or worse. What do you think I can give you? Absolution?" A bitter half-laugh. "I set type, Master Pell. I don't forgive sins. There isn't a font in Christendom cast in that metal."

"No," Pell said. "I know that. I've stopped expecting forgiveness. I expect it about as much as Ansel Kroy expects breakfast." His hands shook now, and he let them. "I came for something else. Two things. First — the network. Whatever's left of it. Maren will want my blood, and she should have it if she wants it, but she needs to know everything I gave them before Verrek uses it again. Every drop point. Every name. The routes I steered clear of because I pitied someone, which is the only reason anyone on your side is still alive — my pity, such as it was, is your surviving map. Take it. Write it down in your own hand. I'll say it slow."

"That's the first thing." Jude's eyes narrowed. "What's the second?"

Pell reached into his coat, and saw Jude tense, and deliberately slowed the motion, drawing out not a weapon but a leather folio, cheap, water-stained, bound with a shoelace. He set it on the stack of paper between them and stepped back from it, hands open and empty, the way a man backs away from a dog he hopes won't bite.

"The ledgers," he said.

* * *

He had copied them over four nights, working by candle after midnight, in a hand disguised as his own hand was not — a clerk's trick learned decades ago and never before put to honest use. The originals lived in the customs house records room, and Pell, with his forgettable face and his button stall and his little courtesies to the sentries, had access that no one thought to guard, because no one imagined the quill would turn on the hand that fed it.

"Open it," he said.

Jude opened it. And Pell watched the type-cutter read, and understood that he was watching the moment the whole rotten edifice of Aldous Verrek's career acquired its crack.

"The casualty returns," Pell said, while Jude's finger moved down the columns. "Every engagement since the occupation began. The official figures go to the provincial archive — that's what the historians will read when the bridge opens. Here." He leaned forward, pointing despite himself, the clerk's itch overriding everything. "Mill district, the eleventh of last month. Official return: nine dead, all armed resistance, all *identified militants*. Nine. Now — I was in the records room when the returns came in from the field, before the fair copies were made. The field report said twenty-six. Twenty-six dead, of whom at least eleven were never near the strike — they were names taken off a tax roll because they matched ages. And the wounded weren't recorded at all, because wounded men recover and can talk." He tapped the page. "Verrek's hand reduced it to nine. Same hand every time. Look at the sevens — see the crossbar, how it hooks left? That's his pen, not a clerk's. He does the reductions personally. He calls it *reconciliation*. There are thirty-one pages of reconciliations in that folio, covering eleven months. Snow over the square, the mill yard, the drownings at the weir that were reported as resistants when they were boys skating where they shouldn't have been. Every number that reached the capital is smaller than the number that happened, and every difference has his handwriting on it."

Jude had gone pale, but their voice, when it came, was steady. "Why forge them downward?"

"Because dead men make martyrs and numbers make policy," Pell said. "A massacre of twenty-six forces inquiry. Nine justifies itself. Verrek isn't hiding the killings — he's resizing them. Making them respectable. When the historians arrive, they'll find tidy books, proportionate books, books that say order was restored at acceptable cost. And the families in the mill district will stand at their windows knowing the true count, and no one outside will ever believe them over a signed ledger." He swallowed. "Except now they can. Because a ledger means signatures, dates, comparisons — and comparison is your trade, isn't it? Set his reconciled figure beside the field reports' witnesses beside the grave-counts the sexton keeps whether anyone asks him to or not. Put it in your pages. Let the town see the arithmetic. Numbers were his weapon; make numbers yours."

"Why?" Jude asked. It was almost a whisper. "Why now? Why risk it? If it's guilt, guilt doesn't need this folio. Guilt needs a rope and a quiet hour."

Pell considered the question honestly, because he had been considering it for four nights, inking copies with hands that would not stay steady, and he deserved to answer it honestly if he deserved anything.

"Because guilt is a debt," he said slowly, "and debts want paying in the right currency, and I finally worked out what mine is. Not suffering. Suffering is easy; the town's drowning in it. My currency is *correction*. I spent a year and a half making the record false — giving them truths to be converted into lies. So the only payment that weighs the same on the other side of the scale is making the record true. Not for Ansel Kroy — nothing pays him. Not for my soul — I've priced that at nothing and found the price fair. For the town. For whoever comes after, reading whatever gets read, when the ice breaks." He met Jude's eyes at last, and did not flinch away. "You won't forgive me. Good. Don't. Forgiveness would let me off the scale, and I need the scale. Use me instead. Use the folio. Use everything I know until the day the historians land, and after that day—" he shrugged, a small, tired motion, "—after that day do with me what the town thinks just. That's not humility. That's just accurate bookkeeping."

For a long moment Jude said nothing. Then they picked up the folio, weighed it in one hand — Pell saw them weigh it literally, the way they must weigh everything, lead and paper and truth alike — and set it inside the type case, sliding it beneath the shallow trays of sorted letters where no searcher would think to dig.

"Tomorrow night," Jude said. "There's a house behind the ropewalk. Knock twice, wait, knock once. Ask for Ostler. Tell her everything, in the order you told me, and if she puts a knife to your throat — and she may — you hold still and keep talking. That's the price of admission and I won't soften it."

"I'll hold still," Pell said. "It's the least I owe her."

He rose to go. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at the dim shop, the shrouded press, the young type-cutter already bending back over the case as if he had never existed — already, he realized, redistributing what he had given them into columns and columns of small perfect letters.

"One thing more," Pell said. "Verrek's clerks audit the requisition tallies weekly, and the week's audit falls due day after tomorrow. Paper, ink, lamp-oil — he knows your consumption better than you think. Feed him a false economy somewhere or he'll start wondering why your supplies run short." He managed something that was not quite a smile. "Forgive the advice. Old habits. I spent a year and a half learning how they think. It seems a shame to bury the knowledge with me."

The door opened on the falling snow, and closed without a sound.

Outside, Pell turned up Cooper's Lane toward the ropewalk with the cold biting his face and the strangest feeling in his chest — lighter and heavier at once, like a man who has set down a sack of stones and picked up a sack of iron and found, against all sense, that iron sits easier on the shoulders. He did not look back at the shuttered shop. He did not need to. Somewhere behind that brown paper, in lamplight, his confession was already being broken into pieces — *t*, *r*, *u*, *t*, *h* — sorted into cases, made ready to be set in lines and locked in a chase and pressed onto paper by the thousand, and for the first time in nineteen days Tobias Pell felt that something he had done might survive him in a shape worth surviving.

Behind him, high on the hill by the customs house, a bell began to ring the evening watch, and he counted the strokes out of habit — and stopped, mid-count, hearing what he had trained himself for eighteen months never to hear: the watch ringing *early*, and the pattern wrong, and boots in the snow coming down the lane behind him faster than any patrol walks.

Day after tomorrow, the audit. But it seemed Aldous Verrek had decided not to wait for arithmetic.
