## 16. Ice Groaning

Wren Corliss heard the river before anyone else did.

It began in the small hours, a sound like a great door groaning on rusted hinges somewhere beneath the town — a long, low exhalation that traveled up through cellar stone and floor joists and into the frames of beds, waking people who did not know why they had woken. By morning the sound had a name in every mouth: the ice was talking. And it was talking too soon.

Wren stood at the loading door behind the print shop, breath steaming, and watched the river with the particular attention of someone whose life had come to depend on reading it. For three months the Vell had lain locked, a road of grey-white plate stretching bank to bank, and Wren had grown used to its silence the way one grows used to a wall — as something permanent, something that held. Now the wall was breathing. Downstream, where the current bent against the pilings of the closed bridge, dark seams had opened in the ice like wounds, and through them the water showed black and fast and impatient.

"Early," said Maren Ostler beside her. The fishwife had come around the alley way with two baskets of sprats she did not need to deliver and did not pretend otherwise. "Three weeks early, by my grandmother's reckoning. Maybe four."

"Is that good or bad?"

Maren looked at her the way she always did when Wren asked a question that answered itself. "The Directorate says the historians come with the first barge. If the thaw is early—" She let it hang. Maren never finished a sentence that finished itself better.

Wren felt the arithmetic settle into her chest like a swallowed stone. The chronicle was nearly done — seventeen issues of the *Vellmouth Record*, printed in the flooded cellar under the sound of the river, distributed through fish baskets and flour sacks and the false bottoms of coffin lids. Seventeen issues documenting names, dates, massacres, the forged ledgers Pell had smuggled out in his final act of penance. But nearly was not done. There were plates still being cut, corrections still being pulled, and Jude had insisted — had *stood over* the type case insisting — on verifying every name against three sources before it went to metal.

Three weeks might have been enough. Three weeks had been the plan.

"The river doesn't negotiate," Wren said.

"No," Maren agreed. "But men do, sometimes, when they're frightened. Verrek's clerks will be packing their ledgers already. Men who expect a long winter pack slowly." She hefted her baskets. "Tell Ravensworth. Tell him the clock has started ticking where everyone can hear it."

* * *

Jude took the news standing at the composing stone, a stick of type half-set in their hands, and for a long moment they did not move at all. Wren had learned to read Jude's silences in the months underground — there was the silence of calculation, the silence of grief, and this one, rarer, which was the silence of a person re-drawing a map in their head and finding every road changed.

"Seventeen days," Jude said finally. "If we're lucky. Less if the warm rain comes up from the south like it did in the flood year."

"We could finish in ten. Pull the last plates in eight."

"It isn't finishing that worries me." Jude set the stick down with the exaggerated care of someone who wanted to throw it. "It's moving. Two hundred pounds of lead and copper across open water, on a river full of ice floes, past a bridge garrison that will be watching the banks the moment the first seam opens. We've had it easy, Wren. The river kept our secrets because nobody could leave and nobody could arrive. The day the barge comes, everything we are becomes evidence."

Wren thought of the cellar — the second press dripping dry in the lamplight, the stacks of issues wrapped in oilcloth, the plates racked like loaves in a baker's shelf. Their whole secret history, weighing less than a dead ox and worth more than the town.

"Then we move it before the thaw," Wren said. "Not after. Before. While the ice still holds."

Jude turned and looked at her, and Wren saw the idea land — saw it the way you see a form take impression on the sheet, ink to paper, sudden and whole.

"On foot," Jude said slowly. "Across the ice. At night. To the far bank — Ostler's cousin's barn, above the ferry landing."

"It's been holding carts all winter."

"It hasn't been *groaning* all winter." But Jude was already moving to the window, studying the river's course with a printer's eye, the eye that found the flaw in every forme. "There. Below the mill race, where the current runs fastest, the ice will be rotten first. But along the near shore to the willow barrow, then out—"

"You've walked it before."

"I fished it before I could write," Jude said. "I know where the ice lies thick and where it lies thin. What I don't know is how much of what I knew is still true." They looked back at Wren, and their face in the grey window light was very tired and very steady. "We'll need to test it. And we'll need to decide what goes and what stays. Everything can't cross."

That night the shop's lamp burned late, and through the floorboards Wren could hear Jude and their father arguing in low voices — not quarreling, but the particular argument of two people who loved each other and disagreed about sacrifice. She caught fragments. Her name once. The word *barn*. Then the father's voice, flat and final: *"You will not put my daughter on that river."*

And Jude's answer, quieter, so quiet Wren almost missed it: *"She put herself on it the day she helped me carry the press downstairs. We're all on the river, Da. Some of us just get to choose the boat."*

* * *

Two mornings later, the Directorate made the clock official.

A crier stood in the market square in a coat too heavy for the warming air and read the proclamation in a voice trained to carry: that by order of Captain Aldous Verrek, Provisional Commissioner of Public Information, a Commission of Historical Reconciliation would arrive in Vellmouth aboard the first vessel of the season; that all citizens possessing documents, correspondence, or printed matter bearing on the events of the occupation were invited — *invited*, the crier said, and the word sat in the square like a hook in bait — to present such materials to the Commission for fair inclusion in the official record; that failure to present seditious materials, once the record was compiled, would constitute an offense against the peace for which no pardon would be available.

No pardon. Once the record was compiled.

Wren stood in the crowd with a basket of washing she wasn't thinking about and listened to the town react around her. Most faces were carefully blank — Vellmouth had learned blankness the way it learned catechism. But here and there she caught the flickers: a mill hand glancing toward the fish stalls where the *Record* passed from basket to basket; an old woman mouthing the word *pardon* to herself as though tasting it; a boy, too young to be afraid properly, asking his mother loudly what the official record was, and his mother hushing him with a hand that shook.

They were afraid of the wrong thing, most of them. They feared punishment. What they should have feared — what Wren felt rising in her throat like cold water — was simpler and vaster: that it would *work*. That Verrek would sit with his clerks in some requisitioned hall and write the winter down as he wished it had happened, and the barges would carry that version out to the world, smooth and sealed and official, and thirty years from now schoolchildren in provinces whose names Wren barely knew would learn that order had been restored to Vellmouth with regrettable but necessary firmness, and no one would know different.

Except whoever held the plates.

The thought went through her like a nail: *that is the whole war. Not the bridge, not the garrison. Whose version crosses the water.*

At the fish stalls Maren was selling sprats with her usual monumental indifference, and Wren bought a paper twist of them she didn't want, and inside the twist, folded small as a moth, was a scrap of newsprint in familiar type. *Commission coming. All couriers stand down until after crossing. Nothing moves but what we choose to move.* Beneath it, in Maren's hand rather than the press's: *Keep your head. Keep your baskets clean.*

* * *

That evening Wren went down into the cellar, and the river spoke to her through the walls.

The flooded cellars had been the chronicle's cradle — the rising water table that had drowned the old press also muffled its rhythm under the groan of the Vell, and the Directorate's searchers, poking through the shop above, had heard nothing but a building settling over its own wet bones. Now the sound had changed. The groan had become conversation — long sighs, sharp cracks like distant pistol shots, a low grinding mutter that never quite stopped, the ice field shifting and straining against itself in the warmer nights. Standing in the lamplight among the racks of drying sheets, Wren felt the sound enter her body and set up a resonance in her ribs. It was not a background anymore. It was a countdown, spoken in a language older than any regime.

Jude was already there, sleeves rolled, pulling proofs of the eighteenth issue — the massacre at the mill gates, corrected twice, the father's sabotaged letters hidden in it like splinters of glass in bread. Wren watched them hold a proof to the lamp and knew from the set of their shoulders that something was wrong.

"We're short," Jude said without looking up. "Plates for the last issue — the ledgers, Pell's material. The comparison tables. They're not cut yet."

"How long?"

"Four days of cutting, if my father works nights. Six if he only works when the clerks aren't watching, which is how he must work now." Jude laid the proof down. "And then the crossing. Which means we cut it close by design, or we cross without the strongest evidence we have, or—" they stopped.

"Or your father finishes cutting and doesn't come with us," Wren said, because someone had to say it.

Jude's jaw tightened. In the lamplight their face looked older than it had in autumn, when history had still happened elsewhere. "He won't leave the shop. He says a printer doesn't abandon his case. He says—" A crack came up through the stone then, louder than any before it, close — perhaps directly below the waterline beneath them — and both of them went still until it faded. Jude exhaled. "He says the letters he mis-cut are his signature. That if the chronicle survives and the shop survives, his work survives in both. That a man's hands belong where they work." They picked up the proof again, studied it, set it in the rack with the others. "I think he's preparing to be arrested, Wren. I think he has been for weeks. And I think the plates matter more to him than he does, and I don't know how to live in a world where that's the arithmetic."

Wren didn't answer right away. She was nineteen and had come to this shop as an apprentice to learn a trade, and instead had learned what a trade cost. She thought about telling Jude something comforting and knew comfort wasn't hers to give.

"Then we buy him time," she said. "Cut faster. Cut in daylight, in plain sight — commemorative work, orders for the Commission. Nobody suspects the man setting welcome-banners for the historians." She heard her own voice take on an edge she hadn't known it carried. "And we test the ice tomorrow night. You and me. Because whether the plates are ready in four days or six, the crossing route has to be true before we bet everything on it."

Jude looked at her across the lamplit cellar, across the racks of truth wrapped in oilcloth, and something in their expression shifted — not gratitude exactly, but recognition, the look of one conspirator seeing another fully for the first time.

"All right," Jude said. "Tomorrow night. Dress warm, and wear wool under everything, because if you go in, the cold will take your fingers before the water takes the rest."

"That's very encouraging."

"I'm a printer," Jude said. "I deal in exact statements."

Above them, faintly, the ice groaned again — a long descending note, like a bell rung underwater, like the town clearing its throat before speaking. Wren blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark listening, learning the sound the way she'd once learned the feel of a well-cut letter under her thumb. Four days. Maybe six. The river would say when, and it had already begun saying.

Upstairs, through the floorboards, she could hear the father's brayer rolling across the stone — working late, working against the clock — and beneath that, softer, the sound of Jude climbing toward the light, and beneath everything, everywhere, patient and enormous and impossible to argue with, the voice of the breaking winter.
