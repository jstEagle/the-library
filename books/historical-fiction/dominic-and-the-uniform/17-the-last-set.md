## 17. The Last Set

The candles had burned down to their third row by the time Jude set the final line of the eighteenth issue. Three days they had been at it — three days of the cellar press groaning beneath them like something alive, of Wren Corliss's ink-blackened fingers, of Maren Ostler coming and going through the coal chute with bundles wrapped in oiled cloth, of their father upstairs keeping the Directorate's clerks amused with lectures on kerning and the virtues of Hungarian cast steel.

The chronicle had grown fat. What had begun as a single sheet of disappeared names was now sixteen pages: the massacre at the mill district, the execution in the square, Verrek's forged casualty ledgers reproduced in facsimile from Pell's stolen papers, an essay on justice written in the careful, deliberate language Jude and Wren had agreed upon after their quarrel over informants' names. *We do not print accusations we cannot prove*, the essay said. *We print what we have seen. We print what was done. Let the reader judge.*

Jude lifted the last composed galley to the stone, checked it against the manuscript one letter at a time, and found it clean. Eighteen issues. A hundred and forty copies of each, distributed across Vellmouth and smuggled out through Maren's cousins to two towns downriver. The plates for all of it lay wrapped in waxed linen in a waterproof crate Wren had caulked himself, waiting for the river.

Waiting for the ice to break.

And the ice was breaking early this year. Everyone could feel it — a restlessness under the town's floorboards, a sound at night like distant cannon, though nothing fired. The river spoke in its sleep. Two more days, the fishwives said. Perhaps three.

"Last page," Jude said aloud, and their voice came out cracked from disuse. "Last page of the last set."

Wren looked up from the press, hair matted flat with sweat, face orange in the candlelight, and grinned the wild grin of someone who has not slept enough to be afraid anymore. "Then we pull it, and we're done."

They pulled it together — Jude working the tympan, Wren the bar — and the eighteenth issue came off the press damp and soft and true. Jude held it up. The type sat square on the page. Their father would have found fault; their father found fault with everything; but privately, silently, Jude thought it was the finest thing that had ever come off that press, or any press, in the history of paper.

That was when the shouting started upstairs.

It came muffled through the floorboards — the shop above, the front room where the Directorate's clerks worked over requisitioned output, checking every jobbing form against Captain Verrek's approval stamps. Shouting first, then running feet, then a crash of metal that Jude knew instantly and intimately: a type case overturned, a font scattered across the boards.

Wren froze with the wet sheet still in hand. "The governor—"

"No." Jude was already moving, snuffing candles down the cellar stairs with their fingers, hot wax stinging skin they didn't notice. "Stay here. Keep the press cold. Don't come up no matter what you hear."

The cellar door opened onto the back stair, and the back stair opened onto the composing room, and the composing room was full of light — too much light, lanterns everywhere, clerks swarming like beetles over the upended case, and in the middle of it all, kneeling on the floorboards with his wrists already bound before him, sat Josiah Ravensworth.

He was not struggling. That was the terrible thing, the thing Jude would carry for the rest of their life: he was kneeling calmly among his scattered letters, looking down at them as if counting them, as if taking inventory of his own ruin. One clerk crouched beside him holding up a compositor's stick with a line of type locked in it, presenting it to another clerk the way a hunter presents a bird.

"Here," the clerk was saying. "Look at the *r*. And the *n*. Look at all of them."

Jude stopped at the foot of the stair, in shadow, and made themselves look.

The line of type read: *BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL DIRECTORATE, ORDER IS RESTORED.* It was the commemorative plate, the one Father had been cutting these six weeks under the clerks' supervision — the plate for the grand broadsheet that would greet the historians when they arrived, celebrating the Directorate's pacification of Vellmouth. Jude had seen proofs of it hanging in the shop, had read them with a sour stomach, had never once looked closely.

Now they looked closely, and saw what the clerk saw.

Every *r* in the line was mis-cut. Not broken, not worn — deliberately, cunningly flawed, the shoulder shaved so that at reading distance the letters printed slightly wrong, slightly weak, so that *ORDER* became something diminished, something apologetic. And the *n*s — once you saw one you saw them all — leaned almost imperceptibly, so that whole lines of the composition carried a faint downward slant toward the right margin, the typographic equivalent of a bowed head. A whole alphabet of small sabotages, invisible until ink met paper, and then unmistakable: the entire commemorative broadsheet, printed by the thousand for the victors' ceremony, would have declared their restored order in letters that drooped, that faltered, that mourned.

Father had been setting his confession into their triumph for six weeks. He had known they would hang it in the square. He had known the historians would paste it in their ledgers. And he had made certain that forever after, whenever anyone looked at the official record, some part of them — below argument, below notice — would see it slumping, grieving, ashamed.

"He knew," the senior clerk breathed. He turned the stick in the light. "Gods above, he *planned* it. Every character. How long has he—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. They were all of them counting backward through six weeks of proofs, through every document the old man had touched.

Josiah Ravensworth raised his head then, and found Jude in the doorway of the stair.

His face did not change. That was the discipline of forty years at the case: a face that gave away nothing, because a printer's face must never flinch at bad copy. But his eyes went to Jude, and then — slowly, deliberately, so slowly it could not be accident — they went past Jude, toward the cellar door, toward the back of the shop, and then back to Jude's face, and he shook his head. Once. A single small movement, like a man refusing a offered tool.

*No. Don't. Go.*

"Take him up," the senior clerk said. "Verrek will want him at the customs house. And seal this shop — nothing leaves, nothing burns. If there are more of these letters, we'll find them." He looked around the composing room, at the cases, the presses, the stone, and Jude saw his gaze travel with professional thoroughness toward the cellar door, and Jude's heart stopped like a dropped forme.

"The cellar's flooded," Jude heard themself say. Their voice sounded far away, borrowed, someone else's. "Half the stock's ruined. Been seeping since solstice."

The clerk glanced at them — a long glance, the glance of a man filing a face away — and then, blessedly, the commotion at the door took him: Captain Aldous Verrek had arrived.

He came in without hurry, as he always did, buttoned into a grey coat too fine for the mud of Vellmouth, and stood a moment surveying the scene with the air of a man admiring a garden he had planted. Then he crossed to Father and crouched before him, and the two old adversaries regarded one another at close range.

"Well," Verrek said softly. "The craftsman." He took the compositor's stick from the clerk and turned it, studying the mutilated letters with what appeared to be genuine appreciation. "Do you know, I showed your work to a man from the capital last month. I told him: this is the best matrix-cutter between here and the mountains. He agreed. He said you could set anything, make any words believeable." He tilted his head. "I wonder now if you've been doing it all along."

Father said nothing. He knelt among his letters with his bound hands in his lap and looked at Verrek with the mild patience of a man waiting out weather.

"You understand what happens now," Verrek went on, rising, brushing his knees. "Sabotage of state printing. In wartime. Under my jurisdiction." He said it without heat, without cruelty, which was somehow worse than either; he might have been reading an invoice. "But I am a reasonable man, Mr. Ravensworth. Names. The people who've been putting sedition on this town's doorsteps — you must have seen something from behind that counter. Give me names, and you'll cut type again. Real type. Honest type."

For the first time, Father spoke. His voice was dry and even, the voice of the trade.

"A letter is a letter," he said. "It says what it says. Ask it nothing else."

Verrek studied him a moment longer. Something moved behind his eyes — disappointment, perhaps, or the pleasure of a man whose suspicion has been confirmed. Then he turned away. "Customs house," he said. "Alone cell. And post a man on this shop. Nobody enters." His gaze crossed the room and rested, briefly, lightly, on Jude — on the ink stains that three days at a secret press had ground into every crease of their hands — and he smiled the courteous smile Jude had learned to dread more than any threat.

"Master Ravensworth. My condolences. Your father was an artist."

They took him out through the front, into the snow, and the bell of his arrest — there was always a bell now, for arrests — rang once across the frozen square.

* * *

Jude did not remember crossing the composing room. They remembered the cellar door closing behind them, the dark, the smell of river water and ink and lamp oil, and Wren's hands catching their shoulders.

"They took him," Jude said. "They took him and I stood on the stairs and did nothing."

"What happened? Jude — *what happened?*"

So Jude told it: the letters, the sabotage, the arrest, Verrek's offer, Father's answer. Wren listened with his mouth open, and when Jude described the leaning *n*s and the shaven shoulders of the *r*s, the boy let out a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob.

"The old fox," he whispered. "Six weeks. Right under their noses. Every proclamation they print from now on will hang its head."

"And it's bought him a cell in the customs house," Jude said savagely. "And probably worse, when Verrek tires of asking nicely. He wouldn't give names. He wouldn't give *me*." They stopped. Their voice had climbed somewhere high and strange, and they hauled it down. "He shook his head at me, Wren. On the stairs. He told me not to follow."

"So don't follow. Not yet." Wren seized their arms. "Listen to me. Listen. The ice breaks in two days. Maybe less. The historians arrive on the first barge, and everything we've got — the plates, the archive copies, the ledger facsimiles — goes across that river or gets burned with this shop. That crate cannot move yet. The river isn't open. You put a boat on that water tonight and the ice takes you inside of fifty yards."

"Then I'll go to the customs house. Tonight. Maren knows the guards' rotation, she—"

"With what? With your hands? There are eight men on that building and a manacle on his wrists and Verrek sitting upstairs with his boots on the table *waiting* for exactly this. You walk in there tonight and you don't rescue anyone — you hand him the cellar press gift-wrapped. You hand him the whole chronicle. Is that what he shook his head to tell you? Is that what forty years of cutting straight letters bought, so his child could throw it away in one night?"

Jude struck his hands away and turned to the wall and pressed their forehead against the cold stone, and for a long time neither of them spoke. Below them the river muttered and shifted. Somewhere in the walls the ice groaned, long and low, like a great animal turning in its sleep.

"I can hear him kneeling there," Jude said at last, quietly. "I'll hear it every night for the rest of my life. Him on his knees, and me on the stairs."

"Yes," Wren said simply. "You will. And if you go tonight, you'll hear something worse. Him watching from the cell window while they drag you in beside him." He came and stood at Jude's shoulder. "Maren will get word to him. She has a cousin on the customs house kitchen detail, there's bread passed through the bars twice a day — she'll find a way to tell him you're safe, that the work is safe. That's something. That's real. But the plates, Jude. The plates are the only thing in this world that can't be imprisoned."

Jude stood a moment longer, forehead to the stone. Then they pushed off the wall, wiped their face once, hard, with the heel of their hand, and turned to the crate.

"Then we pack," they said. "Everything. Tonight, while the guard thinks we're sealed out. We wrap every plate, every copy of every issue, the manuscripts, Pell's ledger pages. We stage it at the coal chute. The moment that ice gives—" They swallowed. "The moment it gives, I'm on the water. And you're not coming with me."

Wren blinked. "Like hell I'm not."

"You're the decoy." Jude met his eyes, and hated what they saw dawning there, and went on anyway, because their father had taught them that a compositor sets the line that must be set, however much it costs. "If I'm caught on the river, Verrek comes back here for the press — and he has to find something burning. You run her hot, Wren. Print the nineteenth issue tonight and keep her printing till the smoke shows. Make him believe the heart of it is here. He'll come for the press." Their voice roughened. "You hold the door just long enough. Then you run for the fish market and you don't stop."

"That's a firing squad you're describing for me."

"It's a chance, which is more than he's got." Jude nodded upward, toward the cells beyond the square. "He shook his head so I'd choose the record. So I'd choose it over him. I'm choosing it. But I won't spend you the same way unless you choose it too — freely, Wren Corliss, with your eyes open."

Wren looked at the crate a long moment. Then at the press, his press, the drowned thing they had raised together out of the floodwater. When he turned back his jaw was set in a way Jude had never seen on him before — the boy gone, someone older standing in his place.

"Freely," he said. "Now hand me the wax. We've got packing to do, and two days of dark left, and I intend to make that man Verrek very, very angry."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the tread of the posted sentry, pacing the length of the sealed shop. And beyond the walls, under the keels of the sleeping town, the river spoke again — louder now, closer, the ice lifting and cracking along its seams like a lock being slowly, patiently, picked.
