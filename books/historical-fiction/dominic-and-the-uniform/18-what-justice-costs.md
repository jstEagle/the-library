## 18. What Justice Costs

Maren Ostler had gutted a thousand fish in the dark before dawn, and she knew the particular quality of light that came just before catastrophe. It was the same grey as the river ice in its last days — thin, uncertain, promising everything and holding nothing. She stood at her stall in the emptying market and watched the customs house across the square, counting windows, counting soldiers, counting the hours until it would begin.

The plan had been settled three nights ago in the back room of the cooper's shop, where the barrels muffled their voices and the smell of oak shavings covered the smell of fear. Jude Ravensworth had laid out the plates on the table — wrapped in oilcloth now, all twenty-two issues of the chronicle, every page of names and dates and testimony that the Directorate would burn if it ever found them — and Maren had watched the young type-cutter's hands tremble and steady themselves, tremble and steady, like a candle flame deciding.

"The thaw is early," Jude had said. "Verrek's historians come on the first barge. We move the plates that night, whatever else happens."

"Whatever else happens," Wren Corliss had repeated, and there had been something in the apprentice's voice that Maren recognized from funerals: the sound of someone agreeing to a thing they could not yet look at directly.

Because they all knew it was not enough to row quietly across a river. The bank would be watched. Verrek knew the thaw was coming too; he had doubled the patrols along the waterfront, and his men stopped anyone carrying anything heavier than a loaf of bread. The chronicle would have to be carried through a wall of rifles, and rifles could not be reasoned with. They could only be turned.

So Maren had said the thing she had been turning over for two days, saying it plainly, because she had learned long ago that plain speech was the only kind worth having.

"You need a fire," she said.

They had all looked at her. The cooper's stove ticked in the corner. Somewhere out in the yard a dog barked twice and gave up.

"A fire draws men the way blood draws gulls. Every patrol on the waterfront will run toward it, because Verrek has taught them that fire means sedition and sedition means promotion. You put a fire in the right place at the right hour, and the riverbank is bare for twenty minutes." She held Jude's eyes. "Twenty minutes is enough. I've crossed worse ice with heavier baskets."

"And after?" Wren asked quietly. "After they run toward it?"

"After," Maren said, "they find it was the customs house."

* * *

It had not been difficult to arrange, which was itself a kind of sorrow. The customs house stood at the head of the quay, a fat stone building where every crate that entered or left Vellmouth was weighed, stamped, taxed, and recorded — including, these last months, the crates of confiscated books that Verrek burned in the square. Its cellar held the ledgers. Not Verrek's forged casualty books, those traveled in his own coat pocket, but the ordinary honest ledgers of forty years of trade: who owed what, who shipped what, who had paid duty on the flour that never reached the mill district during the hunger winter. Records of debts. Records of thefts committed by men who were now called officials.

Burning them was justice, Maren told herself, though she was honest enough to know it was also strategy, and that the two had gotten tangled together somewhere and could no longer be pulled apart.

She had spent her life being invisible in plain sight. A fishwife of fifty-three years, broad-shouldered and loud-voiced, with hands scarred by knives and salt — soldiers did not search women like her, did not follow them, did not trouble to remember their faces. She could stand ten feet from Captain Aldous Verrek himself and he would see only a woman selling herring. That invisibility was worth more than any weapon in Vellmouth, and she had spent it carefully, night after night, walking the route from the cooper's yard to the customs house cellar until she could do it blind.

The oil she had gathered honestly, in the way that everything dishonest must be built from honest parts. Lamp oil, whale oil, a jar of spirits bought in three separate purchases from three separate merchants who each assumed she was stocking up against the cold. She had packed it into two firkins that had once held salt herring, and the smell of the brine-soaked wood covered the reek of the spirits well enough that even the cooper's dog had not given her a second glance.

There was one more thing, and this was the thing she could not say aloud in the cooper's back room, the thing that sat in her chest now like a swallowed stone.

Pell.

* * *

He had come to her at dusk, two evenings past, standing in the doorway of her cottage dripping snowmelt on her floor, looking like a man who had walked a very long way to reach a very short decision. Denny Pell, the informant, the man whose words had cost the network the Mill Lane safehouse and cost Tam Bricker his life under interrogation. The man the chronicle had refused to name, choosing procedures over vengeance, and Maren had disagreed with that choice at the time and kept her silence about disagreeing.

"I heard what you're doing," Pell said. "The fire."

Maren set down the knife she'd been cleaning and did not answer. In her kitchen, silence was a question, and most people answered it without knowing they'd been asked.

"They'll be looking for runners that night. Anyone moving toward the water. They'll sweep the lanes behind the square while everyone's watching the flames." His hands worked at each other, twisting. "I can give them somebody to find. Somebody who looks like he's running. They'll take me, and they'll stop looking, because a captured courier is better than an empty street. Verrek likes to close a case. He'll want to believe the fire had a courier, and I can be him."

"No," Maren said.

"You owe me nothing—"

"I owe you less than nothing, Denny Pell. Tam Bricker is dead and his girl doesn't know why, and you're the reason." She stood, and her shadow filled the little kitchen. "So don't come to me talking about giving. You're not giving anything. You're paying, and the payment's late."

He took it. She watched him take it, watched him absorb the blow without flinching, and understood that he had already flinched at everything a man could flinch at and had arrived, finally, at the far side of fear. That was what frightened her.

"There's a prisoner," Pell said. "In the guardhouse cellar. A boy from the mill, sixteen, taken last week. They questioned him about the press. He doesn't know anything — I know he doesn't, I've seen the lists — but they'll keep questioning until they decide he does." His voice cracked, and he hated it cracking, she could see that. "I did what I did for money, and then I did more of it because I was afraid of what happened to informants who stopped. There's no coming out of that except through. So let me come out of it your way. Let the patrols catch me running from the fire. While they're busy with me, Jude rows. And maybe—" he stopped, started again. "Maybe while everyone's chasing smoke, nobody remembers the boy in the cellar for a night. Maybe that's his night."

Maren looked at him for a long time. The lamp guttered between them. Outside, the river spoke in its new loosened voice, the ice groaning and shifting, the winter beginning to give up its grip.

"If you're taken," she said slowly, "they won't question you gently. And they won't question you briefly. You know what Verrek does with—"

"I sat outside the door while they did it to Tam Bricker," Pell said. "I listened. I know exactly what they do." He met her eyes then, and there was nothing soft in his face, nothing asking for comfort. Only arithmetic. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to use me. That's all I'm good for now, and it happens to be the thing you need."

Justice, Maren thought, was a word the priests used. What happened in the world was smaller and harder than that: people trading what they had for what was needed, and the ledger never quite balancing, and someone always left holding the difference. Tam Bricker was gone and no fire, no sacrifice, no redemption would bring him back to his girl. But the boy in the cellar might live. The chronicle might cross the river. Those were real things, weighable things, and she had weighed worse at her stall every morning of her life.

"Dawn after the fire," she said. "If you're not taken, you go to the tannery lane and knock twice and tell them Ostler sent you, and you spend the rest of your life being useful somewhere far from here." She picked up her knife and went back to the fish, because looking at him any longer would have undone something in her she needed whole. "And Denny. If they break you and you give them names—"

"I won't."

"Everyone says so," Maren said. "Everyone believes it, right up until the irons."

But he shook his head, and she believed him anyway, or chose to, which in that winter of Vellmouth amounted to the same faith.

* * *

The night of the fire came down cold and clear, stars hard as nailheads, the river muttering under its dissolving lid. Maren stood at her stall in the dark market square with her shawl pulled tight, and around her the town slept its last unbroken sleep, not knowing — none of them knowing — that by morning the truth of everything would be floating downriver in an oilcloth bundle.

Wren Corliss passed her stall with a lantern, an apprentice out on a fool's errand, and their eyes met for half a second. Down at the boathouse, Jude was loading the plates. Everything waited on the fire.

At the agreed hour — the third bell of the night watch, when the patrols changed and the new men were slow and cold — Maren went alone through the alley behind the fish market, pulling the handcart with two firkins on it, and let herself into the customs house cellar through the coal chute she had memorized. Her heart beat slow and enormous, each stroke distinct, the way it did when she lifted a net heavy with the day's catch. She worked quickly. Oil soaked into the ledgers' shelves, into the floorboards, into the great timber posts that held up the weighing hall. She laid the trail wide and generous. If it was going to burn, let it burn thoroughly; half-measures killed more people than excess ever had.

At the top of the stairs, before she struck the match, she stood a moment in the dark and thought of her mother, who had sold fish on this same quay and died owing the customs house eleven crowns she could never pay. She thought of Tam Bricker. She thought of the mill district's missing, whose names Jude had printed in the first issue in letters small enough to hide in a hem, and how those names had traveled through her hands to a hundred kitchens, and how the town had begun, week by week, to believe its own memory again instead of the Directorate's broadsheets.

Then she struck the match, and the dark bloomed orange, and she walked out the front door at a widow's unhurried pace and did not look back.

* * *

By the time she reached the square, the customs house was roaring like a hearth the size of a cathedral, and the bells were screaming, and the patrols were running exactly as she had promised they would run. Soldiers poured toward the quay from every direction, bucket lines forming and breaking, officers shouting over the crack of timber. Nobody looked at a stout old fishwife standing at the edge of the crowd with her basket, selling nothing, watching.

It was beautiful, the fire. She hated that it was beautiful. The flames stood up out of the roof in a golden column and the sparks went sideways on the wind toward the river, and the heat reached her face from thirty yards, and the whole town had come out of its houses to stand in the light like moths, faces upturned, mouths open. Even the people who hated the Directorate could not entirely hate this. Fire was older than any regime.

Then, at the edge of her vision, the second part began.

A figure broke from the lane behind the cooper's shop and ran along the waterfront — ran badly, clumsily, a satchel bouncing on his shoulder, the very picture of a courier fleeing the scene of sabotage. Denny Pell, playing his part with the desperate thoroughness of a man performing his own funeral. He ran straight into the arms of the first patrol, and there was a shout, and a scuffle she could see was mostly theater, and then he was down and they were on him, four soldiers pinning a man who had stopped resisting before they touched him.

"Courier!" someone shouted. "We've got the courier!"

And the crowd murmured, and the officers converged, and — Maren saw it happen with her own disbelieving eyes — the entire machinery of the night's pursuit seized onto Pell and locked shut. A sergeant bellowed for him to be taken to the guardhouse. An officer demanded the satchel. Two men peeled off toward the tanner's lane where the runner had come from, but the rest, the many rest, dragged their prisoner away through the snow, and the riverbank, for the space of that golden quarter hour, stood nearly empty.

Somewhere out on the black water, if all had gone as planned, a small boat was crossing.

Maren turned and walked home through the crowd, one more shape among hundreds, and let herself into her cottage, and sat down on her bench, and discovered that her hands would not stop shaking. She held them together in her lap and watched them shake and thought about Denny Pell in the guardhouse cellar, in the same cellar where the mill boy waited, and what the morning would bring there, and whether the boy would use his borrowed night to be moved or forgotten or freed — Wren had plans for that, whispered plans she hadn't fully heard — and whether Pell would still be recognizable by noon.

She had told him everyone says they won't talk. She had not stayed to watch them take him away properly, because some things a network cannot afford to witness, and because she was a coward in exactly one respect and had earned the right to choose where.

Outside, the fire began its long decline, and the crowd thinned, and the cold crept back in. Maren boiled water for tea she did not drink and listened to the river. The ice was speaking constantly now, groans and cracks traveling up through the ground like distant artillery, the winter's spine breaking joint by joint. Tomorrow, or the day after, the channel would open.

And near dawn — she would learn it later, from Wren, breathless and salt-soaked and unable to speak above a whisper — the boat had made the far bank. All the plates. All twenty-two issues. Jude Ravensworth had rowed through the gap in the patrols while the town watched the flames, and had landed on the south shore, and had vanished into the willows with the whole truth of Vellmouth bundled on their back.

But Wren brought another thing too, delivered low and fast in the fish market among the herring-barrels, with eyes red-rimmed and jaw set hard: word from the guardhouse steps. The mill boy had been moved in the night, alive, to the workhouse infirmary — forgotten, at least for one crucial night, exactly as Pell had gambled. And Captain Verrek, summoned from his bed by the fire, had stood before the burning customs house and then before the captured courier, and had looked at Denny Pell for a long, silent moment.

"He knows him," Wren said. "Verrek knows his own informant. Everyone saw him recognize the face."

And then Verrek had smiled, Wren said — a smile with no warmth in it, a bookkeeper's smile — and said, loudly enough for the whole patrol to hear, that the saboteur would be questioned thoroughly, and that his accomplices would follow him to the scaffold by week's end, starting with whoever owned the print shop on Tanner's Row.

Maren set down her cup. Through the window, far off, she could see the smoke still rising from the customs house, a grey feather against the brightening sky — and beyond it, the bridge, closed all winter, its timbers rimmed with rotting ice, waiting for the thaw that would bring the historians, the barges, the end of everything.

By sundown, she knew, they would come for the Ravensworths.
