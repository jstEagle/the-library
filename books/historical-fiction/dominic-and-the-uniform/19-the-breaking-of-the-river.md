## 19. The Breaking of the River

The ice spoke first.

Jude heard it before dawn, a sound like the earth clearing its throat — a long, groaning exhalation that ran beneath the floorboards of the shop and up through the soles of their boots and settled in the roots of their teeth. They had been awake for two days. The chronicle lay finished on the stone: nineteen issues, the complete run, plus the final gathering — the one they had set through the night, the names of the mill district entered in columns as careful as scripture, the account of the square, the account of the ledgers Pell had carried to them like a man carrying his own heart in a sack. Two hundred printed copies, sewn and wrapped in oilcloth. And beside them, wrapped separately, greased and bound in sacking, the plates. Nineteen plates of lead and antimony, each one a page, each one the truth pressed into metal.

The ice was breaking early. Three weeks early, perhaps more. All winter Jude had raced the thaw, and now the thaw had come like a thief over the wall, and with it, on the first barge, the historians.

*The record must survive you*, Maren had said, weeks ago, in the steam of the fish market. *It doesn't have to outlive you by long.*

Jude touched the oilcloth bundles once, then took their hands away. There was no time for reverence. Reverence was for afterward, if there was an afterward.

Down in the cellar, the river had come up under the stones. It did that every spring, flooding the lowest chamber where the second press still stood — the press they had raised from black water in the first weeks, the press that had printed everything, every issue, every name. Now it stood ankle-deep again, as if the river wanted it back, and Wren Corliss was standing beside it with a candle in one hand and ink on both, feeding the last sheets through by feel because there was no time to dry the floor.

"It's going," Wren said, without turning around. Jude knew they meant the ice, but for a moment the words went through them sideways — *it's going*, everything is going, the shop, the town, the winter, all of it going — and then they understood and stepped down into the water, which was cold enough to ache through leather.

"How long?"

"An hour. Maybe less. Listen." Wren held up the candle and they both listened, and beneath them the river made a sound Jude had never heard it make in twenty-two winters: not the mutter under the ice nor the crack of frost, but a deep, muscular shifting, the sound of something enormous rolling over in its sleep. "When it lets go, it'll let go all at once. You want to be on the water before that, or after it's done. Not during."

"Not during," Jude repeated. It seemed like good advice for a life in general.

They had argued about this for exactly ten minutes the night before, and then stopped arguing, because there was nothing to argue. Someone had to be on the river with the plates, and someone had to keep the press running below until the soldiers came, and only one of those tasks could be survived by the weaker rower, and Wren had never lost a bet on the water in their life while Jude had nearly drowned twice before the age of fifteen. That was the whole of the argument. It had ended with Wren grinning through exhaustion and saying, "Besides, they'll never look for a press in a flooded cellar. And if they do, I'll be very surprised-looking."

Now, in the grey before light, Jude looked at Wren and could not find anything to say that would not break something open, so instead they said, "Maren's people will pull you out the customs-stairs way. Pell's diversion won't hold longer than an hour. Don't be a hero past the hour."

"Past the hour I'm a coward, I promise." Wren blew out the candle, and in the sudden dark the sound of the river filled the room like a congregation rising. "Go on. The tide doesn't wait and neither does Verrek."

Jude climbed the stairs with one bundle under each arm — the plates heavy against their ribs like a second heartbeat — and paused at the top, and looked back. Wren stood in the doorway below, half-lit by the brazier, sleeves rolled, ink to the elbows, small and stubborn as a nail. Jude thought of their father's hands on the same press thirty years ago; thought of him now in the Directorate's custody, his sabotaged letters discovered, his face when the clerks had come for him — not afraid, Jude realized suddenly, remembering it truly for the first time. Not afraid. *Signaling.* He had looked at Jude across the square and moved two fingers, twice, toward the river. Run. He had known even then what this morning would cost, and he had paid it with his eyes open, the way he did everything, precisely, without waste.

"I'll come back for you," Jude said down into the dark.

"Don't come back," Wren said. "Send the whole world instead."

* * *

The boat was where Maren had hidden it three nights before, turned and wedged under the pilings of the old fish-stage downstream of the bridge, a flat-bottomed skiff that had fished this river since before either of them was born. Jude dragged it to the water's edge through snow gone soft and rotten, and loaded the bundles amidships, lashed down, weighted low, and stood a moment looking at the river.

It did not look like a river anymore. It looked like a battlefield before battle. The ice that had sealed Vellmouth since the bridge closed now reached bank to bank in shattered slabs, grinding, heaving, ridged up in places like plowed ground, open leads of black water appearing and closing between them with a speed that made the eye doubt itself. Downstream, against the piers of the bridge, the ice had piled into a rampart higher than a man, and beyond it the main channel ran free and furious, brown with snow-melt, carrying whole trees. The air was full of noise — the boom and grind of floes colliding, the hiss of water where it should not be, the creak of the bridge itself, which had stood a hundred winters and sounded this morning like it doubted it would stand this one.

And on the far bank, small in the grey distance, smoke rose from the Directorate's picket fires.

Jude pushed off.

The first hundred yards were the worst of their life. The near shore's ice was broken into pans the size of tables, and the skiff plunged among them, each collision jolting up through the hull and into Jude's spine; they poled and paddled and pushed off slabs with their bare hands until their fingers stopped reporting anything at all. Spray froze on the gunwales. Twice a pan swung toward them with the weight of a house and Jude dug the pole in and shoved and the boat spun away along its edge with a hand's width to spare, and the river did not notice, and carried on.

In the middle of the channel, where the current ran strongest, the water opened entirely and the skiff leapt forward as though it had been loosed from a sling, and Jude understood with a cold clarity that had nothing frantic in it that they were now committed. There was no rowing back against this. There was only across.

That was when the shooting started.

It began upstream — short, disciplined bursts, then ragged answering fire from the town side. Pell, Jude thought. The customs house. Maren's fire, or the beginning of it — a thread of dark smoke was climbing above the rooftops there, and beneath it, tiny with distance, figures were running along the quay. The diversion had begun, which meant the hour had begun, which meant Wren was downstairs right now feeding sheets into the press with the door unbolted, being found.

Jude pulled. Their shoulders were beyond pain; pain required some part of them not already spent. The plates shifted in their wrapping and knocked together with a sound like dull bells, and Jude found themselves speaking aloud to them, to the lead, to the names cut into it — *hold together, hold together* — the way their father spoke to a line of type that was fighting him, and the absurdity of it, talking to metal on a drowning river, made them laugh once, a bark of breath, and then the far bank woke up.

A voice, thin across the water. Then a shot, wild, slapping the river a boat-length short. Then another burst — closer, walking — and Jude flattened against the bundles and rowed one-armed and the bullets came in that terrible irregular rhythm, *crack — slap — crack*, the water jumping white around them, and a slug hit the gunwale with a sound like a knuckle rapped on wood and splinters stung Jude's cheek and they kept pulling, because there was nothing else in the world left to do but pull.

They counted strokes. It was their father's trick for any labor too large to comprehend: don't lift the form, just distribute the next line. One stroke. Two. The cold had passed through pain into a burning absence. Ten strokes. Twenty. A round struck one of the plate-bundles and Jude's whole body clenched around the sound — and the wrapping held, the sacking took the blow, and inside, the type sat solid as a headstone, because their father had packed it, and their father's packing did not fail. Fifty strokes. The current took them sideways and Jude leaned into it, hauling the bow around, and the far shore's reeds came up out of the fog like the teeth of a comb, and then the keel ground into gravel and Jude was out, waist-deep, hauling the boat up the bank alone, shaking so hard the bundles rattled.

Behind them, across the roaring channel, Vellmouth burned in three places and rang with gunfire, and on the quay, at the head of the bridge stairs, stood a figure in a grey officer's coat who had not been diverted by any fire at the customs house, who had come to the river the moment it broke because he too had been racing the thaw, and who was now looking directly across the water at Jude.

Captain Aldous Verrek raised a hand, and the firing stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than the noise. The river roared on, indifferent, and under its roar Jude could hear — or imagine, and would imagine for the rest of their life — the press below the shop, still running, Wren still feeding it, holding the decoy, holding the hour.

Verrek stepped to the edge of the quay. The ice rampart against the bridge piers had collapsed in the night; the channel at the bridge ran open and impassable, a mile of moving water between the town and the far bank, and Jude watched him take that in, watched him understand that he was watching the record leave, every plate, every copy, the true account of everything he had done here — and watched him do the thing that chilled Jude more than any gunshot.

He smiled. He inclined his head. Across a quarter mile of breaking river, Captain Aldous Verrek bowed to Jude Ravensworth as though acknowledging a fellow craftsman — as though to say: well played; we shall meet in the pages yet to come — and then turned and gave his men new orders, and the orders pointed back into the town, toward the shop, toward the cellar, and Jude screamed across the water, words that the river took apart before they got halfway, and stumbled back to the boat.

No. The plates came first. That was the bargain; that had always been the bargain; their father's two fingers, twice, toward the river. But their hands were already pushing the skiff back into the current anyway, already betraying the bargain, when the river itself decided the matter — for downstream, with a boom that Jude felt in the chest before hearing in the ears, the great ice field that had jammed above the bridge let go all at once, and the whole winter came down the channel at once, a moving country of grinding floes, and no boat that ever was built could have crossed that.

The water threw Jude back on the gravel. The skiff slewed, shipped half its load, and grounded again, and the crossing was over, and there was nothing left in the world but to kneel in the shallows with the plates safe above the waterline and watch the town through the freezing spray.

The gunfire from the customs house stopped. From the direction of the print shop, it began — a few shots, then none. Smoke rose thin and straight from the cellar gratings, as if the press itself had caught, though Jude knew better; Wren had planned that too, the oil-soaked rags, the theatre of it, buying the extra minutes that bought nothing now, that bought only time for the last copies to go out through the fishwives' hands into cellars and haylofts and boat-holds across the town.

Jude knelt in the shallows of the enemy's bank, soaked, bleeding from the cheek, hands ruined, and laughed and sobbed in the same breath, because the plates were safe. Because it had worked. Because it had cost exactly what they had all known it would cost, and no one had flinched from the price, and the price was still being paid behind them in a burning town by people who loved them, and somewhere in it, in a cellar filling with the river, Wren Corliss was smiling that stubborn nail of a smile and promising not to be a hero past the hour.

"Hour's up," Jude said to the river. "Come on, Corliss. Be a coward now."

But the river only carried on, hauling its ice toward the sea, and out on the water, riding down the open channel with the ease of a man whose arrangements always held, came the answer to everything: a barge, flying the Directorate's colours, riding high and unburdened — and Jude understood, with a lurch like the ice letting go, that they were watching the historians arrive. The victors' record-keepers, come up the newly opened river to write Vellmouth's story in the Directorate's hand. Coming ashore, in a few minutes' time, to find the shop emptied, the press drowned or burning, and the man in the grey coat waiting on the quay to greet them.

Unless the chronicle reached the world first.

Jude dragged the skiff above the flood line, shouldered the plates — heavier now, so much heavier, as if the names inside had gained weight overnight — and turned to face the marsh road, and the long miles, and whatever lay at the end of them. Behind them the bridge of Vellmouth stood open for the first time in a season, and the first men of the new history were stepping onto its stones.
