## 22. The Second Proof

The shop smelled different now. Jude noticed it every morning when the key turned in the lock — a smell like a house after a fever has broken, the air scrubbed and strange, familiar furniture standing in unfamiliar light. For eleven years the print shop of Aldous Ravensworth had carried the scent of tallow candles burned in secret, of lamp oil hoarded, of fear sweated into the grain of the floorboards. Now it smelled only of ink and lead and the river damp that came up through the planks in spring, and Jude had to teach themselves, over and over, that this plainness was not a trap.

The second press was back where it belonged.

They had raised it from the cellar in the first week after the collapse, four of them hauling on ropes rigged through the ceiling beams while Maren Ostler stood on the stairs shouting instructions she had no business knowing about leverage and counterweight. The river had given the machine back grudgingly, dripping, its iron gone to rust-bloom along the rails, and for three days it sat in the middle of the floor like something dredged from a wreck. Wren Corliss had slept beside it one night, Jude found them in the morning, curled under their coat on the stones, arms around the frame as if the press were a large and patient animal that might wander off again if unwatched.

Now it stood by the north window, cleaned and trued, its platen moving with a sound like a slow heartbeat. It would never be the workhorse again — the rust had gotten into places Wren could not reach without dismantling the whole frame, and Wren, who had learned the machine's body in candlelight and floodwater, refused to let anyone else attempt it. *It saved us*, Wren said, whenever Jude suggested replacing parts. *We can spare it some dignity.*

So the second press printed only one job now, and printed it slowly, and printed it well.

* * *

The archive copy had been the historian's idea — Magister Ilenne, who had come upriver not with Verrek's barges but with the ones after, a thin woman with ink-stained cuffs who read everything twice and wrote down what she read. She had appeared at the shop in the third week after the liberation, before the town had even settled on what to call what had happened to it — *the uprising*, the council minutes said, though the fishwives still said *the winter*, and Wren said *the time of the press* — and she had asked, without ceremony, whether an original set of the chronicle could be deposited in the provincial record.

Jude had said yes. Then had lain awake half the night understanding what yes meant.

Because the chronicle could not simply be handed over. Twenty-two issues, printed in cellars and fish-smoke and the dead hours before dawn, on paper of four different weights and two different colors because supply had dictated, and the early issues were bad in ways that went beyond paper. Issue three had been set in haste and printed crooked, the left margin wandering like a drunkard's walk. Issue seven had lost its final column entirely when the forme had been broken too soon, wet type smearing the last names of the mill district into grey ghosts. And issue nine—

Issue nine had a name wrong.

Jude had known it for months. Had known it since before the ice broke, actually, since a woman named Hetta Brandt had come to the shop one grey afternoon in the thaw with her shawl clutched at her throat and said, in a voice scraped nearly flat, *You have my sister listed among the drowned at the mill race. My sister did not drown. My sister was taken to the customs house on the ninth of Frostmonth and shot against the east wall, and I will not have her remembered dying the wrong way.*

Her name should have been Liesa Brandt, taken on the ninth. Not Liese Brandt, drowned on the eleventh. One letter. A single letter, cut short or cut deep by a hand hurrying in the dark — and Jude had never been able to prove afterward whether it had been their own hand or Wren's, and had stopped trying to prove it, because it did not matter. What mattered was that twenty-two issues of truth contained one error, and the error was small enough that no one would ever catch it, and it was a woman's death, and it was wrong.

In the winter, there had been no way to correct it. Correction required reprinting, and reprinting required type and paper and hours that belonged to staying alive. So Jude had done what could be done: had entered the correction in the shop's own ledger, in their steadiest hand — *Issue 9, col. 2, line 14: read Brandt, taken 9th of Frostmonth, east wall, per testimony of H. Brandt, verified* — and had promised the ledger to the record alongside the issues themselves.

But now, with the second press restored and no soldiers at the door and whole days stretching out unowned, Magister Ilenne had asked her question differently. She had come back a second time, and a third, and on the fourth visit she had stood looking at the old press by the window and said: *You know, in Vellmark they keep documents. In Vellmouth you keep machines. I wonder if you might do both.*

And so: the second proof. A complete resetting and reprinting of the chronicle, all twenty-two issues, on good paper, with corrections entered honestly in the margins, for the archive — and beside it, kept as printed, every flaw and smear and ghosted name, so that the archive would hold both the record and the making of the record, both the truth and the trembling hands that had caught at it in the dark.

Ilenne had called it unprecedented. Wren had called it *printing our own homework*. Their father had said nothing at all, but he had sharpened his graver that evening longer than the steel required.

* * *

They began on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays had been press days all through the winter and the body remembered.

Jude came down before dawn, as they had for two years now, and found Wren already there, which was also memory — Wren had always been first to the cellar, first to the cold work, the apprentice whose apprenticeship had ended somewhere in the middle of a raid with soldiers' boots on the floorboards above and a forme locked and waiting below. No one had ever held a ceremony ending it. There had simply come a morning, sometime after the liberation, when Jude had handed over the setting stick for the day's work instead of keeping it, and that had been that.

They worked issue one first. It was only four pages — the disappeared of the mill district, forty-one names in columns, set in the smallest body they owned because there had not been enough type for anything larger. Setting those columns again, letter by letter, Jude's hands moved through the case on their own knowledge, and their mind drifted along behind, the way it always did at the composing stone, the way their father had told them it would when Jude was nine years old and impatient: *The hands think faster than the head, and about better things. Let them.*

Forty-one names. They had printed these names first in the fourth week of the occupation, when printing them had felt less like journalism than like theft — stealing the dead back from the Directorate's ledgers, where the mill district's missing were recorded as *relocated*. Jude remembered the terror of that first night of distribution, Maren Ostler sliding copies under fish crates in the market while Verrek's patrols walked the aisles. Remembered the terror, and remembered also — and was ashamed of remembering — the fierce, clean joy of it, the sense that for the first time in their life the work mattered, that history had finally consented to happen here, in Vellmouth, in their own hands.

They had been younger then. It had been eighteen months ago.

By midmorning the first pages of the archive edition stood locked in the chase, and Wren pulled the proof, and they read it together standing at the stone, and it was right, and it was strange how ordinary rightness looked. Just ink, evenly impressed. Just names, spelled truly.

*Do you remember,* Wren said, *how cold it was the night we ran this the first time? My fingers stuck to the frisket. I thought we'd ruin the whole impression.*

*I remember being certain someone would hear the press from the street.*

*Someone probably did.* Wren grinned, and it was the same grin from the flooded cellar, conspiratorial still, though the conspiracy had won. *Maren says old Tull the cooper heard it every night and never said a word to anyone. Says now he didn't want to know, but I think he knew exactly, and liked knowing.*

Jude looked at their apprentice — their former apprentice, their partner, whatever the new word was; perhaps there was no new word yet, perhaps words for what they were to each other were still being set, letter by letter, like everything else — and felt the full strangeness of the morning settle over them. That they could stand in daylight and talk about it. That the press could be loud.

*Break it down,* Jude said. *Issue two next. And then nine.*

Wren's hands paused on the quoins. *Nine,* they repeated. Not a question. They knew. Everyone in the shop knew; the ledger correction had been read aloud at the table one supper, at Hetta Brandt's request, because Hetta had wanted witnesses to it.

*Nine,* Jude said. *With the correction in the margin this time. In red, if we've got red.*

*We've got red.*

*Then nine in red.*

* * *

Their father came to the shop most days now, though the doctors in Vellmark said his hands would never again hold a graver steady for fine work. Four months in the Directorate's custody — three in the cells under the customs house and one more in the hospital after, while the provisional council argued over jurisdiction — had taken the last of their precision from him, and everyone pretended not to see how he grieved it, and he pretended not to notice them pretending.

But he came anyway. He sat by the south window with the type cases he was re-sorting, the great winter's jumble of pied letters that had accumulated during the occupation when there had been no time for order, and he sorted them, compartment by compartment, e's and n's and r's, the slow patient alphabet of a man putting a language back together.

He had cut sabotage into that language. Jude thought about it now, watching him across the room — watched his father's ruined hands move among the letters he had once ruled — and thought about the mis-cut e's and the shallow-struck t's that had riddled the Directorate's commemorative broadsides like a quiet disease, discovered too late, worth an arrest and nearly a life. Their father had never spoken of it directly. Once, near the end of the occupation, from the visiting room of the customs house, with a clerk writing down every word, he had said only: *Tell whoever sets your type that a letter is a promise. Cut it true or don't cut it at all.* And the clerk had heard nothing in it, and Jude had heard everything.

Around noon he rose, stiff, and came to look over the standing forme for issue two, and read it as he read everything, lips moving slightly, one finger tracing beneath the lines.

*You're reprinting the errors too,* he said. It wasn't quite a question either.

*The flawed copies go in beside the corrected ones. Ilenne wants the whole record. Including the record of the record.*

Their father was quiet a while. His finger rested at the bottom of the page, where the imprint would eventually sit — *Printed in Vellmouth* — and Jude watched something move across his face that had no easy name, some mixture of pride and shame and the exhaustion of a man who had spent a lifetime believing his craft should stay out of history, and had learned otherwise at the cost of his hands.

*When I was young,* he said finally, *a printer's ambition was to make nothing noticeable. Good work is invisible work, my master used to say. If the reader sees the type, you've failed.* He tapped the page, gently, once. *This winter taught me my master was a coward. Or maybe just lucky. Maybe invisibility is a luxury of untroubled times, and troubled times need type you can see across a square.* He straightened, wincing. *Set the imprint large, Jude. Larger than seems decent.*

He went back to his sorting. Jude stood looking at the forme a moment longer, then reached in and swapped the fourteen-point imprint for eighteen.

* * *

Issue nine took three days.

Not because it was long — it was one of the shortest, six grim columns on the customs-house executions — but because Jude would not hurry it. Every letter was checked twice against the original and once against the ledger. The name came up on the fourteenth line of the second column, and Jude set it fresh rather than redistributing the old type: L-I-E-S-A, and then, separately, in the margin, in red eight-point, the whole truth in small compass: *Corrected. Taken 9th of Frostmonth, east wall. Per testimony of Hetta Brandt. The error was ours; the death was theirs.*

Hetta herself came to watch it printed. Maren brought her, the two women filling the doorway with river wind and herring smell, and Hetta stood at the press with her hands folded tight while Wren worked the bar and the sheet came off damp and true, and she looked at her sister's name for a long time without touching it, because the ink was wet, and Jude understood that the not-touching was costing her something enormous.

*Drowned,* she said at last. *Eleventh of the month. As if she just... slipped. As if the river did it, and nobody.* Her jaw worked. *She was shot standing up, Mr. Ravensworth. Standing, and refusing the blindfold. That's how she goes in the book.*

*That's how she goes in the book,* Jude agreed. *All of them. This copy is yours, if you'll have it.*

Hetta took the sheet like something living. At the door she turned back, and her flatness had cracked open onto something underneath, and what was underneath was terrible and grateful at once. *My sister was nobody's mistake,* she said. *Thank you for saying so in front of everyone.*

Then she was gone, and the press waited, and there was still the last page of nine to run, and after nine there were thirteen more issues, and after the issues the plates themselves to be packed for the barge, and Jude stood a moment in the middle of the shop feeling the size of the thing they had undertaken — the whole winter, reset, weighed, made permanent — and found, to their surprise, that they were not afraid of it. Only humble before it. It was, they were learning, the same posture.

* * *

The last evening of the work, they stayed late, all three of them, and then not all three — their father left at dusk with a nod, saying he wanted his supper, though Jude suspected he wanted to give them the shop to themselves for what came next — and then it was Jude and Wren and the lamps and the last page.

Twenty-two issues. The final issue had been printed originally on the night the bridge broke, in the last hours before the ice groaned apart, and it was the shortest of all: a single leaf announcing that the complete chronicle existed, that plates and copies were crossing the river, that the record would survive whatever happened to the people who made it. Jude had set that leaf with gunfire audible across the water and had never expected to read it calmly. Reading it calmly now, correcting nothing, changing nothing — even the original's cramped setting, even the typo in the fourth line that Maren had teased them about for a year — felt like reading a letter from someone they had been and could no longer quite imagine being.

*Last thing,* Wren said.

*Last thing.*

The colophon. On every underground issue it had been anonymous — *Printed in Vellmouth, by persons unknown* — because anonymity had been armor, and armor had been necessary, and the bravest sentence they had ever composed was five words of deliberate erasure. But the archive edition was not armor. The archive edition was testimony, and Ilenne had been clear, and Maren had been clearer, and Pell was dead, and the fishwives had signed their statements in their own hands, and the time for persons unknown was finished.

*Printed in Vellmouth,* the colophon would say. And then names. All of them. Maren Ostler. Wren Corliss. Edmund Ravensworth. Tomas Pell, who came too late to be forgiven but not too late to be useful. And Jude Ravensworth, who set the type.

Jude lifted the stick.

There was a particular silence in a print shop at the end of the day's work, when the presses are still and the lamps are trimmed and every forme is locked, and Jude had loved that silence all their life without ever examining why. Now they understood it. It was the sound of things held true — of metal fitted to metal, of language fitted to meaning, of a promise cut deep enough to survive handling. Anyone could speak. Type was speech that had decided to stay.

They set the colophon slowly, name by name, and each letter went into the stick with the small precise click that had been the music of their childhood, and they thought of their father's hands and Hetta's sister standing unbowed against the east wall, and of Maren in the market with her crates, and of Wren asleep against the rescued press, and of the river carrying their plates into a wider world while they themselves had stayed behind to face whatever faced them. The last name was their own, and they set it as carefully as all the others, no larger, no smaller, one line among many, because that was the whole lesson of the winter, learned late and paid for dearly: that history was not made elsewhere by people unlike oneself, and was not made by any single hand at all, but assembled — letter by letter, witness by witness, correction by correction — by ordinary craft, imperfect, revisable, and stubborn past all sense.

They locked the forme. Wren inked the press. The sheet came away white-sided and true, and they hung it to dry by the window where the morning light would find it, and stood back together in the lamplight to look at what they had made.

Below the list of names, the colophon's final line sat in its bed of lead, set by hand, letter by letter, and it read:

*Set true, and set again.*
*Vellmouth. In the year the river broke.*

Outside, the water moved in the dark, unhurried, going somewhere — and in the shop the type waited for morning, and the morning would come, and there would be more pages after this one.
