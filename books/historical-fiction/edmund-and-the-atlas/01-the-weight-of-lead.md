## 1. The Weight of Lead

The type told you everything before the words did.

Edmund had learned that at nine years old, standing on a crate to reach the composing stick, his father's hand over his own guiding the brass into the groove. Lead has a memory, Gideon said. It remembers every hand that touched it, every page it made, every lie it carried into the world same as the truth. You can't see it, but it's there, in the wear of the shoulder, in the way an *e* goes thin at the bowl after ten thousand impressions. The type knows what it's done.

Edmund was twenty-six now, and his hands knew the cases without looking — upper and lower, the capitals ranked in their rows like soldiers at inspection, the small letters crowded beneath in their ordered chaos. He could sort blind. Some nights he did, just to feel the craft move through him without the interruption of thought. Tonight he wished he could sort blind, because thinking had become a weight he couldn't set down.

Outside the shop, the city was holding its breath.

You could hear it if you listened — not the usual noise of the Weavers' Quarter, not the carts and the argument and the church bells arguing back, but a silence with edges to it. Martial law had been announced for Friday. Today was Monday. In three days the military governor's proclamation would take effect and the printing district would be sealed: no presses running, no journeymen crossing the checkpoints, no ink moving anywhere in the quarter except what the army authorized. They'd posted the order on the customs house door in letters so large even the illiterate could read the shape of it.

Three days. And Gideon Dravenmoor was burning paper.

Edmund heard it again — the small dry collapse of pages giving themselves to the stove in the back office. It had been going on since before dawn. Not the ordinary burn-bag of a print shop; Edmund emptied those himself, proof sheets and spoiled stock, and he knew their sound. This was different. This was slow. Deliberate. A page at a time, with pauses between, the way a man reads something one last time before he feeds it to the fire.

He set a line of fourteen-point Caslon without dropping a single sort, which was its own kind of miracle given what his hands wanted to do, then wiped them on his apron and walked through to the office.

His father sat at the desk with a cigar box open before him, letters fanned across the blotter like a losing hand of cards. The stove door stood open, breathing orange light into the dim room. Gideon was sixty-one and had been stooped over a compose stone so long his spine had taken the shape of the work, but tonight he sat very straight, and that straightness frightened Edmund more than anything else in the week that had been full of frightening things.

"You'll scorch the varnish on the desk," Edmund said. "Again."

"The desk has survived worse than me." His father didn't look up. In the stove light his face was all planes and shadow, the deep lines around his mouth working as though he were chewing on words and finding none of them fit to swallow.

"What is it you're burning?"

"Old correspondence."

"I can see that." Edmund leaned against the doorframe. He'd learned that posture from watching his father use it on customers who tried to haggle below cost, and he deployed it now with the same flat patience. "Correspondence from whom?"

"From people who are dead or gone, and it's nobody's business which."

"Gideon." His mother had been dead eleven years and he still used her name for his father when he wanted to be gentle, because she'd been the only creature alive who could say *Gideon Dravenmoor* and make it sound like an endearment rather than a sentence.

It worked, the way it always worked — a flinch, quickly mastered. His father gathered three letters from the box, squared their edges against the desk the way he'd square a forme before locking it up, and fed them into the stove one at a time. Edmund caught a glimpse of handwriting on the topmost page — a slanting, elegant hand, nothing like his father's blunt verticals — and a date. 1828. Twenty years old, that letter. Older than Edmund's apprenticeship. Older than most things his father refused to discuss.

"There's a proclamation coming," Edmund said. "Friday. They'll search the shops, Father. That's what searches are. If there's something in this room the army would want—"

"If there were something in this room the army wanted," Gideon said quietly, "it would already be ash by now, wouldn't it."

The words were meant as comfort. They landed like a stone dropped down a well — Edmund listened for the bottom and didn't hear it reach.

* * *

The rest of the morning passed in the rhythms that had held the shop together for forty years, and Edmund clung to them the way a drowning man is said to cling to straw, except that these rhythms were not straw. They were iron. They were the only solid thing left.

At ten, the apprentice Pieter came in late and wet, having run the gauntlet of a patrol on Bridge Street, and reported that the National Guard had stopped every man with ink under his fingernails for questioning. "They're making lists," Pieter said, shaking rain off his cap like a dog. "Names and trades. Master Voss got detained two hours over his pamphlets, and he printed nothing but temperance tracts since spring."

"They're not after pamphlets," said Herr Kessel from the press, where he was re-packing a bearing with grease. Kessel was fifty-eight, had been at the Dravenmoor press longer than Edmund had been alive, and spoke perhaps forty words a week, all of them load-bearing. "Pamphlet men they fine. They're cataloguing. You catalogue what you mean to confiscate."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing to answer.

At noon a customer came in with a water-damaged atlas — a fat Dutch-produced folio, thirty years old, swollen and foxed, its spine split along both cords. Edmund took the commission because commissions were money and money was getting scarce, but also because the repair work steadied him. There was no politics in rebinding a book. There was only paste and board and linen tape and the patient arithmetic of a job done right.

He carried the atlas to the bench by the north window, where the light was honestest, and opened it flat to assess the damage. The maps inside were beautiful in the way old maps are beautiful — coastlines drawn with more hope than accuracy, mountain ranges shaded like sleeping animals, a world divided into empires that half of them no longer existed. He ran his thumb along the broken spine and felt something wrong.

Not rot. Rot gives softness. This gave resistance — a small, dense ridge sewn into the lining where no ridge should be, between the text block and the boards, hidden under a layer of mull that some previous hand had stitched back down with suspicious neatness.

Edmund looked at the atlas for a long moment. Then he looked at the office door, closed now, behind which the stove had gone quiet.

He should have opened it then. He knew that later, in the terrible clarity of afterward — that the whole shape of his life had bent around the moment when his thumb found that ridge, and that every road out of the week ahead branched from the choice of whether to cut the stitching or leave it. But at the time he was a bookbinder's son with a customer waiting and a father burning secrets in the next room, and he told himself the sensible thing, the craftsman's thing: assess first. Open nothing until you know what you're opening.

He finished the estimate. He quoted the woman a price. He wrote her name in the daybook — Frau Halloran, Copper Lane — and told her three days, maybe four, with the disruptions and all. She left. The bell over the door rang and settled, and the shop was theirs again, three men and a boy and a sealed envelope sleeping in the spine of an old atlas, and Edmund went back to sorting type because his hands needed somewhere to be.

* * *

In the afternoon the fever started.

Gideon had looked grey all week — Edmund had told himself it was the weather, the worry, the rationing — but by three o'clock his father was shivering at his desk with his coat still on, and when Edmund put a hand to his brow the heat came off him like a bake-oven. Kessel appeared with camphor and a blanket without being asked, the way Kessel did everything, and they got Gideon upstairs to the rooms above the shop and into bed while he protested in a voice gone thin and papery.

"I'm not dying, Edmund. I'm cold. There's a difference, and I'd think my own son could tell."

"The doctor, then."

"No doctor. Doctors write things down." His father gripped Edmund's wrist with a strength that surprised them both, and for a moment his eyes were clear and terribly present, looking up from the pillow with something that was almost — Edmund would turn the word over for days — almost apology. "Listen to me. Whatever happens Friday, whatever they ask you, you sort your type and keep your hands clean. Do you hear me? Clean hands, clean conscience. The lead remembers, boy. Don't let it remember you doing anything you'll have to forget."

"That's not sense, that's riddle."

"It's the only inheritance I've got left to give you." He let go of Edmund's wrist and turned his face to the wall. Within the hour he was asleep, or past sleep, breathing in shallow pulls, and Edmund stood in the doorway of his parents' old room feeling the floorboards hold him up the way they always had and understanding, in the wordless way the body understands before the mind consents, that something in this house had been coming apart for twenty years and had chosen this exact week to finish falling.

* * *

He went back down after dark. The shop at night was a country of its own — the press a great sleeping animal, the type cases ranked along the wall like a library of unreadable books, the smell of oil and ink and paste that was, Edmund admitted freely, the only smell in the world he loved without qualification. He lit one lamp. He took the water-damaged atlas from the bench where he'd left it, and he carried it to the window light, and he took up the bone folder, the thin one, the one that slips between things.

He told himself he'd only look. Only enough to know what he was binding and whose property he might be handling. A binder's due diligence, nothing more. The razor made one clean pass through the old stitching, careful as surgery, and the mull lifted, and there in a cavity carved into the spine's lining — carved deliberately, expertly, by someone who knew book anatomy the way Edmund knew it — lay a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed thread.

Inside the oilcloth: plates. Copperplate, engraved, six of them, each one heavier than it had any right to be, and colder. He tilted one to the lamp and the image rose out of the metal — borders, seals, columns of names in a formal engraved hand, the unmistakable architecture of an official document. A treaty. Or something wearing a treaty's clothes.

And folded around the plates, sealed with red wax stamped with a crest Edmund did not recognize — a column, broken — a single letter.

He broke the seal because his hands were already moving and had stopped consulting him. The paper was good stock, machine-made, foreign. The slanting elegant handwriting was the same he'd glimpsed that morning in the fire. It began without salutation, as if politeness were a luxury the writer had priced and declined:

*Messrs. Dravenmoor have kept faith these twenty years. The colonel expects the custodianship concluded before the signing. The plates will travel, or the name Dravenmoor will answer for what the plates contain.*

Below the signature, a date. Friday. The day the district closed. The day the treaty was signed.

Edmund stood in the lamplight with the plates in his hands, and the lead remembered him, and the shop around him — the press, the cases, the forty years of honest work — went suddenly strange, like a room discovered to have been built over a cellar no one had mentioned. His father's obscurity. Their narrow, decent, threadbare life. Camouflage, all of it. Not poverty. Hiding.

Upstairs, faintly, the fevered rhythm of his father's breathing came through the ceiling, quick and uneven, counting out the last hours of the family's quiet.
