## 3. What Father Burned

The winter of 1828 came down off the mountains early, and Gideon Dravenmoor remembered it as the winter he stopped sleeping.

He had been thirty-one then, already master of the shop on Candlewick Row for six years, his father two years in the churchyard. The city was not yet the city it would become — no barricades, no martial drums — just a garrison town grown fat on a war that had gone on so long nobody could remember the shape of peace. Colonel Étienne Vasseur was forty and silver at the temples and had the kind of voice that made an order sound like a compliment.

Gideon had printed army broadsheets before. Everyone in the quarter had. It paid the rent between hymnals and shipping manifests, and you didn't ask why the requisitions used three different names for the same regiment. That was the water they all swam in.

So when Vasseur came to the shop on a Tuesday evening, alone, hat in hand like a parishioner, Gideon set him a chair and poured the good brandy and thought nothing of it at all.

"You have steady hands," Vasseur said. He said it looking at Gideon's fingers on the decanter, not at his face.

"Twenty years at the case."

"And your father's eye. They say old Josiah could cut a letter so fine the naked eye couldn't tell it from foundry type." Vasseur smiled. "I have work for that eye."

* * *

They brought the model on Thursday. Not to the shop — Gideon understood by then that this was deliberate — but to a rented room above the chandler's on Fisher Street, where a table had been cleared and lamp oil provided in quantities that suggested long nights ahead.

The model was a page. One page, treaty format, the armistice convention of the northern powers, with its seals and its signatory lines and its marginal annotations in the cramped diplomatic hand. Gideon had printed facsimiles of documents before; every printer had, for museums, for collectors, for lawyers too lazy to walk to their own filing rooms. He looked at it the way he looked at any job: counting the founts, the rules, the ornaments.

Then he read what it said.

The words themselves were ordinary — clauses on demarcation lines, prisoner exchanges, grain corridors. What stopped him was the signature line at the foot. Two names were already engraved there in facsimile, elegant and final, where no legitimate copy would carry signatures at all. And beneath them, a third space, blank, waiting.

"This is a finished instrument," Gideon said. "Signed."

"It will be," Vasseur agreed pleasantly. "That's rather the point."

"I don't—" Gideon set the model down. His hands, which never shook at the case, wanted to shake now, and he pressed them flat against the table. "Colonel. A forged armistice. If this surfaces—"

"It won't surface. That is also the point." Vasseur walked to the window, hands clasped behind him, a man admiring weather. "There are men across the river who will pay a great deal of money for proof that our government signed away the corridor in '26 and concealed it. There are other men who will pay more to prove we did not. I intend to sell the first thing to the second sort of men. You are going to cut me the plates."

"And if I refuse?"

Vasseur turned. He wasn't smiling anymore, and somehow that was worse than any threat delivered loudly. "Your journeyman, Pell. A good boy. Writes letters home to his mother in Verlac every Sunday. His brother is in my regiment." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "And your wife is expecting. April, isn't it? You should be careful coming home through the garrison streets after dark. So much drink in the soldiers this time of year."

He said nothing else. He put on his hat, wished Gideon a good evening, and left the brandy untouched.

* * *

It took him eleven weeks.

Eleven weeks of nights above the chandler's while Marianne slept at home believing her husband was keeping accounts, eleven weeks cutting wax into steel with gravers finer than anything the trade required, working from proofs under a lens until his right eye wept. He cut the text fount first — a close match to the ministry's chancery hand, though not perfect; nothing human was perfect, and he learned to hate himself for taking comfort in that. Then the seals, which meant engraving in reverse, in mirror, the arms of two nations whose ambassadors were at that moment negotiating in good faith somewhere in the east.

He told himself stories all through those weeks. That he would cut the plates badly enough to be useless. That he would finish them and destroy them. That Vasseur would die in the war, that the whole rotten arrangement would dissolve like snow on a sill. He told himself he was protecting Pell, protecting Marianne, protecting the child kicking against her ribs whom they had not yet decided to name.

Edmund. Though Gideon didn't know that yet either.

In March the plates were done: four steel rectangles wrapped in oilcloth, fitting together into a box the size of a prayer book. Vasseur came to collect them on a night of sleet, rubbing his gloved hands, expansive, almost warm. He counted them without unwrapping them, weighing each in his palm like a man judging coins.

"You've done beautiful work, Dravenmoor. I always knew you would." He tucked the bundle inside his coat. "You'll never see me again. That is the best thing I can promise anyone."

Gideon watched him go down the stairs and stood a long time in the cold room afterward.

Then he went back to the shop and worked until dawn.

* * *

Here was the thing he had never told anyone, not Marianne, not the priest, not the dark itself in any language it understood:

The plates Vasseur carried away that night were not the plates.

Gideon had cut a second set. Cruder, faster, cut in half the time because half the fineness — but cut from the same models, bearing the same forged signatures, capable of printing the same lie. He had swapped them at the last hour, delivered Vasseur the copies, and kept the true masters hidden inside the hollowed spine of his father's great atlas of the northern provinces, the one that sat in the shop's back room gathering dust because nobody had wanted a map of a border that kept moving.

Why? He asked himself the question for twenty years and got different answers depending on the season. In bad winters the answer was leverage: a hostage held against Vasseur's hostage held against him. In better years the answer was conscience: someday the truth might need proving, and here it was, in steel, waiting. Some nights the answer was only vanity — the masters were his best work, and something in him could not bear to let the finest thing he ever made walk out the door to serve the ugliest purpose he could imagine.

But underneath all of it, always, was the guilt. He had made the lie. The least he could do was keep the truth of it where he could watch it sleep.

* * *

For twenty years, nothing happened. That was the punishment, really — the nothing. Vasseur vanished into the machinery of the war, surfacing in promotions, in gazettes, in a portrait engraving Gideon once saw in a coffee-house newspaper and had to leave the room over. The war ground on toward whatever it was grinding toward. Gideon raised his son, taught him the case and the stone and the burnisher, watched Edmund's small hands grow steady, and every morning of those twenty years the atlas sat in the back room with its secret spine, and every morning Gideon looked at it once, the way you look at a scar.

He might have destroyed the masters a hundred times. He never did. He told himself it was prudence. He knew it was cowardice — destroying them meant admitting what they were, saying it out loud in the burning of the metal, and Gideon Dravenmoor had built a life out of never saying anything out loud.

* * *

Now the war was ending. Everyone could feel it, the way you feel thaw under a frozen road — the surface still solid, everything beneath beginning to move. And ten days ago a sealed letter had come to the shop, franked with an officer's frank, addressed in a hand Gideon had hoped never to see again outside of engravings.

He burned his correspondence that same evening, crouched at the grate while Edmund sorted type in the front room, feeding in bills of lading and old tax notices and Marianne's last letters one by one, watching the flames take the ink. It was theater, mostly. If Vasseur wanted his papers, a fire in a grate would not stop him. But it felt like action. A man had to do something.

The letter itself he had read once, standing in the doorway with the rain coming down behind him, and then folded into his coat, and then taken out and read four more times by candlelight when the house slept.

*Colonel — for you are a colonel still, whatever else you have made yourself — you will recall certain instruments entrusted to your custody in the year '28. The peace now forming has given them a value they lacked in wartime. My agents will call. Be advised that refusal will be construed as evidence of conspiracy against the state, a charge which your station, your family, and your late associations render extremely credible.*

It was signed with a name that meant nothing to anyone but him: *A.R., per V.* — Aldous Rennick. Rennick, who drank tea in Gideon's kitchen twice a week. Rennick, who had dandled Edmund on his knee. Rennick, who had known about the atlas, or guessed, or been told twenty-eight years ago by a frightened young printer talking too much in a rented room — no. No, Gideon had never spoken of it. But Rennick missed nothing. Rennick had probably weighed the atlas on a shelf and seen the way Gideon never let it be sold, never let it leave the building, the way a man guards a wound he calls a keepsake.

Vasseur's agents were already calling. Not with soldiers — worse, with courtesy. An inquiry about printing commemorative matter for the peace celebrations. A young captain who admired the shop's old presses. Questions asked in the quarter about the Dravenmoors' history, their loyalties, their finances during the war years.

They didn't know where the plates were. Gideon was almost sure of it. Almost. If they had known, there'd have been no letters, no courtesy — just men in the night and a press broken for kindling. What they had was the certainty that he had them, and the patience to make him feel the walls closing by inches, and the knowledge — God help him, someone had given them the knowledge — that Gideon Dravenmoor was a man who hid things rather than destroy them.

He was dying anyway; he had begun to suspect it that winter, the fevers coming closer together, the cough that wouldn't resolve. Perhaps that was why they moved when they moved. A dead man's secrets get auctioned.

He heard Edmund laugh at something in the front room — the apprentice's boy, probably, or some joke among the journeymen — and the sound went through Gideon like a graver slipping.

He had one asset left, and it was the same asset he'd had for twenty years: the truth, in steel, in the dark. Vasseur wanted it to sell the lie. Rennick wanted it to buy silence. Both of them assumed Gideon would do what he had always done — hide, defer, protect the name.

His son was sorting type in the next room. Steady hands. His grandfather's eye.

Gideon took the letter from his coat, read it a fifth time, and began, at last, to think about how to spend what remained.
