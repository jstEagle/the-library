## 4. Three Checkpoints

My father had not set type in four days.

I understood the magnitude of this the way a sailor understands a wind that has stopped. Gideon Dravenmoor's hands were the metronome of our house — the tap of the composing stick audible through the floorboards at dawn, the soft click of quoins being tightened, the ritual sweep of the brush across the stone at night. When those hands went quiet, the whole shop held its breath.

He lay in the back room above the press, propped on pillows gone yellow with age, and his fever came and went like weather. In its troughs he was lucid and worse than delirious — lucid enough to be afraid. I had never seen my father afraid. He had stood in the doorway during the bread riots of '31 with nothing but a hand-press bar in his grip. He had told Colonel Vasseur himself, so the story went, exactly where he could put his requisition orders. Now he flinched when the shutters rattled in the wind.

The letter from the atlas sat between us like a third man in the room. I had shown it to him two nights ago, and I will not forget what his face did: it collapsed, fold by fold, like a forme loosened too fast, all the plates of him shifting at once.

"You read it," he had said, though it wasn't a question, because of course I'd read it. A sealed letter naming the Dravenmoors as custodians of forged armistice documents. A letter sewn into a binding by someone who wanted it found. Vasseur's handwriting, or close enough to fool a court.

Now, on the fourth morning of his silence at the case, he called me up the stairs with a voice scraped down to the wire.

"Edmund. Bring the atlas."

* * *

He was sitting half-upright when I came in, and he had dressed himself, which cost him something — his shirt collar hung open where his fingers had failed the buttons, and there was sweat on his forehead from the effort of it. The letter lay on the blanket beside him. He pushed it toward me without touching it again, as if it were hot metal straight from the casting pot.

"I want you to take them," he said. "The plates. Not the letter — the letter means nothing without them."

"Take them where?"

"San Damiano. The monastery on the ridge, past the river." He said the name carefully, laying each syllable like a line of twelve-point. "It's outside the occupation zone. Brothers of the Reconciled Order. They took no side in the war; they take no side now. If the plates are under their floor when the treaty is signed, they're under God's floor, and no one — not Vasseur, not the provisional government, not your own conscience — can dig them out in time to matter."

"And after?"

"After doesn't concern me anymore." He smiled, and the smile frightened me more than the fever had. "After is yours."

I should have refused then. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is what all of this was supposedly for. I should have said: Father, burn them yourself, you've burned twenty years of correspondence already, one fire more costs you nothing. I should have said: why must it be me, why must I carry our ruin through three military checkpoints in a city where soldiers are stopping boys my age for less than the color of their coats.

Instead I heard myself say, "When is the signing?"

"Nine days. Perhaps seven. They keep moving the date the way a cheat moves his cards." He closed his eyes. "You'll need three days for the route alone. Leave yourself margin. You taught me that, when you were ten and setting your first galley — leave margin, Papa, or the trim takes your text."

I hadn't taught him anything. He was giving me the lesson back, dressed as memory, the way old men do when they're handing you a weight too heavy to offer directly.

"The plates are still in the cellar?" I asked. "Where you hid them?"

"Under the stone by the drain. Where they've been since before you could read." His voice cracked on the last word, and he turned his face to the wall. "Go plan your route, boy. Plan it the way you'd impose a page. You're better at it than I ever was."

* * *

That afternoon I did what he asked, because craft is the only prayer I know. I spread paper across the compose room bench and began to build the journey the way I'd build a broadside: margins first, then the body, then the ornaments last.

The city between our door and San Damiano was a page with three errors in it, and the errors wore uniforms.

**The first checkpoint stood at the Weir Gate**, where the printing quarter funneled into the market district. I knew it intimately; I passed it twice a week delivering work. Two sentries and a corporal, a sawhorse barrier, a table stacked with other people's opened parcels. The soldiers there were garrison men — bored, cold, underfed, interested chiefly in tobacco and the hour of their relief. Boredom was the lever. A bored soldier wants his inspection over more than he wants it thorough. Whatever I carried would have to look like the most tedious thing in the world: dull, heavy, expected. Nothing invites a search like interest; nothing repels one like tedium.

**The second gate was the River Bridge** — the true checkpoint, the one built after the armistice to strangle the printing district's trade. Stone towers, a sergeant who actually looked, a mirror angled to see wagon undersides. They opened books there. They opened *bindings*, sometimes, because the occupiers knew as well as we did that a printer's cart was a smuggler's cathedral — hollow reams, gutters of glued sheets, type hidden in bread loaves. Whatever disguise carried me past the Weir Gate's boredom would have to survive the bridge's suspicion. Two different problems. Possibly two different cargoes.

And there was a third problem at the bridge that I wrote down and then stared at for a long time: the order chits. Every commercial crossing required paperwork showing origin and destination. Any destination I could honestly claim — a bookshop, a bindery — put me on the record, moving suspicious goods, days before an armistice signing. Any destination I lied about was a lie that unraveled the moment anyone checked.

**The third checkpoint wasn't a gate at all.** It was the road up the ridge itself — a picket line, informal, manned by whatever company drew the short straw that week. Unpredictable. No routine to learn, no corporal's habits to study. Against a fixed gate you plan; against a random picket you prepare. I couldn't know who would be there. I could only make sure that whoever it was saw nothing worth remembering.

Three gates. Nine days. One dying father, one forged armistice, and a set of copper plates hidden under a cellar stone that could hang a dozen men or free a city, depending entirely on whose hands held them when the ink dried.

I drew the route clean: shop, Weir Gate, market lane, River Bridge, pilgrim road, ridge, monastery. Then I did what any compositor does with a problem layout — I broke the line and set it again.

The cargo was the thing. Not the route; routes are easy, cargo is character. What travels through three checkpoints unquestioned? Funeral goods. Church goods. And then, somewhere between one line of notes and the next, the answer assembled itself with the click of type into a stick:

Hymnals. A delivery of hymnals, bound in plain sheep, ordered for a parish — heavy, holy, and above all *boring*. No soldier in history has searched a hymnal with enthusiasm. And the plates, wrapped in oilcloth and greased, would lie in a false gutter beneath the sewn signatures of forty singing books, flat as a watermark, invisible unless the binder's stitching was cut — and who cuts the stitching of a hymnal?

I sat back. My hands were steady. Later I would remember being proud of that steadiness and wonder at myself.

There remained the question I had been circling all afternoon the way the eye circles a bad justification. My father wanted the plates buried. Vasseur wanted them delivered. Rennick — Aldous Rennick, who had dined at our table every Thursday for fifteen years, who had taught me the names of clouds — Rennick had come by twice since the letter surfaced, asking questions with the smooth face of a man checking a lock he believed he'd oiled.

And underneath all of them, quiet as a locked forme, sat my own wanting. Because I had read the letter, and I had read my father's guilt in the lines of his face, and some stubborn galley of truth in me kept insisting on what the plates actually were: not our shame, but *evidence*. Proof that the armistice everyone was about to sign was built on a forgery. Proof that could stop a lie from becoming law.

My father wanted them hidden. Vasseur wanted them used. Nobody, apparently, wanted them *read*.

I copied the route fair onto a clean sheet, folded it once, and put it inside my shirt, against the skin. Then I went down to the cellar, knelt at the drain stone, and put my fingers into the cold gap where twenty years of our family's real history had been lying underfoot while I set other men's news above it.

The oilcloth parcel came up heavier than lead has any right to be. I carried it up the stairs and laid it on my father's bed, and he looked at it, and then at me, and said, "Whatever happens at those gates — don't let them find it *on* you. Promise me that much."

"Then where should it be, Father? On whom?"

He had no answer for that. Fever took him under again within the hour, and I sat by his bed listening to his breath go ragged, doing sums in my head: nine days minus one spent planning left eight; three checkpoints; one monastery; and somewhere out in the darkening city, a friend of the family reporting every move we made to a colonel who had been waiting twenty years for exactly this week.

I would leave at first light, with a cartload of hymnals and a lie about a parish that had ordered them.

I just didn't yet know which parish I was going to invent — or that, by the time I reached the Weir Gate, the invented parish would matter less than the state of my hands.
