## 5. The First Gate

The plates went into the hymnals at four in the morning, and Edmund learned something about fear in the doing: it had a rhythm, like a press. It cycled. It struck. It lifted.

He worked by a single candle with the shutters barred, the shop around him dark as a closed drawer. Twelve copperplates, each wrapped in a sheet of oiled paper Gideon kept for protecting type during damp weather. Each plate thin enough — just barely — to slide between the gathered signatures of a hymnal without splitting the spine. He had measured them twice against the books last night while his father slept, or lay awake pretending to sleep; there was no telling anymore which.

The hymnals were real. That mattered to him in a way he couldn't have defended if anyone had asked. Brother Anselm at the monastery of Saint Feron had ordered forty copies three months ago, before the fire took the monastery's east wing and made the order, by any sane arithmetic, obsolete. But the order existed. The invoice existed. Forty hymnals bound for Saint Feron was a fact written in his father's ledger in his father's hand, and Edmund meant to hide inside it like a slug hiding in a line of justified text — invisible unless you knew to look for the space where it didn't belong.

Twelve of the forty carried plates. He sewed them in himself, needle from the binding bench, waxed thread, stitches small as punctuation. His hands were steadier than he expected them to be. Craft did that. Twenty years at the cases taught his fingers a kind of obedience that his heart couldn't overrule.

*You are only doing what the book requires,* he told himself. *A delivery is a delivery.*

The candle guttered. Outside, the city coughed in its sleep — a patrol's boots somewhere on Tanner's Row, a dog, the far bell of the foundry chapel marking the quarter hour wrong, as it had for years. Martial law began officially at dawn. The checkpoints were already manned. By noon, nothing would move through the printing quarter without papers and a reason, and reasons were the one commodity the quarter had run out of.

He packed the hymnals into the delivery crate, twelve among forty, plates facing inward, and nailed the lid, and sat down on the floor beside it with his back against the composing stone, and shook.

Not for long. A minute, maybe two. Then he got up, because the first gate opened at six, and terror, he discovered, could be sharpened into a tool if you refused to let it stay a state of mind.

* * *

Corporal Teodor Brandt had been standing at the Colgate barricade since five, and by half past he had catalogued everything there was to catalogue about boredom, which was that it was not empty but full — full of cold seeping up through boot leather, full of the smell of wet rope and horse dung, full of the same carters coming through with the same turnips and the same sullen faces, wanting the same stamps on the same chits.

Brandt liked the boredom. He would not have admitted this to anyone, least of all himself, but the truth was that a quiet checkpoint suited him. Before the emergency he'd been a customs clerk's assistant in the river office. Ledgers, manifests, seals. When the regiment came through and conscripted the useful and the tall, they'd made him a corporal and put him exactly where his one talent lived: paperwork. Everyone else on the barricade watched faces. Brandt watched documents. Faces lied fluently; paper lied badly, if you knew its habits.

The morning's tally so far: a flour haul with a miller's stamp two days stale (passed, noted), a surgeon's cart (passed, waved through with the respect due to knives), a widow with a trunk she claimed held linens and which, by the sound the latch made, held cutlery (passed; he was not paid enough to care about cutlery).

At six-ten, a printer's boy came through the fog with a handcart.

Not a boy, quite. Young man, ink-stained to the elbow, pale in the way of men who work indoors, pulling a crate marked with the seal of a print shop whose name Brandt had seen on a requisition list — Dravenmoor, it said. On the list of shops to be assessed. Which meant watched.

"Manifest," Brandt said.

The young man handed it over with hands that were shaking.

Not trembling from cold — the morning was chill, true, but everyone's hands were red and still, fists jammed in pockets. These hands shook the way hands shake when a body has decided, without consulting its owner, that it is about to die. Brandt had seen it on recruits, on prisoners, once on a captain. It never lasted. Either the man fainted, or the fear burned off into something usable.

This one was burning it off even as Brandt read the manifest. He could watch it happen: the young man planting his feet, lifting his chin, arranging his face into the mild professional blankness of someone delivering goods. It was almost well done. Almost.

"Forty hymnals," Brandt said. "Bound where?"

"Saint Feron. Monastery order." The voice was steady now too. Good voice. Practiced-flat, the voice of a man reading from a page in his head.

"Saint Feron's burned."

* * *

Edmund heard the words land in his chest like a dropped forme — all the type scattered at once. *Saint Feron's burned.* He knew it was true; Rennick had told him, three days ago, gently, watching Edmund's face over the rim of his tea cup. *The east wing, I'm afraid. Poor Anselm is at his sister's in Vellay.* And Edmund had built his lie for exactly that fact, which was why the lie held when it came.

But the officer hadn't said *the east wing*. He'd said *burned*, plain, and for one white instant Edmund stood at the edge of the whole pit — every assumption he'd stacked, gone.

Then craft caught him. You do not repour a page because one word slurred. You check the copy against the proof.

"The east wing," Edmund said. "Brother Anselm wrote us after. The library and chapel stores were lost — service books, hymnals. We're replacing stock." He let a craftsman's weariness into it, the universal grievance of the trades: "Third amended order since autumn. Half my customers are refugees from their own invoices."

The corporal looked at the manifest. Looked at him. Looked at the crate, and Edmund felt the crate look back, forty hymnals and twelve secrets breathing under the lid.

"Open it."

* * *

There it was, then. Brandt watched the young man's face go through the whole sequence — the flicker, the recovery, the nod — and thought, with the mild disappointment of a clerk finding an honest error in a column of figures: *this one has something in the box.*

"Open it," he said again, and knelt to help, because regulations said inspect, and inspections went faster with two.

The hymnals came out in stacks. Black covers, gilt titles. Brandt pulled one at random, fanned it, checked the stitching along the spine — sound work, better than soldiers' hymnals ever got — set it aside. Pulled another. Third. The carter behind them was cursing about his turnips. Somewhere down the queue a mule complained.

"You're the son?" Brandt asked, because the requisition list had notes attached and one of the notes mentioned a father.

"Gideon Dravenmoor is my father."

"He's on an assessment list."

"Half the quarter is on assessment lists." True. Comfortably true. Edmund kept lifting books out, stacking them, and found that the work was saving him — that his hands, given task, forgot to shake. This was the thing his father had never put into words but had shown him every day of his life: that craft was a place to stand when the ground moved.

Brandt counted. Forty on the manifest; he counted thirty-one out of the crate and stopped counting because the arithmetic was obviously going to hold and his knees were wet through. He fanned one more hymnal — the twelfth? no, the ninth — and the signatures fell open to a psalm of thanksgiving, which struck him as funny in some way he didn't examine, and he closed it.

"Reload," he said. "Chit's stamped. Move along."

* * *

Later — much later, when he could think about it — Edmund would understand that the shaking hands had saved him as surely as the lie had. A man with steady hands carrying hymnals to a burned monastery might have been worth following. A frightened man who mastered his fright in front of you was just a frightened man; the world was full of them; the war was manufacturing thousands daily. Brandt had stamped the chit and turned to the turnips and forgotten him.

But forgetting is not the same as dismissing. And the corporal, reloading his ledger-mind that evening, would pause over one entry — *Dravenmoor, printing, 40 hymnals, St. Feron, passed 06:40* — and find it snagging like a hair in the type. Not the count. Not the manifest. Just the memory of hands shaking, and then not shaking, and how quickly not-shaking had come.

He was a paperwork man. He believed in anomalies. He circled the entry in pencil, lightly, the way a compositor might mark a suspect word for the proofreader — not accusing it, only asking someone to look again.

* * *

Edmund made the second mile before he let himself stop, behind a tanner's wall, hands braced on the handcart's rail, breathing like a man hauled up from deep water. The crate sat behind him, innocent, heavy. Twelve plates. One gate down.

Two to go, and the second gate was manned not by bored clerks but by Captain Delacroix's provosts — the ones who actually read manifests, actually remembered them, actually followed up. He had planned for that. He had planned for hours, nights, mapping the route across the city the way his father mapped a page: margins tight, lines justified, no element placed where the eye would trip on it.

What he had not planned for was the figure leaning against the tanner's gate ahead of him, gloved hands folded, gray coat immaculate despite the hour, smiling the smile that had greeted Edmund across Sunday dinners for twenty years.

"There he is," Aldous Rennick said warmly. "I've been waiting, lad. We should talk before you reach the second gate — about the route you think you're taking, and the one I can give you instead."
