## 6. An Old Friend's Offer

Aldous Rennick had always believed that the worst lies were the ones you told yourself, and so he did not lie to himself now. He stood in the doorway of Dravenmoor & Son, hat in hand, rain running off his coat in threads, and he admitted it plainly: he was the reason Colonel Vasseur knew about the plates.

He was also, still, the man who had taught Edmund to read a chase the way other boys learned to read scripture. Both things were true. He had spent sixty years learning that most men could not bear two true things at once, which was why men like Vasseur ruled and men like Gideon printed.

"Uncle Aldous." Edmund looked up from the stone, hands black to the wrist with ink. He smiled — that was the cruelty of it, the boy smiled — and waved him past the curtain of hanging proofs. "Mind the forme on your left. It's not set yet."

"I've been minding formes since before your father could shave," Rennick said, and hung his wet hat on the peg that had held his hat for thirty years of visits.

The shop smelled as it always had: oil, molten metal, the vinegar bite of damp paper. It smelled like the only home besides his own that Rennick had ever loved. That was the thing Vasseur would never understand, sitting in his office with his maps and his dossiers. You could not betray a place you didn't love. Betrayal required love the way fire required air. The colonel thought informers were motivated by money or fear, and paid them accordingly, and never once considered that the most valuable information moved between people precisely because they loved each other — because Aldous came to dinner on Thursdays, because he knew where Gideon's cough sat in his chest this winter, because Edmund told him, laughing over cards last month, about the water-damaged atlas and the fool customer who'd left it three weeks without collecting.

*Three weeks,* Vasseur had said. *And what was sewn into the binding?*

Nothing Aldous had said could un-say after that.

"You look like a drowned rat," Edmund said, wiping his hands on a rag that was itself more rag than cloth. "Sit. There's coffee if I can trust the kettle."

"Don't trouble yourself." He wouldn't have kept it down anyway. His stomach had been a closed fist since morning. "I came about the atlas."

He watched the boy's face do a small, controlled thing. Not guilt exactly. Recognition. The face of a craftsman who has just been asked about an irregularity in someone else's presswork and is deciding how much truth serves.

"The customer's book? It's repaired. He's meant to collect it Thursday."

"And you opened it."

Edmund set the rag down very carefully, the way his father set down heavy type — with the whole hand, no haste, nothing wasted. "I open every book I bind, Uncle Aldous. You taught me that. A binder who doesn't know what's inside his boards is a man selling pigs in sacks."

"What did you find?"

The silence lasted four seconds. Rennick counted them the way he counted everything now, in the currency of what Vasseur would pay for them.

"A letter," Edmund said. "Old wax, old hand. Nothing to do with me or mine."

"It named your family, lad. I know because I've known your father forty years, and I know what his silences are shaped like." Rennick took the chair by the press, uninvited, because standing made the shaking worse. "I'm not here to trick you. I'm too old and too fond of you to be clever. I'm going to tell you something true, and then I'm going to make you an offer, and you're going to hate me for both. But you'll be alive to hate me, which is more than the alternative buys."

Edmund did not sit. He stood beside the press with his arms folded, and Rennick saw Gideon in the stance so exactly that his throat closed. The same shoulders. The same stubborn architecture of a man built around a secret.

* * *

Here is what Rennick knew, assembled across twenty years the way a compositor assembles a page — one piece at a time, none of it meaningful until suddenly the whole locked together:

He knew that in the winter of '28, a young officer named Vasseur had come through the border city with a requisition and a smile, and that Gideon Dravenmoor had cut plates for him — fine work, secret work, the kind of work a master engraver does not put in his day-book. He knew that Gideon drank hard for a season afterward and never spoke of the army again.

He knew that when the war ground toward its end and old compromises became old crimes, someone had begun asking questions in certain rooms, and that Aldous Rennick, customs clerk, widower, godfather-by-affection to the Dravenmoor boy, had been asked to keep his ears open. He had said yes because they showed him a ledger of his own small smugglings — tobacco, mostly, and once a crate of unstamped pamphlets — and because refusal had a smell, and the smell was blood.

He knew that Vasseur wanted the armistice forgery resurrected now, at the signing, because a forged peace was worth more than a true one to men positioning themselves for whatever came after. And he knew — God help him, he had known since Tuesday — that the letter in the atlas was bait and summons both: Vasseur calling in a debt from a dying man, using the son as collateral.

He had told himself, for exactly one day, that informing was neutral. That knowledge merely passed along did no harm. Then he had seen the list of soldiers Vasseur planned to post at the print quarter gates, and understood that *custodians of forged documents* was a phrase that ended in confiscation, trial, and a rope, and that the rope would be measured for Edmund's neck as readily as Gideon's.

So here he was. Making his offer before the machinery he'd oiled arrived to do its grinding.

* * *

"There are men coming for those plates," Rennick said. "Not thieves. Soldiers. With papers. By week's end, martial law closes this quarter, and the first thing any occupying force seizes is the presses — always the presses, lad, because ink is the one weapon they can't counter. Your father will be questioned. You'll be questioned. And there exists, in a colonel's strongbox, a letter that makes your family look like traitors whether or not a single plate ever existed."

"We're not traitors."

"No," Rennick said. "You're worse off than that. Traitors chose. You inherited."

He leaned forward. This was the part he had rehearsed on the walk over, and rehearsal had not made it easier; it had made it clearer, which was worse.

"Vasseur wants the plates destroyed or delivered — it hardly matters which, so long as the evidence dies. I can get you out. Me, personally, my own cart, my own passes. I've moved unstamped goods through three checkpoints for eleven years; the sentries wave me through like weather. Thursday night, you and the plates ride north in a shipment of tallow, and by Friday you're across the river at Greyfriars, and the monastery is neutral ground, and whatever happens to this city happens without you in it." He spread his hands. "In exchange: silence. Not forever. Just now. You don't testify, you don't publish, you don't stand up at the signing with a martyr's speech. You let the old lie stay buried, because digging it up kills more people than it saves."

Edmund was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the windows. Somewhere upstairs, Gideon coughed — that deep, wet sound Rennick had been pretending not to hear all winter.

"The names," Edmund said finally. "The letter mentioned names. Men whose signatures were forged."

"Some of those men are ministers now," Rennick said. "Decent ones. If the forgery comes to light, they fall with it — guilty men, innocent men, it won't matter. The city doesn't want the truth, Edmund. The city wants the treaty. Give it the treaty. Let the dead keep their secrets; they've held them this long."

"That's not silence you're selling. That's amnesia. They're not the same price."

"They're the only currency accepted."

The boy turned away, walked the length of the shop, touched the type cases the way other men touch a rail in a storm. Rennick watched him and felt the full weight of what he was doing: he was asking this young man to become a smaller version of himself, permanently, in order to remain a version at all. It was, he thought, the standard bargain of every occupation and every marriage and every government he had ever known. It did not stop feeling filthy.

"My father sent for you?" Edmund asked suddenly.

"No."

"He knows you. He trusts you." Edmund faced him. "If I take your cart Thursday, he'll believe you betrayed us. He'll die believing it."

*And I may die regardless*, Rennick thought, because Vasseur did not leave loose ends, and an informant who arranged the escape of the evidence was a loose end with legs. He said none of this. A man could only spend so much truth in one visit.

"Gideon has believed worse of better people," he said instead. "Trust me on that."

Edmund came back to the chair and crouched in front of it, eye level, the way you'd address a stubborn child or a dying friend, and Rennick understood with terrible clarity that the boy was not deciding whether to accept. He was deciding how to refuse kindly.

"Why are you really here, Uncle Aldous?"

Because I informed on you. Because I informed on your father. Because I have been Vasseur's pen for eleven years and tonight I am trying to buy back one page of it. Because I love you, which is not a defense but is also not nothing.

"Because I'm an old man," Rennick said, "and old men know when a building is about to come down, and we like to get the young ones out first."

Edmund held his eyes. Behind the boy's face Rennick could see the whole argument assembling itself — the loyalty pulling one way, the conscience the other, and beneath both, faint as a watermark, the suspicion already taking root. He's too sharp, Rennick thought, with something like pride and something like despair. He'll work it out. He'll work out who told Vasseur about the atlas, about the customer, about the three weeks. And when he does —

"Thursday," Edmund said quietly. "Tallow cart. North to Greyfriars." He straightened. "Let me think on it. Come back tomorrow and I'll give you my answer."

There would be no answer worth having by tomorrow. Rennick knew it the way he knew bad paper by feel. But he nodded, retrieved his hat from the thirty-year peg, and paused at the door with the rain waiting behind him like a debt collector.

"Edmund. Whatever you decide — decide it knowing that some of us who hurt you will have loved you the whole time. It doesn't balance anything. But it's true."

The boy said nothing. Rennick stepped out into the rain and did not look back, because looking back was for men with clean hands, and his were the dirtiest in the city — ink-stained at one remove, forty years of Thursdays at this family's table, and every one of them now evidence.

Behind him, through the closing door, he heard Edmund call to the stairwell: "Father? I need to ask you about 1828."

Rennick pulled his collar up and walked fast, counting the hours until Thursday, and wondering — for the first time in eleven loyal-and-faithless years — which betrayal he would actually be able to live inside.
