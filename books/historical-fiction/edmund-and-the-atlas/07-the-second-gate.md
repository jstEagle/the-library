## 7. The Second Gate

The hymnals weighed nothing and everything. Forty bound volumes of psalms, each with a lead plate sewn flat against its spine beneath the pasteboard, and every one of them a small confession riding on Edmund's shoulder in a handcart.

He had counted the paces from the first gate to the second the night before, walking the route with a customer's ledger as an excuse. Nine hundred paces along the Rue de la Fontaine, past the shuttered coffeehouse where students had chalked *Liberté* on the boards two winters running. Then the street bent toward the river, and there it was: the second checkpoint, straddling the bridge approach like a cork in a bottle.

It was worse than the first. The first had been a bored sergeant and a brazier. This one had barricades — actual barricades, wagon beds upended and sandbagged, with lanterns hung on poles so the soldiers could work through the dusk. And it had an officer. Young, straight-backed, gloves white against a blue coat that still had the crease of its folding in it. A boy playing at war, Edmund thought, and then corrected himself: boys playing at war were the dangerous ones. They hadn't yet learned which questions didn't matter.

*You are a printer's man delivering stock.* He made his face into that sentence and pushed the cart forward.

"Name and business."

"Edmund Dravenmoor, Dravenmoor & Son, printers, Chandlers' Row." The lie about the firm being father and son sat oddly on his tongue; it had been only ever father and apprentice once, and now barely father at all. "Delivery to the Monastery of Saint-Aubin. Liturgical order."

The sergeant wanted the manifest and nothing else. Edmund gave it. The sergeant held it upside down for a moment before turning it, and Edmund felt something almost like pity. The man would stamp anything tonight; the revolution had taught the rank and file that paperwork was how you survived, not how you won.

But the officer came around the brazier with the unhurried step of someone who had all evening and knew it.

"Hymnals," he said. "To Saint-Aubin."

"That's correct, sir."

"How many?"

"Forty."

"For how many brothers?"

Edmund blinked, as if the question were strange. "I wouldn't know, sir. I print what's ordered. Father Anselme placed the order through our agent."

"Mm." The officer picked up one of the hymnals from the top of the stack. He turned it over in his gloved hands. He did not open it — thank God, thank God — but ran his thumb along the spine as though he could read binding quality by touch, which, being an officer's son or a bookseller's, perhaps he could. "Saint-Aubin," he said again. "That's the monastery on the Vaucelles road."

"The same."

The officer looked up. His eyes were pale gray, very steady, and Edmund understood with a cold, sliding certainty that this was the moment, that it had already arrived, that it had been waiting for him since he'd sewn the first plate.

"The Vaucelles road," the officer said, "is closed. Has been since the twelfth of last month. And Saint-Aubin—" he set the hymnal down on the barricade with a small, deliberate click, "—burned on the ninth. You're delivering forty hymnals to ashes, Mr. Dravenmoor."

* * *

Later — in whatever later he would be permitted, if any — Edmund would try to reconstruct the half-second in which his mind went white. There was no thought in it. Only the sensation of the ground opening under the sentence *replacement stock*, which is what his mouth said while his brain stood empty as an unlocked case.

"Replacement stock," he said. "Yes."

The officer waited. That was the terrible thing: he simply waited, hands clasped behind him, giving Edmund rope by the yard.

"The monastery is being rebuilt," Edmund said. "The brothers are lodged at the seminary on the far bank — they kept the sacristy records, or enough of them. Father Anselme wants the new hymnals ready for the church when it's reconsecrated. He asked that they be sent across the river now, so they'd be out of the way of the fires here." He heard himself add, reasonably, "Nobody burns a book twice, sir."

Silence. A soldier behind the barricade laughed at something and cut it off when the officer glanced over.

"You crossed the first checkpoint with this load."

"At the Rue Neuve gate. Yes, sir."

"And the sergeant there raised no objection."

"He inspected the manifest, sir. As yours has."

The officer smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which somehow made it worse. "Sergeant Boileau. A good man. Inspects everything, does Boileau. Manifests, cart axles, boot soles if he's in the mood." He picked up the hymnal again. "Strange, though. I passed through Rue Neuve myself an hour ago and Boileau told me about a printer's cart. Said the driver's hands shook so badly he nearly dropped the pass."

The brazier popped. Sparks climbed the dark like escaping thoughts.

"Cold night," Edmund said.

"It is."

"My hands are always cold, sir. Composing rooms do it. Twenty years of lead type and wash spirits — the feeling goes out of the fingers and never comes back." He held his hands out, palms up, steady now — steadier than they had any right to be, steadier than they'd been at Rue Neuve, as if the fear had burned off and left only craft behind. "Feel them if you like. A surgeon once called them a dead man's hands. He meant it kindly."

The officer studied the hands. Then the face above them. Edmund let himself be studied — let the tiredness show, the ink in the knuckle-cracks, the ordinary poverty of a tradesman out after curfew with a heavy cart — because the alternative was to hide, and hiding was a scent, and this boy would follow a scent.

"Dravenmoor," the officer said. "Chandlers' Row. Yours is the shop with the old atlas sign."

"We are known by our sign, sir."

"My father bought his law books from Chandlers' Row once. Years ago." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "He said old Dravenmoor was the finest compositor east of the river. Said he could set a page blindfolded, by feel alone." The officer set the hymnal back on the stack, squared its edges with two fingers, precisely, the way a man squares papers he intends to keep. "Well. Your stock can't cross tonight. Bridge is closed to commercial traffic until morning inspection — general's orders, nothing personal."

Edmund's heart performed a slow, enormous turn over. "Then I'll—"

"You'll take my escort," the officer said pleasantly. "Corporal Vidal will walk your cart to wherever it's stored tonight, and collect it at dawn for the official inspection. Standard procedure for goods flagged as irregular. Where is the stock going, Mr. Dravenmoor? Some warehouse near the seminary?"

"A friend of the order keeps a cellar. Rue des Tanneurs."

"Tannery row." The officer wrote the address in a pocketbook without appearing to hurry. "Vidal will see you safe there. And I'll look in at the inspection tomorrow — I confess I've developed an interest in hymnals. One hears so few good ones lately."

* * *

They walked. The corporal was maybe thirty, gray at the temples, and mercifully talkative in the weary way of men who had been standing at checkpoints all week. He talked about his feet, the coffee at Rue Neuve, a woman in the rue aux Ours, the price of candles. Edmund answered in monosyllables and let the man's voice fill the street, because his own head was too loud.

*He knows. Or he doesn't know, but he suspects, and suspicion is worse — knowledge can be bought, suspected must be followed.*

Rue des Tanneurs was real. That much was true; he'd chosen it precisely because it existed, a tallow-stinker of a street where a man named Courbet rented cellar space to anyone with coin and no questions. But the plates couldn't stay there. Dawn inspection would find them. He had planned three gates and a sanctuary and now the plan had sprouted a tail — a polite, gray-eyed tail who intended to watch forty hymnals until they betrayed themselves.

The plates had to move tonight. All forty. Out of the hymnals, into something a dawn search would wave through, and gone before the officer's boots came down the cellar stairs.

Which left the third gate. Which left Rennick, who was supposed to meet him past the bridge with papers that would carry the plates the last league to the monastery — the true monastery, the one still standing, where Brother Anselme (a fiction, a name borrowed from a funeral twelve years ago) would receive them into neutral stone.

Except Rennick's papers would now be worthless. Worse — an officer would be watching, and Rennick, dear God, Rennick with his warm hands and his twenty years of Sunday dinners — Rennick would have to improvise, and Edmund had never seen Aldous Rennick improvise in his life.

The corporal stopped. "Here we are. Courbet's, the green door. You want me to help unload?"

"No need, corporal. My back's my own problem."

He said it lightly. He paid the man a small courtesy — a coin for the brazier fund, soldiers always remembered coins — and watched him amble back toward the bridge. Then Edmund wheeled the empty-weighted cart down the alley, knocked the green door, and stood in the dark doing sums.

Dawn was seven hours away. Unsewing forty spines took perhaps four minutes each if his fingers held — call it two hours and a half. Repacking the plates into the false bottom of the cart took another hour. That left three hours to reach Rennick's lodgings, explain why the plan had changed, and invent a new way past a gate now manned by a young officer with a pocketbook and an interest in hymnals.

Three hours. His father lay fevered on Chandlers' Row with a secret twenty years old, and somewhere across the city Colonel Vasseur's man was counting the hours too.

The green door opened. Courbet, candle in fist, peered out at him with the face of a man interrupted at supper.

"You're the cellar," Edmund said, and pushed the cart inside.

Behind him, far off toward the river, a whistle sounded — short, short, long. Checkpoint signal. Passing the word along the line of gates like type moving down a composing stick, letter by letter toward the page:

A cart. A printer. Watch him.
