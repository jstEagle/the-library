## 8. Loyalty's Ledger

The fever came in waves, and Gideon Dravenmoor learned to ride them the way he'd once ridden the press — counting the rhythm, waiting for the trough. In the troughs he could think. In the peaks he was back in Vasseur's tent with the rain coming down on canvas and the colonel's voice soft as tallow.

He lay in the back room above the shop, in the bed where his wife had died, and listened to the sounds of the house below him. The clatter of type. Edmund's footsteps, quick and light, crossing from the cases to the stone. His son still worked. That was good. Work would hold the boy together until — well. Until.

The candle guttered. Someone had left water and a cloth. He drank what he could and watched the ceiling stains spread like continents on a map no one would ever print.

He had been rehearsing this confession for twenty years. He had never expected to deliver it sober, or to an audience, or while his hands shook too badly to demonstrate the one thing that mattered: how a man cuts letters into steel so fine that a lie can pass for scripture at arm's length.

Edmund came up after midnight. Gideon heard him pause on the landing — heard him gather himself, the way you square a forme before locking it. Then the door opened and his son stood there with the lamplight behind him, twenty-three years old and carrying the atlas under his arm like a brick he intended to throw through something.

"You read it," Gideon said.

"I read it."

"Then sit down, and I'll tell you the parts the letter doesn't."

* * *

"In the winter of '28 I was twenty-nine years old and I thought the worst thing a printer could face was debt." Gideon's voice was a dry rasp; he stopped every few sentences to work moisture into his mouth. "I owed money to three suppliers and my father had just died leaving me this shop and a name nobody respected outside the printing quarter. Colonel Vasseur came to me on a Tuesday. I remember it was a Tuesday because Tuesdays we washed the stones.

"He was courteous. That's what I want you to understand first, Edmund, because everything else follows from it. He didn't threaten me. He complimented my kerning. He said he'd seen the memorial broadside I'd printed for the veterans' fund and that the cutting was the finest he'd encountered north of the river. Then he set a document on my composing stone and asked me what I thought of the hand that wrote it.

"It was a field dispatch. An order concerning prisoner exchanges along the eastern corridor. And even then, half-blind with worry about paper costs, I could see it wasn't right. The seal was genuine — cut from a genuine impression, wax softened and re-laid — but the text beneath it had been written by someone imitating the secretary's italic, and doing it competently, not perfectly.

"'It's a copy,' I said.

"'It will be,' he said.

"They needed plates. Not one plate — a sequence. Dispatches, orders, a proposed armistice instrument, all bearing authentic seals, all saying things that had never been said. A war conducted on paper. Men moved by documents that were ghosts. And when the war ended — as wars do, messily, unsatisfyingly — those ghosts would become history. Whoever held the plates could produce the record. Could prove anything. Could unmake anyone.

"Vasseur chose me because I was desperate and because desperation is invisible in a craftsman. People expect a printer to be poor. They expect him to take commissions. I told myself it was typesetting, that the steel would do its work whether or not my heart was in it, that a chisel has no politics.

"Your mother was alive. You were two years old. I did it for eleven weeks."

He had to stop there. The fever surged and he let it, floating on it for a while, and when he surfaced Edmund had not moved. His son sat very straight on the stool with the atlas on his knees, and his face in the candlelight was unreadable, which frightened Gideon more than anger would have. Anger he could answer. This careful stillness was the craftsman's face — the look of a man assessing damage before deciding whether a sheet is worth salvaging.

"There are names," Gideon said. "That's what I have to make you understand. The plates aren't just text and seals. Vasseur required authentication — corroborating endorsements, officers' signatures reproduced alongside the forged instruments. Eleven men signed their complicity by letting me cut their hands into steel. Some did it knowingly. Most did what I did: told themselves the plate was only a tool. Two of them I knew personally. Aldous Rennick's own brother-in-law, if you can credit it.

"And here is the part I've paid for every night since. When the commission was done, Vasseur ordered me to deliver the full set to a courier in the Salt Quarter. Eleven crates' worth of history, packed in oiled paper. And I delivered nine."

* * *

"I kept two," Gideon said. "The armistice instrument and its endorsement sheet. The two that mattered most — the two that would have made the whole fiction credible, because they bore the signature of a general who later became a minister, and the countersignature of a civil commissioner who sits in government today as an honest man, Edmund, as far as I know an honest man, who may have signed under the same coercion I cut under, or worse.

"I hid them inside the atlas. Your mother's idea, though she never knew she'd had it — I sewed them into the binding of the most boring book I owned, a surveyor's atlas nobody had opened since purchase, and I told myself I would destroy them within the year.

"Twenty years, Edmund. Every year I meant to burn them and every year I found a reason. Fear, at first — destroying them proved nothing, since Vasseur had impressions of his own. Then something worse happened: the men on that endorsement sheet began to distinguish themselves. The general argued for supply reform. The commissioner negotiated prisoner releases. Their names became attached to decent things. And I understood that burning the plates wouldn't protect them anymore — it would only erase the one piece of evidence that could ever show *how* their names got onto forged papers in the first place. Or, turned around: the one piece of proof that could hang them, if the wrong person held it and the right moment came.

"So I sat on it. Like a dragon on a hoard I couldn't spend and couldn't abandon. Your mother thought I had debts. She died thinking your father was merely a bad businessman, and I let her believe it because it was kinder than the truth.

"And now Vasseur wants them back. The letter tells me why, though it pretends otherwise. The war is ending. There will be a treaty, and treaties require records, and records require provenance. If Vasseur produces his nine plates plus my two, he owns the peace. He can authenticate any lie he likes about how this war ended and who agreed to what. If the plates vanish — if they burn tonight, in this shop, in this stove — then his forgery stays incomplete. Suspicious. Deniable. The treaty stands on honest paper because the dishonest paper never existed."

Gideon reached out. His hand shook so badly that Edmund had to meet it halfway, and father gripped son's wrist with the strength the fever had left him, which was not much, but was enough.

"Burn them. Take them out of the binding and put them in the stove downstairs and stand there until they're ash and the ash is cold. Don't read them again. Don't copy them. Don't weigh it, Edmund — weighing is what I did for twenty years and it bought nothing but this bed.

"I know what I'm asking. Those men on the endorsement sheet — some of them built real things afterward. Real schools, real reforms, real pardons. Burn the plates and none of that is touched. Keep them, carry them, testify with them, and you don't know — you *cannot* know — what you'll break. Truth is a solvent, boy. It cleans brass and it dissolves solder, and there is no telling, once you pour it, which joints were holding up the roof.

"Loyalty isn't a sentiment. It's a ledger. I've kept mine twenty years in the red, and the interest is due, and I am asking you — I am begging you, my son — to close the account quietly. Let the dead men keep their names. Let the living ones keep their works. Let the Dravenmoors go on being obscure, because obscurity was the only honest thing we ever owned."

His voice failed. He lay back against the pillows, spent, and the fever took the edges of the room and made them swim. For a long moment there was only the sound of the candle and, below, the muffled tick of the cooling press.

Edmund rose. He picked up the atlas. He crossed to the bed and kissed his father's forehead — dry, hot, papery — and Gideon felt him hesitate there, lips pressed to skin, and in that hesitation heard twenty years of his own silence answered.

"I have to think," Edmund said quietly. "There are three gates between here and the monastery, and I promised you I'd carry them. But I promise you this instead: whatever I decide, I decide it as a printer. I'll set the truth the way it should be set — cleanly, with proper leading, both sides justified. No line dropped. No line added."

He went out. Gideon listened to his footsteps descend the stairs, cross the shop, stop.

Then, through the floorboards, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Gideon knew better than his own heartbeat: the lifting of the press cover. The slide of a heavy forme onto the stone.

And underneath it, almost lost, the scrape of the street door being unbolted — hours before dawn, hours before the first checkpoint would open, and voices below, two of them, low and familiar, one of them laughing the way old friends laugh when the trap is already sprung.
