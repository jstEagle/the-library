## 9. The Third Gate

The third gate was not a gate at all. It was the Treaty Hall itself.

Edmund had planned for a barricade, a desk, a bored sergeant with a ledger. What he found instead was the whole apparatus of peace assembled under one roof: long tables draped in green cloth, delegates from four delegations murmuring over final drafts, clerks with ink-stained cuffs, soldiers standing along the walls with fixed bayonets that caught the light of a hundred candles. The armistice would be signed at noon. It was now eleven minutes to.

He came in through the delivery entrance, as he had at every gate, pushing the handcart of hymnals with the plates sewn into their spines — his father's last design, the one trick Gideon had taught him in a fever-haze whisper at three that morning. *Bind them into something holy. No soldier searches God.* The first two gates had believed him because he had looked like what he was: a printer's son, tired, unremarkable, carrying books.

The third gate had believed him too. That was the problem. A clerk had waved him toward the hall's rear corridor — *leave the shipment for the chaplain's office* — and now Edmund stood in a stone passage that smelled of candle wax and wet wool, thirty feet from where history was being written, holding the thing that could unwrite it.

All he had to do was set down the cart and walk away.

His father's voice was still in his ears, thin and cracking from the fever: *Burn them, Edmund. The men on those plates have families now. Some of them are in that room. Bury it, and we all live.*

And beneath it, older, quieter, the voice of the type case itself — twenty years of sorting lead in the dark, of learning that every letter matters, that a single transposed character can turn a treaty into a lie or a lie into evidence. His father had cut these plates under coercion. But forgery did not stop being forgery when the forger felt bad about it afterward.

Edmund set his hands flat on the hymnals and breathed.

*You are not hiding anymore.*

"Master Dravenmoor."

The voice came from behind him, warm as ever, and it went through him like a spike through the palm.

Aldous Rennick stepped out of the shadow of the corridor arch, hat in one hand, cane in the other, smiling the smile that had presided over a thousand dinners at the Dravenmoor table. Behind him, at a discreet distance, stood the young officer from the second checkpoint — Captain Lefevre, his cap immaculate, his eyes narrow with the particular satisfaction of a man who has finished arithmetic.

"You look pale," Rennick said gently. "Long morning?"

"The longest."

"I imagine so." Rennick glanced at the cart, and his smile deepened by a degree. "Hymnals. You do love your little jokes. May I?" He didn't wait for permission. He lifted the top book, ran his thumb along the spine, and felt the ridge beneath the cloth. Something flickered across his face — regret, perhaps, genuine and useless — before the smile reassembled itself. "There they are. Twenty years, and there they are."

"You knew," Edmund said. "Before I found them. You sewed the letter into my father's atlas."

"I delivered a message, nothing more. Vasseur wanted his property back." Rennick's voice stayed soft, confiding, the voice of the man who had once carried young Edmund on his shoulders through the harvest fair. "Listen to me, boy. In ten minutes that treaty is signed, the war is over, the city is saved, and nobody is hanged. All you must do is hand me this cart and walk out through the chapel door. Your father's debts die with his name. Your press survives. I will see to it personally — new type, new premises, anything you need. I have loved your family since before you could read. Do you believe that?"

"That's what makes it unforgivable," Edmund said.

Rennick's face tightened. "Pragmatism isn't betrayal."

"It is when it wears my mother's tablecloth to dinner." Edmund's hands were shaking again, but this time he let them. He lifted one hymnal from the cart and held it up between them like a relic. "You want silence. My father wants silence. Half the men whose names are cut into this lead want silence. And I have spent six days discovering that my entire life was built on top of a lie everyone agreed to keep. So no. No safe passage. No new press. Take the cart if you can."

Lefevre stepped forward, hand on his holster. "That would be theft of military evidence, Master Dravenmoor. Or treason, depending on how Colonel Vasseur prefers to frame it at breakfast."

"And what," Edmund asked, "does he call it when he frames it at a signing?"

Lefevre frowned. Rennick said, sharply, "Edmund—"

But Edmund was already moving. Not away. Through.

He walked past them both, down the corridor, pushing the cart ahead of him with the rattling, unstoppable momentum of a man who has stopped deciding and started doing. Lefevre grabbed for his shoulder; Edmund twisted aside and the captain stumbled against the cart, and then they were through the archway and into the light of the great hall, and a hundred faces turned.

The murmur died. Bayonets shifted. At the head table, the signatories rose — ministers, generals, the gray-bearded mediator from the neutral cantons — and among them, at the far end, sat Colonel Vasseur, resplendent in dress uniform, attached to the delegation as an *advisor on border administration*. He had aged well. He looked like a portrait of himself.

Edmund stopped the cart in the center of the hall, in the aisle between the tables, and discovered that his voice, that quiet, workshop-trained voice which had never in twenty-two years risen above the sound of the composing stick, was suddenly enormous in the silence.

"Gentlemen. Before you sign — you should know what you are signing."

"Remove him," someone said.

"No." It was the mediator, the old man from the cantons, raising one hand. He had the stillness of someone who had ended enough wars to smell one being started dishonestly. "Let him speak. One minute, young man."

Edmund reached into the hymnal's spine and drew out the first plate. He held it high, lead catching candlelight, reversed letters gleaming like a mirror script — and even upside down, even backwards, the men at the front tables who had spent their lives reading documents read it. He saw it land on them. He saw the color leave three separate faces.

"This is a printing plate," he said. "Cut twenty years ago in my father's shop on Cooper's Lane. It is one of seven. Together they print an armistice — a false one, backdated, with terms more generous than any actually agreed. It was commissioned under threat by Colonel Vasseur" — he turned, and pointed, and his finger did not shake — "*that man*, who intended to produce a peace that never happened and hold every signatory hostage to its phantom terms. My father refused to deliver it. He hid the plates instead, and spent twenty years dying slowly of the secret. Yesterday Colonel Vasseur moved to reclaim them, through Aldous Rennick, who stands behind me and whom I considered an uncle until five minutes ago."

Rennick's voice cracked across the hall: "This is a sick boy's fantasy — Gideon Dravenmoor is a forger, whatever the boy claims about coercion—"

"He admits the forgery!" Edmund shouted, and the word rang off the vaulted ceiling. "Ask him why. Ask him who ordered it. Pull the seventh plate from this cart and compare it to the draft on your table, gentlemen — the clause numbering matches Vasseur's private correspondence. I'll wager the colonel's own seal is referenced in the authentication block. Print one proof. Just one. Lead does not lie the way men do."

Silence. The terrible, total silence of powerful men recalculating.

Then the mediator said, quietly, "Captain. Secure that cart. And secure Colonel Vasseur's papers."

Vasseur rose. For one moment the mask was gone and Edmund saw what his father must have seen twenty years ago in a lamplit workroom: not a soldier but a speculator, cold and quick, weighing odds. The odds, just now, were collapsing.

"There is an explanation," Vasseur began.

"There always is," said the mediator. "You may give it at trial."

They took him between two grenadiers, and he passed within six feet of Edmund, close enough that Edmund smelled his cologne, and Vasseur looked at him — not with hatred, Edmund would remember later, but with appraisal, as if pricing him — and said nothing at all.

Rennick did not run. That was the last thing Edmund expected and the thing he would think about most. Rennick straightened his coat, collected his hat and cane, and looked at Edmund with an expression stripped of everything warm, and underneath it something raw and old and grieving.

"Your father would have kept quiet," Rennick said. "It would have been kinder to everyone."

"He tried," Edmund said. "It killed him slower."

Lefevre took Rennick's arm — gently, almost respectfully — and led him away down the same corridor Edmund had entered by. The hymnals were impounded onto a side table, a clerk already bending over their split spines with widening eyes.

The mediator approached Edmund last. Up close his face was a map of fine lines, and his eyes were tired and exact.

"You understand," he said, "that you have just accused a serving officer before four delegations. There will be an inquiry. You will testify under oath. Your family's part in the forgery will be examined in public, in detail, and no one — least of all me — will be able to protect your father's name from what is found."

"I know."

"And you did it anyway."

Edmund looked at the cart, empty now of secrets, ordinary again — just books, just paper and thread. Somewhere beyond the walls, the bells of the printing quarter were ringing the quarter hour, the same bells that had measured his whole life in deadlines and deliveries.

"My father taught me the trade," he said. "First rule of setting type: you cannot fix a page by hiding the wrong letter. You have to take the line apart. All of it. However long it takes."

The mediator studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, the old man almost smiled.

"The inquiry convenes Thursday," he said. "Be prepared to name every man whose name appears on those plates. Including the ones you'd rather spare."
