## 10. Reset Type

The trial ended on a Tuesday in April, and by Wednesday the city had already begun to forget it.

Edmund understood why. Wars end in signatures; trials end in sentences read aloud by men in dusty robes, and a sentence about paper and lead type will never carry the drama of cannon fire. The tribunal's verdict — that the Armistice of Hallerin Bridge had been forged twenty years ago to prolong a war that should have ended, that Colonel Augustin Vasseur had commissioned the forgery and concealed it through two decades of promotions and commendations — was printed in every paper in the quarter. Edmund read his own testimony set in someone else's type and felt strangely outside it, as though the man who had stood before the signatories and spoken was a character he had once set in columns of eight-point body text.

Vasseur fell quietly. No spectacle, no mob at the gates. Stripped of rank, remanded for sentencing, led out through a side door while the crowd outside waited for something more satisfying than a fat old man in a plain coat looking at nobody. The war he had lengthened by six years ended eleven days after the signing — an honest armistice this time, its pages clean, its names true.

The papers called it *the Dravenmoor Affair*. That was the part Edmund could not get used to. For thirty years the family name had been worth exactly what a print shop was worth: little, local, unremarked. Now strangers said it aloud in bakeries. Now it meant something, and Edmund had not yet decided whether what it meant was a wound or a gift.

His father never learned the answer either. Gideon Dravenmoor died in February, in a bed borrowed from the monastery at Saint-Aubrin, with Edmund's hand around his and the fever finally burning itself out like a lamp running dry of oil. His last coherent words were not about plates or forgeries or the men whose names he had protected for twenty years. They were about kerning. *"You always spaced your e's too wide,"* he said, and closed his eyes, and that was the whole of the inheritance passed on: a criticism, which is to say a lesson, which is to say love, in the language of the trade.

Edmund spaced his e's carefully after that.

* * *

Rennick left in March, on a northbound packet out of the river docks, and did not look back at the quay. Edmund knew because he went to watch, though he could not have said why.

Aldous Rennick had escaped prosecution the way clever men escape things — sideways, through technicalities, through the simple fact that offering a bribe that was refused leaves no evidence worth a tribunal's time. But the quarter knew. Printers know everything; it is the occupational disease of people who handle other people's words. Rennick's own firm declined to renew his partnership. His club returned his key by post. And so he emigrated, disgrace folded into his luggage alongside the fine coats, bound for some colonial port where no one would know his name and he would have to build a new lie from the ground up.

He had seen Edmund standing at the rail's end. Of course he had. He had paused — ten seconds, maybe fifteen — and for a moment the old face surfaced through the ruined one: the man who had brought sweetmeats for a boy sorting type, who had argued politics with Gideon over cheap wine for twenty years, who had genuinely, ruinously loved them both.

Then he had boarded, and Edmund had gone home, and had not thought about him again until now, writing this, setting these very lines in his mind the way he set all his lines now: slowly, checking each letter twice.

* * *

The shop on Vellum Street was ash when they finally let him back into the district. The fire had started the night of the signing — Rennick's doing, everyone assumed, though nothing was ever proven, and Edmund had stopped needing it proven. Some accounts you close yourself.

He stood in the ruins in May with his coat collar up against the drizzle and took inventory the way his father would have: the stone floor survivable, the foundation sound, the iron frame of the big press warped past saving but the screw still good. Three cases of type had been in the cellar. Two had cracked when the floor above collapsed. The third came up intact, blackened at the edges, its compartments full of burnt paper and water, and Edmund sat on a broken beam for four hours sorting it letter by letter, because grief needs a task or it turns on you.

When he finished, he had perhaps forty percent of a working font. Enough to print half of anything.

He laughed until he cried, alone in the rubble, holding a lowercase *a* between thumb and forefinger like a relic.

* * *

He rebuilt smaller, and he rebuilt under his own terms, and both decisions surprised the neighbors more than the rebuilding itself.

The new shop went up on Ashline Row — named, the landlord admitted cheerfully, for the fires of last year, which everyone agreed was either grim or fitting depending on temperament — with room for one press instead of three, two apprentices instead of nine, and a window facing east so the morning light fell across the composing stone. He financed it with the modest indemnity the city paid the printing quarter and a loan from the guild, co-signed, unexpectedly, by Madame Ostrer of the Saint-Aubrin monastery, who had accepted a crate of hymnals from a nervous young man at a checkpoint one year ago and asked no questions until it was safe to ask all of them.

"You understand," she'd said, signing the guarantee, "that I am investing in a reputation, not a business."

"Then we're even," Edmund said. "I'm repaying a silence, not a loan."

The first job off the new press was, at his insistence, nothing grand. Not a pamphlet, not a manifesto, not his own account of the affair, which two publishers had offered good money for and which he declined with a sentence he'd rehearsed: *The truth doesn't need my version. It got the real one.* The first job was parish registers for Saint-Aubrin. Baptisms, marriages, burials. Names, dates, honest entries in honest columns. He set every line himself and pulled the first proof by hand and held it up to the eastern light and found it good.

The second job was hymnals. Of course it was.

* * *

One evening in September, a year almost to the day from the night he'd carried the plates out of Vellum Street sewn into a false bottom of a hymnal crate, Edmund stayed late to lock up and found an apprentice's mistake waiting at the stone: a page set backwards, mirror-wise, every letter reversed.

The old Edmund — the one from before — would have corrected it silently at dawn and said nothing, sparing the boy the embarrassment, keeping the small failure hidden as failures had been hidden in his family for twenty years. Paper over it. Burn what explains too much.

Instead he left the reversed page pinned above the composing stone all week, and when Pieter finally noticed it and went scarlet, Edmund gathered the apprentices around and taught them the lesson his father had never gotten to teach him properly:

"The type doesn't lie," he said. "It can't. You can put bad words in it — men do, God knows men do — but the letters themselves only ever say exactly what you set them to say. Your work is your word. Set it so you can stand behind every line."

Pieter nodded. The other two nodded. And Edmund looked at the reversed page — *the truth doesn't need my version* printed backward, gibberish to anyone who didn't hold it to a mirror — and understood suddenly what his father must have felt, twenty years ago, staring down at plates that said one thing while the world was being told another. Gideon hadn't hidden them out of cowardice. He'd hidden them because he couldn't make himself be the mirror that flipped the truth.

Edmund could. Edmund had. That was the whole difference, and it had cost a shop and a name and a father, and he would pay it again, and knowing that — the exact weight of the price, weighed honestly — was what peace turned out to be. Not the absence of cost. Just the certainty of the exchange rate.

* * *

In October, a letter arrived without a return address, postmarked from a port city on the other side of the world. Edmund held it a long time over the composing stone, feeling its weight, thin as it was.

He knew the handwriting before he broke the seal. He'd set enough of Rennick's notes to Gideon over the years to recognize that careful, clerkish hand anywhere — a hand that belonged to a man who had always written more honestly than he lived.

The letter was short. *No apology,* it began, *because you would smell a false one, and I haven't the courage to write a true one.* It reported, in the manner of an old friend making conversation across an uncrossable distance, the weather, the price of paper in the colonies, the strange abundance of oranges. Then, near the bottom, four lines that Edmund read five times:

*I have taken work with a printer here. A small shop. I find I am good at it, which shames me more than the rest of it does. Tell me if there is anything I may send you. Tell me nothing, if that is your answer. Both are fair.*

Edmund set the letter down on the stone beside the sorted type, among the hundreds of small lead letters that said only what you made them say.

Then he took a fresh sheet, inked a pen, and began to write his reply.
