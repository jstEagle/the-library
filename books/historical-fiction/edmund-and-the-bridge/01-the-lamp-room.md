## 1. The Lamp Room

The wind at Greyhaven Point had a voice, and in the winter of 1747 it spoke mostly of endings.

Odile Brightwater climbed the ninety-one steps of the lighthouse with a can of oil in one hand and a lantern trimmed for the evening in the other, counting them as she always did—not because she needed to, but because the count was a kind of company. Ninety-one steps, and every one of them worn smooth at the center by forty years of keepers' boots, her husband's among them, hers now alone. At the top she set down her burden, unhooked the leather flap from the glazing, and let the gale have its argument with the glass.

"Patience," she told it, as though the storm could hear. "I'm coming."

The lamp room smelled of hot brass and whale oil and the faint sweetness of char, a smell so familiar that on the rare occasions she left the Point—to buy flour at Millbeck, once a year, to sit in a pew at a funeral—she found the world outside smelled of nothing at all. Nothing worth the breathing. She opened the reservoir, checked the level with a knuckle dipped quick as a cook testing milk, and began the small liturgy of the light.

Trim the wicks. Wipe the burners. Polish the reflector until it threw back a face.

That last part she had never grown used to. The great silvered mirror caught her whenever she leaned close—a thin woman of thirty-six with salt-grey coming in early at the temples, eyes the color of a winter channel, hands cracked at the knuckles from cold water and lamp acid. Thomas used to joke that the reflector kept him honest; he'd see himself in it, scowling over some stubborn fitting, and laugh at his own sourness. Odile saw only what was missing. A face like a house with one window dark.

She worked anyway. That was the whole secret of the thing, if anyone had asked her—and no one asked, because no one came—the whole secret of surviving a life pared down to its studs: you worked. You worked precisely, and with pride, because precision was the only prayer the sea had ever answered.

The wicks took her longest. Too long and they smoked the glass amber, dimming the beam that the *Providence* out of Kingsport would need come nightfall, or the herring fleet working down from the north, or any one of a dozen hulls that trusted without knowing it to the white eye burning above Greyhaven Point. Too short and the flame guttered. So she trimmed them with scissors honed on the same stone she used for her kitchen knives, snipping by feel more than sight, and when she had done she stood back and lit the lamp and watched the flame stand up straight and clean as a soldier at attention.

"There," she said. To the lamp. To the room. To the gulls wheeling past the gallery rail with their cries like torn linen. "There you are."

The light swung out across the dusk, and the dusk gave way.

* * *

She logged the weather the way monks copy scripture: faithfully, without ornament, twice a day and more when the sky turned traitor. The logbook lived on the desk in the round stone chamber below the lamp, and it was full now—three years of her hand since Thomas, four before that in his, the pages turning gradually from his square confident script to her narrow slanted one, the way grief turns a shared bed cold by degrees.

*Dec. 3rd.* Wind NW, freshening. Glass falling since noon. Sea high on the outer ledges. Gulls keeping inland. *Expect weather.*

She sat with her pen lifted after writing it, looking at those two words. *Expect weather.* Her father-in-law had written the same, and his father before him, she supposed, in whatever log had governed this coast before the Brightwaters took the light. There were only so many things the sea did, and only so many ways to say them. Wind rose. Wind fell. Ships came home or did not.

Three years ago this month the log read, in her own hand, *Fine clear night. Light burning well.* And eleven miles south, on a fine clear night with the light burning well, a merchant brig called the *Merrow* had struck the Teeth with all her canvas set and gone to pieces in under an hour, and Thomas Brightwater, who had signed aboard at Kingsport because the lighthouse paid too little to cover his mother's debts, had drowned within sight of the tower he'd been born under.

Within sight of it. Odile had learned later—hearing it from a fisherman who'd been on the headland that night, hearing it secondhand the way all the worst things come—that some of the men from the wreck washed ashore near the Point itself. That Thomas might have been carried past these very rocks while she stood here trimming wicks and logging weather and believing, in the stupid confidence of a fine clear night, that all was well.

She had not missed a watch since. Not for fever, not for the funeral she'd refused to hold until spring, not for anything. If the sea meant to take another ship off Greyhaven Point it would have to do it with the light blazing in its teeth and her name entered in the log beside it.

The gulls knew her schedule better than God did. At dusk they gathered on the gallery rail, six or seven of them, hunched into the wind with their yellow eyes narrowed against the salt spray, waiting. She went up with the day's scraps—fish heads from young Pellow's boat, a heel of bread gone hard, bacon rind saved three days for exactly this—and scattered it along the stone.

"Greed," she told them, as they screamed and fought. "Every one of you. No gratitude in a single feathered breast."

The largest, a ragged old bird she called the Admiral because he stood apart from the squabbling and merely watched, fixed her with his pale eye and said nothing. He never said anything. He simply appeared at the rail each dusk and departed each dusk, punctual as tide-tables, and Odile had long ago stopped pretending she didn't look for him.

"You'll get yours," she said. She always said it. She never followed through.

The wind was rising. She could feel it in the stonework now, a low hum in the tower like a note held on an organ, and the spray was coming over the gallery instead of merely striking it. Out east the horizon had gone the color of a bruise, purple-black under a thin band of dying gold, and the sea beneath it had changed its mind about being gray and turned instead the deep wrong green that meant deep water moving shallow.

Odile stood at the rail with the empty scrap-bucket hanging forgotten from her hand and looked at that green water for a long time.

Then she went inside, bolted the door, and wrote in the log: *Glass falling fast. Sea making up from the eastward. Expect a gale.*

* * *

The letter came two days later, and it came by the last possible hand.

Winter mail reached Greyhaven Point irregularly at best; the post rider left parcels at Millbeck tollhouse and let the fishermen ferry them out as inclination struck, which meant letters arrived damp, delayed, or not at all. This one came out with young Pellow on the morning after the glass had begun its fall, the boy running up the causeway path with his oilskin snapping like a flag, shouting before he was even in earshot.

"Miz Brightwater! Miz Brightwater, I've a letter—harbor master's compliments—from Kingsport!"

Kingsport. The word landed in her chest the way stones land in still water. Nothing good had ever come to her out of Kingsport. She wiped her hands on her apron twice, though they were already clean, and took the packet from him.

It was sealed with the harbor office's stamp, and it was short. Harbor Master Dunmore's handwriting, brisk and slanting, the hand of a man who wrote fifty such letters a year:

*Mrs. Brightwater — By order of the Board. Barometer observations & masters' reports concur. The first great blow of the season will make this coast within thirty hours, likely by nightfall tomorrow. Expect SE gale rising to storm, with a sea such as we have not seen since the frosts. All vessels warned off. See your oil stocks are full and your lamps in order. The Board relies upon the Greyhaven light as it has relied upon it these sixty years.*

*Yr obedient servant,*
*A. Dunmore*

Below the signature, in a different ink, someone had added a line: *Keep well, Mrs. B. Tom would be proud of how you keep it. — D.*

Odile read the letter standing in the doorway with the wind pulling strands of her hair loose across her face, and she read it twice, and then she folded it along its crease and put it in her apron pocket where it lay against her hip like something warm.

Thirty hours. It was enough. It would have to be.

"Pellow." The boy was shuffling, wanting to be away, sixteen and embarrassed in front of the widow at the tower as boys always were. She made her voice ordinary. "How's your mother's chest?"

"Better, thank you, ma'am. The doctor's stuff worked, mostly."

"Good. Take this to her—" she fetched it from the shelf, a jar of goose-grease and thyme she'd made in October against just such news— "and tell her to take it hot, in milk, mornings. Not evenings. Mornings."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Pellow." He had turned halfway down the path. "Stay home tomorrow night. Whatever your father says about the catch, stay home."

The boy grinned, half-cocksure, half-scared. "Da says it'll be a big one."

"Your da's right. That's why you'll both be ashore. Go on."

She watched him run the causeway until the rocks hid him, then she went in and began to work.

* * *

The next thirty hours she spent the way a surgeon spends the hour before battle: methodically, and aware that method was all that stood between her patients and the dark.

The oil first. She had laid in double stock in November, as she did every year—forty gallons of whale oil in the cellar casks, plus the reserve in the store-room—but she sounded every barrel with a rod and found them sound, transferred twenty gallons up to the lamp-room tank by degrees, and set the spare burners out on the bench in their oiled cloth where her hands could find them blind. She cleaned every pane of the glazing inside and out, though most of them had been cleaned a week ago, because salt film thickened invisibly and a dirty pane could eat a quarter of the light and quarters mattered. She tested the clockwork that turned the lens assembly, wound it, listened to it tick through a full rotation with the even patience of a heart.

She cooked, too, though she had no appetite and would have none for days. Boiled ham. A loaf. Twelve eggs hard-boiled and set in a bowl, because during the great storm of '41—before her time at the lamp, but old Brightwater had told it often—her predecessor had nearly fainted at the wick and afterward swore he'd been three days forgetting food existed. Odile did not mean to forget food existed. Hunger was a signal like the falling glass, and signals were meant to be answered.

Through the afternoon the gulls deserted the rail entirely, and she noted it: *Gulls gone inland by 2 o'clock.* Through the afternoon the sea climbed the outer ledges and then climbed the middle ones, white water bursting upward in columns that she could feel through the floor. By dusk the wind had backed to the southeast exactly as Dunmore promised, and it came in long screaming gusts that made the tower lean—she had measured it once with a plumb-line, in her first winter alone, and found the top of the tower swayed a full handspan in a gale, and had decided not to think about it again.

At dusk she lit the lamp.

She did it with the same care she had given it a thousand times, and yet tonight her hands moved slower, the way a man walks slower toward a door he is not eager to open. The flame took. The beam swung. Out beyond the Teeth the swell was already running mountainous, and somewhere out in all that black turmoil there would be ships—there were always ships, fools and desperate men and merchants racing the season—and every one of them would see this light and shape their course by it or fail to, live or drown, and all of that vast machinery of chance hung on a woman with scissors and a whetstone and a bowl of boiled eggs.

"All right," she said to the gulls' empty rail, to the dark, to the thirty hours Dunmore had given her. "All right. I'm here."

* * *

She did not sleep that night, nor meant to. She sat in the lamp room with the log open on her knee and the storm arriving around her by installments, each worse than the last, and she wrote it all down because writing it down was how she held the night at arm's length.

*11 o'clock.* Wind SE, hard gale. Sea breaking over the lower gallery. Glass still falling.

*Midnight.* Spray over the lantern. Glazing rattling but sound. Kept all lamps burning.

*1 o'clock.* Wind rising to storm. The tower humming. Heard the Teeth plainly over the wind—sea exploding on them, like cannon, a mile out.

Between entries she watched the beam sweep and return, sweep and return, that great slow wheel of light rolling across chaos, and she thought about Thomas the way she permitted herself to think of him only in storms: fully, without flinching. His hands. The way he'd sung badly and constantly. How the last time she'd seen him alive he'd been walking down the causeway with his seabag over his shoulder, turning to wave, and she'd been annoyed about some trifle—a broken latch, a debt to the coal man—and had waved back curtly, and that curt wave was the last thing she had ever given him. Three years of regret pressed flat and carried everywhere, worn under her clothes like a coat no one else could see, and on storm nights it grew heavy as armor.

Somewhere out there tonight, other women were sitting in other rooms with their curtains drawn, not knowing yet. That was the cruelty of it. The widows of this coast were made hours or days before the news reached them, and they walked about and ate supper and slept in the interval, ignorant, whole.

Not tonight. Tonight the light burned, and if any man on any deck saw it, that was the best she could give him, and she would go on giving it until morning or until the sea took the tower, whichever came first.

*3 o'clock.* Storm at its height. Sea making clean breaches over the rock. One shutter gone from the store-room window. All secure aloft.

Toward dawn the wind shifted a point or two and eased, the way storms do when they've spent their fury on the night, and the eastern sky came up the color of wet slate. The sea was still enormous—white-headed hills of water marching in from the southeast, striking the Teeth and throwing spray higher than the lantern itself—but the violence had gone out of the air, replaced by a vast exhausted roar.

And it was then, in that grey half-light with her eyes raw from smoke and waking, that Odile saw it.

A light on the water. Not a star—stars didn't move against the swell. Not a ship's lantern riding at anchor—no vessel could anchor out there and live. It flickered low near the base of the outer ledges, appearing and vanishing behind the seas, and it was coming slowly, helplessly, toward the Point.

She seized the telescope from its bracket, steadied it against the frame, and found the light again between waves. A lantern, small and doused-looking, and beside it—or holding it—something pale that came and went. A boat awash. Or a raft. Or a man.

Or men.

Her heart went hard and quick against her ribs. In this sea, no rowing could make headway against that current; no line could carry; no boat launched from the Point would live five minutes. She knew all this with the certainty of thirty years on this rock. She knew it, and she was already moving, flying down the ninety-one steps two at a time with the telescope still clutched in one fist, because the tide was going out, and at low water the causeway path to the outer ledges stood bare for perhaps forty minutes, and the light was drifting toward the very place where the rocks came closest to shore.

Down, and down, and out into the shrieking dawn with the spray hitting her like thrown gravel, boots slipping on weed-slick stone, the logbook abandoned open on the desk above and the lamp still turning its patient wheel behind her.

The tide was falling. The rocks were showing black and gleaming. Forty minutes, maybe less.

She ran.
