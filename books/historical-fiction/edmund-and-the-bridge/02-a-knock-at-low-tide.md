## 2. A Knock at Low Tide

The storm spent itself sometime before dawn, and Odile Brightwater woke to a silence so complete it felt like another kind of weather.

She lay a moment in the narrow bed beneath the slope of the tower roof, listening to the way the world had changed overnight. For three days the wind had leaned on Greyhaven Point like a debt collector at a door, and now, all at once, it had been paid. The glass in her windows rattled only when she breathed on it. Somewhere below, water dripped from the eaves in slow, counted beats.

She rose, dressed in the dark out of habit — no sense waking anyone, though there was no one to wake — and climbed the ninety-one steps to the lamp room, counting them as she always did. Ninety-one. The same as yesterday. It was a small comfort, arithmetic. The dead did not change their numbers.

The lamp had burned through the night as it should. She trimmed the wick, scraped the char from the glass with a bone knife her husband had whittled for her in the first year of their marriage, and topped off the oil. Outside, the sea lay flattened and gleaming under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, still heaving its shoulders after the violence, the way a man does after weeping. Wrack lay strewn along the shingle in long dark ribbons: kelp, splintered crate-wood, a fishing float, half a gull's nest torn from some cliff and carried out of its geography entirely.

"Nothing for you this morning," she told the gulls, who had gathered anyway on the gallery rail, hunched and patient as creditors. One of them, the big grey-backed one with the notched primary feather that she had privately named the Colonel, tilted his head at her with an expression of frank disbelief.

"I mean it," she said. "The storm's thrown everything about. You'll have to fish like honest birds."

The Colonel blinked. Odile logged the weather in the keeper's book — *Wind veering NW by morning. Sea falling. Barometer recovering.* Her hand paused over the page. She almost wrote something else, something about how the air after a storm always smelled to her like the inside of a church, but there was no one to read it and no sense embarrassing herself even in ink. *Visibility improving,* she wrote instead, and closed the book.

That was the shape of her days now, two years on. Trim the wick. Log the weather. Speak to the gulls because they asked nothing and answered less. She had worn her loneliness so long she had stopped feeling it as a weight and started feeling it as a garment — a coat of good grey wool, close-fitting, warm enough, hers. Other women had husbands or children or sisters who wrote letters. Odile had a lamp, a ledger, and a coastline that required her, and there were worse things than being required. There was being wanted and then left on the tide, which is what had happened to Tomas, and she would take required over wanted-and-gone any morning God cared to offer the choice.

She went down to make tea and discovered what the storm had done to her stores.

* * *

The damage was not catastrophic, but it was instructive. Rain had found its way through a gap in the roof lead she'd meant to fix since autumn, and a corner of the storeroom where she kept her flour and candles had taken a soaking. She spent the better part of the morning moving sacks, spreading them to dry on racks by the stove, mopping, cursing with a fluency she would have been ashamed to display before a living audience. By noon the sun had actually come out, thin and watery, and the tide — which had run high and furious all night — had begun its retreat, peeling back from the point in sheets, uncovering the great black ribs of rock that made Greyhaven a hazard and a lighthouse necessary.

It was on her second trip to the woodpile, arms full of split pine, that she saw the boat.

It was wedged high on the sand below the point, hull up like a dead animal, a small open dinghy with its nameboard gone and its paint scoured to bare gray grain. Abandoned, or lost — the storm could easily have set it adrift from some harbor upcoast and thrown it here like a dog fetching a stick. She stood a moment looking at it, the wind pulling strands of hair loose across her face.

A boat like that meant someone might be missing. Someone might come looking. She ought to report it at Millbeck; Sergeant Denny kept a list of wrecked craft and their owners, and it was a keeper's duty to note such things in her log.

*I know my duties,* she thought, a little sharply, as if someone had accused her. *I have performed every duty asked of me since I was seventeen years old.*

She went back inside. The tide was still going out. She would walk the beach below the point at low water, as she always did after a blow, both because flotsam was useful and because the walking of it was a kind of prayer she no longer said in words.

She did not find flotsam. She found the boy.

* * *

He was asleep against her door.

Not beside it, not near it — against it, curled into the lee of the doorframe where the tower's stone broke the wind, his knees drawn up, his head tipped back against the iron hinge-side, his face turned to the sky with the absolute surrender of the deeply exhausted. He was perhaps eight years old, small for it, dressed in a sailor's jersey far too large for him with sleeves rolled three times, and trousers cut down from a man's, and shoes that had been good ones once and were ruined now, salt-stained and split. His skin had the bluish-white cast of someone who had been cold so long the cold had stopped making its arguments. His lips moved slightly, endlessly, shaping words without sound.

Odile stood very still, ten feet away, the firewood clutched to her chest like a shield.

Her first thought — her honest first thought, the one she would never say aloud to any living soul — was *no.* Not again. Not another mouth, not another mystery washed up on her doorstep, not another grief wearing smaller clothes. She had buried one life on this coast already. She kept her own small flame and her own small ledger and the arithmetic balanced, and children did not balance. Children were chaos with appetites.

Her second thought arrived before the first had finished speaking, which was the way mercy worked, in her experience: it did not wait for permission.

"He'll die there," she said aloud, to no one, and the saying of it decided her, the way putting a thing into words always made it true enough to act upon.

She set down the wood, crossed the shingle-fast ground, and crouched beside him. Up close he smelled of salt and smoke and something underneath both, something burnt and chemical, like the aftermath of a lamp knocked over. His eyelids had a bruised look, the lids themselves nearly translucent, and there was a scrape along his jaw crusted with dried blood, and his hands — his hands were wrong, she thought at first, and then understood: they were clenched so hard and so long that the fingers had stiffened into shapes, as if he'd been carrying something heavy for miles and miles and had forgotten how to let go of the weight even in sleep.

"Boy." She touched his shoulder. Nothing. She shook him gently. His eyes flew open.

They were dark eyes, nearly black, and they looked at her with a ferocity that startled her — not fear exactly, or not only fear, but a kind of desperate assessment, an accounting conducted at terrible speed behind a child's face. She watched him decide something about her in the space of two heartbeats, watched the decision fail, watched him try to run.

He got one knee under himself and swayed, and would have pitched face-first onto the stones if she had not caught him. He fought her — silently, furiously, a wet cat of a fight, twisting and pushing with those stiffened hands — and then the fight simply went out of him like a tide withdrawing, and he hung in her grip, breathing hard, staring past her shoulder at the tower as if it were the thing that had caught him and not a woman in a patched grey dress.

"You're safe," she said, and heard how the word cracked in her throat, unused. "You're — all right. Come inside. You're half-dead of cold."

He didn't resist when she drew his arm across her shoulders and took his weight. He weighed almost nothing. That frightened her more than anything else about him — how little a child weighed, how easily carried, how the whole warm freight of a person could be lifted by one woman with bad knees and a grief habit.

She got him inside, into the warmth of the kitchen, into the chair nearest the stove, and began, with the brisk economy of someone doing the only sensible thing in a mad situation, to strip the wet clothes off him and wrap him in her good blanket, the wool one, the one she'd been saving. She put water on to warm. She rubbed his hands, chafing them, muttering the whole time — nonsense, mostly, the running commentary of a woman who had lived too long alone: *"Foolish child, out in that, where's your mother, God preserve us, the state of these shoes, whoever let you—"*

He let her do it all. He watched her the entire time with those black eyes, unblinking, cataloguing. And he made not one sound. Not a whimper when the warmth came back into his fingers and hurt going in — and she knew it hurt, because his face flinched in rhythm with her rubbing. Not a sound when she pressed the sweetened tea into his hands and guided it to his mouth. Not a sob, not a thank-you, not a question. His lips moved sometimes, that same soundless shaping of words, and stopped.

*Dumb,* she thought, and then corrected herself with a sting of shame: *or struck dumb. There's a difference, and I've no right guessing which.*

It was while she knelt wringing out his stockings that she felt it — the hardness in the front of his jersey, where the folded cloth bulged wrong. A child sleeping against a door in a storm does not carry a parcel.

"I'm going to have a look at what you've got there," she said, keeping her voice level, the way you'd speak to a spooked horse. "No harm in it. But I'll know either way."

He didn't move to stop her. That was somehow worse — the stillness of him, the watchfulness, as if he were letting her, as if this were a test whose result mattered terribly and whose terms he could not influence.

She reached into the front of the jersey and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth and bound with tarred string. It was flat, rectangular, heavier than paper alone, and cold as the sea. At one corner, a seal of dark red wax had been pressed with a signet she didn't recognize — some device, a bridge or a gate or a letter, blurred by a hurried thumb. Whoever had sealed it had done so fast, in the dark, possibly with shaking hands.

There was no address. There was no name. Tucked beneath the string on the outside, folded once, was a single slip of paper, and on the paper, in a cramped hand that slanted downhill as though written by someone leaning into wind:

*Forget the face.*

* * *

Odile sat back on her heels and looked at the packet in her lap for a long moment, and then at the boy, and then at the packet again.

Outside, the tide had reached its bottom and turned; she could feel it in the floorboards' silence, in the held breath of the house. The sun came through the kitchen window and laid a pale stripe across the table, across the salt rings left by wet boots, across the boy's bare feet dangling above the floor.

"Forget the face," she read aloud, though reading aloud to herself was another habit she thought she'd broken. "What face? Whose face?"

The boy's eyes went, briefly, somewhere over her left shoulder — toward the window, toward the sea, toward some picture hung on the air where she couldn't see it — and came back to her. His jaw set. He shook his head, one small deliberate motion, and then folded his arms across his chest over the spot where the packet had been, guarding the absence of it, and looked at the fire as though the conversation were concluded.

"Well," said Odile. "That's settled, then."

She set the packet on the table between them, squarely, where they could both see it. She poured herself tea. Her hands, she noticed, were not quite steady, and she was annoyed at them for it.

Now that she was warm and the immediate crisis of a freezing child was resolved into the slower crisis of a mysterious one, the prudent part of her mind resumed its interrupted argument. *Send for Denny at Millbeck. A child abandoned is the Crown's business, or the parish's. An unsealed packet with no name is nobody's business but trouble's, and you know what trouble looks like on this coast — it looks like men coming up the path asking questions, and it ends with keepers losing their posts, or worse.* The prudent part of her had a voice like her mother's and it was never entirely wrong.

But.

But the boy had been carried here, or had crawled here, to the one door on this stretch of coast that belonged to a woman who owed the sea a husband and the sea's debts a certain grim attention. Somebody had chosen this door. Out of all the doors between here and Millbeck, somebody had brought this silent, stubborn, salt-frozen child through a storm to *her* threshold, tucked a sealed thing into his coat like a coal kept from the fire, and written four words that were not instructions so much as a plea: *forget the face.* As if the face were the dangerous part. As if remembering it could kill.

And there was something else, something she hadn't let herself think yet, thinking it now reluctantly, the way you touch a bruise to learn its size: the boy's face itself. Now that the color was coming back into it, now that he sat upright in the firelight scowling at her teapot, there was something familiar in the architecture of him — the set of the brows, the particular stubborn angle of the chin. She had seen that chin before. She had seen it tilted upward at her across the deck of a fishing smack eleven years ago, in a man who had pulled her out of the water at Colder Bay when her own boat went over, a man named — 

She stopped. Would not finish it. Refused, for one more hour at least, to finish it.

The boy was watching her again. He had picked up his tea and drunk it, all of it, in the methodical way of someone following orders given long ago — *eat when food is offered, always, because you don't know when next* — and he set the cup down with exaggerated care, and looked at her, and pointed one stiff finger at the packet on the table, and then at her, and then laid that finger across his own lips.

*Keep it secret,* said the gesture. Or perhaps: *You weren't supposed to find it.* Or perhaps, and this was the reading that settled into her chest like a swallowed stone: *Please.*

"All right," Odile said quietly. She stood, took the packet, and put it in the tin box atop the wardrobe where she kept Tomas's papers — his marriage lines, his discharge from the *Providence*, the letter from the shipping office that had said, in language designed to soften murder into misfortune, *lost with all hands.* She put it beside her dead husband's documents as though introducing strangers at a party, and closed the tin, and came down, and busied her hands with supper she did not want.

The boy ate everything she gave him and drank the milk she warmed for him, and when she spread a pallet by the stove he lay down on it obediently, on his side, facing the door. Even asleep — she checked twice, by candlelight — even asleep he faced the door, and his fists stayed closed.

Odile banked the fire, trimmed the lamp for the night, and climbed the ninety-one steps to log the day. She dipped her pen. She wrote *Sea moderating. Wrack on beach. Derelict dinghy below the point.* Her pen hovered.

Somewhere below her, in her kitchen, a mute child slept beside her hearth guarding secrets she could feel through the floor like a second heartbeat, and in a tin box above his head lay a sealed packet and four words that were beginning, even now, to work at the edges of her like water at a cliff-face.

*Found a boy at my door,* she wrote at last. *Unspeaking. Unclaimed.*

Then, after a moment, in smaller letters, as if ink could whisper:

*He has Tomas Brightwater's chin.*

She set down the pen and stood a long time in the lamp room, listening to the turned tide begin its patient work against the rocks, and knew — the way she knew weather coming, the way she knew ice before she saw it — that whatever was in that packet, forgetting the face was going to prove the one instruction of her life she could not follow.
