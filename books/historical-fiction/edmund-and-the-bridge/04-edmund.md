## 4. Edmund

The woman trimmed the lamp at dusk and dawn, and Edmund counted the days by it.

Seven trimmings now. Seven times she had climbed the turning stair with her box of wicks and her scissors and her cloth, and seven times she had come back down smelling of hot oil and salt, and said nothing to him that mattered.

She said other things. Small things. *There's bread on the shelf. Mind the gulls, they'll take it from your hand if you let them. Your boots are letting in water; we'll see to that.* Words that fell on him like rain on a window — present, patterning, not getting in.

He watched her the way he had watched the rats in the warehouse: from stillness, from corners, from the long habit of being small enough to be overlooked. She was tall for a woman, and her hands were hard at the edges, rope-callused, one finger crooked where something had broken it and been set badly or not at all. Her hair was going grey at the temples in two clean streaks, like frost had claimed those parts first. When she thought no one was looking she stood at the seaward window with her arms folded, and her face did something he could not read, and after a while he understood that it was not a face making an expression but a face forgetting to hold one.

She was grieving someone. He knew the shape of it. He had seen his mother wear it for the year between his brothers' fevers and her own death, and then he had seen his father wear it worse, because his father had worn it over two griefs stacked together and had carried them out onto the water every night as though the sea might give change.

Edmund did not grieve. He was not sure he knew how yet. There was only a place in his chest where his father's hand had been, closed around his own small one, warm through the glove — and when he thought about it too directly the place burned, so he did not think about it directly. He thought around it. Sideways. In pieces.

* * *

Fragment: the warehouse burning.

Not all of it. Pieces, the way a shattered window holds sky.

The orange light coming up between the floorboards like water rising backwards. The smell — pitch, tar, rope, all the smells of his father's work made terrible at once. His father's voice below him, cutting through the roar, saying *not that way, Edmund, the stairs are gone, the window, do you hear me, the window* — and Edmund had heard, because his father's voice was the one voice in the world that arrived inside your head before it reached your ears, as though it had never needed the air at all.

Then the hand. Gloved, gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise, hauling him over the sill into cold air and snow and the enormous black quiet of the river night. The fire behind them making their shadows run along the frozen mud ahead of them, huge and wrong.

Then the whisper. Crouched behind the capstan with the fire painting the whole harbor orange and men shouting, some of them shouting his father's name, and his father bent close so his breath touched Edmund's ear.

*If anything happens to me — you go to Greyhaven. You find the keeper. You give her the packet and you forget my face.*

He had not understood *forget my face.* He understood it now. It did not mean stop remembering. It meant: stop wearing it where anyone can see. It meant: a boy who looks like Thomas Renner is a message, and messages get delivered to the wrong people, and the wrong people open them.

So he forgot his face. He kept it folded away like the page, and gave the world nothing, and when the smugglers' boat had left him on the shingle below the lighthouse with the packet sewn into his coat, he had walked up the path through the sleet and lain down against her door because his legs were done, and slept the way the drowned sleep, without choosing to.

* * *

On the eighth morning he took stock of what he had.

One page, torn from the ledger the night before the boat landed, while the smugglers argued about tide on the beach and no one watched a boy sitting quiet in the lee of the dory. He had torn it low, near the stitching, so the tear looked like damage and not theft. It was his father's handwriting on both sides, and it named names, and one of the names was the name that mattered more than all the others, and Edmund could read well enough to know it even if he would never say it.

The page lived inside his shirt now, against his skin, folded to the size of a communion wafer. It had lived there through eight days and seven trimmings of the lamp.

One packet, given to the woman. Sealed, whole, unopened as far as he knew — except that on the third day he had found the wax cracked in her sewing box, buried under thread, and he had known she read it, and he had felt the page against his ribs go hot as a coal and had spent that entire day deciding whether to run.

He had not run. Running required somewhere to run *to*, and there was nowhere. That was the truth of it, and he made himself look at it plainly the way his father had taught him to look at knots: *see what it actually is, Edmund, not what you're afraid it is.*

So: the woman had broken the seal. She had read what was in the packet — the manifests, the ledger, the proof — and she had not thrown it in the stove, and she had not carried it to the magistrate's man at Millbeck, either, which is what anyone sensible would have done, sensible people being mostly frightened people. Instead she had sat up two nights with a candle, copying pages in her careful slanted hand, and had then hidden the originals somewhere Edmund had not yet found, and this interested him enormously. A woman who hid things properly was a woman who intended to use them.

But intending and daring were different animals. He had known men — his father's friends, the quiet customs men who came and went by night — who intended everything and dared nothing until it was too late. He needed to know which animal the woman kept.

So he tested her.

* * *

The first test was small. He left the bread knife on the floor beside his plate instead of returning it to the rack, blade toward his own hand, and waited to see if she would mention it. She picked it up without comment, wiped it, racked it, and later set a second spoon by his bowl at supper without a word about why. Passing. Noted.

The second test was harder and he regretted it even as he ran it. He took one of her logbooks off the desk in the lamp room — the old ones, the filled ones, three years back — and put it under the loose board beneath his cot with half of it showing, deliberate, careless-looking. Then he went and sat in the window seat where she could see him not looking at the stair.

She came up an hour later. He tracked her footsteps through the ceiling overhead, around the lamp, across to the desk, the pause, the small silence where a book should have been. Then her boots came down the stairs, unhurried, and passed his door, and paused.

She stood there a long moment. He kept his eyes on the gulls working the tide line and let his shoulders be the shoulders of a child absorbed in birds.

*Found your reading, have you?* she said through the door. Not angry. Almost amused. *Third shelf. The ink gets better after '44; he stopped watering it.*

And she went on down to the kitchen, and the boards did not creak with lying-in-wait, and Edmund lay awake a long time that night doing arithmetic he did not have words for. She had caught him thieving her private books and answered with a joke about ink. Either she was very clever or she was very kind, and he was beginning to suspect — with something that felt uncomfortably like hope, hope being a thing he had decided was finished — that she was both.

* * *

Fragment: his father at the table, drawing lines on paper by candlelight.

*This is a manifest,* his father would say, tapping. *It says what a ship carries. Now — here is the trick, and you must learn tricks early, Edmund, because honest men who don't know the tricks drown first.* And he would show how a manifest could lie, how forty casks of brandy could become twelve with one stroke of a clerk's pen, how the difference went into certain pockets, and how the pockets belonged to men whose names were never written anywhere at all. Except —

Except his father had written them. That was the whole ruinous point of his father. A customs man who trusted paper too much, who believed evidence was armor, who copied and copied and copied into ledgers his employers would have paid any price to burn. The warehouse had not caught fire by accident. Even Edmund, eight years old and pretending otherwise, had known that. Fires chose their targets in Corven Hale's country, and they were always deniable, and the men who lit them always got promoted.

His father had known it too. That was the whisper behind the capstan. That was the packet. That was *forget my face.*

Edmund turned the thought over the way he turned the smooth white stone in his pocket, the one from the beach below the lighthouse, worn round by ten thousand tides. His father had sent him to this woman specifically. Not to the nuns at Ashford, whom he'd also trusted. Not to Tobias Finch on the ferry, whose name appeared twice in the ledgers as a friend. To the keeper at Greyhaven Point. Which meant his father had believed she owed something, or was strong enough, or simply that the light was the one fixed point in a dark country and sons should be anchored to lights.

* * *

The third test, he almost did not run.

It was the coldest day yet, and she found him at the rail below the lamp room watching the ice floes grind past the point with a sound like slow teeth, and instead of telling him to come inside she brought her own coat and stood next to him and said, *My husband used to stand exactly there.*

Edmund went very still.

*Thomas Brightwater,* she said, to the floes, not to him. *Twenty-two years on the water. Drowned last March when the* Merrow *went down with all hands, eleven miles out, in weather the harbor board should never have let her sail in.* A pause. The wind moved her collar. *They called it an act of God.*

Edmund looked at her. She did not look back. Her jaw was set the way his father's jaw had set over the manifests, and something in his chest — the burned place, the place shaped like a hand — shifted and ached, because he suddenly saw the shape of her grief and his own lying side by side, and saw that the same name sat at the bottom of both of them like a stone at the bottom of a well.

She had lost her man to the same machine his father had died fighting. Of course she had broken the seal. Of course she sat up nights copying. She wasn't deciding whether to dare. She had dared already, alone, for months, before he ever washed up against her door.

He should have felt safe. Instead he felt afraid — a new fear, larger than the old one. Because if she went to the assizes with the packet, she would need to survive to get there, and so would he, and the page against his ribs named the man whose men were even now, surely, asking questions along every road from the coast. He had seen what happened to people who carried evidence. He had smelled the warehouse.

That night he did not sleep. Near dawn he heard her rise and dress and climb, and he rose too, and stood in his doorway in the dark with the page warm in his fist, on the very edge of giving it to her — and stopped.

Not yet. Because the page was the one thing that was wholly his, the last piece of his father that no one else had handled, and once he gave it away he would be trusting her with everything, and trust, in his short experience, was the thing that got you in the warehouse after hours. He folded it back against his skin. He would watch longer. He would know her completely first, the way his father had taught him to know a knot.

*You can't rush a splice,* his father said, hands moving, rope and rope marrying into rope. *Rushed work fails under load. And this, Edmund — this will be under load someday. Everything worth keeping is.*

* * *

Nine days after the boat, the woman came down from the lamp room at noon with her cheeks red from the cold and found him at the kitchen table, and instead of the usual small words she sat down across from him, folded her hands, and looked at him straight.

*Edmund,* she said — and it was the first time she had said his name aloud, though the smugglers must have told her, and hearing it in her mouth went through him like a fishhook. *I've read the packet. I know whose son you are. I knew your father — he pulled me out of the water off Harrow Bend when I was seventeen and never let me thank him properly. And I think I know what he wanted done with what he left you, and I think I mean to do it. But I'm not going to carry you anywhere you haven't chosen. So when you're ready — whenever that is — you tell me. Nod, write, point, whatever way suits. But it has to be yours.*

She held his eyes a moment longer. Then she rose, put the kettle on, and began slicing bread as though she had not just laid her whole intention on the table between them like a card turned face-up.

Edmund sat very still. The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. The gulls screamed over the point. Against his ribs, the folded page seemed to beat, faint and steady, like a second heart deciding.

She had said *when you're ready.* She had not said *now*, or *before the thaw*, or *or I leave you here*. Everyone else in his life who had wanted something from him had wanted it quickly.

Below them, far out past the point, the ice groaned — a long sound, deep in the water, the sound the old fishermen called the river counting days.

He put his hand flat against his chest, over the page, and watched her back, and began, carefully, to count too.
