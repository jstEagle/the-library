## 5. The First Checkpoint

The packet did not let her sleep.

Odile had kept it three days beneath the loose flagstone by the hearth, and each night she lay awake listening to it the way a sailor listens to weather — not hearing anything, exactly, but knowing something was there, waiting to break. Paper made no sound. Paper had never made a sound in its life. And yet at two in the morning, with the wind worrying the shutters and the lamp below turning its slow patient circles, she could have sworn she heard the wax seal whispering her name.

On the fourth night she got up, dressed, and took the packet out of its hiding place by feel, because she could not bear to look at it by lamplight. In the dark it was only a shape. A weight. A little bundle of other people's sins wrapped in oilcloth, no bigger than a loaf of bread, heavy enough to hang her.

"They hanged men for less," she said to the darkness, and the darkness, which had been her only conversation for eleven months, said nothing back.

But hanging was for the men who wrote the papers. What happened to women who carried them was never written down anywhere. It simply stopped being reported, like ships lost without wreckage.

She sat on the cold floor with her back against the bedframe and thought about the road north. Thirty miles as the crow flew; fifty as the river bent; and between here and the assize town, three crossings where men in the king's coat looked into carts and asked questions they were paid to ask. The first was Millbeck Bridge, a day's travel by the coast road with the cart and the pony, and everyone knew the tollhouse there had grown teeth since autumn. Seditious papers, they said. Sedition, smuggling, sedition again. As though the whole country had taken to writing treason instead of eating it.

Sedition. She almost laughed. If Sergeant Whoever at Millbeck knew what she carried, he would need a longer word.

*Forget the face.*

She hadn't forgotten the face. That was the whole trouble. She had seen it once, nine years ago, half-drowned and furious, hauling her out of the surf under Greyhaven's southern cliff after her skiff had gone over, and the face had been attached to a voice that scolded her the entire time — *madwoman, madwoman, the sea doesn't want you today* — while his hands worked like a surgeon's. He'd taken hot soup at the lighthouse and left before dawn, and she had never learned more of him than his first name and the smell of tar and oranges. And now that face had a son who didn't speak, asleep in her spare bunk with his fists curled under his chin like a sleeping otter, and a dead father, and a sealed packet, and one instruction she was already failing to follow.

Fear sharpening into craft — that was a thing her husband used to say about rigging. *Any fool can be frightened, Odile. The trick is to be frightened usefully.* Well then. She would be useful.

* * *

In the morning she went down to the store room and took up the lamp-oil flask — a great earthenware jug, glazed brown, with a wicker collar and a stopper of cork sealed in pitch. It held four quarts when full and it was her habit, born of a near-disaster in her second winter when a cracked flask had nearly left the light dark during a gale, to keep a spare always ready, filled and stoppered, in case the working flask failed.

She looked at it a long time.

Then she set it on the bench, knocked the pitch seal loose with the flat of a knife, and drew off the cork. The oil inside smelled of whale and winter. She poured it carefully into the reserve cans, every drop, wiping the rim with a rag until the flask stood empty and clean, and then she turned it in her hands and studied the base.

The glaze was thin at the bottom. Potters were thrifty that way; nobody saw the underside of a flask, so nobody wasted good clay on it. She tapped it with the knife handle and heard not the dull knock of solid ware but something faintly drumlike, and understood that the base was thick but not solid — that some potter in some kiln had left an air pocket in the firing, a flaw, a fault, a small blind lung inside the foot of the jug.

She had hollowed driftwood for candle-molds before. She had bored holes through oakum-hard deck timber with nothing but a nail heated in the lamp. Her hands knew how to make a cavity where a cavity was not meant to be. She wrapped the base of the flask in cloth, set it on the stone floor, and tapped around the circumference of that hidden pocket, patiently, an hour or more, until the glazed bottom cracked free along the seam of the flaw and came away in her hand like the lid of a very small pie.

Inside was a chamber perhaps three inches deep and two across. Not large enough for the whole packet.

That was the lesson, and she sat back on her heels and let it teach her. You could not hide everything. So hide the part that hangs you. The manifests and the bribe ledger — those were damning, but they were numbers, and numbers could be explained away by a woman with a steady tongue: papers found among a dead man's effects, kept for want of a fire, meaning nothing. But the page bearing the initials — *C.H.*, written in a hand she had seen once before on the condemnation writ for the *Providence*, the ship her husband had gone down with — that page was a signature, and signatures were sentences.

She cut that page from the ledger with a razor, folded it small as a communion wafer, wrapped it in waxed silk from an old rain hood, and lowered it into the flask's hidden chamber. Then she mixed fresh pitch — resin and tallow, the same recipe the chandler sold at Millbeck, so that even the seal would look local — and bedded the cut base back onto the jug and poured the pitch over it and smoothed it with a wet thumb until it looked like nothing so much as four decades of careless resealing. Every flask on every cart on that road looked exactly as bad.

The rest of the packet — the bulk of it, the body of the evidence — she hid the way the sea hides things: in plain sight, in layers. The manifests went into the lining of Edmund's spare coat, cut open and re-stitched, stiffened with fish glue so the fold wouldn't show. The remaining ledger pages she rolled tight and slid into the hollow axle-grease horn on the cart, which no searcher on earth had ever wanted to put his hand into, and which she immediately felt foolish about, because if they took the cart apart they would take the horn apart too, and so she took it out again and thought harder.

In the end the answer came from the lighthouse itself, as answers often did. She unpicked the stitching of the cart's seat cushion, packed the pages flat beneath the horsehair, and sewed it up with sail twine in a pattern of stitches slightly different from the original — deliberately different, sloppier, so that if anyone looked closely they would find what searchers always hoped to find: evidence that a seat had been repaired, badly, by a poor woman, and nothing else. Searchers dug where things were hidden well. They ignored what was hidden poorly. Her husband had taught her that too, though he'd been talking about hiding a bottle of gin from himself.

Edmund watched all of this from the doorway with those grey eyes that missed nothing, and when she finally wiped her hands and stood, aching, she caught him staring at the flask with an expression she couldn't read — until he crossed the room, picked up the rag she'd used on the oil, and held it out to her.

"What?" she said.

He pointed at the flask. Then at the lamp above, visible through the open door to the stair. Then he mimed, with terrible economy, a flame going out — fingers pinched, head tipped, eyes wide in mock darkness.

"I know," Odile said. "If I empty my reserve flask, and the reserve fails, the light goes dark."

He nodded once, gravely.

"And if the light goes dark," she went on, kneeling to be at his height, "ships strike the point, and people drown, and I might as well hang myself at home and save the king the rope." She held his gaze. "I've done the arithmetic, Edmund. One dark night, maybe, against one dark life. I'll trim the wicks twice as careful." Then, because silence filled rooms the way water filled holds, she added, "You could help with that. Trimming. If you're to travel with a keeper, you ought to know the trade."

Something moved behind his eyes — not a smile, but the place where a smile would be built later, once the scaffolding was up. He took the trimming scissors from her hand as solemnly as a squire taking a sword.

They practiced that evening, and he was good at it — better than good; his cuts were exact and unhurried, and when she showed him how to read the color of the flame he absorbed it the way sand absorbs tide. Watching him work in the lamp room, his small hands steady in the great glass light, Odile felt something loosen and immediately resented it, because tenderness was a leak, and leaks sank ships, and she could not afford to sink this week.

* * *

They left before dawn on the sixth day, Edmund bundled in the mended coat beside her on the cart bench, the pony leaning into its collar with its usual air of personal grievance. The flask rode in the pannier between a sack of oats and her market basket, in plain view, because the boldest hiding places were the ones nobody believed in. The frost had come down hard in the night; the ruts in the road were iron, and the wheels rang on them like a bell being beaten slow.

Greyhaven fell away behind them by degrees — the tower, then the white line of the surf, then just the smoke of her own chimney standing up straight in the still air, looking, she thought, like a candle someone had forgotten to blow out.

They walked when the road climbed, to spare the pony, and Odile talked as they went — not to Edmund, exactly, but within his hearing, because Sister Verity had warned her that mute children understood far more than they answered, and because talking kept the fear from pooling in her boots. She named the trees. She named the birds. She told him the names of the ships becalmed out beyond the headland, counting sails like sheep. Once, on a long descent, she felt a small weight arrive against her side and realized he had leaned into her without deciding to, the way a compass needle finds north, and neither of them acknowledged it, and it stayed.

Millbeck announced itself by smell — woodsmoke and tanning vats and the river's cold iron breath — and then by sound: the river running loud under the bridge, and under that, thinner, a fiddle in some tavern, and over everything the flat bored voice of authority calling *next*.

The tollhouse crouched at the northern end of the bridge like a toad on a coin. It had grown since her last crossing: a barrier of staves across the road, a brazier, and four soldiers in coats that had been red once and were now the color of old brick dust, stamping their feet and breathing steam. A queue of carts, six deep. Farmers, a fish-hawker, a drover with two oxen that objected to everything on principle.

Odile joined the queue and did what she had rehearsed: nothing. She sat. She let the cart creep. She mended, with numb fingers and enormous calm, a split in her glove, drawing the thread through and through while the queue ahead argued and was searched and waved on.

At the tollhouse, a farmer ahead of her was being turned out of his cart entirely, standing in the road with his hat in his hands while a corporal went through his sacks with a bayonet, prodding cheese, stabbing flour, opening a Bible to check, apparently, whether scripture had turned traitor since Michaelmas. The farmer's face was the face of a man doing sums about his life and getting answers he disliked.

"Next."

Her turn. The sergeant who said it was a different animal than the corporal — older, softer, a man whose belly strained his belt and whose boredom had gone past irritation into something geological. He had a list pinned to the tollhouse wall, and he glanced at it, and glanced at her, with the weary fluency of long practice.

"Toll's twopence. Cart and beast, fourpence."

"Fourpence." She counted it out slowly, letting her cold fingers fumble, giving him time to see exactly what she was: a widow in a good mended cloak, a boy, a pony, a market basket, going north like half the world went north in February. "Cold enough to crack stones," she said. "Is the river holding?"

"It's holding." He took the coins. His eyes did the inventory they were trained to do — basket, panniers, sacks — and snagged, as she had known they would, on the great brown flask. "What's in the jug?"

"Lamp oil." She said it the way she'd say *rain*. "For the light at Greyhaven Point. I keep it, do you know it? The light. Eleven years." She let a note of pride in, warm and true, because true notes were unfalsifiable. "I can't buy it south of the bridge since the chandler closed, so I carry it over myself, every month."

"Oil." He said it flatly. "Not writing ink. Not pamphlets."

"Sergeant." She smiled, tired and reasonable. "I am fifty-one years old and I have not read a pamphlet in my life. I read the weather and the almanac and the shipping lists, and none of those are worth carrying over Millbeck Bridge in February." She reached back and lifted the flask out of the pannier herself — the crucial motion, offered freely, ownership displayed — and set it in his hands. "It weighs forty pounds. If I were carrying sedition, would I pack it in something I had to lift twice?"

He hefted it, grunting. His thumbs moved over the wicker collar. For one heartbeat — one full turn of her blood from head to heel — his thumb rested directly on the pitch-sealed base, and Odile stood in the bright freezing morning and felt the whole world narrow to the size of a thumbnail.

Then he grunted again, set the flask down on the toll shelf, pulled the cork, and sniffed.

"Whale oil," he said, wrinkling his nose.

"The light burns what it's given."

He looked past her at Edmund, who sat with his chin sunk in his scarf, eyes down, the very portrait of a deaf fisher-boy dragged to market — and Odile's heart performed a maneuver she did not authorize, and she kept her face still. "Your boy? He's quiet."

"He's shy of soldiers." Also true. Everything true today; truth was the best mortar. "His father was pressed once. Never liked the uniform after."

The sergeant's own uniform seemed to weigh on him suddenly, or perhaps only the morning did. He waved the corporal over anyway — procedure, the dead god of regulations — and the young man came around the barrier with the farmer's bayonet still in his hand and poked, perfunctorily, at the oat sack, at the market basket, at the seat cushion, which he pressed twice with his palm and dismissed, because it gave like any horsehair cushion in England gives. Odile breathed through the length of it. Under the horsehair, thirty pages waited, and did not speak.

"Where's your paper for the boy?" the corporal said. "Traveling ward, is he? There's orders about—"

"He's my late husband's sister's grandson," Odile said, "going to kin at Ashford, and his papers are at Ashford, which is where we're going, which is why he hasn't got them *here*, corporal, same as your boots are at the barracks whenever anyone asks after them." She said it gently, smilingly, a woman amused by paperwork, and the sergeant laughed — one short bark, the laugh of a man who had spent twenty years being talked at by the public and had come out the far side of rage into philosophy — and flicked two fingers at the barrier.

"Go on, then, missus. Mind the ice on the bridge deck. And tell your chandler his oil's gone up a penny."

"I'll tell him the king says so."

"I never said the king says so." But he was already turning away, already calling *next*, already forgetting her, which was the finest thing a checkpoint could do: forget you completely, professionally, forever.

The cart rolled onto the bridge. The planks were glazed with frost and the river ran black and muscular beneath the gaps, chunks of pan-ice turning in the current like plates at a fair. Halfway across, Odile felt a small hand slide into hers, cold as the rail, gripping hard.

She squeezed back and did not look down, because looking down would mean acknowledging, and acknowledging would mean the shaking started, and the shaking could wait until Ashford. Behind them, faint on the wind, the sergeant was asking the fish-hawker what was in his boxes, and the fish-hawker was saying herring, and the world went on being ordinary, which was the most extraordinary thing it did.

"Nearly there," she said to Edmund, to the river, to herself. "One down."

It was only as they came off the bridge and the road opened northward, white and empty toward Ashford Sluice, that she saw the rider.

He came up the south road at a hard trot, ununiformed, dressed plain black like a lawyer or an undertaker, and he did not join the queue. He rode straight to the tollhouse, leaned down from the saddle, and said something to the sergeant that unbored him instantly — she saw it happen, saw the man's spine change — and then the rider turned his horse and looked down the north road, along the exact line of the cart, the pony, the woman, the boy.

Odile did not hurry. Hurrying was a confession. She clicked her tongue at the pony and kept the pace of a woman with nothing in the world but oats, oil, and a nephew's-grandson to deliver, and she did not breathe easily again for four miles — nor, in truth, ever quite fully again, because whatever the black rider had whispered over the tollbook, she knew its shape already. Papers had gone missing somewhere south. Someone was counting.

And someone, she would learn soon enough at the ferryman's landing, had a name: Rook.
