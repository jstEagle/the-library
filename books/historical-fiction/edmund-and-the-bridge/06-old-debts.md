## 6. Old Debts

Tobias Finch lived a half-mile up the coast road from Greyhaven Point, in a cottage that leaned toward the sea the way old men lean into wind. Odile had walked the path a hundred times in her widowhood, usually with salt fish or a jar of pickled samphire, because grief had made her stingy with words but not with small kindnesses, and Finch — who talked enough for three men and a gull colony — repaid her in eels, gossip, and long silences that never once felt like obligation.

Today she walked the path with a boy's hand in hers and a packet of damning papers sewn into the lining of her coat, and every step felt like walking on ice that hadn't frozen yet.

Edmund came without protest. He had come everywhere without protest since she'd told him, two mornings past, that they were going north. He ate what she gave him, slept where she put him, and watched everything — the gulls, the tide, her hands as she trimmed the lamp — with a patience that unsettled her more than weeping would have. A child who did not cry was a child keeping accounts. She knew that arithmetic herself.

The morning was hard and bright, the kind of cold that made the air feel scoured. Frost furred the tops of the gateposts along Finch's croft, and his boat — the *Magpie*, beached and tarred and long past any water deeper than the estuary — sat on its rollers like a whale that had decided land was simpler.

She knocked. Inside, something heavy shifted, a chair scraped, and Finch's voice carried through the door before he'd opened it: "If you've come about the Methodists again, missus, I told them — God loves me fine, He just don't love my singing—"

He stopped when he saw Edmund.

For half a heartbeat it was ordinary: Finch in his shirtsleeves, grey-stubbled, one hand still holding a tin mug, his face arranging itself into its usual welcome. Then his eyes dropped to the boy, to the set of the boy's jaw, the dark fall of his hair, the particular way he stood with his weight on his heels like a man listening for a floor to give way — and Odile watched the welcome drain out of Tobias Finch's face the way water leaves a breached hull.

"Lord above," he said quietly.

"May we come in, Toby?"

He stepped back without answering. That alone frightened her. In thirty years of knowing him — through her husband, through the years after — she had never once seen Tobias Finch struck dumb by anything.

* * *

His cottage smelled of pipe smoke and boiled tea and the ghost of tar. He put them at the table, poured tea nobody drank, and then sat down across from Edmund and looked at him the way a man looks at a coin he suspects is clipped.

"Turn your head a bit, lad," Finch said.

Edmund glanced at Odile. She nodded. The boy turned his head, slow and deliberate, firelight catching the line of his cheek.

Finch set down his mug with exaggerated care, as though it might go off.

"Jonas," he said. "It's Jonas's face wearing a child, that's what it is. Jonas Marsh, if ever I—" He pressed both hands flat on the table. "Missus Brightwater. Where did you get this boy?"

"He was left at my door. During the storm." She kept her voice level. "With papers. Papers that say Magistrate Hale bought the wreck of the *Speedwell*, and the *Lark*, and half the ships that have gone down off this coast these ten years. Papers I mean to carry to the assize court at Wetherby, across the river, before the ice breaks."

Finch stared at her. Then he began, horribly, to laugh — not merriment but the other kind, the kind that comes when a body has nowhere to put its fear.

"You'll carry them to Wetherby. You. A lighthouse keeper with a mute child and a coat that fits her badly." He shook his head. "And you've come here to ask me — no. No, Odile. I can see it sitting behind your eyes like a stone behind a window. You've come to ask me to run you upriver."

"I'm asking."

"And I'm refusing." He said it fast, the way a man pulls a splinter. "I'm sixty-one years old. My ferrying days are done. The *Magpie* hasn't been wet since the coronation, and my heart—" he thumped his chest, "—is held together with spite and bacon grease. What you're asking is suicide with cargo."

"Tobias—"

"Three checkpoints." He counted them off on thick fingers. "Millbeck Bridge. Weir Lock. Stonecross. And between Millbeck and Weir there's twenty mile of river where a man named Rook has been pulling carts apart since harvest looking for — what did they call it at the tollhouse last month? Seditious papers. Now tell me what's in your coat, missus, that makes you think it's worth drowning us all."

Odile looked at her tea. She had rehearsed this part on the walk, and now found the rehearsal useless; there was no way to say it that didn't sound like madness or presumption.

"Your debt," she said finally. "I'm asking you to pay your debt."

The silence that followed had weight. Outside, a crow went over the frozen garden, cawing twice, and somewhere down the lane ice cracked in a rain barrel.

"That's a cruel thing to bring through my door," Finch said, low.

"It's a true thing. I know what it costs to be reminded. I live with a reminder every day I light the lamp he never came home to." She leaned forward. "But I also know this, Tobias Finch: my Thomas pulled you out of the Scald on a night the sea had no business giving anybody back. Eleven men went into that water and one came out, and the one who came out swore on his mother's Bible he'd not forget it. I never asked you for that debt. Not once, not in six years. I'm asking now, because I am carrying a dead man's justice in my coat lining and a living boy's life in my hand, and I cannot do it alone."

Edmund, at the edge of her eye, had gone very still.

Finch rose. He walked to the window with his back to them, and Odile saw him grip the sill — saw the tendons stand in the backs of his hands — and when he spoke again his voice had changed, gone rougher, older.

"You want to know why I retired from the river, girl? It weren't age." He turned. His face had a strange, wet brightness. "It were a night in March, four years back. Cold like this. Ice going out early, same as the old folk are saying it will this year. I was running contraband then, same as half the coast, brandy and silk down from the Dutch boats, and I told myself it was honest work because the excise men were thieves anyway and everyone needs a story." He laughed, short and bitter. "That night there were a lad came to me with a message. Said his father needed passage north, quiet, tonight, and paid double for no questions. And I took the money, because I always took the money, and I didn't ask whose father, and I didn't ask what the father had seen or written or stolen. I just ran him up to the sluice and set him ashore and went home to count my silver."

Odile's breath had stopped somewhere in her chest.

"Two days later," Finch said, "the customs house at Ashford burned to the ground, and a man named Jonas Marsh was dead in it, and his boy was missing, and there were men on the roads asking after a ferryman who'd moved somebody north that night. Asking polite, mind. With money." He came back to the table and sat down heavily. "I burned my boat's papers. Told everyone my heart gave out. And I have spent four years telling myself I couldn't have known, that a ferryman ferries, that what happened after wasn't mine." He looked at the table. "Four years. And it works most days."

"Tobias," Odile said carefully, "what was the father's name?"

"Marsh. Jonas Marsh. He was an informer against Hale — everybody half-knew it, though none dared say it near the courthouse. And the boy he sent ahead of him, the boy I set ashore at Ashford Sluice in the dark—" Finch lifted his eyes to Edmund.

The boy met his gaze without flinching. Then, slowly, deliberately, Edmund reached up and touched his own chest, and then pointed at Finch, and nodded once.

The color left Finch's face entirely. He looked like a man seeing his own name carved on a gravestone.

"Oh," he said. Just that. *Oh.* As though some door inside him had opened onto a room he'd bricked over, and everything he'd stored there came tumbling out at once.

He got up so fast his chair went over backwards. He crossed to the hearth, stood with a hand on the mantel, breathing. Odile started to rise; he waved her down without turning.

"Don't," he said. "Don't comfort me, missus. I haven't earned comfort in this house for four years." He was quiet a moment. When he turned around, his eyes were red but his voice was steady, and there was something in his face Odile recognized from her mirror — the look of a person picking up a weight they'd set down too soon, finding their arms remembered it.

"The nuns," he said. "At Ashford. Sister Verity — she took the boy in, didn't she? After the fire. There was talk of a child at the convent that winter. Nobody connected it." He looked at Edmund again and shook his head slowly, wonderingly. "You were at my own door, lad. All this time, and you were forty miles south, and now you're standing in my kitchen wearing your father's face, asking me for nothing at all."

"He doesn't ask for anything," Odile said softly. "That's the worst of it. He waits to see who offers."

Finch wiped his face with the heel of his hand, briskly, like a man doing chores. Then he began, of all things, to tidy — squaring the mug on the table, righting his chair, setting the kettle straight on its hook — small orders imposed on a world that had just tilted.

"Here's what happens now," he said, talking mostly to the stove. "You don't go north by the road. Rook's men have been at Millbeck a fortnight; they're not checking for smugglers, whatever tale they spin, they're checking for *papers*, which means someone's told them papers exist, which means Hale knows. If Hale knows, he'll have watchers on the fen paths and the drover roads, and every honest traveler between here and Stonecross is a pair of eyes he's rented." He turned. "You go by water, or you don't go at all."

"By water means past Weir Lock," Odile said. "Past the lock gates, in open view."

"Under them, if we do it right. Eel boats go through all winter; nobody searches an eel boat twice, they stink too bad to linger over." He said it lightly, but his knuckles were white on the chair-back. "My cousin Aldous runs eels out of Fernham. He owes me, and better than that, he hates Hale for reasons of his own. He'll run you. But someone has to go with you to make the introductions and sweeten the lock-passage, because Aldous is honest the way weather is honest — useful, and not to be trusted."

Odile let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding. "Then you'll—"

"I said I was refusing." Finch came back to the table. He sat down across from Edmund, close, so the boy had to look up at him, and something passed between them that Odile felt she had no part of — a ledger being balanced in silence, four years late. "I'm not refusing anymore. God help me, and God forgive the man I was on the river that night, I'm coming with you as far as Weir Lock, and further if the water lets me."

Edmund put out his hand — small, chapped, deliberate — and laid it flat on the table between them.

Finch looked at it. Then he covered it with his own great scarred paw, the way men seal bargains, and funerals, and things older than either.

"There's one more thing," he said, and his voice dropped, and Odile heard the fear come back into it, quieter now, colder. "The night I ran his father north, young master Marsh — you'd have been what, seven? eight? — you stood on my deck while I worked the sweeps. Never cried, never spoke. Just watched me handle the boat. Watched the whole way." He swallowed. "And when I set you ashore, you did this." He raised his own hand and touched two fingers briefly to his brow, a small salute, sailor-fashion. "Eight years old, saluting the ferryman who was about to spend his blood-money. Like you already knew what I was."

He drew a long breath.

"Now there's men coming up this coast asking after a woman and a child, and I'm meaning to find out how much of what's coming is my fault. But first—" he stood, reaching for the oilskin coat on its peg by the door, "—first we're going to see my cousin about an eel boat. Because if I'm reading the sky right, missus, and I generally am—"

Through the little window behind him, far out over the grey estuary, Odile saw the light change: a softness in the west, a smell of thaw on the wind that hadn't been there yesterday.

"—the ice is going to break sooner than anyone planned," Finch said. "And we'd best be on the river before it does."
