## 7. Hale's Men

Sergeant Rook had been at Millbeck Bridge long enough to know the smell of every season, and winter was the worst of them. Not the cold itself — a man could dress against cold — but the smell of the river under ice, that faint sourness of trapped water and dead reeds, like a cellar door left open somewhere beneath the world. He smelled it now, standing in the doorway of the tollhouse with his third cup of chicory going cold in his hand, watching the frost climb the inside of the window glass in feathered patterns.

The bridge groaned once, low and distant, the way it did on hard nights. Rook had learned not to flinch at it. The ice was still a foot thick at the pilings; it had months yet before it did anything worth fearing.

It was the rider he flinched at.

The man came up the north road at a pace no sensible traveler used in February — a hard canter held too long, horse lathered despite the freeze, breath smoking in ragged banners. Rook set down his cup and arranged his face into the mild, stupid expression that had kept him alive through two postings and one bad year in the revenue service. He stepped out onto the frozen mud and raised a hand.

"Road's open," he called. "Toll's tuppence, or—"

The rider drew up short and did not dismount. He sat looking down at Rook from under the brim of a hat pulled low, and Rook took inventory the way he took inventory of everything: tall, lean, gloved to the elbow, a traveling cloak of good broadcloth gone travel-stained at the hem. A city man pretending not to be one. Under the cloak, where his coat fell open as he leaned forward, the dull gleam of a pistol butt riding high in the belt, worn for reach rather than show.

"Sergeant Rook?" The voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of a man reading a list he already knew by heart.

"That's me."

"My name is Quill." He said it without warmth, as if the name were an inconvenience someone else had chosen for him. He reached into his breast and produced a folded paper, sealed with black wax, and held it out without extending his arm — making Rook come to him, which Rook noted, and resented, and did.

Rook broke the seal. The paper inside bore a printed letterhead he recognized and had hoped never to see outside of assize circulars: the office of Corven Hale, Justice of the Peace, Commissioner of Oyer and Terminer for the northern circuit. The handwriting beneath the letterhead was small and precise, each letter finished, nothing hurried about it. That was the thing about Hale's correspondence, Rook had heard men say. It never looked angry. It looked *settled*.

He read it twice.

*Sgt. E. Rook, tollhouse Millbeck Bridge. A woman is traveling north along the river road, age thirty to forty, grey eyes, competent hands, likely posing as a tradeswoman or keeper of some licensed office. She travels with a boy of perhaps eight years who does not speak. They may use false names. She carries documents of a seditious and stolen nature.*

*You are instructed to detain them should they pass your post, and to secure all papers found in their possession. Any papers so secured are to be burned unopened. Nothing is to be forwarded. Nothing is to be copied. You will report to the bearer only.*

*This matter lies outside the ordinary course of justice, and you will be answered generously.*

Rook read it a third time, because the fourth sentence would not sit still in his head. *Burned unopened.* Not examined, not verified, not entered into any book of evidence. Burned. He had served writs for eleven years. He had searched carts full of smuggled brandy and pamphlets that called the King a Dutch farmer's puppet, and every scrap of it, once seized, went into a sack and went to a magistrate and got weighed and entered and argued over by lawyers who were paid to argue. Papers were the whole point. Papers were what made a seizure *worth something*.

Nobody burned evidence before reading it unless the reading was the danger.

"She has a name, this woman?" Rook said carefully.

Quill shifted in the saddle. Somewhere behind the tollhouse, Rook's corporal, young Hesk, was pretending very loudly to chop kindling.

"She has several," Quill said. "None of them will matter."

"And the boy?"

"What of him?"

"He doesn't speak, you say. Is he her son? Her charge? I'm to know what I'm looking at when I stop a cart."

Quill looked at him then, directly, for the first time. His eyes were pale, unhurried, and entirely without curiosity, and Rook understood in that moment that here was a man who had been given instructions and regarded everything not in the instructions as weather.

"You're to look at hands and eyes," Quill said. "Grey eyes, capable hands. And you're to look at the boy's mouth, because he won't open it. Detain them. Send for me. The rest is not your concern."

"And if she comes through before you're back at my post?"

Something moved at the corner of Quill's mouth. It might have been amusement. "Then you'll do what you're paid for, Sergeant, and we'll both find out how much that's worth."

Rook watched him ride on across the bridge, northward, hooves striking sparks of frost off the planking, until the road bent and the trees took him.

Hesk came around the side of the tollhouse, kindling forgotten, axe still in his hand. "Who's that, then?"

"A gentleman," Rook said, "with a letter from a magistrate." He folded the paper and put it inside his coat, next to his heart, which had begun, quietly and without permission, to hurt.

* * *

They spent the next two days searching carts.

It was not unusual work — Hale's circular about seditious papers had come through the assize channels in the autumn, and half the sergeants between Millbeck and the marshes had been turning out peddlers' packs since Michaelmas. But there was a difference in it now, and Hesk felt it too; Rook could tell by the way the boy handled the goods they pulled apart. Gentler than he needed to. Apologetic.

On the second afternoon they stopped a tinker whose cart yielded nothing but kettles and a bundle of almanacs, and while Hesk rifled the almanacs for hidden leaves the tinker stood in the road working his cap and said, too casually, "Looking for someone particular, Sergeant?"

"Why do you ask?"

"Because you've stopped four carts since Tuesday and looked at nobody's face till now." The tinker grinned, gap-toothed, unafraid. "River folk talk, you know. Woman and a quiet boy, was it? Heading north?"

"Mind your cart," Rook said, and waved him through, and stood in the road afterward feeling the conversation sit wrong in his stomach, the way bad meat does. River folk talked, yes. But river folk didn't talk *to* the toll. River folk knew better. Which meant somebody wanted him to know the story was loose — or somebody wanted Quill to think Rook was hearing it loose.

He wrote it all in the post journal that night, in the careful round hand he'd been taught by a schoolmaster father who'd believed handwriting was character: *Two days' watch kept. No party matching description passed.* Then he sat with the pen still loaded, staring at the page, and finally added a line he had no business adding: *Searches conducted per instruction that papers be burned unopened. Requesting clarification in writing as to authority.*

He knew exactly what clarification would come back. He'd seen it once before, at a different bridge in a different year, when a captain had asked a similar question of similar men. The answer had been a visit, and after the visit the captain's journals went quiet, and six months later the captain was collecting rents in a town so far inland it might as well have been the moon. Asking was safe only if you never sent the asking anywhere. He wrote it to see how it looked. It looked like a man building himself a ladder out of a burning house and then standing in the house admiring the joinery.

He dipped the pen again and scratched the line out. Blacked it clean. But the ink had bitten deep, and under the lamp, tilted just so, the words rose through the strike-through like drowned things coming up for air: *burned unopened, requesting clarification, burned unopen—*

He shut the journal.

* * *

On the fourth night Quill came back down the north road, and this time he brought company: two riders Rook hadn't seen before, hard-mouthed men in rough wool doing a poor job of looking like farm laborers, and a mood that came off them like heat off a kiln.

"There's been a sighting," Quill said, without dismounting, without greeting. "South of here. A woman and a child, three nights past, near the Marsh End ferry."

"Then they went around my post," Rook said. "Ferry road doesn't touch the bridge."

"Ferry roads change. Ice changes." Quill's gaze went past Rook's shoulder to the bridge itself, to the dark running shape of the river locked under its white lid, and stayed there a moment longer than Rook liked. "Orders stand. But hear the amendment." He drew a folded slip from his glove. "The papers may be carried in oil. In tallow, in wax, in a flask or a jar. Search containers. Break seals on anything sealed, notwithstanding—" a thin flicker of distaste crossed his face, the first human thing Rook had seen him do, "—notwithstanding what the circular says about merchants' goods."

"You want me to open merchants' property at a King's toll," Rook said slowly, "and burn whatever I find, and enter none of it."

"I want you to stop asking questions in the shape of statements, Sergeant." Quill put the slip into Rook's hand rather than tossing it, pressing it flat against Rook's palm with two gloved fingers, holding it there a beat longer than necessary. "There is a man above you and a man above him and a man above *him*, and the man at the top eats magistrates for breakfast and picks his teeth with their commissions. You are being paid to hold a bridge. Hold the bridge."

They rode south, the two hard-wool men and the city enforcer, and Rook stood on the span in the dark listening to the hoofbeats fade and the ice mutter underneath, and he thought: *a woman and a child*. He thought about the phrasing of the letter. *Seditious and stolen nature.* Stolen from whom? Sedition was a crime against the Crown, and crimes against the Crown went to trial — loudly, publicly, with the papers read out in court so everyone could enjoy the shiver of them. That was half the pleasure of sedition prosecutions, and everyone involved knew it. Nobody tried sedition in the dark.

Whatever these papers were, they frightened no government Rook had ever sworn to. They frightened one man, whose name was on excellent cream-laid paper with a settled, patient hand, and who paid generously, and who wanted fire before reading.

Rook had burned things in his career. Contraband, mostly — brandy on the strand, tobacco in a customs yard, once a whole boatload of tea that had hurt him worse than any of it, being a man of simple tastes. Burning goods was easy. Goods couldn't speak.

But he remembered his father, the schoolmaster, making him copy out the same maxim forty times until his wrist ached: *What is written survives the writer.* He'd thought it nonsense at eight. He was thirty-nine now, and standing on a bridge in the dark, and it had become the truest sentence he owned.

If the papers were burned, the writer survived and nothing else did. Whatever the woman carried died with her, and the woman herself became only a runaway, and the boy only a mute child in the way, and Corven Hale went on eating magistrates' breakfasts until the river froze over hell.

And if the papers weren't burned —

Rook turned the slip of paper in his fingers. Then he held it to his lantern and let it catch, and watched it curl, and dropped the ash off the side of the bridge into the snow, where it made a small grey stain that would be gone by morning.

He was not yet sure what he intended to do. He only knew that when the moment came — and it was coming; he could feel it coming the way the ice felt the thaw, months early and underground — he wanted no more orders in his pocket than the ones he could account for.

Behind him, Hesk's voice, uncertain: "Sarge? There's a light on the south road. Cart, maybe. Coming up slow."

Rook buttoned his coat against the cold and turned toward the sound of wheels on frozen ground, and felt the crack run through him, quiet as the ice beginning to speak under the bridge.

"Light the lamps," he said. "Let's see whose face we're hunting."
