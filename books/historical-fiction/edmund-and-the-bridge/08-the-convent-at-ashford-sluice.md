## 8. The Convent at Ashford Sluice

The convent announced itself before they could see it: first the sound of water being made to work — a great wooden sluice gate chewing the river into obedience — then the smell of bread, woodsmoke and wet stone, and at last the buildings themselves, low and grey against the grey morning, huddled behind a wall as if the whole establishment were trying not to be noticed. Ashford Sluice wore its humility like armor. Odile, stiff from three days of borrowed carts and sleeping in hay, thought she had never seen anything so beautiful.

"You'll want to come at the water door," Tobias Finch said, reining the cart to a stop beneath a leaning willow. He nodded toward a small arched opening where the millrace ran beneath the wall. "Front gate's for pilgrims and tax men. Water door's for people who don't want remembering."

"How do you know this place?" Odile asked.

Finch's face did something complicated. "A man my line of work crosses many rivers, Mrs. Brightwater. Some of 'em owe me." He lifted Edmund down from the cart bed, and the boy landed lightly on his feet, eyes moving the way they always moved — walls first, then windows, then faces. "Sister Verity owes me twice over, and one of those debts has interest on it. Knock loud. The sisters are deaf when it suits them and sharp as needles when it doesn't."

Odile looked down at Edmund. His hand found hers without his seeming to notice he'd done it — a small cold weight of fingers that had learned to reach without asking. Three days since Millbeck Bridge and the oil flask and the sergeant's bored hands turning over their belongings; three days of pretending to be a widow and her nephew going to a christening; three nights of Edmund waking silent in the dark, mouth open around words that would not come out.

"We're nearly there," she told him, and was surprised to find she meant something larger than the convent.

He looked up at her. In the flat winter light his eyes were the color of river ice over deep water, and just as easy to misjudge. Then he squeezed her hand once — a child's gesture performed with a man's deliberation — and together they walked to the water door.

* * *

The woman who answered the knock was sixty if she was a day, tall the way old trees are tall — bent but unbroken — with forearms that spoke of decades of laundry and a face that spoke of nothing at all. Her habit was plain grey wool, patched at the hem. She looked at Finch's messenger boy, then at Odile, then at Edmund, and Odile watched her do in four seconds what Odile herself had spent three days learning to do: take the measure of a stranger's trouble by its posture alone.

"Mrs. Finch," the nun said.

"Sister Verity." Finch's boy grinned. "Tobias sends his regards and says you still owe him for the eel season of '39."

"The eel season of '39," Sister Verity said gravely, "was an act of God, and Tobias knows it. Come in before the frost does. You too, child." This last to Edmund, who had placed himself precisely at Odile's shoulder, half behind her. "We've bread still warm and a fire that doesn't smoke much, which in this house passes for a miracle."

Inside, the convent was warm in the particular way of buildings that have been heated continuously for two hundred years — the stones themselves seemed to give off comfort. They passed through a scullery where two novices were scaling fish, through a corridor smelling of tallow and dried herbs, into a refectory where a dozen women in grey sat eating in silence, and the silence was not empty but full, the way the lighthouse lamp room was full at night: a quiet chosen, maintained, inhabited.

Edmund stopped dead in the doorway.

Odile felt him go rigid against her side. She followed his gaze. The nuns sat in a long row, heads bowed, spoons rising and falling in perfect rhythm, and it took her a moment to understand what he was seeing — because she saw it too, suddenly, through whatever window his silence opened onto. Twelve women who did not speak. A room where no voice was required. A room built for someone exactly like him.

"Oh," Odile said softly. "Oh, child. No one here will ask you anything."

One of the nuns — young, freckled, quick-eyed — caught sight of Edmund's face and did something extraordinary: she raised her spoon, mimed eating, and smiled, all without a sound, an entire welcome conducted in gesture. And Edmund, after a heartbeat's hesitation, raised his own invisible spoon back.

Sister Verity watched this exchange with an expression Odile couldn't read, and said only: "Sit. Eat. Then we'll talk, you and I, while the boy rests."

* * *

The talk happened in a small cell-like room above the sluice itself, where the sound of the water came up through the floorboards like a second conversation running beneath theirs. Sister Verity poured chicory from a battered pot and pushed the cup across the table.

"Now," she said. "Tobias Finch sent you, which means you're either fleeing something or carrying something, and from the way you keep touching that bag under your cloak, I'd say the latter. I won't ask what it is. But I'll tell you what I can see, and you can correct me where I'm wrong." She folded her forearms on the table. "You're a lighthouse keeper. Greyhaven, by your accent and your hands — keeper's hands, oil and brass. You're widowed within the year. The boy is not yours by blood, he doesn't speak, and you'd die before you gave him up. And whatever's in that bag is worth more than money, because money you'd have hidden differently."

Odile stared at her. "How—"

"Forty years of listening to what people don't say, child. It's the order's true trade. We're a contemplative house mostly, but we run a ferry landing, a grain store, and the best information network between here and the assize town, and we do it all without a word passing our lips where it shouldn't." Sister Verity's eyes glinted. "Silence is a discipline. Eavesdropping is a vocation."

The absurdity of it loosened something in Odile's chest, and to her horror she felt tears prick. She looked down at the chicory.

"My husband," Odile said, because she had to start somewhere, and this was the load-bearing wall of everything. "Jonah Brightwater. He served on the *Merrow*, out of Greyhaven. She went down in the October gales two years past. Six hands lost." She heard her own voice go flat and official, the voice she used for the shipping news. "I believed for a long time it was weather. Weather is what you believe when believing otherwise would break you."

"And now?"

Odile's hand went again to the bag. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped again in her spare shift, lay the packet that had arrived at her door tied to a mute child: cargo manifests that should never have left Hale's office, a ledger of payments to men whose names meant wreckers and bribed harbor watchmen, and page after page in a clerk's neat hand recording sums that bought storms of a different kind. She had read them so many times she could recite them. *Received of C.H., for services rendered at sea, per arrangement.* The initials repeated like a refrain. Corven Hale. Magistrate Corven Hale, whose court had condemned the *Merrow*'s sister ship on manufactured evidence, whose name appeared on the harbor board rolls, whose signature — she had checked it against the assize records until her eyes burned — matched the initials to the farthing.

"Now," Odile said, "I know it wasn't weather."

She expected — she didn't know what. Shock. Horror. Instead Sister Verity was very still, and something moved behind her face like ice shifting on a river, and when she spoke her voice had changed register entirely.

"Corven Hale," the nun said. "Sit still, child, and listen, because I'm about to tell you why Tobias Finch owed me twice, and why your boy's face made him pale as whey when he saw it." She rose, crossed to a loose stone in the wall, and drew out a flat tin box. From the box she took a letter, yellowed, creased into softness from much folding and unfolding. "Do you know how the *Merrow* came to be sailing short-handed, with rotten cordage, in late October? Do you know why the harbor board let her go?"

"Negligence," Odile said. "I've cursed them for it every day since."

"Negligence, yes. Purchased negligence." Sister Verity laid the letter on the table but kept her hand flat upon it, as though it might fly away. "This came to us eighteen months ago, carried by a man who came at night through the water door, the same as you. He was a customs officer once — a good one, which is to say an inconvenient one. He had been gathering evidence against Hale for three years. Bribes, false condemnation of ships so their cargoes could be salvaged and sold, insurance fraud dressed as tragedy. He had names, dates, amounts." Her thumb moved across the letter's edge. "And then he made an error. He fell in love. Married a girl from the fishing rows, had a son. A wife and child are levers, and Hale is a man who understands leverage better than any living soul I've met. Our informant grew frightened — not for himself. For them. He wrote to us asking whether the convent could shelter a family, should it come to that."

The sluice thundered beneath the floorboards. Odile realized her breath had gone shallow.

"What happened?" she asked, though some part of her had already begun, terribly, to assemble the answer.

"He stopped writing. We heard later he'd fled north with his family. And then, last spring, word came down the river the way such word does — a customs officer dead in a fire. Warehouse on the Graywater, burned to the ground with him inside. Official finding: accident. Lamp knocked over, papers caught." Sister Verity's jaw tightened. "But warehouses belonging to Hale have a way of burning, and informants have a way of being inside them, and the woman and the boy—" she stopped. Her eyes went to the door, to the corridor beyond, where a silent child was presumably eating bread. "The woman died of fever that winter, before the fire even. And the boy vanished. Tobias searched for him along the river for months. Half the smugglers between here and the marshes were looking, though none would say why or for whom. Finch called it a debt."

Odile stood up. She didn't remember deciding to stand. Her legs simply refused to remain seated while her mind turned over and over.

"His name," she said. "Tell me his name."

"Edmund Voss," Sister Verity said quietly. "The boy's name is — was — Edmund Voss."

The room tilted and steadied. Edmund. Not a name pulled from nowhere, not a smuggler's whim, but the boy's own name, given back to her across all these miles and months. The child asleep against her door in the storm's wreckage. The child whose father had written to this convent begging shelter for a family that would never reach it. Whose father had gathered, over three patient dangerous years, exactly the kind of evidence that now crackled in its oilcloth against Odile's ribs.

"He sent him to me," Odile whispered. "Or someone sent him — but the packet, it's his father's work, it must be, the manifests, the ledger—" She pressed both hands flat on the table to stop them shaking. "Sister, I knew his father. Or I knew of him. Jonah spoke of a customs man on the Graywater, the only honest one, he said, the one who actually boarded ships and counted things. When the *Merrow* went down, that man wrote to me. One letter. He said he was sorry, and that the sea had been made wicked by men, and that someday someone would prove it. I thought it was grief talking. I kept the letter anyway. It's on my mantel at Greyhaven."

"Then you were known to each other after a fashion," Sister Verity said. "And he knew a widow who kept a light, and who owed her life to no one in Hale's orbit. If he prepared that packet before he died — if he arranged for his son and his evidence to travel together to the one person on this coast beyond Hale's reach — then he chose well and he chose cruelly, because he handed you both a burden and a boy, and only God can tell which he weighed heavier."

Outside, gulls cried over the sluice. Odile became aware that she was crying, silently, the tears arriving without ceremony, and that Sister Verity had slid a cloth across the table without comment, the way the sisters did everything — service without spectacle.

"I'm taking him to the assize court," Odile said at last. "The evidence goes before the judges when the circuit sits. That's what the packet is *for*. It's useless in a wall, useless in a grave. Names and sums mean nothing unless a court reads them aloud and Hale stands in the dock hearing them."

"That road runs through three checkpoints and Hale's own jurisdiction," Sister Verity observed. "You know this."

"I know it."

"And the boy. What happens to Edmund while you stand in a courtroom making speeches?"

Odile opened her mouth and found nothing there.

"Because," Sister Verity continued, gently, relentlessly, "here is what I offer, and I offer it once, so weigh it. This house has hidden men from press gangs, women from husbands, gold from commissioners of excise. We can hide Edmund Voss so thoroughly that within a month he'll be Sister Agnes' nephew from York, minding the grain store, learning letters from the novices. He'd be safe, Mrs. Brightwater. Truly safe, perhaps for the first time since the fire. Fed, taught, surrounded by women who ask nothing of his voice. And you—" she leaned forward, and her voice dropped into something almost tender, almost terrible, "you could go on to the assizes alone. Lighter. Faster. Better chance of arriving at all. Leave the boy in peace and go avenge your husband. Isn't that what you want? Both things? Peace for him, justice for Jonah Brightwater?"

And there it was, laid out on the table between the chicory pot and the yellowed letter: the temptation, wearing its best clothes. Odile sat with it. She let herself feel its full weight, because she refused to be a woman who pretended she hadn't considered it.

Three days on the road and she had already learned what fear cost. Every checkpoint ahead was a chance for a sergeant's bored eye to catch something. Every mile was a mile Hale's riders might cover faster. If she left Edmund here — behind grey walls, among the silent, the overlooked, the deliberately forgotten — she could move quicker, sleep less warily, burn fewer hours of the night watching a child breathe. And he would be happy here. She had seen it in the doorway, seen his answer to the freckled novice's mute greeting. This place was built for him. Perhaps, said the temptation in its reasonable voice, it was what his father had intended all along: the letter to the convent, the plan for shelter. You would only be finishing what poor Voss started.

But.

But the boy had torn a page from the ledger — she had discovered its absence on the second night, counted the sheets like a miser and found one missing, and said nothing, and watched him watch her not saying anything. Whatever he had taken, he had taken it for a reason, and he had not shown it to her yet, and that unfinished business sat between them like a debt. He had been given into her keeping with one instruction — *forget the face* — and instead she had memorized it, every bruise-colored shadow under his eyes, every flinch at raised voices, every night he woke with his mouth working around ashes. Forget the face. As if she could. As if anyone who had seen that face asleep against a door in the wind could forget it.

More than that — and this was the thing she could not say aloud in this room with its holy, practical silence — more than that was the simple arithmetic of the matter: the evidence was not hers alone anymore. Somewhere between Greyhaven and Millbeck, the packet had stopped being Voss's legacy and become theirs, hers and Edmund's, a thing they carried together like sailors carrying a boat through surf, each useless without the other. If she left him here she would arrive at the assize court with the proof in her bag and the point of it missing. Courts could hang Corven Hale. Only testimony — a living boy grown into a living man who remembered the fire, who remembered his father — could make the ruin complete and lasting. She was not carrying paper to the judges. She was carrying a witness. Witnesses needed to survive, yes. But they also needed to know why they had survived.

"No," Odile said.

Sister Verity regarded her for a long moment. The sluice roared and settled, roared and settled, like breathing.

"I hoped you'd say that," the nun said, "but I confess I hoped it for selfish reasons. If you'd left him, I'd have spent the rest of my life wondering if I'd talked a child out of his mother." She said it plainly, without apology, and Odile's throat closed on the word *mother* as though it were a bone. "Very well. You rest tonight — both of you, a full night, locked doors and my best dog across the stair. Tomorrow you'll want an early start, because the ice upstream is groaning and once the thaw begins in earnest the crossings turn murderous and the roads fill with soldiers. I'll give you provisions and a letter to Mother Scholastica at the drover's inn past Harrow Bend — she owes this house more debts than Finch could count."

"There's one more thing," Odile said, rising. "Edmund. There's a page missing from the ledger. He took it. I don't know what's on it, but he's been carrying it close as a heartbeat, and I think — I think it matters. I think it might be why he hasn't spoken. Like he's waiting for the right moment to hand over the last piece."

Something flickered across Sister Verity's face — recognition, maybe, or memory of another careful man with another secret document.

"Children keep faith the way we keep silence," she said. "Absolutely, and until it's needed. Don't pry it from him, child. Wait. The moment will make itself known." She stood, gathered the yellowed letter, and paused with her hand on the door. "One thing more, and mark it. If Hale learns where you've been — and Hale learns everything eventually — he will not send ordinary men next time. He'll send Rook. Sergeant Rook, of the Millbeck tolls. A hard man, but I've broken bread with hard men before." Her eyes were distant. "It's the ones who start asking questions you want to pray for. Sometimes they ask themselves right over to God's side of the river. Sometimes they don't."

She opened the door, and the corridor's warmth spilled in, and from somewhere below came the small sound of a child laughing without any sound at all — the novices, Odile guessed, teaching him some game of hands. She went down toward it, her legs still unsteady, the packet solid against her ribs, and thought: tomorrow the road again, the cold again, Hale's country again. But tonight, bread and fire and twelve women who would never ask her boy a question.

She found Edmund in the scullery, solemnly assisting the freckled novice scale a trout with the gravity of a surgeon, and when he looked up and saw her, his whole face changed — the vigilance dropping away for one unguarded instant, replaced by something so nakedly glad that she had to grip the doorframe.

Tomorrow, she promised him silently. Tomorrow we finish it.

She did not yet know that even then, thirty miles downstream, a sergeant with careful eyes was standing in a tollhouse turning a hollowed oil flask over in his hands, and asking his first question.
