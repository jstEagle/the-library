## 9. The Torn Page

The page had lived against his skin for nine days.

Edmund knew the number the way he knew his own heartbeat. Nine days since the fire, since the boat, since Sister Verity's cold hands had pushed him down into the hold among the salt barrels and told him to count until she came back. She had never come back. He had counted to four thousand and something before the smugglers found him, and he had stopped counting after that, because counting was a thing you did when you believed someone was coming.

But the page had come with him. Folded into eight, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft as chamois, sewn into the lining of his coat where a button used to be. He had done the sewing himself with sail twine, in the dark of the smugglers' hold, with his fingers so cold they felt like someone else's fingers. His father had taught him the stitch. *A man who cannot mend his own coat will be dressed by strangers,* Papa had said, kneeling at the hearth with a needle in his great blunt hand, and Edmund had laughed because Papa's stitches always looked like a drunk centipede walking.

Nine days. And now the page lay against his ribs like a coal that would not cool, and every night the woman — Odile, Odile Brightwater, keeper of the Greyhaven light — banked the stove and sat in her chair with her ledger book and did not look at him, and every night the page burned a little hotter through the cloth.

He knew what she wanted. She wanted it the way the sea wants the shore: patiently, endlessly, without ever once saying so.

* * *

He watched her the way he watched everything now. That was the word he had found for it in his own head — *watching*, because he could not call it living, not quite, this half-life of eyes and ears while his voice sat locked somewhere behind his teeth like a coin dropped down a well.

Odile Brightwater moved through the lighthouse the way water moves through a channel: certain of her course, wasting nothing. She rose in the dark and trimmed the wicks. She logged the wind in a small copperplate hand — *WNW freshening by noon, glass falling* — as if the weather were a flock of sheep she was responsible for counting in. She spoke to the gulls on the gallery rail in a low, scolding, affectionate voice, and once he heard her tell a one-legged bird named Corporal that he was a thief and a coward and then give him half her herring.

She had not asked him about the packet. Not once. He had seen her break the seal — he had been pretending to sleep on the settle, watching through his lashes the night she thought he was safely dreaming — and he had seen her face when she read the manifests and the bribe ledger and understood whose initials marched down the margins like an army of small black beetles. C.H. C.H. C.H. He had watched grief cross her face the way weather crosses water, and then he had watched it turn into something harder and straighter, and he had known that she would go north, that she would spend herself utterly on this errand, because it was not only her errand. It was hers and it was Papa's and it was Mama's, all braided together, though she did not know the last two strands yet.

That was the thing. That was the coal burning through his coat.

The packet named Hale. But it did not name him *plainly*. It showed the shape of the crime the way a shadow shows the shape of a man — accurate, but only if you already know what stands in the light. The torn page did not deal in shadows. Papa had written it plainly, in words Edmund had read so many times he could see them with his eyes shut:

*I, Josiah Crane, customs rider for the port of Wexholm, do set down the truth while I have breath. Magistrate Corven Hale took my reports of his shipments and used them to sink the ships of men who would not pay him — the *Glorianna* among them, wrecked on the Shingle with all hands but one, that the survivor might carry terror back to the harbor board. I have carried this too long. If I die, let it be known: Hale bought the storm, and the storm was made of ledgers.*

*All hands but one.* Edmund had not known, until Odile's ledger book fell open one evening and he saw the name written in the front of it in faded ink — *Thomas Brightwater, my husband, keeper of this light* — that the one hand saved from Papa's drowned ship was the hand of the woman who had taken him in. God arranged things sometimes like a knot pulled tight. He did not know whether it was mercy or mockery. He suspected both.

So the page was the keystone, and he held it, and every day he did not give it to her he felt himself become a smaller, meaner kind of boy — a hoarder, a miser of truth — and every day he did not give it, either, he felt the old warning beat in his blood like a second heart.

*Forget the face.*

That was the last thing Papa ever said to him. Not *I love you*, though Edmund knew it, knew it the way he knew salt was in the sea, without needing it said. Not *run*, though he ran. *Forget the face,* whispered into his hair with the warehouse burning behind them and the smoke tasting of pitch and paper, Papa's hand crushing his shoulder through the coat — and Edmund had understood, even at eight years old, even with his whole world going up in orange light behind him, that his father was telling him to forget his own face. To survive by becoming no one's son. To live by letting Josiah Crane die completely.

He had tried. God knew he had tried. On the boat, in the hold, on Odile's doorstep where the smugglers left him like a parcel, he had folded his father's face up small and put it away in the same dark place where his voice had gone. And it worked the way a dam works, which is to say it held, and held, and held, while everything behind it rose.

* * *

It broke on a Tuesday, over herrings.

Or rather, over the lack of them. The storm had kept the boats ashore for three days, and Odile stood at the little stove turning a piece of salt cod as if it had personally offended her, and Edmund sat at the table with his copybook — she had given him a slate first, then paper, as if literacy were a rope she could throw a drowning child — practicing the word *lighthouse* over and over until the *g* stopped looking like a spider.

And she said, without turning from the stove, in the mild voice she used for the weather: "Corporal brought us nothing again. We'll be eating the gull next, at this rate."

And Edmund laughed.

It came out of him before he could stop it — a short, cracked, rusty sound, like a gate that hadn't swung in a year — and it was not the laugh itself that undid him but the way she turned. She turned fast. Too fast for a woman pretending indifference. And her eyes went wide and wet in the space of a single breath, and she looked at him the way people look at a lamp relit in a window they'd given up on, and he saw what his silence had been doing to her for nine days, and the dam did not crack.

It shattered.

"I can't," he said.

His voice came out wrong, half-swallowed, a stranger's voice. Odile stood frozen, the fork dripping in her hand. Outside, the sea worked at the rocks below with its patient patient hands.

"Can't what?" she said softly, and he understood she was afraid to move, afraid to startle the wild thing that had finally spoken, and something in her gentleness made him angrier than any demand could have.

"Can't forget the face." It tore out of him. "He told me to. Forget the face, forget the face, that's the last — that's *all* he — " His hands were fists on the table, whitened. "I'm forgetting it. Every day it gets a bit more — I couldn't tell you this morning what color his coat was. Brown. It was brown. Or grey. It was *one of those* and I can't remember which and you want me to forget him *on purpose*—"

"No." She crossed the room and knelt, still holding the fork, absurdly, a knight at a coronation armed with a fish-fork. "No, child. Listen to me. I have never once asked you to forget him."

"You don't ask me anything. That's how you ask."

She flinched as if he'd struck her. Good, some bitter corner of him said, and then was ashamed, because her eyes were shining and steady and full of a patience that had cost her something to build.

"You're right," she said. "I haven't asked. Because I've watched enough grieving to know that being asked is not the same as being ready. But I'll ask you one thing now, plain, and you may hate me for it or not, as you choose." She laid the fork down on the table with great ceremony, as if disarming herself. "Whatever you're carrying in that coat lining — and I have known since your third morning here, boy, you sleep like a cat on a fresh grave — whatever it is, it is wearing you thinner than hunger ever could. You may keep it forever. It is yours; I'll not pry it from you. But you should know that you don't have to be the only one who holds it. That's all. That's the whole of what I'd say, if you'd let me say anything at all."

Edmund sat very still. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them the great lens turned in its bath of mercury, sweeping its beam across the dark like a slow silver scythe, and the sea said what the sea always says.

"You knew," he said. "About the lining."

"I mend clothes for a living, or near it. A button missing where buttons aren't missing from, and new thread where there was no tear." She shrugged. "I didn't look closer. It wasn't mine to look at."

*It wasn't mine to look at.* He turned the phrase over the way he'd turned the smooth green stone in his pocket, the one from the shingle below the point, worrying it. Nine days she had known and said nothing. Nine days she had fed him, taught him, laughed for him at the gulls, and never once reached into his coat.

Papa would have liked her. The thought arrived whole and unbearable. Papa, who trusted slowly and completely; who had spent three years gathering evidence against Corven Hale like a man building a wall one hidden stone at a time; who would have recognized in this spare, competent, sea-worn woman exactly what Edmund was only now recognizing — that here was a person who kept things. Lights. Ledgers. Faiths. Promises.

His fingers were already at his coat. He had unbuttoned it before his mind caught up with his hands.

* * *

The stitching took longer to unpick than it had taken to sew. His fingers shook. Odile knelt across from him and did not help, which he understood, dimly and gratefully, was its own kind of help. When the last loop of sail twine came free he drew out the little oilcloth bundle, and it seemed impossibly light for what it was, for everything it had cost, no heavier than a moth.

He unwrapped it on the table, between the salt cod and the copybook with its marching spiders of *lighthouse lighthouse lighthouse*, and smoothed the page flat with both palms.

Odile did not reach for it. She leaned close instead, reading over his shoulder like a schoolmate, and he watched her lips move faintly with the words — *I, Josiah Crane* — and watched the color go out of her face and come back changed.

"*The Glorianna*," she whispered. "He wrote it down. He wrote it plain — Hale bought the storm—" Her hand rose halfway toward the page and stopped, hovering, as if the paper were hot. "This is your father's hand?"

"Yes."

"He wrote this when?"

"The night of the fire. He'd finished it that week. He read it to me at supper like scripture and made me say it back." Edmund's voice steadied strangely; the well was giving up its coin at last, and each word came easier than the last. "He said the packet was the case and this page was the soul of it. He said anyone could deny a ledger, but no man can deny a dying confession witnessed and signed." He swallowed. "Then the warehouse burned. He'd been followed. He put the packet in my coat and the page in my hand and he said — he said take it to the Sisters at Ashford Sluice, they know what it is, and then the door came in and there wasn't time for Ashford, there was only time for—"

"Forget the face," Odile said quietly.

Edmund stared at her. "How—"

"Because it's what I'd have said." Her jaw was tight. "If I had a son, and men like that hunting him, and one minute to make him safe — I'd have made him forget me. God forgive us both, it's the only mercy we know how to give." She looked down at the page again, and now her finger touched it, tracing the signature, the date, the two witness marks below it in other hands. "*All hands but one,*" she read aloud, and her voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "Thomas. My Thomas was the one."

"Yes," Edmund said. "I knew. I saw your ledger."

They knelt there on either side of the table, the orphan and the widow, the page between them like a bridge built of one dead man's handwriting. And Edmund found, to his astonishment, that he was crying — silently, without drama, tears simply standing on his lashes and dropping — and that Odile was too, and that neither of them made any move to hide it or comfort it, because it was not that kind of crying. It was the kind that does work. The kind that comes when a weight you have carried alone is suddenly, carefully, set down upon two pairs of shoulders, and the shoulders hold, and you discover the weight has not lessened but has somehow become *bearable*, the way a beam bears a roof only when both ends rest on something solid.

"My husband pulled your father from that water," Odile said at last. "Did you know that? Your father wrote it in his report — *saved from the wreck by one T. Brightwater, keeper of the Greyhaven light, a man of extraordinary calm.* I used to tease Thomas about it. *A man of extraordinary calm,* I'd say, and he'd say, *well, someone in the boat had to be.*" She laughed, wet and broken. "They knew each other, Edmund. Your father and my husband. And now their children—"

She stopped. Corrected herself, deliberately, looking right at him: "*Their child and their widow are sitting at the same table, and Hale thinks he has buried the whole affair under two graves and a fire.*"

Edmund wiped his face with his sleeve. Underneath the grief, something new had been quietly installed, some beam set into a wall, and he tested it the way you test a rail before trusting it with your weight.

"We should sew it back in," he said. "Both pages. Together."

"Together," she agreed. Then, rising, taking down her workbox, threading a needle with the quick sure motions he had already begun to love: "Though I'll do the sewing. Your father's trick with the knot is an abomination, and I say that with respect for the dead."

Edmund laughed again — properly this time, surprised out of it — and the sound filled the round stone room and went up the stair like a lit candle climbing.

* * *

They worked by lamplight, the two of them, heads bent over the coat. She taught him a better stitch. She let him do half the seam himself, and did not pretend his crooked line was straighter than it was, only said, *it will hold, and holding is the whole art.*

When the bundle was sewn home again — packet and confession now one, wrapped as one, carried as one — Odile sat back on her heels and was quiet for a long moment, looking at the small dark shape of it under the cloth.

"Hale's men will be looking," she said at last. Not a question. She had been turning toward north for nine days; he could feel her turning now like a compass needle finding iron.

"Sister Verity said they'd come. Rook's men. They burn what they find."

"Then we won't be found." She said it simply, the way she logged the weather. *Glass falling. We won't be found.* And then she looked at him, and her voice dropped into something rawer. "Edmund. Once we start north there's no coming back to this light until it's finished. It will be cold, and dangerous, and men will try to kill us for what's in your coat. I need to know you understand — not as a child understands, but as a shipmate does. Are you with me?"

He thought about the brown coat. Or grey. He thought about *forget the face*, and understood, finally, that his father had never meant for the face to die — only to be kept somewhere safe until the world was fit to bring it out again. That was not forgetting. That was keeping. And keeping, it turned out, was the family trade on both sides of the table.

"With you," he said aloud, in his rusty new voice, and meant it the way you mean an oath.

Odile nodded once, shipmate to shipmate, and blew out the lamp between them — the great light above needed no help from them tonight — and in the sudden dark her hand found his shoulder and rested there, warm and certain, and did not let go for a long while.

It was two days later, in the cold blue hour before dawn, that Edmund woke to the sound of hoofbeats on the coast road, many of them, riding hard through the frost — and to Odile already standing at the window, dressed, the oilcloth bundle in her hand, whispering the only words that mattered now:

"They've found the Sisters at Ashford. We leave within the hour."
