## 10. Rook Closes In

Sergeant Rook had searched four hundred carts in eleven years, and he had learned that people lied with their hands before they lied with their mouths. The mouth said *nothing to declare, sergeant* while the hands smoothed a coat hem, or shifted a basket half an inch, or — the tell he trusted above all others — went perfectly, deliberately still.

So it was the stillness of the oil merchant's boy at Millbeck that had stayed with him the way a splinter stays, working its way toward the surface for days afterward.

He sat now in the tollhouse with the shuttered lamp burning low and the oil flask itself standing on the table between his elbows like a witness refusing to testify. It was a common enough flask: stoneware, blue-glazed, sealed with cork and wax, stamped with the mark of the Chandler's Arms depot in Greyhaven. He had pulled it from under the sacking in a cart of turnips two days after the woman and child had passed. He had weighed it in his hand, heard the wrong kind of slosh — too thick, too quiet — and drawn the cork on the spot.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and packed tight around a hollow core, had been papers. Or space where papers had been. That was the thing that kept him awake. The flask was built to hide something, and something had been taken out of it before it ever reached his checkpoint — or nothing had ever been put in, and the flask was a decoy, a coin tossed to keep the sentry's eyes busy while the true cargo walked past him wearing a smile.

Either way, he had been played. And either way, whoever had played him had gone north with papers worth hiding from a magistrate's own enforcer.

Rook turned the flask under the lamplight and read the stamp again. Greyhaven. A lighthouse station, a hamlet of forty souls, a chandler who supplied lamp oil to the coast road as far inland as Ashford Sluice. Oil went up that road every fortnight. Nuns bought oil; convents burned lamps all night over their prayers.

Ashford Sluice. He had been turning the thought over since morning, and it would not turn into anything else.

* * *

Corven Hale's man arrived at Millbeck an hour past dusk, riding hard through the freezing fog with his collar up and his horse lathered despite the cold. His name was Quill — a thin, neat, unhurried man who wore good boots and carried a letter with a seal Rook was not invited to break.

"The woman is called Brightwater," Quill said, when they were alone in the tollhouse and the door was shut against the wind. "Widow. Keeps the light at Greyhaven Point. She has a boy with her, perhaps nine years old, dark-haired, does not speak. You let them pass your gate on the ninth."

"I search what comes through," Rook said. "I don't memorize faces for men I haven't met."

Quill smiled the way a knife smiles, which is not at all. "You do now." He laid a second paper on the table — a description, written in a clerk's hand: height, coloring, a grey coat with mended cuffs, a child who made no sound. Then a third paper, and this one was not a description but an instruction, and Rook read it twice because the first reading seemed impossible.

*Where the papers are found, burn them and the place that holds them. Where the persons are found, hold the woman and bring her south. The boy is not to be harmed. The boy is not to be questioned by anyone below Mr. Hale.*

"Burn the place that holds them," Rook repeated. "The place being?"

"Whatever it is. A barn. A house." Quill warmed his hands at the brazier as though discussing freight rates. "A convent, if it comes to that."

"There are nuns at Ashford Sluice."

"There are nuns everywhere, Sergeant. God is generous that way." Quill took back the instruction but left the description. "You have until the ice breaks to find them. After the thaw, the assizes sit at Stonecross, and if these papers reach the court, a great many men discover that their names have been read aloud in public, and Mr. Hale discovers that he has been patient with incompetent subordinates." He paused at the door, and for one moment the smoothness cracked and something older and harder looked out. "I have hanged men for less than a mislaid ledger, Sergeant Rook. Find the widow."

Then he was gone into the fog, and Rook stood alone with the lamp oil and the description of a woman he could not stop seeing: a tall figure in a grey coat, mended cuffs, a boy's hand in hers, both of them looking straight ahead as the cart rolled through his gate, and the boy's hands — he remembered it now, he had noticed it without knowing he noticed it — folded absolutely still in his lap.

* * *

Rook did not sleep. He had stopped sleeping properly years ago, somewhere between the third winter on the Millbeck toll and the night a debtor named Cally had begged him at the checkpoint gate with a sick child in the cart and Rook had turned him back because the regulation said goods crossed at posted rates and men crossed with papers. The child had lived. Rook never learned so, but he chose to believe it, the way a man chooses to believe in fair weather.

He sat with the flask and thought about how a trick works backwards. forwards, a trick is a story: here is a cart of turnips, see the turnips, nothing else to see. Backwards, it is arithmetic. The flask came from Greyhaven. It was traveling away from Greyhaven, north, on the only road that leads to Stonecross and the courts. Between Greyhaven and his own gate lay forty miles of coast road, the marsh crossings, and exactly one settlement large enough to hide a woman and child and careless enough to be found: the convent at Ashford Sluice, which bought its lamp oil from the same chandler whose stamp sat on the flask, which meant the flask had likely been filled — or emptied, or both — within a mile of the sisters' gate.

And there was another arithmetic, quieter and worse. Hale wanted papers burned. Not seized, not returned, not examined — burned, along with whatever roof happened to be over them. Men did not burn evidence that would acquit them. Rook had spent eleven years enforcing the law of the country, and he knew in his marrow the difference between a magistrate protecting the peace and a magistrate protecting himself, and everything about the sealed letter in Quill's breast pocket smelled of the second kind. The boy was to be untouched. The boy. What manner of evidence mattered less than the child carrying it? Only evidence that could hang its bearer's protector — or evidence the protector feared for reasons no ledger fully explained.

He thought of Brightwater. He did not know her face, but he knew her type, having searched her type four hundred times: the coastal poor, the ones the sea widows quarterly. A lighthouse keeper had no stake in sedition. A lighthouse keeper had a dead husband and a mute child and a long cold walk north, and she was running from a man who sent riders in fog with orders to commit arson on consecrated ground.

Rook blew out the lamp and, in the dark, admitted the thing he had been circling all night: he was going to look. And once he looked, he would have to choose, because Quill had left him no third road. Report what you find and burn what holds it, or find nothing, say nothing, and become the sort of man whose incompetence was convenient.

He had been that sort of man for eleven years. He told himself the fog made the roads impassable anyway.

* * *

The roads were, in truth, foul. Sleet had glazed the coast track overnight and the marsh causeways ran black water over ice, so that Rook's journey to Ashford took a day and a half and cost him a shoe sole to the wet and any remaining belief that heaven ordered the weather on the side of magistrates. He traveled alone, out of uniform, in a fishmonger's smock he had used twice before for work he did not write in the log. His subordinate, Corporal Dane, a steady young man with ambitions, asked where he was bound.

"Checking the sluice gates," Rook said. "Ice damage."

Dane's eyes stayed on him a moment too long. Dane counted the hours Rook was gone. Rook noted that he noted it, and filed it under debts he would eventually have to pay.

He came to Ashford Sluice at vespers, when the fen was turning violet and the convent bell sounded small and clear across the frozen ditches. The convent was a low stone pile grafted onto an older chapel, its garden stark, its dovecote smoking gently. A lay sister admitted him to the parlor, and presently Mother Superior Verity came — a spare woman of sixty-odd with ink on her fingers and the unfooled eyes of someone who had heard eleven thousand confessions and remembered nine thousand of them.

"Fishmonger," Rook said, by way of his disguise, and then abandoned it, because lying to this woman felt like lying in church. "Sergeant Rook, of the Millbeck toll. I'm not here in my office, madam. Or rather I am, and that's the difficulty."

She sat, folded her hands, and waited with the patience of her trade.

"You've had guests," Rook said. "A woman and a boy. She'd be tall, grey coat, sea-worn. The boy doesn't speak."

"We shelter many travelers."

"I know it. And most of them are only travelers. But there are men coming behind me who are not checking sluice gates." He laid Quill's description on the table between them and watched her eyes, which did not move to read it, which was answer enough. "There's a magistrate south of here whose name may appear on certain papers. If those papers reach the assize court at Stonecross, that magistrate falls. If they don't, he doesn't, and the people who carried them —" he tapped the description — "become a problem that men like me are paid to make disappear. There's a standing instruction regarding this woman. It involves fire."

Something moved behind the nun's composure — not fear exactly; more like the settling of weight onto known ground.

"You are telling me this why, Sergeant?" she said. "You have ridden a day and a half through sleet to warm me of danger. Forgive an old woman, but I've buried three bishops and I can smell a career when it walks in."

"Because I've decided not to report you," Rook said. "Not yet. Maybe not at all. And I want you to understand what that costs, so you don't waste it." He heard his own voice go low and rough, and let it. "Eleven years I've stood at that gate at Millbeck and enforced the law as it was handed to me, and mostly it was handed to me by decent men. This week it wasn't. This week it came sealed and it says burn. I've turned back starving men for want of papers, madam, and I'll answer for that at whatever court sits last. But I'll not hand a widow and a child to a man who burns houses over a ledger. So. Tell me they're here and I'll ride back slow and misremember the road. Tell me they're gone and I'll believe you if your face tells me to. But don't lie to me cleverly, because clever lies are what got us all into this."

Mother Verity regarded him for a long moment. Outside, the bell finished its vespers and the fen went quiet enough to hear ice shifting in the ditches.

"They were here," she said at last. "I will not tell you where they've gone, because you may yet be forced to swear to things, and I won't put perjury on a man who came to warn me. They are alive. They are moving north. And Sergeant — the boy's father died for telling the truth about Corven Hale. Whatever else you carry home from this errand, carry that."

Rook nodded slowly. It was more than he had asked and less than he needed, and somehow exactly enough.

"The man they sent after them is called Quill," he said, rising. "He's careful, and he's cruel in the efficient way, and he'll work the same arithmetic I worked, though slower, because I've been searching carts on this road longer than he's been polishing boots. When he comes — and he will come — you never saw me. This conversation happened nowhere. If he asks whether a sergeant of the toll passed through, you'll be tempted to protect me by admitting it. Don't. My protection is worth more than my friendship right now, to you and to them."

"And to yourself, Sergeant."

"Yes," Rook said. "To myself also. I'd take it kindly if we didn't pretend otherwise."

At the door he paused, smock smelling of fish and sleet, and looked back at the old woman in the lamplight. Eleven years of regulations stood behind him; ahead lay the dark road home, the log book waiting for his lie about sluice gates, Corporal Dane counting hours.

"Sergeant," Mother Verity said quietly, as if to his back, "what you're doing has a name older than treason. We pray for such men on Thursdays."

"Pray quickly, then," Rook said. "The ice breaks early this year."

* * *

He rode back through the fen in full darkness, following the causeway by memory and the fen lights' faint smear, and it was on the long straight stretch near Harrow Bend that he saw the rider coming south — a thin, neat shape against the snowfield, moving fast, unhurried even at speed.

Quill, already working the road behind him, already halfway to Ashford.

Rook drew his horse into the ditch-shadow and let the rider pass, and sat there in the cold doing the final arithmetic: a day and a half of warning, perhaps two if the sleet held, and after that a careful cruel man walking the same backwards trail — Greyhaven, the chandler's stamp, the oil flask, the nuns — with a letter in his pocket that said *burn*.

He turned his horse north along a drover's cut that no magistrate's clerk had ever bothered to map, and rode to intercept the only road the widow could take.

If he could not unknow what he knew, he would stand on the road where it mattered.
