## 11. Flight Across the Marsh

The cousin's name was Hester, and she smelled of peat smoke and eel-oil, and she did not once ask why a lighthouse keeper and a silent boy needed to cross Ashen Fen in the dark of a February night.

"Boots off," she said instead, when Odile came down the ladder-stairs from Finch's loft with Edmund's hand cold in hers. "Tie the laces round your neck. The mud eats boots, and you'll want them on the far side." She looked Odile up and down with the flat appraisal of a woman who had spent forty years reading water. "You'll do. Can you swim?"

"Yes."

"The boy?"

"I — I don't know."

Hester grunted, as if that answer told her something useful anyway. "Then keep him close as your own skin, and closer if you can manage it."

They left by the back of the eel-sheds, where the reeds grew tall enough to swallow a grown woman whole, and Hester led them into the fen along paths that were not paths — a sequence of hummocks and half-drowned causeways that existed only in her feet, apparently, because Odile could not have reconstructed any two hundred yards of it to save her life. Which, she reflected grimly, might be precisely the point.

The moon was down. The stars gave a thin grey light, no more, and the fen drank even that. Odile learned quickly to stop trusting her eyes and trust instead the give of the ground underfoot: firm here, treacherously soft there, and everywhere the wet black smell of winter rot beneath a skin of frost. Edmund walked just ahead of her, one hand gripping the hem of her coat, and every time his grip shifted she felt it like a word spoken aloud.

He had not spoken since she'd known him. Not once. And yet he said more with that handful of wool than most men managed with a pulpit.

* * *

For the first hour they made good time, or what Hester called good time, which meant they did not lose anyone to a bog. Then, somewhere past the drowned alders that marked the old boundary between Finch's cuttings and the church lands, Hester stopped so abruptly that Odile walked into her back.

"Lanterns," Hester breathed.

Odile listened. At first there was nothing but the wind moving the reeds with a sound like a great congregation whispering behind its hands. Then she heard it: a faint creak and splash, irregular, out of rhythm with the fen. Someone was poling a punt through the drainage channels. More than one someone.

"How many?" Hester whispered.

"Two? Three?"

"Three, then. It's always three when it's trouble." Hester turned, and in the starlight her face was all angles and old scars. "They're on the Millbeck cut. That runs parallel to us till the second sluice. They'll see our lights if we show any, hear us if we're clumsy. So we don't show lights, and we're not clumsy. Understand?"

Odile understood. What she said was: "How far to the drover's road?"

"Four hours, if the drain-cuts are frozen over. Two days, if they're not, and we have to walk round." Hester spat, expertly, into the dark. "And there's the rub, keeper. It's been a cruel warm spell for February. The cuts may be nothing but skin over soup."

Behind them, faint but unmistakable now, came a voice — a man's voice, raised not in alarm but in the easy carrying tone of men conducting a search they expected to succeed. Odile could not make out the words. She found she did not need to. Sergeant Rook had been asking after a woman and child from Millbeck onward; Finch had warned her of that much before refusing her passage north. Some other ferryman, some other informant, had sold them already. The fen was full of ears.

"Hester," she said. "I can pay more."

"You can put your money away," Hester said shortly. "My cousin owes a debt I've heard about my whole life, and debts like that get paid by the family. Besides—" the old woman's teeth showed briefly in the dark— "I've always wanted to be worth hunting. Come on. Faster now, and quieter, which means slower than you think. Follow my steps exactly. Where I step, you step. Nowhere else."

* * *

They went faster, which was slower than Odile thought she could bear, each footfall tested and transferred, Hester's long pole feeling out the ground before them like a blind man's cane. Twice the pole went through a crust of frost-ice into open water beneath with a sound like a shot, and both times they stood frozen, breath held, until the searchers' voices faded or moved on. Edmund never faltered. He copied Hester's movements with an eerie exactness, as though silence and caution were simply another language he'd been raised in, and Odile felt her heart turn over watching him. Whatever childhood this boy had been given, it had not been a childhood. It had been a preparation.

Somewhere in the third hour the ground changed. The frost-skin thinned; the reeds grew ranker; and ahead of them Odile heard the thing Hester had been dreading — a low, restless gurgle, the sound of water moving under ice that was not truly ice anymore.

"Drain-cut," Hester said. "Ashen Cut. Runs straight as a sermon for two miles, ten feet across, deeper than a hanging judge. Used to freeze solid in January." She prodded the surface with her pole. The crust dimpled, wept, held. Mostly held. "We go along the near bank till the ford at the willow, or we swim, or we die. I vote willow. Keep him by you, keeper."

They edged along the cut. The water beside them was black and alive, breathing little bubbles up through fissures in the rotten ice, and Odile kept her fist knotted in the back of Edmund's coat so hard her fingers ached. On their left, perhaps half a mile back now, the lanterns had turned. They were following the cut too. Of course they were. A drain-cut was a road, and roads ran to fords, and fords were where you caught people.

"Hester," Odile whispered. "The lanterns."

"I see them."

"How long to the willow?"

"Quarter mile. They'll be there first."

The arithmetic was simple and terrible. Odile did it anyway, twice, hoping the answer would change. It didn't. Ahead lay a ford that would be watched within minutes; behind lay three boats and whatever men Hale could hire in a fen parish at midnight; below lay ten feet of black water wearing a lie of white skin.

There was a fourth option. She saw it at the same moment Edmund did — saw him see it, in the way his head lifted toward the far bank, thirty feet of broken ice away, where the reeds began again and the road north continued. He was small. He weighed nothing. A boy who had been raised on silence and quick movement might cross a fen-cut on cracking ice the way he seemed able to do everything else — lightly, impossibly.

"No," Odile said, and the word came out of her with a force that surprised them both.

Edmund looked back at her. In the dark she couldn't read his face, only its outline, but she knew what he was doing: weighing, calculating, deciding to save them all by saving himself fastest. It was what the world had taught him. Everyone left; everyone saved themselves first; love was a coat you wore until the water took it.

"Not you," she said. "Not ever you. If anyone crosses ice tonight, it's me going first to test it, and you following exactly where I've been, holding my hand. Do you understand me?"

She could not see him nod. She felt it, in the coat-hem still clutched in his fist — a single fierce tug, and then his small cold fingers finding hers and locking on.

"There's a fallen trunk, fifty yards back," Hester murmured. "Ash. Might span it. Might not. Back we go."

Backward, then, along the bank of the cut, while the lanterns drew level somewhere ahead and their voices carried clear across the flat water now — Rook's name among them, she was nearly sure, a sergeant's clipped cadence giving orders to men who answered too slowly. Her heart hammered so hard she feared it would shake the ice. Every step backward felt like retreat, and retreat felt like the death of everything: the packet against her breast, the confession page sewn into Edmund's lining, the assize court sitting in nine days across a river she hadn't reached yet.

Then Edmund's hand wrenched out of hers.

She would replay the next four seconds for the rest of her life. The crust under his heel, thinner where a spring fed in — she had seen the dark patch, had opened her mouth to call the warning — the crack like a rifle-shot, the white skin folding inward like paper, and the boy dropping through it without a cry, without a sound, as though even drowning he intended to inconvenience no one.

Odile was moving before the crack finished echoing.

* * *

The cold hit her like a wall falling. Not cold — annihilation, the fen-water reaching up her body and stopping her heart's argument mid-beat, her lungs seizing shut, every joint locking. Ten feet across the cut, they had said. She had gone in at the hole; Edmund had gone in further out, swept sideways under the unbroken lid, and she could see nothing, absolutely nothing, only the pale starlit ceiling of ice above her and the black pressing in from every direction like a living thing.

Her hands found reeds. Useless. Found the underside of ice, smooth and endless. Then — cloth. Wool, small, a sleeve. She seized it and hauled, and the weight that came with it fought her, twisting, and for one endless heartbeat she thought: *he is caught, he is snagged on something below, I cannot lift him, God, I cannot—*

Then his fist struck her collarbone, blindly, wildly, alive, and every civilized thought left her body.

What happened after that she did afterward remember only in pieces, the way one remembers a storm: her own face breaking the ice from below, once, twice, the crust being thinner near the hole as Hester had promised; the shriek of splitting frost; air, brutal and sweet; her arms around the boy dragging him up onto the bank through reeds that cut her palms like knives; Hester shouting something from very far away; and underneath all of it, steady as a keel, one enormous certainty, so large it did not feel like her own thought at all:

*You will live. Both of you will live. I have buried one man in this water's kindred and I will not bury you. You are mine now, do you hear me, whoever left you at my door, whatever name you carry — you are mine, and mine does not drown in a ditch.*

He was breathing. That was the first fact, and it eclipsed all others. He was coughing, curled on his side, vomiting fen-water into the frost, and she was kneeling over him with her hands pressed flat to his small back as though she could hold his life inside his body by force.

"Breathe," she was saying — she heard herself, hoarse, unrecognizable. "Breathe, Edmund. Breathe out. Again. Again, love, again—"

*Love.* The word had come out of her mouth without permission. She found she did not want it back.

* * *

They could not stay. However much her arms shook, however blue Edmund's lips had gone and were only now, slowly, horribly, coming back to some color a child ought to have, the lanterns were still on the water, and the crack of breaking ice carried across a fen like a bell. Hester appeared out of the reeds, gray-faced, having stripped off her own outer coat, and they got the wet things off Edmund and bundled him into dry wool that smelled of eels and peat smoke, and Odile realized she was soaked through herself and shivering so violently her teeth chattered like dice.

"Warm spell's done you a favor after all," Hester said roughly, wringing the hem of Odile's skirt with hands that shook only slightly less than Odile's own. "Water that cold should have stopped both your hearts."

"It tried," Odile said.

The boy looked up at her. Water had plastered his hair to his forehead; there was mud on his cheek and terror, recently lived-in, all over his face, and he looked at her the way she had seen sailors look at a harbor light after a bad crossing — not with gratitude, which is polite, but with the dumb animal recognition that one has been pulled from a specific darkness by a specific pair of hands.

His mouth opened.

Nothing came out. But it opened, and stayed open a moment, and Odile understood that he had tried — that some word had climbed all the way up out of wherever he kept his voice and failed at the last gate. She knelt and took his face in her two freezing hands.

"Later," she said. "Say it later. Say everything later, when we're across the river and safe. But hear me now, Edmund." She made her voice as steady as the lamp she tended every night of her widowhood, the lamp that had never once gone dark on her watch. "I am not your father. I cannot leave you at doors. Wherever you go from this night forward, I go, and you go where I go, and that is the arrangement, and neither of us gets a vote anymore. Do you hear me?"

A tear slid down through the mud on his cheek. He nodded, once, hard, and gripped her wrist with both hands, and she gathered him against her chest with the packet — God help her, the packet, still dry inside its oilcloth, still pressed between them like a third heartbeat — and held on.

"We move," Hester said quietly. "The trunk's fifty yards south. If it spans, we cross high and fast, one at a time, and we're a mile ahead of them by dawn. If it doesn't span—" she shrugged— "then we swim, keeper, since you claim you can."

"It holds," Odile said. She did not know how she knew. She only knew that the fen had taken her husband and tried, tonight, to take her boy, and she was finished negotiating with water. "It holds. Help me up."

They crossed on the fallen ash trunk one at a time, Hester first, then Edmund roped to Hester's waist with a coil of eel-line, then Odile, the black water sliding beneath her feet like the river under Greyhaven Point, familiar and indifferent. On the far bank the reeds closed behind them, and ahead the land rose by inches toward the drover's road and the inn called the Halfway House and the last leg of a journey that had, three hours ago, nearly ended ten feet down in a ditch.

Edmund did not let go of her hand. She did not ask him to. Somewhere behind them, fainter now, the lanterns cast about on the wrong side of the Ashen Cut, cursing the ice, and Odile walked north with a dead woman's coat around her shoulders and a living boy's fingers locked in hers, and thought, with a fierceness that frightened her a little:

*Let Hale send whom he likes. Let him send armies. He has never crossed ice for anything he loved.*

By the time the sky went grey they could see, far off across the flats, the smudge of smoke rising from the Halfway House chimneys — and, drawn up in the yard before it, the unmistakable shape of a lock-keeper's gig, and a broad figure standing beside it, waiting, arms folded, with the look of a man who had come to strike a bargain.

"That'll be Tobias," Hester said. "Cousin said he'd meet us at the house. Mind yourself with Tobias, keeper. He's a fair man, but he counts."
