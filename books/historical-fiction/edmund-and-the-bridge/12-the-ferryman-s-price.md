## 12. The Ferryman's Price

Tobias Finch had spent forty years learning what the river forgave and what it did not, and by the winter of his sixty-third year he had concluded that the river was more merciful than most men. It drowned you clean, at least. It did not first make you promises.

He was mending a net on the lean-to porch of his cousin's eel shed above Ashford Sluice when the woman came down the towpath with the boy a half-step behind her, the way a shadow follows a body that doesn't know it's being followed. Finch set down his netting needle. He had been expecting this visit for two days — ever since Sister Verity's girl had come to him with a message written in Odile Brightwater's careful lighthouse hand: *I need the water. You know which water.*

He knew. He had been trying to pretend he didn't.

"Tobias." Odile stopped at the edge of the mud, boots dark to the ankle, her face raw with cold and something under the cold. "You got the letter."

"I got it." He stood, joints complaining, and looked past her at the boy. Edmund — that was the name she'd given him in the message, though the word *Edmund* meant nothing to Finch except as an old ache with a ship attached to it. The child stared at him with grey eyes too old for his years, and Finch felt the ground tilt under a memory he had spent eleven years pretending was only a story he told himself about the man he used to be.

"You're wanting Weir Lock," he said. "Past the checkpoint."

"Before the ice breaks. Before it's too late to cross."

"The ice has weeks in it yet."

"Hale's men have less distance than that," she said. "They found the inn at Harrow Bend. They were close enough to scorch the packet. Tobias, I have to get it to the assizes, and I can't do it without you."

He turned away from her and busied his hands with the net, because hands busy were hands not shaking. The eel shed smelled of mud and iron and the sweet rot of the traps, and it smelled like every year of his life since he'd rowed his last fare across the Greywater and hung up his poles. Retired, they'd called it. Finished. As if a man could ever be finished with the things he'd carried.

"Sit," he said. "Both of you. There's tea inside, if my cousin hasn't let the kettle die."

* * *

The inside of the shed was one room: stove, cot, table, three chairs, and nets hanging from every beam like the shed was drowning slowly upside-down. Finch poured tea nobody drank and sat across the table from Odile Brightwater and made himself look at her straight, because what he had to say deserved a straight face even if it didn't deserve one.

She laid the packet on the table between them. The scorched edge had been trimmed away with a knife, neat as surgery, but the wax seal was broken now and the papers inside were thick enough to make a sound when they settled — a soft paper whisper, like something breathing.

"You've seen what's in it," she said.

"I haven't. I don't want to."

"But you know what it means."

Finch looked into the stove. The fire in there was small and honest and asked nothing of anyone. "I know what it means the way everyone on this river knows what it means," he said. "Ships going out rotten because men were paid to say they were sound. Crews drowning within sight of their own harbor lights. And a magistrate whose court finds no one to blame, year after year, because the blame's been bought before the ships even sail." He paused. "Including the *Merrow*. Including Brightwater."

Odile's face did not move. That was the thing about lighthouse keepers, he thought — they learned to keep their faces the way they kept their lamps. Steady through weather.

"How long have you known?" she asked quietly.

"Known? Everyone suspected. Suspecting's free." He wrapped both hands around his mug. "But there was a night eleven years back when your husband came to me at the Staithe, drunk on exhaustion rather than drink, and told me the *Merrow*'s survey papers were forged. Told me he'd seen them, or seen enough. Told me he was going to write it down and send it to the assize clerk at Stonecross, and he asked me would I carry it upriver, because no ferryman would be searched the way a mail coach is searched." Finch heard his own voice go flat, which was how it always went flat when he came to this part. "And I told him no."

"Why?"

"Because I had a wife with weak lungs who needed the southern air and a house with a roof the harbor board hadn't condemned yet, and because Joseph Brightwater smiled at me and said never mind, Tobias, I'll find another way. And three months later the *Merrow* went down off the Point in weather the harbor master swore took everyone by surprise." He lifted his eyes to hers. "Your husband found another way. He sent it with someone else. And that someone else evidently got it as far as a warehouse in the capital, and that warehouse burned, and the son of the man carrying it walked eleven days north to bring you the ashes. So you see, Mrs. Brightwater, I know exactly what's in that packet. I've known for eleven years. I'm the second man alive who was asked to carry it, and the first one said no."

The stove ticked. Outside, a crow called somewhere over the sluice, and the boy's eyes moved to the window at the sound and back again, quick and precise, a watchman's eyes.

"You're saying yes now," Odile said.

"I'm saying I'll take you to Weir Lock and put you past the checkpoint, because the river owes me nothing but I owe your husband everything, and I'm forty years too old to keep pretending the ledger balances otherwise." Finch set the mug down. "But I'll have my price."

Something in her posture shifted — not fear exactly; readiness. "Name it."

"It isn't coin."

"I don't have much else."

"It's this." He laid his hand flat on the table, beside but not touching the packet, the way a man touches a door he's afraid is locked. "When you stand in that courtroom at Stonecross, you'll read out Hale's ledger and tell them about the wreck he bought, and it'll be a fine righteous thing, and Hale will hang or rot or flee to the continent depending on how the wind sits with the judges. And then it will all stop there, because Hale is one man, and a magistrate is convenient to hang." His voice roughened. "But Hale didn't buy those surveys alone. He bought them *for* men. The harbor board. Six names, some of them still sitting in the Trinity House chamber with gold chains on their necks, signing certificates of soundness for coffins with sails. Your husband named them to me that night, drunk-tired, one after another, and I've carried the six names in my head for eleven years like six stones in my boot. Aldous Crane. Peter Wetherell. The rest I'll give you if you want them, though I expect the packet names them already — Hale kept accounts of everything, and accounts want counterparties."

"They're in it," Odile said. "Names, dates, sums. I've read them."

"Then here is my price." Finch leaned forward. "You don't testify against Corven Hale alone. You testify against all seven. You stand up in front of the assize and you say the words *the harbor board knew*, and you make it stick, and you don't let any clever barrister narrow it down to one wicked magistrate so the rest can shuffle home to their dinners. That's my price. Not money. Not passage. The truth, whole."

* * *

She was quiet a long time, and Finch let her be quiet, because he understood what he was asking and he wasn't ashamed of it, but he was interested to see whether she'd understand it faster than most people would.

At last she said, "You want revenge on the board."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "Revenge is what Hale wants, near enough — he wrecked ships to protect his position, which is revenge against a world that wouldn't give him one honestly. No, Mrs. Brightwater. What I want is to stop being a accomplice."

"You refused once. Once, eleven years ago—"

"Once is enough." It came out harder than he intended, and he let it stay hard, because softness would have been a lie. "Do you know what complicity is? It isn't a deed. A deed happens once and then it's done and you can repent it or brag on it. Complicity is a *condition*. It's water in a well. Every year I drew water from that well — I ferried harbor board men to their meetings, I carried cargo for factors who insured ships they knew were unsound, I kept my mouth shut at the Staithe when widows asked me why, and every time, the level in the well dropped and what came up was muddier. Eleven years I've been drinking my own silence." He gestured at the packet. "That's the only bucket that's come up clean. And I'll be damned — I am *damned*, madam, I believe that literally — if I let it carry Hale alone and leave the other six standing on the bank, wringing their hands, saying what a shock, what a monster, who could have known."

"And if the court won't take it? If they convict Hale and throw out the rest?"

"Then we'll have tried. There's a difference between failing and cooperating with your failure." He looked at her steadily. "I know what I'm asking costs you. Hale killed your husband; the board merely permitted it. Grief points at the hand that holds the knife. But your husband didn't write down only Hale — he wrote down all of them, or meant to, and he died on a rotten hull the board certified as sound. Ask yourself what he'd want. Not what's comfortable. What's owed."

"That's not fair, Tobias."

"No," Finch agreed. "Nothing about this is fair. Fairness is for weather."

Odile looked down at the packet, and her hand came to rest on it — not protectively, Finch thought. More like a woman resting her hand on a grave marker. When she spoke again her voice had changed, gone quieter and further away.

"When I opened the seal," she said, "the first page was the manifest of the *Merrow*. Cargo, crew, insurance. My husband's name in the crew list, third from the top, in a clerk's hand. I sat with it all night. And do you know what I kept coming back to? Not Hale's initials. Not the bribes. A line item. *Repairs deferred, hull planking, estimate £140.* One hundred and forty pounds. That's what his life was deferred for. Less than the price of a good horse and a dog to run beside it." She raised her head. "If I read that and then stood in court and said only *Hale did this*, I'd be doing what the board does — pricing the dead cheaply so the living can sleep. You're right. God help me, you're right, and I hate that you're right, because I wanted this simple. I wanted one villain and one verdict and then the light and the boy and quiet for the rest of my life."

"There's no such thing as one villain," Finch said. "There's just villains with better stationery."

A sound came from the end of the table — small, unexpected, like a plank settling. The boy. Edmund had not made a sound in the whole time Finch had known of him; Odile had warned her in the letter, *he does not speak,* and Finch had taken it for grief or damage the way you would. But the boy had laughed. Once. Short and dry, gone as soon as it came, and Finch realized with a strange falling feeling that the child had laughed at *him* — at the line about stationery — and that under the watchfulness the boy was listening to everything, weighing everything, keeping his own ledger.

Odile looked at the boy too, and something passed between them that Finch couldn't read and wasn't invited into.

"All seven names," she said finally. "In open court. In my own voice. And I'll write them down beforehand so no barrister can talk me into dropping one." She held out her hand across the table, rough-palmed, steady. "Your price is accepted, Tobias Finch."

He took her hand and shook it, and his own hand was not entirely steady, and he didn't apologize for that.

* * *

They worked until dark. That was the shape of an agreement made on the river: sealed with a handshake, executed with labor. Finch pulled his old ferry-boat from under its tarpaulin in the cousin's back slip — the *Magpie*, broad-beamed, shallow-drafted, ugly as sin and honest as bread — and he and Odile checked seams and thole pins while the boy carried gear without being asked twice, loading eel baskets, coil after coil, with the economy of motion of someone who had learned young that useless children get left.

It was while the boy was stowing the last basket that Finch saw it — the boy lifting the packet-wrapped oilskin bundle, tucking it into the false bottom of a basket with a fisherman's neatness, and then pausing, and looking at the basket, and looking at Finch, and placing the bundle deliberately where Finch himself would have placed it. Where a ferryman places the thing he cannot afford to lose.

The boy met his eyes. Held them. A test offered and answered in the same look.

"He watches everything," Odile said quietly, coming up beside him with a lantern. "I stopped underestimating him around Millbeck."

"Aye." Finch tied off a line. "Mrs. Brightwater. One more thing, and then we're done talking till we're through the lock."

She waited.

"Rook," Finch said. "The sergeant out of Millbeck. He was at the tollhouse when you came through — you told the sisters as much. I've had word from a man who drinks at Weir Lock that a party of soldiers came down from the north road two days ago and are quartered at the lockhouse. Rook's party or wearing Rook's coat. Either way, they're not there to admire the weir." He blew out a breath. "My plan is this. Eel boats run the lock at first light — the keeper opens for the fishers before he opens for anything, always has, twenty years of habit. We're an eel boat. Baskets low, boat riding heavy, one ferryman, one deaf fisher-boy, one woman under the baskets. It's worked on this river for smugglers since before my grandfather, and the reason it works is that no soldier on earth wants to put his hands into a basket of live eels at six in the morning."

"And if they do?"

"Then we'll learn something about soldiers." He shouldered the lantern. "Get some sleep, the pair of you. My cousin's barn, hay loft, warm enough. First light comes early and it won't wait."

Odile took the boy's hand — and Finch watched the boy allow it, watched the small leaning-in, the way a spar leans into its rigging — and they crossed the frozen yard toward the barn, two shapes against the snow, and Finch stood alone in the slip with his ugly old boat and his lantern and the black river sliding past under its lid of ice.

Eleven years, he thought. Eleven years of drawing muddy water. Tomorrow morning he'd row the clean bucket straight into the teeth of a checkpoint, and either it came up full on the far side or it didn't come up at all.

From the barn came a thin thread of lamplight, and against it, faintly, the silhouette of the boy raising his arms while Odile spread a blanket over him — and Finch found his throat had gone tight, and he cursed softly at his own weakness and turned back to the boat.

He was checking the lashings a second time when he saw the light on the water.

Far downstream, past the bend, where the canal joined the river: a lantern, moving. Then a second. Then the sound — faint, carried flat across the ice — of oars. Oars, at night, in February, on a stretch of water where no honest man rowed after dark.

Finch snuffed his own lantern and stood very still in the dark of the slip, counting lights. Four. Five. Coming upriver, toward the shed, toward the barn, toward the sleeping woman and the boy with the packet — and moving faster than any boat he'd ever seen move against the current, because whoever held those oars had been told exactly where to go, and exactly who was waiting there.
