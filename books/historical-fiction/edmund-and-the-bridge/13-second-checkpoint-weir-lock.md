## 13. Second Checkpoint: Weir Lock

Dawn came up grey as dishwater over the Weir Lock, and Odile Brightwater lay in the bottom of a ferry skiff beneath a pyramid of eel baskets, listening to the river decide whether it wanted to kill her.

The ice had begun to rot. All night she had heard it talking to itself — long sighs, sharp cracks like knuckle-bones thrown on a table, and underneath it all the low mutter of black water working its way free. Finch said the lock was the safest crossing left between Millbeck and Stonecross precisely because the weir kept the current moving; moving water froze late and broke early, and the king's men who ought to be watching it were watching the road instead. That was the theory. Theories, Odile had learned since December, were things the river ate for breakfast.

"Down," Finch breathed, not turning his head. He sat at the stern wrapped in his oilskins like an old seal, one hand resting on the sweep oar as though he'd been born holding it. Perhaps he had. "Baskets forward, girl. If they tip you out, don't come up till you feel the current take you. And even then — count to ten."

She did not answer. Answering cost breath, and breath made fog, and fog above an eel basket in February would hang them both. She pulled the topmost basket down over her shoulders instead, letting its wicker ribs press against her cheek, and breathed through her mouth against the smell — mud and slime and something ancient, the cold-blooded perfume of the river's own keeping.

The baskets smelled of Edmund.

That thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome, because thinking of Edmund was dangerous. Thinking of Edmund made her hands want to shake. He should have been under here with her — that had been the plan, one body hiding where bodies hide, one standing in plain sight playing his part — but plans changed at rivers, and this morning Finch had looked at the boy's still, serious face and said, "He'll do better than either of us. Deaf boys get leaned over. Grown folk get searched."

So Edmund stood at the bow now, wrapped in Finch's spare coat two sizes too big, a slouch hat dragged low, a willow switch in one hand as though he'd been fishing these waters all his short life. A deaf fisher-boy. They had drilled it by candlelight at the drover's inn: cup your ear, shake your head, point at your mouth and frown. Never speak. Never let them see how quick your eyes are. *You are a boy who cannot hear the world,* Odile had told him, kneeling so they were eye to eye, *so the world must lean down to reach you.* He had considered this with that grave, weighing look of his — the look she had first mistaken for emptiness and now understood as arithmetic — and then he had nodded once, and she had felt the nod go through her like a nail driven home. He trusted her. God help her, the child trusted her, and she was about to lie flat under fish baskets while soldiers walked overhead.

*Forget the face,* the packet had said. She was doing anything but.

The lock announced itself before she could see it: timber groaning, chains complaining, and voices — human voices, several, carrying flat across the water the way sound does in cold air. Finch shipped the oar and let the skiff drift on the last of the current, casual as flotsam.

"Boat! Ho there! Weir Lock, hold off!"

Finch raised one mittened hand. "Ferryman! Eels for Stonecross market!"

"Hold off, I said!" Boots on planking, heavy and numerous. "Lock's shut by order. Search before passage — every hull, every basket, every soul."

Odile closed her eyes. Beneath the wicker she saw nothing, which was somehow worse than seeing. She heard the skiff bump the fender-timbers, heard Finch's line tossed up and caught, heard the small creak of the boat coming against the lock's stone lip. Then boots came down onto the gunwale behind her — someone stepping aboard without asking — and the whole fragile world of the skiff rocked, and she pressed her palm flat against the wet bottom boards and prayed to a God she mostly addressed through weather reports.

"Manifest," said a voice near her feet. Young. Bored the way a whetstone is bored — dull on the surface, edge underneath.

"Eels," Finch said comfortably. "Six dozen, dressed. For the Stonecross fishmongers. Been running this water since before your father courted your mother, Corporal, and no man yet has found treason in an eel."

"Man found papers in an eel-boat at Millbeck," the corporal said. "Man'll find them anywhere, near enough." A boot shifted; the floorboards flexed an inch from Odile's spine. "Tip the baskets."

Her heart stopped — actually stopped, she felt it stumble like a clock striking a thirteenth hour — and then a small figure moved at the edge of her hearing, and the willow switch cracked against the lock wall.

Edmund.

Not speaking — never speaking — but making noise the way a deaf boy might: an impatient, tuneless rapping, switch on stone, and when she risked the smallest turn of her head she saw him gesturing wildly downstream, pointing with his whole arm, his face arranged into urgent alarm. He pointed again. Downstream. Hard.

"What's the brat on about?" The corporal's boot lifted from the boards. Thank God, thank God, it lifted.

"He's deaf, poor mite," Finch said, mild as milk. "Found him on my sister's doorstep last Michaelmas. Can't hear his own name."

"Then what—"

Edmund seized the corporal's sleeve — a liberty that would have earned any hearing boy a cuff — and hauled him toward the stern, pointing. And now Odile heard it too, faint but unmistakable: cracking, a deep structural complaint from the weir's lower gates, the sound of ice giving up its argument with the current. The boy had heard it — or seen it, the shiver in the water, the white seam opening along the weir pool — and whatever else Edmund was or wasn't, he was not deaf, and he was using every ounce of that magnificent hearing to sell them a lie.

"The gates," the corporal said, and there was a new note in his young voice. "Sergeant! Sergeant Brack! The lower gates are going!"

Boots thundered on the planking overhead. Odile counted them — four, five — heard the sergeant's voice crack out orders, heard men scramble for the winch-house and the gate-walks. The corporal hesitated on the skiff, torn between a boatful of eels and a river tearing itself apart.

"Go on, son," Finch said. "Eels aren't going anywhere. That weir is."

The boots left. The skiff rose as weight came off it, and Odile lay in the dark smelling eels and listening to the lock descend into chaos around her.

* * *

From beneath the baskets, sound arrived in layers, and she learned the geography of Weir Lock by ear alone.

Closest: the timbers, sweating and ticking with the thaw, close enough that if she straightened her neck she could touch the underside of the gate-walk. Above that: boots. Many boots. They walked the gate-walks back and forth — the search party, called off her little skiff and set to wrestling with ice instead, their crisis arriving (thank you, Edmund) at exactly the moment they'd been ordered to tear her hiding place apart. Beyond the boots, the river itself, louder now, a continuous grinding roar as the weir pool chewed through its white lid. And threading through everything, voices:

"Slacken the offside chain— slacken it, I said, you'll shear the pin—"

"Where's the flood-gate key? Who's got the flood-gate key?"

"Corporal Vane! Get those baskets off that boat and be quick about—"

No. No, no —

"Can't, Sergeant — the old man's aboard and the boy—"

"Then bring the boy up here and put him to work on the capstan! We're four hands short and the gates won't hold! MOVE!"

There was a terrible moment when Odile understood that her entire plan had just been requisitioned by His Majesty's lock-keepers. Edmund — her Edmund, her silent careful boy — was being marched up the steps to the gate-walk to haul on a capstan bar above her head, while below her the eel baskets waited for a search that had only been postponed, not cancelled.

She could hear him breathing. That was the unbearable thing. The gate-walk ran directly over the skiff, and each time Edmund passed — she learned his rhythm within three passes, the slight drag of his left foot on the turn, the small grunt he didn't know he made when he strained — she could hear him, inches above the wicker, separated from her by half an inch of basket and the whole thickness of the world.

And other footsteps paced beside his. A soldier's, matching him stride for stride.

"You there. Boy."

Silence from above. The capstan creaked. Odile stopped breathing entirely.

"I'm talking to you, fish-bait. You hear me?" A laugh, ugly and comfortable. "Oh — right. The dummy. Here, boy, what's an eel worth these days, eh? What's your old man give you, a penny a bucket? Penny a—" The voice dropped, mock-confiding, obscene. "My cousin says you can make more in a night running baccy than a fisher makes in a year. You know anything about that? You lot all know something about that. Look at me when I talk to—"

The capstan pawl clanked; the walk shuddered as the gates took strain; whatever else the soldier meant to say was swallowed by the groan of timber and the roar of the weir. But Odile had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with February. Because the packet was under her heart. Because *papers in an eel-boat at Millbeck* meant Rook's men had already worked backwards from her trick at the tollhouse. Because the description was out — woman and child, woman and child — and a deaf boy with a lighthouse keeper's fine wool coat under his fisher's slops might match that description far better than anyone had planned for.

She lay still. It was the hardest work of her life.

Above her the war with the ice went on. She pieced it together in fragments: the lower gates buckling, the winch-chains frozen, men hauling on capstans while the weir pool heaved white shards like a litter of wolves. Twice something enormous struck the lock's downstream face and the whole structure rang like a struck bell, and she felt the vibration in her teeth, in the baskets, in the water lapping the hull. Once — very quietly, in a gap between orders — she heard Edmund's step pause above her, and a small tap: knuckle on timber. Two taps. A pause. Two taps.

Their signal. Their silly private signal, invented at the drover's inn over spilled tea — two knocks for *I am well*, three for *danger*. He was telling her he was well. Six feet above her head, hauling a capstan in a soldier's shadow, the boy was checking on *her*.

She pressed her fist against her mouth and did not weep, because weeping makes noise, and noise makes fog, and fog kills. But something settled in her chest while she lay there, some load finding its balance point. Whatever happened at the assizes, whatever became of the ledger and the magistrate and the wreck that took Thomas Brightwater — this boy was hers now. Not by paper, not by promise. By two knocks on timber in the dark.

* * *

The crisis broke — literally — a little after eight.

She heard it build: the gates shrieking, the sergeant's voice rising to a crack, men scattering from the lower walk. Then the river hit the broken gate like a battering ram and the world outside her wicker world turned to thunder. Ice ground against ice; the lock walls trembled; someone screamed something about the sluice and someone else screamed something back, and through it all Finch's voice carried, astonishingly calm, cutting across the din like a keel through chop:

"Sergeant! Your upstream gate's clear enough to pass a boat — let me shoot the lock before the surge takes my hull sideways! You want me wedged in your gates? I'm happy to drown here, but I'd rather not!"

A pause. Roaring water. Then the sergeant, ragged: "Pass — pass, then, and be damned to you! VANE! The rope! Get clear of the—"

The skiff lurched. Chains rattled. The upstream gates parted just wide enough — she felt the walls slide past the hull, closer than courtesy, close enough that she heard mortar crumble — and then the current had them, and the skiff swung out into the broken river below the lock like a leaf off a roof-edge.

Finch handled it. Of course he did. She heard the sweep bite water twice, thrice, felt the hull heel and straighten, heel and straighten, riding the flood with an old man's perfect balance, and slowly — gradually — the thunder fell behind them and became merely loud, and then merely the river, and they were through.

They were through.

Odile waited until the lock was a smudge of noise behind them, until Finch grunted the all-clear, before she pushed the basket off her shoulders and sat up into the grey morning light with eel-slime down her front and her hair plastered to her skull and her hands shaking so badly she had to grip the gunwale simply to remain a person.

Edmund was already scrambling aft. He reached her before she'd finished sitting up, seized her sleeve with both hands — he almost never touched first, almost never — and looked at her with those dark arithmetic eyes, and tapped her wrist twice. Twice. *I am well.*

"Well," she managed. Her voice came out in ruins. "Well indeed, Master Edmund, fisher-boy of the Weir Lock, terror of corporals, savior of—" A laugh escaped her, wild and inappropriate, and she let it, because they were alive, because the sun was trying to come up somewhere behind all that cloud, because Finch was steering them north with an expression of profound disgust at having enjoyed himself. "You brilliant, wicked, wonderful boy. You sent them chasing the ice."

Edmund's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile — he rationed smiles like a miser rationed coin — but the twitch was there, and he tapped her wrist twice more, and then he climbed onto the midships thwart and stood lookout in the bow with his willow switch held like a lance, and if a boy could be said to glow silently, he glowed.

"Stonecross by tomorrow dusk, river willing," Finch said without looking round. "Tonight we lie at the Drover's Arms at Harrow Bend — good beds, indifferent ale, an ostler named Pell who asks no questions." He paused, and his shoulders did something complicated. "Mostly asks no questions."

Odile wrung eel-water from her hem and watched the broken river carry them north. The relief was still singing in her blood, sweet and reckless. They had beaten the lock. They had beaten the ice. Her boy stood in the bow like a figurehead and the packet lay safe beneath her heart and for one whole mile of grey water she permitted herself to believe the worst was behind them.

It was Finch who saw it first — the rider on the towpath, half a mile back, keeping pace without gaining. Sitting his horse the way a man sits when he is not following but *escorting*, patient as arithmetic.

"Now that," Finch said softly, "is a question I don't like the shape of."
