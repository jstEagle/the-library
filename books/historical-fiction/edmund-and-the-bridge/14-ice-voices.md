## 14. Ice Voices

The inn smelled of tallow and wet wool and something burning that was not quite a fire — peat, Finch had called it, cut from the moor and stacked to dry. Edmund sat close to the hearth because the warmth was honest in a way most things were not. It did not pretend. It gave itself up in orange light and crackle and asked nothing back.

The Drover's Rest stood where three droveways met above the marsh, a low stone thing with a roof that sagged like an old man's shoulders. There was a long room for cattle-men home from the fairs, a settle bench by the chimney, and upstairs two rooms the landlady let without asking names, which was why Finch had chosen it. *Nobody asks at the Rest,* he'd said. *Drovers come through too tired to be curious.*

Edmund sat on the settle with his knees drawn up and watched the fire eat the peat, and Odile sat across from him mending a seam of his coat — *his* coat now, though it had been made for another boy's shoulders — and neither of them spoke, because they had fallen into a way of not speaking that was almost comfortable, like a blanket worn soft.

He counted what he had.

One mother-shaped woman who pulled him back from drains and fed him first and never once asked him to be anything but silent. One ferryman who called him *nipper* and slipped him barley sugar when Odile wasn't looking. One page folded into quarters, worn nearly transparent at the folds, hidden now inside the lining of his boot where no one would think to look on a child.

And one sentence.

It had lived in him since the warehouse burned. It moved around inside him some nights like a fish under ice, and he could feel it through the whole of his chest, alive and wanting air, and every day it got harder to keep down there in the dark water.

*Not yet*, he told it. *Not here.*

But the inn was quiet. The drovers were abed or still at their cups down the lane. The landlady had gone up. Rain worked at the shutters in long slow strokes. And Odile sat with her needle going in and out, in and out, her face lit gold on one side and grey on the other, and she looked — Edmund studied her sideways, carefully, the way you study a dog you want to trust — she looked like someone who also had a fish under the ice.

* * *

He had been testing her for a long time without knowing that was what he was doing. Back at the lighthouse, before Millbeck, he had hidden things to see if she'd search: a smooth white pebble under his pallet, half a bannock in his coat pocket. She never searched. At the tollhouse he had watched her hands while the sergeant rummaged the cart — steady, easy, her eyes doing all the worrying where no one could see them. Fear held in the hands' absence was a language he understood. His father's hands had been like that, the night of the fire. Steady at the latch, steady lifting him to the window, shaking only after, only once Edmund was already out and the glass between them.

At Ashford Sluice, Sister Verity had offered him bread and honey and a life behind convent walls, and Odile had gone very still, and Edmund had watched her stillness the way other people watch faces. She wanted to say yes. He could see the yes in her — rest, safety, a boy kept like a lamp kept, out of the weather. And then she had looked at him, really looked, and something in her jaw had set, and she had said no, thank you, Sister, we're expected north, and taken his hand so hard it hurt.

That was when he had begun, secretly, to love her. Not decided to — begun, the way ice begins, invisibly, in the dark, along the edges first.

But loving was not telling. Telling was different. Telling was the sentence.

* * *

Odile bit her thread and held the coat up to the firelight, checking her work.

"There," she said. "Good as new. Better." She smoothed the shoulder seam where she'd reinforced it double. "You'll wear this coat out before I wear out mending it, I shouldn't wonder."

She said such things to fill silence. He knew that. She talked to gulls; the gulls presumably didn't answer either. But lately her talk had changed its shape — less like talk aimed at the air and more like talk laid out near him, like bread left on a step for a shy cat. Not demanding. Just there, if he wanted it.

"The landlady says the ford road dries by morning," she went on, winding thread. "Finch's cousin meets us past the alder stand at first light. So we sleep tonight — real sleep, in real beds, with a door that locks." A small laugh, mostly breath. "I'd forgotten doors lock. I've spent nine years locking one against weather and leaving it open for ships."

She fell quiet then, and the fire took over, ticking and settling, and the rain came round again against the shutters, and Edmund felt the sentence rise toward the surface like it knew the water was thinning.

He thought about his father's hands. Steady at the latch.

He opened his mouth, and what came out was not words at first, just a rusted-hinge sound, unused these many weeks, and then — low, cracked, barely shaped —

"It was Hale's men who lit it."

The words fell into the room and lay there.

Odile's needle stopped mid-stitch.

Edmund stared at the fire. He could not look at her. Saying it had used everything he had; looking would have cost more than remained. His own voice sounded strange to him, a stranger's voice, smaller than he remembered owning. But the sentence was out. It hung in the smoky air among the peat-smell and the rain-smell, and it could never be put back, and instead of terror he felt — emptied. Swept clean. Like a beach after a great wave.

"I saw them," he said, and the words came easier now, tumbling, five weeks of silence finding their feet. "Two men. Lanterns with the shutters closed. My father sent me down the stair to the counting room — for the book, the ledger book, he said, *the blue one, Edmund, whatever happens, the blue one.*" His hands had closed on his knees without his permission. "The stairs were already smoking. I got the book. I couldn't get back up."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled.

"He lifted me to the window," Edmund said. "Off the store-room sill, over the yard wall. His hands weren't shaking. Not until I was through. Then—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Then I heard the beam go."

He had never said any of it aloud, not even fragments, not even to himself in full dark. Saying it made it more real and less terrible at once, the way a wound hurts differently once it's been washed.

Odile put down the mending. She did it slowly, laying the needle in the cloth's fold exactly, as if the small ceremony of it might hold her together.

"Edmund," she said, and then stopped, as if his name alone were almost too much to carry in her mouth.

He finally looked at her. Her eyes were bright and wet and fixed on him with an expression he had no name for — grief and fierceness braided together, tenderness with iron through it.

"My husband," she said quietly, "was called Thomas Brightwater. He served aboard a collier called the *Merrow*. Out of Greyhaven, coal to the river ports, coal and sometimes other cargo the manifest didn't mention." Her hands found each other in her lap and held on. "In November of '44 she went down off the Point in weather every other boat stayed ashore for. Every soul lost but one boy who clung to a hatch cover till dawn."

The rain stroked the shutters. The fire breathed.

"For six years I have told myself it was weather," Odile said. "Weather is God's. Weather can't be hated usefully. You can't testify against a storm." Her voice stayed level but Edmund, who had learned to read stillness the way other children read print, saw the effort holding it level, like ice bearing weight — see it flexing, see it hold. "And then a smuggler left a sealed packet on my doorstep, and inside was proof that the *Merrow*'s sailing orders were bought. That her master was paid to run the bar at night in a gale. That Magistrate Corven Hale signed the payment twice — once to order the wreck, and once to buy the inquiry that followed."

She looked at the fire, and Edmund understood suddenly that she was looking at him in it — at the boy he'd been, at the man-shaped hole beside him.

"They shared a ship, your father's enemies and mine," she said. "Hale's men burned your father's evidence and killed your father for keeping a copy. And Hale's men drowned my Thomas for knowing what the harbor board would not admit." She turned to face Edmund fully. "So when I say I am taking you north, child, I am not taking you somewhere. I am taking us both. Your dead and my dead are the same fight. We are the same family of the bereaved."

Something broke loose in Edmund's chest then — not the sentence, that was already free, but something beneath it, older, a cold knot he'd carried since the window, since the falling beam, since the first night he'd slept against a stranger's door with a packet in his coat and no word in his mouth.

Family of the bereaved. Nobody had ever given him a place inside a word before. Places were for furniture. But she had said it plainly, the way you state a fact of tide or weather: this is where you are. This is what we are.

"You sound like him," Edmund said. It surprised him, coming out. "My father. When he explained things. Like the truth was a job, and you'd already rolled your sleeves."

Odile laughed — a short, startled, wet-eyed laugh — and pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her mouth, and then she reached across the space between the settles and took his hand, and her grip was the grip from Ashford Sluice, hard enough to mean it.

"And you," she said, "sound like a boy who has been carrying a whole warehouse alone. Set it down tonight. I'll carry the watch till morning."

* * *

They sat a long while after that, saying little. The fire burned down to a red heart. Odile fetched them bread and cold ham from their bundle and they ate by the ember-light, companionable, and Edmund found that eating next to someone in silence, after speaking aloud, was its own kind of speech.

At last he drew his foot up and worked the torn page from inside his boot lining — not to give it to her yet, not tonight, tomorrow perhaps, when the light was better — just to feel that it was there, real, safe. His father's hand on the page, in the page. *I, Nathaniel Voss, being of sound mind and short time remaining…*

Odile watched him do it and asked nothing. That was her whole genius, Edmund thought. She asked nothing and noticed everything, and somehow that added up to love.

He put the page back and looked at the fire and said, because it seemed owed now, owed to the open air between them:

"That's why I didn't speak. If I said it, it would be true everywhere. Out loud, outside me." He turned his hands palm-up, empty. "Inside, I could still pretend there was a version where I got back up those stairs."

Odile was quiet a moment. "And now?"

"Now it's true everywhere," Edmund said. "And it's lighter."

She nodded slowly, as if weighing the arithmetic and finding it sound. "Grief's odd that way. You'd swear giving it away means losing the last of them. But it doesn't. It's how we keep them — we pass them hand to hand, the remembering does." She rose, stiff from the settle, and banked the fire for the night, and Edmund watched her move about the little room and felt the strangest thing: that the room was not strange. That he might, against all sense, be nearly home, in the moving kind of home that has no address and keeps a lamp lit anyway.

"Bed," Odile said. "First light comes early, and Finch's cousin waits for no one. He's a Marshwold man; they're born distrustful of mornings."

She took the candle to the stair and paused there, looking back at him in the ember-glow.

"Edmund. Thank you. For trusting me with the fire."

He nodded. Then, because it was true, and because tonight truth had learned to walk:

"And you with the sea."

* * *

Upstairs, in the narrow bed under the eaves, Edmund listened to the rain slacken and the inn tick and settle, and Odile breathing slow and even in the truckle below, and he slept without dreaming of windows.

He woke once, deep in the black hours, to a sound below — boots on flagstone, low voices at the bar, the landlady's murmur. Men come late to a drover's inn. Nothing, probably. Drovers traveled at all hours.

But then, through the gap beneath the door, lamplight crossed the landing — three sets of boots, heavy, unhurried — and one voice said, clearly enough to freeze the blood in his ears:

"She'll have the boy with her. Rook wants the pair of them found before Stonecross. Search the rooms."

Edmund's hand went to his boot.
