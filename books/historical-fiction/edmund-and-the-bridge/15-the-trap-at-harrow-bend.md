## 15. The Trap at Harrow Bend

The orders came at dusk, carried by a boy on a lathered horse who didn't dismount long enough to warm his hands. Sergeant Elias Rook broke the wax with his thumb and read by the light of the tollhouse window, and before he'd finished the second paragraph he understood two things: that Corven Hale wanted a woman and child dead on the north road, and that he, Elias Rook, was expected to arrange it.

*Ambush at Harrow Bend. The bend is a choke. Take six men, take the culvert side, take them in the first volley. Burn whatever they carry. No prisoners.*

No prisoners. For a woman and a boy who couldn't have seen ten winters.

Rook folded the paper along its creases and put it in his coat, and stood a while in the doorway of the tollhouse watching the last grey light fail over the frozen river, and thought about how a man arrives somewhere without noticing the road.

* * *

He had been a soldier once, properly — twelve years in the ranks, a sergeant's stripes bought with blood at Dettingen rather than with a magistrate's favor. He had come to Millbeck thinking a checkpoint was honest work: search the carts, weigh the casks, keep the king's peace on a cold bridge. It was Hale who had given him the commission, Hale whose seal sat on his papers, and Rook had told himself for three years that the seal meant nothing, that a magistrate's authority was just the shape the law took out here where there was no one else to wear it.

Then came the oil flask.

He had turned that trick over in his head for a week now, the way a man worries a loose tooth. A hollowed lamp-oil flask, found in a cart belonging to nobody, containing nothing — and yet it had been *something*, because the search had been too thorough for nothing to be hidden, and the woman driving that cart had held her face like a woman holding a door shut. Rook knew faces. That was his trade as much as anything. And hers had been the face of someone listening to her own heartbeat and praying it kept quiet.

A woman alone. A silent boy. Papers worth burning on sight, if the boy on the lathered horse spoke true — and the boy had spoken true, because the boy was terrified, and terror doesn't lie well.

Seditious papers, they called it. But seditious papers went to London, to committees, to men who wrote pamphlets under French names. Seditious papers did not come with orders to burn them *and everyone carrying them*. Whatever the widow Brightwater carried, it wasn't treason against the Crown. It was something smaller and worse. It was evidence.

Against whom?

Rook poured himself a finger of gin from the tollhouse bottle and did not drink it. He let the question sit in him like a swallowed hook.

* * *

In the morning he mustered his men in the yard behind the tollhouse, breath steaming, frost furring the hinges of the stable doors. Six of them. He picked them the way he always picked them, fast and sure, and only afterward did he understand what his hands had done: he had left behind the two best shots and taken the two worst listeners.

Corporal Denby noticed. Denby noticed everything; it was his gift and his affliction. He was twenty-six, clever, ambitious in the small hungry way of men who had no land and no name and meant to acquire both through other people's business. He stood now with his arms crossed, watching Rook buckle the map case.

"Harrow Bend," Denby said. "That's eleven miles by the high road. There's a shorter way."

"There is."

"You're taking the long way."

"I'm taking the road that isn't drifted," Rook said. "Snow's waist-deep in the cut above Ashford. You want to wade it at dawn with powder wet, you go ahead."

Denby looked at the sky, which offered no opinion, then at Rook, which offered several. "And the ambush?"

"What about it?"

"Where do you want me?"

"Left of the culvert, when we get there. Keep your men off the skyline." Rook swung the map case over his shoulder. "We move within the hour."

"Within the hour," Denby repeated, in the flat tone of a man filing something away.

* * *

The high road north ran white and empty under a sky the color of pewter, hedgerows crusted thick, the ditches frozen into ribs of glass. Eleven miles of it, and Rook rode at the head of his little column and let each mile do its work on him.

Here was the arithmetic he could not escape. If he obeyed his orders exactly, and the widow took the north road today or tomorrow, she died at Harrow Bend with her son, and whatever she carried burned in a culvert ditch, and Corven Hale slept soundly, and Rook went on wearing his commission like a clean shirt over something rotten. That was the path with no stones in it. That was survival.

If he misrouted them — if he marched his men hard all day to a position that watched the wrong stretch of road, if he chose the ambush ground with a flaw in it dressed up as prudence — then he bought her a day. One day. Perhaps two, if the weather helped. And every day he bought came out of his own account.

Because Hale would know eventually. Men like Hale always knew. Not at once — a misjudgment of ground was forgivable, even expected of a country sergeant — but the second mistake would not be, and Rook had already made the first one, hadn't he? The flask. The cart he'd waved through Millbeck with a nod while his own hands shook. Denby had seen that too. Denby saw everything.

So this was the ladder, then. Twelve years in the ranks, three years of Hale's silver and Hale's protection, a pension waiting like an egg in a nest at the end of it — and here he was at the foot of it with a torch in his hand, and the only question left was how much of it he burned before he jumped.

He found he felt lighter than he had in years. That frightened him more than anything else.

* * *

They reached Harrow Bend at midday, and Rook walked the ground like a man buying a horse, prodding, doubting, finding fault.

It was good ground. That was the devil of it. The road kinked around a shoulder of rock above the river, hemmed by a sunk lane with holly brakes on either side, and the culvert mouth gave cover for six men and a clear field of fire across forty yards of ice-glazed mud. Any competent officer would have praised it. Rook walked it twice and pronounced it flawed.

"Too tight," he said. "She won't come through here alone — a woman with a child travels with company or not at all, and if there's company, we're shooting uphill at men who can see us coming round the bend. Look at the sightlines. Forty yards and the sun in our eyes after noon."

"The sun's behind cloud," Denby said.

"Cloud burns off."

"Then we take her tonight."

"Tonight we'll be walking back." Rook pointed north, where the road climbed away toward the drover's country. "There's better ground four miles on. The ford at Nether Hail. She has to cross there or go thirty miles around, and the water's up with the thaw. Open banks, our choice of side, retreat across the ford if it goes wrong." He heard himself laying it out — reasonable, professional, *wrong* — and marveled at the ease of it. Lying, it turned out, was like any other drill. You got fluent with practice.

"Nether Hail's another day," Denby said. "Orders said Harrow Bend."

"Orders were written by a man in a warm study looking at a map." Rook let a little iron into his voice, the old rank voice, the parade-ground voice. "I've been soldiering since before you could grow whiskers, Corporal. I'll tell you when the ground's wrong."

Denby held his eyes a moment longer than a corporal should. Then he touched his hat brim. "Whatever you say, Sergeant."

But that evening, at the cold inn where they billeted four miles past the bend they'd been sent to hold, Rook woke past midnight and heard Denby's voice below the floor, low, through the wall of the taproom — talking to young Pruitt, one of the men Rook had deliberately chosen for his dullness, and even dullness could carry words.

"—went right past the best ambush ground in the county," Denby was saying. "Walked it twice and called it wrong."

"Ground's his call."

"Ground's his call," Denby agreed softly. "Everything's his call lately. Waving carts through. Taking the long roads. Ask yourself what the long roads are *for*, Pruitt. Ask yourself who benefits."

Rook lay in the dark with his blanket to his chin and listened until the voices stopped, and understood that the day he had bought for the widow Brightwater had been purchased with coin Denby was already counting.

* * *

He rose before the others and wrote his report at the inn's sticky table by candle-ends.

*Sgt. Rook to the Commission of the Peace, Corven Hale Esq., respecting the road watch at Harrow Bend.* He got as far as describing the ground, the weather, the disposition of his men, and stopped, pen raised, ink gathering at the nib in a black bead.

What could he write? The truth was a gallows. The lie was a rope he'd be handing Denby. In the end he wrote three spare lines reporting the move to Nether Hail as a matter of ground and tactics, signed it, and sealed it, and then sat looking at the seal as the wax cooled, and thought: *there it is. My hand. My name. Every paper I sign now is a confession dated in advance.*

He sent it north with Pruitt, which was itself a calculation he was tired of making — sending the dull ones on errands so the sharp one stayed near enough to watch. He was managing his own ruin the way he'd once managed a skirmish line, and some cold corner of him noted the irony: he had never managed anything half so carefully in Hale's service as he was managing his treason now.

Treason. Was that the word? He tested it as he shaved by the scummy glass, drawing the razor down his grey-stubbled jaw. The law would call it that, if the law ever got the chance — disobedience, obstruction, aiding fugitives. But the law was the thing Hale kept folded in his pocket like a handkerchief, and Rook had spent three years watching it used to blow the noses of rich men. There was another law underneath, the old one, the one about not murdering children in ditches for a magistrate's convenience, and Rook found that he would rather be hanged by the paper law than promoted by breaking the real one.

He was forty-four years old. He had supposed himself too old for conversions. The morning fog on the inn window, and his own face ghosted faintly over the whitewashed yard beyond — that was the whole of his congregation, and it would do.

* * *

They reached the ford at Nether Hail in the afternoon and Rook set his pickets with genuine skill, because whatever else he was doing, he would not do it badly. If the trap sprang, it would spring properly. That was the last scrap of honor available to a man in his position: competence without appetite.

The ford lay empty. The road lay empty. Cattle tracks, a cart rill frozen hard, a crow complaining somewhere up the alder wood. He put a man on the church tower at Nether Hail village with orders to signal smoke for traffic southbound, and by dusk there was no smoke, and by full dark there was still none, and Rook stood at the ford's edge with the black water sliding loud under its lid of broken ice and permitted himself, briefly, dangerously, to hope.

A day. He had bought her a day, maybe more — because Harrow Bend was behind them now, unwatched, and Nether Hail was a bottleneck anyone with sense would smell from a mile off, and a woman who could hide papers inside a lamp-oil flask had sense to spare. By now she might be past the drover's inn at Coldharbour. By tomorrow she might be at Stonecross, where the third checkpoint waited, where the thing would stop being a chase and become a siege of paperwork and nerve. Where he could not misroute anyone, because the ground was what it was.

His part in it, then, was nearly played. One day purchased. What she did with it was hers.

"Sergeant."

Denby, out of the dark, wrapped to the ears, breath smoking. He stopped at Rook's shoulder and looked at the water a while, companionable as a knife.

"No smoke," Denby said.

"Not yet."

"Strange, isn't it. Orders said Harrow Bend. We're at Nether Hail. And I keep asking myself—" He turned his head, and the lantern-light from the picket post caught the flat shine of his eyes. "—what happens to a man's career, Sergeant, when his commander starts improvising. Whose career, I mean. His, or ours."

"Go get hot tea into the pickets, Corporal."

"In a minute." Denby smiled without warmth. "There's a rider comes through here most evenings, did you know? Carries fish to the market and letters back. Fast horse. Nobody stops him because he's got a pass." He let that sit. "I sent a letter out with him tonight. Just a note. Keeping the commission informed, I thought. Diligence, my father called it."

The water talked under the ice. Rook discovered his hands were perfectly steady, and was grateful to the army for that, at least.

"Diligence," Rook said. "Fine quality in a soldier."

"Ain't it." Denby touched his hat. "Goodnight, Sergeant. Sleep well. I'm sure the widow and the boy are sleeping sound somewhere too — wherever they are — thanks to somebody."

He crunched away over the frozen mud, and Rook stood alone at the edge of the black water with his ladder burning quietly behind him, and did the last of the arithmetic.

By morning, Denby's note would be on a desk in Millbeck or, worse, further south, in a study smelling of sealing wax and cold vanity. Which meant Rook had one night left of being merely suspected. After that he would be *known* — and known men had exactly two roads open to them, and one of them ran through a cell.

Unless.

He turned it over slowly, the way he'd turned the flask over, the way he turned everything now. Hale would send riders — fast ones, angry ones — and they would ride for the obvious place: the road north, the checkpoints, Stonecross. They would expect the widow to run and hide. But she had not hidden yet when hiding was easy. She was going *through*. And there was one crossing between her and the assize court that no rider could use once the thaw truly began, one place where a man with a boat and a rope and no loyalty to anyone's commission might matter more than six men with muskets.

Finch, the ferrymen called him. Tobias Finch. Old debts moved in strange circles on this river.

Rook looked north, where the night hid everything, and made the decision the way he'd once made decisions under fire: quickly, completely, without looking back at what it cost.

At dawn he would march his men back down the road he'd come by — the long way, the slow way — and report that Nether Hair had been watched and held, and somewhere between the two he would lose a few hours that no ledger would ever explain. And after that he would stop being Hale's sergeant at all, and find out what he was instead.

Behind him, upstream, the ice boomed in the dark — a long, deep, splitting note, like a door closing, or opening.

The thaw had begun early.
