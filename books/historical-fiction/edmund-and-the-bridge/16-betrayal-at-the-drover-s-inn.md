## 16. Betrayal at the Drover's Inn

The Drover's Rest sat where the north road pinched between two hills, a low stone building with a yard of churned mud and a sign that swung on a rusted hinge with a sound like a slow complaint. Odile heard it before she saw it — *creak, creak, creak* — and thought absurdly of the lamp room's winch, and of home.

Two days from Ashford Sluice. One night's rest between them and Stonecross bridge, where the assize clerk would look at her papers and her face and decide whether the law began or ended at the water's edge.

"We don't stop," Finch had said that morning, and then the rain had come, sideways and bitter, and Edmund's cough had started — small and dry at first, like a bird testing its voice, and by noon something deeper that Odile felt in her own chest every time it sounded. So when the inn rose out of the wet dusk with its windows glowing amber, she heard herself say the words she'd been refusing for two days.

"One night. We'll sleep in shifts."

Finch looked at her the way an old sailor looks at weather he doesn't like but can't argue with. "One night," he agreed, and reined the cart toward the yard.

The ostler who came out to take their mule was a thin man with quick hands and quicker eyes — eyes that went to the cart, to the eel baskets, to Edmund's face, and lingered there a half-second too long before settling into a servile stoop. His name, he said, was Petch. He said it twice, as if planting it.

"Hot supper, mistress? Good straw beds, dry as church bones."

"Dry is all I ask," Odile said, and counted coins into his palm, and watched him count them again with his thumbnail while smiling at her.

Inside, the common room was thick with peat smoke and drovers' voices. Odile kept her hood up, ordered stew for three, and chose a corner table with its back to the wall. Edmund sat pressed against her side, not eating much, his eyes moving over the room the way they always did — cataloguing doors, windows, exits, men. He was nine years old and had the threat-assessment of a customs officer. She tried not to find grief in that. She failed.

"Stonecross," Finch said quietly, around his pipe, not looking at her. "Third checkpoint. You've thought how you'll pass it?"

"I've thought of little else."

"And?"

"And I'm going to walk up to the clerk and tell him the truth." She let that sit until Finch's eyebrows climbed. "Nearly the truth. A lighthouse keeper, escorting her ward north to his mother's kin. A widow with papers and no reason to lie to anyone."

"That's not a plan, that's a wager."

"Every plan since Millbeck has been a wager, Tobias. The oil flask was a wager. The eel baskets were a wager. At some point the boldest lie is to stop lying about anything that can be checked."

Finch sucked his pipe, unlit — he never lit it indoors, some old ferryman's superstition about fire and timber — and said nothing more. Above them the floorboards creaked as someone crossed the landing. Outside, the sign went on complaining to the rain.

Edmund tugged her sleeve. When she bent close he put his lips near her ear, and though his voice was still a rare and fragile thing, used only in the smallest rooms, he spoke.

"The man by the fire. He asked the ostler about us. Before we came in."

Cold moved down Odile's spine like water finding a seam. "You're sure?"

"He showed him something. Small. Silver." Edmund's jaw tightened. "Petch looked at our cart after."

She made herself chew, made herself swallow, made her face stay bored and tired. Across the room, a heavyset man in a riding coat sat too comfortably alone, drinking slowly, his boots muddy in a pattern that suggested he'd come off the south road recently and wasn't going back out into the rain until he had reason to. Hale's men wore no uniform; they didn't need one. Money dressed them well enough.

"Shifts," she murmured to Finch. "We leave before first light. Earlier. I'll sit up first."

But even as she said it, she knew the shape of the trap already closing. An inn was a bottleneck. Every road north funneled through this yard, and if the man by the fire was watching the road, leaving early meant walking into him. Staying meant waiting for whatever Petch's silver had purchased.

*Forget the face*, the packet had said once, in another life. She almost laughed. Somewhere between Greyhaven Point and here, forgetting had become impossible — the whole country seemed determined to remember her.

* * *

She slept in pieces, in a chair by the window of their room with the packet strapped beneath her cloak and one hand resting on Edmund's shoulder. He'd insisted on taking the second watch and she'd allowed it, because allowing it was a kind of trust, and trust was the currency they'd been paying each other in since the marsh.

It was full dark — that dense, rain-deadened dark just before the sky considers greying — when Edmund's hand closed hard on her wrist.

She came awake without moving, the way he'd taught her without ever meaning to. Below, in the yard: hooves. More than one horse. And voices pitched low, the particular low of men who did not want to wake the house.

She was at the window in three steps, keeping to the frame's shadow. Four riders had come up the south road and stopped at the yard's edge. One dismounted and spoke to the ostler, who'd evidently been waiting up — waiting, Odile thought, like a man owed wages. There was a lantern. In its light the dismounted rider unrolled a paper, glanced at the inn, and pointed — not at the building generally.

At the stable. Where their cart stood.

"They know the cart," she breathed. "They'll search it, then they'll come up the stairs."

Behind her Finch was already awake, pulling on his boots, swearing with the fluency of a lifelong riverman. "Window. The yard wall's six feet but there's a dung heap against it by the far corner—"

"The stable's between us and the wall."

"Aye." He said it flatly, and she understood: the route ran through the worst place, because the only worse places were behind them.

Odile knelt and took Edmund's face in both hands. "Listen to me. Whatever happens, you stay on my left, inside my cloak's shadow. If I fall, you do not stop. You go to the wall, over the heap, and north on the road until you find the milestone with the cross cut in it. Finch knows. Do you hear me? You do *not* stop."

His eyes were enormous in the dark. He nodded once — that single, soldierly nod that broke her heart a little every time — and took hold of the hem of her cloak, and folded himself into her shadow exactly as instructed.

Finch eased the door. The inn slept; the drovers were heavy drinkers and the stairs complained only under weight. They came down through the cold common room, embers breathing red in the grate, and out through the kitchen passage where the smell of last night's suet still hung, and stopped in the black rectangle of the doorway with the yard spread before them.

Rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist. Lantern-light wobbled in the stable where two men moved among the stalls. Their cart stood in the third bay. One of the men had already lifted the sacking over the eel baskets.

And the ostler Petch stood by the stable door, holding the lantern high, pointing things out with his free hand. Eager. Helpful. *Planting his name.*

Something in Odile went very still and very cold, colder than the fen, colder than the sea the night the *Merrow* went down. Not fear. Something older and simpler than fear.

"Cart first," she whispered. "They'll expect us to run for the road. Nobody runs *toward* what they're searching."

Finch gave her a look that said plainly she'd gone mad, and then a second look that said perhaps madness had gotten them this far, and drew the short boat-hook he carried — half weapon, half tool, entirely ferryman.

They crossed the yard low and fast, hugging the stable's outer wall where the shadows pooled deepest. Inside, the searchers' voices: "...nothing under the sacking—" "Check the false bottom, Rook said false bottoms—" The name went through her like a fishhook. Sergeant Rook. The man from Millbeck, the man whose trick she'd turned against him with the oil flask, was still following the thread she'd left him.

She slipped into the stable through the far stall gap, Edmund flowing after her like water. The nearest searcher had his back to them, prying at the cart bed with a knife. Odile's world narrowed to distances: four strides to the cart, the packet's strap biting her shoulder, the hollow flask long emptied and abandoned — everything that mattered now rode against her ribs in oilcloth and wax.

Then Finch, reaching the cart from the other side, stepped on a loose board.

The crack of it was nothing. The silence after was everything.

The searcher turned. For one suspended instant he and Odile regarded one another across the width of a stable — a lighthouse keeper with a boy at her heel, and a hired man with a knife and orders he hadn't yet understood.

"*They're here—*"

The shout tore the night open.

What followed lived afterward in Odile's memory as fragments, the way shipwrecks live: Finch's boat-hook swinging wide and catching the man's wrist so the knife spun away into the straw; the second searcher bursting from the cart bay with a pistol already levelled; Edmund, small and fast, hauling open the gate of the end stall so a startled carthorse exploded between them and the gun. The shot went high and wide and the noise of it in the enclosed dark was like the sky splitting.

"Yard!" Finch roared. "*Run!*"

They burst out into the grey drizzle with the whole yard waking — windows banging, drovers shouting, horses screaming in the stable. From the south road came answering motion: the riders spurring forward, and behind them, horribly, the man from the common room, the patient drinker, running with a pistol in each hand, shouting words she couldn't parse over the blood in her ears.

A shot cracked past her ear with a sound like a wasp grown enormous. Another. Something struck the yard wall beside her and spat stone chips. Then Finch staggered — not fallen, but faltering, his arm jerking up to his shoulder — and Odile got her own arm around him under his coat and discovered warmth running through his sleeve, and thought with terrible clarity: *not the heart, not the heart, keep moving.*

"The wall!" she shouted. "Tobias, the *wall!*"

The dung heap rose against the wall like a filthy mountain, and it was six feet of horror and glory, because no one had thought to guard the ugly side of the inn. She shoved Edmund up first — he scrambled like a cat, was over — then pushed Finch, who swore magnificently all the way, and came over herself as a third shot slapped into the heap below her feet.

The far side was a lane of black mud and hedgerow. North. Everything was north, always north, the compass point her whole year had turned around.

They ran. Mud grabbed their boots; hawthorn clawed at them; Finch wheezed and swore and kept his legs moving through what she could only assume was pure spite. Behind them the inn's yard filled with lanterns and shouting, and someone — Petch's voice, high and self-justifying — was explaining to everyone that he'd told them, told them plain, that the woman and the boy had taken the north road, hadn't he said so—

"He sold us," Odile gasped, when they'd put a quarter mile of dark between themselves and the light. "For silver and a pat on the head, he sold us."

"He sold you cheap," Finch rasped. His face was grey in the pre-dawn murk. "Rook's paying well these days, I hear. Should've held out."

"Don't joke. You're bleeding."

"I'm *grazed*, woman. Bullet took a stripe off me and nothing else, which tells you Hale's men shoot like they're paying for powder out of their own purse." But he let her lower him onto a fallen tree at the lane's edge, and let her peel back his sleeve, and hissed when the cold air found the furrow carved along the outside of his upper arm. Shallow. Ugly. Bleeding freely, which she was glad of — clean wounds bleed.

Edmund appeared at her elbow with a handful of broad leaves, having vanished and returned without a word, and offered them silently. Bandaging material, his eyes said. She took them, and bound Finch's arm with strips torn from her petticoat and leaves packed over the graze because a child had believed they'd help, and because she had decided long ago that a child's beliefs would be honored in her keeping.

"You saved us at the stall gate," she told Edmund quietly, tying the knot. "The horse. That was bravely done."

He looked away, embarrassed, and then back, and something in his face asked a question she answered by pulling him against her good side.

* * *

Dawn came up the colour of dishwater, and with it the reckoning.

They'd made a mile along the hedgerows before Finch had to stop, and they sat in the ditch-bottom shadow while Odile took stock of what the night had cost. Finch's arm, useless for rope work for a week. Their cart, their mule, their baskets, their cover story as eel-fishers — all of it sitting in Hale's hands now, being examined by men who would draw exactly the right conclusions. And somewhere behind them, Sergeant Rook, who had followed the oil-flask backwards across half a county, now holding the thread in both fists.

Worst of all: the road. Petch would sell the obvious truth — they'd fled north. Every man Hale owned would be watching the north road to Stonecross.

"We can't take the road," she said.

"We can't *leave* the road," Finch countered. "Stonecross sits square across it. The bridge is the bridge; there's one way over the river this side of the sea, ice or no ice. That's why Hale's got guns on it."

"Then we go to the bridge, but not by the road. The drovers' paths. Finch, you told me yourself — the drove roads run parallel, a mile east, through the heath. Drovers have been sneering at turnpikes for a hundred years."

"Drove roads," Finch repeated, "which are marked by nothing, cross bog that eats horses, and which I haven't walked since I had hair the colour of that boy's. With one arm." He looked at the pale sky a long moment. "Aye. Well. Better than the road."

It was then that Odile finally made herself unwrap the packet and see what the night had done to it.

Oilcloth, mostly proof against rain. Not proof against lead and fire. Along the top edge, where the folds had gaped during her flight across the yard, a scorch had bitten deep — the cloth charred stiff, and beneath it the documents themselves browned and curled at their margins. The manifest's corner had gone. Two entries of the bribe ledger were legible now only as ghost-impressions, ink burned to sepia shadow. And the page that mattered most — Edmund's father's confession, the page torn out and hidden and surrendered at Ashford Sluice, the page that named Corven Hale in his own dead hand — had a hole burned clean through one corner.

Through one word.

Odile smoothed it with shaking fingers. The sentence survived. *I do swear that the ordering of the wreck…* the burn. *…was done at the instance and direction of C—— H——.* The initials remained, flanked by ash.

"It's enough," Finch said quietly, reading over her shoulder. "Clerks love initials. Half the evidence at any assize is initials and oath."

"It's *damaged*," she said. "It was perfect, and now it's damaged, and if Hale's counsel stands up and says the page is forged, that the burn destroys the hand that could prove it genuine—"

"Then the clerk reads the ledger, and the ledger names payments, and Rook — if Rook stands where I think he might stand — says what Rook saw." Finch flexed his bandaged arm experimentally and grimaced. "Listen to me, Odile Brightwater. You're doing what we all do. Looking at the wound instead of the patient. The paper lives. It's singed, not killed. Things that survive fire are worth more, not less — ask any smith."

Edmund reached out and touched the scorched corner, very gently, the way he touched things that frightened him. Then he said, in his rare, careful voice:

"My father's words are still louder than the fire."

Neither adult spoke for a moment. Odile found she had to look at the horizon.

* * *

They moved at midmorning, Finch grey but upright, Edmund between them with his hand tucked in the crook of Odile's elbow as though he were simply her ward out walking, and the heath opened ahead of them — miles of rust-coloured grass and standing water and low sky pressing down like a lid. Somewhere to the west, invisible beyond a ridge, the north road ran with Hale's men on it. Somewhere ahead, past bog and drove-track and however many hours of Finch's stubbornness, the river waited, and the fortified bridge at Stonecross, and the assize court beyond it with its ice-thinned deadline.

Odile paused once, at the top of the first rise, and looked back the way they'd come. The Drover's Rest was a smudge of smoke against the grey. Somewhere in that smudge, Petch was counting his silver and telling himself he was only a poor man making his way, the way everyone always told themselves.

Trust, she thought. She'd handed it to nuns and ferrymen and mute boys and a traitor with quick hands, and most of them had carried it. The ostler had dropped it. That was the arithmetic of the world — not that trust was foolish, but that it was spent often, and stolen sometimes, and still worth more than silver every day of the week.

"Come on," she said, and turned her face north, where the ice was already talking in the river's voice. "We've a bridge to cross."
