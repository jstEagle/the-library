## 19. The Ice Breaks Early

Odile woke to the sound of a shot fired under her window.

She was out of bed before her mind caught up with her body, hand groping for the poker by the hearth, and it took three full breaths of standing in the cold dark of the inn's upper passage to understand that no one had fired at all. The sound came again — a crack like a musket, then a lower report that rolled up through the floorboards and into the soles of her feet — and she crossed to the window and pulled back the shutter and saw, in the blue hour before dawn, the river.

The ice on the Stonecross reach had begun to break.

It was earlier than anyone had promised. Earlier than Finch had sworn it would be, tapping the ice on Weir Lock with his boot heel and saying, *three weeks yet, maybe four, you've time enough to walk across dry-shod*. Earlier than the assize clerks had reckoned when they set the sessions for the first week of the thaw. The river lay silver-grey under the last of the night, and along its whole length Odile could see the dark seams opening — long weeping cracks where water showed through like blood under skin — and out in the middle channel something vast shifted and groaned, plates of ice grinding against one another with a sound she felt in her teeth.

She had lived beside water her whole life. She knew its voices: the flat gossip of an ebb tide, the warning mutter of a groundswell, the particular silence a sea makes before it takes a man. But this was a voice she had only heard twice, and both times from the safety of the lamp room, watching from a hundred feet above. Down here, at the level of the flood, it did not sound like weather. It sounded like intention.

"Edmund." She turned to the boy asleep in the truckle bed, and put her hand on his shoulder, and he came awake the way he always did now — instantly, silently, eyes open and fixed on her face before his body had finished moving. Three months ago he had slept like the dead. Now he woke like a soldier. She hated what the winter had taught him, and loved what it had made of him, and could not separate the two feelings any longer. "Listen."

They listened together. The river cracked and boomed. A slab somewhere down toward the weir calved off with a splash they heard plainly through two feet of stone wall.

"It's early," Edmund said. His voice, when he chose to use it, remained a rare and startling thing, and she still felt each sentence as a gift. "Finch said—"

"I know what Finch said. The river doesn't read letters." She was already dressing, pulling on wool over wool, her fingers working buttons while her mind worked ahead. "If the thaw has come to us, it's come to them. The assizes will sit as scheduled — judges don't wait on weather, not when there's a magistrate's neck on the calendar. Which means we cross today or not at all."

Edmund sat up in the narrow bed. In the grey light his face was pale and very steady, and Odile thought, not for the first time, how much of his father lived in that steadiness — the man who had walked into a burning warehouse rather than leave his papers behind, because the papers were the weapon and he knew it.

"We cross today," Edmund said.

* * *

By full morning the whole town of Stonecross had come down to look at the river, the way people do at a hanging or a fire, drawn by catastrophe they are glad is not theirs. The bridge itself still stood — the great fortified span with its gatehouse and its toll arch, the third and last checkpoint between them and the courthouse — but it had begun, in the words of one drover, to sing. The stone sang. A low vibration ran through the arches whenever the ice drove against the piers, a note felt rather than heard, and Odile watched a company of Hale's red-jacketed men walk the span and then walk it considerably faster than they'd walked out.

"The assizes sit across that," she said quietly, standing with Finch in the yard of the drover's inn. Across the water, half a mile upstream, the courthouse tower showed its clock face through the haze. Ten minutes' walk, in ordinary times. "And nobody crosses that."

"Nobody crosses the bridge," Finch agreed. He was chewing the end of a cold pipe, unlit; his hands needed occupation these days, since the graze on his arm had taken the rope-work and left him the idle half. "Bridge is closed, has been since first light. Sergeant's men are turning back carts at the toll arch, telling folk to wait for the ferry. Only there's no ferry." He took the pipe from his mouth and looked at it. "There were no ferry this morning. There may be one by tonight, if a certain retired man still remembers how."

Odile looked at him. "Tobias."

"A rope ferry." He said it fast, as though saying it quickly might make it less mad. "A hawser run bank to bank above the ice, a cradle on pulleys, and the current does the work and God does the rest. My father ran one at Ashford Sluice forty years, before they built the lock. I helped build the rigging when I was nine years old." He turned the pipe over. "It's a bad crossing in a good season. In this season it's a wager with the devil, and the devil holds most of the cards."

"But it can be done."

"It can be done." He sighed, and the sigh carried forty years of rivers in it. "Get the boy's boots oiled and your papers dry. And eat something, woman. Nobody testifies well on an empty stomach."

* * *

He worked all day, and they worked with him.

The inn's ostler — not the traitor who had sold them at the last stop, that man gone south with his silver and his guilt, but a new one, young and incurious, mercifully deaf to whispered plans — lent them rope without asking why. Finch requisitioned a ship's boat from a coal merchant who owed him money from another life, a shallow-draft thing with patched planks that smelled of tar and fish, and together he and Odile dragged it on rollers down the frozen slipway while Edmund ran lines and coiled and hauled with the competence of a child raised among boats before boats became dangerous to him.

The hawser was the heart of it. Finch spliced three ropes into one length of nearly two hundred feet, and then came the problem that made Odile's stomach drop: getting the end across. Two hundred feet of open, breaking, running ice, and no way to carry it.

"Tin whistle," Finch said.

"What?"

"You'll see." He limped to the far end of the slipway, weighed the coiled rope in both hands, and threw.

Odile watched the coil arc out over the river and land on the ice shelf forty feet out — and then Edmund was beside her with the tin whistle from his coat pocket, the one Sister Verity had given him at the convent, and he put it to his lips and blew three sharp notes, and from the far bank, where a fisherman had been sitting all morning pretending to fish through a hole in the ice, a boy about Edmund's age stood up and waved.

"Finch's grand-nephew," Odile said slowly. "You arranged this last night."

"I arranged it while you were sleeping, which is what old men do instead of dreaming." Finch began hauling on the shore line, dragging the hawser out onto the ice. "Now pull, both of you, and if you tell the boy's mother I set her son on river ice in break-up season, I'll deny it to the assizes myself."

By dusk the hawser was across, hauled over the shelving ice by two boys and a fisherman, made fast to a bollard on the far bank with turns that Finch checked twice from the near side through a spyglass. Then he rigged the cradle: block and tackle riding the hawser, painter made fast to the boat, a bridle and a steering oar. It was ugly and it was clever and it was entirely a gamble, and Odile stood looking at it in the failing light and felt the fear settle into her the way the cold settled into stone — permanent, structural, something she would simply have to build around.

Across the river, the courthouse clock struck eight. The ice answered it with a boom like artillery.

* * *

They ate the supper Finch's grand-nephew's mother brought down to the slipway — barley broth, bread, cheese going hard at the edges — and it was there, over the tin plates, that Finch said the thing he had been not saying all day.

"You could stay."

Odile set down her spoon.

"Not you," Finch said quickly, eyes on his bowl. "The boy. Word came up from Millbeck with the coal cart. Rook's turned — openly, now, no more misrouted patrols. They say he stood in front of Hale's riders at Harrow Bend with his hand on his sword hilt and told them the road north was washed out. They say Hale's put a price on *him* now too." He looked up. "Rook sent word. He means to hold the bridge approach tomorrow, whatever's left of it, buy you your minutes. But he can't protect what's on the water. If the crossing goes wrong—" He stopped himself. "Sister Verity would take him tonight. There's a carter leaving for Ashford within the hour. He'd be safe, Odile. Safe, hidden, named something else by Easter. You go across alone and testify, and when it's done, when Hale's fallen, you send for him."

The yard was quiet except for the river. Odile looked at Edmund, and found him already looking at her — not afraid, she realized. Listening. The way he listened to everything, cataloguing, deciding, the way he had decided about her months ago over a torn-out ledger page.

"She's thinking about it," Finch said to Edmund, gently, as though the boy needed softening news. "It's not cowardice, lad. It's arithmetic."

Edmund put down his plate. He stood, and crossed the frozen mud of the yard to where the boat lay on its rollers, and put one hand flat on the hull the way other children touch a horse's neck. Then he turned around.

"My father sent me away to live," he said. Every word distinct, unhurried, the voice he spent like coins from a small hoard. "He stayed with the papers and he burned. If I go to the convent, I do the same thing again. Everybody stays and I get sent where it's safe, and then everybody's dead and I'm alive and it isn't worth anything." He looked at Odile, not Finch. "I'm not being left behind. Not again. Not by you."

The words went into Odile like a blade goes into a lock — hard, precise, turning something open.

She thought of her husband, and the log entries in her neat widow's hand, and the years of grief kept like a lamp trimmed and burning for no ship. She thought of the packet against her breast, scorched at one corner, holding the names of dead sailors and the initials of a living magistrate. She thought of the choice waiting for her on the water tomorrow, which she had not let herself name even in her own mind.

"Then you cross with me," she said. "Middle of the boat. Both hands on the gunwale. Whatever happens, whatever I say, whatever you see — hands on the gunwale, Edmund, and trust the rope."

He nodded once, and came and sat back down, and picked up his bread as though he had merely stepped away to check the weather.

Finch was staring at the pair of them. He cleared his throat, which had gone rough. "Well," he said. "Arithmetic's settled, then."

* * *

Odile did not sleep, and did not pretend to try. She sat in the inn's common room with the packet open on her knees for the hundredth time, reading by rushlight, making sure. Manifests. The bribe ledger. The confession in a dying man's hand naming Corven Hale outright — *he bought the pilot off, he bought the board, he bought the storm's silence*, line after line in ink gone brown. And beneath it all, folded separately, the page Edmund had once torn out and hidden from her, testing her, before trust was made explicit between them.

She touched the scorch mark on the packet's edge and thought about fire and water, the two ways everything could end.

Near midnight, the ice began to speak in earnest. The cracks came faster now, overlapping, and once there was a sound so immense — the middle channel giving way, she learned later, the main sheet splitting stem to stern — that the tankards rattled on the inn's shelves and a horse screamed in the stable. Through the window she saw people coming out of their houses with lanterns, and someone shouting that the bridge was closed absolutely, that the army had withdrawn from the span itself, and someone else shouting back that the assize judge had arrived on the far bank and would sit tomorrow morning regardless, God and the river permitting.

Tomorrow morning. The deadline arriving exactly like a verdict — not thunderous, but quiet, procedural, a clerk's voice reading a date.

Finch came down the stairs at first grey light already dressed, already moving, and Odile stood and put the packet inside her coat and buttoned it over her heart.

At the door, Edmund was waiting. He had oiled his own boots.

* * *

The river at dawn was unrecognizable.

Where the ice had lain solid for two months there was now a moving chaos — dark leads of open water racing seaward, and in them a fleet of broken floes, some small as tables, some vast as hayricks, grinding downstream with a hiss and rumble that never wholly stopped. The near shelf still held along the banks, but it was rotting, honeycombed, sagging at the edges into the current. The hawser ran out overhead, taut as a bowstring, its far end invisible in the river mist.

Finch checked every knot twice and then a third time, and swore at a frayed strand, and re-spliced it with hands that did not shake, and Odile understood that this was his way of praying.

"Now hear me." He stood in the bow of the little boat, and his voice had the flat calm she remembered from stories told about him at Greyhaven — the ferryman who went out in the '39 gale because someone had to. "The rope carries us. The oar steers us. The current gives us the middle and takes it away again, and there's nothing in this world we can do about floes but pray and push. Once we're off the shelf there's no coming back — the ice won't hold a second landing here. So it's across, or it's the bottom." He looked at Edmund. "Gunwale."

"Gunwale," Edmund said.

Finch looked at Odile last, longest. "Forty years I've owed your husband," he said. "This morning I pay it. Don't you dare make it a waste."

He pushed off.

The first ten feet were the worst, the boat grinding over the rotting near shelf with a sound like teeth, and then the bow went down into open black water and the river had them. The hawser hummed. The current seized the hull and swung it, and Finch leaned on the steering oar with his whole scarred body, and they began to move — not straight, but crabwise, sliding downriver and across at once, the near bank falling away behind them faster than Odile's heart wanted.

A floe the size of a barn door came at them out of the mist. Finch swore with great fluency and precision, hauled the oar, and the boat kissed it, spun, scraped along its edge with a shriek of planks, and slid free. Edmund's hands were white on the gunwale. He did not make a sound.

"Talk to me, boy," Finch grunted. "Tell me what you see upstream."

"Water," Edmund said. "Lots of it. A big one coming — wide as a cart."

"Wide as a cart. Right. Odile — starboard side, take the boathook, and when I say *lean*, you lean like your saints are watching."

The big floe came. They leaned; the river leaned them; for a moment the boat lay over until the water was a hand's breadth from the gunwale and Odile saw the whole grey sky reflected in it, and Edmund's face above her, and the hawser singing. Then the floe passed under the keel with a shudder and they righted, and Finch laughed — one short bark, entirely feral — and spat over the side.

Halfway across, the rope jumped.

Not a sway. A jump — a violent convulsion along the hawser's whole length, and Finch's head snapped up, and Odile followed his gaze to the far bank, where the mooring bollard had been set in a shelf that was now, visibly, moving. The ice under the bollard groaned, tilted, and slid an inch toward the channel, dragging the hawser's anchor with it.

"It'll hold," Finch said, in the voice of a man who tells passengers storms are ordinary. "It'll hold. Pull, boy — midship line — haul *now*—"

The bollard's shelf gave again. This time the ice went with a crash they heard across the whole river, and the hawser's far end whipped loose from the bank and came snapping back toward them over the water like a living thing — and then stopped, sagging, because the grand-nephew and his mother and two fishermen Odile had never seen were on the far bank hauling the slack hand over hand, digging their heels into the same rotten shelf, re-fastening the rope to a tree with their bare fingers while the ice shifted under their feet.

They were alone in the middle of a breaking river, held by a rope anchored to strangers and a tree.

"Keep hauling!" Finch roared across the water, and the far bank hauled, and the boat crept. Twenty yards. Ten. A floe the size of a cottage came down the channel directly in their path, unstoppable, patient, and Odile saw Finch measure it and measure the bank and choose the bank. "Lean! LEAN!"

The floe passed close enough that Odile could have laid her palm on it. In its wet grey face she saw reflected, absurdly, the courthouse clock.

Then the keel touched mud and shingle and the far shelf, and hands were pulling them out of the boat, and Odile stood on solid ground with the packet hard against her chest and her legs refusing, briefly, to be legs.

Edmund was beside her. Both hands had never left the gunwale.

Finch sat down heavily in the shallows, indifferent to the water, and looked back at the river they had crossed, and began, very quietly, to laugh.

Odile turned up the bank toward the road — the courthouse tower visible now through thinning mist, its clock showing the quarter-hour, the assizes assembling beyond doors she could almost touch.

And then she heard hoofbeats behind her, many, coming fast along the far-bank road from the direction of the bridge — and she turned to see riders breaking out of the trees, red jackets and plain ones mixed, and at their head a figure on a blown horse with one arm bound to his side.

"Sergeant Rook," Edmund said.

Rook hauled his horse to a stop at the top of the bank. Behind him, distantly, from the direction of Stonecross bridge, came the sound of a chase — and of the ice, splitting the great span's last arch like a pistol shot.

"Mrs. Brightwater." He was breathing hard, his face grey with exhaustion and something that might have been relief. "Hale knows. He has riders on every road but this one — I made sure of the roads. What's left is between you and those doors." He looked past her at the courthouse steps, then back, and his cracked lips shaped something that was almost a smile. "Go and be heard. I'll hold the bank."

Behind him, the river took another piece of the bridge, and the sound rolled over the water like the last word of a verdict.
