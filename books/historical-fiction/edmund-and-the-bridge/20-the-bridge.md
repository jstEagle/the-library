## 20. The Bridge

The rope ferry groaned like a living thing.

Odile felt the sound in her palms before she heard it in her ears — a deep, fibrous shudder traveling down the tarred hemp from the far pulley at Stonecross to the cleat where Finch had made fast their crossing line. Beneath the ferry's planked bed, the river had gone from white to something worse: a mottled grey shot through with black veins, the ice rotting from underneath as the thaw drove meltwater down from the high fells. Every few minutes a sound rolled up the valley that she felt in her back teeth, low and vast, like a door being opened in the floor of the world.

"Two minutes' work, no more," Finch said for the third time. His shoulder was bound where the ball at the drover's inn had kissed him, and he favored it without admitting to it, holding his left arm stiff against his ribs as he paid out the guide rope. "Midstream you'll feel her lift when the current takes the hull. Let it lift. Don't fight the river. Fight the river and you'll lose."

"And if the ice goes while we're on it?"

Finch looked at her. He had known her husband; he had owed Thomas Brightwater his life, and he had spent this whole hard winter paying the debt forward, eel basket by eel basket, checkpoint by checkpoint. Now he looked at her with the flat honesty of a man who had run rivers for forty years.

"Then God will know His own," he said. "Get in."

Edmund got in first.

He did it the way he did everything now — quickly, silently, with the economy of a child who had decided that hesitation cost more than courage. He stepped onto the ferry platform, crouched low as Finch had taught them, and gripped the gunwale with both hands. The packet hung inside Odile's coat, strapped against her breastbone with a nun's linen bandage, and beside it rode the torn page in its oilskin wallet — Josiah Marsh's dying confession, a dead man's hand reaching across two winters to name Corven Hale. She could feel them both when she breathed, a doubled heartbeat against her own.

She looked at Edmund and tried, one last time, the argument she knew she would lose.

"You could stay with Finch. Ride back to Ashford with him. Sister Verity—"

"No." It came out small and absolute, a stone dropped into the river's roar. He had found perhaps thirty words since the night at the drover's inn, and he spent them like coins he could not replace. His chin was up. His eyes were hers — dark, watchful, his father's eyes, the eyes of a customs man who had died for a ledger. "You go. I go."

The river answered him before Odile could. Somewhere below Stonecross bridge, upstream where the arches pinched the current into fury, a shelf of ice the size of a tithe barn let go with a crack like a broadside. The whole flood shivered. The ferry jumped an inch against its rope.

"That's our answer," Finch said grimly. "Go now or wait for the assizes to come to you, and they won't." He put his boot against the cleat. "I'll be on the bank watching. If you make the far side, walk — don't run, *walk* — up the court steps and hand it to whoever wears the black cap of office. Understand? Not a constable, not a clerk. Black cap."

"Black cap," Odile repeated.

He shoved off.

The first ten feet were nothing. The ferry swung out on its line over water gone suddenly, horribly swift, brown and muscular where yesterday it had been locked under a lid of white. Then the current took the hull exactly as Finch had promised, and the platform lifted, tilted, and began to travel downstream faster than it traveled across, the rope singing overhead as the pulley at the Stonecross tower took the strain. The bridge loomed above them — three arches of old grey stone, fortified these three weeks with a timber palisade and a tollgate, the assize road north running over its spine. On the far tower, tiny with distance, she could see the red coat of a sentry. Behind her, on the south bank, Finch stood with the slack rope in his fists like a man hauling on the future itself.

And behind *him*, coming up the south road at a hand gallop, four riders.

Odile's heart did not so much leap as turn over, slow and cold, like a keel finding bottom.

"Edmund." Her voice came out very steady, which surprised her. "Don't look round. Watch the far bank. Pick your step off the boat before we touch."

But of course he looked round. He was nine years old and men were chasing them, and he looked round, and then he went rigid, and his hand found hers on the gunwale and gripped it with a strength that would leave marks.

The riders wore no colors. That was somehow worse than colors. They came up the bank road at a pace that scattered a flock of geese into the air like thrown linen, and ahead of them, spurring hard from the direction of the tollhouse, rode a fifth horse carrying a man in a sergeant's coat — Rook, who three nights ago had stood in a doorway in the rain and told her, in a voice not meant for reporting, *"The weir gate, east stair, before dawn. And madam — burn nothing you don't have to."*

Rook reached the ferry landing first. What happened next happened in fragments, the way catastrophes do, each piece distinct and terrible:

Rook wheeled his horse sideways across the landing path, dismounted, and drew the boat-hook from the landing stage with both hands, planting himself between the road and the rope.

The four riders came on without slowing.

"Stand down!" Rook's voice carried over the water, cracked and furious and — she heard it clearly, even at fifty feet, even above the river — afraid. "In the King's name, this is a lawful crossing! Sergeant Rook, Millbeck district, and I'll have the name of the first man who passes me!"

One of the riders answered with a pistol.

The report went up the valley and came back off the bridge stones like laughter. Rook staggered but did not fall — Odile saw the ball tear his coat sleeve, saw him swing the boat-hook in a great flat arc that caught the lead rider across the chest and unhorsed him into the mud of the landing. Then the other three were on him, and it stopped being anything she could watch, and she turned Edmund's head firmly toward the far shore with both hands.

"Don't," she said. "Look at me. Look at *me.*"

His eyes were enormous. "They're killing him."

"They are not," she said, with a certainty she did not own, "because he chose this, and because we will not waste it. Say the black cap."

"The—the black cap."

"Good boy. Far bank. Step off on my count."

The ferry was midstream. Below her boots, through the gaps in the planking, she could see it: the whole grey field of the winter's ice moving now, plates grinding past one another, tilting up in slabs green-black with age, the river beneath running thick with broken floes. A slab the size of a hayrick rose against the near arch of Stonecross bridge and shattered with a sound like the end of a war. The shock wave came up through the rope, through the hull, through her knees.

Then the far pulley tower gave a crack of its own.

She heard Finch scream something from the south bank — words lost instantly to the wind — and looked up in time to see the timber tower on the north side lean. Not much. An inch, maybe two, its footing undermined by five days of meltwater working at the scoured earth beneath it. But the rope went slack for half a heartbeat, and the current, which had been fighting them, now simply *had* them.

The ferry swung downstream like a door on a hinge.

Odile threw her weight on the gunwale. The platform righted, dipped, shipped a half-ton of freezing water across their boots. Edmund cried out — a short, choked sound — and she got an arm around him and hauled him against her body and held on while the rope snapped taut again overhead with a whine like a struck wire. They were twenty yards downstream of the bridge now, dragging toward the weir race, and the north landing stage was sliding past on their left at walking speed, then trotting speed, then gone.

"Hand over hand!" she shouted at Edmund, tearing her gloves off because wet wool was death on hemp. "The rope! Pull us to the tower!"

They pulled. God knew how they pulled — her shoulders already torn to ribbons from the marsh crossing, his arms thin as birch switches — but children are closer to the ground where strength lives, and Edmund hauled the ferry along its line foot by foot while the ice spoke beneath them in a language older than any court. The north tower came abeam. The landing stage, a simple platform of stacked sleepers, rose and fell on the swell.

"On my count. Three—"

The world split.

It was not a crack. It was a *tearing*, long and shrieking, running upriver from the weir like a seam ripping out of the country itself. The whole surface of the river broke into independent nations, and one of those nations — a floe wide as a churchyard, thick as a house wall — shouldered up out of the flood directly beneath the ferry and lifted it.

The deck tilted past any angle a body could keep. Odile went down on one knee, Edmund skidding toward the low rail, and her hand shot out and closed on his collar, and for one full second she held her son — she had stopped pretending to herself months ago that he was anything else — suspended over the running water by the grip of one hand and the linen bandage of the packet stretched taut across her chest.

The strap saved them both, and damned everything else.

Her weight came onto it, and the knot — tied in haste by Sister Verity's steady hands, but tied for carrying, not for this — slipped. The packet tore free of her coat, dropped, bounced once on the tilting deck, and slid.

Time went strange. She would remember it afterward always in freeze-frames: the packet turning over and over on the wet planks like a bird with a broken wing; the oilskin wallet with the torn page riding inside it; the black water beyond the rail, chewing; and Edmund — Edmund wrenching free of her hand with the boneless twist only children can manage and diving after it, both small hands closing on the oilskin at the very lip of the gunwale just as the floe passed its crest and the ferry slammed back down onto open current.

"EDMUND!"

He went halfway over. She caught an ankle, then a knee, then hauled him back over the rail in a single screaming effort that put something in her lower back she would feel for a year. They ended in a heap on the flooded deck, streaming, gasping, the packet crushed between his chest and hers — and she found that her arms had locked around him and would not unlock, and that she was saying his name over and over into his wet hair like a prayer with only one word in it.

"I had it," he was saying into her collarbone, wild, triumphant, terrified. "I had it, I *had* it—"

"You never—" She pulled back and gripped his face in both frozen hands. "Never again. Do you hear me? Never again. Paper is paper. *You* are not." Her voice broke clean in half. "There is nothing written in this world worth going into that water for. Nothing. Except you, coming back out of it. Do you understand?"

He understood. She watched it land in him — the whole weight of what she was saying, the arithmetic of love versus vengeance done in a child's eyes — and he nodded once, and pressed the soaked packet into her hands, and said: "Then hold it. I'll pull."

And he pulled.

The landing stage came around on the next swell and she threw the mooring ring — a loop of the guide rope's spare end she'd coiled at her waist since Weir Lock — over the staging bollard on the second try. The ferry checked, swung, ground against the timbers. She lifted Edmund onto the platform first; a pair of hands in a red sleeve reached down from the tower stair to help, and she did not argue. Then the packet, passed up high, dry in its oilskin. Then herself, dragged up the last four feet by arms that belonged to the Stonecross sentry, who hauled her over the edge and onto solid stone and stood there breathing hard, staring at the river behind them.

Where the bridge was coming down.

It went slowly, which made it worse — the near arch first, shedding its ice crown in a white avalanche, then sagging by degrees as though the old stones were only tired. A sound came out of it that Odile felt in her sternum, deeper than thunder, the sound of two hundred years of crossings ending at once. The palisade timbers folded like straw. The middle arch held for one impossible moment, a grey rainbow over the flood with the sky showing through where the spandrels had fallen away — and then it too leaned, sighed, and was gone, and the brown water closed over the place where the road to the assizes had run.

On the south bank, tiny with distance, Tobias Finch stood with his hat in his hand.

And further up the bank road, limping, coatless, alive — Rook, one arm hanging wrong, leaning on the recovered boat-hook, with three riderless horses scattering geese behind him and a fourth shape motionless in the mud. He looked across the ruined crossing at Odile. She raised the packet above her head. After a moment, he raised the boat-hook, and even at that distance she saw him laugh — a short, disbelieving bark of a man reading his own sentence and finding it, against all odds, just.

"Steps," she said hoarsely. The sentry was already pulling at her sleeve. "The courthouse steps. Black cap."

The assize town lay four hundred yards up the hill, and they ran it — she and Edmund, hand in hand, boots hammering cobbles still slick with morning frost, past shopfronts where faces turned and mouths fell open, past the market cross, up the wide flight of stone steps to the shire hall where the doors stood open for the day's sessions. A beadle started forward to stop them. Behind them the valley was still roaring; ahead, through the doors, came the dry murmur of a court in progress.

Odile did not slow. She mounted the last steps with the packet held high, water wringing from the hems of her skirts, a boy's hand gripping hers, and pushed through into the cool dark of the assize hall — where the barristers turned as one, and the judge upon the bench lowered his spectacles, and somewhere in the front row a man in alderman's black rose halfway from his seat and went the color of tallow.

Corven Hale knew her face. She saw him know it.

"My lord," Odile Brightwater said, laying the oilskin packet on the step below the bench with both hands, as carefully as if it were sleeping, "I bring evidence of murder, and I've come three checkpoints and one drowned bridge to deliver it."

Behind her, faint as a heartbeat, she heard the last of Stonecross bridge fall into the sea-going flood — and the doors of the hall boomed shut upon the sound.

* * *

Hale's counsel was already on his feet, calling for the packet to be seized, sealed, examined for forgery — his voice pitched high and reasonable, a man whistling past a graveyard. The judge silenced him with two fingers and beckoned the beadle.

"Open it," he said. "Slowly. And you, woman—" his eyes moved from Odile's wrecked skirts to Edmund's white face to the red-coated sentry dripping in the doorway, "—you will sit where I can see you. All of you. This court has all day, whatever the river has."

They read the manifests first, then the bribe ledger, page by page by page, and the hall grew very quiet except for the scratch of the recording clerk's pen, which began to hurry. When the clerk reached the entry for the *Merrow*, outward bound, insured twice over, condemned by her own court — when he read the sum paid, and to whom, and for what — the judge removed his spectacles entirely and set them down, and looked for a long moment at nothing at all.

Odile held Edmund's hand under the table and thought of Thomas in the cold green light, and found, to her astonishment, that there was room in her at last for both things: the grief, and this.

Then the doors at the back of the hall opened, and a battered man in a torn sergeant's coat, one arm bound in a borrowed sling, walked the length of the aisle with a limp and a set jaw, and every eye in the assize followed him — including, Odile noted, the eyes of two figures slipping quietly out through the lobby door, one in alderman's black and one in harbor-board blue.

Rook stopped before the bench, drew himself as straight as his ribs allowed, and said, "Sergeant Elias Rook, requesting to turn King's evidence. My lord — there's more names in this book than one."

The judge looked at the fleeing backs, at the ledger, at Odile.

"So I begin to suspect," he said dryly, "we shall need more than a day after all."
