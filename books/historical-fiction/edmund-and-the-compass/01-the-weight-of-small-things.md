## 1. The Weight of Small Things

The sea off Port Meridian was the color of hammered tin that morning, and Tamsin Thornbury loved it the way other women loved a mirror — without flattery, knowing every flaw.

She stood at the rail of the company punt with her feet bare and her toes curled over the gunwale, reading the water below the way her mother read faces: for what it hid. Twenty feet down, the pearl beds lay in pale terraces along the estuary floor, shell upon shell, each one a small sealed argument between oyster and tide. The morning light came down through the water in slanted columns and made the shells glow faintly, like coins left at the bottom of a well by someone who meant to come back.

"Thornbury!" The foreman's voice, Hoskins, cracked across the deck like a whip he kept coiled in his throat. "You're dreaming again."

"Diving," she corrected, without turning around. Dreaming happened above the water. Below it there was only work, clean and total.

She spat into her palm, took up the stone weight and the net bag, and went over the side with barely a ripple, because a bad entry cost you breath, and breath was the only currency the sea paid no interest on.

Down, down, ears adjusting, the world narrowing to blue-grey silence and the drum of her own pulse. The cold closed over her like a hand. At fifteen feet her vision sharpened; at eighteen her body began its quiet complaint, the old familiar tightening behind the breastbone, and she told it, as she always did, *not yet*. Discipline was the whole art of it. Any fool could dive. The trick was coming back up on your own terms instead of the water's.

Her fingers found the first shell by touch — rough lip, good weight, the faint magnetic pull a mature shell seemed to have for a practiced hand. She pried it loose and slid it into the net bag. Another. Another. She worked the terrace in a slow arc, methodical as an accountant, because that was what she was, really: an auditor of the seafloor, collecting the company's profits with her lungs.

Somewhere above, the punt creaked at anchor. Somewhere above that, the town of Port Meridian was waking — the customs house hoisting its flag, the trading company's clerks unlocking ledgers full of numbers that had never once described the world honestly. She thought about those ledgers the way you think about weather coming in from seaward: distant, inevitable, nothing to be done.

Then she stopped thinking, because thinking cost air too, and gave herself to the work. Shell after shell. The small bright clicks of them settling in the bag. Her hands knew every stage of the bed better than they knew the streets of the town.

When the black spots began to crowd the edges of her vision — not panic, just the sea politely reminding her of terms — she kicked for the surface and came up gasping in the bright cold morning, hair plastered to her face, and hauled the bag over the rail into Hoskins's waiting hands.

"Sixteen pound," he said, weighing it with a practiced bounce. "Not your best."

"Not my worst." She pulled herself aboard, dripping. "Water's stirred up along the eastern shelf. Silt off the river mouth."

He grunted. He never argued with her readings; that was the one courtesy the company extended her, though it appeared on no ledger. In six years she had never once failed to fill quota, and Hoskins, whose loyalty belonged entirely to whatever kept him comfortable, had learned to treat competence with a kind of superstitious respect.

"You're worth more than half the men I've got," he said, which was his version of a compliment and also, somehow, an accusation.

"I know," said Tamsin, and began winding her foot wrappings.

* * *

Everything she owned could be carried in two trips, and everything that mattered in one hand. She had made this calculation so many times it had worn smooth, like a stone turned over in a pocket: a habit of mind as much as arithmetic.

That evening, back in the room above the cooper's shop that passed for home, she laid them out on the table while the rain started against the shutters. It was not sentiment. It was inventory, and she did it perhaps four times a year, the way a captain checks his stores — because the day would come when the company or the tide or some clerk's whim would put a deadline on her leaving, and she intended to know exactly what leaving would cost.

Item: one knife, bone-handled, blade worn to a finger's width. It had opened a hundred oysters and, once, in a disagreement at the fish market that neither party wished to remember, persuaded a man to reconsider. It weighed almost nothing. She could carry it forever.

Item: one compass, brass, palm-sized, the lid dented where it had been dropped — or thrown; the stories her mother told shifted with the years. It was beautiful still, the needle-guard glass uncracked, the cardinal points etched fine as eyelashes. And it was useless, because there was no needle in it. She had discovered this at nine years old, prying the lid open expecting the small trembling miracle, and finding instead an empty pivot, a little bare post like a stripped tooth. When she'd asked, her mother had said only, "It came to me broken," in the flat tone that ended conversations in their house before they began. So Tamsin owned a compass that pointed nowhere, which she sometimes thought was the most honest object in Port Meridian. Everything else in town claimed to point somewhere.

Item: her mother's silence. Not an object, but she counted it anyway, because it occupied space. It filled the room downstairs where her mother shelled beans and said nothing about the years before Port Meridian. It filled the pauses where other families kept stories. Tamsin had grown up measuring that silence the way she measured everything else — by what it weighed when carried, and by now it weighed more than all the rest of her possessions together, and she was tired of carrying it, and she did not know how to set it down without breaking something.

Beyond these: two dresses, one good. A straw hat gone soft as old bread. A diving stone and line that were company property, as was the punt, as were the beds, as — if the clerks were believed — was Tamsin herself.

That last item deserved its own accounting. She took the folded paper from its hiding place inside the false bottom of the candle box — not hidden from her mother; hidden from impulse — and smoothed it flat on the table. *Indenture of Service, Tamsin Thornbury, aged nineteen years, bound to the Honorless Meridian Pearl & Trade Company.* She had signed at thirteen, too young to understand that a signature given under necessity is not consent but surrender wearing consent's coat. Six years served. The term, originally seven, had a way of renewing itself — tools charged against wages, boat hire, the mysterious *assessment* that appeared every year like a tide chart nobody published. Her debt, by the company's figures, was larger now than when she'd begun.

She looked at the paper for a long moment, then folded it away. There was no use being angry at arithmetic. Arithmetic did not care. But she caught herself, as she folded it, doing the thing she always did: estimating the weight. One sheet. Less than a knife. Easy to carry — and yet it carried her, which was the joke, and the joke did not get funnier with repetition.

Rain thickened outside. Downstairs, a chair scraped, and her mother's footsteps crossed the floor, unhurried, and paused — Tamsin imagined she could hear it — at the base of the stairs, as they sometimes did, as if her mother stood considering whether to climb toward conversation, and then moved on to the sink, and the silence settled another inch deeper.

Tamsin blew out her candle and lay in the dark listening to the harbor working beneath the rain — mooring lines, a hull knocking its fenders, the far bell of the customs house marking the hour. Nine strokes. She could name every sound the port made after dark, and none of the sounds it made in the offices where her future was calculated quarterly.

She fell asleep doing sums. She usually did. This night's sum was simple: one knife, one broken compass, one silence, one paper chain. Total weight, less than a pearl bag. Which meant, she thought as sleep took her, that she had been free within her own accounting for years now. She had simply never found anywhere to spend it.

* * *

The tide brought the packet boat in two days later, and Tamsin was on the quay when it berthed, because quota required her presence at the shell exchange and the exchange waited for no one's grief.

She liked the quayside best at landing hour, when the port performed itself for strangers: gulls wheeling and screaming their extortionate demands, boys hauling ice, the smell of tar and brine and overripe fruit from the market stalls ranged along the sea wall. Company men in their dark coats moved through the crowd like slow fish through a reef, taking note of everything, nourished by it, part of it, and not of it.

The coastal packet *Wren* came in on the morning tide looking battered even by packet standards, her paint scabbed, her rails salt-white. Passengers came down the single gangway in the usual assortment — a factor's wife with too many boxes, a surveyor with instruments wrapped in oilcloth, two clerks of no consequence. And then, last, a man Tamsin's eyes snagged on before her mind had decided why.

He was tall and travel-worn in the way that isn't poverty — boots ruined by salt but once good boots, coat faded but cut for a gentleman, face browned by hard sun and harder weather. He came down the gangway slowly, not from infirmity but from attention. He was counting. She recognized it instantly because it was her own habit done back at her: his gaze touched the customs house roof, the two constables at the head of the quay, the company clerk checking papers at the gate, the alley mouths, the anchored fleet out in the roads. He was measuring the port by what could get him carried out of it.

Interesting, she thought, and returned to tallying shell crates, and did not look at him again for a quarter hour, by which time he had cleared the landing clerk with a paper that must have been adequate, and walked past the shell exchange, and paused — briefly, a man consulting his memory rather than his eyes — at the notice board outside the customs house, where a fresh bill had been pasted up that very morning.

She read it herself on her way to the weighmaster, because new bills were usually about taxes and occasionally interesting.

NOTICE TO ALL PERSONS. By order of the Meridian Pearl & Trade Company, acting under authority of the colonial administration: all incoming passengers shall be subject to search at the customs house, and all correspondence found upon their persons or baggage liable to seizure as contraband. Persons furnishing information leading to the discovery of unlawful correspondence will be considered favorably.

*Considered favorably.* Company language, all through it — rewards hinted at but never named, threats dressed as procedure. She had grown up fluent in it. What she hadn't heard before was this particular note in it, this tightness, as if somewhere inland a fist had closed around the harbor and was waiting for someone to try to slip through it.

Contraband correspondence. Someone had sent letters, and someone feared them enough to make the whole coast a net.

Tamsin filed the thought away with her other inventory — the knife, the compass, the silence — because that was where she kept things that might someday matter. Then the weighmaster bawled her name and she went to sell the company its own shells at the company's own price, and the stranger and the notice both receded into the general noise of the port.

But that night, laying her possessions out on the table for no reason she cared to examine, she added a new line to the ledger she kept in her head, and it was not an object at all: *a stranger who counts exits.*

She estimated its weight. Lighter than it looked, she decided. The light ones were always the dangerous ones.

Outside, the rain had cleared and the harbor lay flat and silver under a rising moon, and out beyond the heads, unseen in the dark, the quarterly fleet rode at anchor, fat with the season's wealth, waiting for the date everyone in Port Meridian arranged their lives around. Fourteen days, the clerks said. Plenty of time.

Tamsin snuffed the candle, and did not yet know that the net she had felt tightening that morning was already drawn close enough to touch her own two hands.
