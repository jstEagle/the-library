## 2. Orders Come at Dawn

The notice went up before the sun did.

Tamsin Thornbury saw it from the water, which was the only place she ever saw the town whole — a smear of whitewash and corrugated iron stacked against the headland, the company warehouse with its flag limp as a dead man's tongue, and above it all the customs house, whose veranda posts had sprouted a rectangle of fresh paper that caught the first grey light like a patch of new skin on old scar tissue. She had been underwater when the clerk came out with his paste brush and his ladder. She knew this because she could read the shape of mornings in the harbor the way other people read almanacs: which boats moved first, which dogs barked, which windows lit and in what order. A man on a ladder at half past five was an event. The harbor did not tolerate events before six.

She surfaced beside the lugger's hull, spat salt, and held the gunwale while Hosmer hauled up her basket of shell by its rope, the pulley squealing like something being killed slowly.

"You're early," he said.

"The tide's early."

"The tide's always early. You're usually late." He tipped the basket onto the sorting board and began turning shells with a wooden paddle, discarding the empties, keeping the ones that held their pearl-light like a secret kept too long. "There's a paper on the customs house."

"I saw it."

"Didn't know you could read it from out there."

"There's very little I can't see from out there." She pulled herself over the gunwale, water sheeting off her diving smock, and stood a moment wringing the hem while the wind found her and pressed its cold thumb against her spine. From the deck of the lugger the town resolved into detail: the ladder, the clerk, the paste bucket, three men already gathered beneath the notice even though the light was too thin to read by. Men would gather to read anything posted by the company, the way gulls gather over anything thrown from a ship. Not because they expected bread. Because they had learned not to be the last to know.

Hosmer followed her gaze. "Orders," he said, in the flat tone he reserved for the word. It sat in the same drawer of his voice as *fever*, *storm*, and *tax*.

"Orders," she agreed, and went below to change, because whatever the paper said, it would still say it after she was dry, and she had learned long ago that dread keeps. It does not spoil. It waits better than fish, better than milk, better than almost anything except debt.

* * *

By seven she stood in the customs house yard with forty other divers, packers, and beach-hands, arranged in the loose crescent the company preferred for announcements — close enough to hear, far enough to suggest that pressing forward might be noted in someone's book. The morning had burned off its grey and gone hard blue. The flag had found enough wind to lift one corner, and it snapped at intervals like a slow applause for no one.

Mr. Quill, the assistant clerk — a small man made smaller by a uniform cut for someone else's shoulders — mounted the step beneath the veranda and unrolled a document with the ceremony of a priest unfolding scripture.

"By direction of the Resident Factor," he read, "and pursuant to instructions received from the Board, let it be known that effective this day, all persons and effects arriving at this port by sea shall be subject to examination at the customs house prior to release into the township." He paused, wet his lips. "This examination shall extend particularly — I am instructed to say *particularly* — to written matter. Letters, packets, documents, correspondence of any hand or origin. No incoming passenger shall pass the customs line carrying papers unexamined, nor shall any article be forwarded, concealed, or entrusted for the purpose of evading said examination."

He lowered the page. In the silence a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"Examinations will be conducted by appointed inspectors of the company," Quill continued, "at the discretion of the factor. Persons obstructing, delaying, or attempting to deceive the inspection will be fined at Schedule C rates or committed, per their standing."

Someone near the back — Tamsin did not turn to see who; you never turned to see who — asked whether outgoing post was affected. Quill consulted his paper as though it might have changed since he looked at it on the step.

"Incoming only," he said. "At present."

*At present* did a great deal of work in that sentence, and everyone in the crescent heard it do it.

Tamsin stood with her arms folded, thumbs hooked under her collarbones, and watched Mr. Quill's eyes instead of his mouth. Mouths read words. Eyes read instructions, and his eyes kept flicking left, toward the veranda door where Mr. Calloway the Resident Factor sometimes stood to observe his staff perform obedience. The door stayed shut, but Quill performed anyway, straightening between sentences like a plant leaning at a window.

When the reading was done, the crescent broke apart with the studied indifference of people who intended to discuss everything they'd just heard and wanted no one to see them wanting to. Tamsin did not move. She was doing sums.

All persons arriving by sea. Examined at the line. Papers especially. It was not the searching that snagged her — searching was weather in a port; you dressed for it — it was the *particularly*. Somebody had written *particularly* in London or in the factor's back office, and men did not write *particularly* about letters unless a particular letter was coming, or feared to come, or had been reported coming. The harbor net was not being strung for sardines. It was strung for one fish, and every diver, porter, and passenger now walking through the customs yard was going to be shaken out of the water one by one until that fish lay flopping on the boards.

She felt the tightening the way she felt pressure changes on descent: not as thought but as weight behind the ears, a warning that the world above the surface had decided something without consulting anyone below.

"Tamsin."

Her foreman, Garrow, stood at the yard's edge under the warehouse eave, holding two mugs of the company's coffee, which was a liquid rumor of coffee brewed from the same grounds since the previous quarter. He offered one. This was itself information. Garrow offered nobody anything before ten o'clock.

She took it. It was terrible. She drank it.

"You were in the water when the notice went up," Garrow said. Conversationally. Garrow was never conversational before money entered the sentence.

"I was."

"Quill read it well?"

"He read it like a man whose boots don't fit."

A smile touched the corner of Garrow's mouth and withdrew, embarrassed to have been seen there. He was a heavy man gone light in the wrong places, with hands scarred white at the knuckles from twenty years of rope and brine, and he had been foreman of the diving crews since before Tamsin's mother had been a diver herself, which made him either an institution or an obstruction depending on how much you valued your lungs. He had taught her the breathing tables. He had also, once, docked her mother's pay for a month over a basket of shell short by weight, and never explained, and never apologized, and Tamsin had filed both facts in the same drawer because in a port like this they belonged together.

"The factor wants inspectors," Garrow said. "Proper ones. Not the usual customs loafers — men who can read, count, and keep their mouths shut. Women too." His eyes moved over the dispersing crowd without appearing to look at anyone. "There's a consideration attached."

"A consideration."

"A reward, if you want it said plain. For vigilance." He pronounced the word carefully, as though it had been given to him in writing and he was returning it undamaged. "Any inspector whose examination turns up contraband correspondence stands to receive a bonus against their account. Substantial."

Against their account. Tamsin turned the phrase over and felt its edges. Everything in this port ran through *the account* — the great invisible ledger at the company house where her indenture lived, where her mother's debts lived, where numbers were added by men she never saw for reasons she was never shown. A bonus against the account meant the company paying her with one hand what it would take back with the other, or pretending to, or simply writing the number down somewhere she couldn't reach and letting her spend her life trying to believe in it. But a bonus was also a bonus. Numbers on the right side of a ledger were rare enough in her life that she could not afford pure contempt for them, and Garrow knew it. That was why there were two mugs.

"What kind of correspondence is worth a substantial bonus?" she asked.

"The kind the Board says is worth one."

"Garrow."

"Don't say my name like that in the open yard." He drank, watching the harbor over the rim. A coastal packet was working in around the point, late-season, riding high and wrong in the water, the way packets rode when they'd burned through their coal and were making the last miles on canvas and stubbornness. He watched it longer than a man watches a boat he isn't waiting for.

"They didn't tell me what it is," he said finally. "That's the truth, and you can weigh it. What I know is the orders came down fast, ahead of the mail run, ahead of everything — came down so fast Quill's copy still smelled of ink yesterday evening. Something's coming across this water that somebody doesn't want to arrive, and the company means to catch it at the line before it touches shore." He finished the mug and crushed it slightly, absently, in his fist. "And they mean to pay well for eyes that catch it first."

"And if my eyes choose to miss it?"

The silence that followed had texture. Garrow set his crushed mug on the eave-post with great precision, and when he spoke his voice had dropped into the register he used for weights and depths, the honest one.

"Then you'd want to be very sure your eyes missed it, girl, because a reward for vigilance has a twin sister, and she's called a penalty for negligence, and they share a bed in the same ledger. If you stand at that line, you'll be standing there as the company's eyes. Whatever those eyes see or don't see gets written down." He straightened. "I'm to give Quill a name by noon. Yours was mentioned."

"By whom?"

"Does it matter?" And then, softer, because he was not entirely stone: "You dive better than anyone on this coast. You count better than Quill. You've got nothing else they want and nothing they can hold over you except the usual." The usual meant the indenture. The usual meant everything. "Think on it. Noon."

He walked off toward the weighing shed, and Tamsin stood alone in the yard with the empty mug and the feeling of a net drawn one ring tighter around the whole bright harbor.

* * *

She thought on it in the way she thought best, which was underwater.

They shifted to the middle grounds before the tide turned, and she took the morning's dives in the green half-light where the shell beds lay in their pale fields like a drowned moon's surface. Down there the world simplified. There was the rope in her fist, the weight on her belt, the bottom at eleven fathoms, and her own body as instrument — lungs banked, heart slowed to a rower's stroke, everything nonessential paid off and sent ashore. Her mother had taught her the discipline before her mother had stopped teaching her anything: *the water doesn't take from you what you don't bring down*. Fear sank poorly. It dragged. So you left it on the deck with your boots and your coat and everything else you could not carry, and you went down light.

What can be carried. It was the arithmetic underneath all her others. Her knife: carried. The compass, brass-cased, needle-less, useless and therefore beyond theft, which was perhaps why she still carried it. Her mother's silence: not carried so much as worn, like a garment cut years ago for someone larger. The company owned the rest — the bed she slept in, the boat she dove from, the air hours of her days apportioned against a debt whose principal grew like coral, imperceptibly, in the dark. She had never resented the arithmetic itself. That was the strange thing she could admit down here, where honesty cost less. She was good at her life. She could hold four minutes of it in a single breath. There was pride in competence the way there was warmth in a banked fire — not much, but real, and hers.

The resentment, when it came, was never about the weight. It was about the fact that someone else had packed her bag.

She surfaced, breathed, dove again. Between dives she hung on the ladder with the water sliding off her and watched the customs house across the harbor, where a carpenter was already building something at the end of the arrival line — a low bench, a table. A table where bags would be opened and shirts shaken out and letters held up to the light. By noon it had acquired a canvas awning, and by mid-afternoon two constables of the port watch stood near it in the manner of men told to stand near things, and the whole arrangement had the look of a stage dressed before the actors arrived.

For whom? She turned the question like a shell, looking for the pearl in it. Orders down fast, faster than ink should travel. *Particularly* written matter. Rewards for vigilance sized to suggest fear rather than generosity. Somewhere out beyond the point, past the safe anchorages and the pilot station, the world was preparing to send this harbor a piece of paper dangerous enough that the company would pay divers' wages multiplied by ten to intercept it — and was willing, she noted, to arm every greedy, frightened, indebted soul on the waterfront as its informer in the process. That was the true design of a notice like today's. It wasn't the table and the awning and the constables. It was the forty people in the crescent who had heard *substantial bonus* and would now look at every stranger's coat pocket for the rest of the season. The company had strung its net through their eyes, and none of them had felt the knots go in.

Including, possibly, hers. Garrow would give Quill a name by noon. Hers had been mentioned. She had not said no.

She told herself she hadn't decided because there was nothing yet to decide against — that refusing sight-unseen was sentiment, not principle, and she did not trade in sentiment; sentiment weighed too much to carry. She told herself the sensible thing was to stand at the line, open the bags, earn the wage, and let the paper fall where papers fell. One letter more or less in the world. What was that to a woman who measured everything by whether it could be carried?

But the water had taught her another measure too, one she trusted more than arithmetic though she'd have denied it on dry land: the measure of pressure. When the harbor tightened, fish behaved strangely. When the company moved suddenly, someone downstream lost something they could not replace, and the loss was never recorded in any ledger, because ledgers only counted what the company valued, and the company valued paper over people at a rate no diver could read without flinching.

Whatever was coming across that water was going to meet the net. And whoever carried it was going to walk into a yard full of rewarded eyes — hers among them, maybe — and be opened like a bag on a public table.

The packet she'd seen at dawn made the outer anchorage as the light went long, dropping hook with a raggedness that suggested a hard passage. Small boat, coastal-class, one passenger figure visible at the rail in a coat gone stiff with salt spray. Even at that distance, even across a mile of flashing water, Tamsin found her gaze going to the figure the way a compass finds north — no needle to guide it, just some older, unacknowledged instrument in her that had begun, quietly, to swing.

She climbed the ladder, stripped her gear, and went to find Garrow before noon.

"Not yet," she said, when she reached him. "Whoever's name you give Quill — give it tomorrow. I want to see what comes off that packet first."

Garrow looked past her shoulder at the anchorage, then back, and something in his face suggested he had already heard about the packet from someone faster.

"Too late for that," he said. "It's coming in now. And Quill's had word the passenger's asking questions already — at the wharf, before his feet are properly dry. Asking after a man who's been dead two years."

The wind came off the harbor and snapped the company flag once, hard, like a finger being cracked.

"So," Garrow said, "you'll want to decide what your eyes see. Today."
