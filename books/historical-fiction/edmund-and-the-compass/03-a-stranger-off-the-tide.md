## 3. A Stranger Off the Tide

The packet boat came in on the morning tide with her paint peeling and her engine coughing like an old man climbing stairs, and Edmund Roe stepped off her with salt in his boots and a dead man's handwriting against his ribs.

He had dressed for the part three days ago, in the cabin of that same boat while it wallowed down the coast: coat brushed but not new, hat brim cracked just enough to suggest a man who had traveled hard and cheaply, collar softened by sweat so no one would mistake him for a merchant's clerk or — worse — a gentleman slumming through the pearl ports. Gentlemen got searched gently and questioned thoroughly. Poor men got searched roughly and forgotten. He intended to be poor, forgettable, and gone before anyone thought to remember his face.

His boots had not survived the voyage as well as his plan. The left one had taken seawater over the rail during the second night, in a swell that had put half the passengers retching into buckets, and the leather had dried stiff and bloomed white along the seams. Salt-damaged boots. A small thing. But small things were what got a man noticed at a customs house, and he stood a moment on the wet planks of the landing stage letting the harbor resolve itself around him, cataloguing everything the way Vane had taught him to catalogue a strange anchorage twelve years ago: exits first, then eyes, then the rhythm of the place.

The exits were simple enough to read. The landing stage ran out on tarred pilings to a stone quay, and from the quay the town climbed in crooked terraces toward a ridge of whitewashed buildings that might have been beautiful if beauty had ever been permitted here. It was not. Everything in the port faced inward, toward the water, toward the company. Even the church — and there was a church, squat and serious above the warehouses — seemed to orient itself not toward God but toward the counting houses below it.

The company's flag hung limp on its pole above the customs house. No wind this morning. The heat already lay on the harbor like a hand pressing down, and the air smelled of shell, tar, fish blood, and under all of it something sweeter and more expensive — pearl meat, oyster flesh rotting in the sun after the shells had been stripped. A working smell. A smell that meant money moving quietly from the lungs of divers into the ledgers of men who never got their feet wet.

Edmund counted the eyes.

There were always eyes. Every port had them; this port would have more. He picked out three without hurrying, because hurrying was how you told an observer you had something to hide. The first leaned against the bollard at the quay's landward end — a heavy man in company gray, doing an excellent impression of idleness except that his head turned with every passenger coming up the gangway, and his lips moved slightly as he counted, or mouthed descriptions to himself. Descriptions for later. *Middle-aged man, dark coat, bad boots.* The second was harder to spot: a boy of perhaps fifteen sitting cross-legged on a coil of rope near the shell scales, apparently asleep, whose eyes opened — Edmund was almost sure they opened — each time someone set foot on the stones. The third he found only because the third was watching him find the other two, a thin woman in a factor's sash standing back from the crowd with a notebook open against her forearm, writing nothing, waiting for something worth writing.

*Already watched,* he thought, and felt no surprise, only a cold settling in his chest like ballast taken aboard. *Before I even landed. They know a letter went out. They know someone will come asking after it.*

Well. He had known the odds when he stepped aboard the packet at a fishing village forty miles up the coast, paying for his passage in coin he had earned gutting fish for three weeks because he dared not draw on any account the company's agents might trace. He had known it when he sewed the letters into the lining of his coat — not the breast pocket, never the pocket, pockets were the first thing fingers learned to search — in flat oilskin packets no thicker than two fingers stacked, distributed across four seams so that no single cut would take them all.

Two years of a dead man's hand. That was what he carried. Two years of Captain Aldous Vane writing in his fierce downward-slanting script, letters smuggled out in ones and twos — inside a parcel of charts, sewn into a sea bag sold at auction, folded into the lining of a hat that passed through three hands before reaching Edmund's — each one more damning than the last, and the last of them eight months old now, and then nothing. Silence. And a silence that lasted eight months, in Edmund's experience, was not a man being careful. It was a man being stopped.

A gull screamed somewhere overhead and the sound cracked the heat open for a moment, and Edmund made himself move. Not fast. The pace of a man with nowhere urgent to be, a man come to this port for work because work existed here for anyone willing to sweat. He came up the quay with his canvas bag over one shoulder — light, deliberately light, everything in it replaceable — and let the heavy man at the bollard look at him, and looked back with exactly the amount of interest a tired traveler would spare.

"Passenger list," the man said. Not a question. His voice had the flattened boredom of authority long unchallenged.

"Edmund Roe." The name came out smooth. He had practiced it until it belonged to him, until he could say it in his sleep, because it did belong to him — it was real, it was his father's name, and that was precisely why he had promised himself not to use it. Too late now. "Clerk. Come down for the season, if there's paper work to be had."

The man's eyes did their work — face, hands, boots, bag — and Edmund endured it the way one endures weather. The gaze snagged, briefly, on the ruined left boot, and moved on. Nothing about a clerk suggested contraband. Clerks carried pens and debts, not secrets.

"Customs house. All baggage inspected." The man jerked his chin inland without looking at it. "Before you seek lodging, before you seek work, before anything. New orders."

"New orders?" Edmund let mild curiosity onto his face, the curiosity of a man who had heard nothing, who had arrived from nowhere, who had no reason in the world to care.

"Since Tuesday." The heavy man had already turned to the next figure coming up the gangway, dismissing him with the totality of official indifference. "Contraband correspondence."

And there it was, said aloud, flat as a dropped tool, in front of a dozen strangers on a public quay: *contraband correspondence.* Letters were now cargo too dangerous to land. Which meant the company knew what Vane had been doing. Which meant they knew — or feared — that some of it had gotten past them. Which meant the net was not merely out; it was drawn taut, and every man, woman, and child stepping off a boat this morning was a fish to be sorted.

Edmund walked toward the customs house at his unhurried pace and did not let himself think about the seam of his coat where the thickest packet lay, because thinking about it put his hand anywhere near it, and a hand drifting toward a hidden seam was the oldest tell in the world.

* * *

The customs house had been built to impress and had succeeded only in being large. Its facade of pale imported stone glared in the morning sun, and its doors stood open to admit a slow procession of passengers and cargo and suspicion. Inside, the ceiling rose high enough to hold the heat like a kiln, and the noise of the harbor outside became suddenly distant, replaced by the sounds of inspection: the scrape of trunk lids, the slap of ledger pages, the barked repetitions of clerks whose voices had worn down to gravel years ago. *Open it. Unpack it. Turn it out. Next.*

Edmund joined the line of passengers and studied the room the way he'd once studied enemy harbors from a ship's foretop, in Vane's day, when they were both young and the world's injustices were still abstractions to him. Three inspection tables. Behind them, six men in company gray, working in pairs. At the far end, a raised desk where a senior officer sat with a stack of papers and a bell he rang whenever a dispute needed ending — and beside that desk, leaning against the wall where she could see every table at once, a second watcher, this one a woman with ink-stained fingers and a diver's unusual stillness, who was not inspecting anything and therefore must be inspecting everyone.

She was young, mid-twenties perhaps, with the deep-even tan and the economical movements of someone who worked the water for a living, and she held herself apart from the guards with the particular loneliness of competence among fools. Her eyes went from passenger to passenger with a methodical sweep, and when they crossed Edmund — they crossed him twice, he counted — they paused the second time for a heartbeat longer than they had the first.

*She sees the boots,* he thought. Or she saw something else. Rehearsed calm, maybe. He had rehearsed his calm for three days on a rolling deck, and rehearsed things always sat slightly wrong on a man, like clothing cut for someone else. Perhaps she could see the seams of it the way he could see the seams of his own coat.

The line advanced. One table over, a family of island traders was being turned out piece by piece — baskets overturned, dried fish spilling, a grandmother's shawl shaken hard enough to make its bone buttons rattle. The family bore it with the numbness of long practice. Nobody protested. Protest cost more than compliance in a company port; that lesson had been learned before any of them were born and taught to each generation early.

*They're looking for paper,* Edmund reminded himself. *Paper is what threatens them. Not weapons, not spirits, not stolen pearls — paper.* There was something almost magnificent in the fear of it, when you stood back far enough. An empire of shell and ships and armed men, afraid of folded sheets in a dead man's hand. Almost magnificent, if it hadn't also been his life balanced on those same folded sheets.

"Name."

He came to the table. The inspector was young and thick-necked, with the resentful diligence of a man promoted past his abilities and determined to prove otherwise.

"Edmund Roe. Clerk."

"Bag."

He set the canvas bag on the table and opened it himself, helpfully, showing its poverty: two shirts, a razor, a tin of tobacco, a Bible bought secondhand in the fishing village for the express purpose of being searched, a bundle of blank paper and pens to feed the fiction of the clerk. The guard went through it with rough hands, squeezing the shirts, flipping the Bible's pages — and Edmund watched his own breathing, kept it slow, kept his shoulders easy, while beneath the guard's elbows four oilskin packets lay sewn flat against the coat the man was not touching, because the coat was on Edmund's body, and bodies required cause to search, and cause required suspicion, and suspicion was a flower that grew only where something watered it.

"You carry letters?"

"Blank stock and my references." He produced a folded sheet — genuine, from a shipping office up the coast, obtained with three weeks of fish guts and a fabricated history of clerical employment. The guard read it with visible disappointment, as though hoping to find the paper interesting.

"Sign the book."

Edmund signed. *Edmund Roe, clerk,* in a hand he had altered — smaller, rounder, less confident than his own — and the lie of it settled into his stomach alongside the heat and the fear, familiar now, almost comfortable. Two days ago it had still sickened him. Fear wore grooves in a man quickly, and he was learning to walk in them.

"Next."

He took up his bag, nodded the nod of the processed, and walked toward the doors and the glare beyond them — and as he passed between the tables, the woman with ink-stained fingers turned her head and followed him with her eyes, openly this time, without pretense, the way a person looks at a knot in a net they are responsible for mending.

She did not call out. She did not signal the guards. She simply watched him go, and her expression was not suspicion exactly, nor curiosity exactly, but some third thing he could not name — and could not stop thinking about as he stepped out into the hammering sunlight and turned, as any clerk seeking lodging would turn, uphill toward the terraces.

* * *

He allowed himself one alley, one pause, one moment with his back against warm brick and his eyes closed, letting the terror drain out through his boot soles the way Vane had taught him: *"Fear's a tide, Ned. You don't fight it, you time it. Stand still and let it run out of you."* Twelve years since Vane had said that, on a night watch in the Java Sea, both of them younger than they understood. Twelve years, and now Vane was two years drowned — officially drowned, ceremonially drowned, eulogized in a company report Edmund has read eleven times, searching it for the lie — and Edmund stood in a hot alley in the man's last port with the man's last testament stitched against his heart, and the tide of fear ran out of him and left behind what it always left behind: the cold, clean space where determination lived.

He had asked himself the question a hundred times on the packet boat, in the dark, in the reek of seasick passengers: *why are you doing this?* Not for money; there was none in it. Not for justice, entirely, though the word applied. He did it because Aldous Vane had spent two years as a dead man writing the truth in a hand that shook slightly by the final letters — with age, with fear, Edmund couldn't know — and had trusted it to the one person left alive who had called him brother. And because the letters named men. That was the unbearable part. Not statistics, not sums — names. Divers recorded as lost at sea who had never been lost. Estates swallowed by forged certificates of death. Debts extinguished by drowning men who were, in some cases, still walking around other ports with different names, or in other cases — Edmund's throat tightened — genuinely dead after all, killed not by the sea but by the arithmetic that found the sea convenient.

Including, though he did not yet know he knew it, a diver named Thornbury.

He pushed off the wall. Lodging first — cheap, anonymous, near the water. Then the route. Then the man he had come to find, if the man could be found, if the silence of eight months meant anything other than what Edmund feared it meant.

He was halfway up the first terrace stair when he saw the customs-house woman again. She stood at the corner of the shell market with a basket on her hip as though she had been sent on an errand, and she was not looking at him — she was better than that, she was looking at the fish stalls with perfect attention — but the geometry of the market placed her squarely on the path between him and every boarding house near the water.

Coincidence, possibly. He gave it its fair weight. Ports were small; paths crossed.

But when he changed direction, cutting north along the terrace toward the chandlers' row, and thirty seconds later her basket drifted the same way, unhurried, deliberate, a woman shopping who never quite arrived anywhere — Edmund Roe stopped telling himself stories, ducked through a cooperage yard, and came out on the beach road with his heart beating hard and one conclusion fixed in him like a nail driven to the head:

The net had a knot in it, and the knot was a diver's daughter with ink on her fingers, and she had marked him at the customs house, and she was following him — which meant that either she meant to give him to Calloway's men, or she wanted something from him no one else in this whole burning port could give.

Either way, he had perhaps a day. Maybe less.
