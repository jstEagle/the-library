## 4. Handwriting of a Drowned Man

Edmund Roe had learned to read handwriting the way other men learned to read weather, and the hand on the letters against his chest was a fair-weather hand gone bad — a hand that had started life confident and generous and had been taught, over years, to write small.

Aldous Vane had written large once. Edmund remembered it precisely: the ship's log of the *Merrow*, eleven years back, Vane's entries sprawling across the pages like a man who believed the world had room for him. *Calm. Water casks filled at dawn. Roe drunk again but functional.* That last phrase had made the whole crew laugh when Edmund finally read it, and Vane had grinned and said, *Functional is the highest compliment I give,* and Edmund had thrown a biscuit at his head, and the world had been wide and salt-bright and possible.

Now the same hand came across the water in packets no bigger than a hymnal, written in ink so pale it might have been tea, each line compressed as though even the paper were listening.

*E — Do not come by the front door. There is no front door anymore.*

He had read that first letter forty times, in forty anchorages, and every time he arrived at the same cold arithmetic: Aldous Vane was afraid, and Aldous Vane had never in his life been afraid of anything that bled.

* * *

They had met as boys before the mast, two orphans of the coast who discovered quickly that they could split any labor between them — Vane took the sums, Edmund took the blame — and the arrangement had held through three ships, one shipwreck, and a war's worth of boring peacetime. Vane went up; Edmund stayed level. It suited them both. Vane had the kind of mind that saw a fleet where other men saw boats, and Edmund had the kind that noticed when a rope was frayed. Between them they had never lost a cargo worth mentioning and never lost a friendship at all, which was the rarer feat.

Then Vane swallowed the anchor and went ashore — took a position with the Pearl and Spice Consolidated, their old charter company, as an inspector of accounts. *Someone honest has to count it,* he'd written, *or no one will know what honesty would even look like.* Edmund had laughed at the letter and pinned it above his bunk, and for two years it hung there like a joke waiting for its punchline.

The punchline came in the spring of 1845, in the form of a newspaper clipping sent without a note: **CAPTAIN A. VANE LOST AT SEA. PACKET *LARKSPUR* OVERWHELMED OFF THE SHOALS. NO SURVIVORS.**

Edmund had done what everyone does. He had gotten drunk — not functionally — and he had written to the company for particulars and received back a courteous, printed form expressing condolences on behalf of the Pearl and Spice Consolidated, signed by a factor named Calloway whose signature was very fine indeed, all loops and confidence, the handwriting of a man who had never once doubted a ledger in his life.

It was the printedness of it that saved Vane's life, though Edmund didn't know that yet. Because six weeks later, a packet reached him at a pilot station two hundred miles down the coast, addressed in a hand he knew better than his own, and the first line said:

*You attended my funeral. How was the weather?*

* * *

The letters came slow after that. Slow, and coded, and increasingly terrible.

Vane explained it in fragments, the way a man explains a hole he has fallen into — one wall at a time. The Consolidated held debts, and debts on the pearling coast were strange instruments: an indenture, a boat mortgage, a diver's advance against the season's catch. And the company had discovered, somewhere in its long digestion of the coast, a truth simpler and more profitable than collection.

A dead man owes nothing. A dead man's estate, however, owes everything — and an estate passes through the company's hands to be settled.

The first case Vane documented was a diver named Okonkwo, eight years indentured, two years from freedom, drowned — officially — in a squall that no other boat in the anchorage ever saw. His debt did not die with him; it converted. It became a claim against his gear, his hut, his share-book, and the portion owed to a widow who could not read the settlement papers she pressed her thumb to. The company recovered triple what Okonkwo had ever owed, because the company had appraised his estate, and the company did the appraising.

*I thought it was an anomaly,* Vane wrote, in the third letter. *Companies make errors. Then I found the pattern, and patterns are never errors. Patterns are policy.*

There was the schooner master Halloran, drowned within a month of suing the company over a shorted cargo — his suit died with him, his boat passed to the Consolidated at appraisal, his family passed to the road. There were the two brothers from the eastern beds, entered in the death registers on the same date though they'd worked different boats a hundred miles apart, both of them, per the register, drowned in the same improbable patch of sea. There was a woman, name struck through in Vane's copy of the register, whose cause of death was listed as drowning and whose body, Vane noted in the margin in a hand gone suddenly ragged, had been seen alive in the interior settlement four months later, buying flour.

*They do not always kill anyone,* Vane wrote. *That is the part I cannot stop examining. Mostly they only need the paper to say it. A man erased on paper is as good as dead to everything except his own eyes, and his own eyes are the one witness the company cannot subpoena. Some of the drowned are walking around out there. They cannot claim their own names. If they do, they contradict a certificate with a company seal on it, and the seal wins. It always wins.*

Edmund read that letter in a harbor tavern with such noise around him that no one noticed a grown man set down his glass very carefully, as if it might go off.

* * *

The smuggling of the evidence itself had been Vane's masterpiece, and Edmund understood, re-reading the account of it, that the whole design had been built backward from a single fact: nothing leaves this port unsearched except what the port itself sends out.

So Vane never tried to send anything out.

Instead, over nineteen months, he had threaded the evidence into the company's own circulation. A page copied here, sewn into the binding of an accounts ledger bound for a sub-factor down the coast. Names and dates disguised as sample tallies in a shell shipment's manifest, decipherable only with the key Vane mailed separately, months later, in the margins of a prayer book. Death registers photographed — no, copied by hand, hours apiece, in the archive room where Vane's position gave him legitimate access, each copy interleaved into correspondence that traveled under frank as routine business. He had used the company's arteries to carry the description of its own disease. By the time the Consolidated suspected anything, if it ever did, the proof of its crimes would be scattered across forty desks in twelve ports, assembled in no place the company could burn.

Except with Edmund. Edmund was the assembly point. Vane had chosen him for the simplest of reasons: Edmund had no position, no contract, no registered address, no file in any company drawer. He was a free coaster, a cargo-broker of no fixed office, a man who could stand in a room without appearing in its inventory. *You are the only man I know,* Vane wrote, *whose entire existence fits in a duffel bag. Guard it well. I am sending you mine.*

And the letters had come, month upon month, until Edmund held — sewn into the false spine of a Bible, wrapped in oilcloth inside a hollowed-out ship's biscuit tin, folded into the lining of a hat — the complete anatomy of nine falsified deaths, four seized estates, two forged certificates traced to the clerk whose hand produced them, and one memorandum in Factor Calloway's own fine looping script, authorizing, in language of perfect bureaucratic blandness, *the administrative resolution of the Halloran matter.*

Then, fourteen months ago, the letters stopped.

* * *

Silence was the thing Edmund had never learned to carry.

Grief he could manage. He'd buried parents, shipmates, a wife taken young by fever — grief had a shape, a weight, rituals for it, and eventually the weight distributed itself across your shoulders until you walked crooked but you walked. Silence was different. Silence was a rope that went taut and stayed taut, and you stood holding your end with no way of knowing whether anything still held the other.

For the first year he had told himself the obvious stories. The route compromised; Vane gone deeper underground; Vane ill; Vane simply being careful, more careful than even his enormous caution required. Each story bought him a month of patience. Each month of patience cost him a little more sleep.

By the second winter he had stopped telling stories and started moving. He worked the coast downward, port by port, asking questions shaped like nothing — a remark about old shipmates over cards, a mention of the *Larkspur* to a harbormaster, a casual reference to inspectors of accounts to a clerk who owed him money. Most men heard nothing. Two men heard too much, and Edmund watched their faces close like doors, and understood that the word *Vane* had a price on it in places he couldn't see.

The last answer came from a half-blind pilot at a river station, who took Edmund's coin and said: *Company hulk anchored off the pearling coast. They keep men there nobody's sentenced. Ask me nothing else, because I've said a sentence too many already, and I like breathing.*

A hulk. Edmund had served on one as a boy, briefly, before the mast rescued him — a decommissioned hull moored offshore, a floating country with no law but the keeper's. Men went onto hulks and came off them as lists. Sometimes they did not come off them at all, and the list recorded, in a clerk's bland hand, *discharged,* and nobody asked discharged to where.

He had stood on the pilot's rotten jetty with the river sliding past and done the sum he had been avoiding for fourteen months. Either Aldous Vane was dead — truly dead this time, dead in the way that left no letter — or he was in a place designed to produce exactly this silence, and either way the obligation did not change. It had never been conditional on Vane's pulse. *I am sending you mine,* Vane had written, and Edmund had accepted delivery. What a man accepts, he carries.

What he carried now weighed less than two pounds. Nine deaths. Four estates. One memorandum in a fine looping hand. Against it: three checkpoints between the customs house and the quarterly fleet, a coast where the company's writ ran to the tide line and past it, and a name — if the pilot was right, if any of it was right — that might be breathing at the bottom of all those papers, wanting them badly enough to have spent nineteen months sending them ahead of himself like a lifeline thrown in pieces.

* * *

On the last night before the packet sailed, Edmund laid everything out on the floor of a rented room and committed it to memory one final time, because memory was the one courier that could not be searched.

The oilcloth packet, flat against his sternum under his shirt. The key to the manifest cipher, disguised as a laundry list in his coat pocket. The forged clerk's pass he had paid a desperate man too much money to produce, good for one crossing of a toll bridge if the sergeant wasn't diligent. And the envelope — he kept this separate, though there was nothing in it — the plain envelope the first letter had come in, postmarked the week after Vane's funeral, because some proofs mattered more than evidence. It proved that a drowned man had licked a stamp.

He sat back on his heels among the debris of Aldous Vane's life and let himself feel it, briefly and on purpose, the way you check a wound to see if it's healing: the grief, braided tight with the obligation, neither one distinguishable from the other anymore. He had loved this man since they were rigging-boys laughing at each other across a deck. He had mourned him. He had un-mourned him. He would do either again, whichever the coast demanded. But he found, taking inventory of himself as carefully as of the papers, that what was left underneath the grief was not despair. It was anger, and anger was a fuel, and the tank was full.

*If I am taken,* Vane had written in the final letter, *do not negotiate. Do not explain. Papers do not need you to defend them; they only need to arrive. Get them to someone the company cannot buy, and let the numbers do the shouting. And E — if it comes to choosing between the papers and yourself, choose the papers. You were always functional. Functional men are replaceable. This is not.*

Edmund folded the letter along its old creases and put it with the rest.

*I'll decide that on the day,* he said aloud, to an empty room, to a drowned man, to whatever hulk might be holding him off a pearling coast four hundred miles north. It sounded, in the quiet, like a promise made to the wrong party, and he suspected it was.

* * *

The packet boat put him ashore at the colonial port on a morning of hard white light, and Edmund came down the gangway with salt-damaged boots and two pounds of revolution against his chest, doing what he had trained himself to do for fourteen months: looking without seeming to look.

He saw the customs house first, and the new orders posted beside its doors, and the searchers working the line of disembarking passengers with a thoroughness that had not been there, by any harbor gossip he'd collected on the way down, even a month ago. Someone had tightened something. Someone was expecting something.

He saw the foreman by the pearl sheds glance along the passenger line twice — not at faces, but at hands, at bags, at the places a man carries paper — and then speak quietly to a company man who walked away fast.

He saw, and this he catalogued and filed with the coldest part of himself, that the net was not merely imagined, and that he had stepped directly into the center of it with the evidence of nine murders sewn into his shirt.

One searcher detached from the customs house and began working the line toward him. A woman, pearl-diver's build, sun-cured, moving with the economy of someone who measured everything — he would learn that about her soon enough, though he didn't know it yet. She was looking at hands and bags.

Edmund adjusted his duffel on his shoulder, arranged his face into the mild boredom of a coastal traveler, and walked forward to meet the net.
