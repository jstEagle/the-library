## 5. The Compass Without a Needle

The customs house smelled of tar, wet rope, and the particular staleness of paper that had been handled by too many sweating hands. By seven bells I had searched eleven passengers, and by seven bells I had learned again what I always learned: that people carry their lies in their faces before they ever pack them in luggage.

Mr. Hoskins, the senior inspector, stood at the far end of the long bench with a ledger open on his knee, recording seizures in a hand so small it looked like fly tracks. The new orders had come down at dawn — all incoming correspondence to be confiscated pending inspection, all passengers questioned as to purpose of visit, all parcels weighed twice. Nobody said why. The company never said why. It only said *do*, and we did, because the alternative to doing was the gate and the road and no wage at all.

"Thornbury." Hoskins didn't look up. "Packet boat from the southward is tying off now. You take the single gentlemen. Hoskins says you've a light touch."

It was the closest he came to praise.

I took my position at the bench end nearest the door and arranged my instruments the way the manual prescribed: the scale, the probe, the long thin blade for splitting boot heels, the stamp for sealing anything seized. And beneath the bench, where no manual could see, my own small inventory. A knife with a horn handle. A compass case without its needle, brass worn to gold at the rim where a thumb had rubbed it for years. My mother's silence, which weighs nothing and cannot be set on any scale, and which I carry anyway, every day, into every room.

That is how I measure the world. Not in coin — coin comes and goes through fingers like water through a net — but in what can be carried. A woman who has spent her life under indenture learns early that everything she loves will one day be itemized, appraised, or confiscated, so she learns to love things that fit in a pocket.

The packet boat's passengers came up the stairs in ones and twos, salt-stiffened and squinting, and I worked them the way I work shell off a bedrock ledge: efficiently, without cruelty, without mercy. A cloth merchant with sample books full of nothing. A widow with a Bible and a pistol, both declared honestly, which made her rare. Two clerks whose trunks I barely opened because clerks traveling together always talk too much about what isn't in their trunks.

Then came the man in the salt-damaged boots.

He was tall the way driftwood is tall — weathered down rather than grown, all his softness taken by something. His coat had been good once and had been made to stay good through patching that respected the original cut. He carried one bag, canvas, soft-sided, and he held it wrong. Not carelessly. Carefully. The way a man holds a bag when the bag matters more than the coat, more than the boots, more perhaps than the man.

"Purpose of visit?" I asked.

"Visiting," he said. "Family."

His voice didn't rise at the lie. That was the first thing. Most men, lying, let the sentence fall away at the end like something they want dropped quickly. His stayed level, held out flat, presented for inspection like a coin he'd practiced polishing.

"Name?"

"Roe. Edmund Roe."

I opened the canvas bag. Clothing, folded with a seaman's economy. A razor. A tin of tea. A bundle of letters tied in oilcloth — and here I should have stopped, because the orders of that morning were explicit, *all incoming correspondence*, and here was correspondence wrapped against salt water like something worth more than the boat that carried it.

But I was watching his face instead of my hands.

There are two kinds of calm. There is the calm of a man who has nothing to hide, which is loose and slightly bored, and there is the calm of a man who has rehearsed having nothing to hide, which sits on him like a coat cut for someone else. I know the difference the way I know the difference between live shell and dead shell underwater — not by looking, exactly, but by some weight in the thing itself. Dead shell sits wrong in the hand. So did this man's calm.

"You can check the letters," he said. "Nothing in them but family business."

"Everything's family business," I said. "That's what the company says."

Something moved behind his eyes then — not fear. Recalculation. He was measuring me the way I measured him, and finding, perhaps, that the arithmetic was less simple than he'd hoped.

I untied the oilcloth. Inside were letters, perhaps fifteen, bound with cord, addressed in different hands to different names in the port — and every one of them written in the same hand, a hand I did not know and yet knew, a slanted, urgent, beautiful script that pulled at something at the back of my memory like a hook finding flesh. I did not read them. Reading them would have been procedure. Instead I weighed them in my palm, and they were heavy, heavier than paper, the way a thing is heavy when it carries more than its substance.

"Correspondence is subject to seizure under the new orders," I said. "Pending inspection."

"I know," he said quietly. "I read the notice posted at the pier."

He knew. He'd walked up those stairs knowing, and he had come anyway, and he had rehearsed a calm to do it with. A fool would have hidden the letters badly. A desperate man would have hidden them well. This man had not hidden them at all — he had *presented* them, wrapped and honest, because he had judged — correctly, as it happened — that the inspection would be performed by someone who might choose what she saw.

Hoskins was watching from the far bench. The foreman had been walking the floor all morning with the reward money on his breath, saying *vigilance* the way preachers say *salvation*. Fifteen pounds for information leading to seizure of contraband correspondence. Fifteen pounds was two seasons of my debt payments. Fifteen pounds was most of the way to free.

I retied the oilcloth. Slowly. Making the knot look finished.

"Personal effects, no contraband," I said, loud enough to carry. "Pass."

He looked at me for one second longer than gratitude requires. In that second was a question, and an offer, and — this was the part that unsettled me — an acknowledgment. He had seen me see him. Whatever game we were now playing, we had both agreed to play it in the same half-second, without a word, the way two divers signal each other underwater.

Then he picked up his bag and went out through the archway into the morning, and I stamped the next passenger's form, and lied to myself about what I was going to do next.

* * *

I told the foreman I needed the privy. I told my feet nothing at all; they already knew.

He had not gone to the lodging houses. A man with rehearsed calm does not go where the rehearsed go. I found him at the end of the fish quay, sitting on a bollard with the canvas bag between his knees, watching the harbor the way a man watches a thing he is planning to betray or be betrayed by. The tide was turning. The moored fleet rode at their moorings beyond the mole, masts thick as reeds in a marsh, and beyond them the open sea lay flat and silver and indifferent.

"You followed me," he said, without turning.

"You walked slowly."

"That too." He turned then, and up close his face was older than his body, seamed around the eyes with squinting into weather. "Why?"

It was a fair question and I gave it an unfair answer. "You carried your bag wrong."

"Most people carry bags wrong, then."

"Most people have nothing in the bag worth carrying right."

He almost smiled. It was a good smile, wasted on caution. "Edmund Roe," he said again, offering the name like a card he knew I'd already marked.

"Tamsin Thornbury." I sat on the next bollard, at a distance that said conversation and a closeness that said I could reach the knife in my belt if the conversation went poorly. "Inspector, third class, customs house. Which means I know every name on every manifest that touches this harbor, and yours wasn't on the packet list."

"No," he agreed. "It wouldn't be."

"Roe isn't a southern name."

"It's nobody's name. That's the point of it." He looked back at the water. "You read the posted orders. All correspondence seized pending inspection. Do you know what they're looking for? Did anyone tell you?"

"The order doesn't say."

"It wouldn't." He was quiet a moment. On the quay below us, fishermen spilled the morning's catch from baskets, silver flooding across planks, and gulls screamed over it like a debt being argued. "What would you say if I told you I'm carrying letters from a dead man?"

I felt the compass case against my ribs before I felt anything else — that old reflex, my hand wanting the comfort of the worn brass. "The port's full of dead men's letters. Grief writes a lot of post."

"Not grief." He shook his head slowly. "Handwriting. Letters in the hand of a man two years drowned, arriving fresh, month after month, dated after his death. What would you call that, Miss Thornbury? In your professional capacity?"

I should have stood up then. I should have gone back to the customs house, reported the encounter, collected my fifteen pounds, paid down my debt, and continued along the groove my life had been cut into, deepening it year by year until it was a grave with better furniture.

Instead I said: "I'd call it a question I don't get paid enough to ask."

"And if the answer was worth more than fifteen pounds?"

"Then it's worth more than I am." I stood, brushing off my skirt with more force than the dust deserved. "I let you through that checkpoint, Mr. Roe, or whoever you are, and that was the single most expensive act of charity of my life, and I don't fully know why I did it. Don't make me regret it by talking to me in riddles on a public quay. If you have business in this port, conduct it quietly. If you have trouble—" I looked at the fleet, at the masts, at the whole gray apparatus of the company hanging over the harbor like weather, "—you should know this town manufactures trouble faster than it manufactures anything else. And it manufactures a great deal."

"Your mother," he said.

I stopped.

"She was born Marren. Saltwater Marrens, out of the diving families on the east shore." He said it carefully, watching me, and I understood that he had known my name before I ever stamped his form, that the packet manifest had not been his only reading. "Did she ever have a compass? Brass case, hinged lid, made in London?"

The world narrowed to the size of the thing against my ribs. "Who are you?" I said, and my voice came out lower than I intended, and harder. "Not Roe. Who sent you?"

"A friend," he said. "Of a man who owed your family a debt. Who told me, if I reached this port, to look for a diver's daughter with steady hands, because steady hands would be the only kind that could be trusted with what I'm carrying." He rose off the bollard, and standing he was taller than I'd calculated, though I calculate well. "I'm not asking you to believe me today. I'm asking you to remember that I asked. When the net closes — and it's closing, Miss Thornbury, faster than you think — remember there was one man on that packet boat who told you the truth before you had reason to hear it."

"The letters," I said. "In the oilcloth. They weren't addressed to any of those names, were they. The names were a decoy. Whose hand is it? Whose letters?"

He shouldered his bag. For a moment I thought he would actually answer — his mouth opened on it, some word gathered behind his teeth that had been waiting years for daylight —

And then the harbor bell rang twice, the foreman's signal, and somewhere behind us on the quay road came the sound I had heard all my life and never once thought to fear: boots. Company boots. Many of them, in step, moving toward the fish quay with a purpose that had nothing to do with fish.

Edmund Roe — or whoever he was — melted back against the bollard and became, in the space of a breath, a tired traveler resting, and I understood that this too was rehearsed, and that whatever storm was coming into this harbor, it had started arriving ahead of him.

Mr. Calloway himself was coming down the quay road. And he was asking, I would learn within the hour, after a single passenger. A man asking questions about Captain Aldous Vane.

A man who had been drowned two years.
