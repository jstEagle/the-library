## 6. What the Company Owes

The factor's office smelled of ink and camphor, and Mr. Silas Calloway believed both were the smell of order.

He sat behind a desk of blackened teak that had come out on the first company ship twenty years before him and would remain long after, and he arranged his morning the way he arranged everything: correspondence in one stack, requisitions in another, and in a lacquered tray at his right hand the small items that required thought. This morning the tray held three things. A bill of lading for shell bound for the quarterly fleet. A report from the harbor watch listing the packet boats arrived since dawn. And a single sheet of paper, brought to him by the customs house night clerk, on which was recorded — in the clerk's careful commercial hand — a conversation overheard at the pier.

The packet passenger had asked the boatman, before his boots had even touched the planking, whether Captain Aldous Vane still kept lodgings in the port.

Calloway read the line four times. His face did not change while he did it, because he had trained his face as carefully as a merchant trains a ledger: every entry balanced, nothing written twice, no column left open where a stranger might read an unfinished sum.

*Aldous Vane.* Drowned. Certified drowned, in fact, which was a stronger thing than drowned, because a certificate bore the company seal and the seal bore Calloway's own countersignature. Two years gone. The man's effects distributed, his debts transferred against his estate and the estate absorbed, his name struck from the active rolls with the tidy finality of a pencil line ruled through in red.

And now a passenger with salt-damaged boots came asking after him before his feet were dry.

Calloway set the paper down and looked out the window at the harbor. Morning light lay flat on the water, the color of old pewter, and the masts of the anchored fleet stood in their hundreds like a forest of grey needles. Somewhere among the sheds and staging yards and diving platforms, six hundred souls under indenture were waking to the company's bell, and not one of them knew that a dead man's name had just walked off a packet boat into the sunlight.

He did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what incompetent men invoked when they had failed to trace cause. A stranger did not ask about Aldous Vane by accident; names rose to the surface only when something below was pushing them up.

There were possibilities, and he ranked them as he sipped his coffee, which was bitter and thick and taken without sugar because sweetness was for men who needed comforting. Possibility one: the passenger was a creditor's agent, some London firm finally auditing its losses, sniffing at the voided estates. Possible, but creditors wrote letters first and traveled second; they were accountants, and accountants announced themselves. Possibility two: the passenger was a relative, some cousin come to grieve late and claim late. Also possible, also manageable — grief could be compensated, claims could be delayed in chancery until the claimant died of old age. But possibility three sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Possibility three was that Vane had sent word out. That somewhere beyond the horizon, in whatever silence he had vanished into, the captain had spent two years writing letters the way other men spent bullets, and had now found a hand willing to carry them in.

Calloway set down his cup.

He was not a cruel man, whatever the divers' children said about him in their sing-song rhymes. Cruelty was expenditure without return, and Silas Calloway wasted nothing. He had spent thirty years learning the one great truth of the coast: that the trade did not run on pearls but on obligation. Every diver bound by debt, every clerk bound by wage, every officer bound by the quiet understanding that the company's records could ruin any man whose name appeared in them unfavorably. The whole port floated on what people owed. It was a beautiful machine, and it worked because every gear inside it was slightly afraid.

A man asking questions about Vane was a gear turning wrong.

He pulled a fresh sheet toward himself and wrote with the economy of long habit. To the sergeant of the customs house: double the watch at the passenger search, effective immediately, expenses to the factor's discretionary account. Every incoming soul to be examined not only for contraband goods but for *correspondence* — letters carried loose, letters sewn into linings, letters oiled and wrapped, for he knew every trick the smugglers taught and had invented two of them himself. To the toll bridge over the estuary: reinforcement of eight men, and instruction that any traveler giving Vane's name, or asking after company shipping records, or bearing documents in a hand that matched the enclosed specimen, be detained quietly — *quietly* underlined twice — and held for his personal review. To the fleet gate: the same, with the added note that the quarterly loading would proceed on schedule and no irregularity, however small, was to be reported upward except through him.

That last clause was the important one. Information, like pearl shell, was worth most before the market knew it existed.

He sealed the three letters with wax and his signet — a compass rose, an irony he had chosen young and never bothered to explain — and rang for the runner.

Then he sat back, and allowed himself the day's second cup of coffee, and thought about Aldous Vane as he had not permitted himself to think in two years. The man had been the best navigator on the coast, which had made him useful, and the most scrupulous about the crew's accounts, which had made him dangerous. Vane had been the sort of officer who checked the death ledgers against the pay rolls and noticed when the numbers disagreed — noticed that men who died owed the company less than men who lived, and that the company profited handsomely from every certified drowning. He had said as much once, at supper, in that mild voice of his, the way a man mentions weather. *A ledger is a confession written in advance,* he'd said, and Calloway had laughed, and within the month begun arranging the circumstances under which the confession would never be read.

It had been clean. There had been no body, but there rarely was, on this coast; the water took what it took and gave back almost nothing, which was why it made such an accommodating accomplice. The certificate, the seal, the estate proceedings, the widow's settlement — all clean. Except that the widow had refused the settlement. Except that the widow had gone silent instead, and silence, Calloway knew, was not always consent. Sometimes silence was patience.

Now a stranger came asking.

He finished the coffee. Outside, a line of shell barges crossed the harbor mouth, low in the water, fat with the season's take, and Calloway watched them with the satisfaction of a man watching his own bloodstream function. Whatever came up the estuary toward him, he had doubled the walls between it and the fleet. Let the stranger run the gauntlet. Checkpoints were not merely barriers; they were sieves, and everything that passed through them passed through *his* fingers first.

There remained one more item of business, and it was the delicate one, and he saved delicate things for last the way other men saved dessert.

* * *

Tamsin Thornbury was summoned to the factor's office at the fourth bell, which every diver on the platform understood to be either very good news or none at all, because the company never called you in early to tell you anything middling.

She scrubbed the shell-grime from her hands at the cistern until her knuckles burned, braided her wet hair with the quick economy of a woman who had done it ten thousand times, and put on her shore shirt — the good one, the one without the tear at the shoulder — because if the company meant to dock her pay it preferred to do it to a person who looked respectable. She had learned that much theater early. Appearances cost nothing and were therefore the only luxury she permitted herself.

The walk across the staging yard took eleven minutes and she spent them cataloguing, as she catalogued everything. Her knife: on her belt, legal. Her broken compass: in her shirt, wrapped in cloth, invisible. Her debts: uncounted by choice, because an exact number was a weight and she carried enough weight underwater. The summons itself: unexplained, therefore expensive. Nothing the company did was free. If Calloway fed her, the meal would appear on her ledger. If he praised her, the praise would be deducted somehow, in hours or hazard pay or the quiet arithmetic by which gratitude became obligation.

The clerk at the outer office made her wait nineteen minutes, which was also theater — the company liked you to understand that its time was worth more than yours — and then waved her through with a jerk of the pen.

Mr. Calloway's office was exactly as she remembered it from her indenture signing seven years ago: dark, orderly, smelling of camphor, dominated by a man who did not rise when she entered. He gestured to the chair opposite his desk, and Tamsin sat the way she dove — controlled descent, no splash.

"Miss Thornbury." He said her name as though reading it off a manifest. "You've been with us seven years."

"Seven years in October, sir."

"And your record." He turned a page on his desk without appearing to read it. "Quota met or exceeded in five of seven seasons. Zero disciplinary entries. Zero. Do you know how rare that is? Six hundred souls on this station and perhaps a dozen can say the same."

"The water doesn't leave room for trouble, sir."

"No," Calloway said. "It doesn't. Most people find room anyway."

He studied her then, openly, the way he probably studied cargo manifests, and Tamsin had been underwater often enough to know what it felt like when something large regarded her from the blue with intent. She kept her face the way she kept her breath: measured, spent slowly, showing nothing.

"You searched passengers yesterday," he said. "At the customs house. Under the new standing orders."

"I did, sir."

"How many came through your table?"

"Forty-one. Forty if we don't count the woman with the prayer book the sergeant already cleared."

"And of those forty-one, how many would you say were hiding something?"

The question had a shape, and Tamsin recognized the shape. It was the shape of a net being drawn. Someone had come through her table whom the factor cared about, and he wanted to know what she had seen, and he was approaching the way the company approached everything — sideways, through procedure, so that the trap wore the uniform of routine.

"Everyone hides something small, sir," she said. "Tobacco. Letters from home over the declared weight. A girl hiding a love token she doesn't want her father counting. I don't trouble the small things. My orders are contraband and undeclared specie."

"Small things become large things," Calloway said mildly. "A letter is a small thing. Until it isn't."

"Yes, sir."

"One of your forty-one." He folded his hands on the teak. "A man off the morning packet. Coastal passage, third class. Tall, foreign manner, boots ruined by salt. You examined him?"

Her heartbeat did not quicken, because she had trained it not to, in water colder and darker than this office. *Edmund.* She saw him again in memory: the rehearsed calm, the hands too still, the single canvas bag with nothing in it that weighed what a bag should weigh. She had let him pass. She had followed him. She had spoken with him in the shade of the fish sheds and told herself it was curiosity, and she was still telling herself that, and the telling was getting harder.

"I remember him," she said. "He declared a bag, personal effects, no specie. Books, linen, a shaving kit. Clean."

"You found nothing."

"Nothing, sir."

"Nothing hidden? No papers? Nothing sewn, nothing wrapped?"

"Nothing I found," Tamsin said, and each word was a stone laid carefully in a wall.

Something moved behind Calloway's eyes — she would remember later that it might have been disappointment, or might have been satisfaction, and that she would never know which — and he leaned back in his chair, and the whole temperature of the room changed.

"Here is my problem, Miss Thornbury," he said. "This station handles a quarter million pounds of shell a year. Its books, its estates, its deaths and births and debts — all of it passes across perhaps ten desks, of which mine is the last. A concern of that size attracts vermin. Smugglers, claim-jumpers, agents of rival houses, men carrying paper that can disturb the trade." He tapped the desk once, softly. "I need people I can trust at the checkpoints. Not hired sergeants — sergeants can be bought, and worse, bought cheaply. I need someone whose interest is bound to the company's interest. Someone with a future here."

He opened a drawer and slid a document across the teak, and Tamsin looked down at it and felt the floor tilt gently, the way the platform tilted when a swell came through.

It was a promotion of appointment. Inspector of Correspondence, customs house, with authority over the passenger searches she had been performing as drudgery. Wages nearly tripled. A shore billet — no more diving, no more cold and pressure and rapture of the deep waiting to fold her lungs like paper. And beneath the wages, in the clause her eyes went to first, because debt always drew her eye first:

*Applied against outstanding obligations of the named party and her surety.*

Her mother's debt. The number that had hung over their lives like weather, inflated every year by fees she'd never authorized, growing in the dark like something in a cave. This document promised to grind it down. Not erase it — the company never erased — but grind it, year by year, toward zero.

"It's a good offer," Calloway said, watching her read. He said it without vanity, which was somehow worse. "I want you to understand that I know it's a good offer. Women don't rise on this coast, Miss Thornbury. They dive, they pack, they marry, they owe. I am offering you a door that does not exist anywhere else in this port, and I am offering it because you have the one quality I cannot train." He paused. "You notice things. And you don't spend what you notice cheaply. I watched you yesterday. You searched forty-one people like a surgeon, and you reported nothing, because there was nothing worth reporting — but if there had been, you'd have known its weight before you touched it."

*Tam.* Her mother's voice, from years ago, warning her in the kitchen: *When the company gives you something, count your fingers afterward.*

"This position carries conditions," Calloway continued. "Loyalty, plainly stated, because I don't insult people with euphemism. The station has recently attracted attention — a passenger making inquiries he shouldn't, asking after persons and records that are closed. If such a man comes through your table again, or if you hear his name spoken in the yards, or if anyone at all asks you about Captain Vane—"

The name landed in the room like dropped glass.

Tamsin did not move. Below the surface of her face, everything moved — the fish sheds, Edmund's too-quiet hands, the letters she had not seen but knew existed, the word *Vane* spoken by a stranger with salt-damaged boots — and above the surface, nothing. Seven years of practice. Controlled descent. No splash.

"—you will bring it to me," Calloway finished, "directly, and personally. Before anyone else. Not the sergeant, not the clerk. Me."

"And if I do, sir?"

"Then in three years your mother's ledger shows a balance of nothing, and you hold rank no woman on this coast has ever held." He said it the way a clergyman says grace — sincerely, and as though sincerity settled the matter. "I am not a monster, Miss Thornbury. I am offering you the chance to stop drowning for a living. Most people never get offered anything. I have watched this port my whole life, and do you know what I've learned? People don't betray each other for money. They betray each other for *exits*. I am offering you an exit. All it costs is that you tell me what enters."

He slid the pen across the desk.

Tamsin looked at it. Then at the clause about her mother's debt. Then at Calloway's face, which was patient and reasonable and entirely certain that she would sign, because everyone signed, because the machine ran on obligation and she was a gear like all the others.

"The new inspector assumes duties when?" she asked.

"Immediately upon signature."

"Then I'll need until tomorrow's tide to settle my kit and inform my foreman properly," she said. "An appointment like this should be done properly."

A flicker — approval? suspicion? — crossed his face, and settled into a nod. "Properly. Yes. I respect that." He withdrew the pen, blotted the unsigned document, and placed it in its drawer with the care of a man sheathing a weapon he was confident of drawing again. "Tomorrow's tide, Miss Thornbury. The offer keeps until then. Very little else on this coast does."

She rose, bowed her head the correct fraction, and walked out through the outer office past the clerk's incurious pen, across the yard, past the cistern, past the drying racks hung with shell like rows of pale ears listening to the sea.

Only at the gate, in the full wind off the water, did she let herself breathe fully, one long diver's breath, and think:

*He knows a stranger came asking about Vane. He doesn't know the stranger spoke to me. He wants me as his sieve. And I have until tomorrow's tide to decide which side of the net I'm standing on.*

She put her hand flat against her shirt, over the cloth-wrapped compass with its hollow where a needle should be, and felt the small useless weight of it like a promise missing its iron.

Then she turned toward the fish sheds, walking fast, before the afternoon watch could mark her direction.
