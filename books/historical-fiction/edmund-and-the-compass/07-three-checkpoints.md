## 7. Three Checkpoints

Edmund Roe had learned his geography in the worst school afloat: a Greenland whaler where a man's survival depended on knowing exactly how far he could run before the ice closed behind him. He applied the same arithmetic now, standing at the window of a rented room above a chandler's shop, watching the port of Merrow's Landing assemble itself out of the morning fog.

The town was built like a funnel. Everything sloped toward the water — the streets, the drainage, the money, the law. And everything that came into the funnel or tried to leave it had to pass through one of three mouths.

He counted them again, because counting was what he did instead of being afraid.

* * *

The first mouth was the customs house, and he had already been through it once, which meant it would never be so easy again.

It sat at the head of the quay like a fat man at a doorway, all pale stone and green shutters, with a covered porch where passengers from the coastal packets were made to stand while their luggage was opened and their persons patted down by men whose wages came, ultimately, from the same ledger as everyone else's in this town. The Merrow Trading Company did not own the customs house on paper. On paper it belonged to the Crown, presided over by a collector named Hobb who wore an actual sword and spoke of the King's revenue as though the King might someday ask him about it personally.

But Edmund had spent two days learning the shape of things, and the shape was simple. Hobb's salary was paid from customs duties. Customs duties depended on trade. Trade was the company. A man could serve the King or he could serve his family, and in Merrow's Landing those had long ago been reconciled into a single household.

What made the customs house dangerous was not its guards. It was its *method*. The other checkpoints would be looking for a man; the customs house was looking for a thing. Letters. Papers. Contraband correspondence, the new orders said — and Edmund had read those orders himself, pasted to the packet office door in fresh ink, which meant someone upstream knew what was coming. Someone had sent word ahead. Vane's letters had been moving for months through channels Vane trusted, and somewhere along that chain a link had bent or broken, and the news of it had traveled faster than the letters themselves.

So the customs house would open every satchel. It would run fingers along coat linings. It had, Edmund noted, a small iron stove in the corner of the inspection room, and he thought about what a stove was for when the contraband in question was paper, and he decided he did not want to think about it anymore.

Could it be bribed? Hobb himself, perhaps not; men who believed in their own offices were the hardest kind. But the searchers were another matter. There were six of them on the day shift, and they worked in pairs, and pairs could be split — one honest, one hungry, and the honest one outranked or outnumbered at the crucial moment. It could be done. It would cost more than he had.

Could it be survived without bribery? Only by becoming invisible, and he had already failed at that. He had walked off the packet boat three days ago wearing rehearsed calm like a borrowed coat, and someone had seen through the seams. He didn't know who. That was the worst of it — not knowing whether the eyes that had marked him belonged to a searcher, a porter, or a woman with diver's shoulders and a gaze that took inventory of a man the way other people counted change.

* * *

The second mouth was the toll bridge, and the toll bridge frightened him more than the customs house did, though he would have struggled to say why until he stood under it.

It crossed the estuary a half-mile inland from the harbor, where the river narrowed between mudflats before spreading into the sea. Timber pilings black with weed, a deck of planks worn concave by forty years of cartwheels, and at the near end a toll house squatting under its slate roof like a toad. The road beyond it ran south and east — the only land route out of the port that didn't require a boat. Every cart of shell, every cask of oil, every traveler with anywhere to be went over those planks and past that toll house.

The bridge belonged to no one and everyone, which is to say it belonged to whoever collected its tolls, and the collection rights had been leased — Edmund confirmed this from a garrulous ferryman — to a man named Quill, who held the lease from the company and sublet the guard work to four brothers of dubious temper and one sergeant of the colonial constabulary who drew pay from both sides and kept meticulous accounts of the hypocrisy.

Different master than the customs house. That mattered. A conspiracy of checkpoints fails when the checkpoints don't talk to each other, and Quill's bridge and Hobb's customs house communicated the way rival parishes do — with suspicion and delayed letters. What passed one might not pass the other. What was flagged at one might arrive at the other as rumor rather than order.

But there was the flaw in his comfort, and Edmund turned it over and over like a stone with something underneath: the bridge was narrow. Not metaphorically. Physically. Eighteen feet of planking over six feet of brown water, with railings you could see through and nothing alongside but mudflats that would hold a man to his knees. At the customs house there were crowds, crates, confusion — places to be someone else. On the bridge there was only the bridge. You stepped onto it as yourself or you did not step onto it at all.

Could it be bribed? The brothers, certainly; they had the eyes of men who gambled. The sergeant was the question mark. A double-paid man was loyal to neither payer and therefore loyal only to the moment, which made him either the easiest purchase in town or the most expensive.

Could it be survived? He looked at the water and thought: perhaps. The estuary ran deep enough in the channel under the bridge. A strong swimmer, going in quiet, coming up on the far bank among the reeds — but the water was cold even in this season, cold enough to cramp, and the current in the channel ran hard on the ebb, and a man surfacing downstream of the pilings would be visible to anyone who happened to be looking, and guards who collect tolls all day have nothing to do but look.

He wrote none of this down. He carried it in his head, arranged and rearranged it at night like a man stacking cargo that must balance or the ship founders.

* * *

The third mouth was the fleet gate, and the fleet gate was not a checkpoint at all. It was a verdict.

It stood at the seaward end of the loading quay — a fence of tarred palings with a gate wide enough for two carts abreast, flanked by a watch shed and, beyond it, the great timber cranes that leaned over the quarterly ships like patient herons. When the fleet was in — and it was in now, riding high and empty at their moorings, waiting to swallow the season's shell and pearl and oil — the gate became the single throat through which the entire quarter's wealth passed into the holds. Company men manned it. Not constables, not leased guards: company men, in company blue, answering to the company's own superintendent of cargoes, and above him, if rumor held, to the factor himself, Mr. Calloway, whose name Edmund had heard spoken in this town the way farmers speak of weather — with respect, resignation, and no expectation of mercy.

No bribes here. Or none worth the attempt. These men were not wage-earners who might be tempted; they were believers, or the next thing to it — men whose advancement depended on the very ledgers Vane's letters would burn. You could not buy the arsonist's own fire brigade.

And the timing was a wall as well as a gate. The fleet sailed on the first spring tide of the month — eleven days hence, the chandler said, maybe ten. After it sailed, the port emptied of consequence. Whatever the letters proved, they would prove it to a town of clerks and divers while the men they accused rode the horizon in company hulls, untouchable, and the colony's attention scattered like gulls after a fish cart. Evidence delivered late was evidence delivered never.

So: the letters had to go *out* — out of the port, out of Calloway's reach, aboard some vessel bound for a place where a magistrate existed who was not on the payroll, or failing that, into enough hands at once that burying them became impossible. And the only ships leaving were company ships, through the company's gate, past the company's men.

Unless a smaller boat ran the coast at night. Unless a smuggler's hull slipped the harbor after dark and hugged the shoreline north. Edmund turned this over too. Smuggling was a trade like any other; it had its tradesmen. But tradesmen could be bought by the other side just as easily, and Calloway's coin was deeper than anything Edmund carried, which was a canvas satchel, a dead friend's correspondence, and eleven pounds seven shillings.

* * *

He spent the afternoon walking the route.

Not the whole route — a man who walks his whole escape route twice is a man asking to be remembered — but pieces of it, in different clothes, at different paces, the way a thief cases a house by visiting the neighborhood as a neighbor. He bought rope at one chandler and twine at another. He stood at the bridge toll house long enough to learn the brothers' faces and the rhythm of their searches: carts got a poke and a glance, foot travelers got a pat and a wave-through unless they looked poor, in which case they got everything they owned dumped on the planks. Poverty was probable cause in Merrow's Landing. He filed that away.

At the customs house he watched the stove smoke and thought about linings and oilcloth and how much paper a man could carry against his body before his body betrayed the carrying. Quite a lot, if the paper was cut small. But cut small, the letters lost their seals, and unsealed fragments were fragments — deniable, dismissible, worthless before any magistrate who wanted them to be worthless. The letters' power lived in their wholeness. Their signatures, their dates, their terrible continuity. One document was an accusation. Forty documents, dated across two years, each corroborating the last, was a *record*, and records were the one thing men like Calloway genuinely feared, because records could not be stared down.

Which meant the letters must travel whole, and whole meant bulky, and bulky meant a crate or a bale or a barrel, and a crate at a checkpoint invited hands inside it.

He stood on the quay as the light went long and copper, and he let himself, for exactly sixty seconds, feel what he was actually feeling.

Fear. Not the clean fear of a storm — storms were honest, storms told you what they intended. This was the other kind, the low chronic hum of a man alone in a town built to catch him, carrying the last testament of a friend who had gone silent eight weeks ago and might, by now, be anything from imprisoned to fed to the fishes. Aldous Vane had trusted him with paper because paper was what Edmund was good at — reading it, forging it, understanding what it could do. Vane used to say Roe could argue a gull off a fish. But arguments required a listener, and every listener in this port was paid not to hear.

Sixty seconds. Then he folded the fear up small and put it away where he put everything, and did what he always did with an impossible problem: he broke it into pieces until the pieces were merely difficult.

Three checkpoints. Three masters. Three different kinds of lock.

The customs house searched for paper and answered to the Crown-that-wasn't. Survivable by misdirection — make them find something else to find, or better, make them search a man who has nothing, loudly, while the letters walk past in the shadow of authority itself. He didn't yet know what form that shadow would take. He only knew that checkpoints were staffed by men, and men obeyed their own rules harder than they obeyed their masters, and a rule followed confidently was a door opening.

The toll bridge searched for people and answered to greed. Greed was a lock with a known key. The problem was not getting past it; the problem was that a narrow bridge allowed no mistakes, and he had already made one mistake in this town by giving his name too freely to a lodging-house keeper, and names traveled.

The fleet gate searched for profit and answered to the company. It could not be bribed, fooled, or survived. Therefore it must not be faced. The letters would leave this port some other way or not at all — and *some other way*, in a harbor this well-watched, meant a boat that already knew how to be invisible, and a price, and a seller whose loyalty lasted exactly as long as the money did.

He turned back toward the chandler's shop as the lamps were lit, and stopped.

Because the woman was there. The diver — he was nearly certain she was a diver, from the way she stood, weight forward, like someone perpetually about to go under — was at the packet office door across the lane, reading the new customs orders by lamplight. Reading them the way he'd seen magistrates read warrants: thoroughly, unhurried, missing nothing. She had been at the customs house the morning he landed. She had watched him with a face that gave away less than his rehearsed calm ever had. And now she was reading the very orders that had been posted to catch him, and she was reading them *carefully*, and Edmund Roe, who prided himself on reading men, realized he had no idea whether she was the net or simply another fish considering the size of the mesh.

She looked up. Across the lane, across the lamplight, their eyes met for the length of one held breath.

Then she turned and walked away toward the water, and Edmund understood that whatever plan he made tonight, it had just acquired a variable he could not price, could not bribe, and could not stop thinking about.
