## 8. An Indenture Is a Chain

The contract lived in a tin box beneath her mother's bed, and Tamsin had not opened the box in eleven years.

She told herself, crouching there in the dark of the room with the candle guttering, that she had not opened it because she did not need to. She knew what she owed the way a diver knows the weight of her belts — by feel, by habit, by the daily proof of it on her shoulders. The number changed; the weight did not. What was the use of reading arithmetic you could not argue with?

But she had watched a stranger walk off the tide today with calm like armor plate, and she had let him pass when every order posted on the customs house wall said she should not have, and now her hands wanted something to hold that was heavier than a knife.

The tin was cold. It came up trailing a smell of salt rot and lamp oil, and the hinge shrieked so loudly her mother stirred in the next room, murmured, went still again.

Inside: her mother's marriage lines, yellowed soft as cloth. A lock of hair tied with thread gone brown. And the contract — three pages of company paper, dense as coral, folded twice, with her own name at the bottom in ink she barely remembered signing. She had been fourteen. Her father was three weeks drowned. Her mother's hand had guided hers, and she had signed because signing was a kind of breathing, that year. You did it without thinking about it or you stopped.

Tamsin sat back on her heels on the floorboards and read the thing from the top for the first time in her life.

*Thornbury, Tamsin E., aged 14 years, bound to the Meridian Pearl & Trading Company for a term of seven years, said term renewable upon assessment of outstanding account...*

She had known that part. Everyone knew that part. Seven years, renewable. What no one ever said plainly — what was never said at all, only gestured at over ledgers and tally books — was what *renewable upon assessment* meant. So she read on, lips moving slightly, into the country of small print where companies keep their real intentions.

*It is further agreed that the party of the first part shall be held in service until such time as the sum owing under this indenture, together with all charges, advances, equipment levies, medical fees, interest accrued and compounding at the rate of one shilling in the pound per annum, shall be discharged in full.*

She read it again. Then a third time, because the words kept sliding off her mind like oil off wet shell.

*Until such time as the sum owing shall be discharged in full.*

There was no end date. There had never been an end date. The seven years were a fiction, a doorway painted on a wall. The true term of her bondage was *the debt*, and the debt was calculated by the company, charged by the company, audited by the company, and increased by the company — one shilling in the pound, every year, forever, on a principal she had never been shown and could not contest. She could dive until her lungs gave out and the number would grow faster than she could fill it. It was not a chain made of iron. It was a chain made of *arithmetic*, which was worse, because iron can be seen and struck and broken, and arithmetic just sits there being correct.

Tamsin set the candle down on the floorboards very carefully, because her hand had begun to shake, and she did not want to burn the house down before she understood the shape of the thing.

She did sums. She was good at sums; a diver who cannot reckon her catch against her charges starves. Fourteen seasons old when she signed, twenty-five now. Eleven years at the beds, four thousand hours at least below the water, shell enough to fill a warehouse — and every year the factor's clerk had come to the compound with his ledger and read out the figure, and every year the figure had been larger than the year before. Larger. Not smaller. She had assumed — God help her, she had *assumed*, the way you assume the tide will turn — that the number would eventually come down, that the work would eventually touch bottom and begin to rise. That there was a far side to it.

There was no far side. Interest compounded on a principal that included, she saw now, scanning the charge columns appended to the third page: her father's funeral. Her mother's medicine the winter of the fever. The replacement of two knives, one mask, three diving dresses she had never worn — the company leased the good ones back to the divers and charged them for the privilege. A line item marked *educational provision* that corresponded to nothing whatsoever in her memory. And at the top, the original advance paid to her mother in the worst week of their lives: nine pounds.

Nine pounds, eleven years ago. She owed, per the last clerk's reading, sixty-one pounds and change, and the change got bigger every year like a tide coming in that nobody had ever seen go out.

The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Out past the window shutters, the harbor breathed — water on piles, the far-off clank of the night watch at the compound gate, the sound she had fallen asleep to since childhood and had always, until tonight, mistaken for the sound of home.

Her father had died in that water. Had drowned, officially, in the course of his duty, and the company had been *so sorry*, and the company had advanced them nine pounds against the hardship, and the company had put the funeral on the account. He had spent his whole working life filling their warehouse, and they had charged his widow for the box he went into the ground in, and then charged her interest on the box, and then bound his daughter to pay for the box for the rest of her natural life.

Something moved in her chest, slow and vast, like a shadow crossing the bottom of a lagoon.

Edmund's letters — whatever they were, whoever Aldous Vane had been — had seemed to her yesterday a stranger's quarrel. Company men cheating company men, paper against paper, a drama conducted at altitudes she had never breathed. Let the gentry fight over their fortunes. Her concerns were simpler: the tide, the quota, the knife's edge, the price of shell. She had thought herself outside the story.

But the man in the letters had faked drownings. That was the rumor she'd pried out of the customs house gossip like a limpet off rock — the dead man's handwriting proving the company declared men dead who weren't, voided accounts, claimed estates. Faked drownings. And she had thought: what an odd, elaborate crime. Who does that? Who needs to?

A company that already owned the arithmetic of death needed only to borrow the word. She understood it now with terrible clarity, sitting on the floor with the paper trembling in her hands. If you can make a debt immortal, you can make a debtor disposable. A living diver is a charge on the account forever — food, medicine, equipment, age. A dead diver is a closed file. The company did not fake deaths despite its system of perpetual debt. The company faked deaths *because* of it. The drowning was not the crime. The drowning was the *bookkeeping*.

Her father had been worth more to them dead. She did not know it — could not know it, not yet, not as fact — but she felt it in the structure of the pages, in the neat columns, in the tidy round hand that recorded his funeral costs with the same unhurried pen that recorded everything else. Somewhere in some office like Calloway's there was a ledger with her father's name in it, and somewhere near that name there was a sum, and somewhere near that sum there was a decision, and the decision had been made by a man who slept well afterward, who perhaps took a modest gratuity for the efficiency of it, who had certainly never once stood in open water and imagined what cold does to a body.

Anger arrived. It did not come the way she had always imagined anger came — hot, fast, breaking over her like a wave. It came the way the deep cold comes to a diver: from below, gradually, then all at once, until it is in the bones and cannot be shrugged off, until it is simply the temperature of you.

She had been angry before, of course. Angry at foremen, at short weights, at the clerk who charged her for rope wear she could prove she hadn't used. But that anger had always been weather — it blew through, and she bent, and it passed, because there was nowhere for it to live. Now it had an address. It had a clause number. It had one shilling in the pound per annum and a signature she did not remember making.

*Renewable upon assessment.* They assessed her every year like livestock and renewed her like a lease, and she had thanked them for the work. She had taken pride in the work. God help her, she still took pride in the work — in the depth, in the discipline, in the perfect economy of her body in the water — and the cruelest thing the company had ever done was make her excellent at serving it, so that even her competence belonged to them, so that the only thing she had ever truly owned was skill in the service of her own captivity.

Except. She looked at the tin box. Except the compass.

It lay at the bottom of the tin under the hair-tie, small and brass and dull, and she lifted it out and turned it in the candlelight. No needle. Just the empty pivot point, the little jeweled bearing polished bright by years of her thumb. Her mother had given it to her the day she signed — *your father's*, her mother had said, and nothing else, and had gone silent in the particular way she went silent about him, a silence Tamsin had catalogued long ago among the things she owned: a knife, a compass without its needle, her mother's silence.

She had carried it eleven years thinking it was a keepsake. A dead man's trinket. But keepsakes were for people with estates, with legacies, with things that outlasted them that were not debts. Her father had owned almost nothing. Why would he own a fine brass compass — and it was fine, she could see that now, the engraving around the bezel too delicate for a working seaman's kit — and why would it have no needle?

You don't lose a needle from a compass. They are seated, pinned, balanced. You *take* one out.

And why had the stranger on the quay — Edmund, the packet passenger with the ruined boots and the rehearsed calm, the man whose eyes had found hers across the lamplight like a hook finding flesh — why had he been carrying papers in a drowned captain's hand, and why, when he glanced at her belt that morning at the customs house, had his gaze snagged for half a heartbeat on exactly the place where she carried it?

She had thought she was following him out of curiosity. Dangerous curiosity, she'd called it, tasting the phrase like strong drink. She had told herself she wanted to know what he was carrying and whether her lapse at the checkpoint would come back on her head.

That was true. But it was not all of it. She turned the empty compass over in her fingers and admitted the rest: she had followed him because his cause, whatever it was, was the first thing in eleven years that pointed anywhere other than straight down at the pearl beds and straight up at the company flag. Because a man walking off the tide with a dead man's letters was a man who believed paper could hurt the company, and she wanted — oh, how she wanted, with a hunger that embarrassed her — to believe that anything could hurt the company.

Now she didn't have to believe it. Now it was not abstract. Now the letters, whatever names and dates and forged certificates they held, were not some gentry quarrel happening in an altitude above her head. The clause on the third page meant the war Edmund was running from the checkpoints was *her* war, had always been her war, had been fought over her body since she was fourteen years old while she thanked her captors for the employment.

The candle had burned down close to her knuckles. She pinched the wick and sat in the dark, and the harbor sounds came in through the shutter gaps, and she let the anger finish arriving, all the way down, into the marrow.

They had taught her to measure everything by what could be carried. Knife: yes. Compass: yes. Mother's silence: apparently yes, though she was beginning to suspect the silence weighed more than she knew and had been carried on her behalf, at cost, by someone else. Contract: no — the contract was the thing that carried *you*.

Well then. If the chain was arithmetic, arithmetic could be read. Read could be learned. Learned could be argued, contested, exposed — or stolen, burned, sunk past salvage. Somewhere in the factor's office there was a master ledger with her name in it, and the thought of that book, that specific object with its calfskin cover and its smug columns, rose in her chest like a need.

First, though. First there was a man with rehearsed calm and a dead friend's handwriting, and a question she had been carrying unasked for eleven years:

What happened to my father's needle?

She repacked the tin. She left the compass out, wrapped in cloth, in her coat pocket where her hand could find it. Then she sat by the window in the dark until the sky went grey, listening to the tide turn, doing sums of a different kind — not what she owed, but what she was willing to spend — and by the time the first light touched the masts in the harbor she knew exactly where Edmund Roe would go to think, because it was where anyone went in this port to think, and she knew exactly what she would say to him, and it was only one sentence long.

*My father's name was Thornbury. Tell me if it's in your letters.*

She stood, stiff-kneed, and reached for her boots. Down in the lane, early as it was, a door banged. Voices — the compound bell, rung out of season, urgent and wrong.

Quarter muster. Called at dawn.

In eleven years, the company had never once called quarter muster at dawn.
