## 10. First Checkpoint: The Customs House

*Tamsin*

The customs house smelled of tar, wet rope, and the particular staleness of men who sat indoors all day making other people's lives difficult. Tamsin had walked through its doors a hundred times in the company's livery — grey coat, brass button, the small stamped tin badge pinned over her heart that said THORNBURY, T., INSPECTOR, GRADE THREE — and every one of those times she had felt the building press down on her like deep water.

This morning it pressed differently. This morning she was using it.

She had told Edmund the rules on the walk from Brack's loft, her voice low and flat, the voice she used for instructing new divers: *Don't look at the guards. Don't look at me unless I speak first. You're a porter, you're tired, porters are always tired. If anyone asks your name, give them a name that belongs to no one.*

He had nodded once and become someone else entirely. It unsettled her how completely. The man who had stood in the loft last night with his hands shaking around a cup of tea was gone; walking beside her now was a broad-shouldered nobody with salt-damaged boots and the slumped, patient stoop of a man who had carried things for other people his whole life. Only his eyes betrayed him, and only if you knew to look — they moved too carefully, resting on each guard just long enough to catalogue him and no longer.

*Stop that*, she wanted to say. *Guards don't count guards.* But saying it would be worse than the crime.

The queue of morning passengers stretched from the inspection tables almost to the door: packet-boat travelers with their carpetbags, a merchant's wife with four trunks and a temper, two clerks arguing over a manifest. Above it all hung the new order, copied out in a fair hand and posted on the board by the door:

**BY DIRECTION OF THE HONOURABLE COMPANY — ALL INCOMING PERSONS AND EFFECTS TO BE SEARCHED FOR CONTRABAND CORRESPONDENCE. PAPER OF UNDECLARED ORIGIN TO BE SEIZED. REWARD OFFERED FOR INFORMATION LEADING TO CONVICTION.**

Someone had underlined *reward* twice. Someone, she thought, was already dreaming about what a reward could buy.

Tamsin did not join the queue. She walked past it, Edmund a half-step behind her left shoulder, and set herself at the third inspection table as though she had been standing there all morning. The table belonged to Hobbs, an old company man with gout and a fondness for the bottle; he was late, as he was most mornings, and until he arrived the table was hers by custom and by grade.

"Thornbury." Sergeant Pike looked up from the second table, squinting. He was the senior officer on the floor this shift, a thick-necked man who had once dived for shell himself before his lungs gave out, and who had never forgiven the water or anyone who could still hold her breath in it. "You're not rostered."

"I'm filling Hobbs's table till he crawls in." She said it without apology, because apology was an invitation to argument. "You want the queue backing up to the wharf when Calloway walks through? Explain that to him yourself."

Pike's jaw worked. Mentioning the factor was cheating and she knew it, but this game had only one rule worth learning: authority against authority, the company's own weight turned upon itself, so that resisting her meant defending sloth. Pike waved her off and returned to a woman's parasol, which he was examining with the intensity of a man who hoped to find a conspiracy hidden in its ribs.

Tamsin began inspecting passengers. That was the beauty of it — the terrible, elegant beauty — she did not need to pretend. She opened carpetbags and shook out shifts, ran her fingers along trunk seams, held letters up to the light and read the addresses of strangers. She was, in every visible way, exactly what her badge said she was. The letters rode eight feet away in a crate on a porter's shoulder, and no one looked twice, because no one looks twice at what a company inspector is handling for her hired man.

That had been her idea. She tried not to be proud of it, and failed.

* * *

*Edmund*

Edmund had carried crates since he was nine years old. He knew the grammar of it — how to settle the weight so it looked heavier than it was, how to breathe so the effort showed in his shoulders, how to let his face go blank the way laboring men do, their attention folded inward toward their spines. He wore the blankness now like armor, and beneath it his heart kept time like a drum in a marching song, too fast, too loud.

The crate weighed nothing that mattered. Fourteen pounds of dried shell and packing straw, and beneath the straw, wrapped in oilcloth and then in canvas and then sewn into a false lining, eleven letters in the handwriting of a drowned man. Eleven letters that had crossed two thousand miles of ocean in the pockets of sailors and priests and one dying cook, finding their way to Edmund because Aldous Vane had trusted him with the only thing Vane had left to give.

He watched Tamsin work and understood, for the first time, why Vane had spoken of her mother with something close to reverence. There was a stillness in her hands. Every bag she opened, she opened without hurry; every passenger she questioned, she questioned with a courtesy so precise it functioned as dismissal. She was turning the machinery of suspicion against itself, performing vigilance so thoroughly that no one thought to ask where her own vigilance truly pointed.

A bell-captain came down the line calling manifests. Edmund lowered his eyes and counted his breathing. Four in, six out — the old trick for steadying the hands before a fight. He had used it boarding ships in weather that broke masts. It worked less well here, among the smell of tar and the squeak of boot leather, because there was no enemy to strike and no storm to blame. There was only a room full of ordinary men, any one of whom could end everything by growing curious.

"Porter." A guard — young, freckled, bored — stopped beside him. "Whose is that?"

Edmund let the pause stretch exactly as long as a tired man's would. "Miss inspector's," he said, and tilted his chin toward Tamsin without meeting the guard's eyes.

The guard followed the tilt, saw the badge, saw the grey coat, and lost interest in the same motion. Authority was a current in this room, Edmund saw; it flowed downhill from the factor through the sergeant through the inspectors and pooled around whoever stood nearest the tables. Standing in its pool, a porter became furniture. He was beginning to understand what Vane had meant in the fourth letter, the one written in a hand gone ragged: *They cannot see us, Aldous wrote. We are the things they stand on.*

Then Hobbs arrived, and the arithmetic changed.

* * *

*Tamsin*

Hobbs came in smelling of gin and grievance, gout stick striking the boards, and found his table occupied by a Grade Three diver who had no business sitting above her station and every appearance of enjoying it.

"The hell is this?"

"Hobbs." She didn't rise. Rising conceded ground. "Your table was empty. Pike's rule is the queue doesn't stop. Take it up with him."

Hobbs took it up with Pike, loudly, and while the two men argued — a rich, absorbing quarrel, ten years deep in petty wounds — Tamsin made her mistake. She glanced at Edmund.

It was half a second. In half a second she saw what a stranger might see: a porter standing too still, holding a crate that had been in this room too long, watching the inspectors' argument with the fixed attention of a man memorizing exits.

"Porter." Her own voice sounded foreign to her. "With me. This crate needs logging."

She led him toward the tally desk at the end of the row, cursing herself with every step. *Logging.* There was no log for incoming cargo at the customs house; there was a log for outgoing, which was where money lived. She had invented a procedure in panic, and invention was the thing that got people caught — not lies exactly, but the seams of lies showing.

At the tally desk sat Corporal Reyes, who was twenty-three, meticulous, and ambitious in the quiet way of men who wrote everything down. He looked up at her approach with the mild patience of a clerk interrupted at breakfast.

"Crate coming in?" he asked.

"Company property," Tamsin said. "Shell samples from the packet boat. My porter's carrying it to the assay office."

Reyes opened the outgoing ledger anyway. "Manifest?"

"It's samples, corporal. Shell and straw. Do you want me to write *shell and straw* in your book, or shall we save the ink?"

Something flickered in his face — not suspicion, exactly. Diligence encountering inconvenience, and deciding, reluctantly, which it could afford to be. He glanced at the queue, at Pike still locked in combat with Hobbs, at the morning's orders posted overhead with their twice-underlined reward.

"Open it," he said.

* * *

*Edmund*

Later, Edmund would think that the strangest part was how slowly it happened. The corporal's hand coming out for the lid. Tamsin stepping in front of the crate with her body angled just so, her voice going pleasant in the way he had learned, these past days, preceded her worst fear. The straw coming up in fistfuls. The smell of dried shell, faintly sweet and rotten at once, filling the air.

And underneath the straw — he prayed — fourteen pounds of it, laid thick and deep — the flat gleam of shell, and only shell.

Vane's letters were beneath a second layer, under a plank of false lining that Brack's carpenter had fitted so cunningly it took Edmund himself a minute to find the seam the night before. But planks can be lifted. Linings can be cut. All it required was one diligent young man with a knife and no reason to stop.

"Samples," Reyes said, writing. "For the assay office."

"That's what I said," Tamsin said.

"For what shipment?"

"No shipment. Quality survey. The company wants to know what grade of shell the coastal packets are carrying before it sets the season price." She said it smoothly, and Edmund marveled and despaired at once, because the explanation was excellent, and excellence drew attention the way blood drew sharks. Simple men lied badly on purpose. Clever ones lied beautifully and got hanged for it.

Reyes's pen slowed. "Who authorized a quality survey?"

"The factor." She held his eyes. "Mr. Calloway. Would you like me to carry your question to him along with the crate? He does keep office hours till noon."

The pen finished its stroke. Reyes closed the ledger, and Edmund felt the whole edifice of his heartbeat come down an inch at a time, stone by stone, back onto its foundations —

— and then the freckled guard, the bored one, drifting past with his rifle in the crook of his arm, paused, and looked at the crate, and said, in the tone of a man remembering something trivial:

"Sarge said count the extra crates. That one wasn't on the packet manifest. I checked."

* * *

*Tamsin*

The world narrowed to the width of the guard's freckled face.

She heard herself speaking from very far away, calm as deep water: "Extra from what?"

"From anything." He shrugged. "Sarge's orders. Everything on the floor gets matched to paper. That crate's got no paper." He looked pleased with himself. Vigilance was going to buy him something someday; you could see the dreaming in him.

Tamsin's mind ran the currents fast, the way she ran them underwater — no wasted motion, no second chances, take the channel that's open even if it's ugly. The crate needed paper. Paper took time. Time was the one thing the room would not give her, because Reyes was already reopening his ledger, and Pike had ended his war with Hobbs and was looking their way with the slow animal interest of a sergeant scenting something irregular.

"Corporal," she said, and her voice did something she had never heard it do before — it warmed. "Fetch the sergeant, would you? He'll want to sign off on the discrepancy himself. Sarge's orders, sarge's signature." She turned to the freckled guard. "And you — good work. Properly done. Name?"

"Willis, miss."

"Willis. Wait here. If this turns out to be what I think it is, there may be a reward in it."

The word worked on him like brandy. He straightened. Reyes hesitated, torn between diligence and the chance of being present at something, then went for Pike.

Tamsin put both hands on the crate lid, as if steadying it, and spoke to Edmund without turning her head, her lips barely moving.

"When Pike gets here, he'll want it opened in front of everyone. So it opens in front of everyone. Understand? We don't hide anymore. We perform."

"What's in it that we can show?"

"Fourteen pounds of shell," she said, "and my badge. Watch me."

* * *

*Edmund*

Pike arrived like weather. He planted himself before the crate, looked at it, looked at Willis, looked at Tamsin, and Edmund watched the sergeant assemble the situation into the shape most flattering to himself: disorder on his floor, a junior guard doing clever work, an inspector of the lowest useful grade involved in something that might — if the morning's orders meant anything, and rewards meant everything — reflect credit upward.

"Open it," Pike said.

Tamsin opened it. Her hands did not shake. Edmund, watching, understood that he was in the presence of a competence so total it had its own kind of courage, and felt something shift in his chest that he would examine later, at leisure, if later existed.

Straw flew. Shell gleamed — pink and grey and one great golden lip of mother-of-pearl that caught the window light and threw it around the room like a coin tossed to a crowd. Even Pike blinked.

"Quality survey," Tamsin said. "Factor's orders. The crate came off the packet unmanifested because it's company property moving in company channels — it doesn't ride the commercial manifest, it rides mine." She unpinned the tin badge from her coat, and laid it on the edge of the crate with a small bright click that rang in the silence like a struck glass. "Grade Three, Inspector Thornbury, diving division. My authority covers company shell in transit. If Sergeant Pike wants to countermand the factor's survey, he's welcome to send for Mr. Calloway. Otherwise I'll take my samples and my porter and get out of the queue's way."

She picked up the badge. She waited.

Edmund counted his breathing. Four in. Six out. Beneath his feet the floorboards seemed to flex like a deck, and somewhere far off, absurdly, he heard gulls crying over the packet boat's wake, and Vane's voice saying *they cannot see us, we are the things they stand on* — except that Vane had been wrong, Edmund thought, Vane had been wrong about everything, because they could see you, any moment, any morning, any bored freckled boy with a rifle and a memory, they could see you whenever they finally looked—

Pike snorted. "Survey," he said, disgusted. "Half the paperwork in this port comes from Calloway wanting to know the price of something before the seller does." He turned on Willis. "Company property in an inspector's charge isn't extra, you idiot. It's hers. Match it to her." And to Tamsin, with the grudging courtesy of one survivor of the water to another: "Log it with Reyes and get it out of my house."

* * *

*Tamsin*

She logged it. Shell and straw, fourteen pounds, inspector's conveyance, one signature — Reyes's pen scratching in the outgoing ledger, committing the crate officially to a destination it would never reach. Willis watched her go with the deflated expression of a boy whose reward had evaporated into a sergeant's snort, and she gave him a nod, soldier to soldier, because it cost nothing and because some part of her — the part she distrusted most — wanted him to remember her kindly when he learned, someday, whose crate it had been.

Then the doors, and the light, and the wharf.

They walked forty paces before either of them trusted their legs. At the head of the pier, where the shell barges knocked gently against their moorings, Tamsin stopped and set the crate down and found that her hands, so steady through all of it, were shaking now that it was over — shaking like a freed line, whipping with everything that had been held taut.

"We cleared it," Edmund said. He sounded as though he'd run a mile underwater.

"We cleared it *barely*." She laughed once, high and strange, exhilaration and terror indistinguishable in her throat. "Did you see the golden shell? I've never packed a better lie in my life."

"You were magnificent."

"I was lucky." She straightened, and the laugh drained out of her, and what remained was colder. "Edmund. Willis checked the packet manifest. Which means somebody else can check it tomorrow. And Reyes wrote my name in a ledger next to a crate the company doesn't know it's missing — a crate that has to vanish now, today, before anyone counts shell that isn't there." She looked back at the customs house, squat and grey against the bright water, at the twice-underlined word *REWARD* darkening the doorway like a smudge of smoke. "We got through the first gate. But a boy with freckles nearly took us apart, and he'll remember my face for the rest of his life."

Edmund picked up the crate, settling its weight with a laborer's economy, but his eyes stayed on the harbor road behind them, on the small figures moving between the checkpoint lanterns.

"Then we'd best hope," he said quietly, "that the man at the toll bridge has a shorter memory — because I've just realized where Willis will be standing tomorrow."

He nodded toward the estuary, where the bridge's stone arches straddled the tide like a drawn bolt across a door.

"He's being transferred. First thing in the morning. To the second checkpoint."
