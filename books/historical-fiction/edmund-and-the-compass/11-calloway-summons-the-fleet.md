## 11. Calloway Summons the Fleet

Mr. Nathaniel Calloway kept a clock in his office that had never once told the correct time, and this was deliberate. It ran eleven minutes fast. He wound it himself every Sunday evening, and when clerks asked why he did not simply correct it, he told them the truth: *A man who arrives eleven minutes late to everything I ask of him is a man I can measure. A man who arrives exactly on time might be early or might be late, and I would have no way of knowing which.* The clock was a lesson in controlled error. Everything in Calloway's office was a lesson in something.

He sat now with his back to the window, because light on paper was for men who needed to squint, and turned the passenger manifest over twice before he read it a third time. The third reading changed nothing. That was what troubled him.

*Edmund Roe. Clerical. Per packet boat *Larkspur*, coastwise from Merrow's Landing.*

One name on one list among forty names, and yet the name would not sit still on the page. It kept rearranging itself into other words: *Vane*. *Letters*. *Witness*.

The report had come to him secondhand and then fourth-hand, as reports did, passed down through customs clerks like coins growing thinner at each exchange. A passenger had made inquiries. Not loud inquiries — Calloway's informants were not paid to hear loud inquiries; those heard themselves — but the quiet kind, conducted at the harbormaster's counter and again at the shipping office, in the tone of a man confirming an obituary rather than disputing one. *Captain Aldous Vane,* the stranger had supposedly said, or something near enough. *Lost with the *Providence* two years past. Is there anyone still living who served under him?*

Calloway set the manifest down and aligned its edge with the edge of the desk. He was a compact man built like a strongbox, with small hands that never gestured and a voice pitched so low that people leaned in to hear it and thereby came closer to him than they intended. His hair was going grey at the temples in a way that flattered him, and he knew it, because he knew everything about himself that could be turned to account. He did not consider this vanity. Vanity was for men who wanted things they could not use.

Two years ago, on the authority of a sworn declaration and a company-certified certificate of death, Captain Aldous Vane had gone into the sea along with three hundred pounds of debt, two contested claims of salvage, and a ledger's worth of inconvenient arithmetic. The sea was the company's oldest accomplice. It demanded no share of the profits and left no marks that survived inspection. Drowned men could not contest their estates. Drowned men's wages reverted to the house. Drowned men's letters, if any existed, became paper, and paper could be bought.

That last item was the flaw in the design, and Calloway had known it from the beginning. Vane had been careful — careful enough that his death had required nine months of arrangement rather than the usual nine days — but even careful men wrote things down. The trick was never to prevent the writing. The trick was to know where the writing was before anyone else thought to look for it.

For two years, the answer had been: at the bottom of the sea, in the captain's sea chest, in the wreck of the *Providence*, wherever that was. A satisfying answer. A complete answer.

And then a man with salt-damaged boots had stepped off a coastal packet and started asking after drowned men by name.

* * *

Calloway did not rage. Rage was expenditure without return, and he had built his career on the principle that the house always balanced its books. But he did, for perhaps four seconds, close his eyes and permit himself the luxury of imagining the specific shape of the problem, the way a surgeon permits himself to imagine the tumor entire before cutting.

Here was the shape. If Edmund Roe possessed nothing, he was a nuisance, and nuisances could be followed, fined for some invented irregularity of papers, and put back on a packet boat at the company's convenience. If Roe possessed correspondence — if Vane had been posting letters out over months the way a man bails a leaking boat, handful by handful — then somewhere behind that mild clerical face sat the one document in the world that could unmake four years of tidy deaths. And documents did not travel alone. Documents traveled toward magistrates, toward superintendents, toward newspapers in capital cities where the company's name opened doors and closed others. A document in this port was a spark on a stone floor. The quarterly fleet was due to sail in twenty-six days, and every ship that left would carry the story outward whether or not it carried the paper.

Twenty-six days. Calloway turned that number over and found it wanting.

He rose and went to the chart of the harbor that occupied the wall opposite his desk, pinned at the corners with brass tacks gone green. The port lay along its shoreline like beads on a string, and he read the beads as he had read them for nineteen years: the customs house at the north quay, where everything and everyone entering the port passed under company eyes. The toll bridge over the estuary to the south, the single crossing for any road traffic, with its gate and its sergeant and its sergeant's well-fed palm. And at the harbor mouth, the fleet gate — the inner anchorage checkpoint through which no cargo left for the quarterly sailing without inspection, manifest, and seal. Three chokepoints. Three places where the world narrowed to a door and a man with a stamp.

Anyone leaving this port with something to hide must pass all three. Anyone hunting them need hold only one.

It was, Calloway reflected, almost elegant. Almost a mercy, the geometry of it. A fugitive needed luck three times; a hunter needed it once.

But geometry only worked given time, and time was the one quantity on the chart that could not be bribed.

* * *

He sent for Hoskins first, because Hoskins was the customs superintendent and therefore the first bead on the string, and because Hoskins was stupid in the reliable way of men who had been promoted past their competence into positions where their incompetence served as loyalty. Hoskins arrived wiping his hands on his coat, as he always did, as though the files themselves were unclean.

"Double your search parties at the customs house," Calloway said, without greeting. "Every incoming passenger, every crate, every seam of every coat. Effective this morning."

"We posted notice yesterday, sir. Contraband correspondence. My men are—"

"I know what you posted. I wrote it." Calloway let the silence do a moment's work. Hoskins shifted his weight. "What I am telling you is that the notice was not sufficient. There is a passenger in this port — arrived on the *Larkspur*, name of Roe — who may be carrying documents that belong to the company. He will not carry them on his person now. He will have distributed them, or hidden them, or sewn them into something dull. Therefore searching passengers is no longer the point. Searching *everything* is the point, and watching faces more than luggage."

"Faces, sir?"

"A courier looks at his boots. An accomplice looks at you. A man carrying something he means to protect looks at the exits." Calloway tapped the manifest, which he had brought with him to the meeting precisely so that it could be tapped. "Roe. Find out who he speaks to, where he sleeps, what he eats, and who feeds it to him. I do not want him detained. Detention creates paperwork, and paperwork travels upward. I want him *understood*."

Hoskins nodded with the vigor of a man who understood perhaps half, which Calloway accounted acceptable. Half of an order faithfully executed outperformed the whole of one imagined.

"And the reward," Hoskins said, at the door. "The vigilance payment. The divers' crews are asking."

"Post it. Fifty pounds for information leading to the recovery of company property." Calloway did not look up from the manifest. "And see that the diving gangs hear of it first. Water people talk to water people. A stranger in this port has to buy his bread from someone, and everyone who sells bread owes someone a debt."

After Hoskins went out, Calloway sat for a while with his fingertips together, listening to the fast clock tick its eleven false minutes. Then he pulled a fresh sheet toward him and began, in his small precise hand, to make a list. He was a great believer in lists. A worry written down stopped being a weather system and became inventory.

*Item: the passenger Roe. Status: unknown cargo, unknown allies, unknown purpose. Assumption: hostile.*

*Item: the letters, if they exist. Status: dispersed? Assumption: intact somewhere within the port's three gates.*

*Item: the schedule. Status: fixed, public, and — he paused here, pen lifted — negotiable.*

That was the item he stared at longest. Because here was the thing that nineteen years at the trading house had taught him, the thing they did not put in ledgers: schedules were not information about the future. Schedules were weapons lying on the ground, available to whoever thought to pick them up first.

The quarterly fleet sailed on the twentieth, as it had sailed on the twentieth for six years, and every soul in the port from the magistrate to the oyster girls planned their lives around that date. Cargo timed its arrival. Debts timed their settlement. Departures, disguises, escapes — all of it keyed to a rhythm published months in advance, as reliable as tide tables. Anyone wishing to move something secretly out of this port had the whole published calendar to plan against. They knew when the ships loaded. They knew when the fleet gate would be crowded with legitimate traffic, when the inspectors would be harried, when confusion could be rented cheaply.

They knew because the company had told them.

Calloway picked up his pen and crossed out the twentieth. Above it he wrote the twelfth.

Eight days. Eight days stripped off the calendar like planks off a hull. The announcement would go out tonight under the superintendent's seal — *advanced departure to accommodate naval convoy arrangements*, a fiction plausible enough that no one would question it and no one ever did — and by morning the whole port would be scrambling. Stevedores rerouting cargo. Merchants howling. Inspectors working double watches. And somewhere in that churn of disruption, any man holding dead captains' letters would wake up and discover that the comfortable twenty-six days he had been counting on had become eighteen, that his carefully mapped route through three checkpoints now ran through them at double speed, with half the preparation and none of the predictability. Confusion, Calloway had found, harmed the guilty far more than the innocent. The innocent merely suffered it. The guilty were exposed by it, because guilt is a structure, and structures show their cracks when shaken.

He wrote beneath the new date, in letters smaller and firmer: *Let us see what swims up when we drain the pond.*

There was a satisfaction in it that he did not trouble to disguise, though he was alone. Not cruelty — he would have rejected the word, and meant it. What he felt was the pleasure of a man setting a broken bone: necessary force, applied at the right angle, against resistance that did not understand its own anatomy. The port would hurt. The port was designed to hurt. That was what a port was — a place where the empire's business pressed through a narrow opening, and narrow openings, by their nature, pinched.

* * *

Through the afternoon, the machinery moved exactly as he set it, each piece meshing with the next, and Calloway observed it the way he observed everything: from slightly above and slightly behind.

By four o'clock the fleet-gate master had his revised loading orders and had begun reassigning berths, cursing cheerfully, because chaos at the gate meant overtime and overtime meant coin. By five, the toll bridge sergeant had received a sealed instruction — additional watchmen, heightened scrutiny of southbound traffic beginning the tenth, and a reminder that the bridge toll ledger would be audited monthly henceforth, which was Calloway's way of reminding the sergeant that his palm was known and tolerated only insofar as it remained useful. By six, the notice of advanced departure had gone to the printer, and the printer's boys would be running off two hundred copies by lamplight, and by dawn those copies would be pasted on the customs house door and the harbormaster's board and the door of the saloon where the pilots drank, and the whole port would inhale as one body.

Calloway walked the north quay himself in the last of the light. He did this more often than his clerks guessed; a man who managed an empire of paper risked forgetting that paper described flesh. The quay at dusk was its own honest ledger. Shell barges riding low. Dive boats coming in with the evening wind, their crews hollow-eyed from pressure and salt. The pearl sheds exhaling their smell of lime and dead sea-floor. Women sorting shell by lamplight, hands moving faster than thought. And everywhere the company's mark — on the crates, on the boats, on the thin copper tokens the divers wore on cords around their necks, each token stamped with a number that stood for a debt that never shrank.

He watched one of the sorting sheds as he passed. Through the doorway he could see a young woman at the sorting table — Thornbury's girl, the diver, the one whose file he had pulled twice this year because her quota figures were too clean and her debts too stubbornly alive. She sat apart from the others, doing her sums, and even at this distance there was something in the set of her shoulders that he recognized and distrusted: the straightness of a person who has decided that she is competent, and has not yet learned what competence costs.

Hoskins had mentioned her. Said she'd been assigned to passenger inspection at the customs house since the notices went up. Said she was sharp, quiet, and had a mother ashore with debts older than the girl's indenture.

Debts, Calloway thought, walking on. Everything came down to debts in the end. A man like Roe might be moved by loyalty, by grief, by whatever romantic infection had passed between him and a drowned captain — but loyalty could not be counted on, because loyalty had no ledger line. Debt always could. Somewhere in this port was the person Roe needed, or would need, and somewhere in that person's life was a figure owed to the house, and figures could be raised, forgiven, or converted. It was not bribery. Bribery implied corruption of a clean thing. This was simply reading the accounts that already existed and acting accordingly.

The lamps were being lit along the quay as he turned back toward the trading house, each flame a small assertion against the dark, and Calloway permitted himself the day's single moment of satisfaction. The trap was not the doubled guards, though they helped. It was not the reward money, though greed was dependable. The trap was time itself, bent now to the company's hand. Whoever held Aldous Vane's letters had believed they had weeks. As of tomorrow morning, printed and public and undeniable, they had days instead — and men in a hurry make mistakes, and mistakes leave traces, and traces were Calloway's native currency.

He reached his office as the fast clock struck eight, eleven minutes ahead of the town's bells, and stood a moment in the doorway looking at his desk, the chart, the manifest with its one restless name.

Somewhere out there in the lamplit sprawl of the port, in a loft or a boarding room or the hold of a shell barge, Edmund Roe was making plans against a calendar that no longer existed. Calloway almost pitied him. Almost. The feeling lasted the length of a breath, and then he sat down, drew the lamp nearer, and began drafting the order that would move eight days out from under the whole world's feet — and in the morning, when the notices went up and the port woke shouting, the first face he intended to study in the crowd was that of the Thornbury girl, to see whether she looked surprised.

Surprise, in Calloway's experience, was the one expression that could not be rehearsed — and the only completely honest testimony a face could give.

He dipped his pen, and wrote: *Let the fleet sail on the twelfth.*
