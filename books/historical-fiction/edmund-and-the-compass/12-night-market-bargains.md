## 12. Night Market Bargains

The night market did not exist between dawn and dusk. That was the first thing a newcomer learned, if the newcomers were permitted to learn anything at all: that the lane behind the salt houses, the one that smelled of tar and frying fish and the sweet rot of fruit nobody would admit to selling, was an ordinary alley by daylight, choked with crates and cats, and became something else entirely when the company's lanterns went dark along the harbor front.

Tamsin had been going there since she was eleven years old, first carrying her mother's marketing basket, later carrying her own money, which was never much. She knew the vendors by their goods rather than their faces — the woman who sold candied ginger wrapped in oiled paper, the man whose knives were sharpened on a stone he kept warm in his shirt against his skin, the boys who moved through the crowd with lizards' speed, carrying messages no one asked about aloud.

She knew which stalls were honest by the standard of the place, which was to say dishonest only in the ways everyone had agreed not to mind.

And she knew Brack.

Everyone knew Brack, though few could say they had ever sat down with him. He was less a man than a weather pattern — a pressure you felt before he arrived, a change in how the crowd stood. He dealt in passage papers and quiet berths and the kind of cargo that was never written down anywhere it could be found. He was said to have lost a brother to the sea, or to the company — the two were often confused on this coast — and he wore his grief the way he wore his coat, heavy and buttoned and never discussed.

Tonight Tamsin carried everything she owned that mattered, and it fit inside a single drawstring pouch small enough to disappear into her palm.

She had counted the pearls four times before leaving her room, and a fifth time on the walk down, touching the pouch through her coat at every tenth step like a rosary. Thirty-one pearls from the season's diving. Not the great flawless ones the factor's clerks cooed over under their magnifying glasses — those went straight to the company, every shell and gem assessed at the source, weighed against her debt. These were the seconds. The slightly baroque, the off-round, the ones with a flaw in the nacre visible only when the light caught them wrong. Divers were permitted to keep the seconds as a wage of sorts, a concession the company made because it cost nothing and bought patience, and Tamsin had spent two years hoarding hers instead of selling them piecemeal to the jewelers on Fore Street, who paid half their worth and smiled while they did it.

Two years. Thirty-one pearls. Her whole margin of freedom, strung together in her imagination even though they sat loose in the pouch, clicking against each other like teeth.

She had meant them for something. Everyone who saved meant them for something. Hers had shifted over the years — at fourteen she would have bought her mother a house with a door that locked from the inside; at seventeen, passage south to any port where the company's writ ran thin; these last months she had stopped pretending and simply held them, the way you hold a breath, because imagining a future had begun to feel like an extravagance.

Now she spent them all in one night, and the strangest part, walking down through the salt-house lane with the tide sighing somewhere behind the warehouses, was how little ceremony the moment demanded. No pause at the threshold. No looking back. Just a girl with cold hands and a pouch, going to make a bargain because there was no other bargain to be made.

* * *

The market was at full swell when she entered it. Lanterns hung from hooks and spars and the occasional outstretched broom handle, throwing light in overlapping circles so that the lane came and went, came and went, faces appearing and dissolving. A man was roasting chestnuts over a brazier despite the season's heat, because the fire drew people and the people concealed transactions. Someone played a fiddle badly three lanes over, and the badness was itself a kind of cover, giving everyone an excuse to lean close and shout.

She found Brack where she knew he would be, at the back of the chandlery that sold rope by day, in a room behind the storeroom where coils of line hung from the ceiling like the roots of some inverted tree. A boy sat outside the door on an upturned crate, whittling something that would never be finished, and when he saw Tamsin he looked at her hands — divers' hands, scarred and pale-palmed — and then knocked twice on the doorframe with the flat of his knife.

Brack's voice came through the wood, gravel over water. "Send her in, then."

The room inside was low-ceilinged and lit by a single lamp turned down until it barely earned the name. Brack sat behind a table that was mostly bare — a ledger with no writing in it, a bottle of arrack, two cups. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a beard that had once been black and was now the color of wet sand, and eyes that performed none of the warmth his voice suggested. He poured the second cup before she had said a word.

"Diver Thornbury," he said. "Daughter of Maren. Sit."

"You know me."

"I know everyone who works the beds, and I know the mothers of everyone who works the beds, and your mother I've known since before you were a disagreement in the parish records." He pushed the cup toward her. "Drink or don't. It's terrible either way. What I want to know is what brings you to my tree at this hour, because girls like you don't come for arrack and you don't come for conversation, and there's only one thing left."

"Papers," Tamsin said.

"Papers." He said it back slowly, tasting it. "For yourself?"

"For myself. And for a traveler. Two passages, coastal packet or fishing boat or whatever leaves soonest and is least likely to be boarded. And I need a place — a hidden place, a hold, a loft, a barrel — where a man can stay out of sight for several days. Several being however many it takes."

The lamplight moved as someone passed outside the door, and Brack's hand had come to rest, very casually, near the ledger with nothing written in it. She understood the gesture. Names were currency here too, and she was spending recklessly already.

"Not here," she amended. "I'll give you what I have. But the details travel at the pace you decide, and no faster."

Brack leaned back. The chair complained under him. For a long moment he studied her the way she had seen the factor's assessors study shell — turning her over, looking for the flaw in the nacre, the crack that told you where to apply pressure.

"Your mother sent you?" he asked finally.

"No."

"She know you're here?"

"No."

Something shifted in his face — not softening exactly, but recognition. The look of a man confirming that the person across the table had done the arithmetic alone and arrived at a number she was prepared to live with.

"Good," he said. "Mothers are wise, and wisdom is a danger in my trade. Wise people try to save you from the price." He picked up the empty ledger and tapped it against the table edge, once, twice, like a man beating time to a thought. "Now. Papers I can do. Two, coastal run, under names that aren't yours, on a boat whose master owes me and hates the company, which is the best combination available in this world. The hiding place is harder, but I have it. A shell barge, mine, moored past the third buoy. It loads shell for the fleet and nobody searches it because—" he spread his hands, "—it's the company's own profit going into the company's own hold, and Calloway's men search for sins, not for salaries. Your traveler can lie in the shell bin under a false floor with the smell of it in his nose for company. It's not comfortable. Comfort costs more than you have."

"How much?" Tamsin asked. Because there was no sense circling it. Brack did not haggle the way market women did, pretending offense, invoking their children. He named a number and the number was true, and after that you either paid it or you walked out and continued living in the world where you hadn't.

Brack set the ledger down. He looked at the pouch in her hand — she hadn't shown it to him yet, hadn't mentioned it, but of course he knew why she'd come and what a diver might carry — and he surprised her by naming a figure in coin instead. A large figure. Larger than thirty-one seconds would fetch anywhere on the coast, probably; smaller than what he was asking her to buy.

"That's the price," he said. "In money."

"I don't have money."

"Nobody has money. That's why it's the price and not the transaction." He leaned forward, and the lamp caught the wet-sand color of his beard and the flat patience of his eyes. "Here's how it works, diver. You pay me in what you have. Your pearls go toward it, and what they fall short — and they'll fall short, because pearl-buyers are thieves and I buy from thieves — the rest you owe me as labor. Diving labor. My barge needs filling for the fleet load, and a diver of your reputation can fill quota faster than the three men I'd otherwise hire. You work the difference off before the fleet sails. That's the shape of it."

"And the pearls themselves? You keep them regardless?"

"I keep them regardless."

"Then name the labor honestly, Brack. Don't dress it up as arithmetic. Say what it is."

For the first time something that was nearly a smile crossed his face, though it never reached his eyes, which continued their assessment as if the mouth were acting without authorization.

"Honestly, then," he said. "You're buying two forged passages, a false floor, my silence, and my risk. If Calloway finds shell-divers running his checkpoints, he doesn't fine me. He has the constable find a reason, and the reason always exists, and I lose the barge and possibly the use of my hands. So the price is high because the risk is real, and the pearls are a gesture, and the real payment is your work and your nerve. There. Dressed plain enough for church."

Tamsin let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding, and was annoyed at herself for it. She opened the pouch and poured the pearls onto the table.

They sounded different outside the pouch. In her palm, over two years, she had come to know their weight as a private fact, almost a secret identity — *this is what I am worth, unrecorded, unborrowed, mine.* On the table, under the stingy lamp, rolling a quarter-turn and settling against each other with small dry clicks, they became merchandise. Thirty-one small moons, most of them imperfect, catching the light unevenly, worth less than the number Brack had named and more than she had ever possessed at one time.

She watched him count them. He was quick — a single pass of his fingers, sorting by size and luster the way she sorted shell — and she saw him adjust some internal estimate, and she hated that she saw it, and she hated more the small, shameful flicker of pride when his eyebrows conceded that the goods were better than expected.

"Twenty-two," he said. "That's what these will bring me after the buyers take their cut, and I'm rounding in your favor because I knew your mother." He nudged six of them back across the table. "Keep those. A person shouldn't stand in the world entirely empty-handed. Even six pearls is a person who owns something."

"Put them back in the pile."

"No." He said it without heat, the way you decline a second helping. "I've buried a brother, girl. I know what it does to a man to own nothing, and I won't be the one to teach it to you. Six stays. Twenty-five goes. The rest is labor."

"I said—"

"I heard what you said. I also heard you plan to hide a fugitive in my barge and run him past three of Calloway's checkpoints, which means you'll need your wits and your luck, and luck runs out faster for people who've got nothing left to lose. It's a superstition, if you like. Call it that. But the six stay, and if you throw them in the harbor out of pride I'll fish them out and charge you for the drowning."

The silence stretched. Outside, the fiddle had stopped, and in its absence she could hear the harbor itself — hawsers creaking, the slap of water against pilings, the far-off clatter of the customs house securing for the night.

She thought about arguing further and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that arguing was the last performance of ownership, and that performing it would make the loss heavier, not lighter. Brack had offered her a mercy dressed as commerce, and the kindest thing she could do for both of them was accept it without ceremony.

So she didn't thank him, because thanks would have made it a favor, and favors were debts too, and she was trying to stop accumulating debts that other people held the ledgers for. She swept the twenty-five pearls back into the pouch — all but six, which she left on the table, gleaming dully, the last things on earth that were indisputably hers besides her knife, her broken compass, and her mother's silence — and tied the pouch shut, and it was astonishing how light it felt now, how little it mattered, how a weight she had carried for two years could vanish in a quarter hour and leave her standing straighter rather than bent.

"The papers," she said.

Brack took a key from around his neck, unlocked a drawer beneath the table, and produced two documents folded in oilcloth. He opened one flat. She read it upside down, which was a habit of the checkpoint, and found it good — a coastal passage permit in the name of one *Eliza Voss, shell-sorter*, with stamps that looked right and signatures that were probably dead men's, which was the safest kind.

"The second's the same, different name, different boat," Brack said. "They don't travel together, understand? Same destination, different vessels, different days if we can manage it. Anyone watching sees two strangers, not a pair." He refolded them and held them a moment before releasing, and his grip on the oilcloth tightened just enough that she felt the question in it. "One condition. Mine."

"Name it."

"When this is done — whatever's in those crates your traveler carries, whatever trouble he's brought to my coast — I hear it first. Before the magistrate, before the fleet, before anyone. I hear it from you, in this room." His voice stayed level but the flatness in his eyes had gone hard as shell. "There are names I've waited a long time to see written down somewhere that couldn't be burned."

Tamsin held his gaze. She thought of Edmund's letters, oil-wrapped and secret, of names she hadn't yet heard read aloud, of the way Edmund's face closed whenever she asked what was in them. She thought of her father, eleven years drowned, and the company boat that had been first on the scene and last to report it.

"If it's in the pages," she said, "you'll hear it. That I can promise. Whether it holds what you want — that I can't."

"Nothing ever does," Brack said. "It's enough."

He slid the papers across. She took them, and the transaction was complete, and it had taken less time than buying fish.

* * *

The walk back up through the market was different from the walk down. Nothing had changed except the contents of her coat pocket — pearls out, papers in — and yet the whole lane seemed rearranged, the way a room changes when you have decided to leave it. The chestnut man nodded at her. The candied-ginger woman did not. Somewhere a hatch was being battened, and the sound carried clean through the night air like a sentence being pronounced.

Six pearls in Brack's keeping now, waiting for her like a conscience. Two papers that made her a person who did not legally exist. A debt of labor owed to a smuggler, due before a fleet sailed — and the fleet's departure date lived in Calloway's mouth these days, moving like a tide nobody could chart, eight days pulled forward on rumor, maybe more by now. Every hour she spent was an hour spent out of a purse that was draining whether she watched it or not.

At the mouth of the lane she stopped, out of old habit, and checked the darkness the way a diver checks the surface before ascending — a slow turn, reading silhouettes, listening for the particular quality of quiet that means someone else is making it. The coast was clear in the way the coast is always clear until it isn't.

Edmund was where she had left him, in the net-loft above the old sailmaker's shed, sitting with his back against the wall and the letters in his lap even though he had promised — sworn — not to open them again without her. When her boots hit the top of the ladder he looked up, and she watched him perform the same inventory she had just performed outside: her face, her hands, the shape of her coat, searching for what had changed.

He saw it immediately. People who measure the world in what can be carried recognize each other.

"It's done," she said.

"Tamsin." He said her name the way people say *are you sure* without wasting the syllables. "What did it cost?"

"Everything," she said, and sat down across from him, and was surprised to find that saying it cost nothing at all. "Now read them to me. All of it. Every name, every date. You've been carrying this alone since the packet boat, and I'm done being protected from paper." She pulled her knees up and looked at the oil-wrapped bundle in his lap, and her heart was pounding and her voice was steady, and that, at least, the sea had taught her well. "If I've spent everything I own on getting these letters past three checkpoints, then I deserve to know what my father's name is doing among them."

Edmund went very still.

Outside, far below, the tide turned against the pilings, and began, quietly, to come in.
