## 13. The Letters Read Aloud

The loft over Brack's shell-shed smelled of brine and lamp oil and the sweet rot of oyster shells stacked in barrels below. Rain came off the estuary in slow curtains, drumming the tin roof, and beneath that sound — the sound of a harbor keeping its secrets — Edmund laid the oilcloth packet on the floorboards between them and did not touch it for a long moment.

Tamsin sat cross-legged opposite him with her arms wrapped around her knees. She had not spoken since they climbed the ladder. She had not spoken much since the bridge checkpoint, since the water, since he'd watched her surface on the far bank gray-lipped and shaking and had pretended, for both their sakes, not to see it. Now she watched the packet the way she might watch something alive.

"You said you'd read them to me," she said finally. "On the barge. You said when we were somewhere the walls weren't company walls."

"I said that."

"So read them."

He untied the cord. His fingers were steady — they were always steady; Vane had once joked that Roe's hands would stay level through a hurricane, which was precisely why he couldn't be trusted at cards — but there was a slowness in him now that was not calm. He had carried these letters for eleven months. He knew every page the way a man knows the road home: by feel, in darkness, with his eyes closed. Reading them aloud to another person would make them real in a way they had never quite been. Paper could be doubted. A voice in a wet loft in front of a woman whose family had served the company for two generations could not be taken back.

The lamp between them guttered as the rain found a seam in the roof. Brack was below with his boy, moving barrels, and the thump of the work rose up through the boards like a slow heartbeat.

"The first letter is dated the ninth of March, 1845," Edmund said. "Port of Cairn Hollow. It begins—"

* * *

*Dear Roe, if you are alive, which I begin to doubt,*

*I have taken the position of sailing master aboard the *Meridian*, company registry, and I write to you from a ship I am not permitted to complain about, so I will not complain about it. Instead I will tell you what I told no one at the time, because at the time I thought it was grief speaking.*

*You remember Halloran. Boatswain, the *Providence*. Lost overboard in the Coral Passage, April of '43, with four hundred pounds of debt standing against his name and a widow in the port who was owed it. We buried him in the register as drowned-at-sea, witnessed by none, verified by the factor.*

*Roe, I was the man at the rail. There was no man overboard. I have gone over that night a hundred times and I tell you plainly: Halloran went ashore at Passage Key with two trunks and a receipt, and the next morning the log says he drowned, and the factor's clerk wrote *deceased, debts voided, estate to company* in a hand so neat it looked like prayer.*

*I thought then it was one cruelty. I know now it is a method.*

*They do not kill them all. Most are paid to vanish — passage east, a new name, silence purchased with the difference between what they owed and what they had. The ones who won't go quietly are drowned properly, or worse, and the sea asks no questions because the sea has learned that we answer for it. Every certificate bears the factor's seal. Every factor answers to the house. And the house, my friend, answers to nothing on God's water.*

*Burn this after reading. I know you won't. You never burned anything in your life except your own bridges.*

*Yours at anchor,*
*A.V.*

Edmund looked up. Tamsin had not moved. Her eyes were fixed on the page as though she could read it upside down faster than he could speak it.

"Halloran," she said.

"You knew him?"

"My mother washed for his widow." Her voice was flat, careful, a diver conserving air. "I carried the bundle of black cloth back myself. I was eleven."

He turned the page.

* * *

There were nine letters. He read them in order, because order mattered — Vane had built the account like a mason builds a wall, each stone set against the last, and Edmund refused to be the one to knock a stone loose. The second letter named two more men, pearlers, drowned within a month of each other in '44, their debts totaling more than the *Meridian* was worth. The third enclosed a copy of a death certificate, the ink faded but the seal sharp: *I stole this from the factor's office while the clerk was at his dinner. Note the date of death against the date of the tide-tables. A man cannot drown at ebb on a flood tide, yet here he is, drowned, sealed, and filed.*

The fourth letter described the system entire: how the indenture ledgers were kept deliberately illegible, how debts were inflated by fees no diver or deckhand could audit, how a worker who died — or vanished, or was made to vanish — released the company from obligation while transferring any property, any boat-share, any claim, into the house's hands. *We do not merely work them,* Vane wrote, *we harvest them twice. Once in life. Once on paper.*

The fifth, sixth, seventh letters traced names upward — clerks, factors, a magistrate's brother who handled the probate — until the chain reached Mr. Silas Calloway himself, resident factor of this port, described by Vane in a phrase Edmund had always admired for its economy: *a man who believes drowning is simply accounting by other means.*

Rain thickened. Below, the barrel-work had stopped. Edmund lowered his voice anyway.

The eighth letter was shorter. *Roe, they suspect me. I have sent these out piecemeal and pray half of them arrived. If you hold this many, hold them tight. Do not go to the port authorities — I will explain why when I can. Trust no one whose wage the company signs. I am going quiet for a season. If I do not surface by the spring fleet, take what you carry to someone outside the trade entirely. Someone who owes nothing. Someone who can dive deep.* A line had been struck through and rewritten beneath it: *Someone like the Thornbury women, if any of them live. Tell them Aldous remembers.*

He stopped there. His throat had gone dry around the words *the Thornbury women*, and he took a drink of the cold tea Brack's boy had brought up, and made himself go on.

"The ninth is dated fourteen months ago," he said. "It's the last. After that — nothing. I made inquiries in three ports under three names. The company register lists Captain Aldous Vane as lost overboard from the hulk *Integrity*, moored anchorage, no date given, no witness. Drowned, like the rest of them." He let that sit. "Drowned on a ship at anchor, Tamsin. That's how careless they've become. Or how certain."

She was looking at the fire, not at him. "Read the ninth."

*My dear friend,*

*I have seen the ledger itself.*

*I will not say how. Suffice it that for one hour in the factor's office I held the master book of this port's indentures, and I copied what I could before my nerve — or my luck — ran out. The pages I enclose are copies only; the original sits in Calloway's strongroom and must be got at eventually, for copies can be denied, but the original cannot lie about its own arithmetic. Debts that were paid and re-entered. Fees invented whole. Years added to terms in a clerk's hand different from the entry above. And beside certain names, in the margin, a small mark like an anchor. Twenty-three such marks in the pages I saw. I did not have time to learn what the anchor means. I intend to.*

*One name I recognized at once, and I have sat with the recognizing of it for two days now, and I find I cannot put it in a letter without writing the truth around it first, so here is the truth: I loved her mother once, before either of us married sense. I have told you nothing of her because there was nothing to tell that did not hurt. The name in the ledger is Thornbury. Josiah Thornbury, shell-diver, indentured 1838, marked with the anchor, entered deceased by drowning, March 1841, debt of thirty-one pounds twelve shillings transferred to estate — which is to say, to the company, which then held his widow's renewed indenture as surety against a dead man's invented sum. She has been diving to pay her husband's drowning ever since. Calloway will have doubled it by now. They always double it.*

*Roe, I knew Josiah. He was a careful man in the water — they all are, the good ones, it is how they stay alive. The weather that day was flat calm. I was in the harbor myself, refitting. There was no squall, no swell, no reason. And now there is an anchor in a margin, and I understand that the reason existed after all, and it wore a factor's coat.*

*Tell them, if you ever can. Not what I felt — that is nobody's inheritance. Tell them what was done. It is the only thing I have left to give anyone.*

*Hold fast.*
*A.V.*

* * *

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. Outside, the rain went on, indifferent, thorough, like a clerk.

Edmund folded the ninth letter along its worn crease and set it down on the floorboards, and Tamsin still did not move, and he understood that he had just done something that could not be undone — that the words were inside her now, doing whatever words do in the dark of a person, and all he could do was wait.

"March 1841," she said at last.

"Yes."

"He went out on a Thursday. I remember because—" Her voice caught on something and she mastered it the way she mastered everything, by force of practice. "Because I'd found a shell that week. A good one. Mother-of-pearl with a flaw right through the center, like a crack in a window. He said he'd buy it off me when he came back and give it to Mother for a keepsake. He was always saying things like that. Small promises. Things a man says so a child lets him leave in the morning."

"Tamsin—"

"He was a careful diver." She said it again, harder, as if repetition could make it an argument the world would have to accept. "Everyone knew it. Other men took risks; my father never did. He used to say the water doesn't hate you, it just doesn't know you're there, so it's your own job to know it. He counted his breaths. He counted his depths. He came up slow every single time, even when the foreman shouted at him for being slow, even when they docked his pay for it. He *came up slow.*" Her hands had come unclasped from her knees and were pressed flat against the floorboards now, as though she needed to hold the building down. "Flat calm. You said flat calm."

"His own letter says it. Vane was in the harbor that day."

"And nobody asked." It wasn't a question and it wasn't shouted; it came out of her low and level and terrible. "A careful diver, flat calm, dead — and nobody asked. Because asking would have meant looking at the ledger, and the ledger had an anchor by his name, and the anchor meant he was worth more to them drowned than living. Thirty-one pounds." She laughed — one syllable, no humor in it, a sound like a hatch slamming. "Thirty-one pounds twelve shillings. My mother dove fifteen years to pay a debt for a murder, and it wasn't even a large murder. It was cheap. He was cheap to them."

"Yes."

"And Vane knew. Fourteen months ago he knew, and he wrote it down, and he tried to send it, and it reached you, and you—" She turned on him then and her eyes were wet and furious and he did not look away from them. "You carried it. For eleven months. You walked past me at the customs house with my father's name in your bag and you didn't know it was my father, and I searched your crate with my own hands, and neither of us knew, and—" She stopped. Breathed. Diver's discipline: when the chest locks, don't fight it, go still. "Why didn't you tell me sooner?"

"I didn't know your father's name." He said it gently, because it was true and because the truth was the only thing he had to offer her that wasn't worthless. "Vane wrote it, yes, but I've read these letters a hundred times and I never connected Thornbury in a ledger to Tamsin Thornbury of the customs house — names repeat in ports, half the coast is Thornburys and Salters and Grails. It wasn't until you told me about your mother's indenture, on the barge, that the two lines met." He hesitated. "And there's something else. Something I should say before you ask why I'm not saying it."

"Say it."

"The compass. Yours. The one without a needle." He watched her face change. "Vane described it once — a gift he left with a woman in this port, years ago, before any of this. He mentioned it in the fourth letter, of all places, tucked in a paragraph about tides. *She'll know what it means if it comes back without its heart,* he wrote. I didn't understand it then. I understood it when I saw it on your shelf."

"That's chapter enough for another night," Tamsin said, but softly, and she looked at the fire again, and he let it go.

She was quiet a long time. The rain softened to a drizzle and then hardened again. Somewhere below, Brack's boy dropped something and swore and was silenced by a look Edmund couldn't hear. When Tamsin spoke again, her voice had changed — the grief was still in it, but it had been packed down, compressed, like air held in the lungs at depth, waiting.

"Twenty-three anchors," she said. "In the pages he copied. How many pages were in the full ledger?"

"Hundreds. Thousands of entries. If the marks run consistent—"

"There could be hundreds of my fathers." She unfolded herself and knelt and pulled the ninth letter toward her, reading it properly now, her lips moving over Vane's cramped hand, and he watched the exact moment she reached the sentence about the debt transferred to estate, because her jaw tightened and stayed tight. "My mother knows some of this."

It was not a question, but he answered it anyway. "I think she may."

"She renewed her indenture the year after he died. She could have walked away from the beds — widows did, sometimes, if they had family. Her sister was in Cairn Hollow. She stayed. She dove. She made me dive." Tamsin looked up. "She made me dive, Edmund, and every pearl went into a scale against a number that a man made up with a pen."

He had no answer for that. There wasn't one. He watched her fold the letter with exaggerated care, aligning the edges, the way people handle things when the alternative is crushing them, and he thought about the first time he'd seen her — behind a customs-house table, cataloguing a stranger's belongings with those same precise hands — and understood that he had mistaken competence for contentment, the way comfortable men always do.

"What happens to this?" she asked finally. "To the letters. What's the plan — the real one? You said evidence. Evidence goes to somebody. A magistrate. An admiralty court. Somebody with a seal bigger than Calloway's."

And there it was — the question he had been dreading since Cairn Hollow, the question Vane had answered in advance and Edmund had hoped she wouldn't think to ask, because the answer would cost her something she still believed in.

"The ninth letter says not to trust the port authorities," he said. "That's not caution, Tamsin. That's cartography. I've spent eleven months mapping who owns whom in this colony. The harbor magistrate is Calloway's wife's cousin. The probate clerk who filed your father's death is the same clerk who filed Halloran's, and Prosser's, and the two pearlers in '44 — one man, one hand, forty deaths, no questions ever raised, which means no one above him wanted questions raised. The naval office takes the house's shipping dues. The superintendent of police banks at the company's counter. I drew it out once, on the back of a chart, every line of patronage and debt and marriage — and when I was finished there was nowhere on the chart that wasn't inside the web." He paused. "The law isn't broken here. That's the thing no one wants to say aloud. The law works exactly as it was built to work. It was built by the house, for the house. Taking these letters to a magistrate is carrying a lantern into the web and asking the spider to hold it steady."

Tamsin stared at him. He watched her resist it — watched the whole architecture of her remaining faith, courts and contracts and the idea that somewhere above the harbor there was a room where arithmetic corrected itself, take the weight of his words and groan under it.

"No," she said. "No — there's always somewhere. There's the governor. There's—"

"A man appointed on the house's recommendation, who has never once overturned a factor's certification in nineteen years. I checked."

"Then the papers. In the capital. Newspapers print—"

"Newspapers owned by men who insure the fleet."

"Then *someone*—"

"Who?" he asked, not cruelly, and the question landed in the lamplight between them and lay there. "Tell me who, and I'll walk to them tomorrow. I have wanted to be wrong about this for eleven months, Tamsin. I have rehearsed being wrong. But Vane told me the same thing from inside it, and he had twenty years in their service and they tried to drown him for reading a book of accounts. The law will call these letters forgery before lunch and treason by supper, and we'll be the evidence against ourselves."

She got to her feet. Walked to the small window, where the rain ran down the glass in beads, each one carrying a smeared reflection of the harbor lights below — the masts, the customs house, the dark shapes of the fleet at their moorings. She stood there with the ninth letter hanging from her hand, and Edmund watched her shoulders and saw her breathing change: long, measured, deliberate, the rhythm of a woman making her body obey her while her mind worked at a depth where the light didn't reach.

"My father came up slow every time," she said to the window. "He did everything right. And the ledger killed him anyway, in a margin, with an anchor." She turned around. Her face was wrecked and resolved at once, and it was the resolve that frightened him. "Then it won't be the law. Fine. But hear me, Edmund Roe — if justice lives outside the law in this colony, then somebody has to build it a body. These letters don't walk to a courtroom. So where do they walk? Who hears them? *When?*"

"That," Edmund said slowly, "is the problem I've been circling for eleven months. And the quarterly fleet sails in—" He stopped. Looked toward the window, toward the harbor, and the answer to a different question arrived in him all at once, cold, the way water finds a hole in a suit.

"What?" she said.

"How does Brack's boy know the tide tables by heart?"
