## 14. Whose Side Is the Law On

*Tamsin*

The loft above Brack's shell store smelled of brine and lamp oil, and by the light of a single guttering wick Edmund had laid the letters out on a plank table like a surgeon laying out instruments.

Tamsin had read perhaps forty words of them herself. Reading was not her weakness — she could parse a manifest faster than any clerk in the customs house — but these were different. These were sentences that had already killed men, and she could feel their weight through her fingertips without opening them.

"So we take them to the magistrate," she said. It was not quite a question. She had been turning it over since they left the toll bridge behind them, since she had climbed out of the estuary with her lungs full of fire and the letters still beating against her ribs like a second heart. "That's what they're for. Evidence. A sworn deposition. Vane's handwriting, the certificates, the names. You walk into the courthouse and you put it on the bench in front of him and it's done."

Edmund did not answer at once. He was watching her the way he watched checkpoints — patiently, cataloguing.

"You've never been inside the courthouse," he said.

"I've been outside it." She had. Once a year, to renew the company's registration of indentured divers, standing in a line of salt-crusted men and women while a clerk read out their debt balances from a ledger none of them were permitted to see. "It's a stone building on Chandler's Hill. There's a magistrate's chair in it and a flag and a bench for petitions."

"There is," Edmund agreed. "And do you know who paid for the chair?"

* * *

*Edmund*

He had rehearsed this argument a hundred times on the packet boat, in the weeks of lying low before the customs house, and every rehearsal had taught him the same lesson: facts alone convinced no one. Facts were cargo; they needed a carrier. So he carried them now gently, one at a time, like shells handed up from deep water.

"Magistrate Hensley," he said, and wrote nothing down because there was no need — he had copied the name into his memory the way Tamsin catalogued weights. "Appointed to the colonial bench eleven years ago on the recommendation of the Pearl and Spice Consolidated. His salary is drawn partly from crown funds and partly from an 'advisory retainer' the company pays him for maritime arbitration work. I have seen the arrangement itemized." He tapped the third letter from the left. "Vane saw the ledger himself. That's part of why he stopped being welcome on company vessels."

"Arbitration is legitimate work," Tamsin said.

"It is. And it is also how you buy a man without ever touching his hand. Hensley rules on every salvage claim, every cargo dispute, every death-at-sea inquest in this district. The company appears before him four times a year. The retainer is renewed four times a year. You may call that coincidence if you like. Vane didn't."

Tamsin's jaw tightened. He could see her wanting to argue — he had learned that about her, that she fought hardest when she most wanted to be proven wrong.

"The coroner, then," she said. "Drownings go to the coroner."

"Coroner Wray is the company's former shipping master. He signed the certificate for your father."

She went very still. He regretted saying it that way — bluntly, without ceremony — but gentleness would have been its own cruelty, a softening of something that needed to stay sharp if it was going to be any use to her.

"The port surgeon?"

"Brother-in-law to Calloway."

"The harbor officer."

"On the payroll as a boarding inspector since before the war."

"The—"

"Tamsin." He said her name once, quietly, and waited until her eyes came to his. "I have spent two years learning this coast from Vane's letters, and every letter ends the same way: another name, another salary. The magistrate, the coroner, the surgeon, the harbor office, the sergeant at the toll bridge, two of the three customs inspectors — one of whom signs your dive permits. If we carry these letters into a courtroom, we are not bringing evidence to the law. We are carrying it into the company's own front room and setting it on their table."

* * *

*Tamsin*

She turned away from him and looked out the loft's small window, where the harbor lay below like spilled tin, masts swaying, lanterns coming alight along the quay. Somewhere down there was the fleet gate. Somewhere beyond it, the quarterly ships that would carry the proof away or keep it here forever.

She understood arguments. What she resisted was not the logic of what Edmund said — she had checked each name against her own experience even as he spoke, and each one had slotted into place with the ugly click of a lock accepting its key. Inspector Reyes who signed her permits, who had once waved her father's drowning file across his desk with a face like a closed shutter. Sergeant Quill at the bridge, who had held a page of copied evidence in his gloved hand only hours ago and called her *the diver's child* as though she were vermin with a pedigree.

What she resisted was the conclusion. Because if every lawful door was locked, then the law itself was not a shelter. It was a wall with the company's name on it, and she had lived her whole life believing walls kept you safe so long as you stood on the right side.

"My mother believed in the courts," she said finally. "When my father drowned, she petitioned. Twice. Wrote proper letters on borrowed paper. She thought if the facts were true enough, someone official would have to act on them." She laughed, and it came out wrong, dry as old shell. "The second time, the company doubled our debt for 'costs incurred.' That was the word. Costs incurred. For asking."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be precise." She faced him again. "If the law is bought, then what are you proposing? Smuggling paper to strangers? Dropping copies over the side of ships until somebody honest reads one? Because that isn't justice, Edmund. That's weather. It falls where it falls and mostly on people like me."

"That is exactly what it is," he said, "at first. And then it isn't."

He crossed to the table and separated one letter from the rest — the oldest, its edges soft with handling, the ink gone brown. "Vane didn't write these to win a case. He knew better than anyone that no court in this colony would hear it. He wrote them because a secret dies when only one man holds it. Copies, Tamsin. Names, dates, amounts, the whole architecture of it, written in a hand that can't be forged and sent out piecemeal to people the company can't buy all at once. A shipping agent in Melbourne. A widow in Singapore whose husband's name is on a false certificate. Two newspapermen. A chaplain. None of them know each other. Each holds one piece. The law won't touch this — but print doesn't need a courtroom. It needs daylight and enough readers that burying it costs more than admitting it."

"And Vane?" she asked. "Where does your daylight leave Vane?"

Edmund's silence answered before his words did.

"That's my fear exactly," Tamsin said softly. "You'll make the scandal and the company will make an example. They've done it before — that's what these letters prove they do. Men don't just drown off this coast by accident, Edmund. Some of them drown because a ledger needed balancing. And when yours does—" She stopped, hearing herself, hearing the shape of what she'd almost said. *When yours does.* Not if.

Something shifted in her chest, some plate of armor she hadn't known she was wearing grinding half a turn.

"I used to think the company and the port were two things," she said slowly. "The company did its business and the port did its living, and the law stood between them like a seawall. But it isn't a seawall. It's the same water. The magistrate drinks from the company's well. The coroner signs what he's told. My whole life I've been diving under a surface that was owned by the men who watched me from the boat, and I never once thought to ask who owned the boat."

* * *

*Edmund*

He let her walk the length of the loft while she thought, because he had learned that too — that Tamsin Thornbury arrived at decisions the way she dove, on her own breath, and drowning her in argument would only make her come up fighting.

But he watched her hands. She had picked up the compass from her kit without seeming to notice she'd done it — the broken compass, needle-less, its brass worn to gold at the hinges. Her thumb moved over the empty dial in slow circles. He thought of the needle wrapped in waxed cloth in his coat, three thousand miles traveled, waiting. Not tonight. Tonight there was another matter first.

"If you want out," he said, "this is the moment. Brack can hide you until the fleet sails. The company doesn't know yet that you're more than a witness at a toll bridge. You could wake tomorrow and be a diver again, with a season's worth of nothing but water ahead of you, and forget my name."

She turned. In the lamplight her face was half shadow and half something harder than anger.

"Forget your name," she repeated. "And forget my father's. Forget that the drowning I grieved at fourteen was arithmetic. Forget that Inspector Reyes has signed my permit every season with the same pen that signed away the truth of it." She set the compass down on the table beside the letters, deliberately, like a woman laying down a card. "You misunderstand me, Edmund Roe, if you think I'm arguing for the courthouse out of hope. I'm arguing because I wanted to be wrong. I wanted one door to be real. I've spent my life measuring things by what I can carry, and I was willing to carry faith in the law because it weighed nothing."

"And now?"

"Now I find it weighs everything." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Every petition my mother wrote. Every diver who went to the inquest of a brother or a son and heard 'misfortune at sea' read out by a bought man in a borrowed chair. All those years we brought our dead to the law like offerings, and the law ate them and thanked us for the meal." She drew a breath. "So yes. Outside the law. I accept it. Not because I like your smugglers' daylight — but because I've finally seen the alternative clearly, and I would rather be a criminal in a true story than an honest woman in a false one."

He believed her. That frightened him more than the checkpoints had, because he had crossed two oceans carrying paper, and paper could not look at you like that.

"There's a cost," he said. "Once we step outside the law, there's no stepping back. No court will protect us afterward either. Whatever we do, we do as outlaws, and the story will be written by whoever survives to hold the pen."

"Then we survive," Tamsin said simply, "and hold it."

* * *

*Tamsin*

They worked out the shape of it through the small hours, and the shape was this: not a courtroom, not a petition, but exposure — copies made, dispersed, planted beyond recall, timed so that no single death could tidy it away. Edmund knew the addresses. Brack, it turned out, knew the boats. Between the two of them lay a route out of the harbor that had nothing to do with gates or bridges, and the letters would travel it sewn into ordinary cargo, duplicated so thoroughly that burning one copy would be like trying to burn the sea.

It was nearly dawn when they finished, and the light coming grey through the window put her in mind of the orders posted at the customs house — *all correspondence subject to search* — and how far on the other side of that net they now swam.

"We'll need to move within days," Edmund said. "Before the fleet sails."

"The fleet sails when it sails," Tamsin said, and then stopped, because a thought had surfaced in her that she didn't like — the cold professional instinct of a woman who had spent her life reading schedules the way other people read faces. The quarterly departure wasn't a tide. It was a decision, made by men, posted on a board. And men who felt paper moving beneath them had a habit of shortening the runway.

Down on the quay, faint through the loft floor, she heard the shell store open below them — Brack's boots on the stair, early, too early, and his voice calling up before he reached the landing, rough with news:

"You'd both best come down. There's a notice nailed to the customs house door, and you're not going to like the date on it."
