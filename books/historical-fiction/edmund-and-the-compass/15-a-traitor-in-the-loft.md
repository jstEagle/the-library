## 15. A Traitor in the Loft

The boy's name was Sam Hallow, though in the loft above Brack's shell-yard he was called nothing at all. He was called *boy*, and *fetch*, and *hold the lamp steady*, and he had learned to wear those names the way a crab wears its borrowed shell — as if they had always been his.

He was fifteen, small for it, with his dead mother's narrow face and his living uncle's habit of watching doors. He had worked for Brack since he was eleven, first hauling baskets of shell up from the barges, then running messages through the night market when the tide made the lanes impassable to grown men who could not fold themselves small. He knew every gap in the yard's palisade, every board in the loft floor that spoke under weight, and — most usefully, most profitably — every hour of the night when Brack drank rice spirit in the room below and slept like a felled tree until dawn.

That last piece of knowledge was what Calloway's man had paid for.

Not all of it. The man — soft-spoken, grey-gloved, smelling of ink and cloves — had come to him eleven days ago on the seawall, where Sam went to be alone with the gulls and the sound of water worrying the pilings. The man had known his name without asking. Had known about his mother, dead of the fever four years back. Had known that Brack paid him almost nothing, kept him out of what Brack would have called charity and what Sam called cheap labour, and that Sam had lately begun to understand the difference between the two.

"I don't want you to steal anything," the man had said, which was the first lie. "I only want you to look at some papers. Tell me what you see. Names, dates, anything written plain. A boy reads a page, no harm done in reading."

He'd put a coin in Sam's palm. Not a company coin. Silver, heavy, worn smooth by other palms before this one. It had felt warm, like something alive.

Now, eleven nights later, there were six such coins sewn into the lining of Sam's jacket, and the jacket lay folded beneath his sleeping mat like a sin buried shallow, and Sam lay awake above it listening to Brack snore through the floorboards and hating himself in the particular way of a person who has done arithmetic and found the sum correct but the method shameful.

Up here, in the loft where Brack stored his smuggled luxuries — bolts of printed cotton, tins of opium he swore he never touched, a crate of church candles bound for some congregation that did not officially exist — there were currently two guests. The stranger, Roe, quiet as furniture, who slept little and wrote much in a pocket book he thought nobody saw. And the diver woman, Thornbury, who had arrived two nights past smelling of the deep water and of fear, and whose pearls now sat in Brack's strongbox in trade for her silence and her bed among the crates.

And there were the letters.

Sam had watched them since the night the stranger arrived: a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred twine, carried close to the body like a wound. He had watched the diver read them aloud two nights ago, lamplight low, both their faces doing things faces should not do while reading paper. He had heard fragments through the gap where the loft's partition board had shrunk away from its frame — a gap wide enough for a boy's eye and narrower than a man's conscience.

He had heard the word *certificates*. He had heard the name *Quill*. He had heard the diver say, in a voice scraped down to nothing, *my father*.

Tonight the packet lay inside the false bottom of the candle crate, because Brack believed — correctly, as it happened — that no thief in the port would trouble himself to steal church candles.

Sam waited until the moon had gone off the water and the house below had settled into Brack's great tidal snoring. Then he rose from his mat in the corner behind the rope coils, where he slept when guests occupied the loft proper, and he crossed the boards he knew would not speak, three steps and a flat-footed pause and two steps more, and knelt at the candle crate with a sliver of tallow candle pinched in his fingers so the wax would not mark the lid.

The false bottom came up on finger-holds worn smooth by years of Brack's own hands. Beneath it, the oilcloth packet, exactly where it had lain for two days. Beside it, Brack's ledger of bribes and cargoes, which Sam had already copied in part and which the grey-gloved man wanted whole.

But it was not the ledger the man had spoken of last night, when he had found Sam again at the seawall, closer this time, his voice lower.

"The papers are moving," he had said. "They'll run the checkpoints before the fleet sails. When they do, my masters will need to know precisely what they're hunting. Every page. Every name. You understand me, boy? Not a summary. The thing itself, or a copy good enough to fool an expert."

"And if I'm caught?"

"You won't be caught. You're family." The man had smiled then, and it had been the worst part of the conversation, because the smile said: *family is a thing that can be bought, I am buying you, and you are letting me.* "Copy one page. The one with the names in a column. Bring it to the chandlery on Harrow Lane, ask for the price of beeswax, and you'll never see me again."

So now Sam knelt in the dark with the packet in his lap and his heart going like a shorebird's wings against his ribs, and he untied the tarred twine with fingers that had hauled shell since childhood and therefore did not tremble, though everything behind them did.

* * *

The letters smelled of salt and of another climate. Even in the dark, even unlit, he could feel the difference in them — the paper thinner than colonial stock, folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. There were perhaps twenty sheets. He counted by touch, because he dared not strike a light, and touch told him little except that some pages were covered edge to edge in a cramped, driving hand, and others bore the stamped formality of documents — certificates, he guessed, by the uniformity of their headings, the blanks filled in with that same driving hand.

*Forged,* the diver had said through the wall-gap, over and over, as if she were hammering nails into her own grief. *Every drowning forged.*

He chose a page from the middle of the packet, reasoning — with the cold practicality the grey-gloved man would have admired — that a middle page would be missed last, if at all. He took it and the little book of blank paper he had bought with the third coin from a stationer's shop that asked no questions, and he carried both to the window seat where the moonless dark was deepest, and he began to copy.

It was slow work by feel alone. After a quarter hour he risked shading the page with the shutter cracked one inch, letting in the yard lamp's far orange bleed, and the writing swam up at him like things surfacing from deep water.

A column of names. He had been right about that. Nine names, each followed by a date, each date followed by a smaller notation. He copied them stroke for stroke, patient as a copyist monk, and because he was fifteen and curious and had spent his life around men whose business was secrets, he read what he copied.

*Marlowe, J. — drowned 12th March 1845 — estate claimed, debt voided.*
*Okafor, S. — drowned 30th May 1845 — cargo seized, shares reassigned.*
*Quill, D. — drowned 17th August 1845—*

Beside Quill's name, in a different pressure of pen, someone had added a single line, pressed hard enough that Sam could feel the groove with his fingertip:

*Witnessed. He did not drown. Ask the tender crew of the Pelican.*

Below the names, the page continued in prose — accusations laid out with the terrible tidiness of a man who has decided his evidence will be read after he is beyond retaliation. Sam copied the first line of each paragraph and skipped the rest, because time was a hand closing on his neck. But the fragments he caught lodged in him like splinters: phrases about the factor, about certificates signed by a doctor who had died the following winter, about *the fleet schedule altered to bury inquiry* —

And one phrase, near the bottom, that stopped his pen entirely:

*If I go silent, trust no one inside the yard walls. Especially the young ones. They are the cheapest to buy and the easiest to deny.*

Sam looked at the sentence for a long moment. Then he looked across the loft at the shapes of the sleepers — Roe curled in his coat against the far crates, breathing light and uneven, a man who slept the way soldiers do, ready; and beyond him the diver, silent, still, her mat positioned between the door and the stranger with an instinct Sam recognized because he had the same one. Guarding. Always guarding.

Neither moved. Neither knew.

He finished the copy with a steadiness that frightened him more than trembling would have. He compared the two pages side by side in the thin light — his hand was good, better than the man expected, he knew that now — and then he folded the original back along Vane's creases, retied the tarred twine in the same pattern, and lowered the packet into the false bottom, and slid the lid home, and pinched wax over the screw-marks so the crate looked as untouched as a grave.

Except that it wasn't. Except that somewhere between the ninth name and the final paragraph, Sam Hallow had passed from being a boy who looked at papers to a boy who had helped hang men, and he understood this fully, and he did it anyway, because six silver coins sewn in a jacket lining are a kind of truth too, and truths do not stop being true because you are ashamed of them.

He crossed the silent boards back to his corner. He lay down. He listened to the strangers breathe.

In the morning, he told himself, he would take the page to Harrow Lane and be rid of it, and the man had promised he would never see him again, and promises made by men like that were worthless, but hope is not a rational animal and neither, at fifteen, with coins in his jacket, is a boy.

* * *

Morning came grey and busy, as mornings did in the shell-yard, and by the time Tamsin Thornbury climbed the ladder to the loft with bread and dried fish from the market, Sam Hallow was already gone on an errand Brack had shouted up at him — oysters for the noon meal, and mind the shells — and the errand was real, which was the beauty of it, and the boy was three streets gone toward Harrow Lane with a copied page folded flat beneath his shirt, and none of it had touched the morning's ordinary surface at all.

Tamsin found Edmund awake, sitting cross-legged amid the crates with the letters spread before him and a look on his face she was learning to read the way she read water before a dive: surface calm, undertow beneath.

"We should discuss the bridge," he said, instead of good morning. "Brack thinks the toll sergeant can be bought. I think he's wrong."

"Then we don't take the toll road."

"There's no other way across the estuary unless you grow gills."

She set the food down and sat opposite him, and for an hour they bent over the route together, arguing in the low, quick voices of people who have learned that privacy is a currency and they are nearly out of it. They discussed which papers went with whom, and how the evidence should be split so that no single seizure could end them. They discussed timing, and the fleet's departure, and whether Brack's shell barge could pass the second checkpoint on its legitimate papers without drawing the eye of a man looking for a reason to look.

They did not open the candle crate, because the letters were already spread between them. They did not count the pages, because why would anyone count pages they had counted twice already?

And so neither of them noticed that the stack was one sheet lighter than it had been the night before, and would not notice for days yet — days in which that absence would travel across the port in a boy's shirt, arrive at a chandlery on Harrow Lane, and pass across a desk smelling of ink and cloves, where a grey-gloved man would hold it to the light, compare it against nothing, and smile at the quality of the hand.

* * *

At the chandlery, the transaction took less than a minute.

"Price of beeswax?" the man asked from behind a shelf of ship's stores, not looking up.

"Twelve coppers the pound," Sam said. "Sixteen if it's the white."

The man set a wrapped parcel on the counter — a pound of ordinary beeswax, honestly weighed — and beneath it, a second purse, heavier than any before it. Their fingers did not touch. The man still had not looked at him directly, and Sam understood this was mercy of a kind, or contempt of a kind, and could not tell the difference and did not try.

"The page."

Sam passed it across beneath the parcel. The man unfolded it one-handed, scanned it, and something moved behind his face — satisfaction, but layered, like current over current. He read the marginal note beside Quill's name twice. He read the phrase about the tender crew of the Pelican. Then he refolded the page along its new creases and put it away inside his coat, and finally he looked up, and his eyes were pale and friendly and completely empty.

"Well done," he said. "You read it, of course."

Sam said nothing.

"That's fine. Reading is free." The man pushed the purse forward. "One more service, and you're done forever. Nothing to carry. Nothing to copy. Only to listen. When next they plan the crossing — the route, the hour, who carries what — you'll be near enough to hear it. You'll bring it to the seawall, and I'll find you. And then, Sam Hallow, you can buy yourself off that yard entire, and sail anywhere you like, and no one will ever call you *boy* again."

The purse sat on the counter between them, fat with futures.

Sam picked it up.

Outside, the harbour lay bright under the climbing sun, and the quarterly fleet rode at anchor in the roads like a herd of patient animals, and the tide turned on schedule, indifferent, carrying everything toward the sea. Sam walked back through the market lanes with the coins against his ribs and the copied names dissolving behind his eyes — Marlowe, Okafor, Quill, witnessed, he did not drown — and found that the words had already stopped feeling like words. That was the trick of it, he thought. That was how men like the grey-gloved man worked. They didn't make you cruel. They just gave the cruelty time to become arithmetic.

Back at the yard, Brack was bellowing about oysters, and the loft door stood open to the light, and inside it the diver and the stranger were laughing at something — actually laughing, briefly, like people who had forgotten for one second what was coming for them — and Sam Hallow climbed the ladder with an honest basket of shellfish in his hands and murder folded flat against his skin, and said, "Oysters, like you asked," in a clear young voice that no one in that room had any reason yet to doubt.
