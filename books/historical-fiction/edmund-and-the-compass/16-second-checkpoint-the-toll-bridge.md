## 16. Second Checkpoint: The Toll Bridge

*Tamsin*

They saw the bridge before they saw the guards, and the guards before they understood what the guards meant.

The toll bridge crossed the estuary at its narrowest throat, a spine of blackened timber and iron bolts thrown between the port side and the shell road beyond. Tamsin had crossed it a hundred times in her life — as a child carried on her mother's hip, as a diver hauling crates of shell toward the packing sheds, as a woman with a company chit that made the toll-keeper touch his hat. It had never once occurred to her that a bridge could be closed. A bridge was like water. You did not close water.

But it was closed.

A chain as thick as her wrist ran from rail to rail across the roadway, and behind it stood six men where ordinarily there would be one drowsing keeper and a boy to collect coppers. Lanterns hung along the span at intervals too regular for comfort, turning the whole structure into a lit corridor over dark water. Beyond the chain, a sergeant in company grey was reading something aloud from a paper he held close to a lantern flame, and the men around him were listening the way men listen when they have been promised money.

Edmund stopped walking so smoothly that she nearly walked into his back.

"Don't stop," he said quietly, without turning his head. "We're two travelers out for evening air. Keep moving."

"Moving where?" she murmured. "There's nowhere to go but through."

"There's always somewhere." His eyes were doing the thing she had begun to recognize — flicking, measuring, cataloguing exits the way she catalogued what could be carried. "The foreshore path. The oyster racks. We can walk north along the mud and look like lovers avoiding the toll."

"The mud is knee-deep and stinks of rot," she said. "And the toll-keeper's boy knows my face."

She said it flatly, because there was no softer way to say it. Edmund turned then, just enough to glance at her, and she watched him do the arithmetic she had already finished. Her face was known at this checkpoint. Not perhaps by name — the divers' children blurred together in company memory, one brown body among many — but known. She had walked this bridge twice a week for eleven years. She had sold the toll-keeper's wife oysters off her own boat. If any man behind that chain looked at her longer than a breath, he would say: *that's Thornbury's girl, the diver.*

And they were hunting a diver's child. She knew it the way you know weather coming — not because anyone had told her, but because of how the sergeant held his paper, and how the lanterns had been hung, and how the guards were not watching carts but *faces*, turning each traveler briefly toward the light like pearls held up to be graded.

"They've got a page," Edmund said. "Calloway's copied something. That's what he's reading."

"How can you know that?"

"Because no sergeant reads departure orders at sunset with six men and a chain across the road." His jaw tightened. "I gave them my name at the first checkpoint. My real name. I told myself it wouldn't travel fast enough." He exhaled through his nose. "It traveled."

* * *

*Edmund*

He had made a mistake and the mistake was standing forty yards away holding a lantern, and all Edmund could think, absurdly, was of Vane telling him once — years ago, in the forecastle of a ship whose name he'd sworn never to repeat — *a lie is a cargo, Roe. It has weight, it shifts in bad weather, and if you don't lash it down proper it will capsize you.* He had lashed nothing down. He had handed a sergeant his true name like a coin across a counter and walked away feeling clever.

Now the bill had come due at a bridge.

"We split," Tamsin said.

He looked at her. In the last light she was all edges — cheekbone, jaw, the tendon standing in her neck — and utterly calm, which frightened him more than panic would have. Panic he could work with. Calm like hers meant she had already been afraid somewhere deeper down and had come back with a decision.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard the plan."

"I don't need to. Whatever it is, splitting up is the worst of it."

"It's the only part of it." She nodded toward the water beneath the span, where the estuary ran deep and green-black between the pilings, the current sliding seaward with the ebb. "I go under. You go over."

"Under." He said the word as though tasting something spoiled. "Through that current. At dusk. With the letters—"

"With half the letters." She was already unbuttoning her jacket. Beneath it, against her shift, the oilcloth packet lay flat as a second skin; he had watched her wrap it that morning with the same unhurried precision she brought to everything, waxing the folds, testing the knots. Now she unwound it, and the letters came apart in her hands into two bundles, and she held one out to him. "You take Vane's account of the certificates. Names, dates, the factor's signatures. I take the ship manifests and the death rolls. If either of us crosses, Calloway loses."

"And if neither of us crosses?"

"Then we were fools to try, and the fleet sails, and it won't matter what happens to us." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, and he understood suddenly that this was not bravery. It was bookkeeping. She had weighed her life against the cargo and found the cargo heavier, and she had done it so quickly and so completely that there was nothing left in her face to argue with.

"Tamsin." He kept his voice low. The nearest guard was thirty yards off, bored, watching a cart. "That water is cold and it's running ebb and the pilings are furred with weed and barnacles. Divers die under bridges. They die in *good* conditions."

"Yes," she said. "Sometimes. Mostly they die because they dive alone with no one tending their line." She looked at him then, and for the first time since the customs house — since that first duel of omissions over a table and a broken compass — something moved behind her eyes that was not calculation. "So don't let me dive alone. Get across, get past the chain, get to the far pilings, and tend my line. That's your whole task. Can you do that much?"

He thought about saying no. He thought about the honest answer, which was that he could not swim well, that he had spent his life on decks and in counting-houses and had never once gone willingly beneath water, and that the idea of standing on a piling in the dark waiting for a woman who might never surface was a thing he did not have the stomach for.

"Yes," he said. "That much I can do."

"Good." She pressed his bundle of letters inside his shirt, flat against his chest, and her hands were steady and quick and very cold from the evening air. "Oilcloth. Same as mine. If they take you, they take you — eat it if you must, or drop it in the water, but never let it be found on you dry."

"And you? If they catch you in the water?"

"They won't." She smiled, and it was the worst smile he had ever seen, because it was the smile of someone reassuring a child. "I've been holding my breath since I was six years old, Edmund. This estuary raised me. It won't give me up to a sergeant with a lantern."

* * *

*Tamsin*

The hardest part was the waiting.

She crouched in the shadow of the last oyster rack on the near shore, jacket folded beneath her, and watched Edmund walk toward the toll line with the loose-shouldered amble of a man with nowhere particular to be. He had taken off his ruined boots and slung them over his shoulder — barefoot travelers were common enough on the shell road, pilgrims and beachcombers and the occasional destitute clerk — and he had rubbed mud on his shirt collar until he looked like what he was pretending to be: nobody. He was better at being nobody than she had expected. Perhaps everyone from away was. Perhaps that was the one skill the company left you when it took everything else.

At the chain, a guard raised his lantern. Tamsin saw the light swing across Edmund's face, saw him squint and scratch and mumble, saw the guard wave him toward the toll-keeper's shed with the contemptuous flick of a man waving off a beggar. One copper changed hands. Then a second guard stepped in — the one with the paper, the sergeant — and said something, and Edmund laughed at whatever it was, a big easy laugh she had never heard from him, and the moment stretched, and stretched, and Tamsin discovered she had stopped breathing entirely.

Then the sergeant waved him on too, and Edmund shuffled across the planks into the lamplight and out the far side, and was gone among the shell-road carts, and she let her breath out in a long silent stream and hated herself for how much of her relief was not about the letters at all.

*Don't,* she told herself. *He's a courier. You're a courier. Couriers don't watch each other walk away.*

She slid into the water without a splash.

Cold took her first — the shock of it closing around her ribs like a hand — and then the familiar work began, the discipline of a lifetime asserting itself over the animal panic. Breathe down. Three long draws, filling the blood with air the way her mother had taught her before she'd taught her anything else. Relax the hands. Drop the shoulders. The current here ran hard with the ebb, dragging south toward the sea, and she angled upstream of the bridge so the water itself would carry her under the span rather than pin her against the pilings.

The world below was sound and pressure. The estuary at dusk was a green-black country with its own weather: the groan of the bridge timbers transmitted through the water into her bones, the tick and scrape of crabs on the piling weed, the vast slow heartbeat of the tide. She swam down the face of the third piling, following it like a wall in a dark house, and the letters rode against her sternum, oil-wrapped and warm from her skin, and she thought — insanely, in the place where the mind goes when the body is working — *this is the most valuable thing I have ever carried, and it weighs less than a pearl.*

Halfway under the span, her air began to burn.

She had counted on forty strokes. The ebb was faster than she'd judged, and the far pilings were farther than they looked, and at stroke twenty-five the burning started in her chest and at stroke thirty it climbed into her throat and began to speak to her in her mother's voice: *let go, let the water have you, it's easier, everything is easier if you stop fighting it.* She had heard that voice before, three years ago, on the outer bed, when her line had fouled and the world had gone to sparks. She had beaten it then by counting. She counted now. Barnacles under her fingers. One. The groan of timber overhead. Two. The green-black light thinning toward the far shore. Three.

Her hand broke the surface at the far piling and closed on rope — an actual rope, a mooring line, frayed and salt-stiff and the most beautiful thing she had ever touched — and she hung there in the eddy behind the piling with her head barely above water, dragging air in ragged silent gulps, while forty feet above her the lanterns of the sealed bridge swung and the voices of the guards drifted down like weather from another country.

"—diver's child, that's the order, anyone young and brown off the water, you check the boats—"

"Ain't seen a boat all evening, Sarge."

"Then check the *water*, you fool, that's what divers use—"

Laughter. The laughter of men who had been promised money and given a cold bridge instead. Tamsin pressed her forehead against the piling and breathed, and breathed, and did not laugh, because if she started she might not stop, and the sound would carry across the water like a gull's cry, and gulls' cries were exactly what the sergeant was listening for.

* * *

*Edmund*

On the far bank, Edmund discovered that crossing a bridge was the easy part; now he had to be a man loitering at the end of a bridge for no reason, in bare feet, in the dark, within sight of six armed men.

He bought it with theater. He sat on a bollard and worked his boots back onto his salt-swollen feet with much grimacing, cursed fluently at the blisters, asked a passing carter whether the ferry at the upper crossing still ran on Sundays, and generally assembled himself into a nuisance so thoroughly that the guards' attention slid off him like rain off oilcloth. All the while his eyes worked the water below the span, reading the black surface for the thing he both prayed for and dreaded.

She surfaced at the far piling after an eternity measured in heartbeats, and he nearly stood up and shouted with relief, and instead he stood up and limped — convincingly, he hoped — down the shell road away from the bridge, drawing the eye of the nearest guard with the sheer visible misery of his walking, giving her the shore-side darkness to climb into.

She caught up with him a quarter mile on, at the bend where the road left the water and climbed toward the shell beds, wet to the waist, shivering so hard her teeth clicked, and grinning.

"You're mad," he said. "You're completely—" He didn't have a word. He gestured at all of her, the dripping, the shaking, the grin.

"So are you," she said. "You laughed at that sergeant. Where did the laugh come from?"

"I haven't the faintest idea. I think it was Vane's laugh. I seem to have inherited it along with the letters." He took off his coat and put it around her shoulders without asking, and she let him, which was its own kind of answer, and they walked on together into the dark with half an archive each pressed against their skins.

Behind them the bridge burned its little row of lights against the night, hunting a diver's child who was already a mile gone, carrying the drowned man's truth toward the fleet gate in two halves — terror on one side of the water, trust on the other, and between them, all the way along the estuary road, the sound of the tide going out to tell the sea what it had carried.

Neither of them saw the boy on the shell road ahead, small and quick, slipping off toward the port with a copied page folded in his shirt — the page that would tell Calloway, by morning, exactly what his hunters had failed to hold.
