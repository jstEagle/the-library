## 17. Under the Estuary

The estuary at low slack was a different country than the sea.

Tamsin had known this water since before she had known her own name — the brown-green murk where the river's silt met the tide's salt, the way light died three fathoms down instead of ten, the cold that came up through the mud like a hand closing around the ankles. Diving the pearl beds was work. Diving here would be something older and meaner: a negotiation with a place that had never once been paid to be kind.

She crouched in the reeds on the northern bank, a quarter mile downstream of the toll bridge, and unwrapped the oilcloth package one last time. The letters inside were stiff with lamp-black grease, sealed into a second skin of waxed linen, flat as a slate against her ribs when she pressed them there. Brack's man had done the wrapping, muttering measurements — *across the chest, under the arms, she'll need to breathe high* — with the bored competence of someone who had wrapped worse things for worse crossings. It held fast now when she tested it, two fingers hooked under the top edge, pulling hard enough to hurt. Good. If it came loose down there, it would not come loose gently, and she would feel it tear away and be powerless to follow it into the dark.

*Fourteen feet under the pilings,* Edmund had said, pacing out the map of the bridge from memory of the ferryman's gossip. *Thirty yards of open water beneath it, current slack no more than twenty minutes, maybe less if the wind holds from the east.* He had wanted, with an earnestness that almost made her laugh, to come up with some other way. A cart. A bribe. A disguise. All the inventions of a man who had lived his life above the waterline.

"There is no other way," she had told him, not unkindly. "There is only the way my body knows."

Now, alone in the reeds with the mosquitoes singing their thin evening hymn, she let herself say it again, quietly, as if swearing to it. There was only what her body knew. Everything else — the fear climbing her throat, the image of the guards' lanterns swinging along the bridge above, the memory of that copied page traveling toward Calloway like a splinter working toward the heart — all of it belonged to the surface. She would leave it there, folded in her clothes with her boots.

She stripped to her shift, bound her hair, worked a thin cap of tallow and wax through it the way the divers did against the cold. Her knife stayed on shore. A knife was weight, and weight was breath, and tonight breath was the only currency she could not earn back once spent.

The letters went on last. She pressed the package flat beneath her left breast, checked the ties twice, then stood in the shallows and breathed.

Not the panicked breathing of a woman about to do something frightening — she knew that rhythm and despised it, quick shallow sips that only told the body to be afraid. She breathed the way her mother had taught her on the shingle behind the diving huts when she was six years old, before her mother stopped teaching anything at all: in through the nose for four beats of the heart, hold for four, out for eight, until the pulse slowed and the world narrowed to a single clean column of air.

Three times. On the third exhale she bent forward, pulled herself under with two long strokes, and let the estuary close over her head like a door.

---

Down was silence, but not stillness.

The slack was lying to her already — she felt it in the first five strokes, a slow lateral drag against her body, the tide beginning its turn early, impatient. The water tasted of mud and rotted pilings and, faintly, of the tar they painted on the bridge footings every spring, a bitter mineral ghost on the back of the tongue. Visibility was an arm's length and a lie. She swam by dead reckoning and by the pale smudge of the far bank, keeping the bridge's shadow a black bar ahead of her, growing.

Her mother's voice was there, the way it always was underwater — not warm, not kind, but exact. *Head down. Hands quiet. Waste nothing.* Tamsin had spent years resenting those lessons for being the only inheritance she'd been given, and could not spend even one heartbeat on that resentment now. Every stroke was arithmetic. Thirty yards across. Fourteen feet down to clear the pilings' apron of stone, then up under the bridge deck where the guards' lanterns could not reach. Twelve strokes down, twenty across, fifteen up, if her body kept the bargain.

At eight strokes her ears began their complaint, pressure building in the deepening channel, and she pinched her nose and equalized without breaking rhythm, a small violence she had performed ten thousand times. At twelve she leveled out over the stone apron and felt the bottom's cold rise to meet her, and below her, in the black, something shifted — a crab, a log, a fish the size of a prayer. She did not look. Looking cost breath, and breath was running a ledger of its own now, debits accruing with every stroke, no credit coming until the surface broke.

The bridge's shadow swallowed her.

It was darker than night under there, because night has edges and this had none — a blackness with weight, pressing on the eyes, on the chest, on the letters lashed against her ribs. Somewhere above, muffled through timber and water, boots crossed the planking. A voice said something she couldn't parse. A lantern's glow came down through the gaps between boards as faint trembling coins of light, drifting past her face as she swam beneath them, so that she passed through a rain of gold that touched nothing.

*They are looking down at the road,* she told herself. *Nobody looks down at the water. The company has never once looked down.*

The thought should have steadied her. Instead it brought her father with it, unbidden — her father going down through just such dark water with just such certainty that nobody was watching, and the water keeping him. She had been eleven. She had not been allowed to dive for two seasons after, and when she finally went under again her mother had stood on the shingle with her arms crossed and her face like a locked door, and neither of them had ever spoken of it since.

Panic arrived then, quietly, the way it does for divers who have grown careless about grief. Not a scream. A small tight fist closing around the base of her throat, a whisper: *the water keeps people, and you have brought it exactly the thing it might want.*

Her lungs answered the whisper before her mind could. A spasm — shallow, involuntary, the body demanding air it did not yet need but suddenly, terribly wanted. Her stroke shortened. Her hands, which had been quiet, began to hurry.

*Head down. Hands quiet. Waste nothing.*

She forced the count. Four beats in — she had no air to take, so she took the count itself, made her heart keep the time instead of her chest. Eight beats out against closed lips. The fist at her throat loosened by a finger's width. Below her the apron stone fell away as she reached the deep of the channel, fourteen feet, the pressure sitting on her chest now like a child, like a debt, and she hauled herself upward through it toward the far shadow of the bank, fifteen strokes, she would count them, counting was a rope and she would climb it.

One. Two. Three.

Somewhere around seven, the darkness changed its mind.

It happened the way it always happened at the edge of a blackout — not gradually but all at once, the water going soft and warm and reasonable, her limbs deciding the swimming was optional, a sweet gray fog rolling in from the corners of her vision with the gentle insistence of a tide. Her mother's voice cut through it once, sharp as gull-cry: *You do not get to want air. You get to have discipline.* And Tamsin — eleven years old again, furious, grieving, stubborn past all sense — reached down inside herself and found the thing her mother had actually built in her all those winters on the shingle, which was not calm at all, but spite. Spite for the estuary. Spite for the company that priced her lungs by the season. Spite for every ledger ever kept that weighed a person in shell and called the number fair.

She would not give this water anything. Not her breath, not her name, not the drowned man's letters.

Nine. Ten. Eleven. Her hands broke into warmer water. Thirteen, and the pale blur of reeds hung above her like a promise. She did not surge — surging was how divers died in the last five feet, spending everything too soon — she rose through the last of it with the same maddening economy, and her head came out into the cool night air, and the first breath was the loudest sound she had ever heard in her life.

She floated on her back among the reeds for a long moment, gasping in careful increments, letting the sky spin slowly overhead. Stars, wheeling. The bridge behind her, still burning its little lights. Her own heart hammering out its arithmetic: alive, alive, alive.

Against her ribs, flat and greasy and whole, the letters pressed with each beat as if they too had a pulse now. She had carried them. The water had not kept them, and it had not kept her.

*Two halves,* she thought. *I am one half. God help the other.*

---

Getting out of the water was its own slow ceremony. She waded through the reed shallows rather than standing upright, in case the far bank had eyes she hadn't counted, and dressed behind a screen of sedge with fingers that shook and would not obey. The shaking annoyed her. The crossing was done; the fear was supposed to be done with it. But the body kept its own books, and hers were still balancing hours of terror against minutes of competence, and apparently the terror intended to collect for some time yet.

She wrung out her hair, worked the tallow from it as best she could, and pulled on her coat. The oilcloth package came off her chest at last and she held it a moment in both hands — lighter than a pearl shell, heavier than a ship — before wrapping it in the dry cloth Brack had provided and stowing it in the false bottom of her market basket, beneath a layer of oysters bought yesterday for exactly this camouflage.

The plan was simple, the plans that survived contact with the world rarely being the ones drawn in daylight: walk the towpath north, avoid the port roads until the last mile, reach the shell barge by the second bell of the night watch. Edmund would cross the bridge above her, wearing the forged clerk's pass, carrying his half of the evidence sewn into the lining of his coat. They would meet in Brack's loft, and the letters would be whole again, and tomorrow they would begin the impossible arithmetic of the fleet gate.

She was fifty yards up the towpath when she heard the voices behind her.

Lantern light swung along the bank she'd just left — not one lantern but three, moving fast, and the voices carried flat across the water the way voices did on a damp night: men calling to each other with the particular energy of a search that had just found something. She pressed into the sedge. From the darkness across the channel came the ring of boots on the bridge planking, and then a shout, wordless at this distance but shaped unmistakably like *hold him*, and her whole body turned to iron.

Because the figure the lanterns were closing on, the figure running the plank-line of the northern bank with his coat flapping and his half of the evidence still sewn against his heart, was not a stranger.

She knew the set of those shoulders. She knew the rehearsed calm that had abandoned him now, leaving him simply a man running in bad boots.

Edmund had missed the bridge. Or the bridge had missed him — the pass refused, the line sealed tighter than planned, the sergeant asking questions in the wrong key. Whatever had happened on the toll road, it had thrown him down onto the bank, onto *her* bank, into the path of the search party, and the lanterns were gaining, and the estuary lay between them, wide and black and patient, offering exactly one way across that did not end in Calloway's hands.

Tamsin looked at the water. Then at the lanterns. Then at the basket on her arm, where the whole truth of Captain Aldous Vane sat wrapped in oilskin among the oysters — the half that must not be caught, whatever became of the rest.

"Damn you," she said softly, to Edmund, to the tide, to the entire architecture of the night. And she turned and ran — not toward the barge, not toward safety, but back down along the bank, toward the narrowest point of the channel and the water she had sworn, five minutes ago, she would not enter twice.
