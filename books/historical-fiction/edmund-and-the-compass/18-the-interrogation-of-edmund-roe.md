## 18. The Interrogation of Edmund Roe

The line at the toll bridge moved the way grief moves — in fits, with long stillnesses between the hurts. Edmund stood in it and let himself be carried forward a cart-length at a time, counting the things that could hang him.

The forged clerk's pass in his breast pocket. The satchel with its false bottom, holding the smaller half of Aldous Vane's evidence. His face, which two days ago had been no one's business and was now, he suspected, sketched from description on a board somewhere behind that tollhouse window.

And Tamsin, somewhere below the bridge deck, beneath the brown water of the estuary, carrying the larger half against her skin.

He could not think about Tamsin. Thinking about her was a luxury shaped like an open grave. He thought instead about the pass — its paper, its ink, its small catalog of lies. *Edmund Roe, junior clerk, mercantile audit division, traveling under company writ to reconcile toll ledgers at the estuary station.* Brack's forger had done honest work with dishonest materials. The seal was good. The handwriting was good. What was not good — what Edmund had realized only at midnight, lying awake in the shell barge listening to the tide count itself out — was that Edmund Roe did not exist, had never existed, and would be asked to prove it.

A head in front of him, a drover argued about his cattle levy and lost. The guards waved him through with the bored contempt of men whose power was too small to enjoy and too large to surrender. Beyond the tollgate, the bridge stretched four hundred feet of salt-grayed timber over water the color of old coins. At the far end, a second checkpoint. Between them, nowhere to go but across.

"Next."

* * *

The sergeant's name was Hollis. Edmund knew this before he reached the table because Hollis had announced it to the drover — *Sergeant Hollis, company toll authority, move along* — with the pride of a man who had been someone unimportant elsewhere and had found, here, a place where unimportance could be worn like rank. He was fifty perhaps, thick through the shoulders in the way of men who had once hauled rope, with a face that had decided long ago to stop spending effort on expression.

"Purpose of crossing." Not looking up. Looking at a ledger.

Edmund set the pass on the table, squared to the edge, the way a man squares documents who handles documents all day. "Junior clerk, audit division," he said. "Reconciliation of the toll ledgers. There's a variance flagged for the quarter."

That was the trick of it, Brack had said. Don't bring papers to a guard. Bring work. A man with a task is a man with a reason to be elsewhere; a man without one is a question, and questions get searched.

Hollis looked up. Looked at the pass. Picked it up with two fingers as though it might be damp.

"Audit sends a clerk alone? No escort?"

"The escort costs more than the variance." Edmund let a fraction of weariness into it — the universal currency of clerks. "Fourteen pounds, eleven shillings. That's the flag. I'm to find where it went and write down whose fault it is, and I can tell you now nobody at this station is going to enjoy my report."

It was a gamble, the small insult. But small insults were the weather of bureaucracy; refusing one would have been stranger than offering it.

Hollis grunted. Something that might have been amusement moved behind his eyes and was evicted. He turned the pass over. The seal caught the gray light.

"Roe," he read. "Edmund Roe."

"Yes."

"Hold out your hand."

For one cold instant Edmund thought: *they know.* Then Hollis tapped his own wrist. "Chit. Audit clerks carry chits. Tollman's brother-in-law works the customs house, says you people are marked by the wrist. Ink stamp, quarterly renewal."

There was no such chit. There was no such stamp. Edmund knew this because there could be no such thing — Hollis had invented it, or half-invented it, testing the shape of a lie the way Edmund was testing the shape of his own. Two liars at one table, each waiting for the other to blink first.

Edmund laughed. It came out almost right — dry, brief, the laugh of a man sharing a joke at his own institution's expense. "That's the harbor office's little joke," he said. "They proposed the stamps last year. The division refused. Said it made auditors look like convicts." He extended his hand anyway, palm open, empty. "You're welcome to look. If you find a mark, I'll eat the pass."

Hollis looked at the palm. Long enough. Longer than comfort. Somewhere behind Edmund, a cart creaked and a voice muttered about the wait, and the muttering was a kind of music because every second of crowd-impatience was pressure on Hollis to be quick.

"No mark," Hollis said. "No stamp."

"Told you." Edmund withdrew the hand. His pulse was in his throat, ticking like a watch he couldn't stop. "The variance, Sergeant. Fourteen eleven. Do you keep your own toll book, or does the station master—"

"Sit down."

* * *

Sitting was worse than standing. Sitting implied duration. Edmund sat on the stool beside the tollhouse table while Hollis fetched a ledger bound in cracked green leather, and beyond the window the estuary ran wide and slow toward the sea, and Edmund made himself not search the pilings for a swimmer.

She had gone in upstream, Brack's boy had told him, at the willow bend, a quarter mile above the bridge. Oil-cloth wrap, three turns, tied flat. She'd done it a hundred times, she said — deeper, colder, darker, for shells worth less than paper. *Don't watch for me,* she'd said, pulling her suit closed at the throat. *Watching makes you stupid. I've seen it.*

So he didn't watch. He watched Hollis's thumb walk down columns of figures instead.

"Variance," Hollis said at last. "Fourteen eleven." A pause. "Funny figure."

"Funny how?"

"Funny precise. Most variances come in round numbers. Somebody steals a round number. Somebody reports a round number. Nobody flags fourteen pounds eleven shillings unless they want the flag to look like arithmetic instead of accusation." Hollis closed the ledger. Folded his hands on it. For the first time he looked at Edmund fully, and his face, which had spent the interview being furniture, became a door opening onto a room Edmund could not see. "Who wrote your pass, clerk?"

"The division secretary."

"Name?"

"You'd have it in your registry if it mattered." Keep the weariness. Weariness was armor. "Sergeant, I've crossed this bridge six times in two years doing quarterly reconciliation. Your predecessor — Corrigan, big fellow, whistled badly — never asked me for anything but the time of day. Is there a new standing order I haven't seen posted? Because if there is, I'll need to note it, and if there isn't, there are eight carts behind me and a drover up front who already hates both of us."

It was a risk built of true things, which was the only kind of lie that held weight. There *had* been a Corrigan; Edmund had watched him whistle through the line two days ago, before the seals doubled and the faces changed. And the mention of change landed, because change was the one thing every man at every checkpoint had noticed and none had been permitted to discuss.

Hollis's jaw worked. "New orders since the quarter began. Passenger correspondence restrictions. You'd know about that too, wouldn't you, audit man."

"I know what's printed on the customs house wall, same as everyone."

"And what's printed on the customs house wall says any traveler may be detained for inspection of papers and person." Hollis stood. The stool legs shrieked. "Stand up. Arms out."

* * *

The search was thorough in the way of men paid to be thorough rather than men who cared. Hands under the arms, down the ribs, along the seams of the coat. Edmund kept his breathing at the pace Vane had taught him fifteen years ago, on another ocean entirely: *the body believes what the breath tells it.* In. Four counts. Out. Six. The satchel passed from Hollis to a private and back. The false bottom held. Papers of genuine dullness — audit schedules, a columned sheet, a receipt for boot repair — emerged into daylight and were found boring, which was their entire purpose, and were put back.

Then Hollis held the pass up to the window light.

Edmund had known this moment was coming since dawn and had rehearsed nothing for it, because rehearsal showed. The light came through the cheap paper and the watermark bloomed there — a merchant house's anchor-and-crown, correct, purchased from Brack's forger at a price that had made Tamsin inhale — and Hollis studied it the way a man studies a coin he suspects but hopes is honest.

"Good seal," Hollis said.

"It should be. It cost the division enough."

"Mm." Hollis lowered the pass. He did not give it back. "Here's my difficulty, Mr. Roe. This morning I had a circular. Describes a man. Height, build, coloring, accent. Arrived by packet some weeks back, asking after a dead captain. Now a clerk comes through my line who fits the description close enough to make me squint, carrying papers good enough to make me squint harder." He laid the pass flat on the table and set his palm on it. "Close enough isn't enough. I'll not detain a company man on a maybe — I've been a maybe myself, and I know what it costs. But I'll have your particulars for the day book. Full name. Age. Last station. In case a question comes later, and questions do come later."

*In case a question comes later.* Every instinct Edmund owned stood up at once and said *run*, and running was impossible, and the impossible thing had a schedule, and the schedule was a fleet sailing in however many days remained, and the letters were under the water or across it by now or they were not.

Give him the false name. Give him *Roe*, whole and entire, the fiction complete: Edmund Roe, born Deptford, age thirty-four, last station the Penzance annex. Let the lie stand on its own legs.

But the false name was on the pass, and if the pass was ever checked against anything — and passes were checked against things when men like Calloway grew suspicious, when circulars circulated, when sergeants wrote names into day books and day books traveled — then a mismatch between pass and testimony would be the single thread that unraveled everything. Consistency was the whole architecture of the fraud. Break one beam and the roof came down.

So he gave the truth. He heard himself do it, distantly, as though watching a man on the far side of the tollhouse make a terrible, reasonable error.

"Edmund," he said. "Edmund Roe."

Not even the full truth. A half-truth wearing truth's clothes — his real Christian name bolted to a forged surname, which felt somehow more corrupt than either pure thing. But it came out smooth, unhesitating, and Hollis wrote it down.

"Age?"

"Thirty-five."

"Last station?"

"Coastal circuit. South-west run."

Hollis wrote. The pen scratched. Each scratch was a grain of sand going into an hourglass Edmund could not see the top of. When it was done, Hollis blotted the entry, closed the day book, and — finally, with a small ceremonial reluctance, a toll collected in dignity — slid the pass back across the table.

"Reconcile your ledgers, Mr. Roe."

"I always do, Sergeant."

Edmund walked out onto the bridge.

* * *

Four hundred feet of timber, and he felt every plank. The wind off the estuary pushed at his back like a hand that wanted him gone. Halfway across he permitted himself — just once, a debt repaid — to look down and over the rail, at the water sliding green-brown and opaque between the pilings, and to think *she's through, she has to be through, she said don't watch so I'm not watching,* and then to stop thinking it, because she was right about watching.

At the far checkpoint the guards glanced at the pass, glanced at him, and waved him through with the contempt reserved for paperwork. He did not stop walking until the bridge was behind him and the road bent inland through gorse and wind-stunted pines, and even then he walked another mile before his legs agreed to stop.

Then he stopped, and leaned against a fence post, and was sick, quietly and thoroughly, into the ditch. Fear leaving the body by the servant's entrance, Vane would have said. Nothing to be ashamed of. Everything to be ashamed of, actually, but none of it was the sickness.

Because the error was already walking. He could feel it moving behind him at the speed of a written line. *Edmund, Edmund Roe, clerk, thirty-five, south-west circuit.* In a day book. Under a date. Hollis had seemed satisfied — seemed — but satisfaction was not the same as belief, and a man who suspects a maybe and cannot act on it files the maybe away, and files breed. Sooner or later someone would lay the day book beside the circular. Sooner or later someone would notice that the fictional clerk's Christian name matched the hunted man's, because Edmund had been fool enough to flinch from inconsistency straight into recognition. One word. Five letters. A gift wrapped and handed to Calloway's machinery, and no way on God's road to take it back.

He had gotten through. That was the victory. He turned it over, examining it for the flaw he knew was in it, the way Tamsin examined shell for the crack before she priced it.

He had gotten through by giving away the one thing he'd sworn to protect.

The road ran on ahead, inland, toward the smoke of the port, toward Brack's loft and whatever waited there — Tamsin, alive or not; the letters, saved or lost in the estuary's brown silence; the fleet, sailing sooner now, everyone whispered, sooner than anyone had planned for. He pushed off the fence post and started walking, and with every step the mistake kept pace with him like a dog he couldn't lose.

By the time the first rooftops showed through the trees, he had settled it: tell Tamsin first, before anyone, before Brack, before the boy. Tell her plainly, because she counted things by what they weighed and she deserved to know the true weight of what he'd done. *I gave them my name.* Four words, and every one of them heavy enough to sink them both.

Behind him, far back along the road, faint in the afternoon light, a rider left the tollbridge station at a trot. Carrying nothing visible. Carrying, folded inside his coat, a page torn from a day book.

Edmund did not see him. He was already through the trees, and the port had swallowed him, and the name was traveling.
