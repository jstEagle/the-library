## 19. Reunion in the Shell Barge

Tamsin came up out of the water on the far bank of the estuary like something the river had decided against keeping.

Her hands found the mud, then the reed roots, then the iron rungs of an old mooring ladder no one had used since before she was born. She climbed with arms that shook and did not complain — shaking was the body's arithmetic, nothing more, and she had long ago stopped arguing with arithmetic. The oil-wrapped packet was still lashed against her ribs. She could feel it there, a second heartbeat, slightly warmer than her own.

The far side of the estuary was all salt marsh and shell middens, country the company had never bothered to claim because nothing here could be taxed. She walked the last stretch barefoot, having lost one boot somewhere under the pilings, and she catalogued the loss without grief: *one boot, lace torn, sole thin anyway. Replaceable. Nothing replaceable about what I'm carrying.*

Brack's shell barge sat low in a creek off the marsh, half-sunk by design, its hold stacked with mother-of-pearl that stank sweetly of rot and shine. She smelled it before she saw it — the particular breath of a place where shells go to be sorted from their dead.

Edmund was not there.

She told herself the absence meant nothing. He had the toll line to cross; he had papers, forged or otherwise; he had a way of walking through doors as if he'd been invited. Men like that arrived late, not never.

She told herself this twice, and then a third time, and each telling cost more than the last.

* * *

Edmund counted his steps across the toll bridge because counting kept the fear in a ledger where it could be audited.

The sergeant had his name now. His real name, spoken aloud into the wet air like a coin dropped down a well: *Roe.* He turned the mistake over as he walked, examining every face of it. He had rehearsed the clerk's pass, the bored voice, the story about company correspondence bound for the factor's office — and all of it had worked, all of it had held, right up until the sergeant asked, almost kindly, *And who shall I say crossed tonight?* and Edmund, exhausted and flush with victory, answered honestly for the first time all day.

Honesty, he thought, is what happens to careful men at the end of long days.

He cut off the road a mile past the bridge and moved through the cane fields the way Vane had taught him years ago, in another life: heel first, weight slow, watch the birds. The birds told him nothing was wrong. The birds, he reflected, were the only sentries he trusted anymore.

It was full dark when he smelled the shells, and heard Tamsin's voice come out of the dark ahead of him, low and level:

"Stop there. Say something true."

He stood still, hands open at his sides though she couldn't see them yet. "I gave a dead man's sergeant my living name."

A pause. Then, quietly, with something that might have been relief wearing anger's clothes: "Get in the boat, you fool."

* * *

The loft above the shell hold was lit by a single lamp shuttered so it showed only downward. Brack's boy had been sent home hours ago — Brack himself had done the sending, with a look on his face that Edmund couldn't read and didn't try to. The smuggler left them bread, dried fish, and a bottle of something that tasted the way rope smells, and then went below to sleep among his cargo, which Edmund suspected was the only place Brack truly felt watched over.

Tamsin sat wrapped in a blanket that smelled of brine, her hair drying in salt-stiffened ropes. Between them on the floorboards lay the oilskin packet, unwrapped now, the letters squared and counted. Forty-one pages. All present. She had laid them out the way she laid out everything — in rows, measured, accounted for — and Edmund understood it was her way of putting the world back in order after the day had tried to take it apart.

"You nearly drowned," he said.

"I nearly blacked out. Different thing. Drowning takes longer." She said it flatly, but her hands were not steady, and he saw that she was holding them still by force of will alone, the way a person holds a knife over a flame to prove they can. "You gave your name away."

"Yes."

"To a sergeant who will write it down."

"Who will remember it," Edmund corrected. "Men like him don't need paper. They keep a room in their heads with names pinned to the walls." He leaned back against the wall. The wood was cold through his shirt. "I want to tell you how it happened."

"Do I have to forgive you after?"

"No."

"Then tell me."

So he told her. The whole of it — the checkpoint, the forged pass, the long minutes of performance while the sergeant read and reread his papers, the moment the man handed them back and said *go on then* and something in Edmund's chest unlocked like a door swinging wide, and out of that open door came the truth, unbidden: *Edmund Roe.* He described it the way he'd been turning it over all evening — not as a lapse but as a hunger. Two months of false names. Two months of being nobody on purpose. And when the danger passed, some part of him had wanted, just once, to be somebody again, even if the somebody was hunted.

"That's the stupidest reason I've ever heard," Tamsin said.

"It's the most honest one I've ever had."

She was quiet a moment. Then she laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, but a laugh. "All right. That I understand." She pulled the blanket tighter. "My turn, then."

She confessed the way she did everything else: efficiently, without decoration. She told him about the moment under the pilings, halfway through the crossing, when the cold had closed around her chest like a hand and her body had said, quite clearly and in a voice older than language, *stop*. She had almost obeyed. Three seconds more of obedience and she would have breathed water. What saved her wasn't courage. It was the count. Her mother's count, taught to her at seven years old in shallow water with her mother's hand on the back of her neck: *count the strokes, not the fear.*

"But that's not the confession," she said.

"No?"

"The confession is that when I dove under the bridge, I wasn't thinking about the letters." Her jaw set. "I was thinking about my father. About how he went into this same water and never came out, and how the company wrote it down as debt cancelled, and how I've spent eleven years diving in the water that ate him because it was the only thing the company would pay me to do." She looked at the letters, forty-one pages of other people's murders. "I dove under that bridge to spite a piece of paper, Edmund. If I'd died, it wouldn't have been for justice. It would have been for spite. I've been carrying that around since sundown and I'm tired of it."

Edmund considered this. Outside, the tide turned in the creek with a sound like slow breathing.

"Spite got the letters across the estuary," he said finally. "Justice can have them from here. There's no rule that says a thing must start noble to end well."

"That's not how I was raised."

"How were you raised?"

"By a company," she said, "which counts everything and forgives nothing."

* * *

They took turns after that — mistakes traded like currency, each one offered and accepted. Hers: she had let herself be seen at the bridge's western approach; a guard had looked directly at her before she slipped into the water, and she'd assumed the dark would cover what the dark does not cover. His: he had written the route on paper, in cipher, and burned the paper, but burning takes time and he'd stood too long over the flame in an alley where anyone might have passed. Hers: three weeks ago, before she ever met him, she had mentioned to the foreman that she expected a letter from a cousin in the south, and the foreman had repeated it to Calloway, and now any stranger asking after correspondence fit a description already filed. His: the envelope. He still carried the empty envelope Vane's letters had come in, because it was the last thing in Vane's hand that he could hold, and sentiment, Edmund admitted, was just another kind of loose page.

When they finished, the silence that followed was not empty. It was the particular silence of two people who have shown each other their worst inventory and found the sums acceptable.

"Do you know what I keep doing," Tamsin said, after a while. "I keep measuring. It's what I do — everything I own, I ask if I can carry it. My whole life fits in two hands. Knife. Compass. One change of clothes." She looked at him across the lamplight. "And today I nearly died under a bridge, and afterward, lying in the mud getting my breath back, do you know what I felt? Not fear. Relief. Because the packet was dry, and everything else — me, the boot, the rest — was expendable. I've made myself part of the carrying charge, Edmund. Just freight. Just another thing that can be replaced if the cargo arrives."

"That's not true."

"Isn't it?"

"No," he said, and leaned forward. "Because freight doesn't choose what it carries. You chose. Under that bridge, with your lungs burning, you chose to keep going. Freight doesn't get tired. Freight doesn't get afraid. And freight—" he paused, and found the words came harder than he expected— "freight doesn't sit in a smuggler's loft at midnight confessing its sins to a stranger so he'll think better of her. That's not cargo, Tamsin. That's character. I've spent two months trusting no one, and four days trusting you, and I'd trust you under that bridge again tomorrow, and I resent how much I mean that."

She looked at him for a long moment. The lamp guttered, steadied.

"You shouldn't," she said. "Trust me. We've known each other nine days."

"We've nearly died together twice. That's years, in the proper accounting."

Another laugh escaped her — quieter this time, less surprised. Something in her shoulders came down an inch, some load shifted to a better bearing. She reached into the blanket and brought out the compass, the brass case scratched dull, and turned it over in her fingers without opening it.

"My mother kept this hidden for twenty years," she said. "Hidden even from me. Do you know why? Because it was proof. Proof that someone outside the company once thought we were worth a gift. She hid it because hope is dangerous when you're indentured. Hope makes you look up. Looking up gets you beaten down." Her thumb traced the seam of the case. "I've been angry at her my whole life for being small. For surviving instead of fighting. And yesterday I crawled through mud on my belly and I understood her completely for the first time." She closed her fist around the compass. "I'm not her. But I finally know what she was protecting. It wasn't herself."

Edmund said nothing. There are moments when speech is the intruder, and he had learned, in two months of silence, to recognize them.

* * *

It was near dawn when Brack came up the ladder faster than a man his size should move, and they knew before he spoke that the news was bad. Smugglers deliver good news slowly, with relish. Bad news comes up the ladder at speed.

"Change in the schedule," Brack said. He was wiping his hands on his apron, a habit Edmund had noticed meant the man was working something out, or working something down. "Boy came back with word from the customs house. Fleet's departure's posted. Four days."

Tamsin went very still. "It was twelve."

"It was twelve." Brack stuffed the apron back in his belt. "Calloway moved it. Whole harbor's in an uproar — masters screaming about cargoes half-loaded, chandlers working their men double tides. Nobody's ever seen the schedule bent like this, not for weather, not for anything." He looked between the two of them. "Which means he knows something's coming. Maybe not what. Maybe not who. But he's shortened the board because he thinks he's playing against slow men."

Edmund did the arithmetic and did not like the remainder. Three checkpoints had become one. Twelve days had become four. Every hour Calloway had shaved from the schedule was an hour shaved from their margin for error — and their margin had been thin enough to see through.

"Four days," Tamsin said. She said it the way she said measurements — precisely, testing the number's weight.

"Four days," Brack confirmed. Then, grudgingly, almost gently: "You should eat something. Both of you. Dead messengers carry no letters."

He went back down the ladder. Edmund and Tamsin sat looking at each other across the forty-one pages, and Edmund watched her do the thing she always did: reach for the countable, the carryable, the measurable. Watched her find it insufficient for once.

"Four days," she said again, softer.

Then she reached out, and took his hand, and held it — not romantically, not entirely, but the way a diver takes a line: to know where the other one is, in dark water.

The lamp burned low. Below them, the tide kept its own schedule, and the fleet, somewhere out in the gray morning harbor, began — plank by plank, sail by sail, man by man — to prepare for departure.
