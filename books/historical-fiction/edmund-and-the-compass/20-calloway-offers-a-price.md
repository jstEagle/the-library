## 20. Calloway Offers a Price

The summons came the way weather comes to the coast — not suddenly, but with a pressure she felt before she could name it.

Tamsin was rinsing shell at the long sorting tables when one of the company clerks appeared at her elbow, a thin man named Prewitt who always smelled of ink and vinegar. He did not touch her. Company men never touched divers; they considered it beneath them, or unlucky.

"Mr. Calloway would see you," he said. "In his office. At four bells."

"Now?"

"He said at your convenience." Prewitt's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Which Mr. Calloway has determined is four bells."

She dried her hands on her apron and understood, in the way she understood the weight of a shell in her palm before she opened it, that the pearl inside this summons would not be worth having. She walked up from the sorting flats through the yard, past the drying racks hung with mother-of-pearl that caught the late light like a wall of small moons, past the guardhouse where two men watched her pass with more interest than guards usually spent on a diver. The net had been tightening for days — extra men at the customs house, questions at the gate, the fleet's departure moved up without explanation. Now she felt the net's edge brush her own shoulder.

Calloway's office sat above the counting room, up a stair worn hollow in its middle by forty years of boots. The door stood open. He was at his window with his back to her, hands clasped behind him, watching the harbor as if it were arithmetic he was still working through. He was not a large man. He had the particular neatness of someone who had long ago decided that flesh was an untidiness to be managed, and his voice matched: soft, precise, patient as interest accruing.

"Miss Thornbury. Sit, please."

There was one chair placed before the desk. There was, she noticed, no second chair. She remained standing, and something in Calloway's shoulders suggested he had expected exactly that, and approved of it.

"Do you know why the company posts rewards for vigilance?" he said, still facing the water. "Not for courage. Not for loyalty, even. Vigilance. It is because vigilance can be practiced daily, by anyone, at any rank. Courage is episodic. Loyalty is an opinion. But vigilance—" he turned then, and his eyes were pale and unremarkable, the eyes of a man doing sums — "vigilance is a habit. And you, Miss Thornbury, are the most habitual woman I have ever employed."

"I try to be thorough, sir."

"You are thorough the way tide is thorough." He came around the desk but did not sit behind it; he leaned against its edge, which put them nearly level, which was a technique, though she could not yet see for what. "You catalogue. I have read your manifests. Your packing lists are works of art. Nothing leaves your hands uncounted. Which is why I find recent events so troubling."

He let the silence do some work. Tamsin kept her breathing even and thought about nothing at all, which was a skill her mother had taught her without ever meaning to.

"A passenger arrived eleven days ago," Calloway said. "A man asking quiet questions about a dead captain. He passed through your inspection table at the customs house. You cleared him, and a crate of survey instruments, and a porter's chit that our records show was issued to no porter we employ." He raised a hand before she could speak. "I am not accusing you. If I were accusing you, we would be having this conversation downstairs, with different men present, and it would go differently for you. I am asking you a simpler question, and I want you to consider it carefully before answering."

He waited until she looked at him.

"What has that man promised you?"

* * *

She had rehearsed lies on the walk over — three of them, sorted by plausibility — and now found all three useless, because Calloway was not asking what she had done. He was asking what she wanted. It was a different instrument entirely, and he played it slowly.

"Nothing," she said. "He asked directions. I gave them. That's the whole of my acquaintance with him."

"Mm." Calloway crossed to the shelf behind his desk and took down a ledger — not hers, a thicker one, bound in grey cloth gone soft at the corners. He set it open before her on the desk's green blotter, and turned it so she could read it, and Tamsin felt the floor of her stomach tilt, because she knew that binding. She had grown up under that binding.

"Your mother's account," Calloway said gently. "Opened the year your father drowned. Advances against the funeral. Advances against the season lost while she grieved. Advances against the advances — the company extends credit generously to widows of our divers, Miss Thornbury, we are not monsters, whatever is said of us in the taverns. But credit compounds." His finger traveled down a column of figures like a man walking a fence line. "Fourteen years of compounding. Do you know the current figure?"

She didn't answer. He told her anyway. The number was enormous and precise and had been constructed, she understood now, with the same loving thoroughness she brought to a packing manifest — each entry small enough to swallow, the sum impossible to disgorge. Her own indenture was a chain with her own name on it. This was the other end of it, the anchor set in her mother's name, dragging along underneath everything they had done for fourteen years, invisible from the surface.

"She will work it off in the shelling camps," Calloway said, "when she is too old to dive and too proud to beg, unless something changes. You know this. You have known it since you were a child. It is why you pack shell faster than any two women on my flats. It is why you dive the outer bed when sensible divers won't. You have been trying to carry this number out of the water on your back for half your life." He closed the ledger softly. "It cannot be done. I want to be honest with you, because honest is what you respect. It cannot be done. The arithmetic does not permit it. No amount of competence rescues a debt that grows faster than the labor that serves it."

"And yours does?" she said quietly.

"Mine grows slower," said Calloway, "because I am the one holding the pen." He said it without pride and without apology, the way a man admits the sea is salt. Then he opened a drawer and took out a single sheet of paper, and laid it beside the ledger, and Tamsin read her own life rewritten in three lines.

*Indenture of T. Thornbury: discharged in full. Account of M. Thornbury: settled and closed.*

Both lines carried the factor's signature space, empty, waiting. Both were dated already. Dated tomorrow.

"The man calling himself Roe is carrying correspondence belonging to a deceased officer," Calloway said. "Correspondence that, if it circulates, will injure the trade upon which every wage on this coast depends — yours, your mother's, the camp cooks', the boatmen's, the children sorting shell by lamplight. I am not asking you to harm him. I am asking you to find out where he keeps the letters, and send word to me, and then stand aside. The company will do the rest, cleanly, lawfully, through proper channels." He paused. "In exchange, these entries close. You walk out of this office free, and your mother walks out of debt with you, and neither of you owes another season to anyone."

He watched her with those pale patient eyes, and the terrible thing — the thing she would turn over for nights afterward, examining it like flawed nacre — was that he meant it. There was no cruelty in the offer. There was no satisfaction taken, no gloating, no relish. He believed, wholly and calmly, that he was extending mercy. He had seen a capable woman drowning under bad arithmetic and he was throwing her a rope, and the fact that the rope was made of other people's ruin simply did not register as weight to a man who had spent his life moving figures that were never required to bleed.

"Take the evening," he said. "Think like a woman cataloguing, Miss Thornbury. Weigh what you hold. A stranger who has known you eleven days, against a mother who has held your whole life? Paper that promises justice somewhere far away and someday, against a signature that frees two women here and now?" He slid the unsigned paper toward her, an inch, no more. "You measure everything. Measure this."

She looked at the page a long moment. Then she did the thing her hands had learned in deep water, when the chest began to burn and panic offered itself: she slowed everything. She looked at the ledger, at the beautiful terrible columns. She looked at the discharge, at the blank waiting for ink. She looked at Calloway's face, which held nothing but kindness of the coldest, truest kind.

"If I say yes," she said, "when would you want word?"

His expression did not change, but something behind it eased — the easing of a man who has made a sound investment.

"Tomorrow. Four bells. Bring nothing, sign nothing yet. Send Prewitt a message through the yard office if you've located the letters, and by evening your accounts will close." He straightened, and turned back to the window, dismissal and confidence in the same gesture. "You are a careful woman, Thornbury. Careful women survive on this coast. I have always respected that about you."

She went down the hollow-worn stairs into the smell of ink and vinegar, out across the yard where the drying racks burned white in the last light, and no one stopped her, and no one followed her, because as far as the company knew, she was theirs.

* * *

She did not go to the loft. Not directly. She walked the long way, along the tideline below the town, where the evening boats were hauled up and the smell was tar and weed and old fish, and she measured things, because measuring was how she thought.

She measured the offer. That was the discipline of it — she would not pretend the offer was nothing. It was not nothing. It was everything she had wanted since she was nine years old and first understood the shape of the chain: freedom, hers, and her mother's, signed and dated, purchased at a price that was not even being asked of her body or her blood, only of her silence and her attention. Edmund was a stranger. The letters were paper. Vane was a drowned man, officially. The justice the letters promised was a rumor of justice, a thing that might dissolve at any checkpoint, whereas the discharge in Calloway's drawer was real as ballast. Any reasonable woman would take it.

That was what frightened her — not the temptation, but how reasonable it was. Calloway had not offered gold. He had offered arithmetic corrected. He had looked at the ledger of her life and offered to balance it, and only the smallest voice, deep under the water of her, asked the question that spoiled the sum: balanced *for whom*, and paid with *what coin*, and who else's name was written in the margins of that grey book, in columns just as patient, growing just as fast?

Her father's name was in such a column once. She had not known it until ten days ago, when Edmund read the letters aloud in Brack's loft and her father's drowning turned inside out in front of her. The company had written him closed. Settled. A figure resolved.

And Calloway sat above the counting room believing himself merciful.

She reached the stretch below the shell barge and Brack's boy was mending net on the sand, whistling, and she nodded to him and he nodded back and something about his whistle snagged on her ear — too light, too careless, a boy performing idleness rather than practicing it — but she was tired and grieving and she filed it away wrong, filed it under *later* instead of *danger*. She climbed the loft ladder and knocked the pattern on the door, and Edmund opened it, and behind him the candle showed the letters in their oilcloth, the whole fragile weight of everything the company feared.

"Well?" he said. Reading her face. Always reading her face first, the way a man reads weather before trusting a sail.

"He offered to void my indenture and clear my mother's debts," she said. "If I tell him where you keep the letters."

Edmund went very still. Then, being Edmund, he did not argue or plead; he simply stepped aside to let her in, and put the kettle on the small spirit stove, because that was what he did when the world grew heavy — he made tea for whoever was carrying it.

"You're telling me," he said, "instead of him."

"I'm telling you instead of him."

"Why?"

And she found she could not give him the grand answer, the answer about justice and fathers and falsified drownings, because the true answer was smaller and harder and lay in her palm like a shell she'd carried since childhood. She sat down on the crate and held her hands together.

"Because he showed me the ledger," she said. "Every entry. Fourteen years of my mother's life in little numbers, each one small enough to swallow. And he thinks he was offering me mercy." She looked up. "A man who can hold that book and call it mercy will write anything in it. My father is already in a book like that. Closed. Settled. I won't help him close anyone else."

Edmund sat down across from her and said nothing for a while, which was his kind of agreement.

"There's a second thing," she said, and her voice dropped. "When I came up the beach, Brack's boy was on the sand. Watching the water. Whistling." She met Edmund's eyes. "Edmund — who taught him to watch the water?"

The kettle began, very softly, to sing.

Below them, faint under the floorboards, they heard the boy's whistle change tune.

They were on the ladder before it finished the first bar.
