## 21. The Mother Who Stayed Silent

Her mother's house sat at the edge of the divers' quarter where the packed earth gave way to mud and the mud gave way to the estuary's grey tongue — a house of driftwood planks and corrugated iron that had been patched so many times it resembled a quilt more than a wall. Tamsin had walked this path ten thousand times. Tonight she walked it as if the ground might not hold her.

Calloway's offer was still on her skin like oil she couldn't wash off. *Your indenture voided. Your mother's debts cleared. Deliver him.* He had said it gently, which was the worst part. Men who threaten do it loudly; men who mean it do it softly, over tea, with the patience of someone reading a ledger.

She had told Edmund none of it yet. She had told herself she would, after. After she did this one thing first.

The lamp burned low in the front room. Her mother sat where she always sat, mending a dive suit by lamplight, needle rising and falling with the metronome patience Tamsin had inherited in her lungs instead of her hands. Merrin Thornbury was sixty-one years old and had been diving since she was nine, and her body showed it the way a wharf shows the tide: every joint banded with salt, every movement economical, nothing wasted, nothing repaired unless repair was cheaper than replacement.

"Mam," Tamsin said.

"Tamsin." Her mother did not look up. "You're dripping on the floor."

"It hasn't rained."

"Then you've been near the water, and you didn't come from the beds, because the night boats are in and you weren't with them." The needle paused. Now she looked up. Merrin's eyes were the pale grey-green of shallow water over sand, and they had always seen too much. "So you've been doing something you'd want me to disapprove of. Sit down before you drip on anything that matters."

Tamsin did not sit.

"I went through the customs ledgers today," she said. "The dead files. The ones Calloway keeps locked."

Something crossed her mother's face — quick, small, gone. But Tamsin had spent her whole life learning to read water, and her mother's face was water. "Why would you do a fool thing like that?"

"Because a man came off the packet boat carrying letters written by Aldous Vane."

The needle snapped. Not bent — snapped, the steel going in Merrin's fingers like a bone breaking, and Tamsin knew then, before another word was spoken, that her mother had known. Had known for years. The knowledge arrived in her chest not as shock but as a slow flooding, cold at the ankles first, the way drowning actually happens — not like the stories say, all thrashing and noise, but quiet, and upward, and certain.

"You knew him," Tamsin said.

"Sit down, Tamsin."

"*You knew him.*"

"I said sit down." And there it was — the voice from childhood, the voice that came out only when the alternative was panic, and Tamsin heard the panic under it plain as a heartbeat. She sat. Not because she was told. Because her legs had opinions of their own.

Merrin set down the ruined suit. She folded her hands. She took her time about it, and Tamsin realized her mother was deciding something — deciding it the way you decide whether to come up on a short breath or push for the surface you can't see yet.

"Aldous Vane surveyed the outer bed the year you were born," Merrin said finally. "Company man, but not company-made. He'd dive his own lines when the factor wasn't watching, which made him either the bravest or the stupidest officer in the harbor, and I never settled which. He taught me to read the tide tables properly instead of by guess. Your father hated him for that." A pause. "No. Your father envied him for that, and hatred is what envy looks like after it's been married twenty years."

Tamsin's father. Drowned off the middle bed when she was six. Officially an accident; officially logged, certified, filed, closed. She had built her whole life on the far side of that word *officially*, the way you build a house on a filled marsh and hope the ground holds.

"The compass," Tamsin said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "The one you gave me. Without its needle. Whose was it?"

Her mother looked at the lamp for a long moment.

"It was mine," she said. "He gave it to me. Aldous. Before you were born, before your father — it doesn't matter the order of things now, but it mattered then. It was a gift, and I was young, and then I wasn't, and your father—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "When he died, I pulled the needle out and threw it in the estuary, because a compass that points anywhere is a thing that can get you hanged, and I had a daughter to keep alive. A compass without a needle still looks fine on a shelf. That was the idea. Look whole. Go nowhere."

*Look whole. Go nowhere.* Tamsin thought of every year of her life measured in what could be carried, and understood suddenly that the measuring hadn't started with her. It had been handed down, along with the knife and the silence, the way divers hand down lung capacity and bad knees.

"And the drownings," she said. "The falsified ones. Vane's letters name eleven men. Men the company drowned on paper to void their debts. You knew."

"I knew some," Merrin said. "Not eleven. Four. Maybe five. Names that came to me over years, the way things come to women here — not through documents, through absence. A man drowns and his debt doesn't die with him, it moves. It moved to wives. To children. To you." Her jaw tightened. "I knew the pattern. I didn't have proof. Proof is a rich man's tool, Tamsin. It costs money to make and money to buy and money to be believed. We had none of the three."

"You could have told me."

"Told you *what?*" And now her mother's voice cracked open, and underneath the crack was twenty years of held breath. "That the world we lived in was built on paper drownings? You were *six*. What would you have done with it — marched to the customs house and asked politely for justice? They'd have eaten you. They eat everyone who comes alone and empty-handed. My one job, my only job, the single thing I was put on this earth to do after your father died, was to keep you small enough and quiet enough and poor enough in the right ways that they wouldn't notice you. And it worked. You're alive. Forgive me for choosing that."

"It didn't work," Tamsin said quietly. "They noticed me anyway. I'm indentured, Mam. I've been indentured since I was fourteen on a debt they invented. You kept me small and they grew the ledger around me like coral around a wreck."

Merrin flinched. It was a real flinch, full-bodied, and Tamsin hated how much satisfaction she took in it, and hated the satisfaction more than the flinch.

"I know," her mother said. "I know what they did with the numbers. I read your contract every year on your birthday while you slept. Every year the figure was larger. Every year I decided—"

"Decided what?"

"That tomorrow I'd tell you everything. And every tomorrow I got up and dove instead." She laughed, or something near laughter, dry and terrible. "Do you know what silence costs, Tamsin? Everyone thinks silence is free because no one invoices you for it. But you pay in installments. Every year a little more of it. Until you owe so much silence you can't ever speak, because speaking means admitting everything you bought with the quiet, and you find you can't afford the confession."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Outside, the tide was turning; Tamsin could hear it in the pilings of the old jetty, that soft groan of the world changing its mind. Four days until the fleet sailed. Three, if Calloway lied about schedules the way he lied about everything else, and he did.

"Vane's alive," Tamsin said.

Her mother's hands went still in her lap. "What?"

"He's on a hulk offshore. Company prison ship. They've kept him two years writing letters in his own hand, smuggling them out bit by bit — names, dates, forged certificates. Everything. And when the fleet sails, they'll kill him quietly and call it fever." Tamsin leaned forward. "And Edmund is going to get him out, and I am going to help him, and the letters are going past the fleet gate sewn into my dive suit, and I need you to tell me why you're looking at me like I've already drowned."

"Because you *have*," Merrin whispered. "Don't you understand what they'll do? Vane they kill because he wrote things down. What do you imagine they do to a girl who carries them?"

"They won't touch me. I'm contracted cargo. I pack their shell—"

"You think contracts are shields?" Her mother was on her feet now, and the lamplight made a savage shadow of her. "I watched Nesta Coram's boy vanish in '39 for less than this. No trial. No body. His mother dove the middle bed every season for ten years looking for him among the shell, and everyone let her, because letting her look was cheaper than answering her. That's the mercy of this place, Tamsin. That's the whole of it. And you want to walk into the gate with a dead man's words stitched against your heart because—"

"Because it's *true*," Tamsin said, and her own voice startled her, loud in the small room. "Because eleven men are dead on paper and my father is dead in fact and neither of those things was an accident, and because you stayed silent for twenty years and it bought us nothing — nothing, Mam, look around, look at this *house*—"

"Don't you dare measure my house."

"Someone has to! You taught me to. You taught me that everything gets weighed, everything gets counted, everything gets measured by what it costs and what it carries. Fine. Then measure this: your silence cost twenty years and bought a leaking floor. Mine is going to cost something too — maybe everything — but it's buying a man's life and eleven families' names and my own signature off a ledger that was a lie from the first line. Those aren't the same trade, Mam. Don't tell me they are."

The silence afterward was the long kind, the underwater kind, where the pressure builds and builds and you learn whether your ears will hold or fail. Merrin stood in the lamplight and Tamsin watched her mother age in real time, or perhaps just stop pretending not to have aged, and thought about how forgiveness works between people who share blood and a roof and almost nothing else: it doesn't arrive like a boat, whole and landing. It arrives like the tide — partway, retreating, coming back, each time covering different ground.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me tonight," Merrin said at last, very quietly. "I don't think I'd believe you if you offered."

"No."

"Good. Hate me a while longer. It'll keep you sharp." She turned to the corner cabinet — the one with the lock, the one Tamsin had never once seen opened — and took out a key from around her neck, and opened it, and lifted out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that had been oiled and re-oiled so many times the cloth had gone stiff as shell.

"What is that?"

"Your father's papers. His real ones. His diving certificates, his bed rights, his tally records from the seasons before he died — the tallies that show he'd cleared nearly all his debt the month before they logged his drowning." She set the bundle on the table between them like a body laid out. "If his debt was nearly cleared, then the debt they transferred to your indenture was invented twice over. Once when they killed him and once when they billed you for it. I've known that for twenty years, Tamsin. I had it in this cabinet. I could have shown you any day. Any birthday. I chose the shelf over the truth, because the truth was heavy and the shelf was light, and I have carried the wrong one so long my back has grown around it."

Tamsin stared at the oilcloth. Her hands, when she finally made them move, were steadier than she expected — diver's hands, hands that had learned to work while the heart was somewhere else entirely. She unwrapped the top fold. Paper. Ink. Her father's initials in the margin. Numbers that told a different story than the one the company told, the true boring arithmetic of a working man climbing out of a hole honestly, one shell at a time.

"Why give me this now?"

Merrin sat back down across from her. Up close, in the bad light, her mother's face was a map of the estuary itself — channels and shoals and the marks of things that had grounded there.

"Because you were right about the trade," she said. "My silence bought a floor that leaks. Yours might buy something else. And because if you're going to walk into that gate and gamble both our lives, you deserve to gamble with the full stake. That's all I have to give you, Tamsin. Not forgiveness — I haven't earned the standing to ask for yours, and I may never. Just this: I'm done paying into the silence. Whatever happens at the fleet gate, the quiet ends with me."

Tamsin gathered the papers slowly, rewrapping them with the care owed to human remains. When she stood, her mother stood too, and for one suspended moment Tamsin thought — embrace, tears, some clean ending to the evening's bleeding. Instead Merrin reached out and touched the compass hanging at her daughter's belt, the useless beautiful needleless thing, and held it a moment in her palm like a small cold moon.

"He'd have wanted you to have it pointed somewhere," she said. "That's all he believed in. Direction over safety. It killed him eventually. Most beliefs do." She let go. "There's a woman on Shell Row — Widow Ferrow — who does fine metalwork under the counter, needles and clasps and the little hinges for shell boxes. If a person happened to bring her a compass missing its needle, and a needle that matched, she could make it whole again by morning. If a person were foolish enough to want that."

Tamsin's breath caught. "You kept the needle? You said you threw it in the estuary—"

"I lied," Merrin said simply. "It's what I do. I've had twenty years of practice and one good use left in me." She went back to her chair, took up the ruined suit, the broken needle, the whole small inventory of an unremarkable evening, as though nothing had passed between them heavier than weather. "Go on, then. The tide won't wait and neither will whatever fool thing you've decided to do. And Tamsin—"

Tamsin stopped in the doorway. The night outside smelled of salt and tar and turning water.

"If Edmund Roe breaks your trust," her mother said, eyes already back on her mending, "you send him to me. I've waited twenty years to be angry at somebody new."

Tamsin laughed despite everything — a short, wet, surprised sound — and stepped out into the dark.

Halfway down the mud path, the compass at her hip and her father's true papers pressed against her ribs, she saw a light bobbing fast along the shoreline road toward Brack's loft — a lantern moving wrong, moving urgent, moving like bad news. She stood still and watched it pass, and felt the tide of the last hour shift beneath her all over again.

Whatever it was, it was headed straight for Edmund.
