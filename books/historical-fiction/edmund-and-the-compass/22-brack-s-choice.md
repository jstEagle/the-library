## 22. Brack's Choice

Brack had a rule about fire: never burn anything you might need to read later. In twenty years of moving other people's goods past other people's guards, the rule had kept him alive more reliably than any prayer. Paper was memory, and memory was leverage, and leverage was the only currency that didn't rot in a harbor town where the company owned the bank, the magistrate, and half the church.

So when he sat down on an upturned crate in the loft above his shell store, struck a match against his thumbnail, and held the flame to his own safe-conduct papers, he was breaking the only law he'd ever trusted.

The documents caught slowly. They were good paper — heavy stock, company watermark, the kind of thing that opened gates from here to the capital. *Permitted to pass and repass, by water or land, with such goods as shall not be contrary to His Majesty's customs.* Signed in ink gone brown with age, stamped twice, and worth more to a man like Brack than the boat he slept in. He had bought them at auction from the estate of a customs officer who had died of an apoplexy before he could report them stolen. He had carried them through two inspections and one robbery. He had sworn, once, drunk on arrack with a woman whose name he no longer remembered, that they would be pried out of his cold hands.

His brother's face looked up at him from nothing at all — just the shape of the smoke rising, which was nonsense, but the mind does what it likes when it grieves.

*Joren Brack,* the letters said. That was how Tamsin Thornbury had read it out to him three nights ago, her voice flat and careful, the way people speak when they are carrying something fragile across a long room. *Drowned in the course of his duties, June 1845. All wages and shares forfeit to the trading company per articles of indenture.*

Brack had stood in the doorway of his own loft with a crate of contraband tea under one arm and listened to a dead man's handwriting describe his brother's murder as a clerical event.

* * *

He had known, of course. Not known-known. Smugglers don't get to know things; knowing things is how you end up in the estuary wearing your own chains. But he had known the way you know weather. Joren had gone down with a crew of six on a shell boat that every diver on the coast said was sound. The company had paid out nothing — *forfeited shares*, the word was — and when Brack had gone asking, politely, hat in hand, a clerk with soft white fingers had explained to him about the debt Joren had owed against his gear, against his rations, against the very air he'd breathed aboard, so that death hadn't ended the obligation but passed it, and would Brack care to assume his brother's balance? Fourteen pounds, seven shillings. For a drowned man's breathing.

Brack had not assumed the debt. Brack had walked out of that office and gotten drunk for three days and then gone back to work, because grief is a luxury and survival is a trade, and he had told himself for two years that the sea took Joren, that the sea takes everyone eventually, that there was no arithmetic in it worth doing.

Then a pale foreigner with salt-ruined boots had come to his night market stall and asked, in the careful voice of a man testing ice, whether Brack could move a package past three checkpoints. And weeks after that, in the loft, a pearl-diver's daughter had read aloud from letters written by a captain who was supposed to be fish food, and the letters had names in them, and dates, and forged certificates with the same clerk's hand on all of them — the soft-fingered clerk, older now, still employed — and Joren Brack's name was the fourth one down.

Not lost at sea. Sent to sea to be lost. The boat was old, the manifest overloaded, the insurance claimed within the month. A debt erased. A share pocketed. A line in a ledger.

Brack had listened to all of this standing very still, and when Tamsin finished reading she had looked at him with those diver's eyes — eyes that spend their lives measuring distance through water, that know exactly how far away the surface is — and she had said, quietly, "I'm sorry," and he had hated her a little for it, because pity from someone whose father's name was also on that list felt like two beggars comparing sores.

He hadn't spoken to her for a day after that. Then he had come up to the loft with food and rum and said only: "Tell me the rest."

* * *

Now the safe-conduct papers were ash in a tin dish, curling like living things dying, and Brack watched them go with the professional detachment of a man watching a shipment sink.

They were worth passage. That was the thing to understand. Not just convenience — *immunity*. With those papers he could walk past the toll bridge any hour of any day, could load and unload at the fleet gate without a second glance, could sit out any crackdown in some tavern down the coast until the company's attention moved on to softer targets. Every smuggler on this coast had a version of them or wished they did. Brack had spent half his life earning his, and he was burning it in his own loft because a girl who measured the world by what could be carried had asked him for help he couldn't give halfway.

Halfway got you killed. Halfway got your partners killed. If he lent them the barge and the hiding place and kept his papers dry for his own retreat, he was a ferryman, and ferrymen survive wars by belonging to neither bank. That had been his whole philosophy since Joren died: carry what pays, ask nothing, owe no side.

But the letters had changed the arithmetic.

Because if Aldous Vane was right — and Brack had read enough of the man's hand now to believe he was precise the way engineers are precise — then the company hadn't merely killed Joren. It had made him *nothing*. Not murdered, not even robbed: processed. A drowning was a transaction. His brother had been converted from a man into a cleared line, and somewhere a factor had initialed the conversion, and somewhere above that factor another man had approved it, and the whole machine had ground on without a single gear catching, without anyone anywhere having to hate Joren Brack at all.

That was the part Brack couldn't put down. He could have forgiven hatred. Hatred was human. This was bookkeeping.

And a bookkeeping crime, he understood suddenly, sitting in his loft smelling his own immunity turn to smoke, could not be answered with a bookkeeping defense. You couldn't out-clerk them. You couldn't hide behind papers — their papers, his papers, any papers — because paper was their country, and in their country every document was a door they owned the key to.

So burn the doors. Stand in the open. Belong to the side that has no papers.

It was, he thought wryly, watching the last corner of the watermark blacken, the most unprofitable decision of his career. Which was probably why it was the first honest one.

* * *

The boy was asleep in the corner — his sister's boy, taken in when the sister went into the fever ward, a sharp-handed quiet child of eleven whom Brack paid in coppers to run messages and watch the door. The boy stirred at the smell of burning and cracked one eye.

"You burning money again, Uncle?"

"Just cleaning house."

"Mother says you'll clean yourself into the poorhouse."

"Your mother," Brack said, "is a prophet." He ruffled the boy's hair as he passed, and felt something twist in his chest that he refused to examine — the knowledge, arriving late and unwelcome, that he was doing this partly so the boy would grow up in a port where a man's death couldn't be entered as a credit. He examined it anyway, walking down the stairs, because apparently tonight was the night for expensive truths.

The shell store below smelled of brine and low tide. The barge itself lay moored off the little private jetty, low in the water, its hold stacked with cured shell awaiting the fleet's loading days — the one cargo in the whole harbor that company men never searched going out, because searching your own profit was against somebody's religion. Brack had explained this to Edmund Roe with the satisfaction of a magician revealing the mechanism: *they'll tear apart a passenger's trunk to the stitching, but the shell goes through untouched, because every hour they spend inspecting shell is an hour some factor isn't drinking.* The girl had understood it faster. Of course she had. She packed the stuff.

A lantern burned at the far end of the store, and beside it sat Edmund, mending net with the clumsy concentration of a landsman, and Tamsin, asleep sitting up against a stack of crates with her arms crossed over her chest like a woman guarding herself even in dreams. They'd been back from the toll bridge two days. Both of them wore the particular gray exhaustion of people who have recently almost died and haven't yet had time to enjoy not being dead.

Edmund looked up. "You're up late."

"Accounting." Brack dragged a crate over and sat. "Where's Vane?"

"Loft. Sleeping. He ate everything I gave him and asked for more, which Osei says is a good sign." Edmund set down the netting needle. "What kind of accounting?"

"The permanent kind." Brack poured rum into three tin cups, nudged the third toward the sleeping girl without waking her — she'd drink it when she woke; divers always did. "I've been thinking about what happens after."

"After the gate?"

"After everything. Say it works. Say the letters land in front of the superintendent and Calloway chokes on his own certificates. What do you imagine follows?" He answered himself before Edmund could try. "Nothing. Or nothing legal. I've watched this company swallow scandals whole for fifteen years. A factor falls, a factor rises, the shells keep shipping. The only thing that changes is who gets to be afraid."

"So why burn anything?" Edmund's voice was gentle, and Brack realized with irritation that the man had already guessed.

"I came to tell you I'm in. Not as a hired hull. In." He turned his cup slowly on his knee. "I've worked this coast alone my whole life because alone is the only way to keep what's yours. My brother thought different. He signed with the company because he believed in belonging to something. Look where it got him — a line in a ledger and a bill for his own breathing." He looked up. "I'm done being alone about this. And I'm done keeping exits. So: my boat, my barge, my routes, my boys, whatever I have. And this—" he nodded upward, toward the loft, toward the tin dish of ash, "—is gone. No safe-conduct. No back door. When Calloway comes for us, and he will, he finds men who can't scatter because there's nowhere to scatter to."

Tamsin had woken without moving. Divers breathe like that, Brack thought — silent, from low in the chest. Her eyes were open and fixed on the tin dish.

"You burned them," she said. Not a question. She'd seen the ashes before he said a word; of course she had.

"Waste of good paper," Brack said gruffly. "Don't make a ceremony of it. I hate ceremonies."

"What did you say to me the night market, when I offered you my pearls?" Her voice was even, but something underneath it wasn't. "You said, *I don't take payment for other men's wars.*"

"I did say that."

"And now?"

"Now I find it's not other men's war." He held her gaze. "Fourth name down, missy. Right under your father's. You and me are in the same column." He drained his cup. "That's all the speech you're getting. I've got a barge to ballast."

* * *

They worked until the small hours, and Brack threw himself into the work the way converts throw themselves into penance — double-checking lashings, shifting shell to create the cavity amidships where a grown man could lie flat beneath a false floor of strung shell sacks, re-wrapping the oilskin packet of letters until Edmund gently took it from him and said it was wrapped fine the first four times. Vane came down once, ghost-pale but upright, and studied the cavity in silence for a long moment.

"It'll hold a man," Brack said. "It won't hold comfort."

"Comfort," Vane said, "is the one thing I gave up claims to two years ago." He climbed back to the loft, and Brack watched him go and thought about what it costs a man to trust a stranger's carpentry with his life, and decided the captain had paid worse prices lately.

Near dawn Brack sent Edmund up to sleep and stayed below with Tamsin, who was re-sewing the hidden seam in her diving suit with small fierce stitches, the letters' new home pressed flat against the lining. For a while neither spoke. The tide turned outside with its usual indifference.

"My brother," Brack said finally, "used to say the sea keeps honest accounts. Everything balanced. Nothing owed." He turned his cup. "Two years I've hated him a little for dying believing that. Tonight I mostly wish he'd been right."

"He wasn't?"

"No. But maybe accounts can be corrected." He stood, joints cracking, an old man's body for a man not yet fifty. "Go sleep, missy. Fleet sails in four days, and we've one gate left to beat."

She left. Brack doused the lantern and stood in the dark listening to the water slap the hull, feeling lighter and more exposed than he had since boyhood — a man with no papers at all now, nothing to show at any gate, nothing to prove he existed except his own testimony.

Which, he supposed, was the point. Papers were the company's language. From tonight he would speak only for himself.

He was reaching for the lamp to relight it when he heard the step on the jetty — light, quick, wrong. Eleven-year-old feet, running when they should have crept. The door banged open and his sister's boy stood there, chest heaving, eyes too wide.

"Uncle. Uncle — there was a man at the market asking about the shell barges. Paying coppers for talk." The boy swallowed. "I saw him before. He's the one who's been coming round the loft when we're out."

The dark went very still around Brack. Somewhere above them, a page of Aldous Vane's evidence sat copied in a child's handwriting, and a factor named Calloway was reading it by lamplight, and the trap Brack had just disarmed in his own soul was already being built somewhere else, with his name in it.

"How long," Brack said quietly, "has he been coming round?"

The boy's answer was the worst thing he'd heard all year.
