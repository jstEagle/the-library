## 23. Third Checkpoint: The Fleet Gate

Edmund had seen fortresses before. He had seen them in books, mostly — square towers and heraldic gates rendered by men who had never felt a siege from the wrong side of the wall. The fleet gate on the morning of the spring tide was not like the drawings. It was worse.

It stood where the harbor road narrowed to a single throat between the shell-sheds and the water, a timber palisade crowned with iron hooks for lanterns, and beyond it lay the loading quay where the quarterly ships sat low in the water like fed animals. Three masts, four, five — the *Coromandel Star*, the *Aldinga*, two brigs he didn't know, and out at the anchorage, riding dark against the dawn haze, the hulk. The prison ship. Vane's coffin that floated.

He counted from the shade of the cooper's shed, a hundred yards off, a measure of barley in his arms that he had bought for the purpose and now regretted, because it was heavy and because a man carrying grain had no honest reason to stand still this long.

Company men everywhere. Not the usual garrison of bored toll-keepers and half-paid constables — these moved in pairs, muskets slung, and they wore the company's blue facings instead of the militia's grey. At least thirty he could see. Probably twenty more inside the sheds, out of sight, drinking tea and being paid too well to be lazy.

And then, worse than all of it, a figure in a good grey coat stepping out of the customs annex onto the quay itself, hatless, unhurried, a ledger under one arm.

Calloway.

Edmund shifted the barley and made himself breathe. He should have expected it. A man who suspected his own shadow would come to the gate personally when the quarterly cargo went out; the fleet's departure was the single hour in the year when everything the company was worth passed through one pair of gates. But seeing him there — watching Calloway lift a crate lid with his own hands, tilt it to the light, run a finger along a seam of packing straw — turned Edmund's plan into something he could no longer pretend was possible.

The plan had been simple, as Brack had laid it out in the loft with charcoal on a board: hide the letters in an outgoing crate, buy a berth on a coasting vessel for themselves separately, let the paper travel while they watched it go. Simple. Simple things died easily here.

Calloway closed the crate. Said something to the guard captain. The man laughed, and the sound carried across the water flat and clear, and Edmund felt it land in his chest like a dropped anchor.

He set down the barley beside the cooper's door, walked away without hurry, and did not look back once, which was the hardest discipline of all.

* * *

Tamsin saw him before he reached her — saw the way he walked, which was the walk of a man who had gone somewhere to learn bad news and was carrying it home carefully, like a full cup.

She was mending net outside the drying shed, which was work she could do blind and which gave her hands something to do besides shake. They had agreed she would stay away from the gate entirely; her face was known now, and known in a particular way. Two days ago a guard at the fish market had looked at her too long, and the look hadn't been idle. *"Diver's child,"* Brack had said afterward, quietly, when she described it. *"That's what they're calling you."*

So she had stayed among the shells and the nets, and waited, and hated waiting, and now Edmund sat down on an upturned barrel beside her and picked up a shuttle of twine as if he intended to help.

"Don't," she said. "You'll ruin it."

"Probably." He put it down. His voice stayed level, but she had learned his levels now, and this was the lowest one, the one just above silence. "He's there himself."

"Calloway. At the gate?"

"Inspecting every outbound crate personally. Not spot-checks. Every crate. There are soldiers where there were clerks last quarter. And—" he paused, and she watched him decide whether to say the rest, "—they're searching differently. Not for people. For paper. They're opening seams, feeling linings. Someone told them exactly what shape the thing is that they're hunting."

The net hung slack in her hands. She thought of the loft over the shell store, the oilcloth packets, the neat stack of Vane's letters with their patient, damning columns of names and dates. She thought of Brack's boy, quiet little Wren, who carried water and swept and never seemed to be anywhere when the important talk happened.

"How many pages do we have?" she said.

"Forty-one. Plus the certificates."

"And how many does a man need to hang?"

Edmund looked at her. She kept her eyes on the netting.

"One," he said. "One good page, properly read, in front of the right audience. But Calloway doesn't know that. If he has a copy of even part of it—"

"He knows what's in it. He knows whose names. He knows it's real." She tied off the knot harder than it needed. "Then the question isn't whether the gate can be beaten today. It's whether it can be beaten ever, once he's had time to think. Men like Calloway are dangerous twice — once when they're ignorant, and worse after."

Down the strand, the tide was making, sliding green glass up over the flats. Out past the point, the fleet rode at anchor, sails furled and furled again, waiting on nothing but the factor's word. Four days, Brack had heard through the wine trade. Maybe less now.

"We withdraw," Edmund said. "Today. Nothing goes near that gate."

"I know."

"It's not retreat. It's—"

"It's arithmetic," she said. "I know how to count."

But arithmetic offered no place to put the rest of it: the letters sewn into the false hem of her diving jacket, warm from her body, forty-one pages of dead men walking through her mind all night. She had slept with them against the wall beside her head, and woken twice convinced she could hear paper breathing.

* * *

In the long room above the customs annex, Mr. Calloway poured tea he did not drink and laid the page flat on the table with both hands, the way a surgeon lays out an instrument he has used before and respects.

It had cost him eleven pounds and a promise, and it was worth ten times that. One page of Aldous Vane's interminable correspondence, copied in a clerk's careful hand at the smuggler's loft by a boy who counted coins faster than consequences. Not the whole document — a fragment. But fragments were how Calloway had built everything he had. You did not need the whole wreck to know the ship; one plank, correctly read, told you the wood, the nails, the year she was launched.

This plank named three men. Drowned, all three, in the space of fourteen months. All three indebted to the company. All three with estates subsequently claimed against their debts — estates that had been surveyed, valued, and absorbed with a promptness that only looked innocent if one had never worked in accounts. And beneath the names, in Vane's furious tidy hand, the dates of each man's actual last sighting, none of which matched the dates on the burial registers by fewer than six weeks.

Six weeks. Calloway turned the page over. On the reverse, Vane had written a single line, pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through:

*They do not die until the ledger says they die.*

He had read it perhaps forty times now. Each reading produced the same cold, clarifying anger — not at Vane, who was merely an enemy, nor even at the boy who'd sold the copy, who was merely poor. At the carelessness. At the fact that after two years of immaculate management, of drownings arranged like invoices, someone had allowed this much paper to accumulate in one place and then move.

Movement meant a courier. A courier meant a destination. A destination meant a hand waiting to receive — and hands that received evidence were hands attached to men with names, ranks, and addresses, and those could be managed.

He summoned Sergeant Locke with two fingers.

"The passenger lists," Calloway said. "Every arrival since the monsoon changed. Cross-reference against crew rosters of the eastern packet boats, and against the old *Providence* muster — Vane's last command. Anyone unaccounted for. Anyone whose name appears twice."

Locke copied the order into his book without question, which Calloway appreciated. Questions were a habit of the unsuccessful.

"And the Thornbury woman?"

"She dives tomorrow's quota on the outer bed. She will be at the packing shed by noon with shell or without it. Watch which."

"You believe she'll try to ship it."

"I believe," Calloway said, folding the copy away into the inner pocket where he kept the things that could destroy him, "that a woman who spends her life measuring what can be carried has already measured this. I want the gate ready for pearl-shell crates. Every one opened, every one probed, and I will be standing there when they do it. If the letters travel, they will travel in shell. It is the only cargo in this port my own regulations forbid me to delay — profit, gentlemen, is the best camouflage ever devised, which is why we invented it."

Locke hesitated at the door. "And if it comes through?"

Calloway looked out the window at the fleet, at the beautiful patient geometry of masts against grey sky, at the hulk lying offshore like a held breath. Somewhere on that hulk, behind a grate in the aft hold, Aldous Vane was still inconveniently alive, and would remain so exactly as long as his death remained unnecessary. Once the fleet sailed and the colony's attention scattered east with it, a quiet funeral could be arranged ashore. Accidents were simpler in empty towns.

"Then it stops at the gate," Calloway said, "and never reaches any hand worth worrying about. And the people who carried it will have performed, for the record, the act of thieves. Which is a much shorter story, and easier to end."

* * *

They met at Brack's loft at dusk, and Tamsin laid it out on the floor with charcoal lines scratched directly into the boards, because Brack's boy had taken to tidying the chalkboard, and because — though she could not yet have said why — she no longer wanted the shape of the plan visible anywhere Wren might sweep.

"The gate is finished," she said. "For anything that looks like paper, or like us. So we don't send paper, and we don't send us. We send shell."

Brack, folded onto his stool by the lamp, shook his head slowly. "Shell crates get weighed, lass, not searched. That's true today. But weigh this against it: Calloway's no fool. He knows the shell exemption better than the men who wrote it. First thing he'll think when he can't find the letters anywhere else is — shell."

"Then he thinks it already," Edmund said. He had been silent since the gate, working something, and now he set it down. "Which means he watches the shell shipments. Which means we're already caught — unless the thing he watches isn't the thing that carries."

Tamsin looked up sharply. "Say it plainly."

"Decoys fail when there's only one box. They work when there are two, and the enemy must choose." He drew a line with his finger across her charcoal grid. "But there's a deeper problem, and it isn't the gate. It's time. We planned around four days. If Calloway moves the sailing forward — and a man holding a copied page is a man who moves schedules — then everything we build tonight has half a life."

Silence took the loft. Outside, the tide turned with its long sigh against the pilings, and somewhere below, Brack's bargemen sang badly about a girl in Macao.

It was Brack who broke it, and his voice had changed — flatter, older. "There's another thing none of you has said aloud, so I'll say it. Even if the letters pass the gate. Even if they reach whatever magistrate, admiral, or newspaper fool you mean them for." He turned the lamp wick up, and the light caught the scars on his knuckles. "Paper convicts men like Calloway only if somebody reads it out loud where it can't be swallowed. And the one witness who can make those pages sing is lying on a hulk in the roads, scheduled to stop breathing when the fleet sails."

Edmund went very still. Tamsin found she was gripping the edge of a crate, hard enough to hurt her fingers.

"You knew," she said.

"I've known about the hulk since the spring. Everyone in my trade knows what's anchored out there. What I didn't know until your letters arrived was why." Brack reached into his coat and brought out a folded paper, sea-stained, handled soft as cloth. "My brother is one of the names in those pages. Drowned, the register says. Fourteen years ago. I stopped believing it the year after, when his 'widow' got nothing and the company got his boat." He set the letter down on the floor between them like a card played. "So. The message or the man. Choose tonight, because by morning the tide table chooses for you."

Tamsin looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at the letters — at the whole impossible weight of them, forty-one pages that had crossed two oceans to reach this dusty loft — and she watched him arrive, visibly, painfully, at the place she had already been standing since the bridge.

"The gate can wait a day," Edmund said quietly. "Vane cannot."

Above them, unnoticed, the ladder creaked, and settled, and said nothing.
