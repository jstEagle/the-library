## 24. Plan of the Desperate

The loft above Brack's shell store smelled of brine and lamp oil and, faintly, of the blood Brack had not yet washed from his knuckles. Tamsin sat cross-legged on the floor with the oilskin packet open in front of her, and around her, in a rough half-circle, sat the strangest company she had ever kept: Edmund Roe with his salt-ruined boots drawn up beneath him; Captain Aldous Vane, alive against all arithmetic, propped on a pallet with a blanket over shoulders gone thin from two years on a hulk; and Brack himself at the doorway with a pistol across his knees, watching the dark street below as if it owed him money.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere out in the harbor, masts knocked against each other like teeth.

"The gate is impossible," Edmund said. It was the fourth time he had said it, and each time he said it a little flatter, as though repetition could grind hope down to something manageable. "Calloway has doubled the watch. He's inspecting every outgoing crate himself — I watched him do it this afternoon. He turned a consignment of copra inside out with his own hands. He's not delegating. He knows what he's looking for."

"He knows *some* of what he's looking for," Tamsin said.

Everyone looked at her.

She had been turning something over in her mind since sundown, the way she turned shell over in her hands underwater — feeling for the weight of it, the flaw, the place where it might open. She was not a planner the way Edmund was a planner; he thought in routes and timings, ink lines drawn between checkpoints. She thought in water and weight and the small brutal economics of the company that owned her next seven years. But she knew one thing none of them knew, because she had lived inside its machinery since she was nine years old, and tonight it had finally shown her its use.

"Say what you're thinking," Vane said quietly. His voice had a rasp in it now, like rope dragged over stone, but his eyes were still the sharp grey she remembered from stories her mother had never finished telling. "In my experience, Miss Thornbury, the thoughts people make you drag out are the ones worth having."

Tamsin pulled the ledger toward her — not her indenture ledger, which was ash on a beach two coves south, but the work tally she still carried because habit dies hard. She flattened it on the boards.

"Every crate that leaves this port gets searched," she said. "Copra, sandalwood, beche-de-mer, tortoiseshell, mail, personal baggage. Calloway can turn out every one of them personally and he'll find nothing, because we won't put the letters anywhere near them." She tapped the tally page with one finger. "Except one cargo. One cargo in the whole harbor is never opened, never rummaged, never so much as glanced at by an inspector. Can anyone tell me which?"

Brack grunted from the doorway without turning around. "Shell."

"Pearl shell," Tamsin said. "Mother-of-pearl. The company's own profit. Think about how it works — you've all seen it even if you've never thought about it. The divers bring it up. The packers heap it into crates wet from the sea, straight from the sorting tables, and the crates go down the chute onto the lighters and from the lighters onto the fleet. No inspection. Not a random check, not a spot check, nothing." She looked around the circle. "Do you know why?"

"Because it's theirs," Edmund said slowly.

"Because it's theirs," she agreed. "Every other cargo leaving this port is somebody else's profit, so the company wants it weighed and taxed and tallied and poked through for smuggled goods that might dodge the duty. But the shell is the company's money already in hand. An inspector who opens a shell crate slows the loading, and slowed loading costs the fleet tide, and tide is money. There's a standing order — I've seen it posted in the packing shed every season of my working life — *shell moves unexamined*. Signed by the superintendant's office. Calloway can tear apart everything else on the quay, but if he stops the shell shipments he stops his own quarterly dividend, and there isn't a factor in the company's service who would survive doing that to the books."

The lamp flame stood very still, as if it were listening too.

"You're saying," Vane said, "that the one thing they will not search is the thing they are stealing with."

"I'm saying they built themselves a door and nailed it shut behind them." Tamsin heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "I'm saying I have a contract. The fleet sails in four days — three, now, counting from dawn — and before it sails, my crew owes the company its quota. Four hundredweight of dressed shell, packed under my name, sealed with my mark. My hands on the crates. And nobody — not Calloway, not the devil himself — looks inside a crate that carries my seal on shell."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke, and Tamsin let them, because she had learned that silence after a proposal was where its flaws got found. She watched Edmund's face do what Edmund's face always did: run the thing like a clerk running figures, lips slightly parted, eyes moving over some invisible ledger in the air above her head.

"It can't just be dropped in," he said at last. "Even unsearched, a crate passes through hands. Packers, loaders, the lighter men. If a bundle of letters shifts, if shell dries and settles—"

"So it doesn't shift." She was already ahead of him; she had been ahead of him since the idea arrived whole, the way ideas did when you'd spent your life measuring things. "You don't put paper loose in a crate of shell. You oil-wrap it — Brack does it for brandy going up-coast, I've seen the technique — cloth steeped in linseed until it's waterproof, wrapped tight, tied with waxed cord. Then you pack it at the center of the crate, dead center, with shell packed around it wet. Wet shell settles as it drains. It clamps down on whatever's inside like a fist. By the time that crate reaches Sydney or Singapore, whoever opens it finds the letters set in shell like a fossil in stone."

"And the risk," Vane said, and his voice had an edge of something that might have been admiration or grief, "is that if the crate is lost, delayed, or condemned — if one man at one lighter drops it into the harbor to see it sink — then two years of my work, and everything Roe carried across three hundred miles, goes to the bottom in four fathoms of harbor water."

"Yes," Tamsin said simply. "That's the risk."

"And me?" Vane spread his hands. The gesture had a theatrical flourish left over from whatever captain he had been before the hulk, but the hands themselves trembled, and she saw him furious at them for trembling. "Your clever crate sails east with the fleet. I stay here. Calloway holds me on the hulk precisely until the fleet's anchor is up and every eye in the colony is pointed seaward. Your evidence escapes and I hang — or drown, more likely; drowning is tidier, and he has practice arranging it." His eyes found hers, and there was no accusation in them, only exhaustion and a terrible patience. "I don't say this to refuse your plan, girl. I say it so we all know what we're weighing."

"We're not choosing yet," Edmund said quickly. "There may be a way to do both—"

"There is," Tamsin said.

And this was the part she had saved, because it was the part that would cost her most, and she wanted to lay it down last, like the heaviest item into a boat.

"The quota doesn't fill itself," she said. "Four hundredweight, dressed and graded, packed under contract by the thirtieth. My crew's been on the inner beds all month and we're short — short enough that I was going to have to dive the outer bed regardless, and pray the weather held. Now I dive it anyway. Except the outer bed is past the point, where the current runs cold and the water doesn't forgive mistakes. That's where the deep shell is, the big clean nacre the fleet buyers pay premium for. Two days' hard diving. Maybe three, if the tide turns sour." She kept her eyes level. "While I'm filling those crates, someone else solves the problem of Captain Vane. Because I can't be in two places, and neither can any of you, if this works."

"Out of the question," Edmund said.

It came out fast and flat, and Tamsin almost smiled, because she'd known it would.

"Edmund—"

"No." He was on his feet now, and the lamplight made hollows of his face. "You're describing diving the outer bed alone — alone — while the rest of us attempt a night boarding of a prison hulk with a garrison aboard and Calloway already hunting us. If anything goes wrong out there, no one knows. No one can reach you. You'd be under the water holding your life in your lungs and if the current takes you—" He stopped. Drew breath. When he spoke again his voice was lower, and worse for being lower. "I've buried one friend in these waters already. I didn't sail three hundred miles to stand on a point of rock and watch the sea take another."

"Then don't watch," Tamsin said. "Dive with me."

That stopped him.

"You heard me. You want to keep me safe? Get in the water. I need a tender anyway — someone on the lines, timing my bottoms, hauling when I signal. It's skilled work but it can be learned in two days by someone with steady hands, and God knows yours are steady." She rose too, so they faced each other across the open oilskin, across the letters of dead men who were not dead. "You said you came here to deliver paper. Fine. Here's paper that needs delivering, four hundredweight of it, wearing shell for a coat. Same job, different wrapping. And it puts you beside me where you can haul me up if the cold takes my legs — which it has done before, so spare me the speech about what the outer bed does to people. I know exactly what it does. I've priced it."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his face — fear, yes, and stubbornness, and underneath both, reluctantly surfacing, the particular expression of a man recognizing that he has already lost the argument and is only negotiating the terms of his surrender.

"Two days," he said. "You surface when I signal, whether you've filled your bag or not. You eat when I tell you. You stop when I tell you. And if Calloway's boats come near the point, we abandon the quota and run, and find another way, even if it's a worse one."

"Agreed."

"That's settled, then," Brack said dryly from the door, "now that the married couple's arranged the household." He came inside at last, dropping the bar behind him, and Tamsin saw fresh scrapes along his forearm she didn't like the look of. "Which leaves the hulk. And before anyone asks — no, I haven't forgotten my brother's name is written in those letters same as the rest. I'm in. What I want to know is the shape of it."

* * *

They worked through the night, and it was the kind of work Tamsin loved without admitting she loved it: the taking-apart of an impossible thing into pieces small enough to carry.

Vane drew the hulk from memory — the *Providence*, once a man-o'-war, once a transport, now a floating jail anchored in the roads off Gull Point. His charcoal moved slowly; his hand cramped often, and once Edmund had to prise the pencil from fingers seized rigid, and massage them back to life without either of them speaking of it. But the drawing, when it came, was exact. Gratings fore and aft. A sentry platform at the bow, one at the stern. The tide race that swept the northern anchor chain, and — marked with a small, bitter cross — the grate over the orlop ports, iron, three feet square, bolted from outside.

"Bolted," Brack said, tapping it. "So we bring a file and a prayer."

"We bring cold chisels and the slack of the low tide," Vane said. "The bolts are below the waterline at high water. At slack low, they're exposed for perhaps forty minutes, and the sentries on the bow platform can't see down the ship's flank in that light even with a lantern. I know, because for two years I've lain in the dark memorizing exactly what they can't see." He looked up, and there was something in his face that hadn't been there when Tamsin first saw him in this loft — not hope exactly, but its raw material. "Forty minutes. Two men at the bolts, two men watching the platform. And a boat that doesn't knock."

"I have a boat," Brack said. "Gunny-built, tar-black, pulls quiet. It's yours." Then, after a pause that cost him visibly: "It was my brother's."

Nobody thanked him. Thanks would have been a kind of theft.

They divided the nights like coins. Tomorrow, Tamsin and Edmund on the outer bed, filling the quota, packing the first crates under her seal at the sorting shed where her authority ran and no questions were asked of wet shell. The same hours, Brack and one of his men reconnoitring the hulk's anchorage from the mangrove side, counting sentry rotations. The third day, the last crates down the chute to the lighters, and the tide at its lowest after dark, and the chisels at the bolts. Everything balanced on the fourth dawn, when the fleet's topmen went aloft and the great ships shook out their canvas and the colony's attention scattered to the four winds like ash.

"It's too much," Edmund said once, deep in the small hours, staring at the charcoal plan as though it might rearrange itself into sanity. "Two operations on one tide. Either one alone is desperate. Together—"

"Together they cover each other," Tamsin said. "Think it through instead of feeling it. While everyone who might catch us is watching the gate for runaway letters, nobody's watching the water off the hulk. While everyone who might notice a missing prisoner is watching the shell quay for smuggled papers, nobody's counting sentries. Calloway has one suspicion — that the evidence tries to leave by the gate — and a man with one suspicion guards one door." She picked up a piece of shell from the sorting pile, turned it in her fingers: nacre catching the lamplight, that buried shine. "He thinks we're trying to sneak past him. We're going to walk our truth out through his own pocket, wearing his own profits for a disguise. He'll never search it. Nobody ever searches the money."

Vane laughed then — a short, rusted sound, half cough — and pressed his hand flat against the charcoal drawing of the ship that had eaten two years of his life.

"My God," he said softly. "Roe, where did you find this woman?"

"She found me," Edmund said. "At a checkpoint. She was supposed to arrest me."

"Best mistake I ever made," Tamsin said, and meant it, and did not look at him while she said it, because some things were easier to carry unwatched.

* * *

Near dawn, when the others had curled into sleep where they sat — Vane breathing carefully, guarding his injured ribs even unconscious, and Brack snoring like a dispute with the sea — Tamsin stood at the loft's single window and looked down at the harbor coming greyly awake.

The fleet lay at anchor in three lines, masts bare, rigging hung with early mist. Somewhere on that middle line, in a cabin with brass fittings and ledgers bound in calfskin, Mr. Calloway was sleeping the sleep of a man who believed he had already won. He had doubled the guards. He had moved the sailing date. He had bought her mother's debts and offered them back to her as a leash, and when that failed he had taken a boy's beating out on an old smuggler and gotten nothing for it. He was, she had to admit, competent. He was thorough. He was doing exactly what a factor ought to do to protect a quarterly dividend.

He had just never once, in all his careful arithmetic, considered the possibility that the dividend itself could carry the knife.

Tamsin counted what she owned, the old habit, the litany that had steadied her since childhood. A knife. A compass, needleless no longer — the true needle rode in its brass bowl now against her breastbone, and she felt its small weight with every breath. Her mother's silence, which had turned out to be love wearing armor, and which she was learning to carry differently. And now this: a plan, a crew, a debt of four hundredweight of shell payable to the enemy, payable in the enemy's own coin.

Three days. Two tides. One bed of deep water past the point, where the current ran cold and the big shell grew in the dark, and where tomorrow she would teach a landsman to tend a lifeline while she filled the crates that would carry two years of a drowned man's testimony out through the one door in the port that no law, no guard, and no factor of the trading company would ever think to open.

Below her, the tide began to turn, and far out past the headland, under the cold green morning water, the deep beds waited — and somewhere beyond them, black against the brightening roadstead, the hulk *Providence* swung at her chains like a promise neither of them intended to keep.
