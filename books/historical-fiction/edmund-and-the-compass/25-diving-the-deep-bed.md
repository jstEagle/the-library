## 25. Diving the Deep Bed

The outer bed lay three miles past the last marker buoy, where the harbor's shelter gave out and the open water began its long argument with the land. Every diver on the coast knew it by reputation and avoided it by habit. The shell there was fat and flawless — the deep water grew them slow and perfect, like anything that suffered — but the bed sat in forty feet of cold current, over ground that dropped away into blue nothing, and twice in Tamsin's memory it had taken men who knew what they were doing.

She stood at the rail of Brack's barge in the grey first light and looked down at it anyway.

"You don't have to fill the whole quota yourself," Edmund said behind her. He had the lines coiled over his shoulder, her spare knife at his belt, and the look of a man who had rehearsed an argument all night and intended to lose it gracefully. "Brack can hire two more divers. We have four days."

"Three now." She tested the knot on her weight belt without looking at him. "And no diver we can hire will pack that shipment. It has to be my contract, my name on the manifest, my shell in the crates. The one cargo Calloway's own men never search because searching it would mean admitting his profit might be touched." She turned and found him watching her with that careful stillness he wore like a borrowed coat. "You want the letters to travel, they travel inside the company's own greed. That means the quota gets filled. Today."

"The letters are sewn into your suit," he said quietly. "You'll be carrying them down with you."

"I know."

"Tamsin—"

"Edmund." She said his name the way she'd say *hold* to a man on a line, and he went quiet. "I've been diving since I was seven years old. I've held my breath past sense for less reason than this. Let me do the thing I'm actually good at."

* * *

The morning was flat calm, which was its own kind of lie. Flat water meant the current underneath ran unchecked, sliding along the bottom like a hand along a shelf, feeling for whatever wasn't fastened down. Tamsin stripped to her diving suit — the patched canvas one, heavier than the company issue, the one with Vane's letters pressed flat against her ribs in their oilcloth wrapping, sewn in by lamplight while Edmund held the needle steady and pretended his hands didn't shake.

She checked the stitching one last time. Eighteen pages against her skin. A drowned man's testimony riding on the body of a woman the company considered equipment.

"If I don't come up," she said, adjusting the rope girdle at her waist, "cut the crate seals and get the shell shipped anyway. The letters matter more than—"

"No."

"It's arithmetic, Edmund."

"It's not." He took her hands, and his were cold, and steadier than she expected. "I spent two years doing arithmetic with Aldous's life. Counting what could be carried, what could be risked, what a man was worth against a page of paper. I'm finished with it." His grip tightened. "You come up. That's the whole plan. Everything else is detail."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she pulled her hands free, spat over the side into the water — the old courtesy, asking permission of the thing below — and went over the rail feet-first.

* * *

The cold hit her like a door closing.

Forty feet is nothing, on a good day, in warm water, over sand you can see. Forty feet in the outer bed was a descent through layers of temperature, each colder than the last, the light going from silver to pewter to a green so dark it was nearly a sound. She equalized her ears in rhythm — swallow, pinch, press — and let the weights take her down, watching the bottom resolve out of the murk: black coral heads, ridges of shell gravel, and the beds themselves, spreading wide and pale as a field of upturned moons.

The shells were beautiful. She hated them a little for it.

She got to work.

Diving for shell was not swimming; it was farming conducted underwater, in apnea, by feel. She moved along the bed prying the big ones loose with her knife blade, working them free of the substrate, stuffing them into the net bag at her hip. Each shell meant ten seconds bent to the task, then the long kick back up, the gasp at the surface, thirty breaths, down again. The rhythm was older than language in her body. Her mother had taught it to her before she could read. *The sea doesn't care how brave you are,* her mother said. *It cares how patient.*

But patience was a currency, and today she was spending it faster than she earned it.

By the third hour the bag was half full and the current had arrived. She felt it first as a tug at her fins, then as a pressure along her flank when she turned, the whole ocean leaning on her like a drunk on a fence. The water below the bed darkened where the ground fell away, and she made herself not look at it. Divers who looked at the drop-off started thinking about it, and divers who thought about it breathed wrong, and divers who breathed wrong came up too fast or not at all.

Down again. Pry, twist, bag. Down again.

On the ninth dive she stayed too long.

It wasn't a decision. It was arithmetic — her own this time, the cold kind. The quota needed two hundred pounds of shell. The barge crew would pack it by dusk. The fleet sailed in three days. Every dive she skipped today was a dive she couldn't recover tomorrow, and tomorrow the weather might turn, and the day after the gate opened and everything they'd bled for walked into Calloway's trap on the strength of whether her name appeared beside a full weight of shell. So she stayed down when her chest began to burn instead of when it began to speak, and she pried another shell loose, and another, and the burning climbed her throat and became a ringing, and the ringing became a white noise that ate the edges of the world.

Her vision narrowed to a tunnel. The shells in her hands went soft-looking, unreal, like things remembered rather than touched.

*Come up,* said a voice in her head that sounded like Edmund. Or maybe like her mother. Or maybe like herself, years younger, before she'd learned to treat her own body as a tool with a maintenance schedule.

She kicked for the surface and the tunnel shrank to a pinhole and somewhere very far above there was a circle of hammered silver light—

—and then air, violent and enormous, and her own name being shouted, and hands hauling her over the gunwale onto the deck planks where she lay gulping like landed fish while Edmund knelt over her with the rescue line still wrapped around his fists, having hauled the last six fathoms himself.

"Don't," she rasped, before he could start.

"You blacked out for a second. I saw it from here — you stopped kicking."

"I didn't stop. I *arrived late.*" She pushed herself up on her elbows. The sky wheeled gently overhead. "How long?"

"Four breaths you were under longer than your mark." His face was grey. Not angry — worse than angry. Frightened, and trying to file it under something else. "Tamsin. Four breaths."

"Then I've got four breaths of margin I didn't know about." She sat up, spat salt, reached for the bag. "Half full. Two more hours."

"I'm shortening your line."

"You'll do no such—"

"I'm shortening your line," he repeated, in the tone of a man reading terms aloud, "and I'm calling your rests, and if you argue I'll tie a knot in it every time you argue, and by evening you'll be diving off a rope the length of a scarf." He was already re-coiling the line, not looking at her, jaw set. "You told me once that everything you own you measure by what can be carried. Fine. But you're not cargo, Tamsin. You're the only person on this coast I can't replace."

She stared at him. The wind moved. Somewhere below them the outer bed went on growing its slow, suffering perfection, indifferent as a ledger.

"Two hours," she said finally. "Your rests. Your line."

"And you come up when I pull. No negotiation."

"No negotiation," she agreed, and meant it, mostly, and went back over the side.

* * *

The afternoon was war.

Not against the sea exactly — the sea was simply itself, vast and unbothered — but against the smaller enemy, the one that lived in her chest and kept revising its demands. Edmund ran her like a tide table. He counted her rest periods aloud, thirty breaths, no fewer, his voice carrying down the line in vibrations she felt more than heard. He'd rigged a second rope alongside hers, weighted, with knots every fathom, and when she surfaced he read her depth off it like scripture. Twice he pulled her up early, and twice she surfaced furious and alive, which she privately admitted was the point.

Between dives she lay on the deck under a blanket he'd fetched from Brack's stores without asking, and he sat cross-legged beside her with the manifest on his knee, tallying weights in a clerk's hand far neater than any clerk's had a right to be.

"One hundred and sixty pounds," he said. "Forty to go."

"Forty pounds is eight dives."

"Eight dives at proper rests is ninety minutes. We have daylight for it." He glanced up. "You're shaking."

"I'm always shaking after. It's the cold."

"It's not the cold." But he tucked the blanket higher around her shoulders, and his hand lingered a moment at the place where the oilcloth packet rode against her ribs, checking the stitching through the canvas, the way a man checks a wound dressing. "Still sealed."

"Still sealed." She watched the water. "Do you know what I was thinking about, down there? On the long dives. When the burning starts and the mind goes strange."

"Tell me."

"I was thinking about my father." She said it evenly, the way you say a depth sounding. "He worked the middle bed. Never the outer. He always said the outer bed was where the company's arithmetic showed — where they'd spend a diver to save a boat. And then they falsified his drowning anyway, on paper, in an office, dry as a sermon, and collected his debt against his own corpse." She flexed her hands, feeling the knife-calluses. "Every dive today, when it got hard, I thought: this is the part he never got to do. Get tired on purpose. Choose the water instead of drowning in it. There's a difference, Edmund. Nobody ever taught me the words for it until you came off that packet boat with a dead man's handwriting."

Edmund was quiet for a while. Below them the shells waited in their hundreds, pale and patient.

"Aldous used to say something like that," he said at last. "About the navy. About how a man spends his whole life being ordered into danger and calls it duty, and then one day he walks into danger *uninvited*, for his own reason, and understands he'd never once been free before." He smiled faintly. "He said it the night before he let the company think he drowned. I thought he was being dramatic. Sailors usually are."

"He wasn't dramatic. He was right." She sat up. The shaking had settled into something steadier — not calm, exactly, but resolved. "Give me the line. Forty pounds."

* * *

The last dives were the best of her life and she would never be able to explain that to anyone.

The sun lowered and turned the water to hammered bronze. The current eased as the tide turned, as if the sea had lost interest in the argument. And Tamsin dove the way she had not dove since she was young enough to love it — clean entries, long easy glides along the bed, her hands knowing every shell by touch, the whole apparatus of breath and body running like a well-kept engine. The letters pressed against her ribs with every descent, eighteen pages of other people's crimes warming slowly to the temperature of her own blood, and she found she no longer feared them breaking apart down there. Some things got stronger in deep water. She was beginning to suspect she was one of them.

At the seventh-to-last dive, she found the big one.

It lay half-buried at the edge of the bed where the ground began its fall into blue — a mother-of-pearl shell wider than her two hands spread, thick-lipped, heavy as a closed book. The kind of shell that held pearls the size of peas, the kind the factor wrote up separately in gold ink. She wedged her knife under it and worked, and the shell resisted, seated deep in the substrate like a fact someone didn't want known, and her chest began its burning early because she'd spent too much already, and she stayed anyway.

Pry. Twist. The shell groaned loose.

She rose through the bronze light with it hugged to her chest like a child, and broke the surface into the last of the sun, and Edmund's hands were there before her feet found the ladder, hauling shell and woman both aboard.

"Two hundred and six pounds," he said, hefting the bag, laughing — actually laughing, the first time she'd heard it. "You've overfilled the quota."

"Company arithmetic," she said, peeling back her hood. "Always round up. They taught me that too."

They packed the crates together as the light died, working by lantern, laying the shells in straw with the practiced economy of people who had both spent their lives making things presentable for inspection. The letters stayed sewn in her suit; the suit hung drying by the little stove; and between the top layer of shell and the crate lid, in the largest shell, in the hollow the meat had left, Edmund folded a single page — a duplicate, a decoy, a breadcrumb of the truth traveling in plain sight inside the company's own treasure.

"They'll search everything else on the coast," Tamsin said, nailing down the lid. "Every passenger, every trunk, every letter home from every homesick clerk. And this crate they'll wave through with their own hands, because to open it is to admit the pearl trade might be imperfect." She drove the last nail and sat back on her heels. "My father packed shell like this for nineteen years. Nineteen years of filling boxes for men who wrote him out of existence. Today I filled one box that writes him back in."

Edmund put his hand over hers on the crate lid. Neither of them said anything for a moment. The lantern hissed. Out past the harbor mouth, unseen in the dark, the quarterly fleet rode at anchor, four days from sailing, loaded with everything the company intended to keep.

Then boots on the jetty — quick, uneven, familiar — and Brack came up the barge ladder two rungs at a time with his coat flying and a face like weather.

"You'd both better hear this," he said, breathing hard. "Calloway's posted new orders at the customs house within the hour. The fleet doesn't sail in four days." He gripped the rail. "It sails tomorrow at dawn."
