## 26. The Bentham Letter

Tomorrow at dawn.

The words landed in the loft like a dropped anchor, dragging everything after them down into the dark. Edmund watched Tamsin's face as she took hold of them — the small involuntary tightening around her eyes, the way her hands went still on the crate lid, the arithmetic already running behind her expression. He knew his own face was doing the same work. He could feel it doing it: the route laid out and measured, the toll bridge crossed, the shell barge arranged, and now all of it cut down at a stroke to a handful of hours.

"That's impossible," Tamsin said. "The loading alone—"

"Is finished." Brack crossed the loft in three strides and pulled the curtain aside from the little window that faced the harbor. Below, the jetty lanterns burned in their usual order, but the water beyond them was wrong — dotted with lights where no lights had been two nights ago, boats moving between the anchored ships with cargoes slung over their rails. "They loaded through the night watch and they're loading now. I counted six lighters going out before I turned up this street. He's not waiting for the tide tables, he's not waiting for the last consignments. He wants the fleet gone."

"Then we go tonight," Edmund said. "We run the gate tonight, before dawn, while it's still chaos. A loading fleet means strangers everywhere — lightermen, tally-clerks, porters. One more man carrying crates won't be noticed if—"

"No." Brack didn't turn from the window. His voice had gone flat in a way Edmund hadn't heard from him before, and flatness from a man like Brack was worse than shouting. "You can't run the gate tonight because you're not running the gate at all now. Sit down, Roe. Both of you. There's something else."

There was something in the way he said *there's something else* that made the room colder. Edmund sat. Tamsin did not; she stood with her arms folded, watching Brack the way she watched water before a dive, reading what was underneath.

Brack came away from the window at last. From inside his coat he brought out an oilskin packet, small, tied with tarred cord, and laid it on the crate between them with a strange gentleness, as though it might go off.

"This came to me an hour before I heard about the sailing order. A boy brought it to the Shell & Anchor — wouldn't say who sent him, paid him too well to be nobody. It's addressed to me by my own name and to no one else." He worked the cord loose with blunt fingers. "It's Vane's hand. The same hand as the others?"

Edmund leaned forward. The packet lay open now, and inside it was a single sheet folded twice around something hard and small that clinked faintly against the crate boards. Brack drew out the paper and unfolded it under the lantern.

It was Vane's handwriting. Edmund knew it the way you know a voice heard across twenty years of water — the cramped left-leaning script, the letters that started strong and trailed off when the thought outran the ink, the habit of crossing his sevens so they looked like fives. He had spent two years with that handwriting in his coat pocket. He had read those letters until he could recite them. And this was it, unmistakably, impossibly — dated three days ago.

"He's alive," Edmund said. The words came out of him without permission, and for a moment the loft swam. He put his hand flat on the crate. "He's alive. Where—"

"Read it," Brack said. "All of it. Then tell me if you're glad."

* * *

Edmund read it aloud, because Tamsin deserved to hear it whole and because his eyes were not steady enough to be trusted alone with it.

*Roe — or whoever holds this, since I no longer know what name Roe goes by, or whether he breathes.*

*I will be brief, as brief as a man can be who has been brief for two years. You have my letters. If you are reading this, they reached hands I chose, which means someone I trusted kept faith, which is more than I expected of this coast.*

*Here is what your letters do not contain, because I did not know it when I wrote them. They think I drowned. That was always my protection — a dead man is not hunted, only missed. Three months ago a Malagasy trader recognized me at a river station and sold my description to the company for thirty pounds. Calloway's men took me at Kettering Point in the spring tides. I have been aboard the* Wrenholme *since — the hulk off Gannet Rock, if you know the harbor, and if you don't, look for the ship that never moves.*

*They believe I am Aldous Vane, clerk, debtor, forger of nothing but alibis. They do not know what I carried or where the rest of it went, because I gave the truth of myself up slowly and convincingly, being practiced. So I am worth more to them alive and ignorant than dead and discovered — until the fleet sails. Understand this plainly: the moment the quarterly fleet clears the heads, the port stops mattering. Whatever scandal might have caught here will be a thousand miles astern of the men who would answer it. A dead prisoner on a hulk is then a tidiness, not a risk. I have heard the mate of the guard ship say as much to a lieutenant who did not disagree. I have been on ships before. I know how quietly a man can go over the side.*

*So: your letters must pass the checkpoints before the fleet sails, or they pass never. And I will die within a week of its sailing, of a fever, of a fall, of the ordinary accidents that attend hulks. I do not write this to burden anyone. I have made my peace with water; every diver's child on that coast has made theirs. I write it so that no one spends my life buying my death by accident.*

*But I will confess one weakness, being old and tired and permitted one. If it can be done without losing everything — if the letters can go AND the man can walk out — then I should very much like to see the sun from a deck I chose myself, once more, before the account closes.*

*Enclosed, a thing for Tamsin Thornbury's hand, if she lives and if this reaches her. Tell her it was never lost. Tell her it was kept.*

*A.V.*

The small hard thing was still in the bottom of the oilskin. Brack tipped it out onto the crate.

A compass needle. Steel, worn bright along one edge where a thumb had rubbed it for years, and wrapped in a scrap of the same oilcloth, tied with a thread.

Tamsin did not touch it for a long moment. When she did, she picked it up the way she'd picked up shell in the shallows as a child — Edmund had seen that gesture in her, that precise tenderness — and held it close to the lantern, turning it, and he saw her jaw set against whatever moved through her.

"He kept it," she said. "Two years in a company cage and he kept a needle."

"It was her mother's compass," Edmund said quietly. "His gift to her. He told me once—" He stopped. There was too much to say about that, and none of it helped tonight.

"I know what it was." Tamsin closed her fist around the needle. Her knuckles went white. She looked up at Brack. "How does a man on a prison hulk get a letter off? How does he even know about the fleet, the checkpoints, us?"

"The guard mate's wife takes in washing for the officers' mess ashore," Brack said. "And the guard mate drinks, and his wife reads, and somebody paid her a long time ago to keep her ears open. I've used her twice before. Never for anything that mattered this much." He rubbed his face with both hands, and Edmund saw suddenly how tired he was, how much the last days had cost him. "The question isn't whether the letter's true. The question is what you do with it."

* * *

What you do with it. Edmund walked to the window Brack had abandoned and looked out at the harbor, where the fleet's lights rode at anchor like a spilled constellation, and let the two halves of the problem stand up in his mind and face each other.

Four days had been difficult. Tomorrow at dawn was arithmetic that refused to resolve. The fleet gate was built exactly for this — a funnel of company men, every outgoing crate tallied and thumbed, Calloway himself likely standing at the throat of it with his soft voice and his ledgers. Getting the letters through the gate in daylight, in the ordinary press of cargo, had been a gambler's chance. Getting them through in the hours before dawn, with the guards doubled and every lighter manifest checked against the master lists, was not a chance at all. It was a way of handing Calloway the packet personally.

And now layered over that impossibility was a second one. Gannet Rock lay two miles past the harbor mouth. The *Wrenholme* rode there — he had seen her silhouette a hundred times without ever seeing her, that rust-brown hulk that never moved, that every child in the port was told housed the mad and the ruined. To reach her meant a boat, a night, tides, a grate cut in the hull that a starving man remembered from a cell he'd described in a letter written in hope rather than certainty. To reach her meant trusting that the guard mate's wife was honest, that the letter wasn't bait, that Calloway had not extracted from Aldous Vane in five months of captivity the one fact that mattered: that somewhere on this coast, a man named Edmund Roe was carrying the evidence that would hang his masters' accounts.

"They know about the letters," Edmund said slowly, working it through aloud. "Or they suspect. Calloway doubled the checkpoints before any of this. He's hunting something. If Vane told them nothing — and he says he told them nothing — then Calloway is hunting blind. But if that letter is bait—"

"Then the letter was written by a dead man's enemies in a dead man's hand," Tamsin said, "and Brack's washerwoman is bought, and we're already in the net."

"Could be."

"But you don't believe it."

He turned from the window. She stood with the needle still in her fist, and the lantern light made small copper fires along its edge. "No," he admitted. "I don't. That's his hand. That's his caution — telling us not to come, giving us permission to leave him. He's done nothing in that letter but protect the mission, right to the end. That's not forgery. Forgery copies the strokes. It can't copy the character."

"You're sure of that?"

"No," Edmund said. "I'm sure of him. Which is a different thing, and a worse thing to bet a life on, because it's faith, not proof."

Brack had lowered himself onto the sea chest in the corner, and he sat now turning his cap in his hands, a big man made strangely still. "Say the letter's true," he said. "Say he's there and he's drowning slowly and the fleet sails at dawn. Here's what it costs. You cannot do both. Two miles to Gannet Rock and back, a grate to cut or pry, a man half-dead to move quiet and move fast — call it four hours, if the tide serves and nothing goes wrong. Nothing ever goes right, so call it six. Dawn is five hours off. Even if you left this minute with my best boat and my best luck, you'd come back from the hulk with the fleet's topsails already below the horizon and the gate shut behind them." He spread his hands. "The letters die with the tide either way. So choose. The message or the man. Paper or blood. Because the paper saves a hundred families and the man is one old sailor who already gave you everything he had, and I have watched good people break themselves in half trying to answer that sum, and there is no answer that doesn't bleed."

The silence after that had weight, like deep water.

Edmund found he was looking at Tamsin. He hadn't decided to; his eyes went there on their own. Because the sum Brack posed was not abstract to her — she was the hundred families, she was the inflated debt and the forged certificate, she was the name she'd found in Vane's letters written beside her father's drowning. If anyone on earth had cause to say *the letters first*, it was the woman standing in front of him holding a dead man's needle in her closed fist.

She was quiet for a long time. Then she opened her hand and looked at the needle lying across her palm, and Edmund watched her measure it — really measure it, the way she measured everything, by what could be carried and what could not.

"My father weighed less than a pearl crate," she said at last, and her voice was level and terrible. "I know because I carried the crate. The company taught me to count. Counting is easy. You want to know what I've learned that they didn't teach?" She closed her fist again. "Everything I have ever regretted began with a thing I counted instead of a person I didn't."

"Tamsin—" Edmund began.

"No. Let me finish, because I've only just understood it myself, standing here." She faced them both. "If we run the gate with the letters and leave him, we win. We expose Calloway, someday, somewhere, maybe. Names in newspapers in cities we'll never see. Debts forgiven by clerks who never met the drowned. And I will spend the rest of my life knowing that the first free act of my life was to spend an old man's life buying mine. That's not victory. That's the company's arithmetic wearing my face."

"And if we go for him," Brack said, "the letters rot in this loft, the fleet sails clean, Calloway wins, and Vane dies anyway, because a rescue without escape is just two more prisoners."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "That's the trap. It's a good trap. He built it well." She looked at Edmund, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with strategy. "So we refuse the trap. Not the letters OR the man. Both. In one night. In five hours."

Edmund laughed — a short, disbelieving sound — and then stopped laughing, because she wasn't joking, and because some part of his mind, the cold planning part that had mapped three checkpoints in a stranger's harbor, had already begun, treacherously, to try.

"Tell me how," he said. "Even if it's madness. Tell me how."

"Two problems, not one." She began to pace, three steps and turn, the length of the loft, a diver measuring depth. "Problem one: the letters. They were always going to ride out hidden in shell — my shell, my shipment, the one cargo Calloway's own tally-men wave through without opening because opening it costs them money. That hasn't changed. What's changed is the hour. The barge doesn't go through the gate until first light with the fleet's last consignments. So the letters sit packed and sealed and ready, and they need nothing from us except that we survive to see them loaded. The letters take care of themselves, Edmund. They've been taking care of themselves since Vane wrote them. What needs us tonight is the hulk."

"Then the hulk is problem two."

"The hulk is problem two, and here's the shape of it." She stopped pacing. "Gannet Rock sits directly on the fleet's departure line. Every ship leaving this harbor passes within a cable's length of it. At dawn, the roadstead will be full of moving ships — lighters, tenders, the fleet itself warping out on the ebb. A small boat among that traffic is invisible. Nobody questions a boat at sailing tide. But we can't wait for the traffic, because we have to be back before the gate shuts and the heads clear." Her eyes were bright and hard and frightened and certain, all at once. "Unless — unless the letters and the man leave by the same door. Unless Vane doesn't come back to this harbor at all. Unless he goes out on the fleet."

Edmund stared at her. Then he felt it click, the whole scheme assembling itself in his head like tackle running smooth through a block: the hulk lying on the fleet's own track, the last dark hours before dawn, a boat slipping out under the confusion of the loading, Vane lifted off the *Wrenholme* and handed up over the rail of a departing ship — or hidden in the shell barge itself, the same crate-line, the same blind eye, one more body among the company's own profit, carried past the gate that was built to catch papers and never thought to weigh flesh.

"He rides out with the letters," Edmund breathed. "The message and the man in the same shipment. One gamble instead of two."

"One gamble instead of two," Tamsin agreed. "And it fails completely or it works completely, and either way it happens once."

Brack had risen from the sea chest. He stood by the lantern with his cap crushed in his fists, and his face was doing something complicated — the smuggler in him appalled at the odds, the brother of a drowned man lit from behind by something else entirely.

"There's a hole in it big enough to drive the fleet through," he said. "The *Wrenholme* carries a guard of twelve and a swell of tide around that rock that'll eat a rowboat. You'd be boarding a armed hulk in the dark, in under five hours, with a man so weak he can't climb a ladder unaided, and getting away clean before the watch turns." He shook his head slowly. "I've run contraband past gunboats with better chances than that."

"I know," Tamsin said.

"And you're going to do it anyway."

"We're going to do it anyway," Edmund said, and heard his own voice arrive at it before his courage did, which was, he suspected, how courage usually arrived.

Brack looked at them both for a long moment. Then he put his cap on his head, squared it, and became abruptly all business, pulling charts from under the sail locker, and Edmund understood that the argument was over, that it had been over before it started, that somewhere in the last minute the smuggler had chosen.

"Gannet Rock," Brack said, spreading the chart and stabbing it with a blunt finger. "Then listen close, because the tide around that rock turns at half past two, and we get one slack, and one only."

Edmund leaned over the chart, and outside the little window the harbor lights of the fleet burned on, five hours from vanishing over the edge of the world — and somewhere out past them, on a rusted hulk that never moved, an old man who had given up hoping was keeping a needle's worth of hope alive without knowing it was already on its way.
