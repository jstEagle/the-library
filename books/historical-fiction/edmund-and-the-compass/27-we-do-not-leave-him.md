## 27. We Do Not Leave Him

The loft smelled of lamp oil and salt, and the Bentham letter lay on the table between us like a thing that had drowned and been washed back up.

Edmund had read it twice. I knew because I'd watched his eyes make the same pass over the same lines both times, as though the words might rearrange themselves if he only gave them a second chance. They didn't. Vane was alive. Vane was on the *Reprieve*, a hulk anchored off the middle ground where the harbor's refuse went to rot, and the moment the fleet's sails filled, a company man would come below with a rope and a story about how prisoners despair.

"Two days," Edmund said. "Maybe three before they do it quietly. And the fleet sails in four."

"Then we have time."

"We have nothing." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. In the lamplight he looked older than he had on the packet boat, older than he had at the customs house with my hand steady on his crate, older than he had under the toll bridge while I held my breath in the cold brown water and trusted him to walk through the front door without naming me. "Tamsin, look at what this is. It's paper. It was always paper. The letters prove what Calloway did — that's all they can do. Prove it. To whom? We've been over this. There is no magistrate worth the name between here and the capital. The whole point of getting these letters out was that somewhere, beyond this port, someone who isn't bought might read them. That was the mission. That was *his* mission." He tapped the table, not the letter but the space beside it, as if Vane were already a memory to be gestured at. "Aldous knew the risk when he stayed. He wrote it plainly. He said if this reaches the right hands, it won't matter what happens to him."

"He said that?"

"He said exactly that."

"Then he lied," I said, "or hoped, or both. Men write bravely when they think no one will live to check."

I picked up the letter myself and read it again, because I wanted to be sure of what I was arguing for. Vane's hand had deteriorated since the earlier letters — the confident loops gone thin and cramped, written, Brack said, in the dark of the hold by a man rationing his ink like food. But the last lines were clear enough:

*They believe I am their secret. When the fleet sails I am done, and they will say I despaired. If you are reading this, do not spend the truth buying my body back. Spend it well. Spend it forward.*

*But if you can —*

The sentence stopped there. Whatever Aldous Vane could not bring himself to ask for, he had left unfinished, and that unfinished sentence did more work on me than any full plea could have.

"He didn't finish it," I said.

"No."

"Because he thought Edmund Roe, deliverer of papers, would not come for a half-asked favor."

"Because he knows me," Edmund said quietly. "And he knows I'll do what the letter says instead of what it wants."

* * *

We argued until the lamp burned low. I want to record it honestly, because for years afterward people asked me — the few who ever heard the whole story — how we came to decide, and they always imagined some thunderclap moment, some grand declaration. There wasn't one. There was a table, a guttering wick, two exhausted people saying the same true things in opposite directions until the directions bent toward each other.

Edmund laid it out the way he planned everything: coldly, in steps, as if fear were an arithmetic you could carry. The hulk lay off the middle ground, a quarter mile of open water from the nearest shingle, guarded by a watch of perhaps a dozen company men who were not soldiers but had muskets and boredom, which together are worse. The tides turned twice a day; slack water came near midnight. A boat could cross unseen if there were no moon, and there would be no moon tomorrow night. Then the night after. Then the fleet sailed, carrying our shell shipment and our letters and whatever hope traveled inside sealed cargo no one searches because it is the company's own profit.

"And if we take the shell shipment out," Edmund said, "with the letters sewn where your suit carries them, and Vane still breathing his last on that hulk — the truth gets out, and he dies knowing it did. That's the trade the letter asks for."

"Yes."

"And you're saying we don't pay it."

"I'm saying the trade is false." I stood, because sitting felt like agreeing. "You've been telling me for weeks that the company's whole method is making deaths cheap. Falsify a drowning, void a debt, claim an estate. Paper over a person. Every name in those letters — my father among them — died twice. Once in fact and once in ink, and the second death is the one that let Calloway sleep at night. Now you want to save the ink and spend the man."

"That isn't fair."

"It's arithmetic," I said, and heard in my own voice the flat satisfaction of a phrase I'd taken from somewhere better and sharpened for a harder use. "You taught me that justice outside the law has to be paid for in something. Fine. But don't tell me the price is one man's life and call it a bargain. Aldous Vane spent two years writing those letters. Two years passing them out through smugglers' hands, crumb by crumb, at the risk of exactly what has now happened to him. You think the paper is the point? The paper is just him, flattened. He *is* the evidence. A living captain who walked into Calloway's counting house and walked out in chains. A witness whose scars match the ledger. You said it yourself three nights ago — the letters alone might be buried. A man who cannot be made dead again?" I leaned on the table. "That, they cannot bury."

He was quiet a long time. Down in the yard, Brack's boy was moving crates — I heard the scrape of them, the ordinary sounds of a smuggling house pretending to be a warehouse. Somewhere beyond the walls the tide was turning, patient, indifferent, already arranging itself for midnight two nights hence.

"You're asking for the impossible," Edmund said at last. "You know that. Rescue *and* delivery. The same hours. One boat, essentially one diver, four days, and Calloway hunting the water for a diver's child. Either enterprise alone would kill us comfortably."

"Yes."

"And you're smiling."

"I wasn't aware of it."

He put his head down on his arms then, briefly, the way a man does when he has lost and knows it and needs one unobserved moment to grieve the simpler life he might have had. When he lifted his face he was not smiling, but something had reorganized behind his eyes — I had seen it once before, on the packet boat, when he first understood I was following him not to betray him but to know him. Resolve looks different in different people. In Edmund it looked like a man picking up a weight he'd been hoping someone would take from him.

"The message or the man," he said. "That was never really the choice, was it. It was a choice I invented so I'd only have to fail once."

* * *

Brack came up an hour past midnight with bread, cheese, and the particular silence of a man who has been listening at stairs and wants credit for tact.

"Well," he said, setting the plate down. "You've either settled it or killed each other."

"Neither," Edmund said. "We've expanded the operation."

Brack looked at me. I told him plainly: the hulk, the tide, the fleet gate, all of it, folded into one plan that had no right to exist. His brother's name was in Vane's letters — Jem Brack, logged drowned off the outer bed eleven years ago, his debt sold to the factor within the month. Brack had burned his own safe-conduct papers over our fire the night he decided the cause was his, and I had watched a careful man become a committed one, which is a rarer transformation than brave.

He listened all the way through without interrupting, which I learned later was his habit when a plan was either very good or very bad.

Then he said: "The hulk's grating. The one Vane described. Below the waterline on the landward side?"

"Toward the channel," Edmund said. "He wrote that the grate was replaced after a man tried to swim — the new one's bolted, not welded, because the hulk's crew steals everything not nailed down, including their own fittings."

"So it comes off with tools." Brack scratched his jaw. "And the tools go on with a diver. And the diver holds air for—" he looked at me, and I saw him doing sums about lungs and cold water and distance that I had already done in the dark of my own skull, and done worse.

"Long enough," I said.

"Or she blacks out at the grate and drowns ten feet from freedom, and we lose her, the letters, and the captain in one tide."

"Jem," Edmund started — using the brother's name deliberately, a card I hadn't known he held — "is worth—"

"Don't." Brack's voice didn't rise. "Don't spend my grief at me to win an argument. I'm in. I was in before you asked. I'm telling you the costs because nobody else in this rotten port will." He sat down heavily and pulled the bread apart into three pieces with the mechanical care of a man feeding men he'd decided to keep alive. "Here's what it wants. My boat, which they took at the bridge, so first I get the boat back, which means trading the last thing of value I own, which is a favor owed me by a man I'd rather never see again. A cold dive at slack water with tools lashed to your ribs. And then — this is the part you two haven't thought through, because you think in straight lines — then you have a half-dead captain in an open boat with nowhere safe to put him before the gate, where Calloway is waiting for a shipment he already suspects."

Silence. The lamp fluttered.

"So we don't put him anywhere safe," I said slowly, and the shape of it arrived in my head almost fully formed, the way a pearl bed reveals itself when the silt stirs. "We put him where Calloway can't touch him without confessing. We put him *in the shipment.*"

Edmund stared. "In the shell barge."

"In a shell crate, packed like product, riding to the fleet gate with the rest of the company's profit. Nobody searches shell going out. Nobody has *ever* searched it — it's the one sanctity the company keeps, because it's the one cargo that's theirs." I could feel the audacity of it in my teeth, the way you feel a depth change. "Calloway sets his trap for the letters. Let him find a hold full of shell. Let him lift every crate if he wants. What's he going to arrest? Oysters?"

"And Vane rides out with the fleet," Edmund said, "in the belly of the machine that caged him." He began, despite himself, to laugh — low, disbelieving. "He'll appreciate that. He'll appreciate it more than the rescue."

"He'd better," Brack muttered. "Because it's insane, and I'm providing the barge."

* * *

Later, when Brack had gone below, Edmund and I stood at the loft's one window, watching the harbor breathe. Lanterns moved along the quay like slow fireflies. Out past the point, invisible, the *Reprieve* sat black on black water with a living man in its hold, finishing a letter he never expected to survive sending.

"I came here to deliver paper," Edmund said. "That was the whole of it. Carry this, give it to someone honest, go home. I rehearsed it on the boat — I'd hand it over, take a room, drink one glass of something decent, and sail out on the next tide. Clean. Weightless." He turned the empty envelope over in his fingers; he kept it always now, the way I kept the compass. "Do you know when it stopped being possible? It was the customs house. Your hand on my crate. You looked at me for maybe two seconds and decided — I still don't know what you decided — but something opened, and after that I couldn't stop seeing people where I'd only seen roles. Inspectors. Factors. Guards. Dead captains. You made everyone a person, Tamsin, and it's ruined me for efficiency forever."

"Is that a complaint?"

"It's a confession." He set the envelope on the sill. "I'll tell you what I told myself under the toll bridge, giving my real name to a sergeant like a fool, because some part of me was already done hiding. I came to deliver paper. I'm staying to deliver a man. And if it goes wrong — if the tide turns early, if the grate won't give, if Calloway is smarter than the hole in his trap — then I'll have risked everything for one person instead of a principle, and I find, examining myself honestly, that I mind less than I expected. Principles drown gracefully. People don't."

The moonless window showed us nothing but our own reflections, faint as watermarks, side by side.

"Tomorrow I dive the outer bed," I said. "The quota has to be real. The crates have to be full, the shells have to be ours, everything above-board and boring, or none of the rest matters. And the night after, slack water near midnight, I go under the hulk with the tools."

"And I tend your line," Edmund said, "and then I sit in a boat holding position under a prison ship with a rescued captain and a borrowed compass bearing, praying the watch doesn't turn early." He exhaled. "Say it once more. The whole shape of it. I want to hear it spoken aloud by someone sane, so I can measure exactly how far from sanity we've moored."

So I said it. The dive, the quota, the packing, the hulk, the grate, the cold swim home, the crate of shell riding to the gate with its human cargo, the trap sprung on empty water, the fleet sailing with the truth stitched in a diving suit and standing upright in a crate of oysters. Four days. One boat. No law, no magistrate, no permission, no retreat.

When I finished, the loft was very quiet, and Edmund was looking at me with an expression I couldn't name until suddenly I could: it was the way men look at a coastline they intend to remember.

"We do not leave him," he said.

"We do not leave him," I agreed, and blew out the lamp, and somewhere out on the black water the tide turned, right on schedule, carrying our plan out with it toward morning.
