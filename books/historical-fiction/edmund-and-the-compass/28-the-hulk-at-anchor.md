## 28. The Hulk at Anchor

The boat Brack gave us was called the *Maggie D*, and she was a lie in every particular that mattered.

She was registered as a shell-lighter, licensed to ferry cargo between the barge anchorage and the shallows off the outer bed — a workboat's papers for a workboat's shape, broad-beamed and slow, with a hold that smelled of crushed oyster and years of brine. But beneath her false floor, under a lattice of rotted slats that any customs man would take for ballast, there was room enough for four men to lie flat, and a scuttle cut into her bilge that could be opened from inside with one hand. Brack had built her that way himself, he said, back when the company still bothered to chase smugglers instead of becoming them.

"She'll float," he told us on the night beach, his voice pitched low against the slap of water. "She'll float all night if you ask it of her. What she won't do is run. If you're seen, you're seen. There's no outswimming anything with sails."

"Then we won't be seen," Edmund said.

Brack looked at him for a long moment in the dark, and whatever he saw there seemed to satisfy him, or perhaps only amuse him. "That's what they all say," he said, "the ones who come back and the ones who don't."

* * *

They put out two hours before slack water, when the tide had spent most of its strength and the estuary's current ran sluggish as an old man's blood. Edmund took the tiller because Tamsin was needed forward with the line and the lead, and because — though he would not have said so aloud — he wanted something to hold. The *Maggie D* moved through the black water with a sound like breathing, and the port fell away behind them by degrees: first the lights of the customs house, then the tavern district's smudge of orange, then only the harbor beacon turning its slow white eye across the roads.

Tamsin crouched at the bow, sounding every few minutes. She'd tied a rag around the lead weight so it would strike the bottom without a ring, and each time she brought it up she read the depth by feel alone, her fingers walking the wet line like a blind woman reading scripture. She did not need light. She had been reading this water since she was six years old.

"Nine fathoms," she murmured. "Channel's swinging north. We should be coming up on the shoal spit soon."

Edmund checked the letter again. He had read Vane's last communication so many times now that the words lived behind his eyelids, surfacing unbidden in the gaps between thought — *the hulk lies at the eastern mooring, past the shoal spit, where the company keeps what it does not wish seen from shore. Two boats of sentries, one lantern fore, one aft. They change the watch at the turn of tide. There is a grate on the landward side, below the waterline, where they once pumped bilge. It has not been used in years. It is not locked well.*

*It is not locked well.* Four words carrying a man's life in them, written in a hand gone thin and shaky toward the end, as though the writing itself were starving.

"There." Tamsin pointed with her chin.

At first Edmund saw nothing. Then the darkness ahead resolved itself into layers, the way a reef resolves for a diver — first the shape, then the structure, then the meaning. The hulk sat low on the water a half-mile out, a great black absence where the stars should have been, masted but bare-sailed, her yards crossed and silent. A single lantern burned at her bow and another at her stern, twin points of amber that made the darkness between them seem deeper by contrast. Beyond her, farther east, the riding lights of the fleet lay scattered across the roads like a drowned constellation — forty ships waiting for a departure date that Calloway had already moved twice.

"She's bigger than I expected," Tamsin whispered.

"She was a ship of the line once." Edmund eased the tiller over, bringing the *Maggie D* around in a long, patient arc that would set them drifting down on the hulk's landward side with the last of the tide. "Vane served aboard her before she was razeed. He says they cut away two decks and filled her holds with men. Three hundred souls, maybe more, packed into a box that can't sail anywhere."

"And nobody asks about them?"

"The company asked the magistrate to declare the hulk outside harbor jurisdiction. A prison afloat isn't anyone's parish." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and let it stand. "It's the same trick as everything else they do. Put a thing where the law ends, and call the ending order."

* * *

They shipped oars a quarter-mile out and let the tide carry them, which was the whole art of it. A boat under way makes noise; a boat adrift makes none, or nearly none, and the eye of a bored sentry will slide over stillness far more readily than motion. Edmund lay along the thwarts with his cheek almost touching the wet planking, watching the hulk grow against the sky, and beside him Tamsin breathed the way she breathed underwater — slow, measured, spending nothing she didn't have to.

The grate was where Vane had promised. They drifted past it close enough to touch, and Edmund counted in his head as it slid by: below the channel line, half-hidden by weed and the shaggy skirt of old copper sheeting, a square of iron bars perhaps three feet wide, its lower edge kissing the water at low tide. Someone had painted it — once, years ago — and the paint had blistered and flaked until the bars showed rust-brown through the gray. At the grate's corner, where the frame met the hull, a padlock hung from a hasp, and even at this distance, even in starlight, Edmund could see the lock was small. A ship's stores lock. The kind kept for coal hatches and spirit rooms, chosen for cheapness rather than strength.

He marked its position against the hulk's stern lantern and committed the bearing to memory. Then he pressed two fingers to Tamsin's shoulder — their agreed signal — and she let a coil of line slip silently over the side, paying it out to measure their drift while giving the appearance, to any watching eye, of a fishing boat's gear gone overboard.

The sentries changed at the turn of the tide, just as Vane had written. Edmund heard it before he saw it: the scrape of a booted foot on deck high above, a voice too low to parse, the clink of a lantern being lifted from its hook. The bow light swung and dipped as a man carried it aft along the deck, and for eleven seconds — Edmund counted them, eleven seconds exactly — the landward side of the hulk went entirely dark.

Eleven seconds. Long enough for a man to cross a deck. Not long enough for a man to cross a deck carrying another man who hadn't walked in two years.

He turned that arithmetic over in his mind all the rest of the drift, and it did not improve with handling.

* * *

They anchored in the lee of the shoal spit, where the ebb had piled a ridge of sand just beneath the surface and the *Maggie D* could sit quiet among three other shell-lighters doing precisely nothing, indistinguishable from idleness. From there the hulk stood silhouetted against the fleet beyond, and Edmund watched her the way he had once watched enemy forts from the masthead of a merchantman in the Java seas — not looking at the thing itself but at its rhythms, its habits, the small repetitions that no discipline ever quite erased.

In four hours he learned the hulk's litany.

A boat patrolled the seaward side once each hour, pulling a lazy circuit with a hooded lantern, its crew of four more concerned with staying warm than with searching anything. The patrol never came within thirty yards of the landward side; the weed-choked shallows and the shoal spit made the approach awkward, and awkward approaches discouraged routine. On deck, the watch stood in pairs at the bow and stern, near the lanterns, where the wind came least. Between the hours of midnight and two, when the tide slacked and the cold settled deepest, both pairs retreated to the deckhouse for perhaps twenty minutes at a time — Edmund timed them against his pulse, and his pulse against the stars, and got twenty minutes twice, consistent within a breath.

And once — only once, in the smallest hours — a scream came from deep inside the hull. Thin, distant, quickly stifled, the sound of a man crying out in a place where crying out had long since stopped meaning anything. Tamsin's hand found Edmund's arm in the dark and gripped it hard, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that the scream hadn't already said.

"He's alive in there," she finally whispered. "Three hundred men, and one of them is Aldous Vane."

"One of them is Aldous Vane," Edmund agreed, "and in six days the fleet sails, and the day after that, if Calloway keeps his schedule, Aldous Vane goes over the side with a shot bag around his neck, and the hulk reports another death from fever. Fever kills often enough aboard those things that no one counts anymore."

"Then we go tomorrow night."

"We go tomorrow night," Edmund said, "or we don't go at all."

* * *

They came back in the gray hour before dawn, running up the estuary with the young flood under them, and found Brack waiting at the shell barge with coffee boiled strong enough to strip varnish and a face that asked questions so he wouldn't have to.

"It's possible," Edmund told him, crouched over the chart Brack kept rolled in an oilskin tube — a smuggler's chart, annotated in pencil with depths and currents and the private geography of a man who had spent thirty years learning what the official maps left out. "The grate is real. The lock is real and it's poor. The watch has a gap at slack water, twenty minutes, maybe twenty-five. The patrol boat ignores the landward side." He laid it all out flat, point by point, the way he'd once laid out cargo manifests, because numbers were steadier than hope and he needed something steady to hold onto. "What isn't possible is doing it quietly with two people. One swims in and cuts the lock. One holds the boat. That leaves no one to bring Vane out through the shallows, and no one watching the deckhouse, and no margin at all if anything goes wrong. And something always goes wrong."

"So we need hands," Brack said.

"We need hands who can keep their mouths shut and swim if it comes to that, and there are perhaps four people in this port who fit that description, and one of them is currently lying in the dark with cracked ribs because Calloway's men worked him over."

Brack poured coffee without answering. When he did speak, it was to the chart rather than to either of them. "There's a lad named Petchy who runs my lines on the outer bed. Quick in the water, quicker with his fists, dumb as a post about anything that matters and sharp as glass about everything that doesn't. He'll come if I ask. He owes me, and he hates the company, and those two things together make a man reliable enough." He tapped the shoal spit with a blunt finger. "And the widow Okonjo keeps a boat at the creek mouth that draws less water than a gull. She's carried worse cargo than us."

"That's three," Tamsin said. "You've counted yourself?"

"I've counted myself." Brack straightened, and in the lamplight his face looked older and harder than it had a week ago, before the toll bridge and the beating and the burning of his safe-conduct papers. "My brother's name is in those letters, girl. You think I'm standing on the beach for the exercise?"

Edmund studied the chart a moment longer, then looked up. "Then here's how it must fall. Slack water comes at half past one tomorrow night. We cross on the last of the ebb, lie off the spit until the watch goes into the deckhouse. Tamsin and Petchy swim in with the tools and open the grate. I go in after Vane — he knows me, and a man two years in a hole needs a face he trusts on the other side of the bars, or he'll fight us thinking we're company trickery. Brack holds the *Maggie D* at the spit with the widow's boat as relay. If the patrol comes early, we sink to the bottom and breathe on the weed line until it passes." He paused. "If any part of it fails, the others leave. Whoever's inside takes his chances. No one waits, no one doubles back, no one dies for a rescue that's already dead."

Silence held around the lamp. Outside, the gulls were beginning their first arguments of the morning, and somewhere across the water the hulk sat on its chain like a spider in a web made of tide.

"No one waits," Tamsin repeated quietly. It wasn't agreement. It was memorization — the words filed away to be disobeyed later, and Edmund knew it, and knew there was nothing to be done about it, and loved her a little for it, and hated that he did.

Brack rolled up the chart. "One thing more," he said. "You'll want to know what Vane looks like after two years, so you don't lose your nerve when you see it. And you'll want to know what a man looks like after two years in a hole, so you know whether he can swim at all." He met Edmund's eyes. "Because if he can't, someone carries him. And whoever carries him doesn't swim. Do you understand me? The plan has to survive that arithmetic too."

Edmund understood. He had understood it since the scream in the night, since the eleven seconds of darkness, since the small cheap padlock hanging on its hasp like an insult. Every plan was arithmetic in the end, and the sea always collected.

"Half past one tomorrow night," he said. "We go at slack water, and God help whichever of us the tide finds first."

Across the dark water, faint as a heartbeat, the hulk's bell rang the change of watch.
