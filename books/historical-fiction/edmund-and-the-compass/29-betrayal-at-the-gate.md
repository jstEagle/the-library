## 29. Betrayal at the Gate

Mr. Calloway had always believed that betrayal was a matter of arithmetic, and arithmetic never lied to a man who kept his columns straight.

He sat in the factor's office with the shutters drawn against the morning glare, and before him, on green baize, lay a single page of copied correspondence. It had come to him in the night, carried by a boy with salt-cracked hands and the particular emptiness of expression that money buys cheaply in a port like this. The boy had named his price; Calloway had paid double it and deducted the overpayment from the boy's father's tally at the company store, so that in the end nothing left the house that had not already belonged to it.

The page was not much to look at. Water-stained ink, a cramped and hurried hand, a list of names and dates — the names of men the company had drowned on paper so that their debts would drown with them. Calloway read it three times. He did not read it the way a magistrate would read it, hunting for guilt. He read it the way he read everything: as a ledger entry in search of its error.

There was an error here. There had to be.

Vane was dead. Vane had been dead for two years, officially, tidily, with a certificate Calloway himself had countersigned and a grave at sea that no one had bothered to visit. Dead men did not write letters. Therefore these letters were copies, or forgeries, or relics smuggled out piecemeal before the drowning — and in any of those cases, they existed in finite quantity, in one place, in the hands of a small number of people who now had exactly one use for them.

They would try to send them out on the fleet.

Calloway poured himself tea that had gone lukewarm and did not notice. This was the trouble with ports, he had long ago decided: everything in them moved. Cargo moved, tides moved, debts moved, and men who should have stayed drowned came drifting back like driftwood marked with a house's name. Inland, in the counting houses of the capital, a man could build a wall of paper around himself and live behind it for forty years. Here the paper got wet.

But movement, he reminded himself, was also exposure. A thing being smuggled had to travel a road, and a road has only two ends. He knew the fleet's departure — four days hence, moved up from the original schedule by his own order, a stroke he permitted himself a small satisfaction in remembering. Anyone carrying proof of the company's arithmetic had less than a hundred hours to pass the toll bridge, evade the customs house, and clear the fleet gate. Desperate men made errors. Errors were entries, and entries could be audited.

He drew a fresh sheet toward him and began, in his own immaculate hand, a list of every shell shipment contracted to go aboard the quarterly fleet.

* * *

The list took him most of the day, and it was the taking of it that gave him his answer, though he did not know it yet.

Seventeen shipments. Sixteen of them were ordinary consignments: cured fish, hardwood, copra, bales of wool pressed so tight they rang when struck. The seventeenth was pearl shell — mother shell, deep-bed shell, the highest-value cargo the colony produced and the one shipment in the entire harbor that no inspector ever opened.

Calloway sat back and looked at that line for a long time.

It was company policy — his own policy, drafted six years ago, in a memorandum he could still recite — that pearl-shell shipments passed the fleet gate sealed and certified, without breaking of crates. The reasoning had been sound. Shell spoiled if exposed to sun and handling; the divers' quotas were tallied at the beach under a company clerk's eye; the value per crate ran to sums that made pilferage a flogging offense, so no carrier risked tampering. The seal was trusted because the profit was trusted. He had written, in effect, that the company's own greed was the best guarantee of its own security, and he had been pleased with the sentence at the time.

Now a dead man's letters were sitting somewhere in this port, and the only cargo in the harbor guaranteed never to be searched was pearl shell.

He did not celebrate the deduction. Celebration was for men with surplus energy, and Calloway spent none of his. He simply took up his pen and began the second list: every diver indentured to the company, every shell barge licensed to carry, every contract due to load before the sailing.

There were four barges scheduled. Three were worked by crews with no history worth auditing — hired hands, transients, men whose files were thin because their lives were thin. The fourth barge was worked out of the eastern diving ground, and its principal diver was a woman named Thornbury.

Calloway knew the name the way he knew all the numbers that mattered. Tamsin Thornbury, indentured eleven years, debt carried forward annually at the standard inflation, competence rated exceptional by two foremen and insubordinate by one. Mother: Maren Thornbury, widow of a drowned diver, pensioned off at a rate the company described as generous and Calloway's own ledgers described as nothing at all, since the drowning certificate had voided the husband's debt and the pension was charged against charitable reserves that did not exist except as a column heading.

And there — there, where the two columns touched — Calloway felt the first cold touch of something he would later refuse to call doubt.

The Thornbury woman had been at the customs house on the day the stranger arrived. He had her name from the duty roster; he had also, from his own quiet inquiries, a report that she had passed a passenger through as a hired porter without proper papers, and that a guard named Hollis had noted an extra crate and let it go after a conversation he had not reported. Two anomalies, in the same pair of hands, within the same week that a stranger arrived asking questions about Aldous Vane.

Calloway did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what incompetent men invoked when their audits failed. What he believed in was convergence: separate streams finding the same channel, and the channel in this case running through a woman who measured shell for a living and had, by every account, nothing in the world to lose except the debt the company held against her name.

Which meant, he thought, tapping his pen against the desk, that she was either the most loyal employee in the harbor or its most dangerous. And loyalty, in his experience, was a commodity that responded to price.

* * *

He summoned the diving foreman at dusk.

The man came in wiping his hands, smelling of brine and tar, and stood at attention with the particular stiffness of someone who has heard that the factor is asking questions and hopes not to be among the answers.

"The Thornbury contract," Calloway said. "Her current quota for the fleet loading. Read it to me."

The foreman read it. Deep-bed allocation, outer grounds, forty crates certified weight, to be packed and sealed at the beach under clerk's inspection, delivered to the fleet gate by barge on the second day of loading.

"Who packs the crates?"

"She does. Her and her mother, mostly. Company rule — the diver what fills the quota packs it. Keeps the tally honest."

"And the sealing?"

"Waxed canvas, cord, company stamp. Clerk witnesses the stamping." The foreman hesitated. "Begging pardon, Mr. Calloway, but is there a question about her tally? She's the best diver on the eastern ground, always has—"

"There is no question about her tally," Calloway said, and watched the relief move through the man like weather. "There is a question about her cargo. When her barge comes to the gate, I want it held at the quarantine berth. Not inspected — held. No one boards, no one unloads, no one so much as steps on deck until I arrive in person. Is that understood?"

"Held how long, sir?"

"Until I arrive."

The foreman nodded and went out, and Calloway sat alone with his lists, and this was the moment — though he marked it then only as a small irritation, a speck in the lens — when the first crack opened.

Because holding a barge was not catching a smuggler. Holding a barge told him only where the letters were not, if the crates proved clean; and if the crates proved dirty, it told him too late, after the barge was already at his gate, in front of his guards, in front of the fleet's officers and the captain-superintendent's watching eyes. A trap set in public was a trap that performed in public. If the Thornbury woman was carrying the evidence, he needed it taken quietly — taken before it reached any witness at all — or else the exposure he feared would happen on his own doorstep, with the superintendent standing three feet away.

No. The gate was the wrong venue. The gate was theater, and theater favored the accused.

What he wanted was the water between the beach and the gate. An interception on the tide, unofficial, unwitnessed. A barge that developed a leak and put in at a private jetty. A diver's cargo that went down in a squall, regrettable, uninsurable, gone.

Calloway took a fresh page and began planning with the pleasure he otherwise denied himself. He had three men he could use — harbor men, off the books, paid from a fund that appeared in the ledgers as *dredging contingencies*. He knew the eastern diving ground's working hours. He knew the route any barge must take to the gate, because he had approved the route himself. He even knew, from the copied page lying on his desk, the exact flavor of what he was hunting: names, dates, certificates in a drowned man's hand. Enough to hang a company. Enough, if it reached the wrong eyes ashore, to hang him.

He was still writing when the lamplighter passed in the street outside, and he thought — briefly, professionally, without warmth — of Aldous Vane, whom he had once shared a table with at a supercargo's dinner, and whose handshake had been dry and strong and whose eyes had counted Calloway's rings while they talked about wool tariffs. Vane had known, even then. Calloway was almost certain of it now. The letters had not begun when Vane drowned; they had begun years before, in silences exactly like that one, and Calloway had sat across the table adding up the wrong column.

It did not make him afraid. It made him careful, which was better.

* * *

On the third day, the trap went into the water.

Calloway watched it go from the factor's office window, or rather watched the absence of it go: three nondescript boats putting out from the coal jetty at staggered intervals, carrying men with fishing gear they did not intend to use. The plan was simple enough to survive contact with reality, which was his only test of a plan. The eastern ground would report the Thornbury barge departing on the afternoon tide. The boats would close on it in the narrows, board it as distressed vessels needing assistance — assistance being a word that covered many transactions — and bring it to the coal jetty instead of the gate. There, in the privacy of the coal sheds, the crates would be opened by lamplight, and whatever was sewn into canvas among the shell would be found by men whose discretion was purchased by the same fund as their silence. The woman would be detained. The stranger, wherever he was, would follow his evidence or lose it, and either way he would walk into reach.

It was, Calloway reflected, a good plan. It accounted for the tides. It accounted for the guard rotations at the gate, which he had arranged to be thinned at the relevant hour so that no curious sergeant came looking for a barge that had failed to arrive. It accounted for the clerk at the beach, who had been given the day's inspection duties elsewhere. It accounted for everything he knew.

The difficulty, which he could not have accounted for because no ledger in his keeping contained it, was that knowledge is only ever a record of what has already happened.

He spent the waiting hours at his desk, because waiting badly was a weakness and he practiced even his virtues. He signed requisitions. He initialed the fuel tally. He wrote and burned a letter to the superintendent declining a dinner invitation, on the grounds that the fleet's departure required his full attention, which was true, and that he wished to be unobserved for the next two days, which was truer.

At half past four, the report came: barge sighted leaving the eastern ground, under sail, single figure at the tiller.

Calloway rose and stood at the window with his hands clasped behind him, and watched the far water where the narrows ran, and waited for his trap to close with the patience of a man who had spent thirty years learning that the ledger closes eventually, for everyone.

* * *

The barge came into the narrows at sunset, and Calloway, watching through the factor's glass, saw immediately that something was wrong with it.

Not dramatically wrong. Nothing that would alarm a casual eye. But he had read the manifest himself that morning — he read everything — and the manifest described a heavy-laden shell barge riding low and slow, forty crates of wet mother shell at certified weight, a cargo that sat in the water like a loaded wagon. The vessel now standing up the narrows rode high. Too high. Light. Moving quick and eager on the evening breeze, a barge with no burden at all.

His mind began its arithmetic before his feelings could interfere. Forty crates missing. A light hull. A single figure at the tiller — broad-shouldered, bearded, moving with a boatman's roll rather than a diver's economy. And the timing: the barge had departed the eastern ground on schedule, on the reported tide, precisely as his informants had promised. Which meant either that his information was perfect and the cargo had been removed before departure, or that his information had been permitted to be perfect, which was a different animal entirely.

The copied page. The boy. Brack.

Calloway had never troubled to learn much about the smuggler Brack beyond what any factor knows: a loft over the night market, licenses of dubious provenance, a finger in every informal transaction in the port. He had bought the boy through Brack's household because Brack's household was permeable, and he had assumed — the assumption had cost him nothing at the time, which is the price at which all dangerous assumptions are sold — that Brack was a merchant of the ordinary kind, whose loyalty ran to whoever paid last.

Now a light barge sailed up the narrows toward his three boats, carrying a bearded man who was not a diver, and Calloway understood, in the space of one breath, that he was watching his own trap close on the wrong quarry.

He could not signal the boats. There was no signal agreed for *abort*, because abort had not been in the plan; the plan's only branch points had been *success* and *more success*. He watched, motionless at the glass, as the boats converged on the barge in the failing light, graceful as hawks, and he heard in imagination the boarding, the confusion, the swift correction — and then the other sound, the one he had not planned for, rising faint across the water:

A voice, raised not in fear but in negotiation. And then laughter. His men laughing.

The barge turned. Under escort now, two boats alongside, one astern, it came about and made for the coal jetty, and the bearded man at the tiller steered it willingly, waving, cheerful, a smuggler playing his part so well that for one disorienting moment Calloway wondered whether even this was the performance and the real cargo slept elsewhere entirely.

He made himself stop wondering. Wondering was a luxury. He went down to the jetty.

* * *

The coal sheds smelled of dust and whale oil, and in the lamplight the bearded man sat on an upturned crate with his wrists loosely bound, looking around at the three boatmen as if they were all guests at the same indifferent supper.

"Brack," Calloway said.

"Factor." Brack inclined his head with the ease of a man greeting an equal across a market stall. His lip was split — someone had been brisk in the boarding — but his eyes were unhurried. "You've made an error, I think. This is my barge. Empty, as you see. You're welcome to search it twice."

"Where is the shell shipment?"

"What shell shipment?"

Calloway let the silence do its work. Around him his men shifted; the lamplight swung; the tide sucked at the jetty piles below the floor. He had conducted a hundred interrogations in this port, most of them brief, because most men's fear arrived before their strategy. Brack's fear seemed to have missed the appointment entirely.

"The Thornbury barge," Calloway said. "Forty crates, deep-bed shell, contracted to the fleet. It did not sail tonight. Its cargo is not on your barge. Someone moved it, and someone moved it knowing my arrangements — which means someone knew my arrangements, which means your household leaks in both directions, Mr. Brack. You sold me a page. Who else have you sold?"

Brack looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind the smuggler's eyes that Calloway would spend the next day failing to interpret — not triumph, exactly, and not pity, though it wore some of pity's clothes.

"You bought one page," Brack said. "One page out of a stack this thick." He lifted bound hands, indicating perhaps an inch of air. "You think a man like me sells the whole book? You bought what I let you buy, Factor. You caught the barge I let you catch. And now you've got me, sitting on your own jetty, with your own boats, on the night your fleet starts loading — and every hour you spend in this shed asking me questions is an hour you're not spending at your gate."

For the first time in thirty years, Mr. Calloway felt the ledger tilt beneath him.

He did not show it. He ordered Brack secured in the coal shed's inner room, posted two men, and walked out onto the jetty into the wind, and stood looking at the black water where the fleet's masthead lights were coming alight one by one, and forced his mind back to its columns.

It was a bluff. It had to be a bluff. One man, beaten, bound, telling him to look elsewhere — the oldest misdirection in the trade. The shell cargo would come to the gate tomorrow as contracted, because it had to; a diver's quota was a legal obligation, and the Thornbury woman would deliver it or default, and either way she would come to him.

Except that the gate itself was the problem now, and Brack had seen it before he had. If the evidence was in the shell — and the shell was the only cargo in the harbor his own regulations forbade him to open — then the trap he had built at the coal jetty had just taught every watcher on the waterfront exactly where the company's attention was pointed: away from the gate, at an empty barge, for the whole of the night. He had shown his hand to the tide.

Calloway turned back toward the sheds, and stopped.

Through the shed's cracked door, faint in the lamplight, he could hear Brack whistling. Slowly, tunelessly, without a care in the world — the whistle of a man with nowhere to be.

And Calloway, who did not believe in coincidence, stood on his own jetty in the dark and admitted, for the first time in his life, that he did not know which column the next entry belonged in.
