## 30. Brack Does Not Talk

We heard it before we saw it. That is how wrongness announces itself in a port: by subtraction. The gulls went quiet over the north quay, and the tide of morning traffic that should have carried fish crates and rope and shouting women thinned to nothing, and then there was only the sound of our own breathing in the shell barge's dark and the small, ceaseless tick of Edmund's knee bouncing against a crate.

"He didn't come back," Edmund said, for the fourth time since dawn.

"He said he'd be back."

"That's not what I asked."

I made myself stop wringing the rag in my hands. It was already dry; there was nothing left to take out of it. "He said he'd settle the harbor master's man before first light and be back with bread and word about the hulk's watch rotation. He said it plainly, and Brack is plain. If he meant to run us aground, he'd have told us so with a smile and shaken our hands."

"That comforts you?"

"It's all I have," I said. "Comfort isn't on offer."

The loft above the barge smelled of brine and lamp oil and the sweet rot of pearl shell left too long in the sun — the smell of my whole life, rendered down. Through the gap in the shutters I could see the masts of the fleet, still three days out by the old schedule, two days by the new one Calloway had posted yesterday in his own hand, as if he could command the tide itself to hurry. Perhaps he could. Men like him had never once been told no by anything smaller than a storm.

Vane slept in the corner, or lay with his eyes closed pretending to sleep, which for a man who had spent two years on a hulk was probably the same discipline either way. His wrists were still ringed with the pale scars of irons. When he spoke, his voice came out rusted.

"They've taken him," he said.

"You don't know that."

"I was Calloway's cargo long enough to know how he loads it." Vane opened his eyes. They were the flat grey of weathered oyster shell. "If your smuggler walked into a company man this morning, he's already in the back room of the customs house, and Calloway is already pouring him wine."

Edmund stood, and I put a hand flat against his chest before he could finish standing. It was like stopping a door in the wind. "And go where? You'll walk into the same room, and then he pours wine for both of you, and everything Brack bought last night is wasted along with everything we are."

"Then what do we do?"

"What divers do," I said. "Wait at the surface until the water tells us something."

* * *

The water told us something at noon, though it was not the water. It was Brack's boy, Pilo, who came skimming along the seawall with the particular walk children affect when they are carrying news too heavy to run with. He did not come to the loft. He stood at the tide line, turned his face to the horizon, and waited until Edmund strolled past him close enough for a hand to pass between them. Then he spoke without moving his lips, the way children learn from watching their elders lie.

"They took the *Gull*. Took her at anchor, customs men, six of them. Took Uncle to the factor's house." A pause. "There's blood on the quay where they held him. I saw it from the rigging of the *Merrow*."

"How bad?" Edmund asked, low, eyes forward, a man admiring the harbor.

Pilo's jaw worked. "Bad enough that they carried him the last part."

Then the boy was gone, gone the way only children can vanish, sideways into a crowd, and Edmund and I stood looking at the beautiful blue nothing of the bay as if it owed us an explanation.

"The *Gull*," I said. The name sat in me like swallowed stone. Brack's boat was not just his living. She was the last thing his brother had built with his own hands — the brother whose name was written in Vane's letters among the drowned who were not drowned but murdered on paper, written off the way a clerk writes off spoiled stock. Brack had burned his own safe-conduct papers in front of us to prove what the cause was worth to him. Now Calloway had answered in kind, taking the one thing left that proved what Brack had been before he became what he was.

"He won't talk," Edmund said.

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly what frightens me."

Because I had seen men who wouldn't talk. I had grown up among them — a whole coast of people who kept their mouths shut around the company, who learned silence the way coastal children learn swimming, early and thoroughly, because it kept them alive. But silence has a breaking depth like everything else. Every diver knows it: the chest gives, eventually, at some number of fathoms, no matter the strength of the will inside it. The question that had begun clawing at me was not whether Brack would break but how much of him would be left when he did, and what shape the broken pieces would take when they came apart under pressure. A man can give up more than names. A man can give up times, places, faces — and Brack knew all three of ours.

* * *

We spent the afternoon doing the hardest work either of us had ever done, which was nothing.

Edmund re-wrapped the letters in oilcloth with hands that wanted to be doing violence and instead made each fold precise as surgery. Vane lay with his eyes closed and recited, very quietly, the watch rotations of the hulk as he remembered them from inside — which watch drank, which sentry looked landward when the wind came off the water, where the grate gave at slack tide. He was building us a map out of the worst years of his life, laying it down brick by brick in that rusted voice, and I understood suddenly that this was how he prayed.

I mended the seam of my diving suit where the shoulder had torn on the outer bed, stitching the letters' new home into the lining as I went, because if we were going to carry them through the fleet gate, they would ride against my skin, where no company man would dare search a contracted diver packing company shell. My needle moved. My mind would not.

Every hour I measured, the way I measure everything, and what I owned now came to this: a knife, a compass with its needle newly returned, a suit with contraband sewn into its ribs, a season's worth of pearls traded away to a man who might currently be having his teeth broken out of him, and a debt of loyalty so large it had no denomination the company's ledger would recognize.

At dusk, Pilo came again.

This time he came all the way up the barge ladder, which told me the news was worse or better, because caution costs energy and he had stopped spending it. His face decided the question before his mouth opened. Worse and better, braided together, the way most truths arrive on this coast.

"He talked," Pilo said.

The bottom fell out of the day. I felt Edmund go very still beside me, felt Vane's recitation stop mid-watch.

"He told them a departure time," Pilo went on, and now I saw it, the thing in his face that was almost a smile and mostly terror underneath. "Told them plain, gave it up like it cost him nothing, like they'd beaten it out of him fair. Said the packet with the evidence aboard sails on tomorrow's morning tide. Day after next, first light."

"But the fleet—" Edmund began.

"Sails in four days. We heard the new orders read on the quay. And Uncle gave the company a time two days *before* their own ships go." Pilo's voice cracked with the effort of keeping it level. "They believe him. They believe him because he cried, sir, and he doesn't cry easy, and they watched him cry."

I sat down slowly on a crate of shell. Because I saw it whole, then — the shape of what Brack had done in that back room with his face bleeding and his boat confiscated and his brother's name in stolen papers. He had given them a lie wrapped in the truest packaging available, which is pain. He had let them believe they had broken him, because a broken man's information feels like payment collected, and men like Calloway file payments and stop looking further. And the lie was perfect in its direction: it pointed all of Calloway's vigilance at a morning tide two days ahead, leaving the real night — tomorrow night, the actual turning tide, the actual hulk, the actual gate — unguarded by anything except Calloway's confidence in his own cruelty.

"He bought us a day," I said aloud.

"And paid for it with a beating and the *Gull*," Edmund said. "And if Calloway discovers the time was false—"

"He won't discover it until it's past being useful to us," Vane said. His eyes were open now, and there was something in them I hadn't seen before, some banked coal of the captain he had been before the hulk ate his body. "But hear the rest of the arithmetic, because your smuggler paid more than you think. They'll keep him. Calloway doesn't release a man who's told him something; he holds him until the truth of the thing is confirmed, one way or the other. Which means when we go through the gate on the real night, Brack will be in a cell, and every hour we gain from his lie is an hour he spends in Calloway's custody with the truth arriving around him."

Silence took the loft. Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, little lies of warmth strung across black water, and somewhere among the anchored shapes of the fleet, eight hundred men prepared to carry away the colony's attention along with its pearls.

"Then we don't spend his day on ourselves alone," I said.

Everyone looked at me. I found that my hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the last hour and I hadn't noticed, and that the arithmetic in my head had changed denominations entirely. I had lived my whole life measuring what things cost in what could be carried. Brack had taught me today that some things are carried in the other direction — that a man can pick up a beating, a lost boat, a cell, and walk upright under the weight, and call it nothing, and mean it.

"Calloway believes the packet sails the morning after next," I said. "So his guards will stack themselves at the customs house and the toll road tonight and all tomorrow, watching for us a full day early, in the wrong places, at the wrong hours. Tomorrow night is slack tide at the hulk. Tomorrow night is an empty gate watch and a bored sergeant. We go tomorrow night — for Vane, for the letters, through the gate. And we don't go clean."

"No," Edmund said quietly. "We don't."

"We go the way Brack went into that room," I said. "Carrying more than our share."

* * *

We worked through the night, and the strangest part was how ordinary it felt. Pearl diving had taught me that the great moments arrive dressed as labor. You swim out. You check your line. You fill your lungs past comfort and drop, and while the whole world watches for the drama, your hands are simply doing what hands do — prying, gathering, hauling on the rope. Rescue, it turns out, looks the same from inside. Brack's boy ran messages along the waterfront, mapping which customs men were pulled toward the morning-tide watch and which were left where. Vane drew the hulk's deck from memory in charcoal on the back of a sack, marking the grate with an X that looked almost gentle. Edmund forged a second clerk's pass, steadier than the first, and I watched his pen hesitate only once — over the name field — before he wrote *Brack, J.* in it, and I understood he meant it as a promise rather than a disguise.

Near low water, Pilo came back one more time and stood at the foot of the ladder with his cap in his hands, suddenly younger than the week had left him.

"There's blood in the water off the factor's wharf," he said. "The *Gull*'s been put out on the moorings with the company boats. Like she's nothing. Like she's theirs now."

"She's not nothing," I said. "And they're not theirs."

The boy looked up at me, and whatever he saw in my face made him nod and pocket his cap and go.

I stood at the shutter gap a long while, looking at the *Gull* riding her mooring in the last light, small and wrong-footed without her master, tugging at her lines like a dog testing a fence. Tomorrow night we would go out on the turning tide with a dead man's letters sewn against my heart, a live ghost hidden in a shell crate, and a forged pass bearing a brave man's name. Somewhere behind the lit windows of the factor's house, Brack was paying, hour by hour, for a day none of us had asked him to buy.

The tide turned below the quay, right on schedule, indifferent to every plan laid upon it. Four hundred breaths, I thought, from here to the hulk grate at slack water. I had done worse in colder seas for less reason.

I closed the shutter and went to tell Edmund we sailed at midnight.
