## 31. Rescue on the Turning Tide

*Edmund*

The hulk showed itself in pieces as they pulled toward it: first the masthead lantern, a smudge against the stars; then the black mass of the hull, barnacled to the waterline and weeping green slime; then, at last, the shape of it entire — the *Salvington*, once an East Indiaman, now a floating jail with her gunports sealed over and her decks crawling with men paid to forget what she held.

Slack tide. The water between the boat and the hull lay flat as poured lead. Brack's other boat — his last, loaned with the grimness of a man giving blood — carried them in silence, Osei at the oars because he rowed without sound, and Edmund in the bow with his hands open on his knees because he did not trust himself to grip anything yet.

Tamsin sat across from him. In the dark he could not see her face, only the pale suggestion of it, and the line of her shoulders, very straight. She had sewn the letters into her diving suit that afternoon with sailmaker's stitches, and when she moved now there was a faint rustle of oilcloth against skin, like a whisper following her.

"Grate's on the landward side," Edmund murmured, for the hundredth time, more to hear it than to tell it. "Third port from the stern. Vane said the bars were rotten two years ago."

"Two years of rot," Tamsin said. "Either it falls off in our hands or it's been replaced twice."

"Then we improvise."

"You always say that word like it's a plan."

"It is a plan," Edmund said. "It is the only honest plan there is."

Osei shipped the oars. The boat drifted the last few yards and kissed the hull with a sound no louder than a mouth opening. Above them the sentry walk ran along the waist of the ship, lanterns hung at intervals too far apart, the gaps between them deep enough to drown a man. They had counted the watch from shore: eight on deck, four below, one officer, one rotation at midnight.

The lantern nearest the stern had just begun its slow swing toward the bow. Its light would leave them for perhaps forty breaths.

Edmund went up the chains first.

He had told himself, all these weeks, that he was a courier. That he carried paper, and paper was weightless, and weightless things could slip through any gap. He climbed the rusted links with that lie in his mouth and felt it dissolve somewhere around the channel, because nothing weightless had ever felt this heavy going up.

* * *

*Tamsin*

She followed him up the chains the way she entered water: hands first, testing, then the whole body committed without argument. The bars were cold and slick and cut her palms in small familiar ways — she had spent her life holding things that wanted to slip away, and she did not slip.

At the top, the chain vanished into a scupper, and beyond it lay the deck, silvered and empty under the departing lantern. Edmund flattened himself against the bulwark. She flattened herself beside him, close enough to feel his breathing go wrong and then deliberately right again.

Forty breaths. She counted them the way her mother had taught her to count underwater, not as time but as currency, each one spent and gone.

The grate was where Vane's letter promised: third port from the stern, behind a coiled rope and a stack of foul-smelling dunnage. The bars were indeed rotten. Edmund worked the first one loose with his knife's handle and it came away in his hand trailing flakes like dead skin, and the sound it made was a dry whisper, and then the second bar stuck, and stuck, and stuck —

Thirty seconds of nothing but his knuckles whitening and the rust grinding its teeth.

Then it gave. The hole was barely wide enough. A boy could have slipped through; a man who had been fed half-rations for two years might manage it; Edmund, broad through the shoulders, would have to be dragged.

"I go down," Edmund breathed. "You keep watch. If the watch turns early —"

"You whistle."

"If I can't whistle —"

"Then I come in after you," Tamsin said, "and you can waste breath arguing about it later."

He looked at her. Even in the dark she felt it, the shape of everything neither of them had said since the shell barge, since her hand on his face, since the plan grew one impossible limb after another until it stood upright and demanded their lives. Then he put his legs through the hole and lowered himself into the dark of the hold, and she crouched alone above the deck with the sea flat as glass below and the next lantern swinging toward them out of the dark like a slow yellow eye.

* * *

*Edmund*

Below was a stench he had prepared for and failed to prepare for: bilgewater and rot and the particular sourness of many unwashed bodies kept in one place too long, the smell of a place where the company stored the debts it did not wish to see. His eyes would not adjust. There was no light at all except a thread from the grate above.

"Captain Vane," he whispered into the dark. "Aldous. It's Edmund."

Silence. Then, from somewhere to his left, a voice so changed he nearly wept at the change of it — dry as rope ends, cracked, but *lucid*, unmistakably lucid, the mind still sitting upright inside the ruined body.

"Took you long enough, Roe. I'd begun composing your epitaph. It wasn't flattering."

"Say something else. Anything. I need to know it's you."

"What do I know that no one else living knows?" A wheeze that might once have been a laugh. "That you cheated at cards aboard the *Cormorant* in '41 and blamed a Maltese quartermaster who thrashed you within an inch of your worthless life. That your real name is Edmund Roe and your false ones are legion. And that I hid a needle from a compass in a coil of tarred twine in the foretop of the *Meridian* eleven years ago, and never told a soul why."

"That's you." Edmund's throat closed. He got his hands on the voice instead — on the arms attached to it, thin as spars, and the wrists bound in irons that clinked with a sound like small coins.

"Keys are with the master-at-arms, aft," Vane said. "But the hasp on these is sprung. It takes a strong pull and a stronger fool."

"Which are you volunteering me for?"

"Both, lad. Quickly now. The watch turns at midnight, and the tide turns twenty minutes past, and if we're still aboard when it does, neither of us will ever leave."

Edmund set his feet against the bulkhead, gripped the sprung hasp, and pulled. Something tore in his shoulder. The hasp gave all at once and dumped him backward into the dark, and Vane's irons fell open, and then Edmund was hauling a man's whole weight onto his back — a man who weighed less than the evidence in Tamsin's suit, which was its own indictment written in bone.

"Hold to me," Edmund said. "Whatever you hear, whatever happens, hold to me and don't let go."

"Why," said Vane, in the tone of a man reading a chart, "what's going to happen?"

* * *

*Tamsin*

The lantern reached the break of the poop and stopped. The sentry stopped with it.

She heard him before she saw him: boots on planking, unhurried, the boredom of a man four hours from relief. He came along the starboard rail with a musket slung loose and his collar up against the river damp, and he stopped ten feet from the dunnage, and he looked out at the water, and Tamsin pressed herself into the shadow of the cable tier and became a thing without edges.

Beneath her feet, faint as a pulse through timber, she felt movement. Edmund coming back up with his burden. Twenty feet of ladder at the pace of a starving man. Two minutes, maybe three.

The sentry yawned. Turned. Began walking the rail toward the stern — toward the third port, toward the grate, toward the pile of rope that half-concealed an open hole in his prison.

There was no decision in what happened next. That was the part Edmund would not forgive himself for, and the part Tamsin understood better than he did, because she knew what the body did when the arithmetic of survival ran faster than the soul. The sentry's boot found the dunnage. The dunnage shifted. The sentry looked down.

And Edmund — already at the grate, already out to his waist, Vane's weight on his back, one hand full of knife because he could not put it down — saw himself seen.

What followed took less time than telling it. The sentry opened his mouth. Edmund's hand came up. Whether he meant it or not, whether the blade went true by aim or by accident, no one would ever establish, least of all Edmund, who would spend years trying. The man folded over the rail without a cry, and the musket clattered, and the sound of the clatter was louder than anything else that night, loud enough to wake the dead and nearly loud enough to wake the watch.

Tamsin moved. Not toward the sentry — toward the noise. She rolled the musket into the cable tier, dragged the dunnage back over the grate, and had Edmund's collar in her fist before his head cleared the hole, hauling while he pushed, until Vane slid out onto the deck like something landed from the sea, gasping, blinking at the sky.

The three of them froze. The ship held its breath. Somewhere aft a man coughed in his sleep and turned over.

The lantern at the bow swung away on its arc, indifferent.

"He's dead," Edmund said. His voice came out level, which frightened her more than screaming would have. "I killed him. He was going to call out and I — my hand was already —"

"Yes," Tamsin said.

"I didn't decide."

"No," she said. "You didn't. Come away now. Decide later, if you must. Grief keeps. Hanging doesn't."

* * *

*Edmund*

Later — much later, in the boat, with the hulk shrinking behind them into the general darkness — he would take the memory out and turn it over the way he turned over coins to check for clipping. The sentry's face, young, chapped, unshaven, wearing the same expression Edmund had worn on a dozen cold watches in another life. The half-second in which everything hung suspended, in which a shout and a warning and a surrender were all still possible. And then his own hand moving with the terrible competence of a body that had learned violence young and never quite unlearned it, deciding for him, in the space between heartbeats, that Aldous Vane would live.

He had come to this coast carrying letters. Paper. Weightless things. He had told himself the cause was clean, that words could not bleed.

Now there was a man in the estuary mud with Edmund's knife in him, and the tide, turning right on schedule, would carry the story out to sea and never bring it back.

Osei rowed. Tamsin held the tiller with one hand and, with the other, held Vane upright against the thwart, and Vane — half-starved, wrapped in Brack's spare coat, his eyes enormous in a skull the moon made of it — looked from Edmund's face to the dark water and understood, because he had always understood everything, exactly what lay behind them and what lay in Edmund.

"Lad," he said quietly, "if you had hesitated, I'd be dead, and so would you, and so would she, and every name in those letters would stay buried in company ledgers till the paper rotted. There is no version of tonight with clean hands in it. There is only the version where we live."

"I know," Edmund said.

"But it doesn't help."

"No," Edmund said. "It doesn't help."

The current took the boat. The *Salvington*'s lanterns receded, and the alarm he had been bracing for never came — either the watch had not yet missed its sentry, or the company's discipline had grown so rotten that a dead man on a deck could wait for morning like any other piece of inventory. Either way, the silence felt less like escape than postponement.

Tamsin's shoulder was against his. She did not say it would be all right. She had never once lied to him, and he loved her for it in a way he could not yet afford to examine, here on stolen water with blood drying on a blade he had not yet cleaned.

* * *

*Tamsin*

They came into the shallows below the night market as the sky went the color of dirty tin. Brack stood hip-deep to meet them, took Vane's weight across his back without a word, and carried him up the shingle like a sack of shell, and Tamsin walked beside them doing sums because sums were safer than feeling.

One sentry dead. One captain recovered. Four days until the fleet sailed — no, closer to three now, dawn eating the margin. Three checkpoints behind them and one ahead, and Calloway waiting at it with a copied page and a trap built for them, and the only man alive who could testify to everything the letters proved now breathing sea air for the first time in two years and too weak to stand unaided.

In the loft, they laid Vane on Brack's pallet and fed him broth in spoonfuls, and he drank like a man remembering how, and his eyes — Vane's eyes, quick and ironic and utterly alive — traveled the room: the pearls gone from their tins, the crates, the sewing scattered, Tamsin's diving suit hanging with its strange stiff seams, Edmund sitting apart with his knife across his knees, cleaning it over and over though it was long since clean.

"Well," Vane said at last, his voice finding a little of its old keel. "You've got the letters. You've got me. And you've got three days."

"Three days," Tamsin agreed.

"Then listen to me, both of you, because I've had two years in a hold to think about nothing else." He pushed himself up on one elbow, and even that small motion cost him visibly, and he refused to show it. "The letters prove nothing on their own. Paper can be denied, disowned, burned. But a drowned man standing up in front of the fleet's officers — that, they cannot burn fast enough. We don't just need to get past the gate. We need to arrive *loudly*. At the worst moment. Before every witness Calloway can't afford to let exist."

Edmund looked up from the knife. "You want us to walk into his trap."

"I want us," Vane said, "to walk into it smiling."

Outside, the tide turned with a long sigh against the pilings, and the fleet's anchor lights rode the harbor like a string of patient fires, three days from sailing, and somewhere among them a dead sentry had already been missed.
