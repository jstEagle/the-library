## 32. Vane Testifies to the Water

The loft above the shell barge smelled of brine and lamp oil and other people's lives, and to Aldous Vane it smelled like the first honest air he had drawn in two years.

He had been awake since before dawn — the hulk taught you that; sleep on the *Retribution* was rationed like water, stolen in quarters and paid for with pain — and now he lay on a straw pallet watching the light come through the gaps in the floorboards, mapping the room as he had once mapped tides. Low ceiling. One stair. A hatch to the roof that Brack's boy used for smoking, though the boy had vanished two days past and no one would say where he'd gone. A window facing the estuary, its shutter cracked just wide enough to see the masts of the quarterly fleet standing in the roads like a forest of gallows waiting for the wood.

His hands would not stop shaking. That was the body's business, and he let it conduct itself without his interference. Two years of dark and short rations and the slow arithmetic of despair did not leave a man at the door; they followed him in and sat down and waited to be acknowledged. So he acknowledged them. He lay still and let his hands tremble against the blanket and told himself what he had told himself every morning on the hulk, counting the strokes of the ship's bell through iron grating: *You are not finished. A dead man who is not yet dead has work to do.*

Footsteps on the stair. Two sets. He knew them both by now — the girl's quick and light, placed like a diver placing her feet on rock; the other man's measured, careful of creaks. Edmund Roe had always walked like a man entering a church he wasn't sure God was home to.

They came up into the grey light, and Vane propped himself on one elbow and looked at them and felt something in his chest turn over, slow and heavy, like a capstan taking up slack.

Edmund had aged ten years in two. The letters Vane had smuggled out — sewn into oilskin packets, passed hand to hand through three relays of fishermen and one corrupt tide-waiter whose name Vane would carry to whatever grave he finally got — those letters had described Roe as he remembered him: young, scrupulous, a clerk's soul in a sailor's body, too honest for the company and too stubborn to drown in it. The man standing at the top of the stairs was thinner, watchful, with new lines cut around his mouth. But the eyes were the same. Still measuring himself against some standard no one else could see.

And the girl. Tamsin Thornbury. He had known her mother when the mother was a girl herself, diving the inner beds with lungs like bellows and a laugh that carried across the water. He had not known — God forgive him, he had *not known*, or he had known and buried it deep enough that the knowing never surfaced — that the company's ledger held her family's name the way a hook holds a fish. He had learned it in the dark of the hulk, from a guard's careless gossip, and he had spent a night with his forehead against the cold iron of the bulkhead understanding exactly what his silence had purchased.

She was looking at him the way people look at things they cannot decide are real. He supposed he looked the part of a ghost. Two years of hulk had given him a face for it.

"Sit," he said. His voice came out rough as pumice. "Both of you. And then I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to answer it honestly, because everything after depends on it."

They sat. Cross-legged on the boards, the way sailors sit, the way divers sit. The girl put a clay cup of something hot into his hands — chicory, from the smell, bitter and weak and the finest thing he had tasted since his arrest — and wrapped his fingers around the warmth with hers, briefly, matter-of-factly, the way you steady an old man's tremor without commenting on it. He could have wept at that. He didn't. There would be time for weeping when the work was done, if there ever was such a time, which he doubted.

"Here is my question," Vane said. "What do the letters weigh?"

Edmund frowned. Tamsin tilted her head, quick as a bird.

"You've read them," Vane went on. "All of them. Names, dates, certificates of death signed by a surgeon who never saw the corpses — because there were no corpses, only debts that needed drowning. Eleven men and four women over six years, declared lost at sea so the trading house could void their contracts and swallow their estates. You have it all on paper. So tell me: what does that paper weigh? In pounds, in ounces. What can it lift?"

Silence. Beyond the shutter crack, the harbor woke: gulls, block-creak, a bosun's whistle somewhere out in the roads, thin as a knife drawn across cloth.

"A few pounds," Edmund said at last, slowly, testing the ground. "Paper weighs almost nothing."

"Correct." Vane drank the chicory. His hands shook; he let them. "Now here is what I learned on the *Retribution*, listening to guards who talked too much because I looked half-dead and half-dead men aren't worth guarding their tongues around. Mr. Calloway knows about the letters. Has known for months. How do I know? Because the night they took me off the *Meridian* — before the *Meridian* conveniently burned at her moorings with a crew aboard who are also in your papers, gentlemen and lady, page nine, the *Meridian*'s articles — the factor's own man stood on the quay and told me so. He said: 'Captain Vane, paper is the least dangerous thing in the world. Paper can be stolen. Paper can be discredited. Paper can be proven forgery by two sworn clerks and a magistrate at breakfast.' And then he smiled, and I was taken below, and the ship burned."

Tamsin had gone very still. It was a diver's stillness, Vane thought — the whole body gathered and held, spending nothing.

"So," he said. "The letters alone will hang no one. Worse — the letters alone may hang *you*. Because Calloway doesn't need to destroy the evidence. He needs only to destroy its credibility, and he owns every instrument of credibility in this port: magistrates, surgeons, shipping masters, the press that prints the shipping lists. A packet of documents alleging fraud, produced by a fugitive clerk and a bonded diver with no standing and no counsel? That is not a case. That is a curiosity. That is a story the colony tells for a season and then forgets, while the fleet sails on schedule and the ledger grows fat again under a new factor with better manners."

"The superintendent," Edmund said. "Captain-superintendent Harrow. He's company, but he's naval first — twenty years in the service before they seconded him. If—"

"If a living witness stood in front of him," Vane said, "and gave the testimony in his own voice, under his own name, with his scars visible and his service record behind him — then the paper stops being paper and becomes corroboration. Then Harrow cannot dismiss it without dismissing me, and dismissing me means explaining to the Admiralty why a post captain of eleven years' standing spent two years on a prison hulk without trial." He set down the cup. His hands were calmer now. Speaking did that; purpose was a medicine no apothecary stocked. "Paper accuses. A witness convicts. I am the witness. Which means I must be heard — not hidden in a crate, not whispered about in lofts — *heard*, publicly, before that fleet sails."

"And when it sails?" Tamsin asked. Her voice was level but he heard the current beneath it.

"When it sails, this port empties of everyone whose attention matters. Officers dispersed. Passengers scattered to four other colonies where the company's friends hold the benches. Whatever Harrow decides, he decides alone, in a quiet room, with Calloway's successors pouring his wine — and whatever he decides, it stays decided, because the witnesses are gone and the records sail east to be 'archived.'" Vane smiled, and felt his face do something unfamiliar with the muscles. "I have had two years to study how this house kills things. It does not use knives. It uses *schedules*."

* * *

They argued with him for an hour, and he loved them for it.

Edmund argued first from caution — Vane was too weak, the crossing to the gate under guard's eyes was madness, the letters could reach authorities in the eastern ports even after sailing. Vane answered each point the way he had once answered a young officer's objections in a gale: patiently, completely, and with the underlying suggestion that the objection was reasonable and the weather unreasonable regardless.

"The eastern ports," Vane said, "are where Calloway's brother-in-law runs the customs house at Greywater. I checked. From the hulk. Through a guard who owed me for his sister's passage — yes, Edmund, even on the hulk there is commerce; men are men everywhere. Every road east runs through a friend of the firm. This port, today, with the fleet assembled and the superintendent obliged to be seen exercising authority over *somebody* — this is the one hour in six years when the company's size works against it. Harrow cannot afford a public scandal and therefore cannot afford to ignore a public accusation made where others can hear it. Force his hand in the open or lose forever."

Then Tamsin argued, and she argued differently, and Vane found himself leaning forward to listen the way he had once leaned forward to read a chart of an unmarked coast.

"You're asking us to walk you into the gate," she said. "You, the man every description in the factor's office is drawn from. You haven't seen yourself. I have. Two days of food hasn't put back what the hulk took. Any guard who's served here more than a season looks at your face and doesn't need papers." She paused, and her jaw set, and Vane saw her mother in it so plainly that his chest hurt. "So either we make the letters enough, or we find a way to make *you* unrecognizable, or we accept that this plan ends with all three of us in irons and the evidence confiscated. Which is it?"

"It," said Vane, "is the middle one. And for the middle one, I need you, Miss Thornbury, and everything you know about this port's eyes."

* * *

He slept after that — truly slept, dreamless, the first such sleep in two years — and woke in full daylight to the sound of the loft being transformed around him.

Brack was there. Vane had taken the smuggler's measure the first night: a man built like cargo itself, with a face that gave away nothing and a grief he carried like ballast, low and permanent. His brother's name was in the letters — page four, *declared drowned 1843, sloop* Larkspur, *debts assigned to the house*. Vane had written that entry knowing it was a lie, knowing somewhere a woman named Brack was widowed by ink, and he had never expected the chance to say so. He had said it anyway, the second night, in the dark, in the plain words the thing deserved. Brack had listened, and said nothing, and in the morning had moved Vane's pallet closer to the stove without a word of explanation. Vane understood. Some pardons are issued in firewood.

Now Brack was building something out of shell crates and old canvas, and Tamsin stood over a spread of her diving gear with the concentration of a surgeon, and Edmund was at the window slit with a borrowed glass, counting boats in the roads.

"Four days," Edmund reported, without turning. "Fleet sails in four days. They've run signal flags up the harbor mast — departure confirmed, all merchants cleared by the day prior."

"Three," Vane corrected, from the pallet. Everyone looked at him. "Count from tomorrow's dawn, not from tonight. Whatever we do, we do it in daylight among crowds, or not at all. Night work is for thieves, and we are not thieves. We are testimony." He pushed himself upright, and the effort cost him more than he let show, and he paid it anyway. "Miss Thornbury. Come here. Let me see those letters again — the certificates, pages two through seven. I signed them under instruction. I remember the order of names, and if the order is right, then I can prove the sequence proves intent — deaths timed to debt cycles, never to seasons. No court reads a list. But a court reads a *pattern*, and I am going to give them the pattern aloud."

She brought the oilskin packet over and knelt beside the pallet to unwrap it, and as her fingers worked the knots he saw it — hanging at her throat on a cord, tucked inside her collar, a small brass compass with its face scarred and its needle long gone.

He had given that compass to her mother nineteen years ago. Standing on this same quay, more or less, with the girl herself on her mother's hip and the harbor sun on the water. *For the little one,* he had said. *So she never loses her way on the bottom.* He had not known then what the company would take from her family, or that his own signature would one day help bury her father. The compass stared up at him from her collarbone with its empty face, needle-less, direction-less, and Aldous Vane — who had spent two years learning precisely what a man becomes when every instrument of bearing is stripped from him — found he could not speak for a moment.

"Captain?" Tamsin said.

"The pattern," he managed. "Pages two through seven. Begin reading the names, in order, and stop me when I interrupt."

She began to read. Sunlight crawled across the floorboards. Out in the roads, the fleet lay at anchor in all its terrible patience, and Vane listened to the litany of the drowned — the living drowned, the indebted drowned, the drowned-by-arithmetic — and turned each name over in his memory like a coin tested between finger and thumb, checking the weight, checking the ring, building the case he would speak aloud in three days' time with his ruined voice and his ruined body before the one audience that mattered.

At the fourth name he raised his hand and she stopped.

"There," he said. "That is where the pattern turns. That is where I begin."

Beyond the cracked shutter, faint over the water, came the sound of the harbor gun marking noon — and beneath it, another sound, subtler, which only men who had lived under discipline knew how to hear: boats pulling in formation toward the toll bridge, oars feathering together, a patrol moving with purpose rather than habit.

Edmund was already at the glass. What he said next, none of them were ready for.
