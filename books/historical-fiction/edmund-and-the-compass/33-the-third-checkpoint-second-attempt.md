## 33. The Third Checkpoint, Second Attempt

*Tamsin*

The fleet gate at dawn looked like a mouth.

That was how she thought of it, standing in the grey light with the tide still cool on her skin: a mouth of timber and iron, swallowing cargo and spitting ships, guarded by men who had been fed too well to be hungry for anything but trouble. Six weeks ago she would have walked through it the way she always had — head down, ledger number murmured like a prayer, one more body belonging to the company passing one more of the company's doors.

Today she walked in with a drowned man sewn into her diving suit and a living man nailed shut among the shell crates, and she lifted her chin as she came.

The letters rode against her ribs. She had stitched them there herself the night before, by lamplight, with Edmund's hands steadying the oilcloth while hers did the needlework her mother had taught her before she could write. Thirty-two pages against her skin, wrapped in oiled silk inside oiled canvas, flat as a second heartbeat. When she breathed deep, she felt them. When she breathed shallow — the diver's habit, the economy of a body that had learned never to waste air — she felt them more.

"They'll search you," Edmund had said last night, not as objection but as arithmetic.

"They'll search my crate," Tamsin had answered. "They'll search your papers. Nobody searches a diver's suit. It smells like the sea and it fits like a skin, and Calloway's men have never once put their hands on something that smelled of work." She had tied off the last stitch and bitten the thread. "The company only inspects what the company doesn't already own."

Now, at the gate, she counted.

She counted because counting was what she did when fear came — she measured the world in things that could be carried, and right now the things that could be carried were these: four shell crates on Brack's borrowed dray, three of them honest and one of them breathing through a augured seam; two passage papers bought with a season's pearls; one forged clerk's pass bearing the name Edmund Roe, which was now a name somewhere in a sergeant's report, and therefore a lit fuse; one suit of diving leather with thirty-two pages against a woman's ribs; and — she looked sideways at him — one man who had come across the sea carrying nothing but paper, and was about to risk all of it for a person he'd never met until three days ago.

And behind them, out past the mole where the water went from green to ink, the hulk *Providence* rode at anchor with an empty cell and a sentry count that would not stay short forever. By noon, someone would count the hulk's prisoners and find one missing. The question — the whole knife-edge of the morning — was whether that someone counted before or after the *Meridian* warped off her berth.

Calloway believed she sailed at evening tide. That was the lie Brack had bought them with his blood: eight hours of guards watching the wrong horizon.

"Gate," Edmund said quietly, and they moved.

* * *

*Edmund*

There is a particular quality to walking toward a thing that has been built to stop you.

Edmund had felt it once before, years ago, boarding a prize vessel in the Java Sea with Vane beside him — the moment when the plan left your hands and became the sea's, when every preparation resolved into the single narrow truth of the next ten feet. He felt it now. The gate's timbers rose overhead, patched with company paint over older company paint, and beneath the arch stood the inspection tables, and behind the tables stood men with carbines slung casual as fishing rods, and behind *them*, on the raised platform where the manifests were signed, stood Mr. Calloway.

He had not expected Calloway himself. His stomach registered the fact before his mind did — a diver's drop, the ground going soft.

"He knows," he said, low, lips barely moving. "Tamsin. He's not here for routine."

"He's here for us." Her voice stayed level, pleasant, pitched for the queue around them. "Which means he's here for the wrong barge. Look at his eyes."

Edmund looked. Calloway stood with the manifest board under his arm and his gaze fixed — fixed, hungry, certain — on the line of barges coming up the channel road from the western beds. The western beds. Brack's false departure time had been seeded precisely: a shell shipment, a diver's barge, due at the gate within the hour. Calloway had set his trap along that line like a man laying a snare across a rabbit run, and here they came on the eastern road instead, early, quiet, just another porter and just another contracted diver delivering the season's quota to the *Meridian*'s hold.

It would work. Or Calloway would turn his head, and then nothing else would matter.

"Names," said the sergeant at the table. A new sergeant — the one from the toll bridge had been rotated out, thank God, though somewhere in a company file his own real name sat in fresh ink like a splinter working toward the heart. "Cargo."

"Shell quota, Thornbury contract," Tamsin said, and laid down her docket with the easy boredom of a woman who had done this forty times. "Diver, porter. Papers."

The sergeant took her documents. Edmund watched his hands — the way they moved, unhurried, professional — and made himself breathe like a man with nothing in his pockets but lint. The forged pass lay on the table between them, and the sergeant's eye went down it, and stopped.

"Roe," the sergeant said. "You were at the toll bridge Tuesday."

"I was at the toll bridge Tuesday," Edmund agreed. His pulse was a drum, his face was a wall. "Clerk's pass, through freight. Same as I'll be today, if the company's happy."

The sergeant looked at him for one second too long. Edmund held it. This was the part no one taught you — that the gauntlet was not the carbines, not the tables, not even Calloway on his platform; the gauntlet was a bored man's eyes and the three seconds you had to survive inside them.

Then the sergeant stamped both papers without looking up again. "Third crate's open. Rest go through. Diver —" he flicked a finger at Tamsin. "Quota check at the gangway. Move."

They moved. Behind them, the first of the western-road barges rounded the corner, and Edmund heard — did not turn to see, made himself not turn — Calloway's voice crack across the yard like a whip, ordering that barge stopped, that barge *held*, men to the crates, *now*. The trap closing on empty air.

Three crates rolled through the arch. The fourth — the fourth had a man inside it with a knife and a pistol and half a year of prison in his arms — followed at the pace of a patient dray-horse, and Edmund walked beside it with his heart in his throat and his face like weather.

* * *

*Tamsin*

The gangway of the *Meridian* was the widest place in the world and the narrowest.

Widest, because beyond it lay the hold, the shells, the hiding place prepared two nights ago — a false floor above the pearl crates, room enough for a thin man to lie flat in darkness for however long the crossing demanded. Narrowest, because at its foot stood the quota officer with his tally book, and beside him stood someone Tamsin had not planned for.

Her mother.

Margaret Thornbury wore her market shawl and carried a basket of limes nobody wanted bought, and her face was the face Tamsin had known across twenty years of silences — the set jaw, the eyes that gave away everything the mouth refused to. She should not be here. She had promised to be anywhere but here.

"Mum." Tamsin kept her voice at dockside pitch, flat, unremarkable. "What are you doing?"

"Buying limes." Her mother looked at the crates, at Edmund, at the gangway, and her hands tightened on the basket handle until the wicker creaked. "Your Aunt Enid wanted limes."

There were no limes in the basket. Tamsin saw that in one glance and understood everything in the same beat: her mother had come to watch, because she could not come to help, because helping was the thing she had spent a lifetime being afraid of and she had crossed that line already, days ago, in a kitchen with a lamp turned low — and so she had come to stand at the edge of it, close as a body was allowed, holding an empty basket full of nothing.

"You shouldn't linger," Tamsin said gently.

"No." Her mother didn't move. Then, quieter, in the voice she used for the old songs, the ones from before the company, before the indenture, before silence: "Your father checked quotas at this gate. Did you know that? Before. He used to say the tally book never lied because it couldn't afford to." She looked at her daughter, and her eyes were wet and furious and proud, all three at war. "He was wrong. Books lie all the time. People just have to be brave enough to read them properly."

"Mum—"

"Go." Margaret Thornbury stepped back from the gangway, lifted her empty basket like a benediction, and turned to face the yard. "I'll shout if anyone comes. Loud as your father ever did."

Tamsin wanted, unbearably, to touch her. Instead she touched the crate — one knuckle, twice, the signal — and the dray rolled onto the gangway's planking, and the quota officer opened his tally book, and the world narrowed to numbers.

"Thornbury quota. Shell weight?"

"Four hundred and twelve pounds dressed," Tamsin recited. "Crated at dawn, weighed at the bed landing, seals intact." She offered the seals for inspection, lead discs stamped with her own mark, and watched the officer weigh one in his palm the way they always did, the way she had watched men weigh things her whole life — casually, carelessly, because shell was profit and profit was trusted and nobody in the history of the pearling coast had ever smuggled anything inside the company's own money.

"Open the third one," the officer said.

Not the fourth. The third. Tamsin's knees went soft and her voice did not. "Third's wet-bed stock, it'll stink the deck—"

"Open it or I open it."

So they opened the third crate, and it was exactly what it appeared to be: shell, rank with brine, packed by her own hands two nights ago. The officer prodded it with his stick, wrinkled his nose, waved it through. The fourth crate passed on the strength of the third's honesty, and Tamsin understood something then about systems and the people inside them: they searched where suspicion pointed, never where trust lay, and trust was the heaviest thing she had ever carried through a gate.

The hold swallowed the crates. The false floor went down over them. And Tamsin climbed topside into the morning with thirty-two letters against her ribs and the tide turning below, and looked east along the quay to where the customs house bell had begun — far too early, entirely wrong — to ring.

Ring. Ring. Ring.

Someone had found the hulk short one prisoner.

* * *

*Edmund*

The bell changed everything and nothing.

Edmund stood at the *Meridian*'s rail and did the arithmetic with the coldness Vane had taught him in better years: the bell meant discovery, discovery meant alarm, alarm meant that within the hour — less — every checkpoint, every quay, every gate in the port would be hunting a rescued man who was currently lying in a box under his feet. Their window was no longer hours. It was the time it took word to travel from the hulk's boat to the factor's office to the gate, and that time was measured in minutes and being spent even now.

But the bell also meant the trap had sprung on the wrong cargo, in front of witnesses, and Calloway's careful web had caught nothing but Brack's empty barge and Brack himself, and a man who has grabbed at smoke in public is a man who has already begun to look like a fool.

Fools double down. That was the hope. Fools summon everyone to the gate to save face, and crowds gather, and officers assemble, and the captain-superintendent himself comes down from the hill to see why his sailing schedule has become a shambles —

"There." Tamsin's hand closed on his sleeve. Down the quay, a carriage with the superintendent's pennant was swinging through the gate traffic at speed, and behind it runners, and behind them the ordinary tide of dockworkers flowing toward whatever spectacle the morning offered, because spectacles were free and wages were not.

"It's now or it's never," Edmund said. "Vane gets out of that hold into daylight with witnesses, or he goes back to the hulk in a coffin. There's no third option after a bell."

Tamsin looked at him — one long look, the diver's look, measuring depth before committing the body — and nodded once.

"Then we go get our witness," she said, "and we read the books properly."

They went down to the hold together, Edmund prying at the false floor's lip while Tamsin kept her back to the hatch and her face turned outward, a diver guarding water. The lid came up. Grey light fell in.

Aldous Vane climbed out of the crate that had carried him, and stood swaying on the deck of the ship he had been marked for death aboard, thinner than the wind, beard gone to salt and iron, and smiled at Edmund with the whole of his ruined, magnificent stubbornness.

"Late," Vane rasped. "You're both late. Tide turned six minutes ago."

"Then we've six minutes' practice at being fast," Edmund said, and gave him his shoulder, and the three of them went up the gangway together into the noise and the crowd and the gathering storm of the gate — where, across the yard, Mr. Calloway had turned from the seized barge and was looking directly at them, manifest board dropping from under his arm, face going through surprise and calculation and arrival at certainty in the space of a breath.

"CALLOWAY!" Tamsin's voice cracked across the yard, loud as her father's, louder. She had the letters out of her suit already, oilcloth flashing in her fist, held high where every runner and officer and dockhand streaming toward them could see it. "MR. CALLOWAY! You dropped your ledger!"

The crowd turned. The carriage stopped. And the captain-superintendent of the pearling fleet stepped down into the press of his own port to find out what, in God's name, his factor had been keeping from him.

Calloway's mouth opened. Whatever came out of it next, Edmund knew, would decide everything — and behind the factor's shoulder, shoved forward by his men with her wrists already bound, beaten about the mouth and grinning through it, came Brack.

"Tell them," Brack called out cheerfully, blood on his teeth, "the tide's turned!"
