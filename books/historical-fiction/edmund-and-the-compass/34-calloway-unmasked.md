## 34. Calloway Unmasked

The fleet gate at dawn looked like something built to survive a siege rather than to load shell and spice. Tamsin had walked through it a hundred times in her life — the same timber doors, the same iron-studded posts, the same company flag hanging limp in the windless grey — but she had never seen it armed. Lanterns still burned along the palisade. Guards stood in pairs every ten paces, and behind them, stacked like cargo itself, waited the officers of the quarterly fleet in their best coats, summoned before sunrise for what Calloway's writs had called *an inspection of unusual urgency*.

She felt the letters against her ribs, sewn into the lining of her diving suit where oilcloth met skin. Forty pages of other people's deaths, warm from her body's heat.

Beside her, Edmund rolled his shoulders once, the only tell he had never trained out of himself. Behind them, four men carried a crate of pearl shell on shoulder poles, and inside that crate, packed among the shell with straw and salt, Aldous Vane lay breathing shallow and patient, a drowned man waiting for his moment to surface.

"He's here," Edmund said quietly. "Calloway. Left of the gate, by the weighing shed."

Tamsin found him without difficulty. Mr. Calloway dressed like a man who expected to be the most substantial object in any room, and this morning he had outdone himself: dove-grey coat, silver chain across the waistcoat, a ledger bound in green leather held in the crook of his arm as though it were scripture. He was speaking to the captain-superintendent of the fleet — Sir Emeric Hallowes, white-haired, sun-reddened, a man whose signature moved more tonnage than any king's decree. She watched Calloway lean close, watched Hallowes nod, watched the machinery of the port turn on one man's word.

"That's who decides?" she asked.

"That's who signs," Edmund said. "Deciding was never part of his office."

The false departure time Brack had bled for was doing its work. Half the gate's guards had been rotated toward the east road on Calloway's own order, hunting a barge that would not come, leaving the actual gate thinner than it had been in weeks. It was not enough to slip through. They had never meant to slip through. The whole desperate architecture of the last three days came down to this: they did not need an open gate. They needed witnesses.

And here were the finest witnesses in the colony, assembled at Company expense.

Tamsin drew one breath, tasting salt and lamp-oil, and started walking.

* * *

Edmund counted his heartbeats the way Vane had taught him years ago in the Indian Ocean — not to calm himself, but to mark time honestly. Twenty paces from the gate, the head sergeant stepped out, hand raised.

"Name and business."

"Tamsin Thornbury," she said, and her voice carried better than Edmund expected, clear as a struck line. "Diver, third bed, indentured to the Honourable Company. This is my hired porter. We're contracted for the *Meridian*'s shell shipment. Crate's already weighed and stamped — you can check the tally chit."

The sergeant took the chit, turned it over. Edmund watched his eyes move to the crate, to its weight, to the sweat darkening the carriers' shirts.

"Crate opens," the sergeant said. "New orders. Everything opens."

"No," Tamsin said.

It was the first time Edmund had heard her refuse anything outright, and the sound of it changed the air. Heads turned along the palisade. The sergeant's hand drifted toward his baton.

"Everything opens," he repeated. "Factor's instruction."

"Then let the factor instruct me himself." Tamsin raised her voice until it rang off the timbers. "Mr. Calloway! Do you open your own profit? Because this crate is your profit, sir, and your own rules say shell ships uninspected — or has that changed since you signed my contract?"

Silence, the particular silence of men calculating. Then Calloway came forward through the lantern light, unhurried, smiling, terrible in his composure. Up close Edmund could see the fatigue under his eyes, the fine tremor in the hand that held the green ledger — the tremor of a man who had not slept since a boy in a loft copied him half a page.

"Mistress Thornbury." Warm as a father. "Up before the fleet. That's diligence."

"I'm a diligent woman."

"You are." Calloway's gaze slid past her, rested on Edmund, and stayed there with the pleasure of a debt collector finding a debtor. "And this is your porter. Master Roe, isn't it? Or was it another name you gave the sergeant at the toll bridge?"

Edmund felt the trap's teeth even as he smiled. "I give my name wherever honest work asks for it."

"Do you." Calloway turned to the captain-superintendent and lifted the ledger slightly, a man presenting evidence at last, in the proper venue, before the proper audience. He had arranged all of this, Edmund realized — the officers, the urgency, the thin patrol sent chasing shadows so the real audience would stand here unguarded by distraction. Not a checkpoint. A courtroom, with Calloway for prosecutor. "Sir Emeric. Before these ships sail, there is a matter touching the Company's honour. This man arrived in the port bearing forged correspondence — letters purporting to be the hand of Captain Aldous Vane, who drowned with the *Providence* two years past. Forged papers of a dead officer, circulated among Company servants. I have a sworn copy of one page here—"

"You have a stolen page," Tamsin said, "copied by a boy you paid."

"—and I have," Calloway went on, without pausing, without blinking, "a witness prepared to identify both the papers and the man who carries them."

He snapped his fingers. From the shadow of the weighing shed stepped Deputy Factor Harrow — small, grey, ink-stained to the knuckles, the man who had drawn up Tamsin's indenture and her mother's before hers, the man whose handwriting lived on every certificate of death the Company had filed in six years. Harrow would not meet anyone's eyes. His hands shook around a folio of documents.

Edmund understood one heartbeat too late what Calloway had truly prepared: not just an accusation but a demonstration. Harrow would swear the letters were forgeries in Vane's hand — and who could contradict him? The one man whose handwriting mattered was two years drowned. The evidence would die right here, officially, tidily, before the very officers whose testimony would bury it forever.

"Deputy Factor," Calloway said silkily, "you examined the documents seized at the toll bridge. Tell the superintendent what you found."

Harrow opened his mouth.

And from the shell crate came a voice like gravel dragged over wood.

"Harrow, you weasel, if you call my hand a forgery I will climb out of this box and break your spectacles on your face."

* * *

Later, Tamsin could never reconstruct the order of events, only their effect. The carriers set down the poles. The lid came up. Straw spilled. And Aldous Vane rose out of a crate of pearl shell into the dawn light — gaunt, yellowed, wrapped in a diver's spare coat, one hand gripping the crate's rim, the other raised toward the assembled officers in something between a salute and a curse — and the fleet gate made a sound Tamsin had never heard a crowd make: the sound of two hundred people inhaling at once.

"Aldous Vane," said Sir Emeric Hallowes, very slowly, "is dead."

"Sir Emeric," Vane rasped, "I have spent two years being dead, and I assure you it is vastly overrated." He got one leg over the crate's rim and stood swaying, refusing every offered arm, until his balance came back to him the way a ship comes back to its heading. "Two years on the hulk *Retribution*, at your Company's pleasure. Two years legally drowned, so that my debts could be voided and my prize-share claimed. I am told the paperwork was very neat."

He looked at Harrow.

"Neat, wasn't it, Harrow? You wrote it neatly. My drowning. And poor Nye's drowning. And the elder Brack's. And—" he paused, and his eyes found Tamsin's, and something old and sorry moved in his ruined face — "and Thomas Thornbury's. All neat. All in your hand."

Tamsin's knees nearly went. Edmund's hand found her elbow and held.

Calloway moved fast — she gave him that. Even now, even with a dead man standing in his gateway, he did not break. "Gentlemen, this is theatre," he said crisply. "A stowaway claims to be a drowned officer. Any beggar can claim a name. Documents are proof, and the documents—"

"The documents," Tamsin said, and stepped forward, and began unpicking the stitching at her ribs with a diver's fingers, quick and sure, "are here."

She brought them out into the light: forty pages in oilcloth, and she unwrapped them the way she had been taught to unwrap a wounded hand, slowly, deliberately, letting every officer in eyeshot see. "Letters in Captain Vane's hand, smuggled out over eight months. Names. Dates. Certificates of drowning for men who are alive — and certificates for men who are genuinely drowned, killed for what they knew. Filed, stamped, and paid for by the Honourable Company's trading house of this port." She held the bundle out — not to Calloway, not to the guards, but to Sir Emeric Hallowes himself. "Read one aloud, sir. Right here. Let everyone hear what your signature has been sailing around the world on top of."

For a long moment Hallowes did not move. Tamsin watched him weigh things — trade, scandal, the fleet at anchor, the man standing alive out of a crate — and she thought, with a coldness that surprised her: *he will refuse.* She had already begun composing the next move, shouting the names herself, when Hallowes took the letters.

He read. His face did something complicated. He read again.

"Deputy Factor Harrow," Hallowes said at last, and his voice had gone quiet in the way that quiet precedes cannon fire. "These certificates. Your office issued them?"

Harrow made a sound like a hinge. His eyes went to Calloway — one naked, pleading, calculating glance, the glance of a small man measuring which large man would still be standing at the end of the morning — and then, seeing the captain-superintendent holding the letters and two hundred witnesses holding their breath, he measured wrong.

"They were dictated," Harrow said. "Every one. The dates, the causes, the disbursement of estates. Dictated to me by the factor. I have the drafts. I kept the drafts." His voice climbed, cracking, gathering speed like something rolling downhill. "I kept everything, sir, I kept it because I knew — God help me, I knew — the *Providence* was never lost, Captain Vane was put aboard the hulk in March of '45, Thornbury was—"

"Enough," Calloway said, and it was the first thing he had said that sounded like a man rather than an office.

"It is not enough," said Hallowes. "It will never be enough again."

* * *

Edmund stood very still and watched a fortress dismantle itself in real time.

He had imagined this moment a hundred ways down the estuary road, and in none of them did it happen like this: not with swords or arrests or confessions wrung out under lamplight, but with arithmetic. With numbers simply failing to add up anymore in front of enough important men. Calloway kept talking — procedure, jurisdiction, the sanctity of Company charter, the danger of listening to mutineers and smugglers — and with each sentence Edmund could hear the words losing their purchase, sliding off the morning like water off oiled cloth, because Aldous Vane was standing in the light where everyone could count his fingers and toes, and no rhetoric could drown him twice.

Calloway saw it too. Edmund watched him see it — watched the exact moment the factor's strategy shifted from victory to survival. "Sir Emeric, a private word—"

"There is nothing private left this morning, Mr. Calloway."

"The fleet sails on the tide—"

"The fleet," Hallowes said, "sails when I say it sails." He turned to his officers and began issuing orders in a voice worn smooth by thirty years of command: the gate sealed, the *Retribution* boarded, the port's magistrate sent for — and then stopped, and looked at the letters in his hand, and at Harrow's folio of drafts, and said, flatly, "Though I begin to wonder what use the magistrate will be, if what this woman says holds."

"It holds," Tamsin said.

Guards crossed the ground toward Calloway. Edmund braced for violence, for the animal moment when a powerful man understands his power is gone — but Calloway surprised him. The factor straightened his coat, squared the green ledger in the crook of his arm, and stood still while they took hold of his elbows, wearing the expression of a man temporarily inconvenienced by clerical error. Only his eyes moved: once to Vane, once to Tamsin, once — longest — to Edmund, and in them Edmund saw no surrender at all, only bookkeeping. Debts deferred. Accounts to be settled elsewhere, by other means, in some jurisdiction where paper still obeyed money.

"This is not justice," Calloway said evenly. "This is embarrassment. They are not the same thing, and you will all learn the difference at your leisure."

"Maybe," Tamsin answered him, loud enough for the gate to hear. "But today it's both, and today is mine."

They led him away through his own gate, past his own guards, who did not look at him, and the crowd's silence broke into a low murmur that followed him out like a tide going out after a shipwreck, taking things with it.

Vane's legs chose that moment to quit. He sat down hard on the edge of the crate, and Edmund was beside him before anyone else moved, and for a while the three of them — Vane, Edmund, Tamsin — stood in a knot amid the dispersing officers, saying nothing, while the fleet gate resumed being a gate: orders shouted, lanterns doused, the ordinary machinery grinding back to life around the hole Calloway had left in it.

"Well," Vane said at last, to Tamsin. "You'd be Marion's girl."

"I'd be Thomas's girl," Tamsin said.

"Yes." Vane's ruined face did something gentle. "That too. I owe you a great deal, Thomas's girl, and I pay debts badly, as you may have heard."

Around them, Hallowes's clerks were already confiscating Harrow's folio, and Harrow was confessing faster and faster, a man trying to outrun his own signature. Edmund listened with half an ear and felt nothing like triumph. He watched the officers stack the evidence, watched the certificates change hands, watched the colony's entire apparatus of law arrive late to claim a justice that had already happened — happened here, in the space between a woman's stitched ribs and a drowned man's borrowed coat, before any court convened.

He caught Tamsin watching the same thing. Their eyes met, and he saw in hers what he felt in his own chest: the bitter clarity arriving exactly on schedule, cold and clean as deep water. Whatever came next — trials or none, restitution or silence, and every instinct he owned whispered *silence* — it would not be justice as any book described it. The law had been in the gateway all morning. The law had been on Calloway's side of the scales until the scales themselves were tipped by hand.

"Captain-superintendent," a clerk called across the yard, breathless, "the factor's residence — his study has been cleared, sir. Papers burned. The ledgers — the port ledgers, sir, the debt registers — someone's taken them. There's ash in the grate and the strongbox is empty."

Tamsin went very still beside him.

Edmund turned to look at her, and found her looking back with an expression he had never seen on her face before — not innocence, not guilt, something prouder than either.

"Empty," she repeated, to no one in particular. "Imagine that."

"What," Edmund murmured, "did you do?"

She smiled, slow as sunrise over the outer bed. "Nothing the law can charge," she said. "I only corrected somebody's arithmetic."
