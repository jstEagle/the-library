## 36. Burning the Ledger

The news of Calloway's dismissal reached the diving beach before it reached the counting house, because news on the pearling coast travels the way water does — through every crack, without asking permission.

I was mending a net when Brack's boy came sprinting along the tideline, arms pumping, shouting it before he was even close enough to be heard properly. *He's gone. The factor's gone. They walked him out this morning.* The words arrived in pieces and then all at once, and the beach went quiet in that particular way — not silence but a held breath, forty divers and packers and line-tenders all pretending to keep working while their ears did the real labor.

Then someone laughed. One short bark of it, quickly swallowed, like a man coughing in church. And after that the whole beach found reasons to talk very loudly about nothing at all.

Edmund found me by the net racks. He had a way of arriving without seeming to walk toward you, as though he'd simply been standing somewhere else one moment and next to you the next. Two years of moving carefully through ports where questions were dangerous will do that to a man. He crouched, took the needle from his pocket — Vane's needle, the one that had made my broken compass whole again — and turned it over in his fingers without looking at it. A habit now. His hands needed small honest work.

"You heard," I said.

"Half the port's heard. The other half is on its way."

"Is it true they're not trying him?"

"Superintendent's man told the crowd it was 'a matter of internal administration.' Which is the company's way of saying a crime happened, but not to anyone who matters." Edmund set the needle down on my knee, precisely, as if it were evidence in a trial that would never be held. "No magistrate. No restitution. He'll take ship for Melbourne by month's end with his pension and his letters of recommendation, and some other port will get Mr. Calloway, thinking itself lucky."

I looked out at the water. The morning fleet boats were already out over the beds, their sail shadows sliding across the shallows like slow birds. Somewhere under that green ceiling lay shells worth more than every diver who'd ever bled opening them, and above it all hung the comfortable fiction that what had happened here had been dealt with.

"He put my mother's debt at eleven pounds four shillings," I said. "When she died it was nineteen. She never borrowed a shilling after her first season. Do you know how a debt grows when nobody's allowed to see the arithmetic?"

"I've seen ledgers do worse," Edmund said quietly.

"Everyone keeps telling me the law can't give me anything." I stood, brushing sand from my knees. "But there's a thing the law doesn't have to give me, because it was mine before the law ever got involved."

* * *

The factor's office sat on stilts above the harbor road, a weatherboard box the color of old bone, with windows fogged by salt and a door that had been locked for as long as I'd been alive — or so I'd believed, back when I still believed doors belonged to the people who owned them. Today it stood open. Today it could not stand any other way.

The dismissal had emptied the building like a tide going out. Clerks drifted through the corridors carrying armloads of files, unsure whether they were preserving records or looting them, since from where they stood the two acts looked identical. Company men who had spent years not seeing each other's faces were suddenly seeing them everywhere, and no one knew yet which version of loyalty the new order would reward. In the counting room two junior factors argued in whispers over a strongbox whose key had gone missing along with the man who used to wear it on a chain.

I walked in wearing my inspector's sash, because the sash still worked. Authority, I had learned at the customs house, is mostly costume. You wear it correctly and men assume the rest.

Nobody stopped me. That was the miracle of the hour, the small grace the scandal handed me: in the confusion of a falling regime, a woman walking purposefully down a corridor reads as business, not theft. A clerk glanced up from an armload of shipping manifests, saw the sash, nodded, went back to his papers. Somewhere a bell rang for no reason. Somewhere else, men were loading Calloway's personal trunks onto a cart, and the sound of the loading ramp was the only music in the building.

His office was at the end of the hall. The door was open there too, and the room had been picked over already — the good inkstand gone, the wall map of the pearling grounds half-rolled, a chair askew. But against the far wall, in the iron cabinet, the ledgers remained. Of course they did. Ledgers are heavy and boring, and no clerk wants to carry boring weight when interesting things are being carried off all around him.

The indenture registers were on the third shelf. I knew exactly which one, because I had read my own page so many times the memory of it lived in my fingers: the soft spot on the spine where the cover had been thumbed open, year after year, to the Thornbury entry. Debt entered. Interest applied. Debt increased. Interest applied again. My mother's name in faded iron-gall ink, then mine beneath it, transferred like a cargo, like a crate passed from one hold to another.

I took the whole volume. It weighed more than a pearl shell crate, less than a drowning. I carried it out past the arguing clerks and the drifting files and the empty strongbox, past a company man so busy stealing a brass letter-opener that he forgot to look suspicious doing it, and down the stilts' ladder to the harbor road, and no one said a word.

The strange part was how little my heart raced. I had expected to feel like a thief. Instead I felt like a woman retrieving a coat from a hook.

* * *

Edmund was waiting where I'd asked him to wait, at the northern curve of the beach where the driftwood piles up in black tangles and the town's lamps don't reach. He had driftwood gathered in a teepee tall as his chest, and a flask of lamp oil in his hand, and the expression of a man who has decided that if he must be party to something unlawful, he will at least be efficient about it.

"You're certain about this?" he asked. Not objecting. Asking the way you ask a diver checking her own lines — confirming she's tied on before she goes over the side.

"The superintendent buries the scandal," I said. "Calloway retires comfortably. No court hears my mother's name. Fine. I've stopped waiting for institutions to do arithmetic." I set the ledger down on a flat stone and looked at its cover — the company's crest stamped in gold that had dulled to the color of old butter. "But this book says my family owed nineteen pounds we never borrowed. As long as that record exists, some clerk in some office, ten years from now, twenty, can pull it off a shelf and say the Thornburys are still bound. Debts outlive the men who invent them. That's the whole trick. That's the whole *business*."

"So we burn the record, the debt dies with it?"

"That's not how the law would see it."

"No," Edmund agreed. "The law would call it destruction of company property. Possibly theft." He uncorked the lamp oil. "Though I'd argue it isn't theft, taking back the only copy of your own account. It's more like—" he tipped the flask, oil soaking into the driftwood and the ledger both, "—arithmetic corrected."

I looked at him. "That's almost word for word what I was going to say."

"Two years of reading Aldous's letters. Some phrases stick." He struck a match, cupped it against the sea wind. "You want to, or shall I?"

"It should be me. It's my page in it."

The match touched the oil and the fire went up with a soft rushing sound, like a wave heard through a wall, and then the ledger caught and the gold crest darkened first, blistered, and slid off in a black bead onto the stone.

We stood and watched it burn.

I won't pretend it was quick. Ledgers burn slowly; they're dense with years, packed tight as shell, and the fire had to work for every page the way a diver works for every shell on the deep bed — patiently, with stubbornness, taking them one at a time. I could see pages glowing at the edges before the flame took them, and in those few seconds between glow and ash I could sometimes make out entries. Figures. Names. A column of shillings. Once, briefly, the ghost of a date I knew: the month my father signed on for his last season, the month they later wrote down as the month he drowned.

"It's not justice," I said, as the middle of the book collapsed inward in a bloom of sparks. "I know that. Justice would be Calloway in a dock. It would be the company made to pay back every inflated shilling to every family on this coast. It would be my father's death called what it was, in public, by people empowered to call it that."

"And instead?"

"Instead I get this." The heat pressed against my face. My eyes stung, and I let them. "It's smaller than justice. But it's *mine*. Nobody granted it. Nobody will pardon it or commute it or file it under internal administration. I did it myself, with my own hands, and no one can take it back or reverse it on appeal. There's a kind of freedom in an act that no authority approved. Maybe it's the only kind there is."

Edmund was quiet a while. The tide was coming in, long sheets of grey water sliding up the sand, and the light from the fire made our shadows swing huge across the flats behind us.

"When I came off that packet boat," he said finally, "I thought I was delivering paper. Evidence. Something a court would use someday, in a country with courts that work. I've been telling myself that story for two years — that the letters just needed to reach the right desk." He watched a page curl and go. "There's no right desk. There never was. There's just what people are willing to do, standing in front of each other, in front of witnesses, in daylight." He gestured at the fire with his chin. "This is daylight enough."

The last of the binding cracked. The iron clasp fell loose among the coals, refusing to burn, and I picked it out with a stick and left it on the stone to cool — a small curled thing, the only part of nineteen pounds of invented debt that would survive the night. I thought about keeping it. Then I thought better, and threw it as far as I could into the incoming tide. It made a sound too small to hear, and the water closed over it.

* * *

We stayed until the fire was embers, and then until the embers were a black stain on the stone, and the tide licked at the edge of the stain and retreated, and the stars came out in the indifferent way they do.

My mother found us there. I don't know how she knew — mothers on this coast have an instinct for their children's fires that predates any telegraph — but she came down the beach path with a shawl over her head and her hands folded, and she stood at the edge of the firelight looking at the scorched stone the way you look at a grave that's finally been filled in.

"I felt it," she said. Simply. "In my chest. Like something being untied."

"Was it the ledger?"

"It was the *waiting*," she said. "Nineteen years of waiting for someone to tell me what I owed and believing them." She looked at me then, and in the ember-light her face was both older and younger than it had been that morning. "Your father measured everything, you know. Same as you. What could be carried, what could be kept. He'd have understood tonight better than any court ever would."

She didn't stay long. She touched my shoulder once — a touch I am still learning to weigh correctly, after a lifetime of her holding nearly everything back — and went back up the path toward the diving village, where the lamps were lit and people who stayed were settling in for the night.

Edmund and I walked the tideline north, away from the town. The packet boat masts stood against the stars at the harbor moorings, thin and patient as needles. At the top of the sand I took out the compass and opened it, and the needle swung once, twice, and settled pointing out to sea, steady as a heartbeat.

"There's a fleet sailing east within the week," Edmund said, watching the needle rather than me, in the way he had of looking at the thing that mattered by way of the thing beside it. "Aldous means to be on it. Copies of everything — the letters, the certificates, the testimony we took. He says one port can be silenced. Twenty ports can't."

"He's well enough to travel?"

"He stood this afternoon without leaning on anything. He says the hulk taught him patience, and the rescue taught him the rest." Edmund smiled faintly. "He wants to see you before he boards. Says he owes your mother a compass and your father the truth, and he's only ever been able to deliver one of those."

I closed the compass. Out on the water, riding lights moved where the deep beds lay, and somewhere beyond the headland the prison hulk rode at anchor, empty now of the man it had held, soon to be empty of its purpose entirely. The world had not been fixed. I was clear-eyed enough tonight to know that. Calloway would prosper somewhere warmer, and the trade would go on, and somewhere a clerk was already entering new debts in a new ledger with a new gold crest.

But the fire was still warm on the beach behind me, and the book that had held my name hostage was smoke on the wind, and the list of things I needed to carry was growing shorter by the day.

"Aldous sails east," Edmund said. "And we should talk about what we do when the coast is behind us."

I looked at the needle, steady on the open water, and for the first time in my life the answer to *which way* required no measuring at all.
