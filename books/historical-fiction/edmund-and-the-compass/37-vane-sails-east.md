## 37. Vane Sails East

The fleet sailed in three days, not four, and by then Aldous Vane could stand.

Edmund watched him do it in Brack's loft, one hand on the low beam, the other hanging loose at his side as if he were testing whether the arm still belonged to him. Two years in the company's hold and eight days of salt broth and lamplight had carved him down to something like a knuckle of driftwood — all hard grain, no spare flesh — but he stood, and then he walked the length of the loft, and then he stood at the window and looked out at the harbor with an expression Edmund had last seen on a quarterdeck in the South China Sea, when a typhoon was coming and Vane had decided, privately, that he rather looked forward to it.

"You're staring, Roe," Vane said, without turning.

"You're thinner than the letters you sent me."

"I was thinner than the envelope." He turned. The lamplight caught the ruin the hulk had made of him — the cheek gone concave, the hair gone white at the temples where it had been brown, the old scar on his jaw pulled taut by weight he no longer had. But the eyes were the same. That was the thing Edmund had not been prepared for, the night they'd cut him out of the grate and rowed him through the turning tide with a dead sentry's shout still hanging over the water. The eyes were exactly the same. Gray, patient, faintly amused, as though the world were a joke Vane had heard the ending of and was too polite to spoil.

"They'll sail on the morning tide," Vane said. "I've been reading the harbor from this window. The *Coromandel* leads, then the two East Indiamen, then the shell barges under tow. I want passage on the *Coromandel*."

Edmund set down the cup he was holding. "You've been out of the hold for eight days. You can barely—"

"Eight days is longer than I had on the hulk, and I did two years there." Vane crossed to the table where the letters lay, the true ones and Brack's careful copies, weighted down under a piece of coral Tamsin had brought up from the deep bed. He put his hand flat on the copies the way a man puts a hand on a son's shoulder. "The originals stay here. They've done their work in this port — Calloway is finished, whatever quiet shape his finishing takes. But a scandal that lives in one harbor dies in one harbor. The company has factors in a dozen ports, and every one of them has a drawer full of forged death certificates with a different seal on it. The copies go east. Bombay, Singapore, Batong, wherever the fleet touches. I'll put them into hands that can't be bought, because the hands that can be bought have already been bought, and I know which is which."

"You'll be recognized."

"I'll be dead," Vane said pleasantly. "Officially. It's the most useful thing the company ever did for me. A drowned man can walk up a gangway in broad daylight and no one looks twice, because looking twice at dead men is bad luck, and sailors are very superstitious about bad luck." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that had made Edmund follow him into three storms and one war. "Besides, I've spent two years learning how not to be seen. It seems a waste to let the skill rust."

Edmund looked at him for a long moment. There was an argument to be had — there were several — and he felt every one of them lining up behind his teeth. *You're not strong enough. The hulk broke you. If they take you again there will be no grate and no tide and no Tamsin Thornbury to swim the estuary with oilcloth against her skin.* All true. All beside the point. He had known Aldous Vane for eleven years, and in eleven years he had never once won an argument that Vane had decided to lose on purpose, and he had never once changed Vane's mind by standing in front of it.

"You've already spoken to Brack about the passage," Edmund said.

"Last night. He wept a little, which I'll deny to my grave, or rather to my second grave." Vane sat down heavily on the crate — the sitting was still a negotiation between him and gravity — and looked up. "Sit down, Edmund. I want to say a thing or two before the tide does, and I'd rather say it to your face than to your back."

Edmund sat.

* * *

The loft smelled of shell dust and lamp oil and the brine that lived in everything in this port, in the walls and the clothes and, Edmund suspected by now, in his own blood. Below them Brack's boy — a different boy, the first one gone with his coin and his shame to who knew where — was stacking shell crates for the loading, and the sound of it came up through the floorboards like a slow heartbeat.

"I owe you a death," Vane said.

"That's an odd way to open."

"It's an accurate one. You came here with my letters and no plan worth the name, and you should have drowned at the second checkpoint, or hung at the third, and instead you fell in with a diver who reads contracts like a barrister and a smuggler who burns his own safe-conducts for the pleasure of it, and between the three of you I am sitting upright in a loft instead of rotting in a cage at the bottom of a company ledger." Vane turned his cup in his hands. He had barely touched the tea Tamsin had made him; his stomach was still learning to accept gifts. "The sentry on the hulk. The one who died."

Edmund said nothing. He had not spoken of it to anyone but Tamsin, and to her only once, at night, in the shell barge, in the flat voice of a man reporting weather. *He came around the corner. He had a knife. I had a marlinspike. It was over in the length of a breath and it was not a fair breath.* Tamsin had said: *It never is. That's what makes it killing and not dueling.* And then she had put her hand on the back of his neck, briefly, the way you'd steady a spar, and said nothing more, because there was nothing more that was true.

"He'd have sounded the alarm or he wouldn't," Edmund said now. "I've done the arithmetic a hundred ways. It doesn't come out clean."

"It never does. I've killed four men, Edmund, and I can account for three of them cleanly, which is a better ratio than most, and the fourth I think about at dusk." Vane set the cup down. "I'm not absolving you. I'm telling you the weight doesn't lighten. It just becomes yours instead of a stranger's, and one day you'll find you've been carrying it so long you've stopped noticing which pocket it's in." He paused. "You came here to deliver paper. You're leaving having delivered a man. That's a promotion, of a kind, though the pay is worse."

"The pay," Edmund said, "has been catastrophic."

Vane laughed — a real laugh, rusty at first and then full, and Edmund heard in it the man from the *Providence*'s deck eleven years ago, laughing at the storm, laughing at the admiralty, laughing at Edmund's solemn young face when Edmund had informed him that the course they were steering was not on any chart. Vane had said: *Charts are where the sea keeps its opinions, boy. The sea keeps others.* And then he had steered through the uncharted water and brought them out the far side, and Edmund had never quite gotten over it, and had never wanted to.

"I'm not going to thank you," Vane said, when the laugh had settled. "You'd only make a face."

"I would."

"I'll do it anyway, briefly, so it's on the record. Thank you. There." He raised the cup a half-inch. "Now. The copies."

They spent the afternoon over them. There were fourteen — Brack's boy's replacements, written out fair in a clerk's hand that fooled even Edmund, each one wrapped in oilcloth and waxed, each one addressed in a cipher only Vane and three men alive could read. Vane distributed them about his person with the economy of a man who had spent two years with nothing and had learned precisely how much a body could hold: two in the lining of the coat Brack had found him, two in the false heel of a boot, one flat against his sternum in a pocket sewn for the purpose, the rest in a sealed satchel that would go into the *Coromandel*'s mail chest under a name that did not exist.

"The originals," Vane said, "stay with the girl."

"Tamsin."

"With Tamsin." He said the name carefully, the way he said the names of ships he intended to remember. "She's the one who should hold them. Not because she's the most careful — though she is — but because they're hers more than mine now. Her father's name is in them. Her mother's silence. Her own chain, and her own hand that broke it." He looked at Edmund over the table. "You understand that, don't you? You came here carrying a dead man's grievance. She's living it."

Edmund did understand it. He had understood it since the night in the loft when she had heard the letters read aloud and gone very still, the stillness of deep water, and then said, in a voice with no harbor in it at all: *Read the fourth one again.* Her father. The *Larkspur*. A drowning arranged to clear a debt of eleven pounds, four shillings, and the cost of a certificate. Eleven pounds. Edmund had watched her do the arithmetic in her face — the same arithmetic she did with everything, the weighing of what can be carried — and he had understood that some sums break a person open and some sums build them, and that in her the two had happened at once.

"He'll want to see you off," Edmund said. "Brack."

"Brack and I have said what we need. He's a man who expresses himself in burned paper." Vane smiled. "It's you I wanted the hour with. The tide's at six; the pilot boat goes at five. That gives us until five."

* * *

They walked down to the water in the last of the afternoon, Vane moving slowly, one hand on Edmund's arm in a gesture that was half support and half the old captain's habit of taking hold of things he intended to steer. The harbor was gold and rust. The *Coromandel* lay at her moorings with the fleet gathered around her like a family photograph, and the shell barges were already forming up under tow, and everywhere was the noise of departure — hawsers, gulls, the shouted arithmetic of mates counting cargo.

They stopped where the beach curved toward the fleet gate, at the stretch of sand where, ten days ago, a woman had burned a ledger in a small fire and called it arithmetic corrected. The scorch mark was still there under the sand, Edmund knew, though the tide had dressed it over.

"I have a confession," Vane said.

"You have several. I've decided to stop keeping the list."

"This one's short. I sent the letters to you because you were the only man I trusted and the only man I thought might be too stubborn to die." He looked out at the water. "I did not send them to you because I thought you'd succeed. I sent them because I thought someone should know the truth before I went into the ground, even if the knowing did no good. A message in a bottle, Edmund. I never expected the bottle to grow hands."

"And now?"

"Now I've watched a customs inspector walk me past her own checkpoint as a hired porter. I've watched a smuggler hand over a season's pearls for the privilege of hiding me under fish. I've watched a woman dive under a bridge with my whole case against the company wrapped against her ribs." He turned, and the gray eyes were not amused now. They were the eyes he'd used on Edmund once, in a gale, when he'd said *you'll do* and Edmund had spent a year trying to earn it. "The company taught me that the world runs on ledgers. Your generation is teaching me it runs on people who refuse to keep them. I find I prefer the second system, though it's harder on the personnel."

The pilot boat's boy came down the beach then, calling the hour, and the moment broke the way moments do — not shattered but folded away, to be taken out later in some other port, some other life.

They stood facing each other on the sand. Edmund had imagined this part, in the nights of the last week, and in his imaginings it had been difficult — words, grips, the ceremony of parting. In fact it was simple. Vane put out his hand, and Edmund took it, and then, because eleven years and two years and one rescued man deserved more than a handshake, they held each other's forearms the way sailors do, and Vane said, quietly, "Brother," which was a word he had never used before, and Edmund said, "Go east, you old fraud, before I talk you out of it," which was the only answer he could have given without his voice failing him.

Vane stepped back. He looked, Edmund thought, ten years younger than he had that morning and ten years older than the man who had gone into the water two years ago, and both of those were true at once, and both of those were him.

"The envelope," Vane said, at the waterline, turning once more. "You kept it?"

Edmund touched his breast pocket. The empty envelope, salt-stained, addressed in a drowned man's hand, had not left his person since the customs house. "Every day."

"Good. Keep it empty. It's the only envelope in the world that's worth more with nothing in it." And then Vane laughed, and raised one hand, and waded out to the pilot boat's skiff, and did not look back, because he was Aldous Vane and looking back was for men with less faith in their own endings.

Edmund stood on the beach until the skiff reached the *Coromandel* and a figure climbed the ladder slowly, with rests, and was swallowed by the side of the ship. The sun was going down behind the fleet. Somewhere on the *Coromandel*, in a coat lining and a boot heel and a pocket over the heart, fourteen copies of the truth were sailing east into a dozen harbors, and there was nothing left in this port for them to do.

* * *

He found Tamsin on the shell barge, mending her diving suit by lamplight — the same suit, the one with the letters' ghosts sewn into its seams, though the letters themselves were back in Brack's strongbox now, waiting for whatever she decided their future was. She looked up when he came aboard and read his face the way she read water, at a glance, all the way down.

"He's gone."

"An hour past. He stood up on the deck by himself. He waved at the harbor like a man who owned it."

Tamsin nodded and went back to her needle for a moment, and then set it down. "And you?"

Edmund sat down across from her, on a crate that smelled of the deep bed. He thought about the question honestly, which was the only way she ever accepted answers. "Lighter," he said at last. "And strangely lost. I've been carrying him for two years — the letters, the silence, the not knowing. I woke every morning with a dead man's errand and went to sleep with it. Now the errand's delivered and the man's alive and on a ship, and I'm—" He gestured at the lamplight, the barge, the harbor going dark. "I'm a man on a barge."

"You're a man on a barge with a ticket on a coastal packet," Tamsin said. "Two tickets. Brack settled it this morning while you were being sentimental at the waterline."

"You were listening."

"I'm always listening. It's how I've stayed alive." She picked up the needle again, then didn't use it. "There's one thing left before the packet sails. I have to see my mother."

The lamplight moved between them. Edmund knew what that visit was — knew the shape of it from the pieces she had given him, the mother who had known about Vane and the false drownings and had held her silence for years like a diver holding breath, and the daughter who had learned that silence was a kind of drowning too. Forgiveness had been begun between them, in the days after the gate, but begun was all it was. There was a wound in that house on the divers' row that needed one more conversation, and Tamsin was the only one who could have it.

"Tonight?" he asked.

"Tonight. At the turning of the tide, when she'll be home from the sheds." Tamsin folded the suit closed and stood, and in the lamplight she looked, for a moment, like the woman who had walked him through a checkpoint as her porter — calm rehearsed over fear — except that now the calm was not rehearsed at all. It had been earned, the way everything of hers was earned. "Come with me or wait here. Either."

"I'll come," Edmund said. "I'd like to meet the woman who raised the only inspector in this port with a working conscience."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the harbor it lived in. "She'll want to feed you. Refuse nothing. It's how her people say the things they can't say out loud."

They went up the barge ladder together, two people with almost nothing, by design, and walked out along the shore road toward the lights of the divers' row, where a mother who had stayed silent was waiting to speak at last.
