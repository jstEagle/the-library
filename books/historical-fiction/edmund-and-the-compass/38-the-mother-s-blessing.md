## 38. The Mother's Blessing

The divers' row had not changed in the way Tamsin had feared and not changed at all in the way she had hoped. It was still a spine of packed earth between two lines of low houses, their walls made of coral block and driftwood, their roofs thatched with palm that went silver-grey in the salt wind. Smoke stood up from cook-fires in straight columns, because there was no wind at this hour, only the great held breath of the port before the fleet sailed. Somewhere a child was laughing at something. Somewhere a woman was singing the low, wordless song they sang when they oiled their suits, a sound like the sea learning to speak.

Tamsin stopped at the mouth of the row and looked at it for longer than she needed to.

"You're stalling," Edmund said gently.

"I'm cataloguing," she said. "It's a habit."

"What does the list come to?"

She considered the row of houses, the smoke, the smell of lime and fish and frangipani growing wild out of the drainage ditch, the particular quality of lamplight through shell-paned windows that she had never seen anywhere else and had never once thought beautiful until she had almost lost it.

"More than I can carry," she said, and walked on.

Her mother's house was the fourth from the end, because her mother was a woman who believed in being near the sea without being at its mercy — close enough to hear the tide turn from her bed, far enough that no storm surge would ever take her in the night. Tamsin had learned diving at four years old in the shallows behind this house. She had learned silence in its single inner room.

The door was already open. Her mother had heard them coming; the whole row heard everything coming.

Maren Thornbury stood in the doorway with a lamp in one hand and a cloth wrapped around something hot in the other, and she did not say *you came* or *it's late* or any of the sentences that lived at the front of other mothers' mouths. She looked at her daughter the way divers look at the surface from depth — measuring the distance, knowing it must be crossed slowly or not at all.

Then she looked at Edmund, head to boots, taking in the salt-worn leather, the hollows under his eyes, the empty envelope he carried folded inside his coat like other men carried letters.

"He's thin," she said to Tamsin, in the trade-tongue of the row, as though he were not present, as though he were cargo she was assessing.

"All port food," Tamsin said. "He'll mend."

"He'll mend faster fed." She stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before the mosquitoes eat what's left of him."

* * *

The house inside was exactly as Tamsin remembered and entirely different. The same table of ship's planking scrubbed white. The same two chairs and the long bench. But on the wall where a company calendar had hung for as long as Tamsin could remember — the kind the factors handed out free every January, with the fleet schedules printed in gold so you measured your whole year by their departures — there was now nothing. Just a paler rectangle on the coral-block wall, the ghost of the ledger's shadow, and beside it, hanging from the same nail, a hand-net for shell, mended and re-mended and mended again.

Tamsin looked at the pale rectangle for a long moment, and her mother watched her look.

"It came down the day after Calloway was dismissed," Maren said. "Before anyone told me it was safe."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted it down." Her mother set the hot bundle on the table — flatbread and small fish, cooked in oil and lime, the meal she made when words failed — and began unwrapping the cloth without looking at it, her hands doing what her hands knew. "Thirty-one years I let their paper tell me when my year began and ended. One day I woke up and the paper meant nothing anymore, and I found I couldn't get it off the wall fast enough."

Edmund stood just inside the door, uncertain, holding his cap.

"Sit," Maren said, pointing at the bench. "You don't stand in my house while there's food on the table. That's not a welcome, it's an instruction."

He sat. He ate everything, refused nothing, as Tamsin had told him, and with each plate her mother's shoulders lowered by degrees, some private arithmetic of hospitality being satisfied.

Tamsin ate too, though her stomach was tight. There was a thing to be said here and they both knew it, and the food was only the anteroom to it.

* * *

When the plates were pushed back, Maren poured three fingers of arrack into clay cups and sat across from her daughter with her hands folded — hands Tamsin knew better than her own face: scarred along the index fingers from line-work, one nail permanently darkened where shell had crushed it decades ago, knuckles that had taught a hundred knots.

"I'm going to say things now," her mother said. "I'd thank you to let me finish them, because if I stop I may not start again, and I've been starting for thirty years."

Tamsin nodded once.

"I knew about Vane." No wind-up, no preamble. Her mother had always dived deep on the first breath. "Not everything. Not names and dates. But Aldous Vane came to this house in the wet season of '43, before you were born, or just after — you were a baby asleep in that basket, there, where the water jar is now. He brought the compass." Her eyes flicked to Tamsin's pocket, and Tamsin understood that she had known about the needle, about all of it, longer than Tamsin knew. "He told me the company was drowning men on paper. He told me your father's name would be on such a paper someday if he didn't stop them, and then—" her voice caught, held, released, "—and then your father's name was on one anyway, and Vane disappeared, and I made a choice. I chose you."

"I know," Tamsin said quietly.

"Let me finish." But her hand came across the table and covered her daughter's, so the rebuke was half a blessing. "I chose you over the truth. I let them measure me in shell and debt. I let them teach you to measure yourself the same way. And every year the debt grew and every year I said nothing, because saying something would have put their eyes on this house, and their eyes on this house meant their eyes on a girl who could dive deeper than anyone on this coast, and girls who dive deep are valuable, and valuable things get owned." She lifted her cup but didn't drink. "I am not asking you to call it right. I'm telling you what it cost me to do it, which is not the same thing, and I know it's not the same thing."

The lamp flame leaned and righted itself. Out on the water, faintly, the fleet's anchor bells rang the first watch.

"I hated you for it," Tamsin said. It came out steadier than she expected. "Not at first. At first I just thought the world was heavy and we were both carrying our share. But when I read my contract — when I saw the clause, and understood you had signed me into it—"

"I signed you into it to keep you out of the deep beds," her mother said. "Indentured divers work the inner shallows under guard. Free divers go wherever the shell is, and the outer bed kills two or three a season. I did the arithmetic every mother on this row has done. I chose the chain that was likelier to keep you breathing."

"And it nearly didn't."

"No," her mother said simply. "It nearly didn't. I know. I heard about the bridge." Her thumb moved once across Tamsin's knuckles, feeling for damage, an old habit from a thousand dives. "I have spent two years being silent and two weeks being afraid, and I find the second is worse. So here is the last of what I have to say, and then we'll eat more, because grief keeps better on a full stomach."

She turned her cup slowly on the table, once around, like a compass needle finding north.

"I stayed silent because I thought safety was a place you could hide in. A house. A contract. The shallow water. But safety isn't a place, Tamsin. It's people. It's Brack burning his papers. It's a stranger off the packet boat carrying a dead man's handwriting because he gave his word. It's you, walking into Calloway's gate with the evidence sewn into your skin." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and dry, which was worse than tears, which was better than tears. "I hid in silence for thirty years and it kept nothing safe. It only kept me alone. So I'm done hiding, and since I'm done hiding, I'm also done leaving."

Tamsin frowned. "Leaving?"

"I know you have tickets. I know the packet sails on tomorrow's tide — Brack's boy told the whole row before Brack could stop him, and now the whole row is pretending they don't know, which on this street means everyone knows." A ghost of humor crossed her face. "I'm not coming."

* * *

The word landed in Tamsin like cold water in the ear — that sudden pressure, that wrongness of depth.

"Not coming," she repeated.

"I'm staying." Her mother said it the way she announced weather: factually, already past argument. "This row is thirty families. Twenty-two of them dive. Every one of them is indentured or in debt or both, and every one of them watched what happened at the fleet gate and drew conclusions. Calloway is gone, but the company will send another factor, and the new factor will be careful instead of greedy, and careful men are harder to beat. Someone has to be here who remembers how to read the contracts. Someone has to teach the young ones what the clauses mean before they sign them." She spread her scarred hands. "I can't dive anymore. My chest won't take it. But I can read, and I have a loud voice, and I have thirty years of reasons. That's a dowry of sorts."

"You'd be alone."

"I'd be *here*." Her mother's voice sharpened, and beneath the sharpness was the thing she had just promised to stop doing — protecting, choosing for her, the old reflex dying hard. Then she caught herself, and her voice gentled. "And no, I would not be alone. Have you looked out that window tonight? Half the row knows Vane's name now. Old Sefa's brother is on those forged certificates. The Okafor girls' father too. This street has been waiting thirty years for someone to tell us we were robbed, and now we know it, and knowing it together is worth more than any of us knowing it alone." She paused. "That's the lesson I got too late and you got early, my girl. The company never owned this coast. It only owned the paper. We own each other. That was always the stronger title."

Tamsin sat with it. She had spent her life measuring the world by what could be carried, and here was her mother measuring it by what could be *held* — held in common, held in place, held against the tide. Two different units. Both true.

"I wanted you to come with us," she said at last. "I want you to say that's wrong, and you won't, and I hate that you won't."

"That's the shape of it," her mother agreed. "Reconciliation isn't agreement. Nobody tells you that. It's two people finally telling the truth in the same room, and discovering the room holds you both."

* * *

Later, when the lamps burned low and Edmund had been installed on the bench with a blanket that smelled of sun and salt, mother and daughter stood in the doorway looking out at the black line of the harbor, where the masts of the quarterly fleet stood against the stars like a field of winter reeds. Somewhere out there, on the flagship, Aldous Vane slept his first honest sleep in two years, with copies of his letters in a locked case and open water ahead of him.

"He asked about you," Tamsin said. "Vane. On the hulk, barely conscious, first thing he said clear enough to understand was *is Maren alive*. Second thing was *did she ever forgive me*."

Her mother was quiet a long moment.

"Tell him forgiveness is like the tide," she said. "It doesn't come all at once. It comes in increments, and you mark the high-water line as you go." She touched the doorframe. "But tell him the compass found its needle. He'll understand what I mean by that, even if nobody else would."

"I'll tell him."

They stood together, shoulder to shoulder, and neither of them reached for the other, and neither needed to. Some reconciliations are conducted entirely in proximity.

"When you come back," her mother said — not *if*, Tamsin noted, and felt the word settle in her chest like ballast, "when you come back, this door will be open. Not because you belong to anything here. Because you *are* something here. There's a difference, and you've finally learned it, and that's my blessing, if you want one. That's the whole of it. Go, and be something, and come back."

Tamsin swallowed. "The row will change you, staying."

"The row already changed me. It raised you." Her mother smiled, and in the starlight it was her father's smile, the one Tamsin had carried in memory without knowing whose it was. "Now sleep. You sail on the morning tide, and the packet boat waits for no one — I've known that longer than you've been alive."

* * *

Tamsin did not sleep well, and did not try to. She lay on the pallet in the inner room listening to her mother's slow breathing through the wall, memorizing its rhythm the way she had once memorized tide tables, and near dawn she rose, and dressed, and took out the compass.

By lamplight she turned it over. The needle sat true in its pivot now, trembling slightly, pointing steadily at nothing useful and everything necessary — north, always north, whether anyone followed it or not. Her mother's gift, remade whole by a dead man's foresight and a living man's stubbornness. She closed her hand around it and felt the brass warm to her palm.

Belonging was never the company, she thought. It was never the paper, never the debt, never the wall calendar with its golden fleet days. It was the people who stayed — the mother at the open door, Brack with his burned papers, old Sefa singing to her oiled suit, the whole row pretending not to know about the tickets while feeding them for a week. You could not carry a thing like that. You could only carry the knowledge of it, and the intention to return.

At first light, Edmund appeared in the doorway with his cap in his hands and her mother pressing food on him that no rational man could eat, and Tamsin shouldered her bag — light, so light, by design — and they walked out of the divers' row together while the whole street found urgent business at its doorways and windows.

Behind them, her mother stood at the threshold and did not wave. She simply stood, open-handed, in the manner of women who watch boats leave for a living, and Tamsin looked back once, at the top of the shore road, and fixed the picture where pictures cannot be lost.

At the bottom of the road, where the sand began, Brack was waiting by the tideline with his boat and his bruised ribs and a grin that split his face like a hull breach.

"Packet's loading," he called. "And there's a fellow aboard asking after the two of you — packet clerk says he came aboard at anchor last night, paid double for the berth, wouldn't give a name. Only thing he'd say was—" Brack squinted at the scrap of paper in his fist, "—*'tell the diver and the clerk: I know what the superintendent buried, and I have copies too.'*"
