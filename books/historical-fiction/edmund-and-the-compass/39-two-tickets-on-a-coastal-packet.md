## 39. Two Tickets on a Coastal Packet

The packet boat was called the *Merrow*, and she was smaller than Edmund remembered — a stubby coastal trader with salt-scoured rails and a funnel that coughed black smoke in fits, as if the engine itself resented early departures. She had brought him to this port eleven weeks ago with drowned letters in his coat and no notion of what he would find. It seemed right, somehow, that she should carry him out.

He stood at the foot of the gangway with one canvas bag over his shoulder and watched Tamsin come down the quay through the morning crowd. She walked the way she always did, unhurried, upright, her eyes making their constant inventory of the world — measuring, weighing, cataloguing everything by what it weighed and whether it could be carried. But something in her had changed since the gate. Since Vane had stepped out from among the shell crates and the whole rotten machinery of Calloway's ledger had ground to a halt before two hundred witnesses. She still measured things. But now, Edmund thought, she measured them for herself.

She carried nothing but a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, slung across her back the way divers carry their gear. He knew what was in it because he had asked, the night before, and she had told him plainly: the compass, mended; a knife; one dress and one pair of trousers; her mother's shawl; and nothing else. Not one thing else.

"That's all?" he'd said.

"That's all I need," she'd said, and there had been no defiance in it, only arithmetic.

Now she stopped beside him at the gangway and looked up at the *Merrow*'s deck, where a handful of passengers were already settling themselves among crates of dried fish and coils of rope.

"Two tickets," she said. "You bought them?"

"I bought them."

"How much?"

"Tamsin—"

"How much?"

"Nine shillings each. It doesn't matter."

"It matters." She dug into the pocket of her trousers and pressed coins into his hand — old coins, pearl-buyers' tokens mostly, and three silver shillings that must have been Brack's parting gift. "I don't travel on anyone's charity. Not even yours."

He took the coins because it was easier than arguing, and because he understood, by then, that this was not pride exactly — or not only pride. It was the same discipline that let her hang underwater past the point of panic, counting her own heartbeats instead of surrendering to them. She kept accounts. She balanced them. And after a life spent inside a company's ledger, where every column was written against her, she would be damned if she'd start her freedom in debt to anyone, even him.

They went up the gangway together. The mate took their tickets, glanced at their faces with the flat incurious look of a man who had learned that questions cost money, and waved them aft. The engine ticked and shuddered beneath their feet. Somewhere forward, a hatch slammed.

* * *

The quay was already receding when Tamsin found her mother in the crowd.

Marian Thornbury stood apart from the other well-wishers, as she had stood apart her whole life, hands folded, gray hair pinned tight. She did not wave. That was never her way. She simply stood at the edge of the boards and looked out at the ship, and Tamsin looked back, and between them stretched forty yards of harbor water and twenty years of silence, and neither of them needed to cross either.

Yesterday her mother had pressed something into her hands at the loft door: a small paper packet, sealed, heavier than its size suggested. Inside, Tamsin had found two pearl buttons carved from shell so dark it was nearly purple — shell from the deep bed, the dangerous bed, the kind her father had died diving for and her mother had worn mourning for fifteen years without once explaining why.

"For the journey," her mother had said. "So you don't forget what you're made of."

And then, at the last, when Tamsin had turned to go: "Your father's name was on no list. Whatever they wrote about him. Remember that he dove better than any man on that coast, and he came up when he chose to come up, and the water didn't take him. Men like Calloway took him. Don't give the water the blame."

Tamsin stood at the *Merrow*'s stern rail now and watched her mother diminish. She did not cry. She felt the place inside her where crying would happen someday, perhaps years from now, in some quiet room in some far port, and she left it alone. Her mother had chosen to stay — chosen the diving community, the women who mended nets and read the tide tables and kept each other's children when the sea collected its rents — and Tamsin understood at last that this was not abandonment. It was belonging, of the only honest kind. Her mother belonged to people who stayed. Tamsin, it seemed, belonged to the leaving.

Edmund came to stand beside her. He did not speak. That was one of the things she had decided to keep him for — his silence, which was never empty.

* * *

By noon the port was gone entirely, swallowed into the haze behind them, and the *Merrow* was working her way up the coast through a long grey swell. They had found a place in the bow, half-sheltered by a stack of fish crates, and spread themselves out with the careful ceremony of people establishing a household. It took perhaps four minutes.

"This is everything," Tamsin said, and began laying it out on the deck board between them, item by item, the way an auctioneer might — or a diver taking inventory before descending. "One knife. One compass." She held it up. It sat in her palm, brass case dented, glass scratched, and where the needle had once been there was now a needle again — Vane's needle, filed to a fine point and seated on its pin with Edmund's tweezers and two hours of swearing on the night before the fleet sailed. When she tilted it, the needle swung lazily and settled, pointing north with the placid certainty of a thing that had never been lost at all, only waiting. "One compass that works. Which makes it worth more than every shell in the company's warehouse, though I couldn't prove that in any court."

"One envelope," Edmund said, and produced it from his breast pocket. It was, as advertised, empty — the envelope that had held the first of Vane's letters, addressed in that unmistakable hand, water-stained at one corner where it had crossed half the coast hidden in a boot heel. He could not have said why he kept it. Only that the letters themselves had gone to Vane, to copies, to the wide world beyond the port — and this one scrap of paper had been the beginning of everything, and beginnings deserved keeping.

"One shawl," Tamsin went on. "One spare shirt — yours. Two pearl buttons." She touched the little packet without opening it. "Three shillings, nine shillings' worth of ticket already paid, and—" she paused, and something moved across her face that was almost a smile, "—one season's pearls, traded to a smuggler for passage in a shell barge, which means I currently own the memory of my pearls and nothing more. Worth it."

"You'd do it again."

"In a heartbeat. Though I'd haggle harder with Brack. He robbed me and we both pretended he didn't."

Edmund laughed — really laughed, the first time since the hulk, since the sentry whose face he still saw sometimes between sleeping and waking. Tamsin heard the laugh and did not remark on it, but she stored it away, the way she stored everything valuable.

"And that's the whole estate," she said. "Two people, one compass, one empty envelope. Any factor in any port would laugh us off his books."

"Good," Edmund said. "Let him. We're off all the books."

She turned the compass in her hand and watched the needle tremble and settle, tremble and settle, faithful as breath. Her mother had been given this thing by Aldous Vane before Tamsin was born — before the falsified drownings, before the debts, before any of it — a gift between shipmates, or between lovers, or between two people who had each chosen silence for reasons Tamsin would never fully learn. For fifteen years it had pointed nowhere, needleless, useless, a pretty weight. She had carried it anyway. Perhaps, she thought, because it was the only object in her possession that had ever belonged to anything besides the company. Now it pointed north, and she supposed she and Edmund were going north, or somewhere, wherever packets went and factors didn't.

"Do you know what I keep thinking about?" she said. "The clause. Page four of my indenture. *Bound until such debt be cleared.* Eleven words. My whole life fit inside eleven words, and I never even read them until Edmund Roe walked into my customs house carrying dead men's handwriting." She shook her head slowly. "I burned the ledger. I watched the pages curl. And I keep expecting someone to tap me on the shoulder and tell me the debt still exists somewhere, in some office, in some copy — that you can't burn arithmetic."

"You can if the arithmetic was a lie."

"Yes. Well." She tucked the compass into its wrapping. "That's what I'm learning. Most of what I thought was mathematics was just somebody's handwriting."

* * *

The afternoon came on soft and golden, the coast unrolling to starboard in low green hills and the occasional white thread of a waterfall. Edmund sat with his back against the fish crates and a blank book open on his knee — a real book, bought yesterday from the stationer near the gate with almost the last of his money, because he had woken that morning with a sentence fully formed in his head and nowhere to put it.

He had been writing accounts for other men his whole working life, most of them false. First the shipping house in Portsmouth, then Vane's ventures, then the long terrible year of carrying letters he barely understood. Always transcribing, always bearing someone else's hand. Even the great work of the spring — the evidence, the exposure, the fall of Calloway — had finally been another man's testimony, Vane's story told through Vane's papers.

This book was his own.

*We came into the port separately,* he wrote, *and we leave it together, and the whole distance between those two facts is the only fortune I will ever bother to count again.*

He heard Tamsin shift beside him and felt her glance at the page. She did not read over shoulders — another entry on the list of reasons.

"Vane sailed east this morning," she said. "Did Brack's boy tell you? The whole harbor watched the fleet go out. First time in years the quarterly sailing felt like anything but a robbery."

"He'll do well out there. A living witness with copies — Calloway can be dismissed quietly, but he can't dismiss Vane from every court on the coast."

"Mm." She leaned her head back against the crate and closed her eyes against the sun. "Do you think they'll try again? The company. Whoever replaces Calloway."

"Someone will try. Companies don't grieve; they replace." He set down the pencil. "But it will take them years to rebuild what fell in one morning. And by then—" he looked out at the open water ahead, "—we'll be very hard to find."

"Not too hard. My mother has to know where to send letters."

"Then moderately hard to find," he amended, and she smiled with her eyes still closed.

The *Merrow*'s engine settled into its long mid-afternoon drone. A gull hung above the wake, considering. Forward, a sailor began some tuneless song about a girl in Fremantle, and gave it up after two verses.

Tamsin opened her eyes.

"There's one thing missing from the inventory," she said.

"Oh?"

"The list should end with *one future, unpriced*." She said it lightly, but her hand found his on the deck board between them, and her fingers laced through, and stayed. "Because that's the strange thing, Edmund. All my life I owned almost nothing, and it terrified me — every pearl accounted, every debt accruing, nothing of mine. Now I own almost nothing again, and it's a completely different thing. Because this time nobody else holds the ledger. This time, nothing of mine can be taken, because everything of mine is—" she gestured at the boat, the sea, the man beside her, the mended needle swinging true in its battered case, "—carried."

Edmund looked at their hands, then out at the water running gold toward the horizon, and picked his pencil back up.

*Hope,* he wrote at the top of a fresh page, *is measured the same way everything worth having is measured: not by how much there is, but by how little you need.*

The coast slid slowly westward behind them. Ahead, the packet boat pushed her modest smoke into the enormous sky, carrying two passengers, one compass, one empty envelope, and no debts at all — and somewhere beyond the horizon, ports they had never seen were waiting to learn their names.
