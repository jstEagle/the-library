## 40. What Can Be Carried

*Tamsin*

The packet boat was small enough that the sea felt like a passenger too — a third body aboard, shifting its weight, sighing against the hull, occasionally insisting on being heard. Tamsin sat in the bow with her knees drawn up and the mended compass open in her palm, watching the needle tremble toward a north she would never follow.

Behind her, Edmund slept against the cargo rail with his hat over his face and his boots — salt-ruined, sole-split, unreplaceable boots — propped on a coil of rope. He had refused to throw them away. *They carried me here,* he'd said, as if that settled it, and perhaps it did. She was learning that Edmund Roe measured things by what they had carried, not what they cost. It was a strange arithmetic. She was beginning to trust it.

The coast of the pearling port was still visible, a low blue smudge to starboard, thinning by the hour like ink washed from a brush. Somewhere under that smudge: the customs house where she had once held authority like a borrowed coat; the toll bridge whose pilings she had swum beneath while her lungs screamed; the fleet gate, now full of ships sailing east with Captain Aldous Vane aboard, carrying copies of letters that would land in a dozen ports before winter and refuse to die quietly, the way their author had refused to die quietly, the way everything in this story had refused.

She turned the compass over. The needle sat true in its cradle now, seated in the wax-and-brass fitting Edmund had fashioned on Brack's workbench three nights before they sailed, working by lamplight with his tongue between his teeth like a boy. The compass was whole for the first time since her mother had pressed it into her hands at fourteen and said nothing — her mother's whole farewell compressed into an object, because words were debts and her mother had spent her life avoiding debt.

*North is only useful if you intend to go somewhere,* Tamsin had thought at the time. She hadn't intended to go anywhere. She had intended to stay, to dive, to carry what she was told to carry, until the sea or the company took the difference out of her hide.

Now the list of what she owned fit inside one breath:

"A knife," she said aloud, because saying it made it real, and real things could be counted. "One knife, four inches, handle wrapped in tarred cord."

Edmund stirred under his hat but did not remove it.

"One compass, complete, needle original." That last part wasn't strictly true — the needle was Vane's, returned through Edmund across two years and one faked drowning, so that the object was less a possession than a round trip. She said it anyway. Possessions were allowed to have histories. "One diving suit, patched at the left shoulder, letters no longer sewn within."

That had been strange, unpicking the threads. For six days the evidence had ridden against her skin, oil-wrapped, a second heartbeat flat and papery. When she'd finally handed the bundle to Vane on the dock — he standing, which still astonished her; he standing and squinting in daylight like a man reintroduced to the sun — she had felt oddly naked, and then lighter than naked. There was a weight that was not yours even when it touches your skin. She was done carrying other people's weight. She intended to be precise about the distinction for the rest of her life.

"Two tickets," she went on, "already punched, therefore worthless as tickets and valuable as proof we paid." She liked that. The company had taught her to keep records; she kept them now only for herself. "One envelope, empty, belonging to the sleeping gentleman aft, who claims it is sentimental."

"It *is* sentimental," Edmund said from under the hat, voice thick with sleep.

"You're awake."

"I'm auditing." He pushed the hat up. His face had lost the rehearsed calm she'd first seen on the customs-house steps — that careful, practiced nothing she had clocked in half a second and followed out of pure dangerous curiosity. What lived under the calm now looked like weather: changeable, honest, occasionally dramatic. "You've left things off your list."

"Name them."

"The pearls."

Her hand went, without permission, to the empty pouch at her belt. Her season's pearls — every one she'd dived for between the spring tides and the betrayal, traded whole to Brack for passage papers and a hiding place in a shell barge. Given up without ceremony, because ceremony would have meant admitting what they cost. "Traded," she said. "Not owned. Traded things don't go on lists."

"They do if you regret them."

"I don't." And she found, testing the word the way she tested a hold in rock underwater — pressing her weight onto it before committing — that it bore her. "I'd trade them again. Brack's brother is free of a debt that was never his. I call that arithmetic."

"Brack called it robbery," Edmund said. "Affectionate robbery. He also said you overpaid."

"He said that while buying us drinks with my pearls' cousins, so his opinion is compromised." She looked south, where the water darkened over the deep beds she would never work again. Her shoulders still carried the ache of the outer bed, the desperate dives that had filled the quota holding the letters — the plan so audacious it worked precisely because the company never searched its own profit. Her body remembered those dives the way a bell remembers being struck: a hum in the bones, fading but not gone. "One more item," she said. "One habit, worn. Measuring everything by what can be carried."

"That's not an item. That's the ledger itself."

"Then I'm closing the ledger." She snapped the compass shut, and the click of it sounded, in the quiet between waves, exactly like a small door closing on a large room. "New system. From now on I measure things by what they're worth when you can't take them with you."

Edmund was quiet a moment. Then he said, "By that measure, what's this boat worth?"

Tamsin considered the packet boat honestly: the stained deck, the sail with its reef patch, the taciturn master who took their money at dawn without asking names, having asked no names even when they'd needed him to. A small vessel bound down the coast with no destination anyone would envy.

"Worth everything," she said. "Obviously. It's taking me somewhere I chose."

* * *

*Edmund*

He began writing in the afternoon, when the wind backed around and the master let the boat run easy and the motion gentled to something a pen could survive.

He had no desk. He balanced a board across his knees, and the board was warped, and the paper was the cheap kind that drank ink and gave back ghosts, and none of it mattered. What mattered was the first line, and he had been composing the first line in his head since the hulk, since the sentry, since the weight he still carried and would carry — some things did not go on lists because they could not be put down — and the first line came out at last like this:

*I was not there when Aldous Vane drowned, because he did not.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he wrote underneath:

*This account will say things the superintendent's inquiry declined to hear, name men the factor's ledgers protected, and record a justice that occurred outside every court in the colony. It bears my handwriting. For two years I feared that handwriting — feared it as a debt, a summons, a thing that could hang its owner. I am done fearing it. A man's hand is the one signature he cannot forge.*

His old habit would have been to write carefully, obliquely, leaving room to deny. He had come to this coast as a courier, and couriers survive by knowing nothing about what they carry. But the courier was finished. On the third checkpoint, in front of assembled officers and the fleet's captain-superintendent, Tamsin had produced the letters and Vane had stepped out of a shell crate alive, and Calloway's paper empire had collapsed in the space of twenty minutes — and then the empire had not fallen at all, merely changed management. No trial. No restitution. Calloway dismissed *quietly*, the word *quietly* doing more work than any word Edmund had ever seen do, packed off with a pension and a silence, while the superintendent buried the scandal deeper than the deep beds and wished everyone a profitable season.

Justice as exposure, not punishment. Vane had warned them. *A living witness changes everything,* Vane had said in Brack's loft, hollow-eyed and lucid, *but only if heard.* And he had been heard, and it had changed almost nothing except the truth was now known, and Edmund had learned — was learning, the lesson not yet finished — that known truth is not a small thing even when it buys no verdicts. Truth loose in the world behaves like seed. It does not look like much. It is patient in ways institutions are not.

So he wrote it all. The customs house and the guard who noted the extra crate. The toll bridge and the sergeant who now possessed his real name — a mistake that had traveled, that had very nearly arrived ahead of them at the fleet gate, saved only by Brack's false departure time and a beating Brack had bought with his face and his boat. He wrote Brack whole: the smuggler who burned his own safe-conduct papers to prove commitment, whose brother's name sat in Vane's letters among the forged death certificates, who had chosen belonging over survival with the casualness of a man choosing tea. He wrote Calloway honestly, which was the hardest part — honestly enough to admit that the man's final offer had been mercy as Calloway understood mercy: *your indenture voided, your mother's debts cleared, give me the letters and the man.* Temptation rendered almost reasonable. Edmund wrote down how close *almost* was, because accounts that flatter their authors are lies with better handwriting.

And he wrote Tamsin. Not sentimentally — he was not a sentimental man, whatever the empty envelope might suggest — but accurately. The inspector who used her authority against itself. The diver who had gone under the bridge pilings with the evidence against her skin and nearly blacked out and been saved by the discipline of a lifetime underwater. The woman who, told to choose between the man and the message, had said *we do not leave him* in a voice that closed discussion the way her compass clicked shut.

The pen scratched. The coast receded. He glanced up once and saw her in the bow, lips moving, counting her possessions again — or perhaps no longer counting them, simply greeting them, the way you greet the few faces at a table after everyone else has gone home — and he understood that she was doing on her side of the boat, with objects, what he was doing on his with words. Taking inventory. Deciding what was true. Choosing, deliberately, out loud, in ink, what to keep.

His mother had taught him his letters. His father had been a clerk who believed paper was sacred and should be obeyed. Edmund had spent thirty years believing the opposite too completely, which is its own obedience. Now he split the difference: paper was not sacred. Paper was a tool, like a knife or a compass, and tools answered to the hand that held them.

He turned a page and began the chapter on the burning of the indenture ledger, and found himself smiling.

* * *

*Tamsin*

She slept at dusk and woke at moonrise to find Edmund still writing, hunched over his board by the light of the master's storm lantern, which he'd been permitted to borrow in exchange for a story. The story, she gathered, had involved the hulk. The master had not asked follow-up questions. Coastal people rarely did; they knew which questions came back to visit.

Tamsin got up quietly and sat beside him.

"Read it to me," she said.

"Now? It's not finished."

"Nothing's finished. Read it anyway."

He hesitated — the old hesitation, the courier's instinct to keep the contents secret even from the person who'd helped carry them — and then it passed over his face like cloud over water, and he read. The customs house. The duel of omissions, which he described so faithfully that she heard her own evasions played back to her and winced at how transparent they'd been. The night market. The letters read aloud in Brack's loft, and the moment her father's name surfaced among the forged certificates — *drowning, accidental, witnessed* — and grief had detonated inside injustice so hard she'd thought the loft would crack along its seams.

He read that part gently. He did not skip it, though she could feel him wanting to. She made herself listen. Her father had been dead nine years, and officially an accident for nine years, and a lie for nine years, and there was a particular loneliness in grieving a lie — you grieve twice, once for the man and once for the truth, and nobody sends condolences for the second funeral. Hearing his name in Edmund's steady voice, written into a true account, she felt the second funeral conclude. Not peace exactly. Settlement. The estate of the truth finally distributed.

"Keep going," she said, when he stopped to look at her.

He read to the end of what existed: the gauntlet at the gate, Calloway unmasked, the superintendent's burial of it all, the ledger burning on the beach — *not theft,* he'd written in her voice, *just arithmetic corrected* — and then stopped, pen lifted, at the edge of today.

"What comes next?" she asked.

"That's the trouble. I don't know where we're going. So I don't know how it ends."

Tamsin leaned back against the rail and looked at the water. Moon lay on it in a long broken road, running from the stern to the horizon, and she thought: *that's a road. You just can't walk it. You float.*

"Then end it wrong on purpose," she said. "Write the ending where we go back. Where we stay in the port and take over the diving crews and reform the company from inside, patiently, respectably, over thirty years."

"Why would I write that?"

"Because it's the ending everyone wants. Belonging. A place in the scheme. Write it, and then write underneath: *they did not choose this ending.* Let the reader sit with both. People should know what they're refusing before they're sure they refused it."

He studied her. "Is that what you did? Refuse with the alternative in view?"

"Constantly." She smiled. "It's the only honest way. Refusing blind is just contrariness."

He bent to the page. She watched the moon-road slide past the stern and listened to the pen, and thought about her mother, staying behind in the port among the diving community, freed alongside her daughter and choosing the only home she'd ever admitted to having. Their reconciliation was unfinished — forgiveness partial, hard, the wound open but clean now, dressed rather than hidden — and Tamsin had decided that unfinished was acceptable. Some reconciliations were like tides. You didn't finish them. You moved with them, repeatedly, for years, and moving with them *was* the reconciliation.

The blessing had come at the dock, such as it was. Her mother did not embrace easily. She had taken Tamsin's wrist — the diver's grip, firm, below the elbow where the pulse lives — and pressed it once, hard, and said: *"The water taught you to breathe where there is no air. Go breathe where there's none of anything else."* Then she'd walked back toward the huts without turning, because her mother had spent a lifetime silent and was not going to ruin her record with a speech.

Tamsin had stood on the dock laughing and crying in equal measure, and Edmund, wisely, had said nothing at all.

"Done," Edmund said. He showed her the page: the false ending, respectable and terrible, and beneath it the refusal, in ink, signed. "Now it doesn't know how it ends either."

"Good," Tamsin said. "Neither do we. It's the first honest book on this coast."

* * *

*Edmund*

Dawn came up gold-gray and soft, and the coast was gone.

Edmund noticed its absence the way you notice a tooth: by the space where it had been. Two years on this shoreline — two years of checkpoints and ledgers and letters in a drowned man's hand — and its departure took one night and a change of wind. He stood at the stern with the empty envelope in his breast pocket and watched the place where land should be, and felt neither grief nor triumph, but the rarer thing: readiness.

Vane was somewhere ahead of them, east, on a ship full of copies. Edmund had said goodbye to him properly this time, on the dock, man to man, no crates involved. They had shaken hands and neither had let go for slightly too long, and Vane had said, *You carried me for two years, lad. Put me down. Walk on.* Brothers who no longer needed each other daily. The phrase fit.

Tamsin joined him with two cups of the master's terrible coffee and gave him one without asking, which was, he had learned, the deepest form of intimacy she currently offered, and possibly ever would. They drank in silence. The boat ran south-southwest, following the coast toward the packet's port, where they would disembark with almost nothing — by design — and decide, over breakfast, where the rest of a life might be spent.

"I've been thinking about your new measuring system," Edmund said. "Worth measured by what you can't take with you. It has a flaw."

"Name it."

"Under it, everything I can't take with you is worth everything. The system collapses. It makes all values equal at infinity."

Tamsin considered this seriously, eyes on the water. "Yes," she said finally. "That's the flaw. That's the point of it." She drained her coffee and grimaced with genuine appreciation — she liked terrible coffee; it proved someone had made it cheaply and nearby, for people present, rather than expensively and far away, for people abstract. "The company measured everything so it could weigh nothing. I measured everything so I could lose nothing. Same error, opposite direction. This way—" she tapped her temple— "you stop protecting yourself from the arithmetic and start living inside it."

Edmund took out his board and wrote that down, because it was true, and true things belonged in the account regardless of whose mouth they came out of — *especially*, he corrected himself, whose.

The morning warmed. The master whistled at the sail. Somewhere astern, in a port now beyond the curve of the water, a quarterly fleet was loading shell and silk and one set of copies that would not stay quiet, and a woman was teaching girls to dive without telling them whose debt they were filling, and a smuggler with new scars was drinking on credit he no longer feared, and a ledger's ashes were sifting into sand that had never cared about arithmetic in the first place.

Edmund opened the account to a fresh page and wrote the heading he had been saving:

*What We Carried Out.*

Below it, the list. One knife. One compass, complete. One empty envelope. One true account, unfinished. One another.

He read it over, found it short, and found the shortness sufficient.

"Tamsin," he said. "What do we need?"

She didn't turn from the bow. The wind had freshened and her hair streamed back and she stood the way she stood everywhere — planted, economical, a woman who had never once in her life required more footing than the world offered.

"Less than this," she said. "Hold course."

And the packet boat, small and stained and worth everything, ran on south down the bright and empty water, carrying exactly what could be carried, which was all of it, which was enough.
