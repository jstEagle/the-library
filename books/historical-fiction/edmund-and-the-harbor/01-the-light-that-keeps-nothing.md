## 1. The Light That Keeps Nothing

The storm came off the water the way it always did in winter, sideways and full of gravel, and Sable Underhill climbed the tower stairs with a lantern in one hand and his grief in the other, which was heavier but easier to carry because he had been carrying it for six years and knew its weight the way a stevedore knows a crate.

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and burnt oil. He trimmed the wick, wiped the soot from the glass with the rag he kept folded in his coat pocket—Marta's old apron linen, cut down years ago before the softness wore out of it—and turned the crank that set the great lens turning. The light swept out across the harbor, caught the whitecaps, let them go, caught them again. Beyond the breakwater the river ran black and swollen toward the sea, carrying slabs of early ice south like a fleet of broken plates.

"Still there," he said to the water, because he said it every night, and because saying it meant nothing could surprise him. *Still there.* The harbor was still there. The town was still there, huddled under the cliff with its chimneys smoking. And he was still here, keeper of a light that kept nothing in particular, guiding no ships worth guiding since the quarries closed, burning oil the ministry still sent him out of habit or accounting error.

History, Sable had decided long ago, belonged to cities. It happened where the parliaments were, where the bridges were burned and the treaties signed, and afterward it sent its leftovers down the coast—tariffs, press gangs, the occasional refugee—to be absorbed by places like this harbor town the way the sand absorbed the tide: thoroughly, without comment. A man who lived at the edge of things could watch all of it pass by and remain unrecorded. That was not cowardice. He had settled that argument with himself years ago, in the cemetery, standing over two stones. That was arithmetic. A widower with an empty workshop attracted no soldiers, no tax men, no questions. A man made invisible could not be conscripted into anyone's story.

Below the tower, connected by a covered walk he'd built himself the second winter, stood the cottage, and behind the cottage, the workshop. And in the workshop, under sailcloth, half-finished, lay the boat.

He went down to look at it after he'd logged the weather—*wind NNW, heavy, glass falling*—the way he went down every night, though he had touched nothing on her hull in four months. She was a river skiff, shallow-drafted, built for the kind of water that ate keels: twenty-two feet of clinker planking, oak ribs steamed and bent in this very room when Marta was still alive to complain about the smell. The garboard strakes were fastened. The frames stood open like a rib cage, waiting. On the bench beside her lay the tools laid out in their old order—adze, auger, roving iron, a box of copper nails sorted by size into compartments Marta had once used for seeds—and above the bench, hanging from a peg, the half-model of the hull, carved from a single piece of cedar, its lines fair and true and utterly useless to anyone.

He had begun her the spring after the fever took Marta and the child both, in the first mad weeks when a man will do anything with his hands rather than fold them. He had worked on her through that summer and the next, and then one October evening he had stepped back, looked at what he was building—a boat big enough for two, rigged for a river passage, provisioned in his imagination for a voyage he would never take—and understood that grief had been using him as a shipwright. He had thrown the sailcloth over her and hung the lamp, and that had been that. Sixteen months of work, covered like furniture in an abandoned house.

The wind rose through the evening. He ate bread and cold fish and read eight pages of a book about canal engineering, and at the turn of the middle watch he climbed the tower again, checked the bearing of the light against the chart, and saw, far downriver where the harbor mouth met the sea, the brief stutter of a signal lamp answering from somewhere it should not have been. Smugglers' water, that stretch. Old business. Not his business. He logged it—*light observed, seaward, 0240, irregular*—and did not report it, because reporting it would make him part of something, and he had spent six careful years being part of nothing.

That was the creed. *Still there. Still nobody.*

The knock came near dawn, when the storm was at its worst and even the wind seemed to have lost its nerve.

Three blows, small ones, fisted low on the door—the knock of someone who barely reached the handle. Then a fourth, weaker.

Sable stood in the cottage kitchen with the poker in his hand and did not move for the space of ten heartbeats. No one knocked here. The harbormaster sent letters; the supply boat came quarterly; the minister's curate had stopped climbing the cliff path after the second winter. In six years, exactly three people had knocked on this door, and he remembered each of them the way he remembered the names on the chart.

He opened it on a wall of snow and a child.

A boy, seven perhaps, eight—small for the cold, or the cold had made him small. Snow crusted his shoulders and the hood of a coat too fine for it, city wool, ruined now. His lips were gray. His hands were pulled up inside his sleeves, and his eyes—wide, dark, blown wide with terror—looked past Sable into the cottage as if measuring it for escape routes. Ice had formed in the lashes of one eye. When Sable reached for him the boy flinched back a step into the drift, and Sable stopped, and crouched, and held out one open hand the way you'd gentle a spooked horse, and waited, wind moaning over the roof, until the boy came forward on his own and let himself be gathered in.

The boy weighed nothing. He was frozen through, a bundle of sticks and wet wool, and he was clutching something against his chest with both arms beneath the coat, and he would not let go of it even when Sable pried the coat open to get him warm.

It was a note, folded twice, damp at the corners.

Sable carried the boy to the hearth, wrapped him in the good blanket, the one from the chest that still smelled faintly of cedar, set him in the chair closest to the fire, and only then, with the boy watching him with those enormous eyes, unfolded the paper.

The handwriting stopped his breath.

He knew it. He would have known it upside down, in the dark, dissolved by rain. The capitals tall and leaning forward like runners; the *f*s crossed twice, impatiently; the little hooked tails she put on everything, as if even the letters were eager to be elsewhere. Ilse Varn's hand. Ilse, who wrote manifests in three languages and forged none of them because she never needed to. Ilse, whose last letter to him was folded into the false bottom of the tool chest downstairs, because he could not burn it and could not keep it anywhere a man might stumble on it honestly.

The note said:

*Forget the face. Keep him warm. Trust the river man.*

Nothing else. No name. The paper trembled in Sable's fingers, and he realized the trembling was his own.

He looked at the boy. Up close, thawed a little, color coming back into his face like ink into blotting paper, the resemblance was a hand around Sable's throat. The same dark eyes. The same stubborn line of the jaw that he had once, in another life, kissed and laughed at and memorized without meaning to. The boy stared back at him, firelight moving in his pupils, and asked in a cracked voice, in the accent of the capital, far from home:

"Are you the river man?"

Downstairs, under sailcloth, the boat waited in the dark of the workshop, sixteen months unfinished. Outside, the storm drove the ice downriver faster, hour by hour, toward the breaking. And in Sable's chest, behind six sealed winters, something cracked—not loudly, the way ice cracks on the river, but quietly, the way a seam gives in a hull, felt in the soles of the feet before it is heard at all.

"Yes," Sable heard himself say. "I'm the river man."
