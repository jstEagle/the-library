## 2. Instructions for Forgetting

The boy slept like driftwood, half-bobbing on the surface of a fever, and Sable sat beside the cot through the small hours with his hand hovering above the child's brow, not quite touching, as if touch were a thing that could be revoked. The fire in the stove had been fed three times. The kettle had been boiled twice and forgotten both times. Outside, the wind worried at the tower's gallery rail, and the great lens above them turned its slow, indifferent wheel of light across the water, across the ice, across the face of everything Sable had spent eleven years learning not to see.

The note lay on the table where he had dropped it. *Forget the face.* He had read it forty times by lamplight, though it was only four words, though the handwriting had not changed since the last letter he'd burned, since the letter before that. Ilse's hand: quick, slanted, the letters leaning eastward like trees bent by a prevailing wind. She had written him exactly nine letters in their years together, and he could have recited all nine from memory, and he had tried for a decade to forget them, and he had failed at forgetting the way other men fail at sobriety—completely, and every day.

The boy's name, when he finally gave it up between cracked lips, was Tomas.

* * *

Eleven years earlier, on a night much like this one but colder, Ilse Vane had come up over the harbor wall with river water frozen in her hair and a hold full of contraband salt, and she had knocked on the lighthouse door the way you knock on the door of someone you intend to argue with. He had known her two winters by then, known her only as a sail on the dark water, a running light that answered his own in some private code. That night she stood dripping on his stones and said, "You watch us come through every month and say nothing. Either you're blind or you're mine, and I've decided I don't care which."

He should have reported her. It was his duty; the words were written into the very commission he'd signed, the oath with its wax seal, the office of keeper rendered to the Crown and Coast. Instead he had made her tea. Instead he had listened while she laid out her philosophy of rivers—that a river is not a border but a bloodstream, that what flows along it belongs to no government, that salt smuggled from the coast to inland kitchens was not theft but circulation, the water finding its level. She had argued this with such ferocity, sitting in his one chair with her boots steaming, that he had found himself laughing for the first time since Marta died, and the laughter had frightened him worse than any crime.

For six years she came and went with the tides. He learned her face in pieces: the scar through her left eyebrow from a block-and-tackle gone wild, the way she squinted before speaking an unpleasant truth, the particular stillness of her hands when she was lying about being afraid. She never stayed. That was the arrangement and then it was the wound. Duty demanded the light; the light demanded the shore; and Ilse would not be kept anywhere, least of all by a man who kept a lamp burning for strangers. "You're married to that tower," she told him once, in the last winter, and there was truth enough in it that he did not defend himself.

Then came the amnesty. The new harbor master, a man named Corvel with a magistrate's eyes, offered pardon to any smuggler who surrendered and named names. Ilse refused. Sable begged her—begged, a word he had never used before or since—and she looked at him with that squint, that stillness in her hands, and said, "If I give them the others, I'm giving them the river." She sailed north instead, into the deep-water country where warrants went to die, and took with her whatever version of himself he had been becoming. He sealed the rest. He sealed it the way a man seals a hull below the waterline, and he told himself the boat would float.

* * *

Tomas woke at gray dawn asking for his mother, and Sable, who had rehearsed a dozen careful answers, said only, "She'll send word," and hated the mercy of it.

The boy was seven, maybe eight, small for either. He had Ilse's chin and none of her fearlessness; he flinched at the kettle's whistle, at the gulls, at the sound of Sable's boots on the floorboards. But he watched everything. He watched the lens turn. He watched Sable trim the wick and clean the soot from the storm panes, and he asked, on the second morning, "Is that why she sent me here? Because you keep the light?"

Sable's hands stopped on the brass fitting. "What else did she tell you?"

"That you knew her name before she said it. That you'd know her face even if—" The boy stopped, hearing something in his own sentence he didn't understand, and looked at the floor.

*Forget the face.* Not forget the boy. Forget the face—the face that would be hunted, the face that had been seen somewhere it shouldn't be, by someone who shouldn't have seen it. Sable had turned the phrase over until its edges showed. Ilse was alive. Ilse was hiding. And someone, somewhere along the coast, was looking for a woman's face, and had nearly found it attached to her son.

He fed the boy smoked herring and barley bread. He taught him which floorboard creaked and which did not. These were not lessons he meant to teach; they came out of him the way the light came out of the lamp, automatically, from some older machinery of protection he had believed long dismantled.

* * *

On the fourth day the soldiers came to the harbor village, two of them in Coast Authority grey, walking the ice-stiff quay with papers in a leather wallet and the unhurried manner of men being paid by the question. Sable saw them from the ridge path, where he had gone ostensibly for firewood. They stood at Wrenna's ferry house. They stood at the fish shed. They spoke to everyone, and everyone, afterward, looked up at the lighthouse the way people look at a house where someone has just died.

Wrenna brought the news up herself, breathless, wrapped against the cold, her eyes doing arithmetic behind her wrinkles. "Greycoats," she said. "Asking after a woman come south ahead of the storms. Asking after a child traveling alone." She paused on the threshold, not entering, which was itself an answer. "They'll come up here, Sable. A lone man keeping a lonely light—they always ask the solitary ones first."

"Let them ask. I keep a light. I don't keep company."

"Mm," said Wrenna, and her gaze went past his shoulder, to where a small pair of mittens dried on the stove rail, and she said nothing about them, and her saying nothing was louder than the soldiers' questions. Then she was gone down the path, and Sable stood in his own doorway feeling the shape of his invisibility crack like river ice—one flaw, hairline, singing.

That night he took out the letters.

Nine letters, once. Eight, after the amnesty, when Corvel's men had searched the tower and he had burned the most damning one in the lamp itself, feeding it to the flame that was supposed to belong to everyone and no one. The remaining eight he had hidden in the hollow base of the oil reservoir, where no searcher would put a hand, because the oil smell clung to everything and men distrust what clings. He spread them on the table now, in the yellow circle of the lamp, and Tomas slept behind the curtain ten feet away, and outside the ice on the river boomed like a struck drum, once, twice—a sound like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

He burned the first four. They were old ones, early ones, full of routes and rendezvous, the kind of paper that could hang a person twice over. The flames took them cleanly. Ilse's slanted letters leaned east even as they blackened, leaning toward their own destruction with what looked, horribly, like willingness.

The fifth letter he held over the flame and could not let go.

It was not the longest. It was barely a page. *They have a name for me now,* it said, in part. *But you have never once used it, and so I am still, somewhere, the woman you didn't report. Keep the light. If I ever need the light, I will send you what I cannot carry.*

*What I cannot carry.*

Sable lowered the letter. His hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, something had come loose the way the ice comes loose, all at once, after months of pretending to be solid. She had known. Six years ago, in a letter he'd read a hundred times and understood not at all, she had told him what she would one day leave at his door. She had written him instructions for forgetting, and buried inside them, instructions for remembering, and he had burned the wrong things and kept the wrong things and spent eleven years mistaking silence for safety.

He folded the fifth letter along its old creases and put it inside his shirt, against his chest, where it lay like a coal that would not go out.

The other three he returned to the reservoir's hollow. Then he went to the window and looked down the black shoulder of the river to where the harbor lights burned small and obedient, and beyond them, invisible, the road the soldiers would take tomorrow, because Wrenna's silence was a kindness, not a wall, and walls of kindness fall fastest.

Behind the curtain, Tomas murmured in his sleep—a word, maybe a name—and Sable stood in the turning wheel of his own lamp and understood, with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own handwriting on a confession, that the boat in the workshop would have to be finished by week's end.

The ice boomed again, closer now, and did not stop.
