## 3. What the Ice Remembers

The ice spoke first in the dark hours, before the wind rose. Sable lay awake in the loft above the workshop and listened to the river groan like something old turning over in its sleep—a deep, tectonic complaint that traveled up through the pilings, through the stone foundations, into the bones of the bed itself. Eleven days since the boy had come to his door. Nine since the first soldiers had walked the harbor road. The ice had been making noise for three of those nights, and each night it spoke a little louder, a little more urgently, as if the river were counting down to something.

He dressed by feel and went down without lighting a lamp, because he knew every board and tool by the shape of his own hands, and because lamplight drawn to a window was a habit he could no longer afford. Tomas slept on the cot beneath the workbench, curled small around the carved gull Sable had given him the second night, when the boy's sobbing had threatened to crack them both open. In sleep his face went slack and young, and Sable could see the map of Ilse in it—the same sharp hinge of jaw, the same stubborn line of brow. He stood a moment too long looking. Then he took up the lantern anyway, shielding its glow with his coat, and knelt at the boat.

She was nearly done. That was the miracle he did not let himself name aloud. Twenty years of half-measures and she stood whole along her ribs now, planked and caulked, her gunwales fair and true, needing only thole pins, a mast step, paint—small things that were not small things, because small things failed in big water. His hands moved over her hull in the shielded light the way a man touches a face he is memorizing.

*Forget the face.* The note still lived folded in his coat pocket, riding against his heart like a splinter working toward it. He could not burn it. He had burned the other letter—Ilse's last, three lines of farewell written in a hand that slanted hard left, written the week she married her duty and her trade and left him standing on this same shore—and he had watched the ash of it rise off the iron plate and thought: *there, that's done, that's clean.* But the note with the boy's life folded inside it would not take the flame. Some things refused the fire. He understood, lately, that they were the only things worth keeping.

Tomas stirred. "Is it the ice again?" The boy's voice came thin and level, the way voices went when children learned that fear was better carried quietly.

"It's the ice."

"Will it break tonight?"

"Not yet." Sable banked the lantern. "The river talks before it moves. It's telling us what it remembers."

"What does it remember?"

Sable considered the question longer than a tired man should have. "Everything," he said finally. "Every spring it breaks and every winter it heals, and it keeps the scar of both. Come morning I'll show you."

* * *

Morning showed him the soldier instead.

Corporal Vane came down the harbor road alone this time, which was worse than coming with company; it meant he was comfortable now, meant the questions had graduated from procedure to habit. He wore his grey greatcoat open and his smile the same way, and he stopped at the foot of Sable's steps while Sable stood in the doorway deliberately framed against the dark interior, so that nothing behind him could be read.

"Keeper Underhill." Vane touched his cap with two fingers, an insult of casualness. "Fuel stores holding out?"

"Barely."

"And the boy? Still about?" Vane's eyes drifted past Sable's shoulder with the unhurried insolence of a man who already believed what he hoped to find. "Harbormaster says you've taken in a nephew. Funny—I didn't have you down as a family man."

"He's my late wife's kin. Passing through to her people upcountry."

"Upcountry." Vane turned the word over like a coin he suspected. Behind him, at the neighboring cottage, Marta Kellner's curtain shifted—a small movement, but Sable caught it, and caught the shape of her silhouette lingering after. Marta who had brought bread the first week. Marta whose son owed his conscription exemption to the very garrison now combing the coast. "Well. Orders are orders. We're to log all persons under sixteen moving through the district. If the lad's traveling, he'll need papers."

"He has them."

"Of course he does." Vane smiled. "Everyone does, these days. Give my regards to the boy, Keeper. Boys grow fast in winter. Practically different children by spring."

He walked on toward the fish sheds, and Sable closed the door and stood with his hand flat against the wood, listening to his own pulse. Through the gap between shutters he watched Marta Kellner step out to her fence, wrap her shawl tight, and look—not at him, but at the harbor road, in the direction a message travels.

That afternoon she sent no bread. She sent her youngest instead, a girl of seven, who stood at the gate and recited, woodenly, that her mother said the keeper should mind his lamp oil, as there'd be inspections, and inspections counted everything in a house. Everything. The girl ran off before Sable could answer, and he understood the warning for what it was: Marta had been asked questions, or expected to be, and whatever loyalty forty years of neighborliness held was already bending under the weight of what it cost her.

He told Tomas none of it. Instead he worked.

* * *

They finished the thole pins by lamplight that evening, and the mast step, and while the glue set Sable sat the boy between his knees on the workshop floor and drew the river in spilled sawdust with a blunt finger.

"The channel runs here, close under the far bluff where the current cuts deepest. Never trust the near shore—snags, drowned fencing, an old scow sunk deliberate to catch fools. When the ice goes, it will go all at once, like a door kicked open, and the water behind it will be fast and filthy and cold enough to stop your heart in a minute. You don't fight the current. You read it. See where the surface smooths—that's the deep vein, that's your road. Where it wrinkles white, that's rock below. You keep to the smooth."

Tomas traced the channel with his own finger, solemn as a scholar. "And where the smooth ends?"

"There's a pool above the mill weir, slack water, safe to land. You'll see a split oak on the north bank, struck by lightning before you were born. You put in there and you wait, and you don't wait long, because—" *because a cart comes at dawn, because Ilse arranged it all in a letter I cannot burn,* "—because waiting on a riverbank in February kills as sure as swimming does."

The boy nodded. Then he leaned back against Sable's chest, boneless with trust in a way that undid him, and said, "You know the river like Mama knew the sea."

Sable's hand stopped on the boy's hair.

"She talked about you." Tomas said it to the sawdust river, matter-of-fact, terrible. "Before she went away. She said there was a man who kept a light and pretended it wasn't for anybody. She laughed when she said it. Not mean. Like it was her favorite story."

The lamp hissed. The ice groaned downriver, long and low, and Sable heard in it twenty years of his own voice saying *I keep the light, that's all, I keep the light*—and heard, beneath it, the truth he had sealed up as carefully as any hull: that invisibility was never safety. It was just drowning done slowly, done politely, done in installments. He had buried himself alive and called it peace, and a child was sitting in the grave with him, warm against his chest, calling it a favorite story.

"Tomas." His voice came out rough. "Listen to me. Whatever happens—if soldiers come, if we're separated—you remember the channel. The smooth water. The split oak. Can you hold it?"

"I can hold it."

"That's my boy." The words escaped before he could weigh them, and he found he did not want them back.

It was full dark when the knock came—not Vane's knock, not a knock at all but fists, many-fisted, and torchlight swinging wild across the shutter gaps, and the sound of the harbor road filling with boots. Downriver, with a shriek like a world ending, the ice began to break.

Sable put out the lamp.
