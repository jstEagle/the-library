## 4. Edmund and the Harbor

The ice broke at dusk, with a sound like the world's spine giving way.

Sable stood in the doorway of the lighthouse and listened to the river tear itself apart below the point—great slabs shouldering free, grinding, the whole frozen length of the water waking after four months of silence. He had waited for this sound the way other men waited for bells. Behind him, in the lamplit round of the keeper's room, Tomas sat on the floor with his back against the boat-model, lacing boots too big for him, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left to say that the river had not already said.

The boat was finished. It had been finished since noon: seams payed, thole-pins fitted, a scrap of canvas rigged on a short mast, the hull named in Sable's careful letters along the stern—*Edmund*, because Ilse had once told him, laughing into her collar in the cold loft above the chandlery, that if she ever had a son she would give him a name no soldier could spell. She had kept half the promise. The boy was Tomas. The name would have to do its work downstream.

"They'll come tonight," Sable said. Not a question. He had watched the lanterns move along the far shore since morning, unhurried as gulls, closing like fingers.

Tomas looked up. He had stopped looking frightened days ago; now he only looked ready, which frightened Sable more. "You said the ice would hold till it didn't."

"That's right."

"Then we go when it doesn't."

*We.* Sable let the word sit in his chest a moment, warm and terrible, before he took it out again. "You," he said. "I stay. A light has to be lit, and I'm the one they expect to light it." He crouched, took the boy by the shoulders, felt the thin bones beneath the wool coat—the coat Ilse's sister must have dressed him in, the last night, before the long walk through the snow to a door Sable had pretended for twenty years he did not keep. "When you clear the point, you hug the north bank where the current runs slack. Past the ferry ruin there's an eddy behind the willow bar—rest there if you must, but not past dawn. By dawn you want the oxbow, and from the oxbow the river does the work. Do you hear me? Let the river do the work."

"And you?"

"I've lived here thirty years," Sable said. "The river knows me."

It was not entirely a lie. It was enough of one.

They ate what was left without ceremony—bread, salt fish, the last of the blackberry preserves Tomas had refused all week and accepted tonight, as if sweetness were a provision for a journey rather than a pleasure. Then Sable banked the stove, checked the oil in the great lamp, and carried down the stairs the thing he had built in secret alongside the boat: three tins of lamp oil, a coil of pitch-soaked rope, and the old signal flag from the chest at the foot of the tower, dry as a wick after thirty winters in the salt air.

"You're going to burn it," Tomas said from the doorway. Not the boat. The tower.

"A light is a promise," Sable said. "This one's been keeping nothing for a long time. Tonight it can keep something true." He looked at the boy a long moment—at the face that was Ilse's face rearranged by some patient hand into someone new, the same stubborn jaw, the same eyes that gave away everything. "Forget the face," the note had said, and he had failed at it from the first hour, gloriously, irreversibly. Some things were not meant to be forgotten. They were meant to be spent.

He knelt then, in the shingle at the foot of the tower, and did the thing he had not done since the funeral: he embraced the boy. Tomas held on with the desperate strength of the small, and Sable pressed his mouth to the cold crown of his head and said, into the hair, the words he had never said to Ilse when it mattered: *I loved your mother. I loved her the way the sea loves the shore—not gently, but always.* Whether Tomas heard or understood, he could not say. It did not matter. Some sentences are spoken for the speaker's soul.

Then he put the boy in the boat.

* * *

The river took the *Edmund* the way a hand closes over a coin—swiftly, surely, without malice. Sable stood on the landing stage and watched the small sail dwindle against the last grey light, riding the broken ice south toward the harbor mouth and whatever lay beyond it, and he did not call out, and the boy did not look back, and both of those mercies cost him more than the tower would.

Then the lanterns came up the path.

Six of them, maybe seven, moving through the pines in the disciplined cadence of men who had done this many times and expected nothing interesting. Sable climbed the tower stairs two at a time, joints protesting, heart strangely quiet, and reached the lamp room as the first boots hit the gravel below. He lit the great lamp. He turned the mechanism one last time, and the beam swung out across the black, heaving water, across the floes, steady as it had been every night of his stewardship—and then, deliberately, slowly, he poured the tins of oil over the floorboards, over the stairwell, down the shaft where the weights hung, and touched the pitch-rope to the flame.

Fire went through the lighthouse like memory through a quiet man.

By the time soldiers reached the base, the tower was roaring from the inside, a column of gold and orange visible, Sable knew, from every farm and every patrol post on both banks—a beacon screaming *here, here, here* while somewhere downriver a boy hugged the slack northern current past the ferry ruin. Sable walked out the side door into the wind, unnoticed or unremarked, an old man among burning miracles, and made his way down to the water's edge, and sat on the cold stone, and watched.

The heat reached him even at fifty yards. Sparks went up like a reversed snowfall. The lamp glass burst in a bright coughing shower, and the great lens—one hundred years of ground glass, the pride of some dead engineer—caught the fire and threw it outward in wheeling spokes of light across the bay, so that for one impossible minute the harbor was lit as it had never been lit, every wave picked out in copper, every floe a shard of amber, and Sable thought: *Ilse, look. Look what we finally did.*

He was crying. He noted it distantly, the way he noted weather. He was not sure for whom—for Ilse, gone eleven years to a smuggler's grave in a country whose name he'd never learned; for Tomas, running the dark river alone with a borrowed name; for the man he might have been if he had ever once chosen loudly instead of quietly. Perhaps for all of them. Grief, he found, did not divide well at the end. It pooled.

Boots crunched on the shingle behind him. He did not turn.

"The keeper," a voice said—not a question, not quite an accusation. An officer came around into his sightline, young under the cap brim, firelight swimming in his eyes. "Where's the woman's boy? The one they brought to this coast."

Sable looked at him. Thirty years of invisibility sat in his chest like ballast, and he felt it shifting, lifting, going out of him the way the light was going out of the tower. He thought about lying. He had been so good at lying, so practiced, so safe.

"He went with the river," Sable Underhill said, and his voice did not shake, and it was the truest sentence he had spoken in thirty years. "And you'll find, lieutenant, that the river keeps better secrets than I ever did."

Behind them the tower groaned, leaned, and folded inward in a blizzard of sparks, and the harbor went dark for the first time in a century—and Sable, watching, felt history close over him like water, not drowning him but bearing him up, at last, at last, part of everything.

The officer studied him a long moment. Then, quietly, almost kindly: "You'll come with us anyway."

Sable rose, joints aching, and looked once more at the black river running south. Somewhere past the willow bar, past the oxbow, a small boat was doing its work.

"Yes," he said. "I imagine I will."
