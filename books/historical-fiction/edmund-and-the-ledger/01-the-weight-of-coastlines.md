## 1. The Weight of Coastlines

The cold in the workshop had a texture to it, Petra Vane thought — not the clean cold of open air but the damp, mineral chill of stone that had stopped pretending to be warm years ago. It seeped up through the floorboards and into the bones of her hands as she worked, and she had learned not to fight it. Master Orenveld used to say that a mapmaker's hands should be cold, that warmth made ink run eager and lines go soft. *A coastline drawn by warm hands will lie to you,* he'd told her once, standing at this same table with his sleeves rolled back and his knuckles white with age. *Cold hands tell the truth.*

He was eleven weeks dead now, and the workshop still kept his habits. His coat still hung on its peg by the door, shoulders slumped in the shape of a man who would never fill it again. His magnifying loupe sat in its velvet cradle like an eye that refused to close. And his atlas — the atlas that had killed him, though no one in Vhalen would say so aloud — lay open on the great table under her lamp, waiting for the last coastlines to be inked.

Petra dipped her pen and began.

The plate before her showed the northern shore of the Serevan Gulf, every inlet and sandbar rendered in Orenveld's meticulous hand except for the final stretch, left blank because he had died before the survey boats could make their last pass. She had walked that shore herself in the gray week after his funeral, pacing off distances with a knotted cord while the imperial soldiers watched from the seawall and pretended not to notice her. They hadn't cared about a grieving woman counting paces. That was the small mercy of being unimportant: you could carry anything past a soldier's eye if your face said *nothing worth seeing*.

She worked the line slowly. The gulf's coast curled like a question mark, and where it met the estuary she laid in the mudflats with stippled dots, each one placed by hand, each one a place where a man could drown or a boat could cross, depending on the tide and the hour. Orenveld had understood that maps were never neutral things. A map was a weapon that happened to look like art. He had spent forty years making sure his weapons were true.

And they had killed him for it. Not with a blade — nothing so honest. They had killed him with questions, with summons, with the slow bureaucratic strangulation of a man who refused to surrender his life's work to the Office of Imperial Survey. Three months of interrogations, then a fall down the bureau stairs that everyone called an accident because calling it anything else required courage no one in Vhalen had left.

Petra set down her pen and flexed her fingers. Outside, the light through the salt-fogged window had gone from pewter to iron; the siege had a way of eating daylight whole. Somewhere beyond the harbor chain, the enemy fleet sat at anchor like patient wolves, and somewhere behind her, in the chancellery district, clerks were sharpening quills for the census.

She had heard them talking about it in the bread queue. A full accounting, they said. Every household logged, every trade recorded, every able body assessed for conscription, every holding assessed for seizure. The empire was hungry, and hunger begins with a list.

Her eyes went to the shelves along the north wall, where the atlas lived when she wasn't working on it. Forty-one plates, each one mounted on linen-backed paper, each one heavy as sin. She had already weighed them — she was ashamed of how many times she had weighed them, lifting each sheet in her palms like a mother judging an infant, calculating. The full atlas was too much. Nine pounds of paper and truth, and whatever she carried out of this city would have to be carried through streets full of census takers and checkpoints and men with clipboards and cold eyes.

So she had begun dividing it. The essential plates — the coasts, the passes, the deep-water channels — went into a leather satchel she had hidden beneath the floorboard under Orenveld's chair. The rest, the duplicates, the drafts, the beautiful failed attempts, stayed on the shelves. If orders came, the shelves could burn. The satchel could not.

It felt like betrayal, choosing. She imagined explaining it to him, and heard his voice in reply, dry as old vellum: *A map that exists only in one place is a corpse, girl. Truth needs to be copied or it dies.* He would not have mourned the drafts. He would have mourned only the losing.

She returned to the gulf coast and worked until her lamp guttered, then trimmed the wick and worked on. The stippling of the mudflats became a rhythm, pen-tap-pen-tap, and in the rhythm she found the strange peace that craft gives to grief — the sense that as long as her hands were moving, he was not entirely gone, that the work continued him the way a river continues the mountain it drains.

* * *

The census taker came on a Tuesday, though Petra did not know the day until later, days having lost their names in the siege and regained them only when something happened to mark them.

She heard him before she saw him: the knock next door, at the Ferrow household, polite as a debt collector. Then voices — Mrs. Ferrow's high and fraying, the visitor's low and unhurried, the voice of a man reading from a form. Through the wall Petra caught fragments. *Occupants. Ages. Trades. Any sons between fourteen and fifty.*

Then a silence worse than any shouting.

When she dared to the window, she saw him in the street below: a narrow man in a gray bureau coat, ledger under his arm, writing as he walked. Behind him, Tam Ferrow — sixteen years old, always whittling something, always grinning — came down the steps between two soldiers, his hands empty, his grin gone. He did not struggle. That was the terrible part. He looked back once at his mother's window, lifted his hand in a small wave meant to comfort her, and let himself be led away toward the conscription house like a boy going to school.

Mrs. Ferrow's cry came muffled through the walls, and Petra stood in her cold workshop with ink drying on her fingers and did not go to her, because what was there to say? *I'm sorry* was a coin so debased in Vhalen that it bought nothing. Instead she memorized it — the wave, the empty hands, the sound of a mother breaking — and folded the memory away with all the others, because grief, too, was a thing you carried, and hers was getting heavy.

The census taker passed her own door without stopping.

She watched him do it. He reached the workshop's threshold, glanced at the brass plate — *ORENVELD & VANE, CARTOGRAPHERS* — and paused. Just a moment. Long enough for her heart to climb into her throat and sit there. Long enough for her to see his face in profile: young, tired, with the particular pallor of men who worked indoors for institutions that consumed them.

Then he moved on, and Petra exhaled, and hated herself for exhaling, because Tam Ferrow had not been permitted to move on.

But the pause stayed with her. He had read the name. Everyone in the bureau knew the name Orenveld; the old man's refusal had been a minor scandal, whispered about in chancellery corridors. A census taker who recognized that name might come back. Might report it. Might, with one entry in a ledger, convert nine pounds of truth into confiscated paper.

That night Petra did not sleep. She took the satchel from under the floorboard and repacked it, plate by plate, weighing each against the others as if weight were the only measure that mattered. Coastlines first. Then the channel charts. Then the passes through the Karst, which the empire wanted sealed and the free cities needed open. She wrapped them in oilcloth and bound them tight, and when she finally blew out the lamp, the dark of the workshop felt less like absence than like ambush.

In the morning, she would begin inking the final plate — the master chart itself, the one Orenveld had guarded hardest, the one whose existence the bureau had never confirmed.

She did not know that the census taker had written her street in his ledger that evening, in his small careful hand, with a note beside it that would change everything:

*Orenveld workshop — holdings unverified. Return required.*
