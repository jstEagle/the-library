## 2. One Question Too Many

The knock came at mid-morning, three raps, measured and unhurried — the knock of a man who had never once been made to wait and did not intend to begin today.

Petra set down her pen with a steadiness she did not feel. The final plate lay half-inked beneath her hands: Orenveld's master chart, the coastline he had spent forty years measuring and forty more refusing to surrender. She covered it with a drying cloth as though hiding a wound, then crossed the workshop and opened the door.

He was younger than she expected. Census takers in Vhalen were usually old men grown gray over their ledgers, men who had learned to make bureaucracy a kind of mercy, taking their time, forgetting small things. This one was perhaps thirty, with ink-stained fingers that no proper bureau man would have permitted himself, and eyes that moved past her shoulder into the workshop before they ever settled on her face.

"Edmund Corliss," he said. "Imperial Bureau of Census and Assessment." He produced a folded document bearing the seal of the occupying authority — the double-headed eagle pressed deep enough into the wax to bruise it. "I'm here to verify the holdings of this establishment."

"The establishment is a mapmaker's workshop."

"It was." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Master Orenveld is recorded as deceased. Under the Seizure Statute, his holdings pass to provisional imperial custody pending inventory. I'm required to catalog everything: instruments, papers, plates, finished works." He paused. "You are?"

"Petra Vane. His apprentice."

"Then you'll want to be present for the inventory." He stepped inside.

She watched him work for two hours, and it was like watching a surgeon operate on someone still living. He handled each instrument with care — the brass alidades, the plumb lines, the master's long dividers worn bright at the hinges by forty years of Orenveld's thumb — and logged each one in a ledger written in that small careful hand. He was thorough. That was the horror of him. A careless man she could have deceived. Edmund Corliss counted sheets of paper.

When he reached the cabinet where the unfinished atlas lay, Petra's mouth went dry.

"These plates are unrecorded," he said. "No registry entry, no deposit stamp. Unlicensed cartographic work." He looked up at her, and for a moment something flickered behind the officialdom — curiosity, or its cousin. "This is the master projection. The southern approach. And this—" he turned a plate toward the light, and she saw the harbor depths rendered there, the channels Orenveld had sounded in secret for a decade, "—this is hydrography the navy would pay a kingdom for."

"It's incomplete," Petra said. "Worthless as it stands."

"Nothing is worthless as it stands." He laid the plate down gently, almost tenderly, and wrote in his ledger. Then he asked the question.

It should have been routine. *Who worked on these? Who had access? Where are the duplicates?* She had answers prepared for all of those. Instead he said, quietly, not looking up from the page:

"Who else has seen these maps?"

The room went very still. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere down the street a door slammed, and Petra thought absurdly of the neighbor's son, gone nine days now, and his mother who still set a bowl out at supper.

"I don't understand the question's purpose," she managed.

"It's not on the standard form." He seemed almost surprised by his own words, as though the question had come from somewhere outside him. He wrote it anyway — she watched him write it, saw the note appear beside the entry: *inquiry re: prior viewers.* "Standard procedure asks about possession. Possession can be corrected; you confiscate, and the matter ends. But viewers—" He stopped himself. Closed the ledger. "Prior viewing constitutes knowledge, and knowledge cannot be confiscated. Only people can."

He gathered his things. At the door he paused, half-turned, and for one unguarded moment he looked less like a census taker and more like a man standing at the edge of something he could not name.

"Complete nothing further," he said. "If these plates are finished, they become registered contraband. As they are — unfinished, unseen — they are merely..." He searched for the word. "Ambiguous. Stay ambiguous, Miss Vane."

Then he was gone, and Petra stood alone in the cold workshop with her heart hammering out a single thought: *he knows what he has just done to me, and he hates it.*

* * *

Edmund Corliss walked back to the bureau the long way, along the seawall, because he could not bear to walk the short way, which passed the holding pens.

He had asked eleven hundred questions in four months of service. *How many hearths? How many souls? Livestock? Arms? Persons absent, persons concealed?* Eleven hundred questions, and every one of them had been a key, and every key had opened a door he had helped nail shut behind him. He had taken the neighbor boy's name — Tomas Wren, seventeen, shipwright's apprentice, marked *fit for labor* — and written it in his ledger in his small careful hand, and told himself that refusal would only have meant another taker coming, a worse one, a soldier who would not have noted that the boy limped and so spared him the mines.

That was the arithmetic of survival he performed daily: each small compliance weighed against a smaller mercy, the whole sum never once balancing.

But today he had asked a question that was not on any form, and he knew — he had known even as his mouth opened — that it was not a question at all. It was an accusation. *Who else has seen these maps?* asked of a lone woman in a dead man's workshop was not inquiry; it was a noose with her name already on the tag. When the flags went up on that atlas — and they would go up, the moment his supervisor read *master projection, southern approach* — everyone whose eyes had touched those lines would answer for them. Her answer would be *no one,* and it would save no one, least of all herself, because the bureau did not believe in *no one.* The bureau believed in lists, and a list must always have names on it.

He sat on the seawall in the gray drizzle and thought about her hands. They had been steady when she opened the door, steady when she lied to him — *worthless as it stands* — steady everywhere except her fingertips, which had ink under the nails in the exact pattern of a woman who worked plates by candlelight, late, alone, finishing something she had been told not to finish.

He thought: *she is going to die for lines drawn on calfskin.*

He thought: *I wrote her street in my ledger, and my hand did not shake, and that is the most damning thing about me.*

By evening he had made his decision, which felt less like a decision than like a tide finally allowed to turn.

* * *

The knock came again near midnight, softer this time, almost apologetic. Petra opened the door with the master's boning knife held low against her thigh — and lowered it when she saw him standing hatless in the rain, no lantern, no ledger.

"You shouldn't be here," she said.

"No," he agreed. "Neither should you. May I come in?"

She stood aside. Inside, by the light of a single candle, he did something she would remember for the rest of her life: he opened his official ledger, tore out the page bearing her name and the question that damned her, and fed it to the candle flame. It caught, curled, went to ash in the inkwell.

"That buys you confusion, not safety," he said. "They'll have my field copy regardless. But it delays the cross-check. Days, perhaps. Not weeks."

"Why?" The word came out harder than she intended. "Why burn your own record? Why warn me? You took the Wren boy. Everyone says the taker who came to our street was thorough. Thorough men don't do this."

Something crossed his face — grief wearing duty's uniform, or duty wearing grief's. "Thoroughness is just cowardice with good penmanship," he said quietly. "I told myself every time that a gentle taker was better than a cruel one, that I softened what couldn't be stopped. Four months of telling myself that." He looked at the atlas under its cloth, at the corner of the master plate just visible, the southern coastline running like a lifeline across the vellum. "And then I stood in this room and asked you a question I invented all by myself, and heard exactly what it was. So now I'd like to know what these maps are *for*, Miss Vane, since apparently I've decided they matter more than my career. Possibly more than my neck."

Petra studied him a long moment — the rain-wet hair, the ink-stained fingers, the exhaustion of a man who had been at war with himself far longer than Vhalen had been at war with anyone.

"They're for whoever comes after," she said. "Orenveld believed borders were lies told in ink, and that the truth was the coastline itself — the water doesn't take sides. He spent his life charting what actually *is*, so that when the empires finish tearing each other apart, someone will know where the real world is underneath. This atlas isn't contraband, Mr. Corliss. It's the antidote."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to shield it.

"Ambiguous until it's finished," he murmured. "So it must be finished. Quickly. And then it must be gone."

"There is a way out," Petra said slowly, hearing herself commit to it even as she spoke. "The catacombs under the old salt quarter. They flood at high tide. The bureau thinks they're impassable. Orenveld didn't. There's a channel he mapped twelve years ago that surfaces beyond the wall."

Edmund was silent. Then he began, in his small careful hand, on the back of a scrap of waste paper, to draw from memory the times the bureau changed its watch — because of course he knew them, of course he had memorized them, because thoroughness was the only thing he had ever been allowed to be.

"Three days," he said. "I can give you three days before anyone looks twice at your file. After that—"

"After that we'll be elsewhere," Petra said, and was startled to find she meant *we.*

Their eyes met across the candle, across the ash, across the atlas that had already cost one life and was now, quietly, spending theirs. He did not correct her. Neither of them named it. Some things, like coastlines, are best surveyed slowly, in the dark, trusting the water to hold.

Outside, the rain thickened toward the sea. And neither of them noticed, until morning, the second page missing from Edmund's field copy — nor knew that his supervisor had already sent a rider to the harbor garrison with a single line:

*Orenveld atlas flagged for immediate confiscation.*
