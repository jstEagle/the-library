## 3. What the Water Keeps

The ledger room of the census bureau smelled of tallow and wet stone, and Edmund Corliss had come to hate it the way a man hates his own hands.

He sat at the third desk from the window, where the light was worst, transcribing the holdings of a city that no longer existed. That was the truth of it, though the ink said otherwise. Vhalen had been a port once — gulls, salt, bells counting the tide — but three months of siege had reduced it to an inventory. Households. Heads. Grain stores. Names in columns so neat they looked like fence posts driven into the page, each one marking a grave that hadn't been dug yet.

The atlas file arrived at midday, carried by a clerk with the apologetic stoop of a man who brought bad news often and had stopped apologizing for it.

"Flagged from the Ministry," the clerk said. "Master mapmaker's workshop, Salters' Row. Unfinished cartographic works, plates, instruments. Priority confiscation." He set down a single sheet bearing the ministry seal, black wax still soft enough to dent with a thumbnail. "Surveyors go Thursday."

Edmund read the order twice. *Unfinished* — that was the word that undid him. He saw the workshop again in the flat light of memory: Petra Vane bent over the last plate, hair pinned up crooked, the magnifying lens making one eye enormous and strange. He had stood in that room with his ledger open like a mouth and asked her, because the form demanded a ninth question and he had never before been a man to leave a question unanswered: *Who else has seen these maps?*

She had gone very still. And he had understood, in the space of her silence, exactly what he'd done.

Thursday. Three days.

He looked at the ledger open on his desk, at the column awaiting his entry for Salters' Row. Around him, forty clerks scratched in unison, a sound like insects in a wall. No one looked up. No one ever looked up; that was the mercy and the horror of bureaus — you could do anything in them provided you did it quietly, in small characters, between the lines.

Edmund dipped his pen. Wrote: *Salters' Row, premises of the late Master Corvin Aldous. Works assessed: incomplete, plates damaged by damp, negligible strategic value. Recommend deferral pending reassessment.*

His hand did not shake until he signed it. Then it shook, and he let it, because a steady signature would have felt like a lie.

Three days. It would end him when it was discovered — not with trumpets, but with a summons, a closed room, a name struck from the rolls. He found that he did not much care. What surprised him was what he cared about instead: not the atlas, not the borders it might redraw, but the way Petra had poured him tea in a chipped cup two nights ago and said, "You ask questions like apologies," and he had laughed — actually laughed — for the first time since the siege began.

He finished his shift. He walked out through streets that smelled of smoke and low tide, and he did not go home.

* * *

They worked by candlelight, the candles rationed to stubs, the flame guttering whenever the wind found a gap in the shutters. Petra spread the catacomb chart across the workbench and weighted its corners with grinding stones, and Edmund watched her hands — ink-stained, sure, moving over the old survey lines like a reader's fingers under familiar words.

"The western stairs flood first," she said. "Corvin showed me. The water comes up through the cistern channels with the rising tide — waist-deep at the worst hour, chest-deep if the wind pushes." Her finger traced a passage beneath the harbor wall. "But here. This gallery runs dry for six hours either side of low tide. Six hours to cross, or you drown in the dark with your lungs full of the sea."

"And after the gallery?"

"The sea stair. Old smugglers' landing, below the fish market. There's supposed to be a boat."

"*Supposed* to be."

She looked up at him then, and the candlelight did something to her face he would carry the rest of his life, however long that proved to be. "Everything is *supposed* to be, Edmund. The siege is supposed to lift. The ministry is supposed to be just." She smoothed the chart. "I'll take the boat if there is one. I'll swim if there isn't."

"You can't swim carrying an atlas."

"Then I won't need to." But her voice caught on it, and he reached across the bench and took her hand, ink-slick and cold, and she let him hold it.

"I falsified the entry," he said. It came out quieter than he intended. "Deferral. Reassessment. You have until Sunday night, and then someone will check my figures, because someone always checks, and—"

"Edmund."

"—and when they find it, they'll find me, and that part doesn't matter, but what matters is that you're gone before it does, that you're past the harbor bar with the—"

"Edmund." She came around the bench. She took his face in both hands the way she handled the vellum plates, carefully, as though he were something irreplaceable. "I know what it costs. I've known since the night you came back after hours with your hat in your hands like a boy caught stealing. Do you think I don't count?"

"I think you shouldn't have to count me among the costs."

"But you are," she said, simply, terribly. "And I would spend you anyway, and hate myself for it, and do it again. Is that the confession you wanted? Because here is the other one—" and her voice broke, just slightly, on the words, "—I love you. In the space between tides. In the time we bought with your handwriting. I love you, and it changes nothing, and it changes everything."

Outside, the siege guns spoke, far off, patient as a clock. Edmund pulled her against him and held on, and neither of them said anything true for a long while after that, because everything true had already been said, and the rest was only breath and heartbeat and the sound of water moving somewhere beneath the floor, keeping its own ledger, forgiving no one.

* * *

Sunday came in grey and smelling of rain that wouldn't fall.

Petra bound the atlas to her chest with oiled cloth and cord, plate by plate wrapped against the water, until she was thick-bodied with it, awkward, armored. Edmund watched her dress the pages the way other men dressed wounds. The duplicates — proofs, practice sheets, everything that might prove the atlas existed — lay stacked on the hearth beside the kindling.

"The tide turns at the fourth bell," she said. "Six hours of dry gallery. If I'm not at the sea stair by the tenth bell—"

"You'll be there."

"If I'm not, burn everything and get out of the city through the north gate. Promise me the north gate, Edmund, promise me you won't—"

A knock.

Not a knock — a fist, iron-shod, on the street door below, and then voices, and then the splintering sound no door makes except once.

Edmund moved without deciding to. He put himself between her and the stairs, absurdly, uselessly, a clerk's body offered as a barricade. "The cellar," he said. "Now. The entrance behind the paper racks — go."

"They're early—"

"They were never going to be late." Downstairs, a soldier's voice called out the ministry seal, the confiscation order, the corrected figures — *his* corrected figures, read aloud like an indictment. "Fourth bell, Petra. The gallery dries at fourth bell." He shoved the duplicates into the hearth and struck the flint, and the proof sheets caught all at once, maps of coastlines curling orange at their edges, bays and harbors blooming into fire. "Go. Don't look back. Looking back is how they catch people."

She caught him anyway — one hand at his collar, one fierce kiss that tasted of ink and salt, and then she was gone down the cellar stairs with the atlas pressed to her heart, and the fire was climbing the curtains, and boots were on the workshop steps.

Edmund turned to face the door and fed another armful of maps to the flames, and thought, with a calm that felt almost like happiness: *the water keeps what it keeps. The rest is ours.*

The first soldier filled the doorway, sword drawn, shouting something about the fire, about the order, about him.

Edmund smiled, and kept burning.
