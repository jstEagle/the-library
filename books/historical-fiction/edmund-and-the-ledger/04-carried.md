## 4. Carried

The scriptorium at Vellamare smelled of beeswax and salt, and in the seven months since Petra had arrived there, she had learned to love it the way one loves a borrowed coat — gratefully, knowing it was never meant to be hers.

She worked at the long table by the eastern window, where the light came up gold off the harbor each morning and made the vellum glow like something alive. Around her, a dozen scribes bent over their own commissions: charters, psalters, a merchant's book of tides. None of them knew what lay beneath the linen cloth at her elbow. None of them needed to.

The atlas was finished now. That was the strange part — that it could simply be *finished*, after everything. Master Orenn's final plates, the ones he had died rather than surrender, the ones Petra had inked by candlelight while census takers walked the streets of Vhalen counting souls like coins into an emperor's purse. She had completed the last of them here, in this quiet room, under another name. Here she was not Petra Vane, apprentice, fugitive, widow-of-no-one. Here she was Maren Ostrevich, a journeywoman cartographer from the northern coast, recommended by no one, owed nothing, asking nothing.

The name fit like a shoe half a size wrong. She had stopped noticing after the first month.

She lifted the linen cloth and looked at what they had made — what Orenn had begun and Edmund had died to buy and she had carried through black water to finish. Forty-one plates. The coastline of the whole shattered empire, drawn true for the first time in two hundred years. The drowned harbors marked in blue so deep it was nearly black. The mountain passes Orenn had walked himself as a young man, before his hands shook. The city of Vhalen itself, rendered with such loving precision that Petra could find the workshop on it still — a small square building on Inkwell Lane, its roof drawn flat and innocent, as though fire had never touched it.

The atlas would leave tomorrow. That had been decided last week, when the delegation from the Meridian Compact arrived at Vellamare — six men and a woman in travel-stained grey, carrying letters sealed in three colors of wax. They were mapmakers too, but of a different kind. They did not draw borders; they unmade them. Petra had watched them at supper, listening more than speaking, and understood that these were the hands the work had been waiting for. Somewhere in their ledgers were the drafts of treaties not yet signed, boundaries not yet argued, a world being quietly reassembled from the wreckage the empire had left behind.

Orenn's maps would redraw the coast of three nations. Edmund's lie had made that possible. Her arms had carried it across the water.

And none of them — not Orenn, not Edmund, not she herself — would appear anywhere in the record. The Compact would receive the atlas as an anonymous gift, "the work of a master of the old school." Anonymity had been the price of acceptance; a named atlas was a target, and a target could be burned. So the work would live and its makers would not, which Petra had come to believe was the correct order of things. Work survived. People were what got spent.

* * *

That evening she did what she did every evening, though she never let herself do it until the lamps were lit and the other scribes had gone to their cells. She unlocked the small cedar box she kept beneath the floorboard under her cot, and she took out the page.

It was a single sheet of bureau paper, cheap and fibrous, ruled in faded columns. *Household survey, district nine, ward four.* At the top, in a clerk's careful hand: the workshop's address, its assessed holdings, the number of persons resident — *one (1), female, cartographic apprentice.* And at the bottom, in the margin, in a different hand — smaller, hurried, pressed hard enough into the paper that she could feel the ghost of the strokes with her fingertip — a note that should never have been written:

*Holdings overstated per inventory error. Re-survey recommended. No further action this quarter.*

Three days. He had bought her three days with a lie written in his own bloodless bureaucratic hand, knowing the falsification would be found, knowing what finding it would cost him. The ledger entry was the only thing left of him. No grave — the workshop's ashes had swallowed whatever the soldiers left. No portrait. No letter; there had been no time for letters, only for the confession in the dark between tides, his voice low against the sound of the water rising in the catacombs, telling her what she already knew and had not dared to say first.

*Go,* he had said. *I'll give the fire something to eat.*

She had gone. She had felt the heat at her back like a second sun as the boat slid out past the breakwater, and she had not looked back, because looking back was a luxury the atlas could not afford.

Some nights the page was a wound. She would hold it and feel the whole weight of it — the neighbor's son taken by the first census taker, Orenn dead at his own table, the boy who had run messages for them found floating in the harbor, Edmund standing in the smoke with his little brass stamp still in his pocket because they hadn't even let him keep that. Some nights she thought: *this is all there is. A clerk's lie on cheap paper. This is what a man amounts to.*

But other nights — more of them now, as the months went soft and golden at Vellamare — the page was something else. It was proof. Not of death but of *choice*. Somewhere in the empire's vast machinery of counting, one man had held a pen over a column of numbers and decided that some things could not be counted, could not be seized, could not be conscripted. One man had looked at the ledger and seen through it. The empire had recorded everything and remembered nothing. This page, this scrap of fraud, was the only honest document either of them had ever produced.

She kept it because it was the only record he existed. But she had come to understand, slowly, the way tide works out a shoreline, that she did not keep it for him.

She kept it for herself. Because she was the record too. Because every line of the atlas was a sentence in a language only the two of them had spoken — his steadiness in the way she ruled her lines, her grief in the depth of her blues. When the Compact's envoys turned the plates tomorrow and murmured over the precision of the harbors, they would be reading Edmund Corliss without ever knowing his name. He would exist in the shape of every border they redrew. He would be in the world, everywhere, anonymously, forever.

That was not a grave. She had finally stopped calling it one.

* * *

On the morning of the handover, Petra rose before the bell and dressed in her good grey dress, the one with the mended hem, and carried the atlas down to the scriptorium wrapped in oilcloth against the harbor damp. The Compact's woman — her name was Ilse Varga, and she had eyes the color of slate and a way of listening that made you want to tell her true things — met her at the long table alone. The others waited outside. This, too, had been arranged: one witness, not six.

"You're certain," Ilse said. Not a question, quite. "Once it changes hands, it doesn't come back. And neither does the credit."

"I'm certain."

Ilse unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of someone who understood exactly what she was touching. She turned the plates slowly, one by one, and Petra watched her face and saw the moment — somewhere around the twelfth plate, the drowned harbors — when the woman stopped seeing paper and started seeing power. Saw the treaties this could anchor. Saw the wars this could prevent, or failing that, end sooner.

"Whoever made this," Ilse said quietly, "understood that a map is not a picture of the world. It's an argument about what the world owes its people." She looked up. "I'd like to shake the maker's hand."

Petra extended her own. Ilse took it, held it a moment longer than courtesy required, and something passed between them — not recognition, nothing so dangerous, but a kind of kinship. Two women who knew what paper weighed.

"The northern coast," Ilse said, shrugging the atlas into its traveling case. "You said you were from there. If the Compact's work goes as intended, there will be schools there within five years. Real ones. Cartography, among other things." She smiled, small and dry. "They'll need teachers who know the old surveys."

After she had gone, Petra stood a long time at the eastern window, watching the case disappear down toward the harbor, toward the ship, toward the world.

Then she went back upstairs, lifted the floorboard, and took out the cedar box one last time — not to mourn, but to decide. Because Ilse's words had opened a door she had not known was there, and beyond it lay the northern coast, and schools, and students who would need to learn not only how to draw a coastline but why it mattered who was allowed to see it.

She set Edmund's page on the table in the morning light and read it once more, all of it, the columns and the lie and the small fierce handwriting. Then she folded it — carefully, along its original creases — and slipped it inside the front of her bodice, over her heart, where she had carried the atlas through the dark water.

There was more to carry now. There always would be.

She blew out the lamp, though the sun was high, and began to pack.
