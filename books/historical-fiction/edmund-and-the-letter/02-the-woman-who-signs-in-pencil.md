## 2. The Woman Who Signs in Pencil

The fog came up the river on the first Tuesday of November and did not leave again for nine days.

Marguerite learned to drive through it the way a sailor learns to read water — by feel, by the ghost of kerbstones beneath the tyres, by the amber smudge of lamps that appeared and dissolved like thoughts one decides against. The Napier's headlamps carved two yellow tunnels that ended three feet from the bonnet and gave nothing back. She drove with the window open an inch despite the cold, because the cold kept her sharp, and because she had discovered in her first winter of this work that a driver who could not see must listen — for the bell of a brewer's dray, the cry of a muffin man, the particular wet slap of hooves that meant a horse had gone lame ahead and carts would be slewed across the road like spilled ink.

Adela Crane sat behind her in the tonneau with the hood down, because Adela said hoods made her feel buried alive, and because — Marguerite suspected — being seen was half of what Adela lived for. Even wrapped in fog, even reduced to a voice and a fur collar, Mrs. Crane wished to be visible. *There goes Adela Crane*, the fog ought to say. *Out in this, for the cause.*

"Kingsway," Adela said, "and then I'll want ten minutes at the Holborn pillar."

Marguerite turned them south off Gray's Inn Road. Somewhere to their left a church clock struck eleven and got lost halfway through, the sound swallowed before it finished.

She had been driving Adela Crane for two years and four months. She knew the arithmetic of the woman's days the way she knew the gear ratios of the car: the Tuesday letters, the Thursday committee, the Sunday silence that Adela filled with dictation while her husband attended his church and his constituents and his convictions, none of which included his wife's. Marguerite knew which hotels Adela took tea in and which she refused; which editors could be flattered and which must be bullied; that Adela took her coffee scalding and her criticism never. She knew the weight of the woman's hand on the back of her seat when she leaned forward to give an instruction, light as a bird landing, gone as soon as it had arrived.

What Marguerite did not know — what she had begun, this grey November, to understand she did not know — was anything true.

* * *

The ritual was always the same, and it was the part of her work Marguerite loved best, though she'd have cut out her tongue before admitting it.

In the evening, after the last delivery, she would take the satchel into whatever room she'd been given — a servant's attic in Hampstead, a back bedroom above the safehouse in Clerkenwell, once a linen cupboard in a hotel in Brighton that smelled of lavender and other people's holidays — and she would go through it by lamplight. Letters to be delivered stayed in the satchel. Letters to be memorized were read twice, folded along their original creases so no new line would show, and burned.

There was a brass dish she carried for the purpose, bought with her own money in her first week, because burning correspondence in a bedroom grate left ash, and ash told stories. The dish held it neat. She had learned early that everything about this work was a matter of containment: what fit in the satchel, what fit in the dish, what fit in her head. Her life, measured out, came to roughly fourteen inches of leather and six inches of brass and whatever the skull could hold without leaking.

That November night, in the attic room in Hampstead with rain ticking against the slates, there were seven letters in the satchel.

Five were ordinary. A note to the treasurer of the Bath branch about subscription arrears, written in Adela's secretary's fair round hand and signed in ink. A letter to a Manchester printer confirming quantities — five thousand pamphlets, delivered plain-wrapped, invoices marked *educational materials*. A courteous, glacial reply to a newspaper editor who had suggested, in print, that suffragists were unwomaned by their politics; Adela's response was a masterwork of the form, three paragraphs of courtesy wrapped around a blade. Two circular letters, duplicated, going out to forty names apiece.

Marguerite read these quickly. They went back in the satchel.

The sixth letter was different, and she knew it was different before she broke the seal, because it was sealed at all. Adela sealed perhaps one letter in twenty; the rest went out under the common flap, businesslike, unashamed. This one was in an envelope of heavier stock, and the direction on it was written not by the secretary but by Adela herself — a narrow, upright, rather beautiful hand that slanted against all expectation to the left, as if even her writing leaned away from the reader.

The address was a single initial. *E.*

No street. No city. Only *E.*, and below it, in smaller characters, a number: *4.* As though there were a series. As though E. might be found at 1, or 2, or 3, if one knew where to look.

Marguerite sat very still. The lamp hissed. Rain moved across the slates like fingers counting them.

She should not open it. She knew the rules, and the rules were simple: she read what she was given to read, she memorized what she was told to memorize, and she asked nothing, ever, because asking was the one currency Adela Crane did not accept. In two years Marguerite had watched cleverer women than herself ask questions — gentle questions, loyal questions, questions dressed up as concern for the movement — and she had watched Adela smile at them, thank them, and quietly arrange their lives elsewhere. A organizer sent to Glasgow. A treasurer eased toward retirement. Not cruelty, exactly. Pruning.

But the letter was already in her hands, and the flap was already lifting under her thumb, and some part of her — the part that had driven through fog for two years listening for what she could not see — had decided before the rest of her caught up.

Inside was one page, in pencil.

Not ink. Pencil, soft lead, the strokes slightly blurred where a palm had rested against them, the whole letter carrying that grey, provisional look of something written to be erased. And Marguerite felt the cold come up through the floorboards and into her spine, because she had seen this before. She had seen it a dozen times over two years and had filed it, obediently, under nothing — but now, with the evidence in her lap, the file opened itself.

Every letter signed in pencil was a letter she had been ordered to burn.

It was Adela's habit, when handing over the evening satchel, to tap certain envelopes with one finger and say, in the tone she used for train times: *Those you'll keep in your head, Miss Sterling. Nothing on paper.* And Marguerite had memorized them faithfully — instructions about money, mostly, sums routed through names that belonged to no one; occasionally warnings; once, memorably, a complete fabrication intended to be *seen* being destroyed, though Marguerite had understood only later that she herself had been the audience for that performance. She had thought the pencil letters were tactics. Caution. The natural hygiene of an illegal movement.

She had never thought to ask why a woman who trusted her typist with five thousand pamphlets' worth of sedition would sign her most dangerous letters in a medium that could be rubbed away.

*Dear E.,* the letter began. No endearment beyond the initial. The handwriting was careful, slower than Adela's usual racing script, as though each word had been weighed before it was set down.

*I have received yours of the 29th. You are right that the matter cannot continue as it has, and wrong about nearly everything else. I do not ask your forgiveness. I ask only that you let me finish what I began to repay. Four years is not such a long time. Some debts are measured in more than years, and mine was measured in more than money.*

*You say I have forgotten. I forget nothing. That is my affliction and my profession both.*

*Send word by the usual means when you are ready to conclude. I will make provision. It will be final, and it will be enough, and afterwards we need not write again.*

*— A.*

Beneath the signature, faintly, as though the pencil had hovered and almost added something and thought better of it: a small mark, like the ghost of a second word, rubbed out.

Marguerite read it twice, as the rules required. Then a third time, which broke the rules.

*The debt is repaid. It will be final, and it will be enough.*

Four years ago. What had happened four years ago? She cast back through the ledger of Adela's life as she knew it — the public ledger, the one printed in newspapers and repeated at meetings — and found the year Adela had spent abroad. It was spoken of rarely and always in the same terms: her health, poor thing, the doctors insisted, a year on the Continent, Switzerland chiefly, such a trial to be away during the fight. Marguerite had heard the story so many times it had worn smooth as a pebble. She had never once thought to turn it over and look at its underside.

A debt owed to someone called E. A repayment four years in the making. A woman who signed in pencil because pencil could be erased, and who entrusted the erasure — the burning, the forgetting — to Marguerite herself.

She held the page over the brass dish and struck the match, and her hand was steady, because her hands were always steady, that was the thing people said about her, steady as a surgeon's. The paper caught. The pencil-darkened lines went orange at their edges and curled. *I forget nothing*, the fire said, and then it didn't say anything anymore, and Marguerite sat watching the ash settle in the dish with the peculiar vertigo of a woman who has just realized the floor of her house is thinner than she believed.

She was trusted with everything. That was what she had believed, and what Adela had wanted her to believe, and what she had built two years of competence upon. Trusted with the passes and the lists and the names of women whose liberty depended on discretion. Trusted to burn, trusted to remember, trusted to drive through fog on instinct alone.

Trusted with everything except the truth. Because the truth, apparently, was the one cargo that did not fit in the satchel.

* * *

The next morning the fog had thinned to a pale gauze, and London emerged looking scoured and guilty, like a face after crying. Marguerite brought the Napier around to the house in Hampstead at half past eight and found Adela already on the step, hat pinned, gloves on, a woman assembled entirely of purpose.

"You're prompt," Adela said, settling into the tonneau. It was not praise. It was inventory.

"The roads are clear, ma'am. The fog's lifted."

"Mm." Adela unfolded a newspaper, snapped it once, and became a silhouette of industry. For a mile neither of them spoke. Then, without lowering the paper: "You burned everything last night?"

"Everything I was told to, ma'am."

A pause, precisely long enough to be noticed and precisely too short to be challenged. "Good." The paper rustled. "There'll be another tonight. Same treatment."

"Yes, ma'am."

They crossed Haverstock Hill. A milk cart stood at a corner, the horse steaming, the milk cans catching the thin light like a row of dull coins. Marguerite changed gear and watched the road and did not look in the mirror, and all the while the question sat in her mouth like a stone.

She was not a woman who asked questions. She had built her entire standing on the fact. But she had lain awake until two o'clock turning the pencil over in her mind, and she had arrived somewhere she did not like: the realization that obedience without understanding was not loyalty. It was only obedience. And a person could be perfectly obedient and still be used — could be, in fact, most easily used *because* she was perfectly obedient, because she burned what she was told and asked nothing and mistook the shape of her trust for the substance of it.

"Mrs. Crane," she said, to the windscreen, to the road. "May I ask something?"

The newspaper lowered an inch. "You may ask."

It was beautifully done. Permission granted, answer reserved. Marguerite almost smiled. "The letters I burn. The ones in pencil. Is there a reason they're not in ink?"

Silence. The wheels hummed. Somewhere behind them a man shouted at a cab horse, and the sound came and went like weather.

"Ink is permanent," Adela said at last, and her voice was pleasant, conversational, the voice she used for explaining things to journalists she considered slow. "Pencil can be disputed. A penciled document may be denied, revised, claimed as forgery. Ink commits a person, Miss Sterling. I commit myself in ink to the cause and to nothing else on God's earth." The paper rose again. "Is that philosophy sufficient for your morning?"

"It's sufficient, ma'am."

But it wasn't, and they both knew it wasn't, because the answer explained the medium and not the message. It explained why one might write dangerously in pencil. It did not explain why the dangerous letters all went to *E.*, or why a debt four years old was being concluded now, in November of 1913, in a hand that hovered and almost wrote a second word and rubbed it out.

Marguerite drove. She was good at driving. She could hold a car on a wet camber at thirty miles an hour with her mind elsewhere, and she did so now, down the hill into the smoke and noise of the city, past shopfronts being unshuttered, past a newsboy bawling something about the House of Lords, past the whole waking apparatus of a capital that ran on paper — ledgers, timetables, love letters, warrants — every sheet of it insisting on permanence, on record, on proof.

At the Holborn pillar Adela got down to post a letter — in ink, Marguerite noted, in ink and under her own hand — and stood a moment in the thin sunshine consulting her watch, and for those few seconds her face was unguarded, tired at the edges, older than her photographs. Marguerite looked at her through the windscreen and felt the two loyalties inside her shift against each other like ice floes. She loved this woman. She had given this woman two years of her life and her steadiness and her silence, and she had been paid in purpose, which is the coin that buys deepest. And she had just discovered that the account had a second book, a private ledger, kept in pencil, in a hand that leaned away.

Adela returned, gathered her skirts, sat. "Clerkenwell," she said. "We're due at the safehouse by eleven, and I want the Rivington Street workshop after. There's a consignment coming in from Birmingham that shouldn't stand about." She settled back. "And Miss Sterling — tonight's letter will need the usual. Keep it in your head. Nothing on paper."

"Nothing on paper," Marguerite agreed, and put the car in gear, and drove them east toward Clerkenwell, toward the smell of ink and hot metal and the only people in London she trusted — carrying, in the dish of her memory, a letter addressed to *E.*, number 4, full of debts and endings, and carrying also, though she did not yet know its shape, the certainty that whatever was being concluded, whatever had been paid off at last after four years, was not money at all.

It was something Adela Crane had signed for in pencil because she had always meant, from the very beginning, to deny it existed.

And Marguerite — who forgot nothing either — was going to find out what.
