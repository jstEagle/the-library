## 3. The Workshop on Rivington Street

Rivington Street kept its secrets the way the river kept its dead — quietly, and not always forever.

Marguerite had learned its geography by heart in the first month of her service: the narrow turning off Shoreditch High Street where the pavement gave way to cobbles worn round as knuckles; the shuttered chapel with its boarded windows; the wall where some anonymous hand had painted VOTES FOR WOMEN in white and someone else had painted over it, and a third hand had painted it again, so that now it ghosted through three coats of grey like a drowned face beneath ice. The workshop itself was a former piano factory, its ground floor given over to what appeared from the street to be a maker of printed programmes for musical evenings. The sign — *Hart & Sons, Printers* — had been there so long that the paint had gone the colour of old teeth, and no one in the neighbourhood could have said when Hart or his sons had last been seen.

The truth was on the second floor, up a staircase that smelled of paraffin and machine oil, behind a door with a latch that Tom Ainsley had rigged so that lifting it wrong rang a bell somewhere in the cellar.

Marguerite lifted it right. She always did.

The heat struck her first, as it always did — the heat of the press, of the small iron stove that was never allowed to go cold, of twenty bodies working in a space meant for ten. Then the smell: ink, solvents, the sweet burnt tang of type-metal, the ghost of glue and damp wool. Then the noise, which was less a noise than a weather — the clatter of the treadle press, the shuffle of paper, voices pitched low out of habit even when there was nothing to hide.

"Miss Sterling," said Mr. Prew, who ran the place, without looking up from the forme he was locking into the chase. He said it the way a churchwarden says the hour. He was sixty and had hands like tree roots and had been printing illegal things since before most of these children were born, first in Manchester, then in Paris, then here, and he spoke of the law the way a sailor speaks of weather: something to be read, respected, and worked around.

"Mr. Prew."

"Your lot came Tuesday." He nodded toward a stack of crates against the far wall. "Twelve hundred pamphlets. *The Prisoner's Voice*, new number." He spat a fleck of tobacco into a tin cup. "Tell Mrs. Crane we need more of that thin stock if she means to keep mailing them inside Holloway. The ordinary paper they confiscate at the gate."

"I'll tell her."

"And tell her—" he stopped, wiped his hands slowly on his apron, and for a moment looked older than sixty, "—tell her we may want to think about moving the good type. The expensive type. Before winter."

Marguerite felt the floor tilt very slightly, though it had not moved. "Why before winter?"

Prew put his pipe between his teeth, unlit, which was how he smoked when he wanted to say nothing further. "Because winters are long," he said, "and men get bored, and bored men talk to other men who buy their drinks. Go up. They're all upstairs pretending to fold newsletters."

* * *

They were not pretending, exactly. Upstairs, in the long room under the slanted roof where the rain made its own opinions known against the glass, six young people sat around a trestle table folding, addressing, and sealing copies of *The Suffragist* while Winifred Dodd read aloud from a smuggled account of force-feeding in Walton Gaol in a voice pitched for drama class. But the moment Marguerite came through the door, the reading faltered, faces turned, and Marguerite understood — as she understood every time, and was embarrassed every time — that she arrived carrying something they needed: news of the outside, of Adela Crane alive and upright and driving east in a motor car, proof that the thing they were printing and folding and risking themselves for had a body, a face, a voice that got into motors and went places.

"Well?" said Winifred, dropping the account. "Is it true about Parliament? Is it true she stood up in the rain outside the House for four hours?"

"She stood up in the rain for four hours," Marguerite said, taking off her gloves finger by finger, "and then she took tea, and complained about my driving, and dictated eleven letters. Which is more than any of you managed before lunch, I'll wager."

Laughter. Someone pushed a chair at her. A girl of perhaps seventeen called Nellie slid a folded newspaper across the table to her with the solemnity of a sacrament, and Marguerite took it and set it aside because she had learned that the folding must go on, that in this room you earned your welcome with your hands.

She sat down among them and folded. She was good at it — quick, exact, the creases sharp — and she knew they noticed, and she knew why it mattered to them, this woman who drove Mrs. Crane's car folding newsletters like any volunteer, and she let them notice. It was, she thought not for the first time, the only currency she had ever been rich in: competence. Give her a task and she would do it perfectly and no one would ever need to ask whether she was lonely.

Across the table, at the sorting end where the addressed bundles accumulated, Tom Ainsley was setting type.

He did not set type at the table, of course — he carried down the composed lines to the press below in galleys, and came back up for more — but he sat apart from the folders with his frame and his tweezers and his little brass bodkin, and he worked the way masons work, unhurried, each letter placed and checked, and every few minutes he rose with a galley in both hands and went down the stairs and came back up, and Marguerite tracked all of it without looking at him. Two years of practice had made an art of this: knowing exactly where he was in the room by the sound of his breathing, the creak of the third stair, the way he whistled — barely, almost subvocally — fragments of music-hall songs he was too self-conscious to whistle properly.

He was twenty-six, two years older than she was. He had been a compositor at a respectable firm in Fetter Lane until the lockout, and had come to Rivington Street because, as he put it, "a man might as well print what matters and starve honestly." His hands were stained permanently black in the creases — Marguerite had once watched him try to scrub them clean with paraffin and a nailbrush for a full five minutes before giving up — and he had a small scar through one eyebrow from a flying sort, and he wore his collarless shirt open at the throat because the loft was hot, and when he caught her looking at him — which happened, occasionally, despite everything — he did not look away quickly, as a shy man would, nor hold the look boldly, as a forward one would. He simply looked back, a moment, the way one looks at weather one has decided to walk in anyway.

"You've mis-set 'suffrage' again," Marguerite said, because she could not say anything else.

Tom turned the galley toward her, unoffended. "Where?"

"There. Third line. You've spelled it 'sufferage.' Twice."

Nellie leaned over. "Has he? Tom, you've been spelling it wrong for a fortnight, I told Win, I said, either he's in love or his eyes are going."

"Nellie," said Winifred Dodd, warningly, delightedly.

"It's the *f* and the *u*," Tom said gravely, examining the line. "They conspire. Any compositor will tell you." But he corrected it, and Margueride saw — sideways, through her lashes, in the manner perfected over two years — that while correcting it he was smiling at the type and not at anyone, and that his ears had gone faintly red, and she folded her newsletter with great attention and felt the ridiculous warm lift in her chest that she had felt for two years and had done precisely nothing about, because there was the movement, and there was the work, and there was Adela Crane's satchel full of secrets riding in her memory everywhere she went like ballast, and none of it — none of it — left room.

Besides. She had seen, over two years, what happened to people in this house who loved each other. The police took the men. It was nearly a policy.

* * *

Tea came up at four, as it always did, in two brown pots carried by whoever had drawn the duty, and everyone crowded toward the stove, and the talk turned — as it always turned — to the same question, asked in the same careful half-voice.

"They were asking after us again," said Fred Halloran, the youngest of the forgers, a pale boy with ink permanently under his nails and a genius for documents. "Tuesday night. Man came into the Britannia asking Charlie Price about employment. Printing work. Whether he knew anywhere a steady lad could get on."

"In the Britannia," repeated Prew, from the doorway, in a voice that closed the conversation like a lid.

"He was just—"

"In the *Britannia*." Prew came in and sat down heavily. The room quieted. "Which is why Charlie Price is no longer welcome in the Britannia, and why Fred is no longer welcome to tell me things over pints, and why from tonight anything anyone hears in any public house comes to me alone, in daylight, through that door." He pointed the stem of his unlit pipe at each of them in turn. "I've had this trade thirty-eight years. I've been raided in Manchester, in Leeds, in Paris, and once in Brussels, which I still maintain was a mistake. In every case but Brussels it began the same way. Not with a warrant. With a man buying drinks."

Silence. The kettle ticked on the stove.

"We're careful," Winifred said, but her voice had lost its drama-class flourish.

"We are," said Prew. "We are careful, and we are lucky, and those are different things, and only one of them lasts." He looked around the room, and Marguerite saw his gaze rest a half-second longer on each young face — Nellie, Fred, Winifred, the quiet brothers named Pike who never spoke — doing sums she could not see. Then his eyes came to her, and something in them softened by a degree. "Mrs. Crane knows?"

"She will by tomorrow."

"Good. Tell her also—" and now his voice dropped into the flat register he used for business — "that the passes are getting harder. The railway men are stamping differently since spring, and the Continental tickets are near impossible without real stock. If she wants women moved north ahead of the arrests she thinks are coming, we can manage it, but slower than she'd like, and dearer."

"She thinks the arrests are coming," Marguerite said. It wasn't a question.

"Everyone thinks the arrests are coming," said Prew. "Some of us are merely honest enough to arrange our affairs."

* * *

It was later — the tea drunk, the newsletters bundled, Nellie and the Pike brothers gone down into the blue November dusk toward their lodgings — that Marguerite found herself alone upstairs with Tom, gathering the string and waste paper while he distributed his type back into the cases, the little iron music of it, *a* into its compartment and *e* into its compartment, each letter home in its slot.

"You could stay," he said, to the type case. "There's a meeting. Win's brought biscuits. Actual biscuits, from her aunt in Putney."

"I've letters to deliver before dark."

"You always have letters."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "That's the position."

He put down the tweezers. Outside, a cart went past on the wet cobbles, and the lamplighter was working his way along Rivington Street, and the gaslight through the sloped window fell across the table in long amber bars. Tom leaned against the case and looked at her — openly, this time, no sideways artifice possible — and Marguerite had the sensation, familiar and unbearable, of standing on a platform while a train she had decided not to board gathered steam.

"How is she?" he asked. "Really. Not the version for the newspapers."

"Tired. Magnificent. Frightened, though she'd deny it under oath." Marguerite wound the string around her fingers. "She signed something in pencil today."

"Pencil?"

"When she means a thing never to have existed. Ink for history, pencil for the rest." She heard herself say it and was surprised; she had not planned to say it; it had simply come up the way blood comes to a surface.

Tom considered this. He had, she remembered, a compositor's relationship to such distinctions. "Ink's for the record," he said. "Pencil wipes off." And then, quieter: "What was it?"

"I don't know yet."

"But you'll find out."

Marguerite looked at him. "You say that like it's a virtue."

"It is," Tom said. "In a house full of people who'd rather not know things, it's the rarest virtue there is." He picked up the tweezers again, and put them down again, and said, with the elaborate carelessness of a man defusing something, "When you find out — whatever it is — you'll want someone to tell it to. That's how secrets go bad. Kept alone." He shrugged, eyes on the case. "Speaking as a professional keeper of confidences. Everything I set goes through my hands and out of my memory, mostly, except the bits that don't, and those I've learned you have to give to somebody or they turn on you."

The lamp guttered. Downstairs, Prew locked the chase of tomorrow's forme with three soft iron blows, like a heartbeat.

"And if the somebody repeats it?" Marguerite asked.

"Then choose your somebody well." Tom smiled — the scar through his eyebrow lifting, the whole face briefly young — and began putting away his tools. "Biscuits are on the sideboard, Miss Sterling, should your letters keep."

They did not keep. She took her coat and her satchel and went down the stairs that smelled of paraffin, past the sleeping press, out the door with the trick latch, into the street where VOTES FOR WOMEN ghosted beneath its grey paint like something waiting to surface.

She was halfway to the car before she saw it: a man leaning against the lamp-post at the corner of the street, hat brim low, collar up, doing nothing at all — and doing it with the particular patience of a man paid by the day to do nothing in one spot.

He did not follow her. He did not write anything down. He simply watched her cross to the motor, and light a match against the wind to check something in a notebook he did not open, and Marguerite drove out of Rivington Street with her heart beating oddly and one thought arriving with total clarity, the way certain truths arrive — not reasoned to, but simply *there*, complete:

Someone had been standing on that corner for some time. And the workshop — warm, laughing, full of children folding newsletters — had been watched all evening, by more than one pair of eyes, and none of them hers.

Behind her in the satchel lay nine letters to be delivered by dark.

One of them, she now understood, might already be evidence.
