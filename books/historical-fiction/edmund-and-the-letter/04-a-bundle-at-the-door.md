## 4. A Bundle at the Door

The rain came in off the river before dawn, a fine grey insistence that turned the lamplight of Camberwell into haloes and the cobbles into a long dark mirror. Marguerite drove through it with her collar up and her hands bare — she had never learned to like gloves on the wheel; she wanted to feel the road, even when the road was only water and stone — and beside her on the seat the satchel sat with its six letters, dry under the oilcloth, each one already read.

She had read them by the bedroom candle at half past four, as was her habit: two appeals for funds, one furious resignation from a branch secretary in Leeds, a note from a Manchester printer about paper shortages, an invitation to speak at a hall in Croydon, and one letter signed not in ink but in pencil, which meant it went into her memory rather than into the post-bag, and would go from there into the stove before the house woke. That one had been short. It concerned money — forty pounds, sent to no name but a poste restante in Southampton Row — and it ended with a sentence Marguerite had recited to herself all the way through Southwark until the words wore smooth and meaningless with handling: *You will understand that this closes the matter entirely.*

Adela Crane signed such letters in pencil because pencil could be denied. Ink made promises; pencil only made marks that might have been anyone's. Marguerite had learned this in her first month of service, and had learned too what the pencil-signatures meant: that she was trusted with everything except the truth. She had made her peace with it the way one makes peace with weather. There was no use resenting rain.

The safehouse stood on a quiet turning near the church — a tall narrow house with a green door and a brass plate that said nothing at all, which was the point of it. It belonged nominally to a widow who did not exist, and housed in practice a shifting population of women: organizers passing through London, girls fleeing homes that had turned dangerous, occasionally a printer's apprentice sleeping in the parlour chair between shifts at the Rivington Street workshop. Adela called it *the way-station*, in the tone other women used for their children's names.

Marguerite brought the motor round the mews and into the side court where the kitchen door waited, and it was there — as she cut the engine and the silence rushed in, loud after twenty minutes of rain drumming the hood — that she heard the sound.

At first she thought it was a cat. The court sheltered cats; they got into the coal cellar and sang to each other through the wall. But this was not the complaint of a cat. It was thinner and more rhythmic, more desperate, a small insistent wail that rose and fell and rose again, coming from low down, from the doorstep itself.

She sat very still with her hands on the wheel.

Then she took the lamp from its bracket, climbed down, and went to look.

* * *

The bundle lay on the flat of the top step, tucked against the door as though someone had set it down gently and then run. A shawl, grey-blue, good wool gone soft with washing. Inside it a face — a small red furious face, screwed shut, mouth open on that thin unending cry — and two fists no larger than walnuts, working at the air.

An infant. Days old, perhaps; a fortnight at the outside. The cord-scar still showed at the navel where the shawl had gaped.

Marguerite knelt. Her skirt went into the puddle at once and she did not notice. She held the lamp close, and the baby stopped crying for a moment, blinded or startled, blinking at the light with eyes the colour of a storm cloud over water, and looked — there was no other word for it — looked at her.

"There now," Marguerite said, uselessly, the way one speaks to anything frightened.

And then she saw the note.

It was pinned to the shawl with a common steel safety-pin, and folded twice, and it had been written — she saw this before she had even unfolded it fully, saw it in the angle of the strokes before a single word resolved — in pencil.

Her hands knew before her mind did. Her hands had held sixty of these notes, had smoothed them flat on lamp-lit tables, had memorized and burned them. She knew the way the downstrokes leaned slightly left, as though the writer were always pulling back from what she wrote. She knew the peculiar flat-topped *f*. She knew the hand the way one knows a voice heard through a wall.

*Forget the face,* the note said. *The debt is paid.*

No signature. No signature was needed. The handwriting was Adela Crane's own.

Marguerite stayed kneeling on the wet stone with the lamp in one hand and the note in the other, and the rain came down on her uncovered head, and for a long moment she could not make the two facts sit together: the child, and the hand. Each was impossible alone. Together they were something she had no shape in her mind to hold.

The baby began to cry again.

That broke the spell. She put the lamp down, gathered the bundle up — it weighed almost nothing; it weighed less than the satchel, that was the obscenity of it, she carried heavier papers every day of her life — and tucked the shawl close around the small hot face. The crying dulled to a hiccupping whimper against her collarbone. The warmth of it came through her coat like a coal laid directly on her skin.

She let herself into the kitchen with her elbow, set the lamp on the table, and stood in the grey light with the child in her arms while the fire in the range breathed its last warmth of the night at her back.

*Forget the face.*

Marguerite Sterling, who could recite from memory the contents of every letter she had burned in two years of service — who kept in her head, filed and exact, the names of women whose safety depended on her forgetting nothing — looked down at the face in the shawl and understood that she had just been ordered, by a woman not yet awake in the house above her, to do the one thing she had never once been asked to do.

To forget something on purpose.

She found she was already memorizing it. That was the horror. The small creased eyelids. The milk-blister at the corner of the upper lip. A birthmark, faint as tea, below the left ear. Her mind took these things and shelved them without asking permission, because that was what her mind did, that was the whole architecture of her usefulness, and no note pinned with a steel pin could unpick it now.

* * *

Mrs. Quill came down first — Mrs. Quill who was not called Mrs. Quill by anyone who knew better, the housekeeper-of-record, a broad calm Cornishwoman who had survived three raids and one marriage and regarded both with similar philosophy. She stopped on the bottom stair with her candle raised, took in the scene — Marguerite soaked, the motor still ticking in the court, the bundle, the smell of it, which is a smell no one mistakes for anything else — and said only:

"Well. I'll put the kettle on."

"It was on the step," Marguerite said. "There's a note."

"I don't see any note," said Mrs. Quill, setting her candle down with the finality of a judge's gavel. "I see a wet driver and I see a baby, and neither of them is my business beyond a warm towel. Whose is it?"

"I don't know," Marguerite said, and it was nearly true, and the nearly rang in her ears.

By seven the household was awake and the kitchen was full. The baby had been fed — Mrs. Quill produced astonishing competence from somewhere, warm milk and a clean cloth and a lifetime's knowledge in her big hands — and slept at last in the bread drawer of the dresser, lined with a tea towel, looking absurd and holy, like something hidden by a child playing at smugglers. The young women of the house crowded and cooed and invented theories: a servant girl from the big houses on the Grove; a married woman from Clapham; some poor creature on the boats to Ireland. The theories were kind. They were also, Marguerite noted, wrong, and she listened to them the way one listens to weather reports for another country.

Adela came down at half past eight.

She descended into the kitchen in her morning greys, hair pinned without ceremony, spectacles on — few people ever saw Adela Crane in spectacles, and those who did generally reconsider their opinions of her humanity — and she was mid-sentence about the Croydon engagement when she saw the bread drawer.

Marguerite watched the sentence die on her lips.

It lasted perhaps two seconds. Two seconds in which Adela Crane's face, trained by a decade of public life to betray nothing, betrayed everything: the paling, the stillness, the flicker downward toward the drawer as though the floor had opened. Then it closed again, the face, like a ledger shutting, and when she spoke her voice was level and cold and perfectly ordinary.

"Whose is this?"

"It was left at the kitchen door before dawn," Marguerite said. "There was a note."

"Give it to me."

Marguerite took the folded paper from her breast pocket — she had carried it there since the court, against her heart, like evidence, like a wound — and held it out. And here she did the thing she had never done in two years of service, the thing her body did without consulting her: she watched Adela's face as she read it.

There was no recognition. Of course there wasn't. Adela read her own handwriting every day; a woman does not recognize her own hand the way one recognizes a stranger's. But Marguerite saw the reading, saw the meaning land, saw the pause lengthen by a single heartbeat past what the words themselves required — *forget the face, the debt is paid* — and saw Adela fold the note twice, precisely, along existing creases, and put it into her own pocket, and look up.

"Miss Sterling. A word."

* * *

They took the parlour, the front room with the furniture sheets on it and the dust-sheeted shapes of chairs like seated ghosts. Adela closed the door and stood with her back to it, and for a moment neither woman spoke. Rain still worked at the window. Downstairs, muffled, the household resumed its noise, and beneath it — faint, insistent, patient — the baby cried again and was hushed again.

"You read everything," Adela said at last. It was not a question.

"Everything you give me."

"And you remember everything."

"You know I do."

Adela took off her spectacles and folded them. Without them her face was older and rawer, the face beneath the public face, and Marguerite saw with a strange lurch that she was afraid. Not of exposure, exactly. Something finer than that. Afraid the way one is afraid on ice.

"The note is mine," Adela said quietly. "You know it is. You knew it before you handed it to me — I saw it in your face, Miss Sterling, you have never once been able to hide a thing from me, it is your single most inconvenient quality." She moved to the window, parted the sheet an inch, looked out at the rain. "I will not insult you with a denial. I will ask instead for the thing I have always asked of you, and always been given."

"Which is?"

"That what is in your head stays there. That this morning never happened. That there is no note, no child, no debt — that you drove through the rain and found a dry court and an empty step." She turned. "The girl will be taken to the Foundling Hospital before nightfall. Their intake is discreet; the child will be received, christened fresh, given a number, given a chance. It is the kindest of the available mercies, and I have considered all of them, believe me, at greater length than you can imagine. Mr. Hobbs will drive her in. You will say nothing to anyone — not to Mrs. Quill, not to the workshop, not to Tom Ainsley, not to God if you can manage it."

"The father," Marguerite said. Her voice sounded far away to herself.

"Is not alive," said Adela, "or might as well not be, which comes to the same thing." And then, softer, with something almost like the old warmth: "Marguerite. You think I am made of stone. Perhaps I am. Stones are what get built with. This cause is the first thing in thirty years worth building, and it stands on a reputation, mine and others', and a breath of scandal — a whisper of a child, of *where*, of *when* — and it comes down, and it takes five hundred women down with it, women with jobs and no protections, women who go to prison for this, women who are force-fed in Holloway with rubber tubes while doctors call it feeding and the newspapers call it discipline. You have read the letters. You know precisely what falls when I fall. Weigh that against one infant who will not remember, and tell me my arithmetic is wrong."

Marguerite could not tell her the arithmetic was wrong. That was the terrible thing. She had read the letters; she knew what fell. She had spent two years measuring her life in what fit inside a satchel, and she knew the weight of what Adela carried, or thought she did.

But the bread drawer. The small fists. The eyes the colour of a storm over water, looking at her.

"She's three weeks old," Marguerite said.

"She is an argument I cannot afford," Adela said, "and neither can you. Nightfall, Miss Sterling. Hobbs will take her. You will forget the face."

She went out. The door clicked behind her with the softness of all final doors, and Marguerite stood alone among the sheeted ghosts, listening to the rain, and downstairs the baby cried and was hushed, cried and was hushed, like a pulse, like a second heartbeat the house had grown overnight.

*Forget the face.*

Marguerite took out the satchel and set it on the dusty table, and opened it, and looked at the six letters lying there in their neat order — five to deliver, one to burn — and understood suddenly that the satchel had changed. Nothing in it had moved, and yet everything was different now, because for two years the satchel had held only other people's secrets, secrets given to her freely by the woman who trusted her with everything except the truth, and now she held a secret *of her own*, unwritten, unpinned, warm, breathing in a bread drawer downstairs.

A secret with a face. A secret she had been ordered to forget and had already, helplessly, memorized: the milk-blister at the lip's corner, the tea-coloured mark below the left ear, the storm-grey eyes.

Nightfall, Adela had said. Before nightfall, Hobbs and the hospital and a number instead of a name.

Marguerite buttoned the satchel slowly. Outside the window the rain was easing, and through the thinning veil of it she could see the court, the wet stones, the empty top step where the bundle had lain — and, printed sharp and brief in the gutter-wash before the water took it, the clear impression of a boot heel, a man's boot heel, planted deep, facing away from the door.

Someone had carried the child here. Someone had stood on that step in the dark and run.

And Adela had said *Mr. Hobbs will drive her in* — Hobbs, who was in Southampton, Hobbs, who had been in Southampton for eleven days.

Marguerite stood very still among the ghosts, and began, methodically, terribly, to count.
