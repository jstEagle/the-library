## 5. Adela's Arithmetic

The rain had not stopped since dawn. It came down in grey sheets against the windows of the safehouse on Mecklenburgh Square, blurring the gardens into watercolour, and inside the house the air was thick with the smell of wet wool and coal smoke and, faintly, of milk — because upstairs, in the room that had been meant for storing pamphlets, a child was sleeping in a laundry basket lined with a suffragist banner.

Marguerite stood at the foot of the stairs for a long time before she climbed them.

She had driven Adela home from a committee meeting that afternoon in silence. Not the companionable silence of two women who have shared four years of fog and fuses and the rattle of a crank-started motorcar — no, this was the other kind, the kind with weight in it, the kind Marguerite knew from letters she had read and was never meant to have read. Adela had sat in the rear seat with her gloved hands folded and her face turned to the streaming window, and Marguerite, watching her in the mirror, had understood that Mrs. Crane knew. Knew that the driver knew. Knew about the basket upstairs.

Now the house was emptying. Mrs. Prynne had gone out for a doctor who would not be told anything useful; the two young volunteers who slept at the safehouse had been sent to a lecture on the Franchise Bill, an evening wasted gladly. Only the rain kept them company, and the child, and the truth.

Adela's study was on the first floor, at the back, where the light was bad even in summer. Marguerite did not knock. In four years she had never once entered without knocking, and something told her that tonight the courtesy would be a cruelty — that if she allowed herself the small ceremony of a knuckle on wood she would lose her nerve entirely, stand in the corridor like a servant, and let the moment close like water over a stone.

So she opened the door, and closed it behind her, and turned the key in the lock.

Adela Crane sat at her desk with her back very straight, as she sat everywhere, as she had sat through heckling in Norwich and arrest warrants in Bow Street. She was fifty-one years old and handsome the way a cliff is handsome: something you admired from a distance and did not lean against. Her silver hair was pinned without a single loose strand. On the desk before her lay a sheet of paper and a pencil — *a pencil*, always the pencil — but it was blank, and her hand was nowhere near it. Her hands were folded, and they were not steady.

"You locked the door," Adela said. Her voice was level. It was always level. That was the thing people misunderstood about her; they thought her coldness was temperament, when it was craft.

"Yes," said Marguerite.

"Someone might need me."

"No one needs you tonight more than I do, and I have you."

The rain filled the pause. Marguerite crossed the room slowly, her boots loud on the bare boards — everything in this house was either confiscated or borrowed, nothing permanent enough to carpet — and stopped at the edge of the desk, where the lamplight could reach her face. She wanted Adela to see her face when she spoke. She wanted no possibility of being mistaken for an employee.

"I carried your letters for four years," she said. "I drove you through the fog to Holloway gates at five in the morning. I memorized what you told me to burn and I burned it and I never asked. When Mr. Crane's men searched the motorcar in March I sat on the satchel and lied to a magistrate's clerk with my heartbeat in my ears, and you told me afterward I had done well, and I lived on that for a week." She drew a breath. The words were coming faster now, and she let them; she had rehearsed none of this, and rehearsal would have ruined it. "I have earned one question. One. And I am going to ask it, and you are going to answer it, and then you may decide what I am to you."

Adela looked up. In the lamplight her eyes were the colour of tea left standing, and there was something in them Marguerite had never seen there before — not fear exactly, but the vigilance of a creature that has run so long it has forgotten any other posture.

"Ask it," Adela said.

"The child at your door this morning," Marguerite said. "Whose handwriting was on the note?"

For the first time in four years, Adela Crane looked away from her.

* * *

It was a small motion, the turning of the head, a few degrees toward the dark window and the rain. But Marguerite felt it like a door slamming. She had spent four years learning this woman the way one learns a language — by immersion, by necessity, in the dark — and every inflection had a meaning. The level voice meant control. The folded hands meant control. The straight spine meant control. But the turned face — the turned face meant shame, and shame in Adela Crane was a country no map had ever charted.

"I recognized it," Marguerite pressed on, softly now, because softness was worse than accusation and she knew it. "Left slant, heavy pressure, the way you cross your t's twice when you're writing fast. I've burned forty of your pencil letters. I know your hand better than your husband does. 'Forget the face. The debt is paid.'" She recited it flatly, without drama, the way one recites evidence. "You wrote that note, Mrs. Crane. You wrote it to yourself, or to God, or to whoever keeps the ledger you believe in. And you left it pinned to a baby."

"Don't." The word came out cracked down its middle. Adela recovered it at once, smoothed it, set it back in its case. "Don't take that tone with me, Miss Sterling. You are twenty-six years old and you drive a motorcar. You do not know what a life costs."

"Then teach me," Marguerite said. "That is all I'm asking. Teach me the sum. Because I've been doing the figures all day and they don't come out. A woman leaves a child at a door in the rain with instructions to forget her own daughter's face —"

"— How do you know it's a daughter?"

The question dropped between them like a coin on stone.

Marguerite went still. "I didn't," she said slowly. "Until you said so."

Adela closed her eyes. When she opened them again the cliff-face had a crack running through it, and behind the crack was not weakness, exactly, but something older and more terrible: exhaustion. Four years' worth. Perhaps nine years' worth. Marguerite watched it surface like something drowning coming up through dark water, and understood that she was seeing, for the first and only time, the whole of Adela Crane — the private ruin beneath the public monument.

"Sit down," Adela said.

"I'll stand."

"Sit *down*, Marguerite." And it was the first time in four years the woman had used her Christian name, and so she sat.

* * *

Adela rose and walked to the window. Rain crawled down the glass in ropes. She stood with her back to the room, and when she began to speak, she spoke to the garden, in the voice she used for dictation — measured, economical, each sentence a parcel tied and sealed.

"In 1904 I went abroad. You will have heard the official version. My health. A lung complaint. Switzerland, then Italy. Nine months, and then I came home thin and spiritual and full of mountain air, and everyone said how much better I looked, and my husband told his colleagues I had been restored to him." A pause. "I was not ill. I was pregnant."

The fire shifted in the grate. Neither woman looked at it.

"The father is dead now, and I will not give you his name, and you will not ask. He was — he mattered once, which is more than most men manage. It doesn't signify. What signifies is the arithmetic, and I will come to that. We stayed in a village above Lake Lugano. A widow took me in. The birth was — " her voice did not waver, but it thinned, like ice thinning over water, "— difficult. Eleven days I did not get up. And when I did get up, there was a person in the room with me. A small person with opinions and red fists and my brother Thomas's exact chin, God rest him, and I looked at her and felt nothing. Do you understand? Nothing. I waited to feel the great thing the novels promise, and it did not come, and I thought: it will come tomorrow. And tomorrow. And I loved her the way one loves a country one has never visited — abstractly, dutifully, from a distance of nine hundred miles that I had chosen myself."

"And Edith," Marguerite said quietly. "'E.'"

"My sister came out to Lugano when my month was nearly come. She arranged everything. The widow, the doctor, the story. She took the child to her own house in Brighton and passed her off — God help us all — as a foundling taken in from charity, which is a lie with a long tradition among our class. Edith wanted children. Edith got none. For eight years she raised my daughter as her niece, and wrote to me in pencil, because we agreed that everything between us should be burnable." Adela's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "You noticed the pencil. Of course you noticed the pencil. You notice everything; it is why I chose you, and why I am telling you this now, which I swear to you I swore I would never do."

"Why now, then?" Marguerite asked. "Why did Edith send her back?"

The silence that followed was long enough that Marguerite counted the clock in the hall striking the quarter-hour. Then Adela turned from the window, and her face had reassembled itself — the crack sealed over, the cliff restored — but her hands, when she folded them again, folded too carefully, the way one splints a broken finger.

"Edith's husband died in June. There were debts he had concealed. The house in Brighton goes next month. She cannot keep a growing girl on what remains, and she will not go to relatives, because relatives talk, and a girl of eight asks questions that a girl in a basket does not." Adela's voice remained perfectly level. "So she brought her to the one address in London where a child can be left and lost in the same hour. And left the note. Which I did not write, Marguerite, whatever your clever eye believes — Edith wrote the note. But it is my hand she copied, because she knew that when you saw it, you would say nothing. She gambled on your discretion. She won."

"She gambled wrong," Marguerite said. "Because here I am, asking."

"Here you are." Adela returned to the desk and stood behind her chair, both hands upon its back, as though it were a lectern or a rail. "And now I will give you the arithmetic, since you demanded the sum. Listen closely, because I will only say it once. Herbert — my husband — sits on the bench that sentences your friend Tom Ainsley's comrades. If this becomes known, it does not ruin me alone. It ruins the bill. It ruins the amnesty campaign. It puts every woman in that workshop within reach of a Home Office that is hunting us already, and gives Calloway's people a lever long enough to move the whole movement. One child, Marguerite. One child, known to three living people, against ten thousand women who will lose their meetings, their papers, their legal defence funds, in a single season." She paused. "You think I do not love her. You are wrong. I love her precisely as I love the cause — which is to say, I have weighed her, and I know her value, and I know what her price would purchase. That is not the absence of love. That is love with its eyes open."

"That is a balance-sheet," Marguerite said. Her own voice sounded far away to her, foreign, very cold. "You have described a balance-sheet."

"Yes," Adela said simply. "I have. And someone in this house must keep it, because the world does not forgive sentimental bookkeeping. Not in politics. Not to women. If a man did this, it would be called statecraft. If I did it, it will be called monstrosity, and the same newspapers that print my speeches will print my shame at breakfast, and my daughter will grow up under that name, and Herbert will put her in an institution before she is nine because he cannot abide a scandal he did not author." She leaned forward over the chair-back, and for one moment the lamplight showed Marguerite something raw underneath the arithmetic — a woman at the bottom of her own well, looking up. "The foundling hospital does not ask names. Its intake is a door that closes. By Christmas she will be registered, christened, apprenticed in fourteen years' time to respectable trade, and no one — not Edith, not Herbert, not you, not I — will be able to find her. That is not cruelty. That is the kindest wall I can build her."

Marguerite stood. Her knees were unsteady and she was ashamed of them, and so she made herself stand fully, squarely, the way she stood on the running-board of the motorcar in high wind.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "my mother put me on a train to Liverpool Street with two shillings and an address, and told my aunt I'd gone ahead on my own accord. I remember the platform. I remember her gloves. I did not see her face, because she turned away, and I spent eleven years deciding that meant she didn't love me." She picked her words up one at a time and laid them down like stones. "Your daughter will do that arithmetic too, Mrs. Crane. Children always do it eventually. They count backward from their own loneliness and the figures always come out the same: somebody chose the world over me. Your balance-sheet balances in London. It does not balance in that child."

Adela did not answer. The rain ran down the glass. Somewhere below, in the hall, the clock ticked on with the idiot patience of clocks, and upstairs — faintly, through floorboards and plaster — a child cried once, briefly, and subsided, and Marguerite watched the sound go through Adela Crane like a bayonet and come out the other side as nothing at all.

"You have your answer," Adela said at last, quietly. "By nightfall she goes to the hospital. Mrs. Prynne has the cab engaged for seven o'clock. You will not be part of it; I release you from that. Drive me to Parliament instead. There is a vote."

"There is always a vote," Marguerite said. She walked to the door and turned the key, and then, with her hand still on it, she turned back. "One more figure for your sum, Mrs. Crane. Free of charge. This morning I held her while Mrs. Prynne found clean linen. She stopped crying when I picked her up. She has your chin too."

She unlocked the door and went out, and did not hear whether anything followed her down the stairs, because the blood was too loud in her ears.

In the hall, Mrs. Prynne was waiting with her coat already buttoned, and her old face was grey. "Miss Sterling," she said, low. "There's a woman at the kitchen door. Won't give a name. Says she's family." A pause, and the house seemed to lean in around them. "Says she's come about the child — and she isn't leaving till she's seen her."
