## 6. Tom's Hands

The presses had a language, and Tom Ainsley spoke it the way other men spoke of weather or football. The Wharfedale in the corner said *clank-hiss, clank-hiss* when it was happy and something closer to a cough when the rollers wanted cleaning; the small hand-press by the window had a voice like a knuckle rapping twice on a door, asking to be let in. He had spent two years among these voices, two years with ink worked so far into the creases of his hands that no lye or pumice would ever fully fetch it out, and he had come to think of himself as a man made partly of type — sorts lodged under the skin, a comma scar on his left thumb where the knife had slipped his first winter. His father had wanted him in the shoe trade. His father was four years dead, and Tom had never once regretted choosing letters over leather.

That morning — the morning the child came — he was setting a column of the *Suffrage Worker*, fourteen ems wide, and doing it badly because Marguerite Sterling was crossing the workshop floor with a baby in her arms.

He set the word *justice* as *justcie* and did not notice.

She came in through the yard door with the rain still on her shoulders, and she held the bundle the way he had seen men hold things they meant to defend: not gently, exactly, but with a kind of ferocious care, both arms wrapped, chin dipped toward it as though she could stand between it and the whole gray weight of London. The workshop went quiet in stages — the boys at the guillotine first, then Mrs. Pank at the folding table, then old Hargreaves, who claimed he had gone deaf in the Boer War but somehow always heard gossip arriving. Even the Wharfedale seemed to hold its breath between strokes.

"What's this, then?" Hargreaves said, and Tom wanted to throw a quoin at him.

"Her name is on the note," Marguerite said, which was not an answer at all, and Tom watched her understand that even as she said it — watched her close her mouth around whatever else she'd been going to say, the way she always did, information going back behind her teeth like coin into a purse. She read everything they carried, everyone knew that, and told nothing. It made her valuable and slightly feared and, to Tom, unbearably beautiful, though he had never managed to say so in any arrangement of words that didn't sound, in his head, like a misprint.

"She'll catch her death," Mrs. Pank said, already rising, already unpinning her shawl, because there was a species of woman whose response to any crisis was linen.

"There's a crate by the stove," Marguerite said. "Line it with the proofing paper."

"The proofing paper's got print on it."

"Then she'll sleep under the news," Marguerite said, "like everybody else in this movement," and carried the child to the back room herself before anyone could ask another question.

Tom looked down at his composing stick. *Justcie*. He slid the sorts out one by one and began again, and thought: she will not give that baby to the Foundling Hospital. She has the face of a woman deciding not to obey, and I have seen that face every day for two years and never once tired of it.

* * *

The workshop on Rivington Street was, by daylight, an honest business. There was a sign in the window — JOB PRINTING DONE NEATLY, TERMS MODERATE — and for the most part the sign told the truth. They printed invoices for a shipping concern in Limehouse, programmes for a choral society in Islington, funeral cards for half the parish of St. Botolph's. Between the honest work, in the hours when only trusted hands were in the building, they printed other things. Pamphlets. Leaflets. And twice a month, on paper stock bought in three different counties, travel documents that would pass a casual glance at Dover or Harwich and, if examined closely by a man who knew his business, would fail — but the men they were made for were running from worse than a customs officer's frown.

Tom did the fine work. Forging was typesetting done at the limit of its skill, and he was good at it, though he kept the pride somewhere he couldn't see it, the way you keep money you're ashamed of. He had cut steel punches for the watermarks' blind stamps. He knew how many times a genuine railway pass had been folded by the average clerk, and he folded his counterfeits the same number of times, creased them with a bone folder, and rubbed a little graphite into the folds so they'd look travelled. It was, he sometimes thought at three in the morning, a strange way to earn a conscience. But then he'd remember the woman in Hull, the one the passes had carried across the Irish Sea ahead of her husband's brothers and their handcuffs, and the strangeness would settle into something like purpose.

The rumor had come round again that week — it came round every few weeks, on the breath of some volunteer who'd heard it from a cousin — that the Yard had a man inside one of the movement's houses. A listener. Nobody stopped work. That wasn't how it was done. But Hargreaves started burning his drafts instead of binning them, and Mrs. Pank locked the type cabinet with a key she slept in her corset, and Tom took to leaving the building each night by a different street, walking a slow square around the block before heading home to his lodgings in Clerkenwell. You learned to live with dread the way sailors lived with water: not without it, just practiced.

Into this atmosphere, on a morning of rain that fell with the patience of something that intended to go on all week, arrived a baby nobody would name, and Marguerite carrying her like a verdict.

* * *

At noon the workshop emptied for bread and cheese at the coffee house on the corner, and Tom stayed behind, ostensibly to clean the rollers. Actually to be alone with the fact that Marguerite had not come out of the back room for four hours except to fetch hot water, and had asked no one for help, and had accepted Mrs. Pank's shawl with a nod and nothing else, and had stood at the window with the child against her shoulder rocking in that slow metronome people seemed to know without being taught, and Tom had found he could not set a single line of type while she did it and had given up pretending.

When the others had gone he crossed the floor and knocked on the door frame with the back of his hand, softly, the way he'd knock on wood to keep off bad luck.

"It's me."

"I know who it is." Her voice was rough with the hour. "You've got ink on your knuckles. You always do. Come in if you're coming."

The back room had been a storeroom until the movement grew and needed somewhere for meetings too delicate for the main floor. Now it held two chairs, a table, a map of London with pins in it that Adela Crane alone was permitted to move, and a packing crate lined with printed proofs, in which the baby lay sleeping with her fists curled up beside her ears like a boxer waiting for the bell. On the proofs, upside down, Tom could read a fragment of the pamphlet they'd run Tuesday: *no taxation without representation* — and beneath the sleeping cheek of an infant maybe three weeks old, the word *VOTE* in twelve-point bold.

Marguerite sat with her back to the window. The light came through her wet hair and turned the edges of it copper. She looked, Tom thought, like a woman who had been awake since yesterday arguing with someone invisible and losing.

"Hospital by nightfall," she said, before he'd chosen a chair. "That's the order. Foundling Hospital, Guilford Street. They take them in through a revolving basket in the wall, did you know that? So nobody sees anybody. You ring, you put the child in, the basket turns, and a nurse writes down what she's wearing and that's the end of the record. Twenty thousand children through that wall and the mothers might all have been ghosts."

"You've read about it."

"I read everything." She said it flatly, and he heard the joke in it and the wound in it, lying side by side like two sorts in the same stick. "It's my one accomplishment."

"They don't take all of them," Tom said. "The hospital. There's a vetting. Character references for the mother, questions about the father—"

"I know how it works, Tom."

"—and the intake book is open to the governors, and one of the governors, I'm told at the printing trade dinners, is a Home Office man, because they like to know what becomes of babies who arrive without histories. Babies are very useful to departments. They grow into workers, soldiers, witnesses. A baby with no history is a blank form, and governments love blank forms."

She turned her head slowly and looked at him the way she looked at a letter before breaking the seal.

"Why do you know that?"

"Because I set a job for the governors' annual dinner in March. Menus, place cards. I listen to printers' talk the way you listen to letters. Same crime, different tools."

The baby stirred and settled. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere out front the Wharfedale's iron had ticked as it cooled, a sound like a clock winding down.

"What are you proposing?" Marguerite said.

"That we don't use the basket in the wall." Tom crouched by the crate, not touching the child — his hands were always dirty; it felt wrong to touch anything that clean with them — but studying her, the tiny blue-veined lids, the milk-drunk slackness of her mouth. "A private placement. A family that wants a child and asks no questions. There are such families. There's a widow in Kent — sister-in-law of a man who does our paper stock, a decent woman, lost her own girl to scarlet fever, talks about it the way other widows talk about husbands. She'd take this one tonight if I sent the wire."

"And the papers? A private placement wants papers. Who she is, who gave her up, permission—" Marguerite stopped. He watched her get there ahead of him, because she was quick, quicker than she liked anyone to know. "Oh," she said quietly. "You'd forge them."

"I'd write them," Tom said. "There's a difference. Forging copies what exists. This doesn't exist yet. I'd be making true documents for a false history, which is either worse or better than my usual work, I genuinely don't know." He flexed his fingers; ink cracked faintly in the creases. "A certificate of placement, signed by a midwife who doesn't exist. A letter of introduction naming her aunt. A christening record if the widow wants one — churches don't check much, God being presumed to prefer mercy to paperwork. Three days' work. Two, if the rain keeps everyone indoors."

Marguerite was silent long enough that the rain became very loud.

"This isn't your order to countermand," she said at last. "Adela said hospital. If it goes wrong — if it's traced —"

"If it goes through the hospital it's traced the moment it's born into that book. Blank form, Home Office governor, and somewhere down the years a clerk with a good memory and a bad disposition." He paused. "And there's the other thing. Whoever left her at the door wrote that note. 'Forget the face.' Someone wants her erased, Marguerite. The hospital erases beautifully. But erasure can also be arranged from outside. A child in a register is a child that can be found by whoever comes looking. A child with a false aunt in Kent is a child who's been moved the way we move everything else in this house — quickly, quietly, and ahead of the police."

"That's treasonous logic for a baby."

"Everything in this house is treasonous logic. We print seditious pamphlets next to hymnals. Why should the Almighty get the better stock?"

And then she laughed — a short, low laugh, surprised out of her, the first he'd heard from her in days, maybe weeks — and something in Tom's chest turned over with the heavy, familiar clumsiness of a press taking its first pull of the morning. He loved her. He had loved her since the first winter, since the night she'd waited in the freezing yard with the engine running so Hargreaves could warm his hands between deliveries, since he'd watched her memorize six lines of a letter and drop it in the stove and recite those lines perfectly eleven days later to a woman in Hackney who wept at hearing them. He loved her stubbornness the way he loved good paper stock: for its weight, its grain, its refusal to tear along the lines you expected. He had never said it. He suspected he never would, because Marguerite Sterling measured her life in what fit in a satchel, and he had no idea how to fold himself small enough to be carried.

"I'll ask her," Marguerite said. "Adela. Not today — today she's not to be approached by anyone, least of all on this. But I'll find the hour." She looked down at the child, and her voice changed, dropped into something private. "She was left with instructions to be forgotten, Tom. Somebody wrote that with a steady hand and meant it. I can't make myself agree that a basket in a wall is mercy."

"No," Tom said. "I didn't think you could."

The baby opened her eyes then — they were dark, unfixed, aimed at nothing and therefore at everything — and regarded the underside of a pamphlet with what Tom chose to interpret as contempt. He put his hand flat on the edge of the crate, an inch from her blanket, careful of the ink.

"What would we call her," he said, "on the papers?"

Marguerite was quiet again. Out front, the yard door banged; voices; Mrs. Pank returning with the others, the world filing back in.

"We'll need a name that opens doors," she said. "Something respectable. Something dull." And then, softer, almost to herself: "We'll think of something. We have until nightfall."

* * *

Except they did not have until nightfall, because at half past two a cab drew up outside the Rivington Street premises, and a man got out of it who did not look like a policeman — policemen never did, the dangerous ones — in a neat coat and a bowler with the rain still standing on it in beads, and stood a moment reading the sign in the window as though considering having some printing done.

Hargreaves saw him first and swore with great fluency and no volume at all.

"License inspection," the man said pleasantly, when the door was opened to him. "Home Office. Mr. Calloway. Just routine, I'm sure."

Tom, at his press, kept pulling proofs with hands that did not shake, and thought of the mailing list upstairs, and of every letter Marguerite had ever carried, and understood with perfect clarity that routine was the most frightening word in the English language.

Behind the closed door of the back room, a baby slept under the word *VOTE*.
