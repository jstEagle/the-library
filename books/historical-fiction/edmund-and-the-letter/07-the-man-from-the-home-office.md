## 7. The Man from the Home Office

The fog came up the river on Tuesday and did not leave until Friday, and it was on Wednesday of that grey, unwashed week that Mr. Calloway first came to Rivington Street.

Marguerite saw him before he saw himself, if such a thing can be said — she saw him in the way he moved, which was not the way policemen moved. Policemen walked as though the pavement owed them something. This man drifted. He was tall without being imposing, forty perhaps, with a face like a well-read book: nothing startling on any single page, but a great deal accumulated between them. He wore a good coat, dark grey, brushed but not new, and he carried an umbrella furled though no rain had fallen since morning. He stopped at the workshop door and consulted a card in his hand as though confirming an appointment he already knew by heart.

She was at the window of the flat above the print shop, where Mrs. Quill kept the tea things and the ledger and the tin box of emergency money taped beneath the floorboard under the dresser. Marguerite had come to collect Adela's correspondence — two letters had arrived by the morning post, and one of them bore the Home Office franking that made her stomach tighten before she'd even broken the seal — and she had been standing at the window doing what she always did when she stood at windows: reading the street.

She read him now. Grey coat. Umbrella dry. Card consulted once, then put away with the small satisfaction of a man who has confirmed his own memory is reliable. And then he looked up — directly up, not at the shopfront but at the window, at her — and inclined his head a fraction, the way one gentleman acknowledges another across a club room.

Her skin went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

* * *

By the time she reached the street he had knocked, and Tom had opened the door, and the two men were shaking hands with the elaborate courtesy of strangers who are not strangers at all.

"—Calloway," the man was saying. "Home Office. It's about the printing license, I'm afraid. A matter of some paperwork." He smiled. It was a good smile, warm even, the smile of a man who liked people and was genuinely sorry for whatever it was his work required of him. "I do hope I haven't caught you at a bad hour."

Tom's hands were ink-stained to the second knuckle, as they always were, and Marguerite watched him fold them behind his back with a deliberateness that would have been invisible to anyone who didn't know those hands. "We're always at a bad hour," Tom said pleasantly. "That's printing. Come in, sir, mind the type cases."

The workshop smelled of ink and hot metal and the paraffin stove Mrs. Quill insisted on even in October, and underneath all of it the ghost-smell of the coffee they drank at all hours because sleep was a luxury the movement could not afford. Four volunteers sat at the long table, setting type by lamplight: two girls Marguerite knew only as Ruth and Sally, a boy called Ned who was all elbows and devotion, and old Mr. Prynne, who had set type for thirty years and claimed he could read a page through his fingertips like a blind man reading Braille. They looked up when Calloway entered, and Marguerite watched their faces do what faces do — the quick flicker, the too-careful return to work, the studied indifference that is itself a kind of confession.

Calloway noticed everything. That was the horror of him. He noticed it the way a lover notices — attentively, tenderly, missing nothing — and Marguerite felt the violation of it in her chest before she understood its shape.

"Charming premises," he said, turning slowly. His gaze crossed the press — the old Albion, second-hand, bought with money whose provenance three separate people had lied about — and lingered there a moment with the appreciation of a connoisseur. "You don't see many of these still working. Most shops have gone over to the cylinder machines." He touched the frame of the press with two fingers, lightly, the way one touches a horse's neck. "This one's got history in it."

"It's got ink in it," Tom said. "History's extra."

Calloway laughed — a real laugh, unforced — and Marguerite felt something turn over inside her, because she recognized the technique now, had seen it used by the best salesmen and the best priests: make them like you first. Make them forget, for just a moment, that liking you is not the same as trusting you.

"Mr.—?" He produced a notebook. Not a police notebook, nothing so crude; a slim leather thing, private-looking, almost intimate.

"Ainsley."

"Ainsley." He wrote it down. "And you hold the license?"

"The license is in the name of the firm. Prynne & Co., jobbing printers. Bills of lading, funeral cards, theatre programmes." Tom gestured at a shelf where samples stood in a row: a christening card, a menu for the Clapham Constitutional Club, a trade circular for a boot manufacturer. All genuine. All printed here, between the pamphlets, between the passes. The cover business was real enough to survive most inspections, because it was not entirely a cover; they did take honest work, and honest work paid the rent that dishonest work could not.

Calloway examined the samples with the air of a man buying a hat. "Funeral cards," he murmured. "Very respectable. And the license itself — may I?"

Tom fetched it from the drawer beneath the ledger, and Marguerite's heart performed a slow, deliberate somersault, because the drawer also held the mailing list — or had held it, until Tuesday night, when she herself had carried it out in the satchel and locked it in Adela's desk in Cavendish Square. But Calloway did not know that. Calloway looked at the license, copied the number into his leather notebook, and handed it back with thanks.

Then he said, mildly, conversationally, the way a man remarks on the weather: "You do a fair volume of circular work, I should think. Mailing lists and so forth. Addresses are half the trade these days — knowing who wants what."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above them a cart rattled past on the wet cobbles.

"We keep our clients' particulars confidential," Tom said. "Same as a doctor."

"Oh, absolutely. Quite right." Calloway closed the notebook. "I'm not asking to see anything, Mr. Ainsley. I want to be clear about that." He smiled again, and this time the warmth in it was worse than coldness, because it was patient. "I'm a paperwork man, you understand. My whole career is paperwork. And paperwork has this quality — it accumulates. Quietly. In drawers. In satchels." His eyes moved, briefly, to Marguerite, standing by the door with the empty canvas bag over her shoulder, and then away, courteous, incurious. "One day someone opens a drawer and finds that ten years of ordinary life has become evidence. It's nobody's fault. Paperwork doesn't intend anything. It just waits."

Nobody breathed. Old Prynne's fingers had gone still on his type stick, and Marguerite saw the old man's knuckles whiten around the lead.

"Well," Calloway said briskly, as though the visit had been entirely social. "I'll want the renewal form filed by the end of the month — standard business, my secretary will send the papers. And—" he paused at the door, umbrella in hand, and turned back with the expression of a man remembering a kindness "—Mrs. Crane sends her regards, I believe? She's been ill, I hear. The cause loses so much when its leaders are laid low." He shook his head gently. "Give her my best wishes. Mr. Calloway. She'll find the name in the file, if she ever needs it."

He tipped his hat to the room — to each of them individually, Marguerite noted, a small bow for Ruth, a nod for Sally, a glance at Ned that made the boy flush scarlet — and let himself out into the fog.

* * *

For a full minute after the door closed, nobody spoke. Then Ned said, "He knew about Mrs. Crane," in a voice pitched high with alarm, and Ruth said, "They all know about Mrs. Crane, you idiot, she's in the newspapers," and the argument started, and Marguerite stood by the door with the satchel against her hip and let the argument wash over her while she thought.

*He never asked to see the list.* That was the sentence that kept returning, turning itself over like a coin under a thumb. *He never asked, because he didn't need to ask. You show a man your empty hands only when you want him to believe they're empty.*

She had carried letters for two years. Hundreds of them. Letters to MPs and editors, to prison governors and solicitors, to women in Manchester and Glasgow and a dozen towns whose names she knew now by their postmarks alone. She had read every one of them by lamplight in doorways and parked motorcars, memorizing what must be burned, burning what must be burned, and she had told herself all this while that she was a courier, a servant of the cause, a necessary instrument — that the paper passed through her hands the way water passes through a pipe, touching nothing, staining nothing.

But paper waits. Calloway had said it with the tenderness of a man describing a disease he had spent his life studying. Every letter she had ever delivered existed somewhere — in a recipient's drawer, in a sender's copybook, in the memory of a postman, in the carbon flimsy of a typist who talked in her sleep. Two years of her careful, secret, intimate reading was not discretion. It was complicity, itemized. If Calloway opened the right drawer, he would not find Adela Crane. He would find *her* — the woman seen leaving No. 14 Cavendish Square every Thursday with the canvas satchel, the woman whose face a constable in Brighton might remember, whose name a landlady in Croydon had taken down in a lodging register because the law required it.

She had believed she was carrying secrets. She understood now that she was carrying evidence, and that the difference between the two was only whether the man with the notebook had arrived yet.

"Marguerite." Tom's voice, low, beside her. The others had drifted back to their type, the argument subsiding into the particular silence of people agreeing not to be afraid in front of each other. "You've gone white as a galley proof."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. What did he—"

"He didn't want anything," she said. "That's what frightens me. Men who want something you can satisfy. Men who want *nothing*—" She stopped, because the true sentence was forming and she did not want to say it aloud in a room with four pairs of ears. "I have to get these letters to Cavendish Square. Mrs. Crane will want to hear about this within the hour."

"I'll walk you to the car."

"There's no need."

"There's every need," Tom said quietly, "and you know it as well as I do," and she did know it, and so she let him fetch his coat.

* * *

The fog had thickened toward evening, pressing against the lamps in soft grey fists, and the little Hupmobile crawled east through it with Tom walking beside the running board — not touching her, not taking her arm, simply present, a shape in the murk that any watcher would have to account for. They did not speak until they reached Gray's Inn Road, and then Tom said, without preamble:

"He was counting."

"What?"

"The girls. Ruth and Sally. When he bowed at the door. He looked at each of them a moment too long — long enough to fix the face. I've seen men do it at the races, memorizing horses." He was quiet a moment. "He wasn't inspecting the license, Marguerite. He was building a picture of the room. He'll go home tonight and write down four names he doesn't have yet, and next month, when the renewal papers come, there'll be five questions where there used to be none."

She gripped the wheel. The fog swallowed the lamp ahead of them and gave it back, swallowed and gave back, like a pulse. "The mailing list," she said. "It's out of the building. I took it Tuesday. But the letters—" Her voice did something she hated, thinned and frayed. "Tom, the letters I carry. If they're keeping copies anywhere — if anyone keeps copies —"

"Adela burns hers."

"Adela burns *hers*. But a letter has two ends." She heard it leave her mouth and heard, a half-second later, what she had actually said — the thing she had known for two years and never once articulated, the structural truth of her whole secret life: *every letter I deliver is a thread, and someone at the far end holds the spool.* "Some of those women keep everything. Corroboration, they call it. For the history books. And history books get subpoenaed."

Tom was silent for several steps. The fog beaded on his hair, on his shoulders; he looked like a figure half-erased.

"Then we burn both ends," he said at last. "Starting tonight. I can have warnings out by the morning post — coded, nothing on paper that means anything on its own. 'Renew your subscription.' 'The winter stock is short.' The women will understand."

"They'll panic."

"Some of them will panic," Tom agreed. "Panic burns paper faster than caution does. Let them."

She glanced at him — a quick look, there and gone, the kind of look you give a fire. Two years she had loved him in the wordless way you love a comrade, which is to say without admitting it, without permitting it, filing it under a name it could not bear. And now here he was in the fog proposing arson as a kindness, and she found that her love had grown a specific face and a specific voice and a specific terrible competence, and that this made it infinitely more dangerous than it had been at breakfast.

"Tom," she said. "If they raid this place—"

"They won't. Not yet. Calloway's a paperwork man, you said it yourself. Paperwork men wait until the file is complete." He said it steadily, but she saw his hand close briefly around the strap of his own collar, a small unconscious violence, the body confessing what the voice withheld. "Besides, they'd need a reason. A warrant. Something concrete."

*Something concrete.* She thought of the satchel on the seat beside her, holding two letters, one of them franked by the Home Office itself. She thought of the pencil-signed letters she memorized and burned, week after week, and wondered — for the first time, with a cold sliding sensation, like ice shifting underfoot — how many other hands had held those same pages before they reached her. How many clerks, how many secretaries, how many lovers and sisters and servants. A secret is only as small as the number of people keeping it, and Adela's secret, whatever it was, had begun to feel less like a secret and more like a city, vast and lit and full of doors.

They reached Cavendish Square as the lamps were being lit, haloes of orange trembling in the wet air. Tom stopped at the edge of the square, at the boundary where his world ended and Adela's began.

"I'll post the warnings tonight," he said. "And Marguerite — the renewal papers. When they come, bring them to me before anyone signs anything. I want to see what questions a Home Office man asks on paper. A man's questions tell you what he already knows."

"And what does Calloway already know?"

Tom looked back the way they had come, into the fog-filled street, at nothing visible at all.

"That we exist," he said. "Which is more than he knew last week. And less than he'll know next week, unless we're faster than he is." He touched the bonnet of the car, once, lightly — a gesture so like Calloway's touch upon the press that it startled her — and stepped back into the murk. "Go on. She'll be waiting. And Marguerite—" he paused, and for a moment the strategist fell away and it was only Tom, ink-stained and earnest, twenty-six years old and frightened "—whatever's in that satchel tonight. Read it twice."

She drove the last hundred yards alone, tires hissing on the wet stone, and let herself into the house with the key she was not supposed to have, and found the drawing-room empty and the desk unlocked, which was unlike Adela, and on the blotter, weighted beneath the paperknife, a single sheet in that unmistakable upright pencil-hand:

*Burn this after reading.*

Marguerite stood very still. Outside, the fog pressed at the windows like a listener. She thought of Calloway's leather notebook, closing softly, softly, on four names he did not yet have. She thought of paper, waiting patiently in drawers, accumulating.

Then she picked up the page, and began to read.
