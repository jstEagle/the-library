## 8. Edith Arrives

The letter on the blotter was short. That was the first thing Marguerite noticed — that Adela Crane, who could fill four pages with the machinery of a single argument, who could make a paragraph do the work of an army, had written only six lines.

*You will have questions. I will not answer them. The child goes to the Foundling Hospital before nightfall. Do not name her to anyone. Do not let anyone see her face longer than is necessary. Some things are not secrets because they are shameful; they are secrets because shame is a fire and the cause is made of paper.*

*Burn this after reading.*

Marguerite read it three times. The fog ticked against the glass. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, the house breathing in its sleep, and she was aware of herself standing in the empty drawing-room with her gloves still on and her hat still dripping rain onto the carpet, holding a page that weighed nothing at all and yet pulled downward like a stone dropped into deep water.

*Shame is a fire and the cause is made of paper.* It was the sort of line Adela said aloud at meetings, and the room would go quiet, and women would look at their hands as if checking them for ink. But written here, in pencil, in the private upright hand that meant destruction, it sounded less like rhetoric and more like arithmetic. Adela had counted what a child cost, and had found the sum payable.

Marguerite carried the letter to the hearth. The coals were grey with ash but still alive underneath; she laid the paper among them and watched the edges curl, watched the words go brown then black then gone — *Foundling* vanishing first, she thought, or perhaps *nightfall*, she could not be sure, the flames were quick and did not distinguish. She stirred the ash with the poker until there was nothing legible, which was a skill she had, one of the skills she had, and stood back with her hands trembling only slightly.

She had burned hundreds of letters by now. She had never once burned one so easily obeyed.

Upstairs, the child slept in the laundry basket lined with a shawl, in the narrow room behind the servants' stair where they had put her at dawn — not the nursery, God forbid, not any room with a name that suggested permanence. She slept with the whole-hearted abandon of the very young, one fist curled against her cheek, her breath making a small weather of its own. Marguerite looked down at her for a long moment and thought: *I know your mother's handwriting better than I will ever know your face.*

Then she went down to the kitchen to wait, because Adela had said the girl from the hospital would come by evening, and waiting was the other thing Marguerite knew how to do.

* * *

Adela came back at four, smelling of fog and coal-smoke and the particular metallic tang of public halls, and did not ask how the morning had gone, which was itself an answer. She hung up her coat with the efficiency of a woman putting away a season of her life and said, "Has it fed?"

"Twice. Mrs. Dill warmed milk over the kettle."

"Good." And then, as if remembering that gratitude was a currency worth spending even here: "You've been steady about this, Miss Sterling. I won't forget it."

There was no time to answer, because the bell went.

Not the front bell — the side bell, the tradesmen's door, rung once, sharply, with the confidence of someone who had rung bells all her life and expected doors to open as a matter of course. Mrs. Dill wiped her hands and went, and Marguerite heard the door open, and heard the voice that came through it before she heard anything else, because the voice carried the way certain voices carry, trained or born to it, filling the kitchen passage like cold air finding the gap under a door.

"I am Mrs. Harcourt-Vane. My sister is at home. Do not trouble to announce me; I can find my own way."

Marguerite had read Edith's name exactly once, in the letter addressed to *E*, and had burned it within the minute, and had told herself the burning was sufficient. Now she understood that some letters are not finished when they are burned. This one had walked out of its own ashes and up the garden path.

Adela had gone very still by the dresser. For a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — Marguerite saw something pass across her employer's face that she had never seen there, not during arrests, not during Calloway's visit, not once in two years: it was fear, but fear of a specific and domestic shape, the fear of a woman hearing her sister's voice in her own kitchen.

"You," Adela said quietly, without looking at Marguerite, "will take her into the drawing-room. You will keep her there. Whatever she asks, you do not know. Whoever she threatens, you are not frightened. Can you do that?"

It was not a request, and it was also — Marguerite understood this in the same instant — a test, though of what, precisely, Adela could not have said, because Adela herself could not have said what the child was worth. Marguerite took off her apron. She noticed herself doing it: folding it, setting it on the dresser, smoothing her hair in the little mirror beside the clock. Rituals of composure. Armour assembled in four seconds.

"Yes," she said. "Go up. Take the back stair."

Adela went. Her footsteps on the uncarpeted treads were entirely silent; it was extraordinary, Marguerite thought, how quietly that woman could move when she wished to disappear inside her own house.

* * *

Edith Harcourt-Vane was standing in the middle of the drawing-room when Marguerite entered, having let herself in, as promised, and she was examining the room the way a customs officer examines luggage — the framed photographs turned slightly to read them better, the cushioned chairs assessed for comfort, the whole interior priced and found wanting. She was forty perhaps, dressed in dove grey with sable at the throat, and she was handsome in the way Adela was handsome, the same bones, the same level dark brows — but where Adela's face was built for conviction, Edith's had been finished, somewhere along the way, for grievance.

"You're not my sister," Edith said, turning.

"No, madam. I'm Miss Sterling. Mrs. Crane's driver and secretary."

"Her driver." Edith's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "How modern. Does she still call it *the cause*, or has she moved on to some newer phrase? She does love a phrase." She removed one glove, finger by finger, unhurried. "You may tell her I've come about the child."

"I'm afraid I don't know anything about a child, Mrs. Harcourt-Vane."

And there it was — the lie, out in the air between them, smooth and complete, delivered in Marguerite's own ordinary voice as if it were an ordinary thing. She felt it leave her like a swallowed coin falling free: a strange lightness, and immediately beneath the lightness a nausea, slow and rising, because she had never said it before, a sentence that was simply not true, whole cloth invented in the moment, and it had come out of her *fluently*, without a seam.

Edith looked at her. It was a long look, appraising, and Marguerite understood that she was being weighed — not for truth, which Edith could not test, but for quality. Whether she was the sort of servant who broke, and how quickly, and how loudly.

"No?" Edith said. "How curious. A child abandoned at this house at dawn, in the rain. The midwife who attended the birth lives four streets from here and has a cousin who cleans silver in this parish. In London, Miss Sterling, everything arrives eventually at everyone. News is the one thing this city manufactures faster than lies." She sat, at last, choosing the chair nearest the cold fireplace, and arranged her skirt across her knees with a small precise violence. "Now. I will ask you again, and this time I will ask you to consider your position. Where is my sister's daughter?"

*Your sister's daughter.* So Edith knew. Had always known, perhaps — had known abroad, had known at the birth two years ago, had been the *E* to whom debts were owed. The letter in the satchel came back to Marguerite entire, every word of it, because that was the curse of her memory, that nothing burned in her mind as easily as paper burned in a grate: *the debt is acknowledged and I hope soon discharged.* Two years of discharge. And now the debt had come home wearing sable.

"I think you should sit down while I fetch you some tea—" Marguerite began, and stopped, because Edith laughed.

"Oh, don't *fetch*. Fetching is for women who don't matter. Listen to me instead." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped into a register that was intimate and cold, a voice for small rooms and large demands. "My sister has spent fifteen years telling England how to live. She lectures on purity. On duty. On the sacred obligations of motherhood — oh yes, she gives that lecture, I've sat in the hall and listened to her give it, third row, applauding. Meanwhile there exists a child with her eyes who cannot be spoken of, and a husband who pays men to follow her carriage, and a movement that would eat her alive by Thursday if the story reached the papers. I am not a vindictive woman, Miss Sterling. I am a patient one. There is a difference, and the difference is that vindictive people strike first and patient people strike correctly." She drew off the second glove. "I want to see the child. I want to satisfy myself that she is well and properly kept. And then I want assurances. If I am refused, I shall write first to my brother-in-law — who will believe me instantly, because he has spent years hoping for exactly this post — and then, if he is too slow, which he may be, to the editors. Mr. Marsden at the *Standard* owes me a favour. Several favours."

Every word landed. Marguerite could feel each one landing, like coins counted out onto a table. And beneath the counting she could feel the other thing, the sick thrill of it, because this was the moment the whole architecture became visible: Adela's marriage, Adela's platform, Adela's fifteen years of phrases — all of it standing on a floor of paper, and Edith holding the match.

She thought of the child asleep behind the servants' stair. She thought of Tom, this morning, saying *we'll move the type by Friday* with ink on his knuckles. She thought of Calloway's notebook closing softly on four names.

And then she heard herself speak.

"If you write to Mr. Marsden," Marguerite said, calmly, folding her hands, "he will print nothing."

Edith's brows rose a quarter-inch. Encouraged, or merely surprised into stillness.

"The *Standard* printed Mrs. Crane's open letter on prison feeding in March," Marguerite continued, and her voice went on without her, fluent, terrible, sourcing itself from two years of memorized correspondence, from every letter she had ever read and burned, "and retracted nothing. He has met her, madam. He respects her, in the way journalists respect the people they most regret respecting. If you send him this story, he will sit on it for six months and use it as leverage against someone else entirely, and you will have spent your only weapon for nothing. As for your brother-in-law—" she paused, delicately, precisely, "—do you imagine he doesn't already suspect? A man who hires men to watch his wife's carriage learns to watch for other things. Your letter would not shock him. It would *relieve* him. It would confirm the story he tells himself. And then the divorce would be his doing, in the papers, in his own words, with himself cast as the wounded party — and your sister would walk out of that wreckage more dangerous than ever, because martyrs raise money, Mrs. Harcourt-Vane, and prisons fill halls, and scandal fills them faster. You would make her career. You would make her *immortal*. Is that what you want?"

Silence. The clock on the mantel ticked through four full seconds, five, and Marguerite stood inside the silence feeling it hold her weight — and feeling, beneath the steadiness, the sickness rise another inch, sweet and foul at once, because none of what she had said was calculated invention exactly; it was true, or near-true, assembled out of the true things she had read and burned, and that was somehow worse than lying. She had lied fluently. She had lied *well*. She had discovered, at thirty-one, in a drawing-room that smelled of cold ashes, that she had a talent for it.

Edith regarded her for a long time.

"Well," she said at last, softly. "Well. She keeps better staff than I gave her credit for." She began, unhurriedly, to draw her glove back on. "Very well, Miss Sterling. You have delayed me. You have not deterred me — learn the difference; it will serve you. I will remain in London. I will call again." She rose, smoothing her skirt. "Tell my sister: patience is my virtue, and I have exercised it for two years at considerable expense. Tell her the account is open, and I intend to close it while I still recognise the child as mine to claim. Every day she delays adds a condition. Today I wanted only to see the girl. Tomorrow I shall want more."

She collected her muff from the chair-back, crossed to the door, and paused there with her hand on the frame — and for one moment the grievance drained out of her face and left something older underneath, something tired and nearly human, and she said, in a different voice entirely:

"She was born in February. During a hard frost. My sister held her for eleven days and did not sleep for the last three, and when they took her away Adela did not cry, and I have hated her since for not crying, because I did." Then the shutters came down, glove snapped at the wrist, chin lifted. "Good afternoon, Miss Sterling. You will be seeing me again."

The front door closed. The bell above it shivered and stilled.

* * *

Marguerite stood alone in the drawing-room and discovered that her knees were unreliable. She sat down abruptly in the chair Edith had vacated — it was still warm, which she resented — and pressed the backs of her hands against her mouth and breathed.

*Fluent.* The word kept ringing. She had done it without rehearsal, without a tremor, had dismantled a blackmail threat the way she folded letters, along the established creases, and the thrill of it was still singing in her blood like the first sip of wine — and underneath the singing, the sickness, spreading. Because a talent like that did not arrive from nowhere. It arrived from practice, from environment, from two years of measuring her life in other people's secrets until deception had soaked into her hands the way ink soaked into a compositor's, invisible, permanent, part of the skin.

She got up. She went up the back stair, quiet as Adela, quiet as guilt, and opened the door to the narrow room.

The child was awake. Not crying — awake, watching the ceiling with the grave attention of infancy, fists working at the air as if trying to take hold of something just above her. When Marguerite's shadow crossed her, the small face turned toward it, and the eyes — grey, Adela's grey, unmistakably — fixed on her with total, unguarded trust.

Marguerite picked her up. It seemed suddenly unbearable not to.

"There is a woman downstairs," she told her, very low, "who says she is yours to claim. And a woman downstairs from her"— meaning Adela, meaning the floors between — "who says you must vanish by nightfall. And I—" Her voice caught. She pressed her lips to the downy crown of the child's head and started again. "And I told my first lie today, and I was good at it, and I don't know yet whether that saves you or damns us both."

The child seized a fold of her collar and held on, surprisingly strong, the way infants hold — with the whole hand, with the whole self, as though the world were a thing to be gripped and kept.

Behind Marguerite, in the doorway, a floorboard creaked.

She turned, the child still in her arms — and Adela was standing there at the top of the back stair, silent as she had risen, silent as smoke, and her face was pale and composed and completely unreadable, and in her hand she held a small travelling case that Marguerite had never seen before.

"Not the hospital," Adela said quietly. "Not tonight. Things have changed. Come downstairs — both of you. There is something I need you to hear, Miss Sterling, before you decide whose side of this story you are on."
