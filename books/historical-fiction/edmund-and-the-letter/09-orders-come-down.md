## 9. Orders Come Down

The summons came at half past six in the morning, delivered by a boy on a bicycle who did not dismount but flung a folded scrap of paper against the window of Marguerite's lodgings like a man feeding a fire. She heard it strike the glass and lay still a moment in the grey light, listening to the bicycle's chain tick away down the street, and understood before she touched the paper that something had ended in the night.

The note was in Adela's hand — pen, not pencil, which meant it was meant to survive: *Come to Chenies Mews at seven. Come by way of the canal. Do not stop for anyone who knows your face.*

Marguerite dressed without lighting the lamp. There was a skill in this, she had learned — moving through the dark rooms of morning without announcing herself, buttoning her boots by feel, winding her hair in the dim square of window while the fog outside stood in the street like a congregation waiting for a verdict. Two years ago such an instruction would have frightened her. Now it only sorted itself among the others, filed with all the small clandestine procedures that had become the architecture of her days: which streets to avoid on Tuesdays, how long a letter might sit unopened in the satchel before the wax began to sweat, the particular quality of silence that meant a policeman was bored and the particular quality that meant he was interested.

She took the canal path as instructed. The water moved beneath the mist like something alive and ashamed of itself, and barges sat low and patient along the towpath, their cargoes hidden under tarpaulins stiff with cold. A heron stood in the shallows with the absolute conviction of a churchwarden. Marguerite walked fast, her breath coming out in white flags, and let herself admit, only to the rhythm of her own steps, what she already knew: Calloway had come back once, and men like Calloway always came back twice.

* * *

Adela Crane received her in the back room of the mews house, standing — she never stood when she received people unless the news was bad; sitting was for persuasion, standing was for arithmetic. The room smelled of coal smoke and cold tea. On the table between them lay a map of Shoreditch, weighted at the corners with a sugar tin, a paperweight, a brass candlestick, and a copy of *The Suffragist* folded to conceal nothing in particular, which was Adela's idea of irony.

"Sit," Adela said, and then, as Marguerite pulled out the chair: "No. Never mind. You'll want your hands free."

She turned the map around so it faced Marguerite. Rivington Street was marked with a cross of red ink so fresh it had bled into fibres of the paper.

"The raid comes this week," Adela said. "Not next month, not when it's convenient. Thursday, if I am any judge, and I have learned to be a judge of policemen. Everything leaves Rivington Street by Wednesday night. Presses, type, paper stock, the mailing lists, the plates for the passes. The workshop is to be a shell when they come through the door. Mr. Asquith's government will be handed an empty room and a smell of ink, and they may make of that whatever story suits them."

"How do you know Thursday?"

"Because Calloway came to me yesterday." Adela smiled, and it was the worst thing Marguerite had seen in some time — that composed, unhurried smile, the smile of a woman balancing books. "Politely. He is always polite. He asked whether I found it difficult, employing persons of uncertain loyalty. He asked about my driver, in fact. By name."

Marguerite kept her hands still on the edge of the table. "And what did you tell him?"

"That you had driven me for two years and never once been late." Adela looked at her, held the look a moment longer than comfort allowed, and returned to the map. "He wanted me to know he knew things. That is half their trade, the knowing and the letting you know. The other half is patience, and he has more of it than I expected, which is why we are done being patient ourselves."

She began to give orders in the tone she used for them — level, unhurried, each instruction laid down like a rail ahead of a train. The presses would go out in two loads, Tuesday midnight and Wednesday, disguised as a bookbinder's consignment and a consignment of church furniture, because no constable in London looks twice at a crate marked *Methodist Chapel, Hoxton*. Tom Ainsley would supervise the packing of the type; the young ones would be given other errands, scattered, ordinary, so that no single arrest could net more than two names. The mailing list Marguerite would carry herself, in three sections, to three addresses, over three days, so that no one route and no one satchel ever held the whole of it.

"And the letters," Adela said, and here she paused, and went to the writing desk, and Marguerite watched her back and felt the old alertness rise in her like water in a lock.

There were eleven letters on the desk, bundled and sealed. Adela set them in the satchel one by one, naming each recipient as she did so — a councillor in Bethnal Green, a donor who preferred to be a rumour, the editor of a provincial paper whose courage required weekly maintenance. Ten letters. Then she took up the eleventh from a drawer, and did not put it in the satchel, but held it.

"One more," she said. "This one I haven't written yet. Sit down after all, Marguerite. Take a note."

Marguerite sat. Adela did not dictate to her own secretary for this; she had dismissed the girl an hour ago. She dictated standing, facing the window, where the fog was beginning to lift off the rooftops in slow rags.

*"To Mrs. Edith Marchmont,"* she said. *"My dear Edith. I write in reply to yours of the fourteenth, which distressed me more than I can politely express—"*

Her voice was perfectly steady. That was the horror of it, Marguerite thought later; the voice never varied, never hurried, never softened. It was the voice she used for platform speeches and committee minutes, the voice that made rebellion sound like bookkeeping.

*"—You ask what provision I intend to make. I intend to make it. The sum discussed will be conveyed through the usual channels within the fortnight, and upon receipt you will find it sufficient, as I believe you will find your discretion sufficient also. What was entrusted to us both is now settled. It cannot be unsettled by feeling. Feeling is a luxury of women who have nothing to lose, and neither of us, thank God, is that woman any longer.*

*I remind you — not unkindly, I hope — of what we agreed in Geneva, before either of us had cause to doubt the other's good sense. Some things, once spoken, cannot be unsaid, and some faces, once known, must be altogether unknown. You have my word that this is accomplished. Give me yours, and we may go on being sisters in the only sense left to us: the sense in which we keep each other's silence."*

Adela stopped. The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a horse shifted in its traces and the mews creaked.

"You'll deliver that yourself," Adela said, turning round. "To Bayswater. Number 14, Chesham Place. Not by post — nothing of this goes near a post office. And you'll wait for no reply. If she offers you tea, you have another appointment. If she weeps — and she will, she weeps beautifully, it is her one unfailing accomplishment — you will be sympathetic and you will leave."

Marguerite wrote the last line of shorthand, her pencil moving on the paper the way Adela's own pencil moved when she signed the letters that were to die. She noticed this, and hated noticing it.

"May I ask," she said, keeping her voice level, "what sum buys a sister's silence these days?"

"You may not." Adela said it without heat, almost gently, and came to stand over the table. "You may ask me anything else, and one day perhaps I'll answer even that. But listen to me now, because this part matters more than the letter." She rested her fingertips on the map, on either side of the red cross over Rivington Street. "After Wednesday, the workshop is dead and its people are scattered and safe. After Wednesday, there is nothing left to find except what we choose to leave. You understand what that means?"

"It means the letters I've been carrying for two years were evidence," Marguerite said, "and now they're the whole case."

"They were always the whole case. Evidence is only memory written down. That's why this last round is the last — everything that could hang someone is going into the fire tonight, and what remains in circulation will say nothing that matters. But you—" Adela's hand closed briefly on her shoulder, a rare touch, gone almost before it arrived. "You will drive as you have always driven. Carefully, quietly, exactly. One more week, Marguerite, and then we are through the worst of it. Can I rely on you?"

It was not really a question. It had never really been a question, in two years. Marguerite heard herself say, "You can rely on me," and heard how easily it came, and wondered when precisely she had become a person who said such things without checking first whether they were true.

* * *

The letters went out over two days, and the city helped her and hindered her by turns. Tuesday wore a thin bright sun that made the streets feel exposed, every doorway a potential witness; Wednesday closed over again in fog, and Marguerite moved through it gratefully, a woman inside a grey room the size of London. She delivered to Bethnal Green a letter that read as dull committee business and carried, folded inside its second page, instructions that were not dull at all. She delivered to the anonymous donor a request for money that was also, in its phrasing, a farewell. Each time she left an envelope behind her she felt the satchel grow lighter and stranger, the way a ship feels lighter after cargo is unloaded — riding higher, more visible, more at the mercy of the weather.

On Wednesday afternoon she drove to Bayswater.

Chesham Place was a different country. The houses stood in a row like dowagers at a funeral, cream-painted and curtained, their area railings polished by servants whose existence was itself a kind of argument. Marguerite left the motor at the corner, walked the distance as instructed, and gave her card — a plain card, no name but *M. Sterling*, no affiliation anywhere on it — to a parlourmaid whose apron could have stood up on its own.

Edith Marchmont received her in a drawing room the colour of weak honey. She was smaller than Adela, softer, with the same bones under the flesh and none of the same discipline, and she was holding a handkerchief already, before a word had been spoken, as though grief were simply the outfit one wore indoors in Bayswater.

"You've seen her," she said. "You've come from her. Oh — is she well? Is she angry?" And then, before Marguerite could arrange a face: "Did she send money?"

"She sent a letter," Marguerite said. "I'm to wait for no reply."

Edith took the envelope and broke the seal with a motion like tearing silk, and read it standing by the fireplace, and Marguerite watched her face do several things in sequence and settle on wounded dignity, which appeared to be its resting position. When she finished, she read the last paragraph again — the paragraph about faces known and unknown — and her mouth thinned.

"'Accomplished,'" she repeated. "'Settled.' As though one settles a bill." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and sharp at once, and Marguerite understood that this woman was far more dangerous than her sister, because she wanted things and Adela only calculated them. "Does she imagine I don't know? Does she imagine I can't find out where the child went?"

"The letter says the matter is settled," Marguerite said, and was surprised at her own voice: flat, correct, Adela's arithmetic in miniature.

"I know what the letter says." Edith came toward her, handkerchief twisting. "I know what she does with words. I was reading her letters before you were driving her car, girl. Tell me—" and here the sharpness faltered into something almost human, almost pitiable, "—tell me only this. Is the child well? Whoever has her. Is she well?"

And Marguerite, who had carried the baby through the workshop in the rain, who had felt that small furious weight against her chest, who had memorized a face she'd been ordered to forget — Marguerite looked at this grieving, greedy, dangerous woman and told her the only true thing in the house:

"Yes. She's well. She's loved."

Edith pressed the handkerchief to her mouth. For a moment neither of them moved, and the fog pressed at the long windows, and somewhere upstairs a clock counted with terrible patience.

Then Edith lowered her hand, and the calculation came back into her face like a tide returning over sand, and Marguerite knew, walking back down the steps and along the polished pavement to the motor, that the money would buy less than Adela believed. Grief like that did not close accounts. It only opened new ones.

She got in, started the engine, and reached automatically into the satchel to confirm the day's work was done — ten letters delivered, the eleventh handed over, nothing left but the empty envelopes' ghosts.

Except there was not nothing. At the bottom of the satchel, folded once and placed where she would only find it after Bayswater, was a note in Adela's hand, in pencil.

*One final delivery tomorrow. Bring the satchel empty and bring it back full. And Marguerite — burn this note.*

She sat for a moment with the engine idling and the fog curling pale against the windscreen, and understood, with the clarity that sometimes arrives too late to be innocent, that Adela trusted her with everything except the truth — and that tomorrow's delivery, whatever it was, had been written in pencil because it was never meant to exist by nightfall.

She did not burn the note. She folded it away instead, into the lining of her glove, and drove back east through the thickening dusk, carrying, as always, one secret more than she had been given.
