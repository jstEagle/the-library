## 11. What Tom Saw

The workshop on Rivington Street smelled of ink and coal smoke and, lately, of milk. Tom Ainsley had set type for six years and could read a room the way he read a galley — by its weight, its balance, the places where the pressure sat too heavy. The safehouse above the workshop had been a printing office, then a meeting hall, then whatever it needed to be, and now it was a place where an infant slept in a dresser drawer lined with a shawl, and where every person who climbed the stairs spoke more quietly than they needed to.

He was setting the fortnightly bulletin that evening, the small hand-press clacking in the corner where young Winnifred worked the treadle, when Marguerite came down with the baby against her shoulder. He knew her step before he saw her — quick, even, the step of a woman who drove through fog at all hours and had learned not to hesitate. But tonight there was something folded into it, a hitch like a misprint, a letter set upside down in an otherwise clean line.

She crossed to the window. Rain ran down the glass in long grey ropes, and the gaslight from the street came up through the wet and broke into pieces on her face. She looked out at Rivington Street as if she expected it to answer a question.

"You'll catch your death standing there," Tom said, because it was what one said.

"I'm deciding something," she said, without turning. "Don't talk to me while I decide."

He went back to his case. That was their arrangement, unspoken these two years: she decided things, and he made them real. She brought him words that needed to become passes, certificates, letters of introduction for women fleeing husbands or the law; he gave them ink and authority. He had forged a marriage certificate once, a railway pass twice a week, a coroner's letter that had gotten a girl out of Leeds and into Manchester before anyone thought to look. He was good at it. He liked being good at it. What he did not say — what he had carried since the first winter, since the night she'd come in with sleet frozen in the seams of her coat and asked him, in the flat voice of someone who has been awake thirty hours, whether he could make a document that would survive a policeman's squint — what he did not say was that he loved her.

He set the line again. *Forget the face,* Adela Crane had written, and everyone upstairs pretended the drawer was empty.

* * *

Marguerite stood at the window a long time. Then she took from her coat a letter — he saw it only in profile, a fold of paper held low against her hip, the way you hold something you have already decided is yours — and she read it again, standing, in the bad light, her lips barely moving.

Tom kept his eyes on his type. He was a man who noticed things professionally. He noticed that she had gone still in the way people go still over a wound. He noticed that when she finished reading she did not put the letter back where letters went — the dispatch box under the stairs, the locked drawer of Adela's desk — but folded it small, very small, and pushed it deep into the pocket of her driving coat, the one with the oil-stained cuff, and pressed her hand flat over the pocket as if sealing an envelope.

Then she picked up the baby and went back up the stairs, and the door closed behind her with the soft click of a thing done.

Tom stood in the smell of ink and let his heart do something complicated.

He understood what he had seen. He was not a fool, though he played one well among the volunteers, laughing at their jokes, mending their bicycles, letting them believe the quiet typesetter noticed nothing. He had seen Marguerite Sterling hide a letter from Adela Crane, and he understood — the way you understand a sentence whose meaning arrives a beat after the words — that the machinery of the movement, which he served with his hands and his silence, had just developed a fault line, and that Marguerite was standing on it.

He should feel afraid. He noted this too, distantly, the way you note weather. Informants were rumored; Calloway had come and gone like a draught under a door; every comrade might be a door left open. A hidden letter was evidence. A hidden letter about the child was worse than evidence — it was the whole house of cards in one fold of paper.

But what he felt, standing at his case with a half-set page dying in the stick, was not fear. It was something warmer and more shameful: relief. Relief that she had done it. Relief that the woman he loved was not only loyal, not only the perfect instrument everyone believed her to be, but a person who could break, who could choose, who could steal a piece of truth and press it to her body like contraband. He had loved her competence for two years. He found, tonight, that he loved her disobedience more.

* * *

He found her after midnight, in the kitchen at the back of the ground floor, where a fire burned low and the baby slept in the drawer pulled out beside the hearth. Marguerite sat with her boots off and her stockinged feet on the fender, and she was not reading, not writing, not doing any of the industrious things she did with her hours. She was watching the child breathe.

"Tom," she said, without turning. She always knew him by his step. "You should be asleep."

"So should you."

"I don't sleep in houses with secrets in them." She said it lightly, but her hand moved, just slightly, toward the coat hung on the chair back — the coat with the pocket. "I keep thinking I'll wake up and everything will be where it's supposed to be."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then I'll have to make it so." She turned then, and the firelight caught the exhaustion under her eyes. "Tom. Can you forge a placement certificate?"

He didn't ask why. Asking why was how men got hanged. "A private placement? Not the hospital intake?"

"Not the hospital. Never the hospital." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "A respectable widow in Kent. Mrs. — " she stopped, and he watched her invent the name in real time, watch the lie assemble itself behind her eyes with the fluency of a woman who had spent two years memorizing other people's words. "Mrs. Hartley. Near Tenterden. She lost her own daughter. She has room and no curiosity. I can get her agreement through a cousin of Winnifred's. But paper makes it true, Tom. Paper is the only thing in England that makes anything true."

He crouched at the fire and fed it a knot of wood, buying himself a moment, because his hands wanted to shake and he would not let them. "I'll need a registrar's format. Kent county, Tenterden district. I can cut the border ornament from the parish circulars we print. The watermark's the hard part — I can simulate it with a wash, but it wants drying flat under weight for two days."

"How long?"

"Three days. Four if the rain keeps the paper damp."

"Four days." She looked at the drawer, at the small rise and fall of the blanket. "She'll be four days older when she leaves than she'd have been. Do you think four days matters, to a person's whole life?"

"I think," Tom said carefully, "that four days is the difference between a foundling number and a name."

Marguerite closed her eyes. When she opened them she was, for one unguarded second, entirely undone — and then she gathered herself the way she gathered the satchel each morning, everything folded and strapped down. "Thank you," she said. "No one can know. Not Adela. Not anyone."

"No one," he agreed. And then, because the fire was warm and the hour was lawless and he had carried the words for two winters and could not carry them past a third: "Marguerite."

She waited.

"I've loved you since the first winter," he said. "Since the night you came in with ice in your coat seams and asked me for a document that would survive a policeman's squint. You were so tired your voice had gone flat, and you thanked me like I'd handed you bread when you were starving, and I thought — there it is, then. That's the whole of it. I've never said it because you measure your life in what fits in a satchel, and I didn't want to be cargo."

The fire ticked. Above them a floorboard settled. In the drawer, the baby sighed and did not wake.

Marguerite looked at him for a long time. He braced for kindness — the gentle deflection of a woman who trusted him with everything except that — but what came instead was quieter and stranger.

"I know," she said. "I've known since the second winter. I kept it the way I keep everything. Carried, not told." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "It seems tonight I'm keeping several things that way."

"Is that a refusal?"

"It's a postponement." She reached out and touched his wrist, briefly, the first deliberate touch in two years, and it went through him like type through a press. "When this child is placed — when the letter in my pocket is either delivered or destroyed — ask me again. On a day when nothing is burning."

"That's a dangerous promise to offer a forger," he said. "We're trained to remember everything."

"Then remember that," she said, and took her feet off the fender, and went to stand over the drawer, and the conversation was over — but not ended. He understood the difference. Some sentences, like some documents, are only waiting for the right seal.

* * *

He worked the next three days with the concentration of a man praying. He cut the Kent border from a parish circular by lamplight, mirror-reversed on the zinc plate, and printed it faint enough to pass for age. He aged the paper over the stove, turning it like toast, until it had the soft ivory of a document that had lived in a drawer. He wrote the certificate in a clerk's hand he had practiced for years — round, patient, incurious — and he invented for Mrs. Hartley of Tenterden a late husband, a parish, a doctor's signature borrowed from a man who had died believing in nothing so useful as forgery.

*Aunt to the child,* the certificate read. *Placed privately, with the knowledge and consent of the family.*

Family. He set the word and looked at it a long moment. Somewhere in a fine house across the river a Member of Parliament ate his dinner believing his wife's arithmetic was clean, and here in a basement in Shoreditch a typesetter was manufacturing a family out of ink and wishfulness. It occurred to him that all of England was built this way — on documents, on signatures, on the great collective agreement not to look too closely at the seal.

On the third evening Marguerite came down while the wash was still tacky, and stood over the drying certificate between two sheets of blotting paper and two flat irons, and read it top to bottom twice.

"It's beautiful," she said.

"It's a crime," he said.

"Yes." She smoothed the edge with her thumb. "It's both. Most true things are."

She rolled it, tied it with string, and slid it inside her coat — into the other pocket, the clean one, so that now she carried a stolen letter on the left and a forged future on the right, balanced like a driver balances two horses. Tom watched her do it and felt the strangest peace of his life, the peace of a man who has finally committed fully to something and can stop pretending otherwise.

They were accomplices now. There was a word for what that meant between two people, and it was not loyalty, and it was not duty, and he suspected it was better than both.

"Kent," he said. "When?"

"Saturday. Before dawn. I'll take the Brighton road as far as Tunbridge and cut across." She paused at the foot of the stairs, the baby's spare clothes already packed in a basket at her elbow, and looked back at him. "Tom. If Calloway comes back while I'm gone—"

"He'll find presses and pamphlets and a lot of innocent-looking volunteers."

"And you?"

"I'll be at my case," he said. "Where else would a typesetter be?"

She held his eyes a moment longer than the sentence required. Then she went up the stairs, and he heard the drawer slide home, and the small sounds of a child being fed in a house full of secrets.

He turned back to his case and began, methodically, to distribute his type back into its compartments — e's to the e-box, a's to the a-box, every letter returned to its proper place, the way letters are supposed to go, the way lives are supposed to go. Outside, the rain had stopped. The street lamp threw a bar of light across the floor, and in it he could see the dust turning, slow and golden, like something deciding whether to settle.

He was reaching for the last of the type when the street door opened below, without a knock, and footsteps started up the stairs — unhurried, official, the footsteps of a man who has been let in rather than one who has broken in — and Tom knew, before the light reached the landing, before the polite voice called up asking whether the printing office was still open, that Mr. Calloway had come back.
