## 12. Calloway's Second Visit

He came up the stairs the way weather comes — no hurry in him, no apology, only the certainty of arrival.

Tom Ainsley stood with a galley of type cradled against his chest, eleven pounds of lead and words that could be called sedition by any magistrate with an afternoon to spare, and he did not set it down, because setting it down would have been a confession of something, and holding it gave his hands somewhere to be.

"Evening," said Mr. Calloway from the landing. He was exactly as Tom remembered him from the first visit: grey coat, grey hat held at the correct angle of politeness, a face built to express mild interest in other men's affairs. The gaslight up here was poor and it did him no harm. Some faces improved in bad light because there was less of them to see. "I hope I'm not interrupting. The door was unfastened."

"It sticks," Tom said. "We wedge it."

"Clever." Calloway came fully into the room, and behind him, on the stairs, stood another man — younger, broader, doing nothing so refined as pretending to browse. He looked at the press, the paper stock, the cabinet of drawers where the type slept in its compartments like a city seen from above. He had the stillness of a man memorizing furniture.

Calloway removed his hat and held it against his chest as though the room were a church, which, given what was printed in it, Tom supposed it might be.

"You'll forgive a second call," Calloway said. "The Home Office takes a thorough interest in the licensing of printing premises. It would be negligent of me not to be thorough." His eyes went over the workshop the way a customs officer goes over luggage. "You've moved some things since I was last here."

"We move things every week," Tom said. "Paper comes in, pamphlets go out. That's generally how a print shop conducts itself."

"Mm," said Calloway, in the tone of a man filing something away rather than agreeing with it. He drifted along the bench, trailing one gloved finger above — never quite touching — the ink tins, the rollers, the stack of blank stock. "Mr. Ainsley, isn't it? Typesetter. Two years with the firm."

"There isn't a firm," Tom said. "There's a cause and a press."

"There is always a firm," said Calloway mildly, "somewhere underneath. Money has to come from somebody, orders from somebody else. Even the Lord's work has a treasurer." He turned, and now the mildness in his face rearranged itself into something more attentive, the way a lens adjusts. "But that isn't why I've come back tonight, Mr. Ainsley. Or not chiefly."

He let that sit. Behind him, the broad young man had drifted toward the back window and was looking down into the yard, not at anything, which was worse than looking at something.

"A curious matter," Calloway went on. "A matter entirely outside my usual remit, you'll understand — I deal in seditious libel, unlawful assemblies, the feeding of the Constabulary Gazette — but a colleague in Brighton has asked a courtesy of me, and I am, if nothing else, a courteous man." He replaced his hat on his head, unhurried, settling it with two fingers. "A child, Mr. Ainsley."

Tom felt the type dig into his chest. Eleven pounds of it. He shifted his grip half an inch.

"A girl," Calloway said. "Some twenty months old at the time of her disappearance, which was — let me be precise, my colleague was very insistent upon dates — the fourteenth of March, 1911. A hired carriage on the seafront at Brighton. The nurse stepped inside a hotel for perhaps four minutes to fetch the mother's wrap. When she came out, the carriage was empty but for a folded blanket, and the child was gone." He recited it flatly, like scripture learned by rote, and that flatness was the frightening part; a man inventing a story embellishes it, but a man working from a file delivers it dry as dust. "The newspapers carried it for a fortnight. There was an inquest of sorts — an inquiry, I should say. No body, no charge, no arrest. The family preferred discretion. Families of that sort usually do."

"I don't know anything about it," Tom said.

"No," Calloway agreed pleasantly. "I didn't suppose you did. You see, the reason I mention it here — in a printing office, of all places, and I do apologize for the intrusion — is that inquiries have a shape, Mr. Ainsley. They have a geography. And this particular inquiry keeps arriving at addresses that interest my department." He smiled without warmth or the pretense of it. "A woman who worked as a nurse in that household took a position the following winter with a ladies' charitable society. That society shares a post-office box with an organization that prints pamphlets. Pamphlets are printed in places like this one. You follow the thread and the thread leads through rooms such as this, past presses such as yours, and I begin to wonder whether the thread is being followed or whether it is being *fed*."

The word *fed* landed in the room and stayed there.

"I couldn't say," Tom said. His mouth was dry. "I set type. Words go in, words come out. I don't ask after nurses in Brighton."

"No," said Calloway. "Very few people ask after nurses. That is rather the point of nurses." He moved toward the door, then stopped, as men of his profession know to do, and delivered the real sentence in passing, casually, as though it were an afterthought and not the whole evening's purpose: "It occurs to me — purely as a matter of arithmetic — that a child who vanished in the spring of 1911 might well, by now, have been carried through any number of doors in this city. Carried by someone in a hurry. Someone anxious to see her settled somewhere quiet, somewhere permanent, before anyone thought to ask after her face." He put his hand on the banister. "If such a child passed through your acquaintance, Mr. Ainsley — even once, even briefly — discretion is a virtue, but it is not infinite. Neither, for that matter, is my patience, though I dress it better than most."

He descended the stairs. The broad young man came away from the window last, and paused at the head of them long enough to look at the type in Tom's arms — at the words, upside down and backwards, mirrored in the steel — and then followed his master down, and the street door closed behind them both with the softness of a door that knows it will open again.

Tom stood a long while in the cooling room. The press ticked as the metal contracted. Down in the yard, a cat crossed between shadows, and beyond the wall a cab rattled past carrying someone whose evening contained no inspectors at all.

Then he set down the type, put on his coat, and went to find Marguerite.

* * *

She was at the safehouse on Charlotte Street when he came, sitting in the kitchen with the baby asleep against her shoulder — a sight that had become ordinary in ten days and should never have been allowed to — and she knew before he finished closing the door. She had learned to read men the way she read letters: by weight, by fold, by what they declined to say in the first sentence.

"He came back," she said.

"He came back." Tom pulled off his cap and turned it in his hands. "Not about the license. Not about the pamphlets. About—" He glanced at the child, and Marguerite watched him decide, deliberately, not to lower his voice, because lowering it would make the thing true in a way it wasn't yet. "About a little girl missing from a carriage in Brighton. March, 1911. A nurse, a hotel, four minutes. He had the date to the day."

Marguerite did not move. Moving would wake the baby, and waking the baby would give her somewhere to put her hands, and she needed, for one moment longer, to feel precisely how cold her own were.

Brighton. March, 1911.

The year Adela Crane had been abroad — *the year abroad*, everyone said it lightly, the year of her sister Edith's scandal, the year Mrs. Crane's health required the continent, the year that appeared in no diary and produced no letters, because Marguerite had read everything that passed through that satchel for two years and there was a hole where those twelve months should have been, a hole shaped exactly like a woman and a secret and a sea crossing.

Twenty months old at the time of her disappearance. This child in her arms was — she did the arithmetic and hated herself for doing it, hated how quickly it came, the way all of Adela's arithmetic came — this child would have been twenty months old last March.

"They're not hunting us," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, level and strange. "They're not hunting the presses, or the passes, or the mailing list. Those were the excuse. He's hunting her." She looked down at the small sleeping weight, the fist curled against her collarbone, the absurd perfection of the ear. "And he came to Rivington Street to look for her. Why Rivington Street, Tom? Why our door?"

Tom sat down slowly across the table from her. In the lamplight his face was honest and frightened, and she loved him, and she noted the loving of him the way one notes the time — automatically, uselessly, in the margin of a page one cannot finish reading.

"He said something," Tom said. "About threads. About inquiries having a geography. He said the thread kept leading through places like the workshop, and then he said—" Tom's jaw worked. "He said he wondered whether the thread was being followed or fed."

*Fed.* The word again. Marguerite laid it beside everything else she knew and watched what it touched.

Someone had talked.

Not guessed — talked. Because Calloway had not come to Rivington Street chasing rumor. He had come with a date. A date to the day, a carriage, a nurse, a hotel, four minutes. Rumor does not keep calendars. Files keep calendars, and files are made of statements, and statements are made by mouths, and their secret had lived in exactly three mouths that mattered — Adela's, Edith's, and now hers — plus whatever hands had carried the child from wherever she had spent those first months to the safehouse step in the rain.

Hands. There had been hands. A bundle does not walk to a doorstep.

She thought of the volunteers. Winnie with her ink-stained cuffs and her laugh like a struck match. Old Ferris who slept in the back room and asked no questions, which was itself a kind of question. The girls who came on Thursdays to fold pamphlets and sing quietly so as not to wake whoever was sleeping upstairs. She thought of each of them opening a door — not to the police, not on purpose, no one would ever do it on purpose, but a door is a door, and a priest is a door, and a wife is a door, and a man in a public house buying rounds and asking friendly questions about the interesting lives of political ladies is a door with the latch already lifted.

Every comrade a possible door left open. Every kindness a crack for the cold to come through.

"Marguerite." Tom's voice, low now despite himself. "What do we do?"

She realized the baby had woken anyway — that she had been rocking her without knowing it, the small body shifting against her, one hand opening and closing on the air. She looked at Tom across the table and saw that he was waiting to be told, that he trusted her to know, and the trust was a weight like the type had been, eleven pounds of it against her chest, and she almost laughed at the symmetry of it and did not, because laughter would have broken into the other thing.

"We don't do anything differently," she said at last. "That's the first rule now. We change nothing — not the routes, not the hours, not the faces. The moment we change, we tell him what we know, and what we know is worth more than what he knows. For now." 

"And after now?"

"After now—" She stood, and the baby's head lolled against her shoulder, and Marguerite looked at the window, at the dark street beyond it where any passerby might be paid four shillings a week to note what time the lights went out. "After now, someone in this house has spoken to someone outside it, and I am going to find out who, because Calloway doesn't have the child yet. He has a date and a geography. Which means someone gave him a beginning and not an ending — someone talked halfway and stopped, or talked without knowing what they were saying." She met Tom's eyes. "Halfway is more dangerous than all the way, Tom. Halfway means he'll come back until he gets the rest."

She carried the child up the narrow stairs to the room where a cot waited that had been built, in another life, for crates of pamphlet stock. At the landing she stopped, because below her, through the floorboards, she could hear the Thursday volunteers arriving early — voices, laughter, the scrape of chairs, Winnie's struck-match laugh rising above the rest, bright and loyal and utterly unknowable, and Marguerite stood in the dark listening to the sound of her friends and trying to hear, beneath it, the faintest note of a door swinging open.

The baby sighed against her neck and slept on, oblivious, twenty months old twice over, and wanted for nothing in the world but warmth.

Downstairs, someone began to fold pamphlets.
