## 13. The Informer

Calloway had asked about a child. That was the sentence Marguerite carried through the days that followed, turning it over the way one turns a stone to see what lives beneath it. Not pamphlets. Not passes. Not the mailing list, though he had lingered over it like a man lingering over a lover's handwriting. A child reported missing from a carriage in Brighton two years ago.

Which meant someone had talked. And not merely talked — someone had connected three things that no single person at Rivington Street could connect: the workshop, Adela Crane, and a baby who had arrived at the safehouse door with rain on its blanket and a note in pencil. The child was barely a week in their keeping. The Brighton carriage was two years gone. Whoever had bridged that distance knew more than any of them — or had been told more than any of them.

Marguerite began, as she did everything, with paper.

She sat in the motor by the kerb on Charlotte Street with her collar up against the damp and made a list in her head, because a list on paper was a thing Calloway could someday hold. Twenty-three volunteers came regularly to Rivington Street. Nine were girls under twenty. Of those nine, five handled correspondence; of those five, only three had been present the dawn the bundle appeared — Tom, old Mrs. Quill from the typesetting room, and a girl named Nora Fitch, seventeen, freckled, devout, who sang hymns under her breath while she folded pamphlets and believed, with the whole burning sincerity of her years, that the cause was a form of prayer.

Nora. Marguerite turned the name over too, and did not like what she found beneath it.

It was Nora who had wept when the police horse was beaten back from the crowd in Trafalgar Square, wept for the *horse*, mind, while women bled at her elbows. It was Nora who had once asked Marguerite, shyly, whether it troubled her conscience — all this deception, all these forged papers, lies stacked like type — and whether God could be expected to sort the good intentions from the bad on Judgment Day. And it was Nora whose eyes had followed the baby through the workshop that first morning with an expression Marguerite had mistaken for tenderness and now recognized as something closer to terror. The terror of a girl who has seen a sin and does not know where to put it down.

A Catholic girl, besides. One of the few among them. Confession was as ordinary to Nora as breakfast.

Marguerite did not go to Nora first. She went to the priest.

* * *

Father Bleaney's church stood off Gray's Inn Road behind a curtain of plane trees, a small grim building with a soot-darkened spire, the kind of parish that served the Irish laborers and the servant class and asked no questions of anyone. Marguerite parked the motor some streets off and walked, because a woman arriving by motorcar outside a Catholic church in Somers Town would be remembered, and she could not afford to be remembered.

She had rehearsed nothing. She never rehearsed; rehearsing made lies stiff, and stiff lies cracked. She simply pushed open the heavy door and let the smell of the place take her — candle wax and stone and old incense, the particular sweetness that clings to Catholic churches the way coal smoke clings to London itself. It was mid-afternoon, between devotions, and the nave was nearly empty: one old woman in black, motionless as furniture, and near the side altar a tall stooped figure arranging candles, his back to her.

"Father Bleaney?"

He turned slowly, the way old men turn, as if the body must be consulted first. He had a long face, grey-eyed, unremarkable, the face of a man who has spent forty years being unmemorable on purpose. "I'm he."

"I wonder if I might speak with you. Privately." She let him look at her — a woman of perhaps thirty, plainly but well dressed, gloves on, voice steady. She watched him decide what she was. Widow? Employer? Convert? "It's about one of your penitents."

Something moved behind his eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "Then I fear I can't help you, madam. What is said in confession—"

"—is sealed. Yes. I know." She softened, lowered her gaze, became a woman asking rather than demanding. "I'm not asking what was said. I'm asking whether a girl came to you recently carrying something too heavy for her. Whether you advised her, afterward, to take her burden to someone with authority over it. Someone in government."

The silence stretched long enough that the old woman's rosary could be heard clicking bead by bead.

"Miss Fitch," Father Bleaney said at last, and it was not a guess, which told Marguerite everything, "is a child of scrupulous conscience. She was distressed. When a soul is distressed, I direct it toward lawful remedy. If a wrong has been done — an innocent party in danger — the seal of confession does not oblige me to stand idle forever. There is counsel. There is the proper authorities." He drew himself up, and she saw that he was not ashamed; he had made his accounting and slept well. "You'll understand, madam, if I say that whatever else may be happening in certain houses in this city, the law of England is not the enemy of children."

"The law of England," Marguerite said quietly, "put the Cat and Mouse Act on the books this spring. The law force-feeds women in Holloway through tubes. I've driven those women to prison gates, Father. I know exactly how the law feels about innocents."

"That is politics." His voice was mild, immovable. "I concern myself with souls."

"And Mr. Calloway of the Home Office concerns himself with evidence. Did he tell you he was hunting a baby? Or did you believe him when he said he was saving one?"

She saw then — the small tell, the flicker toward the confessional and away again — that Calloway had come here more than once, that he had cultivated the old man patiently, tea and patriotism and gentle questions, until the priest had become, without ever quite saying so to himself, an instrument. Nora had confessed a sin. Bleaney had laundered it into civic duty. And Calloway had walked out with the shape of a secret he could spend years learning to read.

Marguerite thanked him for his time. Her hands did not shake until she was back in the motor.

* * *

She could have gone straight to Adela. That was the correct move, the strategic move: report the breach, let the machinery grind. Expel Nora before the week was out, scatter suspicion, close the door the girl had left open. Adela would do it without sentiment and call it necessity, and the cause would survive, and Nora Fitch would be somewhere in the fog with her conscience and her hymns and her ruined usefulness, and everyone would agree privately that it had to be done.

Instead Marguerite drove to Rivington Street and found Nora alone in the folding room, up to her wrists in pamphlets, singing under her breath — *Jerusalem*, of all things, the anthem they'd all adopted, and there was something unbearable in hearing it sung by the person about to be sacrificed to it.

"You missed the distribution run," Marguerite said from the doorway.

Nora startled, laughed, pressed a hand to her heart. "Miss Sterling! You walk like a cat, everyone says so. No — Mrs. Quill took the Islington lot herself. Said I looked peaked." She said it lightly, but her eyes went to the table, to the pamphlets, anywhere but Marguerite's face, and her freckled hands resumed folding with a sudden furious diligence.

"Nora." Marguerite crossed the room and stood across the table from her, and did not smile. "Look at me."

The girl looked. Seventeen, and her eyes filled before she could stop them.

"I'm going to ask you one question," Marguerite said. "And I want you to consider very carefully before you answer, because your answer decides what happens next — to you, and to people you love. Have you spoken to anyone outside these walls about the child?"

The folding stopped. Nora's breath came once, twice, ragged. Then, in a whisper that seemed to come from somewhere far beneath the pamphlets and the hymns and the banners:

"He said it was the right thing. Father Bleaney said — he said if an innocent was in danger, it wasn't betraying anyone, it was — Miss Sterling, I didn't tell him whose baby. I swear before God I never said her name, I never said Mrs. Crane, I only said—" Her voice broke apart entirely. "I only said there was a child, and she ought to be somewhere safe, and I couldn't sleep, I couldn't *sleep*—"

"There was a child," Marguerite repeated, very evenly, "from a carriage in Brighton. Two years ago. How did you know about Brighton?"

And Nora, weeping freely now, told her. Told her how, unable to sleep after the baby came, she had gone to Father Bleaney not once but three times, and how on the third visit he had asked gentle, terrible questions — had the child been taken? was she stolen? was she being hidden from her lawful guardians? — until Nora, cornered by her own guilt, had given him the truth she had overheard: that the baby had come back to the safehouse after two years away, that she had been gone since Brighton, that the debt mentioned in the note meant the family wanted her returned and *couldn't* be seen to want it. Nora hadn't understood the half of what she said. She had only known she was drowning and grabbed at the first hand offered.

"He said the authorities would find her a good home," Nora finished miserably. "That's all I wanted. A good home, and no more secrets. Miss Sterling, are they going to arrest me?"

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment — the chapped hands, the ink on her cuffs, the whole small mortal weight of her — and felt the arithmetic begin in her own chest, cold and familiar, Adela's arithmetic, and hated it.

Because here was the truth laid bare: expel the girl, and the expulsion itself would confirm to Calloway that his information was sound. He would tighten his net. He might follow Nora home; he might squeeze her again, harder, and next time she would give him names, because seventeen-year-olds who have betrayed once can be made to betray twice. Keep her, and every day she remained was another day for Bleaney's gentle questions, another confession, another leak. Mercy, in either direction, was a liability wearing different clothes.

There was a third option, of course. It sat in Marguerite's mind like a candle in a dark room, dangerous and obvious.

"Nobody is arresting you," Marguerite said. "But listen to me now, and listen as though your soul depends on it, because it does — yours and other people's. You will speak to no one. Not Father Bleaney, not your mother, not God Himself except silently. You will fold your pamphlets and sing your hymns and you will carry this, Nora, the way the rest of us carry things. Can you do that?"

Nora nodded, hiccupping, wretched.

"And Nora — the man who came to Father Bleaney. Describe him."

"A gentleman. Kind-seeming. Grey coat, grey eyes. He gave me a shilling once, for my tram fare." She stared at her hands. "He asked me did I love England. I said yes."

"So did I," Marguerite said, and left her to her folding.

* * *

She found Tom in the typesetting room, pulling proof sheets in the amber light of a work lamp, his sleeves rolled, forearms dusted with ink. Two years of loving him quietly had taught Marguerite to read his silences as fluently as any letter, and tonight she needed a reader more than a lover, though watching him wipe his hands on a rag she allowed herself one unguarded second of wanting a life where neither of them was ever required to keep a secret larger than supper.

"Calloway's informant is a priest," she said, closing the door. "Bleaney, off Gray's Inn Road. The girl is Nora."

Tom set down the rag. "Nora? Sweet little Nor—" He stopped, worked it through, and his face did what his face always did: showed everything. "God. She'd confess the weather if she thought it was sinful."

"She's confessed the child. Twice removed — through the priest to Calloway. And Tom, Calloway isn't fishing anymore. He knows there was a baby. He knows about Brighton. He's assembling something, piece by piece, and the pieces aren't about pamphlets." She sat on the edge of the composing stone, and for once let the tiredness show in her voice. "We can cut her loose. Adela will want to. Expulsion, distance, deniability. It's the sensible move."

"But?" Tom said, because he had learned her endings.

"But cutting her loose tells Calloway he's right. And keeping her means betting everything that she's frightened enough to stay quiet and pious enough not to burn." Marguerite looked at her own hands, ink-stained now too, from the composing stone's edge. "Mercy is a luxury, Tom. We've always known that. I just didn't expect it to arrive wearing a sixteen-year-old's face."

Tom came and stood beside her, not touching, though his shoulder was an inch from hers, and that inch was worth more to her than most touches. "What does your instinct say? Not strategy. Instinct."

"My instinct says Nora Fitch couldn't hold a secret if we sewed it into her lining. My instinct says expel her." She exhaled. "My instinct also says that everything Adela has built — every speech, every march, every woman who found her spine in that workshop — should not collapse because a child was kind-hearted and a priest was ambitious. So instinct loses."

They were quiet. Downstairs someone laughed; the presses thumped like slow hearts.

"There's another way through," Tom said slowly, and Marguerite heard in his voice the same idea she had been circling all afternoon, the candle in the dark room. "If the child stops being here — if she's placed, properly, privately, papers forged, trail closed — then there's nothing for Calloway to find. Not tomorrow, not ever. Nora's leak leads nowhere. The priest's information goes stale. We don't have to choose between mercy and safety, because we make the whole question vanish."

"With forged placement papers. For the daughter of an MP." Marguerite looked up at him. "Do you hear yourself?"

"I hear myself volunteering," Tom said, "and I notice you're not stopping me."

"It isn't our decision to make." She stood, straightening her jacket, resuming the armor of competence that had carried her through every locked gate and burning letter of the past three years. "It's Adela's. Everything is Adela's. But I'll tell you this, Tom Ainsley — when I lay it before her, I'm going to lay it as the only option, because it is. The hospital intake is a black hole, yes, untraceable — but Calloway already knows a child exists. A child in an institution can be *found*. A child in a widow's cottage in Kent with honest-looking papers cannot."

"And Adela will agree to that?" Tom asked quietly. "Adela Crane, who measures everything in column inches and votes? She'll agree to forge documents and place her own daughter with strangers, off the books, on your say-so?"

Marguerite picked up her gloves.

"She'll agree," she said, "because I'm going to make her believe it was her idea."

She was at the door when Tom spoke again, softer.

"Marguerite. Whatever she asks of you for this — count the cost first. Before, not after."

She paused, hand on the latch, and thought of all the letters she had memorized and burned, all the weights she had carried in one leather satchel, and how the satchel had never yet torn, and how everything tears eventually.

"I always count," she said, and went down the stairs to drive through the fog to Adela Crane's house, where the lamps were lit and the arithmetic waited.
