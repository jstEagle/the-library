## 14. Adela's Bargain

Marguerite knew something had changed the moment she came through the safehouse door, because the house was quiet in the wrong way. Quiet, in that place, usually meant industry — the muffled clatter of a mimeograph behind a curtain, the whisper of pamphlets being folded into parcels addressed to addresses that did not officially exist. This quiet had a held-breath quality to it. The girl at the washstand wouldn't meet her eyes. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked and settled, like the house itself shifting its weight.

Adela Crane was in the back parlour, standing at the cold hearth with her hands folded behind her back — the posture she took when she was composing a speech, or dismantling one of her husband's arguments over dinner, or deciding how much truth a room could bear.

In her other hand, half-hidden by the fall of her grey skirt, was a letter.

Marguerite saw it before she understood it. She saw the cream-coloured paper, the pencil-grey crease where it had been folded twice, the small tear at the corner where the seal had been broken carelessly — or deliberately, so that it could be broken again in front of someone. She had carried enough letters in two years to read their anatomy at a glance, the way a doctor reads a face.

She set down the satchel. It seemed suddenly very heavy, and also very light, as though all its contents had turned to smoke and she was holding only the leather.

"You went through my things," Marguerite said.

"Your room," Adela corrected, without turning. "Your things are your own. Though I confess I have never been entirely clear on where one ends and the other begins, in your case. You live inside this movement the way a thread lives inside cloth." She turned then, and her face was composed, courteous, terrible. "The letter to Edith was not delivered."

"No."

"And there is a widow in Kent who has been paid — I traced the payment; it was not difficult; money leaves footprints like a drunkard leaving a ballroom — to take in a foundling who does not exist." Adela's voice never rose. It never needed to. It was the voice of a woman who had spent twenty years making men in Parliament feel as though they had wandered into an argument already lost. "And you meant to carry the child there yourself. Tomorrow, by the morning train from Victoria. Two changes at Tonbridge. You packed a valise."

"How—"

"The landlady irons your collars and reads your room while she does. Everyone is readable, Marguerite. That is the first lesson of this work. You taught me the second: that even the most loyal hand will close around a thing if the thing is warm enough." She lifted the letter slightly, so the lamplight caught the paper. "You kept this instead of delivering it. Why?"

Because it said *disappear entirely*. Because the foundling hospital's intake books were designed by law and custom to be untraceable, a machine for making children into no one. Because the baby had gripped her finger in the night with a strength that seemed absurd in something so small. Because you signed this in pencil, Marguerite thought, because everything you sign in pencil is something you intend to deny.

What she said was: "Because it wasn't yours to send."

Something moved behind Adela's eyes — quick, quickly governed. She crossed to the lamp and sat, arranging her skirts with the unconscious precision of a woman who had been photographed eleven times that year and knew exactly which angle survived. "Sit down, please."

Marguerite remained standing. It was a small rebellion, almost childlike, and they both knew it.

* * *

"I am not going to rage at you," Adela said. "I want you to understand that at the outset, because I suspect you came up those stairs braced for rage, and rage would be simpler for both of us. Rage says: you have betrayed me, and here is the price. What I have to say is worse." She smoothed the letter flat against her knee, once, as if ironing a crease out of the conversation itself. "I am going to ask you for help. And then I am going to take everything from you, and make you grateful for it."

"That sounds like my mother's second marriage."

For a moment — a single, unguarded instant — Adela laughed. It transformed her. For that instant she was not the leader whose photograph hung in a thousand kitchens; she was a tired woman of forty-six with ink on her cuff, sitting in a cold parlour in Bethnal Green. Then the moment closed over, like water over a dropped coin.

"Edith wrote to me yesterday," Adela said. "Not to me — through the solicitor, which is her way of spitting. She knows the child was not placed at the hospital. She has friends among the parish registrars; God knows how; she has always collected small wicked people the way others collect stamps. She knows, which means the letter you withheld has become worthless as a promise and valuable as evidence. If it surfaces now, it proves premeditation. It proves we intended to erase a child from the record. My record, Marguerite. My husband sits on the committee that governs those records."

"I know what it proves. I've read it."

"Yes." Adela looked at her for a long moment. "Yes, you would have. That is the trouble with you. I gave you every letter in England to carry, and you insisted on reading them."

"You let me believe you didn't know I read them."

"I have known since the winter of 1912, when you recited a passage to Inspector—" she stopped herself. "Since a long time. It never troubled me. A courier who memorizes what she burns is a safe. But safes are for valuables, and I made the error of forgetting that you were also a woman. That is my fault, and I will pay for it, but you will pay first. Sit down, Marguerite."

This time Marguerite sat. Her knees had opinions of their own.

* * *

Outside, the fog had come up off the river again, the great yellow-brown wool of it that swallowed church spires and made every street its own small island. A cart rattled past the window and its driver coughed the wet, barking cough of the men who worked the docks. Marguerite listened to it fade and thought: Tom is at the workshop tonight, moving type. Tom is loading cases onto a cart in the dark. Every minute I sit in this room is a minute closer to Rivington Street and Calloway's quiet men in their quiet coats.

"Here is my arithmetic," Adela said, and Marguerite flinched at the phrase — *arithmetic*, the same word she herself had used, alone, at three in the morning with the baby breathing against her shoulder. "Edith wants silence. Silence can be bought with money, but money buys only postponement; she has expensive tastes and a longer life ahead of her than her conscience. Silence can be bought with confession, but confession written down is a loaded pistol left in a drawer, and Edith does not keep drawers locked. There is a third currency." Adela folded her hands. "Disappearance."

"Whose?"

"Yours. The child's. And — in every way that matters — mine." She said it evenly, as though reading a train timetable. "Consider it as Edith would consider it. A scandal requires a witness. A story requires a teller. If the woman who drove my motorcar, who carried my correspondence, who is known — and you are known, Marguerite, more than either of us ever intended — if that woman removes herself entirely, then what remains? A rumour about a sister nobody has seen since 1910. Edith's threats collapse without a corroborating shape. There is no Marguerite Sterling to point to. No letters in your handwriting. No satchel."

"And the child?"

"Goes where you were already taking her. To Kent. To the widow." A pause, thin as paper. "It is a good plan. Yours, I mean. I am merely proposing that its author execute it."

Marguerite heard the words arrive one by one, each with its own little detonation, and understood — in the way one understands drowning, all at once and far too late — the shape of the thing being offered. Not forgiveness. Not punishment, even. A trade. Her whole life, folded twice and sealed, handed over in exchange for the erasure of one page.

"You're asking me to leave the movement."

"I am instructing you to, and dressing it as an invitation, and we will both pretend otherwise because pretence is the last luxury either of us can afford." Adela rose and went to the window, her back straight, her reflection ghosted against the fogged glass. "You will take the child to Kent within two days — before Calloway widens his search, before Edith grows impatient. You will resign your post. You will give me your word that you will not write to me, nor to anyone in the organization, nor attend so much as a platform meeting, for as long as I live. In exchange—"

"In exchange nothing happens to me."

"In exchange nothing needs to happen to anyone. That is the entire elegance of it." She turned. The lamplight made her face half shadow. "Tom Ainsley stays on the evacuation list. His name moves to the top of it tonight. The workshop's people are scattered before the week is out. Your testimony — which I know you would never give, but which exists in you regardless, the way a splinter exists under skin — is removed from circulation. Everyone walks away. That is the bargain."

"And if I refuse?"

Adela came back toward the chair and stood over her, close enough that Marguerite could smell the bergamot on her gloves and beneath it the coal-smoke of every public meeting she had ever addressed.

"If you refuse, then I must do what any responsible person does with a liability. I remove you myself. I will say — regretfully, convincingly — that you embezzled movement funds. I have account books that can be made to agree with me. You will lose Tom, the work, the cause, the only version of yourself you have ever liked, and you will lose the child besides, because Edith will get what Edith wants either way, and she wants the hospital." She said it without heat, without cruelty even — that was the horror of it; it was spoken the way one reads a weather report. "I do not want to do that. I want very much not to do that. I am offering you the version of this night that lets us both continue to respect ourselves at breakfast. Take it."

* * *

Marguerite sat very still. The fire had gone out hours ago and the grate held only white ash, and she fixed her eyes on it because looking at Adela's face had become unbearable and looking away entirely would be surrender.

Two years. She measured her life in what fit in the satchel — she had said so once to Tom, jokingly, in the winter, over burnt coffee, and he had laughed and said *then yours is a very heavy life, Miss Sterling* — and now the satchel was being confiscated along with everything else. The dawn drives through empty streets with Adela beside her dictating letters. The first time a policeman saluted the car and she had felt, absurdly, that the salute was for her. The nights of burning papers in the grate, feeding the fire page by page, learning secrets the way other women learned recipes. The way Adela had once put her hand on Marguerite's shoulder after a bad rally and said *you are the steadiest person I know*, and Marguerite had walked home through the snow feeling lit from inside.

All of it, traded for a cottage in Kent and a child that was not hers.

Except — and here was the knife turning in its own wound — except that it *was* the best plan. She had lain awake constructing exactly this architecture: the private placement, the vanishing, the starving of the scandal until it died of want. Adela had not invented the bargain. Adela had simply caught her own plan out of the air, examined it, and improved upon it by adding the one clause Marguerite had refused to write: that she herself must go.

Because she was a witness. Because she was known. Because love, in Adela Crane's ledger, was a liability itemized like any other.

"You planned this before I came through the door," Marguerite said quietly. "The letter going missing only gave you the occasion. You'd been waiting."

"I had been considering," Adela said. "There is a difference. Planning implies appetite."

"Does it? Then call it whatever soothes you." Marguerite stood. Her legs held. She was surprised by that, and by the steadiness of her own voice, which sounded — she realized with a strange detachment — exactly like Adela's. Cold, level, parliamentary. The student's revenge: she had learned this tone in this woman's presence, learned it without meaning to, the way one learns an accent. "I'll take her to Kent. Day after tomorrow, as planned. I'll resign my post. I'll vanish."

"Marguerite—"

"But hear the terms as I understand them, so there is no confusion later, between us or in front of anyone else." She picked up the satchel and slung it across her body, and its weight was familiar and unbearable at once. "You keep your secret. I keep the child alive and untraceable. And Tom Ainsley walks out of Rivington Street free, whatever comes — if he is arrested, if there is a trial, if Calloway builds a case out of ink and type, you will spend every ounce of that formidable reputation getting him clear. Not because I ask. Because it is part of the price, and you have just told me you pay your debts."

The silence stretched. Fog pressed against the glass like something trying to get in.

"Agreed," Adela said at last.

"Then we're finished, Mrs. Crane." Marguerite reached the door. Her hand was on the latch when the words came out of her, unbidden, low, the truest thing she had said all evening: "You signed it in pencil. All of this. Every promise you've made me tonight. Pencil, because you mean to erase it."

She heard Adela draw breath behind her — to deny it, or perhaps, for once, to admit it — and she did not stay to learn which. She went down the stairs into the fog-wrapped street, past the girl at the washstand who still would not look up, out into the yellow-brown dark where every lamppost burned its small stubborn circle against the vast indifference of London.

Two days, then. One more dawn drive. One last delivery — the only cargo that had never been in the satchel at all.

She was halfway to the corner before she remembered she had forgotten to ask the question that mattered, and stopped dead in the middle of the empty street, fog coiling around her hem.

Who had searched her room — Adela herself, or the landlady? Because if it was the landlady, then the landlady could be paid by someone else. Someone patient, polite, with a Home Office manner and questions about printing licenses.

Someone who might already know about a widow in Kent.

Marguerite turned up her collar and walked faster, toward Rivington Street, toward Tom, carrying the bargain against her ribs like a stone — and telling herself, with every step, that the sound of footsteps beginning somewhere behind her was only the fog playing tricks, only the city, only the ordinary noise of being followed by the future.
