## 15. Night Move

The fog came up from the river that night the way it always did in November, rolling north over Holborn and settling into the streets like something that had paid rent. Tom Ainsley stood in the doorway of the workshop on Rivington Street with his collar turned up and his hands in his pockets, watching the lamplighter make his slow way along the far pavement, and he thought: *this is the last night this room smells of ink.*

He had set type in this room for two years. He knew it the way a man knows his own mouth — the loose brick behind the composing stone where the spare quoin keys lived, the particular complaint of the Albion press when you threw her weight too fast, the smell of the linseed rag boiled every second Thursday because Marguerite Sterling had said once that a dirty rag smelled like dishonesty and everyone had laughed and then, absurdly, obeyed her. She could do that. She could say a thing lightly and make it law.

She was upstairs now, in the safehouse next door, packing a satchel he had watched her carry for two years as though it were a part of her body. He did not know everything that went in it. He suspected nobody did — nobody except Adela Crane, and perhaps not even her.

"Tom." Whistler's voice, low, from inside. "Cart's coming."

Tom pushed off the doorframe and went in.

* * *

They worked by shielded light, which is to say they worked badly and quietly, which is worse than working badly and loudly if you are trying to move three presses out of a city that has begun, politely and without announcement, to close its hand around your throat.

The first cart came at half past eleven: a coal merchant's dray with a driver who never once looked at the workshop and was paid well enough never to. They took the Albion apart in the reverse order Tom had assembled her a hundred mornings — platen off, tympan wrapped in sacking, the iron body skidded down the step on greased planks while four men held their breath and the frame groaned like a ship. Every sound was enormous. Every sound was a knock at the door.

"Not so fast," whispered Dunmore, the old compositor, his hands steady on the rope while younger hands slipped. "Slow is quiet. Fast is bells."

So they were slow. Slow as a funeral. And all the while, out on the corner by the shuttered chandler's, a man stood under the gas lamp with an umbrella he did not open against the mist, smoking one cigarette down to the wire and lighting the next from its ember, and Tom counted him the way you count a tooth that has gone bad — with your tongue, over and over, hoping to be wrong.

Two watchers now. There had been one on Tuesday, loitering opposite the pie shop with the studied boredom of a man being paid by the hour. By Thursday there were two. Calloway's men were easy to know once you'd seen them; they had a politeness about them, a patience, the unhurried manner of clerks. The Home Office did not send wolves for printers. It sent bookkeepers.

"Second cart's not coming till one," Dunmore said. "That leaves the small press, the type, and the paper."

"The paper goes in the coal cart," Tom said. "Wrapped as fuel. If we're stopped, we're moving stock."

"And if they unwrap it?"

Tom looked at the bales of paper stacked against the wall — pamphlets half-printed, a hundred sheets of the new pass forms with their careful forged watermarks, the whole argument of their lives lying there in reams. "Then we're stopped," he said.

* * *

At ten past midnight the door opened without a knock, three short raps having been agreed upon and ignored, and Marguerite came in with fog in her hair and the baby bundled against her shoulder under her coat.

The room did what rooms do when she entered, though by now most of them would have denied it under torture: it arranged itself toward her. Dunmore straightened. Whistler put down his cigarette. Tom discovered he had been standing crooked for an hour and stood straight.

"She wouldn't settle," Marguerite said, by way of explanation or apology. "Adela's people are nervous tonight and babies hear it."

The child — she was nearly two now, toddling along the safehouse corridor on fat determined legs, saying *Da* indiscriminately at every man including the milkman — looked out at the room with grave dark eyes and then buried her face in Marguerite's neck, and Tom felt the familiar useless ache that had been living under his ribs since the first winter and had lately grown teeth.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. "Not with her. If it comes tonight—"

"Then I'm another pair of hands," Marguerite said. "Hold out your arms."

He held out his arms because there had never been a moment in two years when she asked him for anything and he did not hold out his arms, or his hands, or whatever she required, and pretend it cost him nothing. The baby transferred with the solemn deliberation of royalty and gripped his lapel and stared at him, and Marguerite stepped back and looked at the dismantled press, the sacked machinery, the bales going out to the dray, and he watched her take inventory of it the way she took inventory of everything — quickly, completely, and without any change of face.

"It's really happening," she said. Not a question.

"Tonight. All of it. The last cart before dawn, and after that anyone left in this building after six is either very brave or a witness."

"Which are you?"

"I haven't decided," Tom said, and saw her go still.

Because it was true, and they both knew it was true, and neither of them had said it aloud yet: someone had to stay. The plan — Adela's plan, delivered through Whistler with the flat economy of a woman signing death warrants before breakfast — required that when Calloway's men came to Rivington Street, they should come to a workshop that appeared to be in operation. A locked and empty building told them everything. A building with lights, with a man seen carrying type cases to a cab, with evidence of work interrupted — that told them almost nothing, bought hours, muddied the water. Someone had to be the interruption.

It did not need to be Tom. It needed to be somebody expendable, unmarried, unencumbered, whose arrest would cost the movement a typesetter and no more than that. There were three such men. Tom had simply, in the last week, made sure to be standing nearest whenever the question came up, the way you position yourself near a draft to stop it reaching someone else, and Marguerite had noticed — she noticed everything, it was her profession — and had said nothing, and her silence had been getting louder for days.

"Tom." Her voice was very even. That was how you knew. "The small press can be carried out by two men in twenty minutes. You could be in Manchester by Saturday."

"Yes," he said. The baby pulled at his collar. He detached the small fist gently and held it instead, and the child gripped his finger with astonishing conviction. "But then who tells them the lights are on?"

* * *

They loaded the second cart between one and two in the morning, and it went worse than the first, because the fog thinned briefly around half past and showed them to the street like items on a shelf.

Whistler went out first and stood in the middle of Rivington Street ostentatiously checking a pocket watch until the nearer watcher, bored beyond endurance or called away by something, drifted round the corner toward Wentworth Street. It bought them four minutes. In four minutes they got the small press onto the dray, and then the type — and the type was the heartbreak of the night, because type does not hurry. Forty cases of it, lead and wood, sorted and combed over two years by Dunmore's patient hands, and each case passed man to man down the line in the dark like buckets at a fire, and Tom found himself thinking that this was what the movement actually was: not the speeches in the halls, not Adela Crane's beautiful cold certainty, but this — forty cases of type passed hand to hand in the fog at two in the morning by people who would never be thanked.

Marguerite worked the line with her coat still smelling of the baby's warmth, passing cases, and once, when a case slipped between Dunmore's arms and the floorboards, she caught it one-handed against her hip without breaking rhythm, and Dunmore said, "God save us, girl," and she said, "Mind the ligatures, Mr. Dunmore," and Tom loved her so much and so hopelessly that he had to turn and count type cases for a while.

By three the room was bare. Bare rooms have a sound, he thought — not silence exactly, but a hollowness, like a mouth after speaking. The scorch marks on the floor where the stove had stood. The pale rectangles on the walls where notices had hung for two years. His composing stone, wiped clean, looking like a gravestone.

Whistler shook his hand. Dunmore shook his hand and held it a moment too long and said nothing, which from Dunmore was a eulogy. The carts went east toward the goods yard and the waiting railway, and the fog closed behind them, and Tom stood in the empty workshop with a candle, a chair, a table, a copy of yesterday's evening paper, and instructions to look busy.

* * *

Marguerite came back at four. He heard her before he saw her — the outer door, the pause, the three soft taps that meant nothing and were only habit — and when she came in she was alone, the baby handed off somewhere safe for the night to a woman in Clerkenwell whose name appeared in none of their papers, and she stood in the doorway of the emptied room and looked at him sitting at the table with the newspaper as if he were a portrait of something.

"They've gone?" she said.

"Gone. Manchester by Saturday, the rest scattered. You?"

"Letters to deliver before eight. The last round." She came further in. The candlelight did what it liked with her face; it always had. "I read them already. I read all of them. Old habit." A pause. "One of them is to Edith."

He knew about Edith. Everyone by now knew the shape of it if not the substance: the sister, the money, the threat hanging over Adela Crane like weather. He waited.

"It's not my business what's in it," Marguerite said, in the voice of a woman rehearsing a sentence she did not believe. Then, quieter, almost to herself: "I used to think that. Two years I've thought that. Trusted with everything except the truth, and grateful for it. Grateful." She looked at the bare walls. "You'd think a person could get tired of being trusted."

"Marguerite." He stood up. "Say the thing you came to say."

For a long moment she didn't. He watched her decide not to say several things — he could see them queueing behind her eyes — and then she crossed the room and stood close enough that he could smell the fog on her, coal smoke and rain and underneath it something like clean linen, and she said:

"Don't let them take you quietly. If Calloway comes, you make them charge you with something specific. Make them write it down. Paper, Tom. It's the only language they respect and the only weapon we keep."

"That's the message? Delivered before eight?"

"No." Her jaw tightened. "The message is—" and there she stopped, and the candle fluttered in the draught from the door, and for one suspended second the whole unsaid country between them lay open on the table between the newspaper and the candlestick, vast and surveyed and ready for settlement, and he opened his mouth to cross it.

And she stepped back.

"You'll want sleep," she said, in the level competent voice of Marguerite Sterling, driver of Mrs. Crane, keeper of letters, and picked up her gloves from where she had laid them on the table. "There's tea in the tin. Don't light the lamp until it's light; they watch for lamps."

"Marguerite—"

"At the door—" she paused with her hand on the latch, her back to him, and the fog stood in the open doorway beyond her like a congregation. Whatever she said next, he thought, would be the truest sentence of his life, and hers, and he braced for it the way you brace for weather.

She said: "Deliver your letters safe."

And was gone.

* * *

He sat back down at the table and did not read the paper. He listened to her boots on the cobbles, quick and even, fading south toward wherever the car was kept, toward the letters, toward Adela Crane and the whole grinding machinery of loyalty, and he thought about the arithmetic of the night: three presses saved, forty cases of type saved, four men saved, one man offered up, and one word — one single word — withheld by two people who were both, by profession and temperament, excellent at withholding.

*Sacrifice without ceremony*, he thought. That was the movement's whole liturgy. Nobody sang. You just carried the type.

At ten past five he lit the candle again, unfolded the newspaper with a crack that sounded obscene in the empty room, and arranged himself to be caught reading. Outside, footsteps returned to the corner by the chandler's — two men now, unhurried, bookkeeping. Somewhere across the city Calloway was asleep or pretending to be, and tomorrow, or the day after, or within the week — the raid would come down Rivington Street like the tide, and it would find Tom Ainsley, one candle, one chair, and a room scrubbed of everything except the crime of looking interrupted.

He turned the page. He read about shipping rates. He thought: *if they take me, she'll know by the evening paper, and she'll be in some lane in Kent or a yard in Manchester, and she won't cry, because she doesn't, and I never told her, and she never told me, and we will have managed the only cowardice of our lives in perfect coordination.*

The footsteps crossed the street.

The knock, when it came at last — heavy, official, three raps that meant everything — landed on the door at twenty past six, with the fog thinning into a grey ordinary morning and the lamps going pale, and Tom Ainsley folded his newspaper, stood, and went to answer it as the first cart of the raid rattled into Rivington Street behind a polite man in a good coat who was not smiling, and who was carrying, Tom saw with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear, a piece of paper that looked very much like a warrant — and very little like a warrant for pamphlets.
