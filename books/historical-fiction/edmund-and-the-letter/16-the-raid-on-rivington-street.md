## 16. The Raid on Rivington Street

The presses went quiet at half past eleven, and in that silence Tom Ainsley heard the street.

He had lived above Rivington Street for two years and knew its noises the way a man knows his own breathing — the milk cart at five, the shutters of the tobacconist at eight, the arguments that bloomed outside the public house and wilted by midnight. Silence was not one of them. Rivington Street was never silent; it only ever pretended, the way a hunted animal pretends, ears up, heart hammering, waiting to see what the stillness conceals.

He stood at the workshop window with the lamp turned low and watched the fog move down the street like something poured rather than blown. It came off the rooftops in slow ropes, thickening around the gas lamps until each one wore a halo of dirty gold. Somewhere beyond it, boots on pavement — not one pair but several, moving with the unhurried coordination of men who had been told where to go and did not need to hurry.

"Tom." Mrs. Pargett's voice from the stair, a whisper worn smooth by use. "They're here."

"I know."

"Cart's two streets gone by now. Presses with it." She came up beside him, smelling of ink and cold tea, her grey hair pinned crooked against the night's work. "You don't have to be the one who stays."

"Yes I do." He said it lightly, because it was true and truth carried better that way. "Someone has to answer the door looking bored. If they find an empty building they'll tear the whole row apart looking for where we went. If they find me asleep over a typesetting case and a half-finished pamphlet about parliamentary hypocrisy—" he gestured at the bench, where he had left a page deliberately unfinished, four lines short, the em quads still loose in their stick— "they'll find exactly what they expected to find. A printer. Nothing more."

"They took three men out of Clerkenwell last month for less than a pamphlet."

"And released two of them." He smiled at her, and saw her not believe him, and loved her a little for the stubbornness of her worry. "Go on, Mrs. P. The cart won't wait for goodbyes and neither will the night."

She gripped his wrist once — hard, brief, the whole of her farewell compressed into five fingers — and went down the stairs. He heard the street door, heard it close soft as a drawer.

Then he banked the lamp, sat himself at the bench, and began setting type he had no intention of printing.

* * *

They gave him forty minutes. Long enough to set two columns' worth of words about liberty and the shame of the House of Commons, long enough to read each word twice and think, absurdly, of Marguerite reading letters by lamplight — she who read everything, remembered everything, burned what could not be kept. He wondered where she was tonight. He had kissed her hand at the corner of Chancery Lane nine hours ago and she had looked at him the way she looked at sealed envelopes, as though there were a message inside her she was not yet permitted to open. *When this is done,* he had almost said. *When this is done I'll tell you the thing I've been carrying.* He had not said it. Some words you keep for a day when the street is not full of police.

The knock, when it came, was almost gentle. Three raps, polite as a parson.

Tom wiped his hands on the rag, leaving fresh black crescents across his palms, and opened the door.

There were six of them in the fog, and one of them was Calloway.

The inspector stood slightly forward of his men, hatless under an umbrella held by a constable, and he looked at Tom the way Tom had seen him look at the mailing list — patiently, thoroughly, as if memorizing him for later use. His coat was dry. His face was mild. Everything about him was mild, which was precisely what made him terrible.

"Mr. Ainsley," Calloway said. "Working late."

"It's a living."

"So it is." The inspector stepped past him into the workshop without asking, the way a man steps into his own kitchen. His eyes moved along the benches, the cabinets, the stone floor, the ceiling beams, cataloguing. "May I ask what you're printing?"

"A pamphlet."

"On?"

"The dignity of the working man."

"Mm." Calloway lifted the galley Tom had been filling and read it upside down without apparent effort. Something moved behind his face — not anger. Disappointment, perhaps, or its cousin. "Where are the other presses, Mr. Ainsley?"

"What other presses?"

"This shop lists three machines on its insurance schedule. I count one." He set the galley down with a small click, precise as punctuation. "One press. One apprentice — absent. One foreman — present. No stock to speak of. Either your business is failing, Mr. Ainsley, or your business was never printing."

"My business is printing."

"Then you'll have no objection to showing me your order book."

And that was the trap, Tom saw, laid flat and simple in the lamplight. The order book was clean — Mrs. Pargett had scrubbed it herself, substituting innocent commissions for the names of every committee and correspondent — but the substitutions were recent, the ink of some entries not quite matching the rest, and Calloway would see it, because seeing things was what Calloway *did*. The order book would hang him or it wouldn't, and either way the real cargo — the passes, the plates, the letters, the whole paper heart of the movement — was rolling south out of London in the dark, and every minute Tom kept this mild man standing in this room was another mile between the presses and the net.

So he shrugged. "It's on the shelf. Help yourself."

Calloway helped himself. For a long while there was no sound in the workshop but the tick of the cooling press and the turning of pages and, far off, the fog-muffled roar of Shoreditch going about its secret business. Tom set more type while the constables went through the drawers with cotton gloves. He set the word *citizen* and the word *shame* and the word *vote*, sliding them into their places one by one, small metal certainties, and he thought: whatever happens to me tonight, these letters are still true. They cannot arrest the alphabet.

At last Calloway closed the book.

"You've nothing to say to me," the inspector said quietly. It was not quite a question.

"I've said my piece. Pamphlet's nearly finished if you'd like to wait for it."

Something flickered then — the ghost of respect, or the shadow of regret, hard to tell which in that face. "Mr. Ainsley," he said, settling his hat on his head, "I am obliged to detain you and this premises pending further inquiry. You may bring your coat."

"That's kind." Tom fetched his coat from the peg, and the letter from its lining — the placement certificate, folded small, already delivered days ago into Marguerite's keeping, so that the paper he carried now was only a receipt for lamp oil and a tram ticket, the most innocent litter of his life. He let the constables take his arms at the door, and paused there, one boot on the threshold, and looked back at his shop: the press sitting patient as a horse in its traces, the type he would never distribute, the lamp he had forgotten to turn fully out throwing its small gold circle onto the floorboards like a coin left behind on purpose.

"Mind the em quads," he told the nearest constable. "They roll."

* * *

Marguerite learned of it from a newspaper bought at a railway platform in Tonbridge, and the learning arrived the way all the worst things arrived that year: folded, printed, and impossible to put down.

She had chosen the slow train deliberately — the stopping service, third class, a carriage full of soldiers' mothers and commercial travellers, because slow trains were not watched and fast ones were, and because the baby slept better with the rhythm of frequent halts. The child — she could no longer call her *the child*, even in her own head; she was Lucy now, the name Tom had entered on the forged certificate in his beautiful upright hand, *Lucy Sterling, infant, placed to the care of Mrs. E. Hartley of Pluckley, Kent, being the niece of the said party* — Lucy lay against her shoulder under a shawl, breathing in the small hot way of sleeping infants, entirely unaware that she was contraband.

The newspaper came aboard at Tonbridge with a porter's cart. Marguerite bought it for the baby's sake — something to shield her face from the window, something ordinary — and unfolded it with the idle intention of skimming the shipping news.

*Raid on Shoreditch Printers,* said the headline. *Home Office Action Against Sedition.*

The carriage seemed to tilt. She read it twice, the words refusing to settle into sense the first time, swimming like type under water.

*Acting upon information received, Inspector H. Calloway of the Home Office yesterday evening conducted a search of premises in Rivington Street, Shoreditch, believed to be connected with the production of seditious pamphlets and forged documents. Four persons were taken into custody, including the proprietor of the establishment, a Mr. T. Ainsley, aged twenty-six. A Home Office spokesman declined to speculate as to whether further arrests are expected.*

Four persons. Taken into custody. Including—

She made herself breathe. Around her the carriage went on living its indifferent life: someone laughed at the far end, a kettle whistled somewhere near the engine, the soldier's mother opposite offered her a barley sugar and she took it with fingers that did not shake, because ten years of carrying secrets had taught her hands obedience even when the rest of her was falling.

*A subsequent examination of the premises,* the article continued, *revealed little of interest to the authorities beyond a single printing press and quantities of ink and type material. It is understood that no documentary evidence of substance was recovered.*

No documentary evidence of substance.

Marguerite read that sentence four times. And then, slowly, folding the newspaper into quarters, eighths, a hard small block she pressed against her mouth so that no one would see what her face was doing — laughing or weeping, she honestly could not have said — she let the understanding arrive.

Nothing. Ink and type. They had found *nothing*.

Every letter she had carried for two years — every pass, every list, every page written in Adela Crane's careful pencil — was already gone. Moved south in the night on carts she had helped load, driven by hands she trusted, hidden in places Calloway's men would need a year and a miracle to find. Tom had stayed behind to stand in front of an empty room and be arrested for a pamphlet, and his sacrifice had worked, was working at this very moment, was rolling through the dark countryside in the same direction she was, and he was in a cell for it, and she was free, and the baby slept against her collarbone smelling of milk and soap, and victory and guilt arrived in the same hour and refused, thereafter, to leave.

She looked out the window. Kent was coming on green and slow — orchards, oast houses, a church spire dissolving in drizzle. The land of the widow Hartley. The end of the errand.

*Forgive me,* she thought — not to God, particularly; she had never been sure anyone specific was listening, though she addressed her thoughts to someone all the same. To Tom, then. To Tom in his cell. *Forgive me for being the part that got away.*

But even as she thought it, she knew he would refuse the apology absolutely. That was the whole of him, the whole strange gift of him: he had chosen the empty room so that she would not have to choose it. Love as complicity, love as arithmetic, love as a door held open in a raid. When this was done — when he walked out of wherever they were keeping him — she would tell him everything. She would tell him about Edith's threats and Adela's pencil and the confession she was carrying folded against her ribs like a splinter working toward the heart. But that was tomorrow's freight. Today she had one delivery left, and it weighed nine pounds and slept against her shoulder, and it was the only consignment in all her years of carrying that she was glad to be rid of and broken to lose.

The train slowed for a halt. Signal arms dipped against the wet sky. The soldier's mother leaned over and touched Lucy's shawl with one knuckle. "Yours?" she asked.

Mine for one more hour, Marguerite thought. Mine in every way that matters, and in no way the law will recognize.

"Yes," she said, and was surprised to find it came out steady. "She's mine."

The train gathered itself and rolled on into Kent, and Marguerite Sterling unfolded the newspaper again with her free hand and read the article one last time, committing it to memory — the date, the wording, the phrase *no documentary evidence of substance* — because memory was the satchel she could never be searched, and somewhere in London a man in a cell might one day need to know exactly what his night's work had bought.

She did not yet know that a motor car waited at Pluckley station. She did not yet know that Edith Crane had hired it, or what price silence had climbed to since morning.
