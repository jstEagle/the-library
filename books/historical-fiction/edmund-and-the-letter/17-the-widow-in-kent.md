## 17. The Widow in Kent

The train left London in a grey light that seemed less like morning than the memory of one, and Marguerite sat against the window with the child beneath her cloak and did not sleep.

The baby had decided, with the arbitrary mercy of the very young, that this was an adventure. She had slept through Clapham Junction and woken at Tonbridge, and by the time the carriage emptied and the hop country began — the hills stitched with strings, the oasts standing like fat white bells asleep in the fields — she had discovered Marguerite's cravat and conducted a long, silent negotiation with its end, her fist closing and pulling, closing and pulling, as though testing whether the world held things that could be gripped.

Marguerite let her hold on. There would be someone else's collar for her soon enough.

She had rehearsed the journey the way she had once rehearsed deliveries: the route, the timings, the small contingencies of failure. In her satchel — the old satchel, the one she had measured her life by for three years — there was now only what Tom had made: the certificate naming Mrs. Hartwell of Kent as the child's aunt, the letter of introduction in his careful hand, forty pounds in notes folded into a chemist's envelope, and a christening gown that belonged to no history at all. The rest of the satchel's life had been burned or moved or scattered ahead of Calloway's men, and Marguerite had found, packing it that last morning, that she felt its emptiness not as loss but as something closer to lightness — the strange vertigo of a woman setting down weight without knowing yet whether her arms will remember how to hang.

*You take her yourself,* Adela had said, in the voice she used for motions and resolutions, the voice that made requests into facts. *No one else. If you give her to a courier, you give her to chance.*

And beneath it, unspoken but present as pressure on a bruise: *and then you go too, Marguerite, and my secret goes with you, and we are both of us finished with one another.*

The child pulled the cravat again. Outside, rain had begun, soft and sideways, silvering the hedgerows.

* *

Mrs. Hartwell met them at the crossroads, as arranged, under an umbrella of faded green and a bonnet that must have been twenty years old and kept like a friend.

Tom had described her in the forged papers and then, later, in truth — he had found her through a printer's cousin who knew a sexton who owed him a favour, which was how all honest things were done in that season — and he had said: *she lost a daughter to consumption two winters back. She is not desperate, exactly. She is empty, and she minds the shape of the empty.* Marguerite had thought she understood that sentence until she saw the woman's face when the cloak was drawn back and the child looked up at her with the grave, unhurried appraisal she gave to all new worlds.

"Well," said Mrs. Hartwell. Not to anyone. Just *well*, the way one says it standing before a thing too large for speech. Then she gathered the umbrella over all three of them and said, "It's half a mile, and it's coming down proper. Can you carry her, or shall I?"

"I'll carry her," Marguerite said, and heard how her arms tightened, and saw that the widow saw it, and said nothing more.

They walked the lane together, wheels of some distant cart groaning along the turnpike, rooks lifting out of a ploughed field like scraps of burnt paper. The rain smelled of chalk and wet wood and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the cottage chimney ahead — a low, long-built place with roses gone leggy against the wall and a bucket catching drips at the corner of the porch, ringing softly, patiently, like a clock that counted nothing.

Inside it was warm and close and smelled of bread and lamp oil and lavender kept in a dish on the range. There was a chair by the fire already drawn up, Marguerite noticed. A shawl over its arm. A cradle — no, not a cradle; a drawer lined with a blanket, the old expedient, and beside it a proper basket, new, with ribbon on the handle, bought within the week. The widow had known for days that a child was coming and had spent those days preparing, and something about the ribbon on the basket undid Marguerite more thoroughly than any argument ever had.

"You'll take tea," Mrs. Hartwell said. It was not a question. She took the baby with both hands, the practiced swoop of a woman who had done this work before and been hollowed by its ending, and the child went to her — went easily, went *willingly*, gripping the green wool of her collar just as she had gripped the cravat — and Marguerite stood in the doorway with the rain running off her hem and understood that the hardest part was not going to be the leaving. It was this. The easiness.

She sat for tea because refusing kindness now would have been its own cruelty. Bread and butter and black tea poured from a brown pot, and the widow talked — about the hens, about the state of the lane in winter, about a nephew in Ashford who could drive them to register whatever needed registering, "for papers are papers, and I don't hold with mysteries where a child's naming's concerned." She said it comfortably, without suspicion, and Marguerite thought: *if only you knew what your honesty is worth, madam. It would buy half of Whitehall.*

The fire ticked. The child slept against the widow's shoulder, one fist open against the green wool, and Marguerite watched the rise and fall of that small back and tried, methodically, to fix it — the exact weight of the head she had carried against her own collarbone for six nights; the particular smell of her, milk and soap and something warmer underneath, something that was simply *baby* and belonged to no one; the way she frowned in sleep, a tiny vertical crease between the brows that was, God help them all, an echo of another frown Marguerite had seen across a hundred committee rooms.

*Forget the face,* the first note had said, in pencil.

*I am making a poor job of it,* Marguerite thought. *I have been making a poor job of it since April.*

"Her name," the widow said, into the quiet. "On the certificate it says — well, it says what it says, and I'm not asking after the paper. I mean for myself. What do you call her, day to day? A child wants a name in her ears, not just in ink."

"Eleanor," Marguerite said, because Tom had chosen it, and because it was the first lie of the whole enterprise that felt almost like a gift. "Eleanor."

"Eleanor." The widow tried it the way one tries a key. It turned. She nodded, satisfied, and shifted the sleeping child higher, and then she looked at Marguerite across the small warm room — directly, without coyness, with the flat clear gaze of a woman who had buried a daughter and therefore had no patience left for the games of the living — and asked:

"Whose is she?"

The kettle ticked on the range. Rain came off the porch in threads. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and gave up.

Every answer Marguerite owned rose in her at once, the whole trained arsenal of them: *the sister of a friend; a family misfortune; a girl who died; a girl who married badly; none of your concern, madam, and here is the certificate.* Three years of carrying other women's secrets had taught her a hundred doors through which truth might be smuggled out dressed as anything but itself. She opened her mouth to choose a door.

And closed it.

Because the widow had earned better, and because Marguerite was so tired of doors, and because — she understood it in that moment with the clarity of cold water — she was never going to be asked this question again. Whatever waited for her after this cottage — Manchester, the provincial press, the long apprenticeship of an ordinary life — no one there would ever stand across a warm room holding her whole history in their hands and ask the one question that mattered. This was the last door. It would never open again.

"A woman," Marguerite said, "who loved her and couldn't keep her."

It came out steady. That surprised her. The widow watched her a moment longer, reading the words for forgery the way Marguerite herself read letters, and finding none — or finding, perhaps, the one forgery that is indistinguishable from truth because it is true in every particular except comfort.

"That'll do," Mrs. Hartwell said at last. "That'll do fine." And then, softer, turning her cheek against the child's hair: "Plenty can't love what they keep. Loving's the rare part."

Marguerite set down her cup. Her hands were perfectly steady, which struck her as a small betrayal of everything happening inside her.

* *

She stayed an hour more, no longer, because staying longer would have been theft.

The widow walked her to the gate under the green umbrella, the child transferred at the last moment back to Marguerite's arms for the goodbyes — a mercy or a cruelty, Marguerite could not decide, and suspected the two had long ago become the same thing. Eleanor woke, considered the rain with distaste, and reached past Marguerite's shoulder toward the cottage chimney smoke, the warm room, the basket with its ribbon, with the whole-hearted imperative of a small creature who knows where she belongs.

"She's a sensible article," the widow said approvingly. "Knows a roof when she sees one."

"Yes," Marguerite said. Her voice cracked precisely halfway through the word, and she converted it, mid-break, into a cough, and pressed the child back into the green wool arms, and stepped away, and shut the gate.

The latch clicked. It was a small sound. It went through her like a bayonet going through cloth.

She walked down the lane and did not look back. She had told herself this on the train, sternly, the way one tells oneself not to touch a hot iron, and she obeyed it now with the whole rigid force of her will — eyes forward, boots in the mud, the lane unspooling grey and silver ahead — while behind her she heard the cottage door close, and heard, muffled by rain and distance, a voice beginning to sing. Something low and tuneless and domestic. A song with no words she could catch, that was not meant for her, that would go on being sung in that kitchen for years, at bathtimes and bruised knees and Christmas eves, to a child who would grow up thinking the song had always belonged to her.

*She will be loved,* Marguerite told the rain. *That was the whole of the arithmetic. She will be loved, and I will be elsewhere, and that is the bargain, and I have paid worse prices for worse causes.*

It was almost true. It would do until nightfall.

At the bend of the lane she permitted herself one indulgence, planned in advance like all her crimes: she stopped as if adjusting her boot, and in the small mirror inside her hatbrim — a driver's habit, checking the road behind — she caught one last frame of the cottage: chimney smoke, leggy roses, the green umbrella folding itself away on the porch.

One frame. Then she straightened the boot, and put the mirror away, and walked on, and the picture closed over behind her like water.

* *

The trap from Ashford was waiting where Tom's cousin had promised, driven by a silent man who tipped his hat and asked nothing, and Marguerite sat in it with her empty satchel on her knees and watched the hop gardens run backwards through the rain and felt, gradually, the full weight of the day arrive in her body — not grief exactly, or not only grief, but the specific exhaustion of a woman who has set down the last of her burdens and discovered that the burdens were the scaffolding.

She was nobody's courier now. Nobody's driver, nobody's keeper, nobody's archive. Adela Crane's secret was walking away from her at four miles an hour behind a horse, and tomorrow it would belong entirely to the past, and she would belong to Manchester, and a printing press, and a man who had confessed to loving her in a forge-lit workshop while forging papers for a stolen child, and all of it stretched ahead of her like the wet road: ordinary, unmapped, terrifyingly free.

She opened the satchel. Nothing in it now but her own things — a hairpin, a half-crown, the chemist's envelope gone soft with handling. Three years of her life, and it fit in one hand.

The trap crested the ridge above the turnpike, and there, below, where the lanes converged by the milestone, Marguerite saw the carriage.

It was too fine for the road and too wrong for the weather — a closed brougham, dark green, polished to a shine that the rain could not dull, with a pair of grays standing steaming under the trees and a driver who sat very still in the way of men paid to notice everything. Marguerite knew carriages the way priests know prayers. She knew that livery. She had seen it, three years ago, drawn up outside a house in Kensington where a young woman with fierce eyes and a pencil had hired her on the strength of a handshake, and she had seen it since outside drawing-rooms and garden parties and once, memorably, outside the House of Commons in an evening drizzle, waiting for the member for —

The member for somewhere important. The husband.

The trap slowed. The brougham's door opened before either vehicle had fully stopped, unhurried, deliberate, the gesture of a woman who had never in her life waited for a door to be opened for her — and there in the aperture, wrapped in grey fur despite the season, sat Edith.

Edith, who had returned the child. Edith, whom Adela had paid. Edith, who had wanted money and then, when the money proved insufficient, had wanted something that money could not buy — and Marguerite's hand closed on the empty satchel as every instinct honed by three years of reading letters told her, coldly and completely, what a woman like this did next.

"Well," said Edith Crane, pleasantly, into the rain. "How convenient. I had hoped to find you before you vanished north. Do get in, Miss Sterling. We should talk about sisters — and about trials."

Behind her in the trap, the silent driver reined in. Ahead, the grays tossed their heads, and the rain came down on the milestone, and Marguerite understood that she had not yet finished paying.
