## 18. Edith's Price

The road out of Kent unspooled grey and wet beneath the fly-wheels, and Marguerite rode it the way one rides away from a graveside — too fast to think, too slow to forget.

She had done it. The child was delivered. The widow's face — broad, sun-creased, kind in the practical way of women who kept hens and orphans with equal efficiency — was already becoming a memory Marguerite would have to manage carefully, the way she managed everything: folded small, packed tight, carried without looking at it directly. *A woman who loved her and couldn't keep her.* She had said it aloud and the saying had torn something loose in her chest that was still bleeding as the countryside gave way to hedgerows and the hedgerows gave way to the brick shoulders of the outer suburbs.

Rain came on again past Sevenoaks, a fine insistent drizzle that beaded on the leather of her driving coat and turned the lane into a ribbon of pewter. She had taken the motor only as far as Maidstone and surrendered it there to a man who asked no questions for cash paid upfront; from now on she moved by train and trap and her own two feet, a woman travelling light, a woman with nothing in her satchel but one letter that weighed more than all the others ever had.

She did not see the carriage until it drew alongside her on the Tonbridge road.

It happened with the smooth inevitability of something long rehearsed. A smart brougham, dark green, matched greys, slowed at her elbow, and the window let down with a soft clatter, and there — framed against the velvet squabs like a portrait hung slightly crooked — was Edith Crane.

Or rather, Edith *Fairhurst* now; she had taken back her maiden name's correction years ago, or so Adela's careful memoranda had it. It hardly mattered. The face was the same face Marguerite had studied in the photograph on Adela's desk: Adela's bone structure softened by indulgence, Adela's mouth given over entirely to displeasure.

"You walk like a woman with an errand half done," Edith said. Her voice was low, well-bred, unhurried. "Get in, Miss Sterling. You'll catch your death, and I have a proposition that will keep better in the dry."

Marguerite did not break stride. Two years of London traffic had taught her that a motor could follow a walker indefinitely, and that dignity was best preserved by continuing to move. But the rain thickened, and the alternative was three miles to the station in a soaking, and pride was a luxury that belonged to women who hadn't just handed a baby to a stranger in Kent.

She stopped. She opened the door herself. She climbed in and sat opposite, spine straight, satchel flat across her knees, hands folded upon it like a woman in church.

The window went up. The carriage resumed its progress, wheels hissing on wet gravel.

"You've been to see the child placed," Edith said. It was not a question.

"I've been to Kent," Marguerite said. "What I did there is my affair."

"Then we shall both pretend." Edith smiled, and it was terrible, because it was Adela's smile worn by someone who had never needed to hide anything behind it. "You know, I wondered for months which of Adela's people would crack first. I had money on the typesetter. He has honest eyes. Honest eyes are a weakness in your trade."

"You know nothing about my trade."

"I know you're sitting in my carriage with a letter in that bag that was never delivered. Don't look startled — I'm not omniscient, merely patient. My sister writes to me when she wants something. When she wants something very much, she writes in ink, and includes money, and hopes I'll go away. This time she wrote in ink, included money, and hoped I'd believe the child was in the foundling hospital, where intake records drown themselves quietly within the year." Edith leaned forward. Rain-light moved across her face. "But hospitals keep registers, Miss Sterling, even untraceable ones. Registers can be consulted. And a friend of mine consulted, and found no entry matching the dates. Which means my niece was not surrendered to the state. She was placed privately. By someone. With someone. In a cottage. In Kent."

The carriage swayed around a turn. Somewhere ahead, a woodcock burst from the hedge and was gone into the rain, and Marguerite envied it with her whole body.

"If you've had her watched—"

"I've had *you* watched. There's a difference, and it matters to what comes next." Edith settled back, arranging her gloves finger by finger, a small ceremony of composure. "You took her yourself. You didn't trust the hospital — quite right too, they lose children the way governments lose papers — so you carried her to a widow who will raise her as her own and never ask twice. It was, I confess, better done than I expected. Adela chooses loyal people and then wastes them."

"She doesn't waste anyone."

"No? What are you, then, at this moment? Sitting in a stranger's carriage, soaked to the knee, carrying evidence that could hang your lover's—" Edith paused, tasting the word, finding it useful, "—your *colleague*, while the woman you broke every rule for pretends you've gone to plant roses somewhere."

"Tom is not—" Marguerite stopped. Too late she heard the shape of what she'd almost confirmed.

Edith heard it too. Her smile deepened by a single degree. "Your colleague," she repeated softly. "Whose trial is set for the fourteenth. Whose name appeared in the papers this morning beside the words *incitement* and *conspiracy* and *forgery of official documents*. Who took the whole weight of Rivington Street onto his own shoulders so that others could scatter — I read the depositions, Miss Sterling; I have friends among the clerks too. A man who did that is either a saint or a fool, and either way he will be transported or imprisoned for years, because the Crown needs a body and the Home Office hates to leave a room empty-handed."

Outside, the rain worked steadily at the fields. The horses' hooves struck fire from the flints in the road. Marguerite became aware, distantly, that her heart was behaving badly — not hammering but stuttering, missing beats the way a clock misses when something has got inside the works.

"What do you want?" she said.

"*Now* we are being honest." Edith drew off one glove. "Money bought my silence about the existence of a child. That was cheap. Existence is an abstraction; abstractions don't shame anyone. But a child placed privately, growing up ten miles from where Adela entertains cabinet wives — that is not an abstraction. That is a time bomb with dimples. So my price has changed."

"I don't carry money anymore."

"I know precisely what you carry." Edith's eyes went to the satchel and stayed there. "I want it in her hand. Her hand, holding her pen, pressing down hard enough to bite the paper. I want Adela Crane — Mrs. Crane, wife of the honourable member for Bradford North, that pious hyena — to write me the truth. All of it. Brighton. The year abroad. The birth. The letters to 'E'. Why she sent my niece away with instructions to *forget the face*. Every word she has spent eight years making sure no living soul possessed."

* * *

They had stopped, at some point, before a gated lodge with chestnuts dripping overhead, and Marguerite stared through the wet glass at nothing and understood that she had been here before — not in this carriage, not on this road, but in this exact position: holding other people's truths in her lap, deciding what deserved to survive the day.

Everyone bargained. That was the whole machinery of the world, once you got close enough to see the gears. Adela bargained with duty; Calloway bargained with procedure; Tom had bargained with his own freedom on a night of carts and lanterns, staying behind with his sleeves rolled and his joke ready so the others could slip into the dark. And now Edith bargained with a sister's ruin, and wanted it notarised.

"Why?" Marguerite said. "Why do you want it written? You already know most of it. Blackmail works just as well on rumour."

"Does it?" For the first time, something moved under Edith's composure — quick, raw, quickly covered. "A rumour can be denied. A rumour dies with its teller. I have been the keeper of my sister's rumour for eight years, Miss Sterling, and I will tell you what it has earned me: Christmas alone, letters answered through solicitors, and a sister who looked at me across a breakfast table in Geneva and said, *you may have the boy's memory, Edith, but you will not have this*. She thinks grief is a currency only she knows how to count." The glove creased in Edith's fist. "I want it in ink because ink cannot be retracted. Because when Adela writes it down she must say, in her own hand, that she chose the cause over the child, the movement over me, the *speeches* over a baby's crying — and having said it, she can never again pretend she was forced. She was not forced. None of us were forced. That is the part she has never forgiven any of us for knowing."

The rain eased. Through the gap in the chestnut branches, weak sunlight lay along the wet road like a blade laid down but not put away.

"And if she refuses?" Marguerite asked.

"Then I speak to her husband. Not the press — the press is vulgar and forgives clever women. A husband is under no such obligation. He has wanted an excuse to divorce her since 1909; I shall simply hand him a better one than the ones he invents nightly. And the trial—" Edith shrugged, elegant, merciless. "The trial proceeds. Your typesetter hangs his future on the movement's respectability, and respectability is precisely the thing I shall remove. Without Adela's good name behind the defence, he is just a forger with ink on his cuffs."

"That's not justice. That's leverage."

"Of course it is. What did you think it was — *principles*?" Edith laughed, short and bitter as a cough. "Welcome to the family business, Miss Sterling. We deal in truth the way other families deal in land. Badly, and forever, and always at each other."

* * *

The carriage set her down at the station as dusk came on, lamps waking yellow along the platform, and Marguerite stood a long time after the sound of the greys faded, watching the rails run together in the distance to a single bright thread.

She thought about the letter in her satchel. Adela's confession existed already in miniature — she had read it, memorised it, burned the original in the grate at Rivington Street a lifetime ago, and could reproduce it line for line in a hand that was nearly Adela's own, so long practised had hers become at forging her mistress's pencil. She could give Edith what she wanted tonight and Adela need never know. That was one door.

Behind the second door stood Tom in the dock at the Old Bailey, his honest eyes finding hers across the courtroom, believing — because she had never yet lied to him — that the movement, that Adela, that *she* would find some way to pull the evidence out from under him. And she knew now, with the cold certainty of the Kent road still in her bones, that Adela would not. Adela's arithmetic had no column for Tom Ainsley. If his conviction served the silence, his conviction was affordable. Everything was affordable to Adela except the truth spoken aloud.

Unless it was written.

And there was the hinge, wasn't it. The confession Marguerite held in her memory could save Tom only if it surfaced in the right quarter — if the raid's tidy evidence were suddenly complicated by a document proving the Home Office's true interest, the child, the cover-up, the reason a minister's wife had needed pamphlets burned and drivers exiled. But a document like that required Adela's hand. Required Adela to *know*. Required Marguerite to stand in front of the woman who had given her purpose and demand, plainly, at last: write it, or he goes under, and I go to Edith myself.

It would cost her everything Adela had built her into. Perhaps it would cost Tom his liberty anyway. Perhaps it would cost the cause its figurehead on the eve of the vote, and twenty years of work would gutter out because Marguerite Sterling had once read a letter by lamplight and remembered it.

The train came in breathing steam. She boarded it. She sat with the satchel on her knees and watched Surrey slide past in the wet dark, every lit window a household keeping its own secrets competently, and by the time the lights of London rose up ahead of her — vast, orange, indifferent — she had stopped pretending there was a choice.

There was only an order of operations, and a door she had to open first, and a pen she had to put into Adela Crane's hand.

The train slowed. Somewhere beyond the platform crowd, in the direction of Holloway, a cell was waiting for a typesetter with ink-stained fingers, and Marguerite stepped down onto the wet boards with her satchel gripped in both hands, and turned toward the house on Gower Street where the lamps were always lit, and where the woman who signed in pencil was waiting to learn what her driver had decided to carry home.
