## 20. The Trial of Tom Ainsley

The cell smelled of wet stone and other men's fear, and Tom Ainsley had learned to measure time by the sounds that came through it — the rattle of the bolt, the shuffle of boots, the particular cough of the warder who had a wife in Holloway and therefore treated the suffragists' accomplices with something like tenderness. Morning was the kettle two floors down. Noon was light crossing the high window in a slow grey bar that never quite reached his hand no matter how he stretched. Night was the river, faintly, and the memory of ink.

He had been eleven days in custody when they came to tell him there would be a trial after all.

"Tuesday," the warder said, not unkindly, through the grille. "Old Bailey. Sedition and conspiracy, they're calling it. Printing without license to begin with, but the Crown wants it dressed up proper."

"And if I'm convicted?"

The warder looked at him the way men look at a horse they have been ordered to lead to the knacker. "Two years, likely. Could be more, given who you printed for." He shifted. "There's talk upstairs. Something's gone wrong with their evidence."

"Something always has," Tom said, and lay back on the plank bed, and did not sleep.

* * *

They gave him a borrowed collar and tie for the trial, and a coat that hung off his shoulders like a shed skin. In the van he sat across from a boy of perhaps nineteen who had been caught carrying seditious pamphlets to Brighton, and the boy's hands would not stop moving, folding and unfolding a scrap of paper, and Tom found himself talking — about typesetting, about the way a well-cut letter sits in its body size the way a stone sits in a wall, bearing weight without being seen — until the boy's hands went still.

"You love it," the boy said, wondering, as though it were strange to love anything that had got you arrested.

"I love what it makes possible," Tom said. "That's different, and more dangerous."

The Old Bailey swallowed them at half past nine. He had imagined the courtroom a hundred times in eleven days, and it was nothing and all of it: the gas lamps turned low against the pale morning, the smell of beeswax and damp wool, the great dark panelling going up and up like the inside of a coffin built for a city. He was put in the dock with three others — Callum, who ran the press; young Ruth Ellery, who had distributed; a Welshman named Pryce whom Tom had known only as a voice on the stairs. The fourth chair stayed empty. That was him. He stood apart, because he had stayed behind, and staying behind was its own kind of testimony.

Counsel for the prosecution was a dry, precise man with a voice like a ledger being totalled. He laid out the raid on Rivington Street with the satisfaction of a man describing a trap that had sprung: the presses seized, the type confiscated, the half-set forme of pamphlets still standing on the stone, and — here he paused, letting the courtroom lean in — letters. Correspondence. Documentary proof, he said, linking the workshop to persons prominent in public life.

Tom felt the dock's iron rail cold under his hands and made himself breathe the way you breathe when setting type by feel alone, in the dark, when one wrong sort and the whole page betrays you.

"The correspondence," the prosecutor said, "was recovered in the premises, catalogued item by item, and will be produced to the jury."

It was produced. Or rather, it was not.

The clerk called for Exhibit Fourteen, the bundle of correspondence, and there was a silence with a shape to it — the particular shape of a hole where a document ought to be. Counsel frowned. The clerk conferred with someone below the bench. Another name was called. Another absence.

"My lord," the prosecutor said at last, and his ledger-voice had lost a line of its arithmetic, "I am instructed that certain exhibits have been... mislaid in transit."

Mislaid. Tom kept his face still and his eyes lowered, the posture of a man overwhelmed by the majesty of the law, and inside himself he was laughing and praying in the same breath, because mislaid was a word with a story behind it, and he knew whose hands stories wore when they arrived dressed as coincidence.

The judge, a heavy man with the patient eyes of someone who had tried a hundred conspiracies and believed in none of them deeply, asked whether the Crown could produce a catalogue of the missing items. They could produce an inventory — seized, signed, witnessed. But an inventory is not evidence. An inventory proves only that something existed once, and the existence of pamphlets proves nothing that the pamphlets themselves, absent now, were meant to prove.

Then the defence did something Tom had not expected, which was anything at all.

A barrister Tom had never seen before rose — retained, he learned later, by fees paid through a firm of solicitors that did not exist — and asked, mildly, whether the Crown's difficulties extended also to its witnesses. Whether, in the confusion of the raid, statements taken from informants might likewise have gone astray. And then, before the prosecutor could rally, the barrister produced a single sheet of paper and asked leave to read it into the record: an unsigned statement, he said, received that morning, concerning the provenance of certain documents said to have been recovered on Rivington Street.

"It asserts," the barrister said, "that the correspondence in question was fabricated for the purpose of entrapment. That the handwriting attributed to a person of prominence was in fact the work of a copyist engaged for that purpose. It names no one. It accuses no one. It merely suggests — and my lord will forgive the suggestion — that the Crown's case rests upon paper which can no longer be produced, described by witnesses who can no longer be found, in terms which this statement now calls into question."

The courtroom made a sound like wind finding a gap in a window. The judge let it die.

"Whoever wrote this," he said dryly, "has a better understanding of reasonable doubt than half my profession."

Tom did not look up. But he thought: pencil. She signs in pencil. She writes the things that must be burned in pencil, so the erasure is always possible, always hers. And somewhere in that thought was the shape of a confession — Adela Crane putting her own hand to paper, naming her own guilt in whatever coded form Edith had demanded or mercy had dictated, and sending it out anonymous, deniable, a grenade with the pin drawn by love or blackmail or both. He would not learn the truth of it for weeks. He knew only, sitting in the dock with the borrowed collar biting his neck, that the paper in the barrister's hand had come from somewhere, and every road he could imagine from that somewhere passed through a woman he loved, driving a motorcar through fog.

* * *

The verdicts came down on the third day like weather.

Callum: twelve months. Pryce: bound over. Ruth Ellery: released, the magistrate looking at her over his spectacles with something that in a harder man would have been contempt and in him was nearly pity, telling her to go home to her mother and think of her future. The gallery murmured. Then Tom's turn, and the courtroom rearranged itself around him the way water rearranges around a stone.

Thomas Ainsley, typesetter, twenty-six years of age, of no fixed abode since the eleventh of March. Charged with conspiracy to print and distribute seditious matter, with forgery of travel documents, and with obstructing officers of the Crown in the execution of a warrant.

The magistrate read the charges slowly, as though tasting each one.

"As to forgery," he said, "the instruments of forgery were seized. The documents alleged to have been forged were not — having been, I understand, placed beyond reach before the raid concluded. The Crown cannot forge a case from a press alone." A pause. "As to conspiracy, the evidence of association is strong. You remained at the premises when you might have fled. That is either loyalty or folly, Mr. Ainsley, and I confess I cannot determine which, and neither, apparently, can the evidence."

Tom looked up then. He couldn't help it.

"Therefore," the magistrate said, and set down the paper, and Tom watched the whole machinery of the next two years of his life get picked up and set aside, "you are discharged with a severe warning. Seek lawful employment. Distance yourself from persons of seditious tendency. If you appear before these benches again, Mr. Ainsley, the present deficiency of evidence will not repeat itself."

A warning. Words, and words cost nothing, and Tom stood in the dock while the thing that had been two years of his life dissolved into a caution and a cleared throat, and he found that his legs did not entirely trust him, and that his eyes were hot, and that relief, properly understood, was indistinguishable at close range from grief.

The warder — the one with the wife in Holloway — walked him down the stone stairs to the discharge door and pressed a shilling into his hand, which was against every rule, and said, "Go home, son."

"I don't precisely have one," Tom said.

"Then go somewhere warm," the warder said, "and stay there."

* * *

The door opened onto Saturday morning, and London hit him all at once: coal smoke and herring carts, a barrel-organ grinding out something cheerful three streets away, gulls screaming over the market, the enormous indifferent noise of a city that had carried on perfectly well without him. Eleven days. It felt like years and like no time at all. His coat was still the borrowed one. His hands still smelled faintly of prison soap under everything else, a smell he suspected would live in his skin now the way ink lived under his nails.

He had rehearsed this. That was the absurdity — he had lain on the plank bed composing the scene: walk out the discharge door, turn left, count five paving stones, and there would be no one, because why would there be? The workshop was scattered. Callum was inside. Ruth had a mother in Brighton. Marguerite —

Marguerite was gone to Kent. He had put her on the road himself, four nights before the raid, with the child and the forged certificate and a lie about following later, and he had watched the motorcar's lamps shrink down the Rivington Street fog and understood that he was watching the only future he wanted drive away without him, and had gone back inside to move type by candlelight until his shoulders screamed.

So he had rehearsed emptiness. He turned left. He counted paving stones. One. Two. Three.

Four.

She was leaning against the iron railing opposite the discharge door with the satchel on her shoulder, wearing a grey coat that had been good once and a hat the wind had opinions about, and she was not reading a newspaper, not studying the passers-by, not doing any of the thousand things a person does to make waiting look accidental. She was simply standing, facing the door, as if she had known to the minute, as if she had been standing there since dawn, as if the whole apparatus of the Old Bailey had been constructed for the sole purpose of delivering him to that stretch of pavement.

His rehearsed speech — casual, wry, *didn't expect the welcome committee* — fell apart in his throat.

"They told me Tuesday," she said. "But I didn't believe them. Courts lie the way governments do. So I came today as well, and yesterday, and the day before."

"Three days," Tom said. His voice came out wrong, gravelly. "You waited three days on a pavement?"

"On a pavement, in a café, in a doorway when it rained. There's a coffee stall on the corner where the man stopped asking after the second day." She shrugged, and the shrug broke halfway through, and what was underneath it was not composure at all. "I had nowhere else to be. That's not quite true. I had everywhere else to be. Manchester, to start with. But I found I couldn't — I couldn't get on the train while you were still inside. It seemed wrong, leaving a country that had you in it."

He crossed the pavement. It took him a long time, or no time; he would never be able to say. What he remembered afterward was not the walking but her face as he approached, how she held absolutely still, the way you hold still when a wild thing comes near and you do not want to frighten the moment away.

"Two years," he said, when he was close enough that he could see the exhaustion under her eyes, the chapped wind-burn on her cheeks from Kent roads, the small scar of a paper cut at the corner of her thumb that he had memorized without meaning to. "I had a speech."

"I know. You get a look when you're composing. Your jaw does something."

"Does it."

"Like now."

He laughed, and the laugh cracked open into something else, and he pressed the back of his hand briefly, hard, against his mouth, because a man does not weep on a pavement outside the Old Bailey, except that every rule he owned had just been spent along with the two years he hadn't served.

"The magistrate said to seek lawful employment," he managed, "and distance myself from persons of seditious tendency."

"Well," Marguerite said. Her chin came up, and there she was — the woman who could lie fluently to a sister, carry a child through a police cordon, keep a nation's secrets in a leather bag and her face like still water. Except the water wasn't still now. It was shining. "You've come to the right person. I know a printer in Manchester who owes me a favour, and I don't intend to be easy to distance."

"The satchel," he said, because he needed something ordinary to hold onto, and nodded at it. "Still full?"

She lifted it off her shoulder and held it between them, and it hung light, too light, slack in a way it had never hung in all the months he had watched her carry it through fog and rain and surveillance.

"Emptying," she said. "Letter by letter. I'll tell you about it on the train. Some of it you'll want to burn straightaway and some of it you'll want to keep, and I've decided I'm done deciding alone which is which."

He reached out and took the strap from her — took the weight, such as it was, the habit of weight — and shouldered it, and she let him, and that small transfer of a bag from one hand to another felt like the largest ceremony of his life. Around them London roared on: carts, gulls, the organ-grinder, a newspaper boy shouting something about votes and Parliament that neither of them stopped to hear.

"Manchester, then," Tom said.

"Manchester," she said. "And on the way, there's something in Kent we should — " She stopped. Not because she had run out of words, but because she had run into the edge of something she was not yet ready to say, and Tom, who had spent two years loving her in silence, recognized the look of a person composing a sentence they intended to survive.

"There's something in Kent," she began again, more quietly, "that I have to show you. Or someone. Before I can tell you the rest."
