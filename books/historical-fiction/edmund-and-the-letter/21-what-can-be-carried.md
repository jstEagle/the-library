## 21. What Can Be Carried

The resignation was written on a Tuesday and delivered on a Wednesday, because Marguerite Sterling had spent four years learning that timing was everything — when to turn a corner, when to dim the lamps, when to leave a letter on a desk so it would be found at precisely the hour its recipient could do the least damage with it. She left Adela's letter where Adela would find it over breakfast, propped against the sugar bowl, and she waited in the motorcar outside with her hands folded in her lap and the engine ticking like a cooling hearth.

Adela came down the steps at half past eight, still in her dressing gown, the letter crushed in one fist. She did not tear it. She stood on the pavement in the thin gold light of an October morning and read it again, though she had clearly read it already, and Marguerite watched through the windscreen as the woman's face went through its whole catalogue: surprise, then the quick arithmetic she could never stop performing, then something older and rawer that Marguerite had never seen there before and would never see again.

When Adela reached the car she did not open the door. She bent instead, so that they spoke through the glass, which seemed fitting. Four years of conversations through barriers.

"You are not angry," Adela said. It was not a question, but it wanted answering.

"No," Marguerite said, and rolled the window down so there would be nothing between them but air. "I'm not angry. I want you to know that. If I were angry it would mean I expected something different from you, and I don't. I know exactly what you are, Mrs. Crane. I've known since the night the baby came. I just can't carry any more of it."

"Carry it for me a little longer. The amendment—"

"The amendment will pass or it won't, and it will pass without me, because it was never mine to begin with." Marguerite heard her own voice, level and clear, and marvelled at it. For years she had rehearsed this conversation in the dark of the garage, and in every rehearsal she had wept, or shouted, or been talked around in ten minutes by that patient, relentless voice. But rehearsals were a kind of fiction, and she had learned from the best that fictions could be revised. "You gave me purpose when I had none. You taught me to drive, to read a room, to hold my tongue. I'll be grateful until I die, and I will also leave, and both of those things are true at once, and you of all people should understand how two truths can share a single page."

Adela's jaw worked. Behind her, the housekeeper drew back from the window like a woman leaving a stage.

"Where will you go?"

"Manchester. There's a printer — Hargreaves & Son, on Withy Grove — who needs hands and doesn't ask questions. Tom has written to them already."

"And what will you print in Manchester? Pamphlets? Agitations?" A flicker of the old contempt, the old fire.

"Bills of lading," Marguerite said. "Wedding invitations. Whatever pays." She let that sit a moment. "Perhaps a pamphlet or two, if the cause is honest and the money is up front. I haven't sworn off the work, Mrs. Crane. Only the secrets."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere down the street a milk cart rattled, and the horses' breath hung in the cold like small ghosts. Adela straightened. She put the crushed letter into the pocket of her dressing gown — she kept things, always, even what she should have burned — and looked at Marguerite through the glass with an expression that might have been respect, or grief, or the close cousin they shared.

"You were the best of them," she said. "The very best."

"I know," Marguerite said gently. "That's why I'm going."

She turned the ignition and the engine caught, and Adela stepped back onto the pavement, and Marguerite drove away without looking in the mirror, because she had learned in Kent — on a lane between hop fields, with a child's weight still ghosting her arms — that some things could only be survived once, at speed, facing forward.

* * *

Tom met her at Euston with both their trunks and a paper of ham sandwiches and the particular brightness in his eyes that meant he had not slept, had instead sat up all night composing and discarding futures.

"There's a flat above the shop," he said, before the porter had finished loading. "Two rooms and a copper in the scullery. Mrs. Hargreaves says the rent comes out weekly and the stairs are steep, and then she fed me seed cake and told me about her son who's in Canada. I think we're approved of."

"We're approved of on the strength of seed cake?"

"On the strength of my hands." He held them out, palms up, across the little table in the third-class carriage: broad, ink-stained at the cuticles no matter how he scrubbed, steady as type cases. "A typesetter's hands, she said. Her father will have use for those hands."

Marguerite took one of them and held it. Two years she had loved him quietly, across the clatter of the Rivington Street workshop, and one winter's confession in a doorway, and now his hand in hers was simply a fact of the world, ordinary as weather. She found she had no rhetoric for it. After Adela, after Edith, after all those pages of persuasion and bargain, the plainness of it undid her more than any speech could have.

The train pulled out through the roofed gloom of the station and into grey daylight, London peeling away in terraces and gasworks and sudden surprising gardens. Tom ate his sandwich. Marguerite did not eat hers. Between her feet sat the satchel.

He noticed where she was looking. He had stopped pretending not to notice things; that was part of what loving him had cost and given.

"It's heavier than it looks," he said.

"It's exactly as heavy as it looks. That's the trouble." She lifted it onto her knees. The leather had gone soft as a dog's ear from four years of handling; the brass buckle had been mended twice with wire. Inside were letters. Not many — perhaps thirty — but each one a stone. Some were copies Adela had ordered made before originals were sent. Some were originals that had never been sent at all, that had come back unclaimed or been intercepted and ransomed and carried home. A few were letters Marguerite had been instructed to burn and had not burned, and she had asked herself a hundred times whether that had been disloyalty or foresight, and had finally stopped asking, because the answer changed nothing. They were hers now. That was the only verdict that mattered.

"What will you do with them?" Tom asked quietly, as the train gathered itself and the city thinned toward fields.

"That depends on what they weigh," Marguerite said, "once I take them out one at a time."

* * *

Manchester announced itself by smell before sight — coal smoke and river and the sweetish rot of the tanneries — and then by sound, a low industrial hum that never entirely ceased, even at three in the morning, even in fog. The flat above Hargreaves & Son had a window that looked out over Withy Grove, and from it they watched the printers come and go below, men in shirtsleeves and paper hats, moving with the unhurried certainty of people whose labour leaves something legible behind.

Old Hargreaves was seventy and half-deaf and had been setting type since Victoria's coronation, and he examined Tom's hands for perhaps four seconds before grunting approval and putting him on the Wharfedale press. Mrs. Hargreaves examined Marguerite rather longer.

"You'll be wanting work," she said on the first evening, setting down a tray of tea in the cramped sitting room. "Not marriage. I can see it in your face. Women who've driven motorcars don't settle for darning."

Marguerite laughed — really laughed, the first time in months — and said she'd take whatever was going.

What was going was the ledger. Hargreaves & Son kept accounts going back to 1859, and the most recent five years were chaos, and Marguerite spent her first fortnight in Manchester with her sleeves pinned back, ruling columns in pencil, bringing order out of other people's arithmetic. It was, she realised somewhere in the second week, the same skill. Reading what people wrote to each other. Understanding what they owed. But ledgers confessed only money, and money, whatever else might be said of it, never lied about being lied to.

At night she opened the satchel.

They had agreed on the method the first Sunday, sitting either side of the small black stove in the back room with the rain working at the window: one letter at a time, aloud if she chose, silent if she chose, and then into the fire, and no discussion unless she asked for one. Tom sat with his mending or his newspaper or, more often, with nothing at all, his hands quiet in his lap, and kept watch while she read.

The first was trivial — a note about train times, a coded reference to a meeting long past, everyone involved long dispersed or long forgiven. It burned green at the edges and curled like a living thing. The second was harder: a plea from a woman in Bristol whose husband had found her pamphlets and threatened to put her in an asylum, asking whether the movement protected its own. Marguerite remembered writing the reply under Adela's dictation, remembered the careful comfort of it. She did not know how the story had ended. She would never know. That was the tax on every page: they ended mid-sentence, these lives, and she had been the road between them, never the destination.

Some nights she read nothing. Some nights she read one line aloud and couldn't finish. Once — a letter from Adela to Edith, a duplicate, dated before everything, full of a sisterly fury that was indistinguishable from love — she read the whole thing twice and then held it over the coals a long time, watching the flame come up the paper toward her fingers, and Tom moved his chair closer without a word and put his hand at the small of her back, and they stayed that way until it was ash.

"Why keep them at all?" he asked her once, late, when the stove had gone down to embers and the room smelled of char. "You could have burned the lot in London, unread."

"Because carrying them was the only way I knew how to hold what happened," she said. "And emptying them is the only way I know how to set it down. A thing has to be looked at properly before you can put it down, Tom. Otherwise you only think you've put it down, and it gets up in the night and walks."

He considered this with the seriousness he gave to kerning and to her.

"And when the satchel's empty?"

"Then I'll find out what I weigh without it." She smiled at the embers. "I may be very light. I may blow away."

"I'd tie you to something," he said. "I'm good with knots."

It was not poetry. It was better than poetry; it was the voice of a man who fixed presses and forged certificates and meant every plain word. She leaned against his shoulder and let the last of the heat reach them, and upstairs in the street a late tram sang its wire-song through the wet dark, and for the first time in four years Marguerite Sterling fell asleep without composing tomorrow's route in her head.

* * *

The satchel emptied slowly, because she let it. Twenty-eight letters became twenty became twelve became seven, and November came down off the Pennines in sleet, and the shop below filled with Christmas orders — programmes, menus, a thousand cards for a cotton firm — and the rhythm of the days grew thick and warm as proofing ink. She learned the names of the apprentices. She learned which public house did the best pork pies and which tramline avoided the hill. She learned that she could walk down a street in this city carrying nothing but her purse and her own opinions, and that no one who passed her was anything to her, and that this anonymity, which would once have felt like erasure, felt almost like health.

On the second Sunday in December, with frost ferning the window and the two of them wrapped in every blanket Mrs. Hargreaves could spare, she took out the last letter.

She had saved it deliberately, and knew it. It was the earliest thing in the bag: a letter in Adela's hand, written in the year abroad, in pencil, to E. To Edith, before the child, before the debt — a letter that was simply a sister talking to a sister, homesick and funny and fierce, describing a market in Biarritz, a hat she could not afford, a conviction forming in her like a photograph developing: *I am done asking politely. When I come home I intend to be unbearable.*

Marguerite read it twice. It contained no secret worth the name. It contained only a woman, younger than Marguerite was now, becoming herself.

"She was a person once," Marguerite said, surprised to hear her own voice rough. "Before all of it. She was funny."

"People stay people," Tom said. "That's the worst of it. Even Calloway goes home to somebody."

"Don't say that. Let me have one monster."

He laughed, and she held the letter to the fire, and watched Adela Crane at twenty-five go up in a bright rush of flame — the hat, the market, the terrible resolve — and when it was gone she turned the satchel upside down over the stove anyway, and nothing fell out but a smell of old leather and a faint dust of graphite, years of pencil signatures shed like scales.

The bag weighed nothing now. She weighed it in her hand, weighing the truth of it, and found her eyes were wet and could not have said precisely for whom — for the woman on the pavement in her dressing gown, for the child calling another woman Mama in a cottage in Kent, for the girl she had been at twenty-two with capable hands and an empty life, measuring herself in errands.

Tom knelt and fed the fire properly, log by log, building it up against the frost.

"What will you carry now?" he asked. "Now that it's empty?"

Marguerite looked at the satchel, soft-eared, wire-mended, faithful as a horse. She thought: *a face I promised not to forget. A name I gave no one. The weight of my own two truths.* She thought: *whatever fits.*

"Oh," she said, "I expect I'll find things. I seem to be the sort of person things get left with."

As if summoned, there came a knock at the door below — the shop door, heavy and double-rapped, the knock of someone official or desperate, and sometimes in this world the two were difficult to tell apart. Through the floorboards they heard old Hargreaves descend, slow on the stairs, and the murmur of voices, and then, rising up through the joists, unmistakable in the frozen silence of the night, a voice Marguerite knew: measured, courteous, patient as surveillance itself.

Mr. Calloway, of the Home Office, asking after two people lately come from London.
