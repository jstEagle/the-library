## 22. The Face Remembered

Kent in June was a country made of green light. The hop bines climbed their strings in ranks up the hillside like regiments of patient women, and the oast-houses stood round-shouldered and white-capped against a sky the colour of washed linen. Marguerite Sterling came down out of the hills on the early omnibus from Ashford with a carpet bag on her knees and five years folded inside it like a letter she had never dared to post.

She had not meant to come this way. That was what she told herself, and it was nearly true. There was business in Canterbury — Tom's press needed a new set of rollers, and the Manchester firm had an agent there who could supply them at cost — and when she had traced the route on her map on the kitchen table in Chorlton-on-Medlock, her pencil had wandered south of the city, down through the Weald, and stopped, quite of its own accord, at a crossroads village whose name she had carried in her mouth for four years without ever saying it aloud.

Tom had watched her pencil stop. He had said nothing. He only reached across the table and turned his teacup so its handle faced her, the way he did when he wanted to say something tender and could not find the shape of it in words. *Go,* the cup said. *And come back.*

So she had gone.

The war had put its thumbprints on everything between London and the coast — hoardings with pointing fingers, young men in khaki who moved like old men, fields where the hops were picked now by women with red hands and tired songs. But Kent itself was stubbornly itself. The lanes still smelled of crushed fennel and hot dust. The church spire still stitched the sky to the earth at the same old seam. And at the crossroads, where the lane bent toward the cottage that had once been described to her in a forged certificate's careful type — *a respectable widow, the child's aunt* — the blackberries were coming in green and hard on the hedge, exactly as they must have been coming in the day she handed over a bundle of blankets and walked away without looking back.

She had kept that promise for four years. She intended to keep it one hour longer, and then to break it just slightly, just once, in the safest way a person can break a thing: by looking.

* * *

She left her bag at the inn — a low-beamed place with a sign of a leaping stag and a landlady who asked no questions, which was a quality Marguerite had learned to prize above charm or beauty or even honesty — and walked out along the lane as the morning warmed. Bees worked the clover at the verge. Somewhere behind a far hedge a dog was making a slow, contented argument with a cart horse. Her heart conducted its own small riot under her ribs, and she let it. She had spent too many years being composed for other people's sake. Adela Crane had taught her composure; the leaving of Adela Crane had taught her that composure was sometimes only fear wearing its good coat.

Four years ago she had been twenty-six and carrying more than a baby up this lane. She had been carrying a letter she had refused to deliver, a secret she had refused to surrender, and a future she could not yet see, because every future she had ever owned had belonged first to someone else — measured out, like the letters, in what fit inside the satchel.

The satchel was in the carpet bag at the inn. She still had it. She would always have it. But it held nothing now but Tom's spare collars and a tin of type-cleaner and, folded into the lining where no customs officer or inspector would ever think to look, a single curl of infant hair wrapped in waxed paper, which she had taken — stolen, if stealing it was wrong, then she had been a thief since 1914 and did not repent — from the widow's doorstep on a morning of rain, when no one was watching, when even God, she hoped, had looked away.

The cottage came upon her all at once, the way remembered places do: smaller than memory insisted, and yet exactly right. Whitewashed walls, a roof of peg tiles gone soft with moss, a garden running riot and glorious — beans, roses, a row of beehives along the fence with their traffic rising and falling like a town heard from a train. A washing line. A cat asleep in the middle of absolutely everything, insufferable and golden.

And a child.

Marguerite stopped at the bend of the lane, in the shade of an elder tree, and looked.

The child was four years old, or nearly. She was kneeling in the dirt of the garden path with a wooden spoon and an enamel bowl, conducting some ceremony of mud and marigold petals with the absolute gravity of a bishop. Her hair had deepened from infant gold to a warm brown that caught the sun and gave it back redder. When she stood — when she stood, Marguerite's breath went somewhere below her ribs and stayed there — she stood with her chin lifted and her shoulders square, in the way of a person addressing a crowd, or a magistrate, or history.

Oh, thought Marguerite. Oh, you poor magnificent child. You have her stance. You have the very stance of a woman who will spend her life telling England things England does not wish to hear.

The cottage door opened, and the widow came out wiping her hands on her apron — a broad, weathered woman, grey-haired, with the kind of face that had been pretty and had decided prettiness was a poor investment. The child ran to her, spoon aloft, delivering some urgent report about the marigolds. The widow listened with complete attention, then laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the laugh of a woman who had discovered at fifty-something that love was not finished with her after all — and swung the girl up onto her hip with the ease of long practice.

*Mama.* The child called her Mama. The word floated across the garden, clear as a bell over water, and struck Marguerite in the chest with the flat of it.

She had prepared herself for many things on this lane. She had prepared herself for jealousy, for grief, for the animal pang of seeing another woman's hands where hers might have been. What she had not prepared for was relief — a great slow flood of it, rising through her like water finding a channel — because the child was happy. Not tolerated, not kept, not managed. Happy. Sunburnt. Bossy with a wooden spoon. Loved with the ordinary, enormous, unspectacular love that does not need to justify itself before any tribunal in the world.

Marguerite had given the child to this garden instead of to the foundling hospital's untraceable intake, and here was the interest paid on that choice, compounding daily, in petals and mud and laughter.

She stood under the elder tree and did not approach.

That was the instruction, after all. The first instruction, written in Adela Crane's pencil on a note pinned to a bundle of rain-wet blankets: *Forget the face.* For years Marguerite had understood it as a cruelty — one more order issued by a woman who measured lives like letters, keeping what was useful and burning what was inconvenient. Only lately had she begun to suspect that it might also have been a mercy, and that Adela, whatever else she had been, had known what Marguerite knew now, standing in the shade: that the face was not something a person could forget. It was only something a person could decline to claim.

She would not forget the face. She would carry it, the way she had carried the memorized letters, the way she had carried the confession, the way one carries anything true — not displayed, not denied, simply present, a weight that becomes a shape that becomes part of the body.

* * *

She stayed an hour. She could not help it; she could help nothing that morning, and found she did not want to. She sat on a stile two fields off where the garden was still visible, a smudge of white wall and bee-fence among the hops, and watched the small pageant of the morning. The child helped carry in the washing and dropped most of it. The widow scolded with a smile. The bees kept their books. Once, the girl stood at the gate and stared directly at the stile — children do that, they see everything at the edges of the world — and Marguerite's whole life gathered itself into her throat; but the child was looking past her, at a butterfly, and then at the sky, and then she was called in for bread and jam, and the moment closed over like water.

Marguerite got down from the stile. Her legs were unsteady, and she laughed at herself, quietly, alone among the hops — a short wet sound, half grief and half something that had no name but felt suspiciously like peace.

She thought, as she walked back along the lane, of all the faces she had ever declined to claim. Calloway, the polite inspector with his questions like laid table settings, who had died the previous winter — so the newspapers said — still hunting conspiracies that had dissolved into printing shops and provincial respectability. Edith, who had taken Adela's confession in writing and never used it, because blackmail, Marguerite had finally understood, had never really been about winning; it had been about being seen, and once the paper existed in her drawer, she was seen, and the hunger quieted. The young volunteer who had confessed to a priest, whom Marguerite had protected at the risk of everything, and who had sent her a card last Christmas from Canada, married, thriving, unaware that mercy had ever been strategic.

And Adela.

* * *

Adela Crane was in the newspapers again that week. Marguerite had read it at the inn over breakfast, holding the page at arm's length as though distance might improve the prose. *Mrs. Adela Crane, the veteran organizer, was received at Westminster yesterday, where Mrs. Pankhurst's services and those of other leaders of the long campaign were acknowledged...* The vote had come the previous year — partial, grudging, hedged with property qualifications, a door opened a hand's width and called emancipation — and the men who had once set detectives on Adela's presses now stood up in the House to congratulate her on her persistence. She had been photographed on the Embankment in grey silk, hatless, sixty-one years old, her jaw as level as it had been over the arithmetic in the safehouse in 1913.

*A debt is paid,* she had written once to E, in pencil, and burned nothing herself, having always had Marguerite to burn things for her.

They had met one last time, in the spring of 1916, at a tea shop near Victoria. Adela had grown thinner. The war had taken her husband — the hostile MP, dead of pneumonia in a training camp, mourned by the nation — and it had taken, Marguerite suspected, several other things she could not name in public. They had spoken of Manchester, of the workshop people scattered to safer cities, of the price of coal. Then Adela had set down her cup and said, in the voice she had used for dictating letters: "You did the right thing, Marguerite."

"No," Marguerite had said. "I did my thing. It happened to be right. Those are different, and you know it better than anyone living."

For a moment something moved behind Adela's eyes — the old cold ledger closing, or failing to close — and then she had smiled, and it was almost warm. "I signed in pencil," she said, "because I never trusted myself in ink. I knew that about myself. It was the one honest thing I did for thirty years." She had pressed sixpence into the saucer for the bill, risen, squared her chin the way her daughter squared hers in a Kentish garden, and walked out into the traffic without looking back. They had not corresponded since. Some truths are carried, not told; some farewells are the same.

Marguerite wondered — she allowed herself this, walking the sunny lane with her hands in her pockets — whether Adela knew where the child was. Whether, on certain evenings in her honourable grey house, she took out a pencil and wrote a sentence to an address she did not let herself confirm, and then burned it in the grate with her own hand, finally, learning late the work she had always delegated. She decided it did not matter. The debt was paid. The arithmetic was done. The child had jam.

* * *

At the inn she wrote two notes while her tea went cold.

The first was to Tom, in ink, because she had learned the value of ink from a woman who never used it. *Saw the rollers. Saw a little else besides. Coming home Thursday on the afternoon train. Meet me, or don't — I know which you'll choose — M.* Underneath, after a moment, she added: *The stag at the inn has a bad eye and I have told the landlady you will fix it, as you fix everything. Bring the satchel when you come. I have something to show you.*

It was true, in its way. She had the curl of hair in the lining, and she had decided, sometime between the stile and the second pot of tea, that it was time. Not to tell everyone — never that; the secret would die with her and with the widow and with Adela, and that was the bargain she had made with her own conscience long ago. But Tom had forged the papers that saved a life and confessed his love in a room full of drying ink, and he had waited four years without once asking her what she carried. A man like that could be shown the lining of a satchel. A man like that had earned the whole weight of it, the shape of it, the truth that she carried and would carry until carrying was done.

Because that was the thing she had come to Kent to learn, standing under an elder tree with her heart going like a telegraph key: that she was done measuring her life in secrets. She had emptied the satchel letter by letter into the fire in Manchester, and the fire had taken them, and she had remained. She was not the letters. She was not the satchel. She was the woman who had read them, chosen among them, and walked away from the reading desk into her own life — and her own life, it turned out, contained a printer in Chorlton with ink-stained knuckles and a terrible singing voice, and a press that smelled of oil and midnight, and a future with no instructions pinned to it whatsoever.

The second note took longer. It was addressed to no one, or rather to a name she had invented for herself years ago and used only once since: *To the woman at the stile, from the woman at the gate.* In it she wrote only this:

*She calls you Mama, and it is the truest word in England. Thank you for your garden. One day, if she asks, tell her she stands like her mother. It will be true in every sense you know, and in one you needn't explain. Burn this.*

Then she went out to the inn's small fire in the back yard, where the landlady's boy was feeding the range, and fed the note in after the day's wrapping-paper, and watched the pencil-words — for she had written it in pencil, deliberately, in fair tribute — curl and brighten and go to ash among the cinders.

* * *

The afternoon train came through the hop country at four, trailing steam along the valley like a scarf. Marguerite sat by the window with the carpet bag on her lap and the empty satchel beside her, and watched Kent run backwards: the oasts, the churches, the long green aisles of bines, the crossroads with its hard green blackberries, and — for one instant, at a level crossing where the train slowed for cattle — a flash of whitewashed wall and a line of washing and a small figure on a gate, arm raised, waving at the engine with the whole imperious confidence of a person who believes the world stops for her.

Perhaps, Marguerite thought, pressing two fingers briefly to the glass, to the place where the small hand had been — perhaps it should.

The train gathered speed. The wall slid behind the hops. She did not look back; she had kept enough promises to know how, and broken enough to know why. Instead she faced forward, toward London, toward the junction, toward Manchester and the press and the man who would be standing on the platform with his collar crooked and his eyes doing that thing they did when he would not say what he meant.

She did not forget the face. She never would. It rode with her the way the letters once had — memorized, undeliverable, precious — a single true page carried in the satchel of her, which was the only archive, she understood at last, that some documents are ever meant for.

Outside, the light began to go gold over the Weald, and the child waved until the train was a rumour and the rumour was gone, and the bees kept their books, and the evening came on, gentle and unhurried, across a country at peace with its own green silence.
