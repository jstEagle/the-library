## 1. The Unfinished Coastline

There is a particular cruelty in inking a dead man's coastline, and I have had twenty years to become an expert in it.

The northern shore of the Duchy runs, according to Master Aldous Whitlock's final survey, in a long reluctant line from Cape Merrow to the salt marshes, indented here and there with harbors too shallow for anything but smuggling. I have spent four months on that shoreline alone. This morning I would finish it. The last stretch — the drowned coast below the fens, where the sea has been eating the land for a century and no two surveys agree — waited on my desk like a debt.

The guild workshop smelled of oak gall ink and tallow and the particular dust that accumulates on vellum that has been argued over. Dawn came through the south windows in gray slats, and I bent over the plate with my ruling pen and did not let my hand shake.

This is the trick they do not teach you at apprenticeship: grief is a matter of line weight. You cannot weep at 0.3 millimeters. You cannot mourn and hold a consistent hatching interval across six inches of coast. Precision is a room you can shut yourself into, and I have lived in that room since I was nineteen years old, since the day they hung — or said they hung — Aldous Whitlock on Warrant Hill and I stood in the crowd with the other apprentices because Provost Halloway said it would be improper not to.

Improper. The guild's favorite word, after *accurate*.

I drew the low-water line first, then the high-water, then the marsh symbols between them, each one a small tufted mark like a head of wheat drowning. The fen country resisted me, as it always resists cartographers. Half of what I knew about it I knew from rumor — fishermen's gossip, a smuggler's tale told to a man who told it to Aldous who told it to me with one eyebrow raised, meaning: *file this under things we cannot verify.* The guild does not permit rumor on a plate. Rumor is unlicensed knowledge. Rumor has not paid its fees.

So I drew the coast as the charts said it should be, and where the charts disagreed with each other I drew a compromise, which is what official maps are mostly made of.

"You're in early."

Halloway's voice from the doorway. He has a gift for arriving exactly when a person has just thought something disloyal; after twenty years I suspect him of smelling it.

"Someone has to finish it," I said, without lifting my pen. "The Duke's men want the atlas by the equinox."

"The Duke's men," Halloway said, "want it by the equinox *corrected*, which is a different animal entirely." He came to stand at my shoulder, hands clasped behind his back, a stout man gone comfortably gray, the kind of person strangers confide in at funerals. "You've heard, then."

"Heard what?" I had heard nothing, which meant I was about to hear something.

He picked up the trial proof — the rough pull I keep beside me so the final inking stays honest — and held it to the window light, squinting at the fens the way one squints at an old acquaintance behaving badly. "There's a commission. Royal Historical Commission, very grand, very new seals. They'll review all boundary atlases produced during the war years and issue certified editions." He set the proof down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you'd handle something that might be rigged. "Including his."

"His atlas was finished by his apprentice," I said. "Me. It's mine under guild law."

"It is the guild's under guild law," Halloway said mildly, "which is why I'm telling you before the announcement rather than after. Paloma." He turned and looked at me directly, which he almost never does; Halloway's whole manner is averted eyes and folded hands, a man permanently apologizing for occupying space. "They will make changes. Shorelines, boundaries, the fen districts especially. It will be presented as refinement. Survey error, wartime expedience. You mustn't take it personally."

"Take what personally? You haven't told me what they'll change."

"No," he agreed pleasantly, "I haven't," and he wished me good morning and left, and I sat there with my pen loaded and my coast half-finished and felt something press against my ribs from the inside, patient, like a prisoner who knows the sentence and is content to serve it.

* * *

Here is what I know about Aldous Whitlock's death, in the order I learned it:

That he was arrested in the last winter of the war, charged with falsifying military surveys — a charge so absurd that the whole workshop laughed, because Aldous Whitlock falsifying a survey was the sun rising in the west, if the sun were famously scrupulous and also the army's chief cartographer.

That the laughter stopped when the tribunal didn't release him.

That he was executed on the fourteenth of Marrow-month, twenty years ago this season, and that his property — instruments, library, unfinished plates — passed to the guild, and his apprentices passed to other masters, and I passed to Halloway, who was kind to me in a way I mistook for compensation rather than containment.

And here is what I do not know, though I have spent twenty years not knowing it professionally:

Why the charge at all. Why a man whose entire career was accuracy would be accused of inventing fog banks on artillery charts — which was the specific accusation, fog, of all things, fog being the one phenomenon every surveyor agrees is provisional. Why the tribunal records were sealed. Why, when I asked Halloway about it in my second year under him, young enough still to ask questions with my whole face, he looked at me for a long moment and said, "Some errors are load-bearing, child. Move one and the building comes down on whoever's standing underneath." And then taught me gilding for a month, as if to fill my hands.

I have kept the silences he gave me. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, in the privacy of my own skull, where I conduct most of my trials. I finished my apprenticeship. I took the guild oaths, both of them, including the second one they administer quietly. I corrected the master's plates when the guild told me which lines were now incorrect. I redrew the estuary at Greywater three times because the harbor dues depended on where the channel legally ran, never mind where the water actually went, and I did it neatly, and I slept, most nights.

But some silences are not empty. Some silences are full of a thing shaped exactly like a question, and if you carry one long enough — twenty years, say — it acquires weight the way vellum does, imperceptibly, until one day you try to lift it flat and find it has curled.

* * *

Midmorning brought the announcement formally, as announcements are brought: a crier in the courtyard, then a notice nailed to the guild board with three fresh seals and a ribbon the color of a healing wound.

*By order of the Royal Historical Commission: all atlases bearing witness to territorial settlement in the late civil conflict shall be subject to review and correction. The atlas commonly known as Whitlock's Northern Survey, completed posthumously, is hereby summoned for certification. Corrections to be supervised by appointed historians of record.*

*Completed posthumously.* As if the atlas had finished itself, out of grief. As if I had not spent eleven years on it — the first five learning enough to be worthy of it, the rest doing the work with his notes open on my left like a hand on my shoulder.

The workshop gathered around the notice in the way workshops do, which is to say everyone read it and nobody said anything true. Young Fenwick from the bindery whistled. Old Maren, who has survived three administrations by being technically deaf, asked whether anyone wanted her stool.

"A correction," said Corvin, senior draughtsman, in the satisfied tone of a man who has always found Aldous Whitlock's reputation an inconvenience to his own advancement. "Well. His coastal work was always speculative below Cape Merrow. Rumor-work. Everyone knows it."

I did not look at him. I am proud of this, in retrospect; I am less proud of what I said, which was nothing, which was the silence I have been trained into, worn like a uniform.

But inside the uniform, something moved.

Because Corvin was wrong, you see, and wrong in a way I could prove. Below Cape Merrow the master's coastal work was the finest in the atlas — I have his field notebooks, I have copied them out twice for love of the hand in them — soundings taken in weather no commission boat would sail, tide tables cross-checked against fishermens' memory going back generations. He mapped that drowned coast the way a doctor examines a body he loves. And the commission proposed to *correct* it. Which could mean only one of two things: either their new surveys were better than his, which I would want to see, receipts in hand; or the truth of that coast had become inconvenient to somebody, in which case the corrections would not be corrections at all.

They would be erasures. And I know what erasures look like, because I have been making polite ones for eleven years.

* * *

I worked through the afternoon and lied to myself about it in the standard way — just until the light goes, just this one section — until the windows went blue and the others drifted out and I had the workshop to myself and the lamps to trim.

The drowned coast. I came back to it the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Here is the thing I have told no one, the small treason I keep folded at the bottom of my competence like a coin in a hem: there is a gap in the master's fen survey. A deliberate one. In his notes, the sequence of stations along the drowned shore runs clean and continuous except for one stretch — nine miles below the old fen settlements — where the entries stop being observations and start being *provisions*. Fog symbols drawn where his barometer says clear. Marsh marks where his own soundings say firm ground. He wrote the coast wrong, there, on purpose, in his careful beautiful hand, and I have known it since my third year, and I have inked his false fog faithfully every time the plate came back for a new pulling, because I assumed — I *chose* to assume — it was another of his jokes, another of his tests for whichever apprentice was sharp enough to catch it.

I never caught it out loud. I never asked. That is the coin in my hem: I noticed, and I said nothing, and I have told myself for seventeen years that saying nothing was diligence.

Aldous Whitlock did not put jokes on artillery-grade surveys. I knew that when I was twenty-two. I have known it all along, the way you know a door is locked by running into it once and never again testing it.

Tonight, with the workshop empty and the announcement drying on the board outside, I tested it.

I pulled his field notebook for the fen stations — it lives in the locked cabinet with the others, and yes, I have the key, I have had the key for a decade, that is what finishing someone's atlas makes you — and I laid it beside the plate and read the nine miles of fog. And this time I did not read it as a joke or a test or a quirk of a brilliant difficult man. I read it as what it suddenly, horribly resembled: a map made by a man standing between an artillery corps and something he wanted the guns never to find.

Fog, invented on the chart, in the exact place where the fen country dips toward the drowned coast. Where — if rumor is to be believed, and the guild says it may not be — there had once been settlements. Fen towns. Little wet huddle of places, tax-poor, chart-poor, the kind of places that appear in the Domesday annexes and vanish from everything printed afterward.

I sat back. The lamp guttered. My ribs felt crowded.

"Load-bearing errors," I said aloud to the empty workshop, borrowing Halloway's phrase, turning it over. "Move one and the building comes down."

I had always heard it as a warning. Tonight, for the first time, it sounded like instructions.

* * *

I closed the plate. I cleaned the pen properly, because Aldous Whitlock's ghost can see me and would comment otherwise. I locked the cabinet, banked the lamp, and let myself out through the side stair where the third step doesn't creak if you trust it.

The city was doing its evening arithmetic — lamplighters, watch bells, the smell of coal and river. I walked home through streets I could draw from memory to a scale of one inch to the mile, which is the sort of thing that happens to you in this trade; the world becomes a draft you are forever correcting.

At my door, a man was waiting.

Not a guild man — no colors, no satchel of proofs, and guild men stand like they are owed the doorway. This one stood like a guest who isn't sure of the invitation. Traveling cloak gone shiny at the shoulders, road dust to the knee, and under the street lamp a face maybe ten years older than mine, weathered in the particular way of someone who spends his life outdoors and doesn't apologize for it.

"Mistress Almeida." Not a question. He knew me. That was my first alarm, filed and noted. "Forgive the hour."

"The guild office handles commissions," I said. "In daylight."

"I'm not here about a commission." He glanced up and down the lane, unhurried but thorough, a man checking sightlines out of habit rather than fear. Then he took a letter from inside his cloak and held it out, and I nearly laughed, because I am a sensible woman and strangers with letters at night belong in bad novels.

Then I saw the direction panel.

You spend enough years studying a hand — copying it, correcting it, dreaming about it in the boring practical way you dream about your own fingers — and you stop reading addresses and start reading *hands*. The address on that envelope was written in a fine sloping script with a peculiar flourish on the capital letters, each one crowned with a small unnecessary loop, like a bird taking off.

I would know that hand in the dark. I have inked it for eleven years. It has been dead for twenty.

"Who wrote this?" My voice came out steady, which surprised me; the rest of me was not steady at all.

"You'd best read it," the stranger said. "And then, mistress, I think you'd best come with me — because the historians reach the archives by week's end, and there's a place north of here that won't survive their visit unless you do."

I broke the seal. The wax carried no sigil I recognized — just a thumbprint, pressed while warm.

Inside, in the looped and living capitals of a hanged man, were four words and a name I had not seen written anywhere in twenty years:

*Mistress Almeida — bring the plates. Come to the orchard. — A.W.*
