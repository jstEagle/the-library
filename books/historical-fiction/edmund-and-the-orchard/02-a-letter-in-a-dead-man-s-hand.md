## 2. A Letter in a Dead Man's Hand

The knock came at the hour when honest people have already eaten and dishonest ones are only beginning their work, which is to say it came at dusk, in the rain, on a night I had planned to spend alone with a bottle of ink and my grief, both of which I had been diluting all week.

I nearly didn't answer. I want that recorded. In every version of this story I have told since — to magistrates, to printers, to myself at three in the morning — I am tempted to leave out how long I stood in my narrow hallway with my hand on the latch, deciding whether the world outside was worth the trouble of letting it in. But honesty is the whole point of what follows, so: I stood there for the length of eleven raindrops against the window glass, and I very nearly let the dead stay dead.

The man on my step was soaked through, youngish, with the kind of face that has learned not to expect kindness — sharp at the cheekbones, soft nowhere, a week of road dust ground into the creases around his eyes. He held no lantern. He held nothing at all except his arms, crossed tight against the cold, as if he'd already decided that whatever he'd come to give me could be delivered by posture alone.

"Paloma Whitlock," he said. Not a question. People rarely make it a question; the name does the work for them.

"You have the advantage."

"Ivo Reyes." He said it like a coin he expected me to bite. "I need a moment of your time, mistress, and I'll need you to keep your voice down while you spend it."

There are perhaps four sentences in any language that will get a guild cartographer to open her door wider rather than slam it shut, and *keep your voice down* is one of them. I stepped back. He came in the way water comes under a door — quietly, thoroughly, filling the low places first.

* * *

He would not sit. He stood in my workroom dripping onto floorboards I had oiled that very morning, and he looked at the atlas — Master Aldous's atlas, the great unfinished coasting of the contested marches, twenty years of my life spread open under weights — and something moved through his face too fast for me to name it.

"You're finishing it," he said.

"The guild commissioned me to. It's his final work. It deserves—"

"It deserves what it gets." He turned. "Do you know what I did before I came here? Three days ago I stood outside the guildhall and listened to a herald read the announcement. The atlas is to be 'corrected' by the Royal Historical Commission. Corrected." He smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "They're going to take a dead man's life's work and sand off everything inconvenient, and they've found a woman willing to hold the sandpaper."

The oil on the floorboards suddenly seemed a very foolish extravagance. "If you've come to insult me, you've had good value. The door is behind you."

"If I'd come to insult you I'd have done it in the street where more people could enjoy it." From inside his coat he drew an oilcloth packet, wrapped twice and tied with waxed cord, and set it on my worktable between the burnisher and the pot of gall ink as carefully as a man lays down something that might go off. "This isn't from me. It's from him."

"From whom?"

"Read it."

I untied the cord. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I remember noticing the steadiness and distrusting it, the way you distrust a calm sea when you know what's underneath. Inside the oilcloth was a letter, folded once, written on paper gone soft at the edges with handling — he had carried it a long way, close to his body, and the paper had learned his warmth.

And then I saw the handwriting.

I want to describe what that is like, seeing a dead man's hand again after twenty years. It is not like seeing a ghost. Ghosts, I'm told, are vague things, translucent, deniable. This was not vague. This was the specific, irreducible fact of Aldous Whitlock's penmanship: the capital letters flourished like iron gates, each one a small piece of architecture; the descenders on his *g*s cut long and slightly leftward, as though the letter were trying to escape the line; the peculiar way he crossed his *t*s with a single impatient stroke that always overshot the crossbar by a hair. I had spent six years of my childhood copying that hand because he made me — *a cartographer who can't forge a hand can't detect a forgery, Paloma, and half of mapmaking is detecting forgery* — and so I did not merely recognize it. I knew its weight, its moods, the way it tightened when he was tired and loosened when he was pleased.

The dead do not write. That is the arrangement. That is the entire foundation on which I had built twenty years of careful, precise, honorable grief. And there on my table, in ink faded to the brown of old blood, the dead had written:

*Mistress Whitlock —*

*If this reaches you, then Ivo has done what I asked and you are reading it, which means you have not burned it unread, which means you are either braver or more curious than I taught you to be. I suspect both.*

*I will not insult you with apologies in a letter. There is no room in ink for what I owe you. Come instead. Bring the original plates — the true plates, not the fair copies the guild keeps, and you know the difference better than anyone alive. Bring them north, past Harrow Fell, to the orchard above the drowned valley. You mapped the salt roads once from rumor. Map yourself along one now.*

*The commission arrives within the month. After them, there will be nothing left to bring.*

*You will be angry. Be angry on the road. It is a long road and anger walks well.*

*— A.W.*

I read it three times. On the third reading my legs informed me, without consultation, that they were no longer in the business of holding me up, and I sat down hard on my drafting stool, which rolled backward and struck the wall with a knock that sounded, absurdly, like a door closing.

"That's his hand," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, a surveyor's voice, reporting elevations. "That's his hand and he's been dead for twenty years. They hanged him. There were witnesses. There was a — there's a monument."

"There's a monument," Ivo agreed. "Granite. Very tasteful. The Duke paid for it himself."

"Then this is a forgery."

"Is it?"

And that was the cruelty of it, you see. Because I looked at the letter — really looked, the way I had been trained to look, the way he himself had trained me to look — and I could not find the forgery. A forger reproduces shapes. This letter had the *habits*. The slight rightward drift of the lines when he wrote quickly, as he had clearly written this, urgently. The ink pooled at the ends of strokes where he paused mid-word to think — he never lifted the pen when he thought, he'd rest it on the paper and the ink would bloom, and I used to scold him about it when I was twelve years old and full of authority, and he used to say *ink remembers where a man hesitates, girl, that's more than most of us manage.* 

Ink remembers where a man hesitates. This letter hesitated four times. Once before *apologies*. Once before *true plates*. Once before *orchard*, and once — longest, deepest bloom of all — before my name.

No forger alive could have known that. No forger alive could have known that he rested his pen on the paper when he thought, or that I would know to look for it.

"He's alive," I said. The words tasted like biting through the foil on a new plate — bright, metallic, wrong. "He's been alive for twenty years and he let me — he let me stand at his execution—" Except I hadn't. I had been nine. I had been told. I had grieved a boy's clean grief and then a woman's complicated one, and all of it, all twenty years of it, had apparently been a *cartographic decision*, an area left blank on the chart, waters marked unsurveyed.

Ivo watched me come apart with the patience of a man who had expected it and budgeted for it. "My father came from Merrow Fen," he said. "You won't have heard of it. That's rather the point. Your master drew the map that erased my town, and then he didn't hang, and my family burned anyway, and I have spent eleven years finding someone who could read what he wrote. So believe me when I say I have no love for the man and less patience for his mysteries. But the letter is his. I've had it checked by eyes better than mine."

"What eyes? Whose?"

"That's not mine to tell. Not yet." He crouched, finally, bringing himself level with my seated disbelief, and for the first time I saw that beneath the suspicion in his face there was fear — real fear, worn down to the quick, the kind that has been carried so long it has become structural. "Mistress Whitlock. The commission arrives within the month. When they finish 'correcting' the atlas, every record that contradicts the Duke's title goes into a furnace, and they do it with the same ceremony they used for your master's hanging. I need those plates. He needs you to bring them. And you need to decide very soon whether you are the woman who holds the sandpaper or the woman who draws the map."

I looked at the letter a fourth time. At the bloom of ink before my name, deep as a well.

*You will be angry. Be angry on the road.*

Outside, the rain kept on, indifferent, thorough, mapping the city street by street with no one to correct it. Somewhere across town the guildhall stood dark, its archives locked, its folios in perfect order, its dead accounted for. And somewhere north, past Harrow Fell, above a valley I had drawn as drowned because a chart told me to, a man whose funeral I had mourned was waiting to explain why.

I should say I felt betrayed. I did — but betrayal requires trust, and trust requires certainty, and what I actually felt in that moment was worse than either. It was vertigo. The sensation, familiar to anyone who has ever discovered an error in a map they trusted, that the ground itself has been redrawn beneath you, and that every distance you ever measured must now be measured again.

I picked up the letter. I folded it along his creases.

"How long do we have?" I asked.

Ivo Reyes stood, and dripped, and almost smiled.

"Less than you'll wish," he said.
