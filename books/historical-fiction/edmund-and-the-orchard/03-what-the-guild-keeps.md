## 3. What the Guild Keeps

The Guild of Cartographers kept its archives the way a miser keeps coins: counted, catalogued, and shown to no one who might spend them. I had been inside the undercroft perhaps a dozen times in twenty years, and each visit had the quality of visiting a wealthy aunt — you admired what was permitted, and you did not ask where the rest of the money went.

I went down on the morning after Reyes left, with his letter folded against my ribs like a splint. Sleep had not come; instead I had spent the small hours doing what Aldous taught me to do with an impossible problem, which was to reduce it to its components. A letter in a dead man's hand. Either the hand was forged, or the man was alive, or — and this was the component I kept circling, because it was the one that hurt least to examine — the record itself was wrong. The guild said he died. The guild had said so for twenty years. The guild, I reminded myself as I descended the worn stairs into the cold of the archive, was the institution that had tried him and executed him and then written down that it had done all three.

If I wanted to know whether the dead could write, I would have to ask the paperwork.

The undercroft occupied two floors beneath the guildhall, and it smelled the way old paper smells when it has given up pretending it will ever be read again: dust and size and the faint sweetness of foxing ink. Lamplight fell in yellow pools along the stacks. Old Master Corvin presided over the ledger desk, or rather dozed behind it, his spectacles pushed up into white eyebrows, a man who had outlived his own interest in geography. He stirred when my boots hit the stone.

"Whitlock." My married-sounding surname still amused people; they knew I had taken it from my master, not a husband. "Rare bird. Nobody comes down here unless they're writing a history or dying."

"Then consider me ambitious," I said. "I need the trial records. Aldous Whitlock, twenty years past. Commission of treason."

Corvin's eyebrows came down like shutters, and for a moment I saw the archivist underneath the dozing old man, quick and wary as a rat in a wall.

"That's a sealed folio," he said. "Restricted. You'd need the Provost's counter-signature for anything in the treason class."

"Then I'll get it."

He looked at me a long moment. Twenty years I had been coming to this desk with requests, and in twenty years he had never once looked at me like that — like a woman carrying something in her pockets she ought not to have.

"You were fond of him," Corvin said. Not a question.

"He taught me everything I know about coastlines," I said, which was true, and which had the advantage of being the sort of thing one says without meaning anything by it. "The guild is correcting his atlas. It occurred to me that if I'm to ink over a dead man's work, I should at least know how he died."

That, apparently, was the right answer, or the right shape of answer. Corvin grunted, pulled a ledger toward himself, and made a note in a hand so cramped it might have been a form of encryption. "Send Halloway's slip down before noon," he said. "And girl—" I was forty-one years old and he called me girl; the archive does things to time— "the folio you want is Folio Nine. If you find it thin, that's how it came to us."

* * *

It took me until the afternoon to understand what Corvin had meant, and until the evening to admit to myself that I had known from the moment I opened the box.

Folio Nine lived in the restricted stack, third row, behind the surveys of the northern marshes that no one had ever surveyed properly because no one had ever wanted to know what was there. The box itself was unremarkable: oiled cardboard, brass corners gone green, a wax seal on the lid stamped with the guild's compass rose. I broke the seal with the little knife I use for trimming quills — I remember thinking, absurdly, that I should be more careful, that seals are meant to be preserved, that breaking one cleanly is a courtesy to whoever comes after.

The courtesy turned out to be wasted.

A trial record, in the guild's practice, is a transcript: questions asked, answers given, judgments rendered, each entry dated and initialled by the recording clerk. Aldous's trial had lasted four days. I knew this the way everyone at the guild knew it, the way one knows the date of a battle one never fought in — as a fact worn smooth by handling. Four days of testimony produce, at standard clerk's pace, somewhere between eighty and a hundred leaves.

The folder in my hands held thirty-one.

I laid them out on the reading table under the lamp, and I did what any cartographer would do: I measured the gap. Thirty-one leaves, closely written both sides. Sixty-two pages of record for four days of trial — generous arithmetic, but even at the most expansive rate it accounted for barely two days. And the physical evidence was worse than the arithmetic. Trial transcripts are bound in sequence; the guild sews its records through five stations, and where leaves have been removed the sewing holes remain, small dark mouths in the spine gutter telling you exactly how many voices have been taken out of the choir. I counted the orphaned holes. There were enough for sixty-eight missing leaves.

Someone had gone through this record with a razor and removed two-thirds of what Aldous said in his own defence.

I sat with that for a long while. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above me the guildhall clock struck six, and I did not hear it finish.

And then there was the seal.

The surviving leaves ended, as such records do, with the sentence and the clerk's attestation. But the last leaf was not original — I could tell before I touched it, because the paper was wrong. Guild stock of twenty years ago was rag paper, greyish, with watermarks of the compass rose set at every third interval; the final page was smoother, whiter, a wood-pulp stock the guild hadn't used in my lifetime, watermarked with nothing at all. Upon it, someone had written a summary of the sentence in a clerk's hand that belonged to no clerk I had ever met, and beneath it, where the attestation should have carried the guild's rose, there was instead a seal I did not recognize.

It was a tower. A single tower, crenellated, on a shield, with a key beneath it. Pressed deep and clean in wax the colour of dried blood.

I am a professional. I have spent my life teaching my hands to notice what other hands overlook — the half-degree off true, the coastline drawn from hope instead of survey. I noticed everything about that seal, and I understood none of it, and the combination of total observation and total incomprehension is, I find, the exact recipe for fear.

The guild's records bear the guild's mark. That is the entire theology of archives: the map proves the land because the guild vouches for the map. A foreign seal on a guild transcript is not a clerical error. It is a signature on a forgery, or a receipt for something sold.

I copied the tower-and-key mark into my pocket book — I copy things automatically, the way some people pray — noted the sewing-hole count, the leaf numbers of the surviving pages, the date of attestation versus the date of the replacement leaf (eleven months later; eleven months during which the record had been somewhere, or something had been done to it), and closed the box.

Then I sat in the yellow lamplight among the northern marshes and thought about the fact that I was going to have to tell someone, and about who, in this building, it would be safe to tell.

There was only one name, and it was the wrong one, and I knew it was the wrong one, and I went anyway. That is the thing about the safe world of rules: when it cracks, you do not run from the crack. You run to the person who taught you the rules, because you still believe, against all evidence, that they will explain the breakage as weather, as accident, as nothing.

* * *

Provost Halloway received me in his study at the top of the guildhall, where the light is best and the view is of the harbour, and where he kept — I see now that he always kept — his chair turned slightly so that a visitor must squint toward the window while he himself sat comfortably against the light. An interrogator's arrangement, disguised as hospitality. I had sat in that squinting chair since I was nineteen. I had never once noticed it until that night.

Halloway was seventy, broad still, with a scholar's stoop and a farmer's hands, and he had been kind to me for twenty years with the particular kindness of a man who feels he owes a debt. When Aldous was condemned, his apprentices were dispersed, and Halloway had claimed me personally, finished my training himself, sponsored my elevation, defended every promotion. Everyone said I was fortunate. I said I was fortunate. Tonight I put the box on his desk between the decanter and the bust of Mercator and watched his face while he read the inventory of my findings.

To give the man credit, he was very good.

His eyes went to the sewing holes first — a professional's instinct — and something moved in them that I might, in a generous mood, have called pain. Then the seal. At the seal, his thumb moved, just slightly, across the surface of the inventory sheet, as if he could feel the wax through the paper.

"A tower and key," he said quietly. "Yes."

"You know it?"

"I know of it." He set the sheet down and aligned it with the edge of his desk, a squareness of placement so precise it told me everything his face would not. "Paloma. What you've found is regrettable. It is also not your business, and I say that with love."

"With love," I repeated.

"With the genuine article." He poured two glasses of the guild's indifferent sherry and gave me one, and his hand was steadier than mine. "You are the finest hand this guild has produced in a generation. You have an atlas nearing completion, a name that will outlive you, and a commission of historians arriving within the month to make that name official. You are standing at the door of everything you worked for. On the other side of that door is a razored folio and a seal nobody living can explain, and if you walk through it, you will lose the door forever. Weigh those two things, girl, and tell me honestly which is heavier."

"The truth," I said. "It's heavier. It's always heavier. That's why it holds a map flat."

He smiled at that — a real smile, briefly, the old smile, the one from my apprenticeship when I'd said something sharp about projections and he'd laughed and corrected me. And then the smile went away and he was old, and tired, and looking at me with an expression I had never seen on him and would spend the rest of my life correctly filing as guilt.

"There are pages in every archive that exist to be gaps," he said. "The guild runs on continuity, and continuity occasionally requires that something be cut out and the binding sewn tight over it. You of all people understand erasure. You've spent your career drawing coastlines that omit a hundred things by convention. Sandbanks that shift. Villages too small to matter. A whole town once, in the northern fen country, that appears on no chart we hold—" he stopped himself, a fraction too late, and I felt the word *Merrow* move through the room like a draft, though neither of us had spoken it.

"Who cut the pages?" I asked.

"The pages are cut," Halloway said. "Attribution is a luxury historians can afford because they arrive after. Cartographers arrive before, and their discipline is knowing what to leave unmapped. Leave this unmapped, Paloma. For your master's sake, if not your own." He paused, and here his voice gentled in a way that frightened me more than any threat could have, because it was the gentleness of a man handling something already broken. "For his sake especially. If Aldous Whitlock could speak from wherever the guild sent him, he would tell you the same thing I am telling you now: some doors are locked for the protection of the people on *this* side."

"And the people on the other side?"

He drank his sherry. Above us the clock struck nine, and this time I heard every stroke, and counted them, the way you count anything you cannot otherwise hold.

"Go home," he said. "Finish the coastline. It's beautiful work. Let the historians correct what needs correcting, and in ten years you'll be Provost yourself, and you can decide then which pages deserve to survive you." He refilled my glass unasked, which he had never done, and pressed the stopper back into the decanter with a click that sounded, in the quiet study, like a lock turning. "Promise me you'll sleep on it. That's all. One night. An old man asking a favour of a woman he thinks of as a daughter — grant me that much."

I granted it. I said goodnight. I walked home through streets slick with the first rain, and I did not think about razors or seals or fens; I thought about the word *daughter*, and how carefully he had placed it, the way you place a weight on a scale to stop the needle swinging.

It was only at my own door, fumbling the key, that I understood what had troubled me most about the conversation, and it was not the warning. Warnings are honest; warnings say *stop, or else*. What troubled me was that Halloway had never once asked what I found. Not what the seal depicted, not which leaves remained, not a single question of fact — and Halloway was Provost of the most rigorous institution in the kingdom, and rigour begins with questions.

He hadn't needed to ask.

He already knew what was in Folio Nine. He had known, I suspect, for twenty years. And a man who knows what was cut from a traitor's trial record, and who guards the cutting, and whose voice goes gentle as lint when he speaks of it — that man is not merely an archivist of erasure.

He is one of its authors.

I let myself in, lit no lamp, and stood in the dark of my rooms with the letter from a dead man against my ribs. Tomorrow, Reyes had said, less than you'll wish. I took out my pocket book, looked again at the tower and the key I had copied in the archive's yellow light, and added beneath it, in the smallest hand I could manage, the one thing Halloway's gentleness had confirmed beyond all argument:

*He is afraid of the same map I am.*

At dawn I would pack the plates.
