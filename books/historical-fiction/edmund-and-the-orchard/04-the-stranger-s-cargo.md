## 4. The Stranger's Cargo

I had rehearsed the speech eleven times on the walk from the docks. Not the whole speech — just the opening, the part where a woman opens her door at dusk and finds a stranger standing in her stairwell holding a letter from a hanged man, and you have about four seconds before she screams or sends for the watch.

*Madam Whitlock. Forgive the hour. I bring word of your master.*

Four seconds. That was my budget.

She used three of them looking at my boots, which was fair — they were bad boots, and I'd learned long ago that people judge you by your boots before they judge you by your dead master's handwriting. Then she took the letter, and something happened to her face that I have spent years learning to read, because it kept happening in different faces: the particular collapse of a person who has carried grief so long it has become furniture, and someone has just told them the furniture can walk.

I did not expect what she said next.

"You'd better come up before the landlady sees you. She reports strangers to the guild for a discount on her dues."

Not screaming, then. Hospitality with an edge of conspiracy. I filed that away under *promising* and followed her up stairs that creaked like a confession.

Her rooms were what I expected and also not. Expected: small, cold, ink-stained, the smell of a workshop pretending to be a home. Not expected: the walls. Every wall hung with maps — finished ones, half-finished ones, some that were barely more than coastlines abandoned mid-thought, like sentences she'd walked away from. And in the corner, under a dust sheet, something large and rectangular that she caught me looking at and moved to stand in front of, casually, the way a person stands in front of a door.

"Whitlock," I said, by way of introduction, though I'd already given my name twice. "Ivo Reyes."

"I know. You said." She didn't offer her hand. Guild-trained. Hands are for work; trust is for no one. "You said you knew my master. My master has been dead twenty years."

"Yes."

"And yet he writes letters." She held up the page between two fingers, careful as a surgeon, and I watched her thumb hover over the capital *M* in *Merrow* — the one with the little inward spiral Aldous put on his *M*s when he was being careless and happy, which was rarer than eclipses. "So either this is a very good forgery, in which case you are a fool to show me, because I could hang you with it. Or it is genuine, in which case you are a greater fool still, because you've shown a guild cartographer proof that the state executed the wrong man, or didn't execute him at all, and either way someone paid for it."

"Which do you think it is?"

"I think," she said, "that nobody forges a spiral like that. A forger would make everything beautiful. This is beautiful and lazy in exactly the right places." Her voice did something complicated on the last words. She set the letter down flat on the table, smoothed it once, and looked at me properly for the first time. "Sit down, Mr. Reyes. Tell me how a man comes to carry a dead man's mail."

*

Here is what I told her, more or less, minus the parts I decided to keep.

My father was born in Merrow Fen. I never was, because by the time I was born there was no Merrow Fen to be born in. There was a marsh, a road, a milestone with the numbers scratched off it, and on certain official maps, a blank space so clean you'd never guess anything had been removed — the way a pulled tooth leaves a gap that the tongue keeps finding anyway.

I grew up on the stories anyway. Refugee children grow up on stories the way other children grow up on bread. My mother sang songs about a town I couldn't find on any chart, and when I was old enough to look for it and found nothing, I made the mistake of saying so at the table. She didn't cry. She got up, took down the salt box, and drew the whole town on the tabletop in coarse grain — the church, the ferry steps, our street, the orchard road — and then she said, *There. Now you've seen it. Now it's yours to remember,* and she swept it into her palm and it was gone, and I understood, aged nine, exactly how towns die.

I told Paloma some of this. The salt box. The milestone. I left out my mother's death, because that was mine.

"For twenty years," I said, "the survivors scattered. Forty-some families, winnowed down. They don't write to each other. They don't meet. They learned that two Merrow Fen people in one room is a fire waiting for a match. But they kept things. Papers. A hymnal with names written in the back. My mother kept a scrap of a survey map — a fragment, a corner, with field boundaries and a charter line drawn on it in a hand she recognized, because everyone from the Fen recognized it. The man who surveyed their town. The army's own cartographer, who came before the end, measuring everything, being kind to children, asking questions no soldier had ever bothered to ask. Aldous Whitlock."

"He never spoke of it," Paloma said quietly. "Any of it. Twenty years, and I thought I knew him."

"Good. That means he was careful." I leaned forward. "Now here is the part that concerns you, Madam Whitlock. Your master didn't just survey Merrow Fen. He documented it — boundaries, charters, holdings, the lot. Proof, if anyone had cared to look, that the Duke's grant was a lie written over living villages. And after the burning, when the victors needed the town erased *properly*, someone went hunting every record of it. Every map. Every deed. Every witness. The papers survivors carried were worth more than their lives, and everyone knew it, so the papers went into hiding too — buried, sewn into coat linings, memorized and burned. Scattered like the people themselves."

"So find the pieces," she said. "Collect them."

"That's what I've done. Eleven years of it. Letters to strangers signed with my mother's maiden name. Work as a cooper, a carter, a coffin-maker's apprentice — people tell a coffin-maker things. I have fragments in four languages, two of them dead, and cipher marks all through them. Because your master was clever, Madam Whitlock. He didn't sign his surveys. He hid them inside ordinary charts — corrections, marginal notes, a system of ticks and turns that only someone who knew his conventions could read. The fragments are dumb without the key. I have a crate under my bed that might as well be sealed pottery from a vanished civilization."

"And you need someone who knew his hand."

"I need someone who knew it *intimately*. Who apprenticed under him. Who can look at a stray diagonal tick on a drainage chart and know whether it's a survey mark or a smudge." I spread my hands. "There are perhaps five people alive who could do it. Two are dead. One went mad. One I cannot find. And one," I said, "is you."

The lamp guttered. Neither of us moved to fix it.

*

I want to be honest about what I felt sitting in that room, because honesty is the only currency I had left by then, and I was spending it carefully.

I did not trust her.

Understand: this woman had spent twenty years inside the Cartographers' Guild. The same guild whose seals were on the order that erased my mother's town. The same guild whose historians were, even now, preparing to canonize the lie in a corrected atlas — I knew about that; knowing things was my trade. Paloma Whitlock had inked her master's coastline with steady hands while men with my father's face rotted in unmarked graves. She had thrived inside the machine. Whatever grief she kept behind her ribs, she had kept it *comfortably*, and comfortable grief forgives everything.

And she was looking at me the way a guildswoman looks at cargo: assessing weight, provenance, whether it would damage the rest of the hold.

"You're wondering if I'm bait," I said, because naming a thing takes half its power.

"I'm wondering what happens to me if you're watched."

"Then wonder this instead. If I were watched, would I lead watchers to a woman who can hang me with a single letter? Everything I have — eleven years, my mother's name, a crate of paper worth more than my life — I handed to you tonight in a stairwell because I had no better option. That's not a trap, Madam Whitlock. It's a surrender. Traps are built by people with alternatives."

Something shifted in her face. Not softening exactly. Recalculation.

"Call me Paloma," she said. "And answer me one thing plainly. Why now? Why after eleven years?"

"Because the commission arrives within the season, and when it does, the erasure becomes permanent. Officially certified. After that, even the fragments stop mattering — the lie will have a seal on it." I hesitated. "And because the letter said *before*. Your master wrote that you must bring the plates *before* they come. Which means he knows the schedule somehow. Which means whatever he's protecting is on a clock, and clocks don't care about my caution."

"The plates." She stood, walked to the dust-sheeted rectangle, and stood over it the way a person stands over a sleeping animal they're deciding whether to wake. "You know what's under this sheet?"

"I assumed the atlas."

"It's the atlas. His atlas. The true one — the one they'll 'correct' into fiction next month. I've been finishing his coastlines for six years thinking I was completing a monument. Now you're telling me I was completing evidence." She pulled the sheet off. The frame underneath was plain oak, unremarkable, but the plates racked inside it caught the lamplight — copper, engraved, dense with the fine furious detail of a man drawing a world the way it actually was. "If these are what your letter says they are, then I've been polishing a bomb for six years and calling it devotion."

"That's often what devotion is," I said, and immediately regretted the glibness, but she almost smiled, which told me something about her too.

*

We argued for two hours. I want that on the record, because later — much later — people would ask how we came to ride north together, and the truth is we argued our way into it, suspicion by suspicion, like two merchants haggling over a price neither could afford.

She didn't trust my motives. Fair. I gave her the ledger: my father taken on the second night, my mother's silence, the coffin-maker's apprenticeship, the eleven years, the four languages. She poked holes in it. Good holes. She asked why I hadn't gone to foreign publishers years ago with what I had, and I said because fragments prove nothing and endanger everyone named in them; she conceded that with a grimace, having clearly imagined the reverse — what her own guild would do to sources.

I didn't trust her motives either, and I said so, to her face, because I had decided that if we were going to do this, we would do it without the politeness that gets people killed.

"You were trained by them," I said. "The guild that tried your master, erased my town, and is about to certify the whole thing with a ribbon and a seal. Twenty years inside. Why should I believe one letter flips you?"

"Because it isn't one letter." She said it flatly, without heat, and it landed harder than heat would have. "It's the archive razors, Mr. Reyes. I've seen things for years that I explained to myself because explaining them was cheaper than the alternative. Trial records with pages cut out. Survey grants that vanish. Coastlines I was ordered to draw that contradicted older charts I wasn't allowed to see. I told myself the guild was sloppy." She looked at the plates. "Sloppy doesn't spiral its capital M's. Careful does. If Aldous was careful enough to fake his own death and hide a village inside a cipher, then the guild that condemned him wasn't sloppy either. It was working. And I was a part that worked."

I studied her for a long moment. The lamp had burned low; her face was half shadow, half the gold reflection off the copper plates, and she looked like a woman standing at the exact edge of the world she'd lived on, discovering it had always had a far side.

"Terms," I said.

"Terms," she agreed.

"One: nothing travels until I've seen where the letter points. I don't give the crate to anyone, including you, until I've met whoever wrote that letter alive."

"Agreed. Two: the plates stay with me. They're mine by apprenticeship and by grief, and I'll defend both."

"Agreed. Three—" I stopped. This was the hard one, and I made myself say it. "Three. When this is done, whatever's done — if you get the chance to protect yourself by protecting the guild, you take it. I won't blame you. But you warn me first. I've been sold before. Never again by surprise."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "Counter-term. Same applies to you. If your revenge ever asks for something that costs the innocent, you tell me before you pay. I've read what vengeance does to careful men."

"Vengeance hasn't been invited," I said. "But yes. Agreed."

We shook on it, which surprised us both — her hand was calloused in the specific pattern of a drafting pen's grip, mine in the specific pattern of coffin lids, and we each noticed the other noticing, and something passed between us that wasn't friendship yet but was at least a shared fluency in the grammar of work done in the dark.

*

She packed that night. I watched from the window while she moved through the room with terrible efficiency — instruments wrapped in flannel, ink bottles waxed and corked, and the plates... the plates she handled differently. Slower. At one point she stopped, ran her thumb along an engraving, and read aloud a string of tiny characters in the margin, and her voice cracked in the middle like ice under a boot.

"Cipher?" I asked.

"Instructions," she said. "*To whoever finishes this: check the tidal tables against the Fen chart. Do not trust the third estuary.*" She laughed, a short broken sound. "Twenty years, and he left me homework."

"He left you everything else too," I said. "You just hadn't unwrapped it."

She looked at me sharply — she had a talent for looking sharply — but didn't ask what I meant, because the letter was lying on the table between us, and the letter meant one place, and the place meant morning.

We left before dawn, two figures with bad boots and heavy cases, out through the fish market where the smoke hid us, and I remember thinking that I had spent eleven years collecting pieces of a dead town and had never once, until that stairwell, collected a living person.

I didn't fully trust her. I want that understood. Trust is earned in years and she had given me days.

But as we cleared the north gate and she glanced back once at the city — not wistfully, the way you glance at a home, but the way you glance at a witness you hope won't talk — I thought: *she's afraid of the same thing I am. That we'll do all this, and lose, and the lie will simply continue, well-fed and sealed.*

Fear, held in common, is a kind of rope. Not a strong one. But we tied ourselves to it anyway.

Three miles out, at the fork where the King's Road splits from the old drovers' track, she stopped, frowned at the track, and said slowly, "He made me survey this route. Twice. From rumor, never from direct measurement. He said rumor teaches the map to breathe." She turned to me, and her face in the grey morning light was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to. "Ivo. How many of those routes did he make me learn?"

"As many as he could," I said, "I'd wager."

She stared down the drovers' track the way you'd stare at a letter you'd been carrying unread for years, and then shouldered her case, and we went north — toward an orchard that officially did not exist, and a man who officially had been dead for twenty years, and whatever was waiting there that had frightened Aldous Whitlock enough to break two decades of silence with one spiral-capitalled plea.

I did not know then that the commission was already counting the days. But the letter had said *before*, and *before* is the most urgent word in any language.
