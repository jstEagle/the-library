## 5. North by Smugglers' Light

We left the city through a gate I had drawn eleven times and never once walked through.

That is the cartographer's curse, if you want it plainly: I could render the Salt Gate's battlements to the width of a hair, could tell you the drainage grates ran north-northeast and that the tanners' stalls encroached three feet into the right-of-way every Michaelmas. But I had never stood beneath the actual arch, never smelled the actual brine off the carts, never felt how the stones dip in the middle like an old man's shoulders. Maps are true the way portraits are true — faithfully, and from a distance, and always of someone who has already gone.

Ivo went first, hood up, walking with the unhurried gait of a man who has been stopped by watchmen before and does not care to be stopped again tonight. He had insisted we leave before dawn rather than after dark, on the grounds that guild messengers keep scholar's hours and would not ride until they'd eaten. "Halloway's people," he said, "will be thorough. Thorough men breakfast."

It was the most generous thing anyone had ever said about my former colleagues, and I resented how much comfort I took from it.

The plates rode against my back in a oilcloth wrap I had sewn myself two nights earlier, in the interval between deciding to flee and being able to admit I had decided to flee. There is a particular hour — I have since learned it is well known to fugitives — when you stop packing as though you will return. The second needleful where you sew for keeps. My apprentice atlas went in last, the false-bottomed frame Aldous himself had taught me to build, back when I thought he was teaching me vanity: *A mapmaker who cannot hide her own work*, he said, tapping the lining, *is only a clerk with better handwriting.*

I had laughed then. I was nineteen and everything was a lesson and nothing was aimed at me.

* * *

By the second mile the city had fallen behind into its own smoke, and the road became the thing Aldous had actually spent ten years putting into me, which I am only now writing down honestly: not draftsmanship, not the geometry of projections, not even the beautiful discipline of the contour line. He had put roads in me. Specific ones. Roads that appear on no charter, no survey, no commission document — roads I had drawn once, early, in an exercise he set me, and which he had then made me burn.

"You mapped those from rumor," Ivo said. It was not a question. We were walking a track so faint the grass grew in the middle of it, along a ridge that smelled of sheep and iron, and I had just told him, without thinking, that there was a culvert two miles ahead that flooded in any rain and that we should climb above it while the going was dry.

"From rumor," I agreed. "And from a drunk ferryman, and from half a smuggler's confession, and from a ballad." I heard myself and winced. "You may laugh."

"I'm not laughing." He had stopped. In the grey light his face did something complicated. "How does a guild cartographer come to hold forty miles of unrecorded salt routes in her head?"

"Master Whitlock taught them to me."

"As what? An exercise?"

"I thought so. He called it 'drawing the country that doesn't wish to be seen.' I assumed it was a philosophical flourish. He was fond of flourishes." I touched the strap of the plate-wrap, the gesture that had become my rosary. "He made me draw the routes three times and burn each one. He said the third drawing was the one that mattered because it lived nowhere but here." I tapped my temple, feeling foolish even as I did it, and then stopped feeling foolish, because Ivo was looking at me the way you look at a locked chest whose key you've carried for years without knowing.

"He wasn't teaching you routes," Ivo said slowly. "He was burying them in the one place no historian would ever search."

We stood on the ridge with the wind pushing at our backs and neither of us spoke, and I understood — in the way you understand a thing all at once, the whole weight of it arriving before the shape of it — that my apprenticeship had not been an apprenticeship at all. Or rather, that it had been one, but to a trade wider than the guild's charter. Every odd exercise, every burned sheet, every eccentric demand — *learn the fog patterns of the Fen coast, girl; recite the tidal creeks backward; why do you suppose a drowned valley holds its water like a secret?* — had been Aldous storing something in me. A message sealed in living wax. Not sent. Entrusted.

He could not write it down anywhere. So he wrote it in me.

"Well," I said at last, because wryness is the only dignity available when you have just discovered your entire life was someone else's contingency plan, "it's flattering, I suppose, to learn you were your master's safest archive. Though I'd have preferred a raise."

Ivo snorted, and we went on.

* * *

The salt routes deserve their own honest paragraph, because history — official history, the kind printed on good paper — will tell you the old salt trade died with the Fen ports, and official history is wrong in the specific way it is usually wrong: not lying, merely tidy. The trade died; the routes remained, like veins in a body whose heart has stopped. Smugglers kept them. Then smugglers' sons forgot them. Then only the land remembered, and the land tells no one anything unless someone asks in exactly the right language, which is what a survey is, or would be, if the guild ever surveyed things it had decided not to see.

I had asked. Under duress, under laughter, over ten years of small cruelties of pedagogy, Aldous had made me ask the land everything, and the land had answered, and now I walked the answers.

We came down off the ridge at midmorning to the culvert I had promised — flooded, sure enough, brown water standing fat across the track — and climbed the detour path above it that existed only as a sheep-trail suggestion. Below us the drowned valley opened, green and silver and utterly innocent, holding its water like a secret.

"That's Merrow Fen down there," Ivo said quietly. "What's left of the lake side of it."

I looked. I had drawn that valley twice from rumor, both times burning the sheet, and seeing it with eyes was like meeting a correspondent of twenty years. The notch in the western hills where the fog pooled — yes, exactly where I'd placed it, smug little nineteen-year-old that I'd been. The creek that braided. The drowned orchard lines visible as darker shadows under the shallows, rows of dead trees still standing in the flooded ground like a congregation.

"They let the water up," Ivo said. "After. To grow the lake over the town. Drowned valleys don't show crop marks, don't show foundations. You can walk the shore of a lie and it looks like weather."

"The Duke's survey shows open water," I said. "I've inked it. Three times." My hands remembered the coastline, that smooth confident blue, and I felt suddenly sick at my own craftsmanship, at how beautiful a lie can be made by patient hands that simply follow instructions. "I thought it was just geography. A valley flooded for a reservoir. Nobody told me towns get erased."

"Nobody told you lots of things." He said it without heat, which was worse. "That's the arrangement. The guild stays safe by not knowing, and its members stay safe by not asking, and everyone's ignorance gets bound in leather and put on a shelf and called knowledge."

I didn't argue. Partly because he was right, mostly because arguing required admitting I had spent twenty years being expertly, comfortably blind, and my pride needed the rest of the day to negotiate its surrender terms.

* * *

We slept that first night in a charcoal burner's hut that appeared on no chart I'd ever seen and which Ivo knew of anyway, which irritated me more than I can properly express. There is nothing quite so humbling as being out-local-knowledge'd in your own specialty by a man whose maps are entirely imaginary.

The second day we crossed the high moor by the coffin-path — so named, per my drunk ferryman of fifteen years ago, for the bodies it once carried down to consecrated ground — and I began keeping what I privately called the Ledger of Aldous's Ghost. Every time I knew something I should not know, I counted it. A spring that ran sweet when every other water on the moor tasted of peat: known. The ford that lies about its depth except at dawn: known. The ruined chapel whose north wall shelters a hollow deep enough for two people and their baggage and their mutual distrust: known, known, known.

By dusk of the third day the ledger held thirty-one entries, and I stopped counting, because the total had become unbearable in a new way. Not frightening. Grieving.

Because here is what I finally understood, walking a smuggler's lightless road with my dead master's plates on my back: he had expected this. Not this week, perhaps. Not this route. But some hour, someday, when the silences he'd spent twenty years pressing flat finally swelled, he had made sure that the person most likely to come looking would be able to travel the whole distance on the roads inside her own head. He had built me a bridge out of lessons and burned every plank behind him so no one else could follow.

Or — crueler thought, and truer — so that I could not be traced. A map can be seized. A letter can be intercepted. But a girl who recites tidal creeks backward for practice is evidence of nothing.

"He loved you," Ivo said that night, apropos of nothing, watching me feed the tiny fire. We had reached the stage of acquaintance where he volunteered one observation per evening, always the observation I least wanted.

"He abandoned me," I said. "Twenty years of thinking my master died a traitor's death, and learning my letters from strangers, and defending his name to idiots at the guild who called him the Fen Butcher's pencil. Twenty years, Ivo. If he loved me, he had an interesting way of scheduling it."

"And if he hadn't," Ivo said, "you'd be inking the Duke's coastlines right now, safe and stupid, until the commission corrected you out of a job." He shrugged. "Men like him don't get to love in the present tense. They love forward. Into people who'll still be standing later."

I poked the fire harder than the fire deserved. "You rehearse that speech?"

"I'm his son in everything but blood," Ivo said. "Who do you think taught me to love forward?"

* * *

The fourth day the routes narrowed into what I can only describe as Aldous's signature.

Every surveyor develops a hand — a bias in their north arrows, a habit of hachure, some private tic in how they break a contour. Aldous broke his contours slightly east of true, a flaw I had mocked him for and inherited involuntarily. And now the landscape itself began doing it. Where a stranger would route a path along the obvious valley floor, our track climbed instead to an eastern shelf, exposed to view from the west but invisible from the roads. Again. And again. Four crossings in succession, each choosing the eastern bench, each irrational by any logic of grade or distance, each perfectly rational by the logic of a man designing a corridor that could not be watched from the direction soldiers would come from.

"It's his hand," I whispered, on the fourth one, stopping dead in the track. "The routes are drawn in his hand. I'd know it anywhere. I'd know it —"

— the way I knew the capitals of a letter that arrived three days ago from a man officially executed twenty years ago. Yes. That way.

Ivo waited, letting me have the moment, which was either kindness or patience and is often indistinguishable.

"He couldn't leave a paper trail," I said, working it aloud because saying things makes them survivable, "so he left a trail in the world. He surveyed these paths himself. Cut them himself, maybe, or bribed the cutters. And he built the whole sequence the way he builds a map — with his own biases in it — knowing that if anyone ever followed it, the one person who'd recognize the hand would be —"

"You," Ivo said.

"Me." I laughed, and it came out wrong, closer to the other thing. "All those years I thought he was teaching me to draw. He was teaching the ground to speak, and teaching me its language, and the two lessons were the same lesson, and I burned the sheets because he told me to and kept the knowledge because he made me, and now I'm walking home along his handwriting toward a man who has been dead for twenty years, and I don't know whether to weep or bill his estate."

"Weep later," Ivo said, not unkindly. "There's a fog coming off the fen, and Sera says the third stile past the black thorn is rotten, and if you know that already, I'll stop talking."

I knew it already.

* * *

We crossed into the fen country proper on the fifth night, traveling the last hours by dark on purpose, because the final approaches were visible from the Duke's new military road and Aldous — his handwriting said so, in the grammar of its choices — had wanted them walked unseen. The fog came up around our knees like something alive and domestic, a dog greeting us home, and I navigated by sensations I had no right to possess: the smell-shift where peat gave way to orchard soil, the sound of water changing its mind beneath a turf bridge, a quarter-mile of path that hummed differently underfoot because it was corduroyed with old logs under the moss.

Once, far behind us on the military road, lanterns. Two, maybe three, moving with the unhurried confidence of men carrying papers that authorize them.

Guild messengers eat breakfast, Halloway's thorough men. But thorough men also travel all night when the prize justifies it, and I pressed my palm flat against the plate-wrap on my back and stood very still among the apple trees — actual trees, living ones, the first we'd seen, absurd and miraculous on that drowned margin — until the lights crawled past whatever horizon fen fog permits.

Ivo watched me. When the lanterns were gone he said, "They're a day behind. Maybe less."

"Then we shouldn't sleep."

"We should sleep exactly four hours," he said, "because tomorrow we arrive, and exhausted people meet their dead looking like fools." He paused. "She'll know you, you understand. Sera Voss. He wrote about you. Every year, another letter. You were the thing he was proud of."

Something in my chest performed a maneuver I chose not to examine. "He never sent for me."

"No," Ivo said. "But you were always coming. He just made sure that when you came, you could find the way." He settled against an apple trunk, hat over eyes, conversation concluded. "Sleep, cartographer. You're nearly there. The last mile's his best sentence."

I did not ask what he meant. I saved it, the way Aldous had taught me to save everything — folded small, pressed flat, stored against the day the silence swells.

Four hours later we rose and walked the last mile of a dead man's handwriting, and over the fog ahead, impossibly, rose terraces — green steps cut into the shoulder of the drowned valley, and on them, trees. Hundreds. Trees that had been carried out of a burning town in buckets and fists and children's arms twenty years ago and planted here where no record would ever find them, and the fog parted around the highest terrace like a curtain, and a woman stood at the top of the steps with a lantern, waiting, as though she had read the same letter we had.

She was looking at me, not Ivo, and she was smiling the way people smile at something they have been promised for a long time.

"Paloma," she said — and I had never heard her voice in my life, and somehow I knew the name belonged to her, because of course Aldous's letters had names in them, his letters had everything in them, I just hadn't been allowed to read them yet — "you took the eastern benches. All four. He said you would."
