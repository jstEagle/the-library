## 6. The Orchard at World's Edge

The last day of the climb, Ivo stopped talking.

He had talked for six days straight — about salt prices, about the idiocy of guild road taxes, about the correct way to gut a trout, a subject on which he held views of evangelical intensity — and I had come to understand that his chatter was not nervousness but camouflage. A man who talks constantly is a man who cannot be questioned. But on the morning we turned off the drover's track onto a path that did not officially exist, he went quiet as a closed book, and the silence had a different texture than his usual one. It was the silence of a man approaching something he loved and could not defend.

The path climbed through birch scrub so thin it looked like the hill had been combed with wire. There was no sign of habitation. There was no sign of anything, which was precisely the point; I had spent three days trying to fix our position on the mental chart Aldous had built into me twenty years ago, and three days failing, because this country had been left blank. Not surveyed-blank. *Left* blank. Somebody had drawn nothing here on purpose, the way you leave a margin around a thing you mean to cut out.

"You're doing the face," Ivo said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're redrawing the world in your head and disliking what you find." He didn't look back at me. "Your master did that too. Stood on ridgelines looking like a man doing sums against God."

"You knew him?" It was a question I had asked in a dozen forms over six days and gotten a dozen non-answers. "Before the orchard. You said you spent years finding someone who could read his cipher. When did you first —"

"Later," he said. Which was Ivo's way of saying *when I trust you*, which was Ivo's way of saying *perhaps never*, and I was learning to let him have it.

We climbed until noon. Then the scrub ended all at once, the way a sentence ends, and we stood on the first terrace of an orchard that should not have been there.

Terraces cut into the hillside, old ones, the retaining walls faced with stone laid in the herringbone pattern I knew from Fen-country field boundaries — a pattern that appeared on no surviving map because the fields it belonged to no longer survived either. Apple trees in long ranks, perhaps two hundred of them, heavy with fruit so late in the season it seemed almost stubborn. Beyond and below them, the land fell away into a valley that had been drowned before my grandmother's grandmother, a grey sheet of water lying flat as poured pewter under the afternoon sky, holding the reflection of clouds with the patient accuracy of a mirror that has nothing else to do.

Nobody had ever mapped this. That was my first thought, professional and immediate: *nobody has ever mapped this.* My second thought arrived slower and sat down harder on my chest: *this is why.*

"It's beautiful," I said, stupidly, because Ivo was watching me and I could not give him the other thing.

"It's forty people," he said. "Remember that when you start admiring it. Beauty is what you get instead of safety."

A dog found us before a person did — a lean yellow bitch who came up the terrace at a trot, smelled Ivo's hand, and then sat down in front of me with the air of an official conducting an inspection. By the time she had finished sniffing my boots, my satchel, and the hem of my coat with particular attention to my pockets, three figures had come up from the cider press below: a young man with a scarred forearm, a girl of perhaps twelve carrying a basket of windfalls, and behind them, leaning on a stick but clearly using it as an ornament rather than a necessity, an old woman with white hair braided around her head like a crown.

She looked at Ivo first and something passed between them — not warmth exactly, recognition of a debt being called in. Then she looked at me, and her face did a thing I have only seen on the faces of people meeting ghosts.

"Pip," she said.

The word went through me like a splinter finding its old channel. I had not heard it in thirty years. I had not heard it since I was six years old and a tall man with ink-stained fingers had swung me around a workshop by my wrists calling me *Pip, little Pip, my apprentice magpie*, before I was old enough to be anyone's apprentice, before the word apprentice meant anything except that I got to sit on a stool and grind pigment while he worked.

"I'm sorry?" I said, in the voice of a woman whose entire profession is the suppression of error.

"Sera Voss," said the old woman, to me, though I hadn't asked. "And you are Paloma Whitlock, journeyman cartographer, admitted to the Guild of Surveyors four years after your master's execution, commendation for precision, no family, no husband, no vices worth the name. He wrote to us about you." She came closer, stick tapping the stones. "Every year, a letter, when a letter could be risked. Every year the same postscript. *Is the magpie flying? Does she suspect what I taught her?*" Her eyes were quick and brown and terribly kind. "He said you'd come if anyone did. He said you were the best hands he ever trained and the most frightened heart he ever broke, and that both of those things were his fault."

"I don't—" I began, and stopped, because the lie available to me was so poor that even my mouth refused to carry it. I looked past her at the terraces, at the herringbone stone, at the drowned valley shining below, and heard myself say, in the small voice of a six-year-old grinding pigment: "He used to call me Pip."

"Yes," said Sera Voss. "Well. Come down out of the wind, child. Forty years I've waited to feed you, and I won't have it said I kept a Whitlock standing on a hilltop."

* * *

They fed us in a long low house built into the hillside like something the hill had grown rather than permitted, with a hearth at each end and a table that had clearly been lengthened twice by splicing in new planks. The whole community came through in relays over the evening — I counted them without meaning to, an occupational tic: thirty-nine, then with Sera and the scarred young man, forty-one, close enough to Ivo's figure of forty — and every one of them looked at me the same way, with an attention so direct it felt like being read aloud from.

They were, I understood slowly, checking me for resemblance. Not to Aldous — to themselves. To the town. A girl brought her grandmother to look at me; the grandmother studied my face and said something in a dialect I didn't know, and the girl translated, blushing: "She says you frown like the ferryman's wife used to. From the Fen."

"There is no Fen," I said automatically. "Merrow Fen was —"

"— drowned," half a dozen voices said together, gently, and someone laughed, not unkindly, and a man near the hearth said, "There she goes, the guild speaking," and someone else shushed him, and I sat with my bowl of barley and lamb and understood that I had just done the thing they had all been waiting to see whether I would do. Recite the official absence. Say the erasure out loud as though recitation made it true.

"I'm sorry," I said. "It's reflex. They teach you that the blanks on the map are blank because there's nothing there. Nobody teaches you that somebody drew the blank."

Sera Voss, across the table, nodded slowly, as if I had finally handed in homework. "That's the first honest sentence out of your mouth," she said. "Took you four hours. Your master took four years."

Afterward she walked me out onto the terrace above the cider press, where the late light lay along the rows of trees and everything smelled of fruit going soft and sweet in the grass. The drowned valley below had gone the colour of hammered tin.

"Tell me about the trees," I said, because it was safer than telling her about the splinter in my chest that had been working deeper all evening, and because I am a coward in the ways I was trained to be.

"Carried out," she said simply. "The night of the fire. Grafted whips, root stock, saplings in pots, whatever hands could hold. Forty-one of us came up out of the Fen that night and every family carried a tree." She put her hand on the nearest trunk, bark silvered and furrowed. "This one's a Merrow Pippin. There are maybe sixty of the variety left alive in the world and every single one descends from trees that walked out of that fire in people's arms. The Duke's surveyors wrote that the orchards of Merrow Fen were destroyed entire. They wrote the truth by accident and didn't know it — destroyed entire, yes, every tree burned. And here they all are." Her smile had a blade in it somewhere. "You can't drown an orchard in records, child. Someone always carries the rootstock out."

I put my hand on the tree beside hers. The bark was warm from the day.

"Why am I here, Sera? Truly. If Aldous has been writing to you for twenty years, if he's been safe, if all of this exists — why summon me now, with plates, with deadlines, with historians coming?"

She was quiet a moment. Below us, someone began turning the press and the smell of crushed apples rose up sharp and green.

"Because the letters stopped being enough," she said. "Paper remembers what we tell it. But paper isn't witnesses. We are forty old grudges wrapped in wool, Paloma, and we are dying off one by one like everybody else. Tam's father went two winters back. Old Maren last spring. When we go, the truth goes back to being what the Duke's historians say it is — a story with no living mouths." She turned those quick brown eyes on me. "But documents, properly made, sworn, witnessed, sealed — documents live longer than bodies. He taught you to make documents that outlive their makers. That's what you're for. That's what you've always been for. He just needed you grown first."

"Grown," I repeated. "Twenty years grown. He could have sent for me at any point in twenty years and instead he let me ink a dead man's coastline."

"And what would you have done," said Sera Voss mildly, "if a letter had come, in his hand, in year three? Year five? Year ten, when you'd just made journeyman and your whole standing hung on a thread?" She waited, and let me sit in it. "You'd have gone to the guild provosts, or you'd have gone alone and badly, or you'd have gone and brought attention down on this hill like a lantern into a hay barn. He chose your ignorance because ignorance was armour. Don't hate him for it yet, child. Hate him tonight if you must. But wait until you've seen what he's been keeping."

My chest did something complicated. "You've seen him. Recently. He's—"

"Alive," she said, "which will be the second-worst shock of your week, and older than you're expecting, which will be the third. As for the worst—" she patted my hand where it lay on the warm bark, and her grip was stronger than her stick suggested, "—the worst shock is usually yourself, arriving. It always is, at this age."

From below came the groan of the press, and voices, and Ivo laughing at something — actually laughing, a sound I hadn't heard from him in six days of trout-gutting doctrine — and the drowned valley gave back the last of the light like a coin returned.

"One more thing," Sera said, as we turned toward the house. "Tomorrow, early, we take you to him. He doesn't know yet that you've come. Ivo kept that from the letters deliberately — said if it went wrong on the road, better the old man grieve once than twice." Her mouth compressed. "But fair warning, Paloma Whitlock. Fair warning, Pip. When he sees you, he is going to say something unforgivable. He always leads with the unforgivable. It's how he asks to be forgiven."

The stars were coming out over the valley, in patterns no charter had ever granted to anyone.

"And what," I said, "do I lead with?"

Sera Voss considered this with the seriousness of a woman who had buried friends and trees and a whole town and still took questions seriously.

"Whatever's true," she said. "It'll be the first time in twenty years either of you tries it. Should be quite a morning."
