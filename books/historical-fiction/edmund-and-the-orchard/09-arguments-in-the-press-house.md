## 9. Arguments in the Press House

I had argued before kings' councils. I had argued before a tribunal that was already decided, standing on a scaffold with my neck measured for the rope that would not, in the event, take me. I have argued with surveyors who insisted a river ran where their pride said it ran rather than where their instruments did.

None of it prepared me for arguing with Paloma Whitlock in my own root cellar, with the smell of apples going soft in the dark and her hand flat on the atlas as though it were already hers to give away.

She had not moved from the table. Neither had I, if truth be told, though I told myself I remained standing at the shelf because my knees preferred the cold to the effort of sitting again. Fifty years of kneeling over drafting tables collects its debts all at once, and it collects them in the joints.

"Say it plainly," she said. "You want it burned."

"I want it *safe*," I said, "and burning is the only safety left that I believe in."

"That is not plain, Master. That is a man dressing up despair as prudence and calling it a coat."

I had forgotten — no, I had not forgotten, I had *filed away*, in the drawer a man keeps for things too sharp to handle daily — how quickly she could find the soft place in an argument. She had been nineteen and I sixty when they took her from me, and even then she could gut a careless sentence like a fishmonger, apologizing the whole while. She apologized now, too. That was the worst of it. She said "forgive me, Master" in the same breath she used the word *despair*, as if courtesy were a blade sheath.

"His name was Corvin Braille," I said.

She blinked. "Whose?"

"The man who hanged." I took down the lamp from its hook, more because my hands wanted employment than because the cellar needed light; we had been working by one lamp already and the second only doubled the shadows. "You'll have read of him, or you won't. The histories call him a forger of documents in the Duke's cause, which is a polite way of saying he copied charters. He was my friend. He was the one who found the original grant — Merrow Fen, four hundred households, sealed under the old king's signet, forty years before the war. He copied it twice. He kept one copy and sent me the other, and then he stood up in a tavern in Harrowgate and read the thing aloud to a room full of dockmen because he believed — this is the part that undoes me, Paloma, after twenty years of turning it over — he believed that once people *knew*, the knowing would do the work by itself. That truth was a wheel set rolling and nothing could stop it."

The lamp guttered. Down here the air came through a vent shaft that breathed when the valley wind did, so the flame leaned and righted itself like something listening.

"They stopped the wheel," I said. "It took them nine days to stop it. And every one of those nine days, men who had heard Corvin read the charter were taken from their beds, and the ones who could be made useful were made useful, and the ones who couldn't were hanged at the crossroads with a paper pinned to them saying *sower of sedition*. Forty-one people, Paloma. I have the list. I drew the map of those crossroads myself, afterward, for the commission, because I was still the chief cartographer then and nobody had thought yet to wonder about me. Forty-one names. I can recite them faster than I can recite the rivers of the eastern march, which tells you which document I have revised more often."

Paloma was quiet. I watched her take it in the way she used to take in a difficult projection — head slightly tilted, lips moving, translating the argument into geometry behind her eyes. God help me, I loved her for it and I resented it in the same heartbeat, because I knew what the geometry would tell her: that my story proved exactly the opposite of what I wanted it to prove, and she would see it before I finished speaking.

"So your evidence," she said slowly, "is that a truth told once, aloud, in a tavern, without copies, without testimony, without maps — a single performance, unrecorded — got forty-one people killed and changed nothing." She looked up. "Master, you have just made my case better than I could."

"It proves that they will kill anyone who carries it."

"It proves they killed one carrier of one copy. It proves nothing about a hundred carriers of fifty copies across a strait they don't control." She stood, finally, and pressed both palms to the table, and I saw that her hands were inked to the second knuckle — she had been copying since before dawn, my corrections in her lines, my hatching in her shadows, and some traitorous corner of me noted with professional satisfaction that her lettering had improved past anything I managed at her age. "And it proves something else you're not saying. Corvin died *and the land stayed stolen anyway*. His death bought the Duke his title. Silence didn't protect anyone in the end — Merrow Fen burned, Sera's husband burned, forty-one strangers burned, and the maps in the guildhall still show empty meadow. Twenty years of your silence, Master, and what has it purchased?"

"Twenty years," I said, and my voice cracked on it like a dried reed, "of forty living people upstairs who would be bones in a fen if I had published. Ask Sera whether silence purchases anything. Ask Tam's mother."

"I intend to," said Paloma. "In fact, Master, I refuse to let this be decided between the two of us at all. You taught me that. First lesson, first day, ink on my fingers before breakfast: *the map is never the mapper's alone.* A survey belongs to the ground it records. This cellar holds the record of four hundred households. Let the ground vote."

* * *

Sera Voss called them together in the press house, which is what we had taken to calling the long shed where the cider press stood, because it was the only roof big enough for the whole orchard and because, I suspect, Sera liked the symbolism of deciding the fate of documents in the building where fruit was crushed into something that kept.

Forty-two souls, counting me. Counting Paloma and Ivo Reyes, who stood at the back with his arms crossed and his face doing what it always did, which was refusing to commit to any expression a watcher could use against him. Counting Tam, who sat near the door the way he always did, near enough to run, and who would not look at his mother.

Sera did not make a speech. Sera had buried a husband and a town and had no patience left over for rhetoric. She said, "Aldous wants to burn the true atlas. The cartographer wants to send it out into the world. We've heard them both. Now speak, all of you, and whoever speaks loudest about courage speaks first about whose children stand watch tomorrow." Then she sat down on a cider barrel and folded her hands, which was her way of saying the floor belonged to us.

I spoke first, because it was my funeral we were debating and I felt entitled to a word at it.

I gave them the version I had given Paloma. I tried to give it better — I am not a poor speaker when the matter warrants — and I watched their faces while I did it, faces I had lived among for twenty years, faces I had taught letters to, faces I had doctored through fevers and mapped escape routes for in my head during the bad nights when hoofbeats came down the valley road. I owed them honesty, and honesty meant telling them the price I had already paid once on their behalf: my name, my guild, my grave with my own name carved on a stone that lied.

"Burn it," I said, "and we stay alive. Publish it, and some fraction of us die well. I have done the arithmetic of this trade for fifty years, and I tell you the dead cannot read."

Silence, then, and the wind in the press-house slats.

Then Maren Oakes — third generation orchard keeper, born in the fen before the fire, a woman who remembered running with her sister's hand in hers — said, "My sister is bones in a fen, Aldous, and she cannot read either. But my granddaughter can. What do the dead get from our caution?" And that, I confess, stung worse than anything Paloma had said, because it was my own argument returned to me wearing a face.

Old Hew, who never agreed with anyone, agreed with me, loudly, and cited the commission's soldiers and the number of them, which he estimated at sixty and which everyone understood to mean *too many*. Danya, the weaver, said her loom could carry patterns into cloth that a searching soldier wouldn't recognize, and wasn't that also publication, and if publication in thread was safe why not in ink — and Hew told her thread doesn't testify, and Danya said neither does ash, and around it went.

Tam spoke last of the adults, and his voice shook, and I saw his mother close her eyes.

"I wrote to my brother," Tam said. "Three years. Every letter read, maybe, every letter a knife pointed at this orchard, and I did it for love of him, and I'd do it again, and I don't know what side of this I'm on except—" he stopped, and started again, quieter. "Except I know what it is to hold a true thing and not say it. It eats you. That's all. That's everything I know."

Then Paloma stood, and unrolled the proof sheet she had been working on since dawn, and pinned it to the press beam with two apple-wood pegs, and let them come and look, and they came and looked, and the room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet before a map.

It was the valley. Our valley, and the drowned fen below it, drawn twice — once as the Duke's surveyors had recorded it, empty meadow, no town, no charter, no graves; and once as it was. The second drawing held the streets of Merrow Fen under the water line, rendered in the faint blue hatch I had invented for submerged foundations thirty years ago and never once had occasion to use until tonight. Four hundred doorways. The mill. The church with its leaning tower. Names, where we knew them, along the lanes.

"My master says the dead cannot read," Paloma said. "Perhaps not. But the living can read *about* the dead, and the dead in this water have been unread for twenty years. Master Aldous fears that publishing kills. I say a truth unpublished dies twice — once when you burn it, and once more, slower, when the historians finish their correction and write *there was never a town here* into every book in the Duchy. The first death is quick and the second one is forever. I know which I'd choose for my own name. I know which I'd choose for theirs."

She put her hand flat on the proof sheet, over the drowned church.

"You have spent twenty years being safe," she said to the room, gently, terribly. "Has it felt like living?"

* * *

The vote, when Sera finally called it — by raising of hands, no secret ballots, because Sera said fear works best in the dark and we were done conducting business in the dark — went thirteen for burning, sixteen for publication, and the rest abstaining, some because they genuinely wavered and some, I think, because they could not bring themselves to raise a hand either for or against me.

Thirteen and sixteen. A majority of three souls and an orchard torn down its middle like a survey page.

I looked at the count and felt something in my chest loosen that I had mistaken for a bone setting wrong these twenty years. They had voted against me. Good. Let it be their courage and not my sacrifice; let the guilt be distributed where courage is, which is everywhere and evenly, instead of pooled in one old man's cellar.

Sera wrote the tally in the orchard ledger, in her careful schoolmistress hand, and dated it, and said, "Recorded. Whatever comes of it, we chose it awake." Which is more than most towns can say of most of their history.

Paloma began rolling her proofs. Ivo Reyes pushed off the wall at last and said something low to Tam, and the two of them put their heads together over a sketch of the salt road, and I understood that the argument was over and the logistics had begun, which is how all real decisions announce themselves.

"One condition," I said, and my voice carried further than I intended in the sudden busy quiet. Everyone turned. "If it goes out, I go with it. Not abroad — I'm half-blind and no use on a boat. But my confession goes in the front matter. The falsified execution order, in my own hand, signed. If the truth hangs anyone, let it hang the man who deserves the rope."

"Aldous—" Sera started.

"That's the condition," I said. "The atlas was mine. The lie was mine. The signature on that order was written by my pen if not my heart, and a record that cleans me is not the record I spent twenty years keeping."

Paloma met my eyes across the press house, over the heads of the frightened and the brave, and I saw her understanding it — really understanding, the way she understood a coastline, taking its whole measure at once — and nodding once, slow, the way her old master used to nod over a plate he finally approved.

It was Tam, at the door, white-faced, who broke the moment.

"There's dust on the north terrace path," he said. "Riding dust. A lot of it. And it's coming early."
