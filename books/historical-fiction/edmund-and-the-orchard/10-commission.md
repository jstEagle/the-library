## 10. Commission

The pigeon arrived on a Tuesday, which I mention only because Tuesdays had become, by that point in my life, the day I trusted least. Bad news has a sense of occasion. It does not come on ordinary days when you are prepared for it; it comes when the bread is proving and someone is laughing and the whole valley smells of apples, and it lands on the roof of the dovecote with the smug finality of a stone dropped down a well.

The bird belonged to Tam. That was the first wrong thing. Tam kept pigeons the way lonely men keep dogs — for company, not correspondence — and he had sworn to me a dozen times that none of his birds carried rings from anywhere south of the fens. This one wore a ring stamped with the Duke's seal, small and red and official as a birthmark.

He brought it to me at the terraces' upper wall, where I was pretending to prune a tree I had already pruned twice out of pure nervousness. He held the message tube out at arm's length, the way you might hold a snake you suspected of being venomous but couldn't prove.

"It came in an hour ago," he said. "I didn't read it."

"You didn't read it?"

"I can't read most of it. There are words." He said *words* the way other people said *rats*. "There's a seal, though. You know seals."

I did know seals. I had spent four years of my life studying seals — wax ones, inked ones, the great embossed monsters that ate whole paragraphs of a charter and left nothing but authority behind. I took the tube, cracked the wax with my thumbnail, and unrolled the strip of paper inside.

It was written in a clerk's hand, the kind of hand that has never once in its life been asked to hurry. It was addressed, interestingly, not to anyone but to *the inhabitants of the valley of the drowned fen, whom it may concern* — which is bureaucratic language for *we know you're there and we have decided not to say so*.

I read it twice. Then I sat down on the pruning stool, because my legs had made an executive decision without consulting me.

"Well?" said Tam.

"It seems," I said, and found I had to clear my throat, "that we are about to receive visitors. Distinguished ones. The Royal Historical Commission for the Certification of Landed Title is pleased to announce its intention to survey the remains of Merrow Fen and its environs, in order to establish — and here they use their own phrase — *the continuous and lawful occupation of the said lands by His Grace the Duke since time immemorial*."

Tam frowned. "Time immemorial. Is that long?"

"That's before memory. That's so long ago that nobody who could argue with it is alive."

"Oh," said Tam, in the flat voice of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. "Except us."

"Except," I said, "us."

* * *

I want to be fair to the commission, in the way that a man wants to be fair to the wolf that has him by the leg: precisely, coldly, with full attention to the teeth. The commission itself was, on paper, a reasonable institution. After the war, somebody had to decide whose version of twenty years of bloodletting would be written into the land records, and the victors had done what victors always do — they had appointed scholars to make their theft look like geography. A commission of learned men, traveling with instruments and clerks, certifying titles, correcting boundaries, adjusting history until it lay flat against the Duke's claims like a sheet pulled tight over a corpse.

They were expected in the valley within a fortnight. The letter said so with the cheerful confidence of an appointment one has no intention of keeping flexible. They would arrive with soldiers — the letter mentioned this as a matter of logistics, *for the commissioners' security*, as though ruins were famous for ambush — and they would survey, measure, sketch, and certify. And then Merrow Fen would be legally, permanently, irrevocably empty. Not burned. Not massacred. Simply never there.

Paloma read the letter after supper, standing in the doorway of the long house with the lamplight behind her, and I watched her face while she read. She has a scholar's face when she works — everything happening behind the eyes, the features themselves holding still like a pond holding a sky. But at one line her thumb whitened where it gripped the paper, and I knew which line it was without looking.

"The commissioners are listed," she said. Her voice was level. Too level, the way ice is level right up until it isn't. "Master Corvel, herald-at-law. Doctor Anselm Ruy, antiquary. And—" she stopped.

"And?"

"Provost Edmund Halloway." She looked up at me. "Of the Cartographers' Guild. He's my mentor. Or he was." She handed me the letter as if it had grown heavier in her hands. "Ivo. I told you about the archive. The razored pages. The replacement folios with the seal I didn't recognize."

"You said."

"This is the seal." She tapped the commission's mark on the letter's head. "Or near enough. He wasn't just tidying up after Aldous's trial. He's been *curating* it. Twenty years. And now they're sending him here to certify the erasure he's been maintaining."

I confess something, since this account will likely be read by no one who loved me: in that moment, my first feeling was not fear. It was satisfaction, ugly and bright, the kind you're ashamed of a heartbeat later. Because I had spent years looking for the hand that had cut those pages, imagining it as something large and distant, a system rather than a man. And now the system had done me the favor of putting on a face — a specific face, attached to a specific name, arriving at a specific date, within walking distance of my knife.

That is not a thought a good man has. I record it anyway, because the truth of these years is that none of us were entirely good men, and a record that flatters its keepers is worth exactly what the Duke's atlas is worth.

* * *

Aldous took the news better than any of us, which frightened me more than the letter had.

He was in the root cellar, as he usually was in the evenings, working by a single tallow candle with his face close enough to the vellum to scorch his beard. His eyes were nearly gone now; he drew by touch and memory and by having Paloma check his lines, and there was something terrible and holy in watching him work — a cartographer mapping by feel, like a blind man crossing a room he built himself.

When Paloma read the names to him, he set down his stylus with great care, exactly in the groove he'd worn into the table over twenty years, and was quiet for the length of ten breaths. I counted. It's a habit from bad years.

"Halloway," he said at last. "Little Edmund Halloway. He'd be provost by now. I told them he had the makings." He almost smiled. "He helped stage my death, you know. Did Paloma ever tell you that? A young guild officer, weeping through the whole arrangement, insisting the records at least be *ordered*, even if they had to be false. Order was his religion. I used to think it would save him. Now I think it's what they'll steer him by."

"They'll steer him straight to us," I said.

"No." Aldous shook his head slowly. "Halloway doesn't find things, boy. He buries them. If he's coming, it means someone above him suspects the ground isn't as flat as he's reported all these years, and he's coming to flatten it personally." He turned his ruined eyes toward me, or toward the place where I had been standing a moment before. "Which means we have a fortnight to become either invisible or undeniable. Invisible won't hold. So."

"So," I agreed, because it seemed to be my turn to say it.

* * *

We told the orchard the next morning, assembled under the oldest tree with the light coming down through the leaves in coins. Sera Voss stood beside me while I read the letter aloud, because she had decided years ago — I never learned exactly when — that I had a trustworthy face, a judgment I have spent every day since trying to deserve and mostly failing at in interesting ways.

I got through the part about the survey and the certification, and then I read the list of names, and a sound went through the forty of them like wind through wheat. Not loud. Just movement. Fear moving through a body of people who had practiced being still for two decades.

An old orchard-keeper named Brann — he had carried three saplings out of the burning town on his back the night of the massacre, and had the shoulders still, wide as a doorframe — spoke first. "Then we go deeper. Higher pastures. We've wintered up there before."

"And leave the trees?" someone asked.

"The trees stay. Trees are why we're alive, but they've rooted. We haven't."

"We could scatter now," said a woman named Dell. "Tonight. Before they come. Split up the way we always swore we would."

"Scatter, and carry what?" Sera Voss said, and her voice wasn't raised at all, which meant everyone heard it perfectly. "Carry the memory in our heads? Heads fail, friends. Heads drown, heads burn, heads grow old and forget the names of streets. For twenty years we've been a village with no church and no book and no grave marker, and I have buried too many of you to pretend that's enough. The plates exist now. The proofs exist. For the first time since the fires, the truth exists *outside our skulls*. And you propose to run from the one thing that outlives us."

Silence, except for the leaves.

Sera looked at me. "Tell them what you told me. About your father."

I hadn't planned to tell them anything about my father. That is generally my position on my father. But forty faces were waiting, and Sera Voss had asked, and there are older women whose requests function as law.

"My father died at Merrow Fen," I said. "Not in the fighting. There wasn't any fighting, as you all know better than I do. He died because the army had a map that showed his town as empty marshland, and maps are how soldiers decide what exists. I spent eight years finding this place. Eight years following rumors and forgeries and dead men's handwriting, and I'll tell you honestly — half that time I wanted revenge, and I'm not going to insult you by pretending otherwise. But somewhere on the salt road I stopped wanting the Duke's men dead and started wanting them *informed*. Corrected. Proven wrong on paper they can't burn fast enough." I looked down at the letter in my hand, with its fat confident seal. "They're coming here to write us out of existence officially. Permanently. With instruments and witnesses and a record. Well. Two can keep records."

Nobody cheered. Good communities don't cheer before danger; they do arithmetic. But I watched Brann's shoulders settle, and Dell stop looking at the high passes, and Tam — young Tam, whose trouble was still two storms away and known to none of us — nod slowly to himself like a man signing something.

* * *

That evening Paloma and I spread the true plates across the long table and began the grim mathematics of evacuation versus publication. She worked with the speed of a woman who has been rehearsing this conversation her whole life without knowing it.

"If they search properly," she said, tapping the cellar plan, "they find the press. It takes four men a day to break down and move, longer to hide. The plates themselves are portable, but portable means *findable*. We need a route out that doesn't touch any road they'll survey, and we need it chosen and provisioned before they arrive, not improvised after."

"The salt road," I said. "The flooded section. Your master mapped it from rumor. I've walked it twice."

"Twice." She looked at me over the lamplight. "In what season?"

"Flood season."

"Then you know where the culverts are."

"I know where three culverts *were*. Floods renegotiate."

She wrote *salt road* in the margin of her working copy and underlined it twice, and something in the economy of that gesture — no argument, no hesitation, trust extended to me on the strength of two wet walks — made me understand that whatever suspicion I still carried toward her had quietly died somewhere on the road north, and I hadn't marked the grave.

"Ivo," she said, after a while. "Halloway. When he comes."

"What about him?"

"He taught me precision. He taught me that a map is a moral document, that every omission is a decision. He said that to me when I was nineteen and I built my whole career on it." Her stylus turned over and over in her fingers. "And then I find razored pages and his seal on replacement folios. Either he's a liar, or—"

"Or he's a man who chose order over truth and has spent twenty years calling it protection," I said. "I've met that man. I've *been* that man, some winters."

"He'll recognize me. Guild escort credentials, my face, my hand — he certified my journeyman work himself. I could walk straight through his inspection."

"Or he could walk straight through yours," I said. "You're thinking of going out to meet them."

"I'm thinking," Paloma said carefully, "that nobody in this valley knows the official language of surveys and title law except me. And if the commission is going to be steered — misdirected, fed the ruins we choose, shown the emptiness we arrange — then it needs a guide fluent in their grammar." She set the stylus down. "It's dangerous."

"It's insane," I said. "You'd be volunteering to stand in front of the man who erased your master and narrate the crime scene in his own vocabulary."

"Yes," she said, in the mild tone of a woman confirming the weather. And that was the moment, though I wouldn't fully admit it for another week, that I stopped thinking of Paloma Whitlock as the guild-trained girl Aldous had wisely kept ignorant, and started thinking of her as the most dangerous person in the valley, myself included.

* * *

The fortnight began dying at the rate of one night per night, which is how fortnights die, but faster than any of us liked. On the fourth evening, Tam came running up from the watch post below the drowned valley, and I knew before he reached us that the arithmetic had changed.

"Riders," he panted. "On the south ridge road. Flying guild colors and the commission's banner both. They're not at the fen — they're a day out. Maybe less. The main party's behind them, but the *banner's* ahead."

Paloma was beside me, and I felt her go very still.

"A day," she said. "The letter said a fortnight."

"The letter," I said, "was written for people who had a fortnight."

Below us the orchard glowed in the last light, forty roofs' worth of careful invisibility, apple boughs heavy with a fruit that had walked out of a fire twenty years ago and refused ever since to stop growing. Somewhere on the ridge road, lanterns were swinging closer, carrying among them a thin, precise, guilt-ridden man with a razor's patience and a seal I now knew by heart.

The clock had not merely started ticking. It had skipped most of its hours while our backs were turned.

"Get Aldous," Paloma said quietly. "And wake Sera. Everyone to the long house." She was already rolling the plates, her hands steady, her voice almost gentle, which from Paloma was the surest sign of fury I ever learned. "If they're early, then so are we."
