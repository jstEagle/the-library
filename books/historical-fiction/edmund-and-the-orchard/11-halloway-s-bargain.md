## 11. Halloway's Bargain

The flag of scholarship, it turns out, is a very specific thing: white linen, a border of guild blue, and an embroidered compass rose that cost somebody real money. It was flying from the lead mule of the commission's advance party before I ever heard hoofbeats on the valley road, which tells you something about the order of operations in this duchy. First the banner. Then the mules. The soldiers came after, riding unbannered and unremarkable through the mist like a bad thought you're trying not to finish.

I saw the banner from the upper terrace, where I had been pretending for two hours to sketch soil samples. Pretending is a skill the guild taught me well; I only wish it appeared on my certificates.

"He's early," Ivo said beside me. He'd gone very still, the way he did when fear arrived and he hadn't decided yet whether to entertain it.

"Who is?"

"The one walking in front. Not the captain — the old one in the plain coat. He asked the villagers' names at the ford. He thanked a woman for directions he didn't need." Ivo's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Soldiers don't thank people."

I knew who it was before I could see his face. There is a particular gait to a man who has spent forty years in archives — shoulders folded inward around the shape of folios, head tipped as though perpetually reading a shelf label just past your left ear. Provost Halloway walked into our valley the way he walked into everything: apologetically, and with terrible consequences pending.

* * *

He came up under a flag of truce and a basket of oranges, which was such a Halloway gesture that I nearly laughed. Oranges, in autumn, in a border valley — they must have cost him a month's stipend, or been requisitioned, which amounts to the same confession. He set them on Sera Voss's table in the long house like a man laying down exhibit A.

"Paloma." He said my name the way he used to say *quod erat demonstrandum* — with satisfaction, as if I were the conclusion of an argument he'd begun years ago. "You've been well, I hope. You look thin. Scholarship doesn't feed you the way it should."

"The guild always said hunger sharpened the hand."

"The guild says many things." He looked around the long house with frank curiosity — the drying apples strung from beams, the children's slates leaning by the door, the loom with its half-woven cloth of undyed wool. His eyes catalogued everything and, I noticed, lingered longest on what wasn't there. No ledgers. No charts. No vellum anywhere. Twenty years of quiet had made these people careful, and Aldous's schooling had made me careful enough to help them put away anything papered before the banner came up the road.

Sera Voss poured him cider because hospitality is a habit older than any war, and he drank it and complimented it and set down the cup, and then we performed the little dance of *how may I be of service* until even the children had drifted out to the orchard to give us room.

"I'll come to it, then," Halloway said at last. "I'd like to walk with you. Alone. The captain has his inspections; I have mine. Mine are botanical." He smiled his mild smile. "I have always wanted to see an orchard grown from cuttings no record admits exist."

That was the moment I understood he knew. Not suspected — knew. Knew the way a man knows a watermark: hold the page up to the light and the truth is simply there, patient, waiting for someone to bother looking.

We walked up through the terraces. The morning smelled of wet grass and bruised fruit. Somewhere below us Tam was singing, badly, a song about a miller's daughter, and Halloway paused to listen to it as if it were testimony.

"You razored the pages," I said. We were alone among the trees, out of earshot of the sentries he himself had posted back at the road. There seemed no point in circling. "Chapter four of the trial proceedings. Merrow Fen. I found the stubs."

"Yes," he said, without a flicker. "I did. With a bone-handled knife I still own. You have no idea how long I stood in that archive deciding. Two hours. The lamp burned down twice." He touched the bark of one of the apple trees, gently, the way you'd touch a memorial. "And now here you are, Paloma. Standing in the margin of everything we cut out."

"We."

A rueful breath. "Yes. We. That's partly why I've come early, ahead of the captain, ahead of the commission proper. Because there is a version of the next fortnight in which nobody else has to know what you already know, and I confess I have come to buy that version if I can."

"Bargains usually name their price before they praise the scenery."

He stopped by the wall at the top terrace, where the stones went silver with lichen, and looked out over the drowned valley — the drowned town beneath it, invisible, doing what this country does best, which is holding things down. When he turned to me his face had shed its provost's polish entirely, and underneath was something older and tireder and, horribly, kind.

"Here is my offer, and I make it once. Come back with me. Your standing restored — full restoration, your name entered in the rolls again, the Whitlock apprenticeship recognized retroactively, which matters more than you perhaps realize, since as it stands you legally do not exist as a cartographer. A chair eventually, when I retire, and I am old enough that 'eventually' is not the empty word it once was. And the atlas." He held up a hand before I could speak. "Not seized. Corrected. Under your supervision. Your annotations preserved, your coastline kept — it is beautiful work, Paloma, I've seen the proofs, the whole commission admires it even as they amend it. You would be the hand that makes the lie legible. That is not nothing. That is more than any living cartographer has been given."

"And in exchange."

"The plates stay where they are. Buried, burnt, sunk in the fen — I don't ask and you don't tell me. The past stays buried with them." He said it softly, the way you'd offer someone a blanket. "Protection requires silence. It always has. That is the entire discovery of my life, Paloma, and I give it to you free, ahead of the bargain, because I think you deserve to hear it from someone who paid for it."

I should say now, in whatever honesty this account allows, that for one whole minute I wanted it. I want that written plainly, because later chapters will make me sound nobler than I felt on that lichen-silver wall with the wind coming up cold off the water. I wanted my name back. I wanted the workshop smell of oak gall and hot wax, the specific squeak of good vellum under a fresh-nibbed pen, the company of people who believed — who got to believe — that precision was a virtue rather than a weapon waiting to be issued. I had grieved for twenty years inside those rules and the rules had held me up when grief would otherwise have folded me flat. A person does not easily spit out the scaffold she's been standing on.

Halloway watched me want it. To his credit, he didn't push. He picked an apple off the low branch beside him, turned it in his scholar's hands, and waited.

"Tell me about the execution," I said finally. "Tell me the part that isn't in the archive. The part that made you keep a bone-handled knife sharp for twenty years."

He set the apple down on the wall untasted.

"There were four of us who knew," he said. "The officer commanding at Merrow Fen, the undersecretary who drafted the order, myself, and Aldous. The officer is dead — dysentery, unpoetically. The undersecretary is dead — worse, but he made choices. That leaves me and a man whose death certificate bears my seal." He turned the signet ring on his little finger, an old habit I remembered from lectures, the gold catching light like something trying to signal. "I didn't stage it out of courage, whatever you've reconstructed in your head. I staged it out of guilt, which is a cheaper metal and bends easier. I had certified the maps that aimed the guns. Do you understand? My surveys. My contour lines, my gridded squares, my beautiful precise hand telling artillerymen exactly where the church stood and where the houses clustered thickest. They didn't burn a town, Paloma. They burned a map executed in flesh, and I drew it."

Below us, faintly, Tam was still singing about the miller's daughter, and I hated him a little for it, and loved him, and hated myself for both.

"Aldous came to me three nights before the execution was scheduled," Halloway went on. "He didn't threaten me. That was the unbearable part — he laid my own survey plates on my desk and asked me to check the figures. As one professional to another. As if I might find an error. As if there were an error to find." His voice did something small and broke slightly and repaired itself. "There was no error. So when he proposed the swap — the prisoner already condemned in his name, the physician I trusted, the grave with two men in it instead of one — I signed it. I falsified the certificate of execution with the same hand that had falsified the town into emptiness. Symmetry. I told myself it was symmetry."

"And the razored pages?"

"Were me protecting him. Or protecting myself. By year ten I honestly couldn't have told you which, and I stopped asking." He looked at me then, directly, and I saw the whole architecture of the man laid open: every archive corridor, every locked folio, twenty years of careful scholarship built over a cellar he never once entered. "I have protected this secret, Paloma. Through two commissions and a coronation and the deaths of everyone who could contradict me. I have protected it the way you protect anything — by making sure no light ever gets near it. And it has worked. Nobody has hanged in twenty years. Nobody has burned. Children have grown up. That is not nothing either, whatever your master's conscience tells him at night."

"But now the commission comes to certify the land empty," I said. "To canonize it. To carve the lie into the permanent record with your seal on the preamble. And your protection becomes, for the first time, an act rather than an absence. You'd have to author it."

Something moved behind his eyes — the flinch of a man hit in a place he'd shown to no one. "You argue like him."

"I was taught by a dead man. It narrows the curriculum."

For a moment he almost laughed, and I saw how much easier the last twenty years would have been for him if he'd had anyone to laugh with about it, and how completely he had arranged his life so that he never would.

"Think of the arithmetic," he said quietly. "Forty-odd lives up here, hidden and whole. One book. If the plates surface, the Duke's title wobbles, the crown panics, soldiers come, and the arithmetic ends in ash — I have read enough history to draw you the curve, Paloma, I could do it in ink before supper. If the plates stay buried, those children grow old picking apples. You get your life back. I get to die having protected something instead of having destroyed it." He spread his hands, palms up, the honest merchant's gesture, and God help me it was persuasive; it was designed to be; he had probably rehearsed it against the dark of his archive for a decade. "Silence isn't cowardice. Sometimes it's the only roof that holds."

"Whose silence?" I asked. "That's the flaw in your roof, Provost. It's load-bearing, and the people holding it up never agreed to stand there. Sera Voss has carried it twenty years. Tam carries letters he never should have had to hide. Aldous carried it so long his eyes gave out under the weight. You're offering me a place under a roof other people are holding up with their teeth."

"They agreed—"

"They survived it. Those aren't the same word, and you of all people know the difference between a source and a witness."

He was silent a long moment. Wind moved through the orchard and a few early-fallen apples rolled somewhere down the slope with soft knocking sounds, like a distant, polite applause, or knuckles on a far-off door.

"I came with oranges," he said at last, absurdly, looking at his own hands. "I told myself if the conversation went ill I would at least have brought oranges." He straightened, and the provost reassembled over the man piece by piece, collar first, then voice. "You will not answer me now. I wouldn't trust anyone who answered quickly. But Paloma — the captain arrives within the week, and the commission behind him, and they are not me. I can slow a search. I can misfile a report. I cannot hold back what happens when a soldier finds a cellar, and cellars, in my experience, are found." He took up his walking stick. "Bring me the plates before they do. Restore yourself. Let the dead rest — including," and here his voice went gentle in a way that was crueller than any threat, "including the one you've been grieving for twenty years, who is not the man you remember, and does not want to be found, and has had twenty years to send for you and never did. Ask yourself why he never sent, if your presence in his design mattered half what you believe."

He walked down through the terraces without hurry, pausing once to examine a tree's bark with genuine scholarly interest, and I stood at the wall wanting to throw an apple at his back and knowing he'd only catalogue the trajectory.

Because that was the barb, wasn't it. Not the chair, not the restoration, not even the arithmetic of forty lives. The barb was *he never sent for you*. Twenty years of loving a ghost through the discipline of pen-work, twenty years of finishing his coastline stroke by stroke because it was the only way left to sit in a room with him — and Halloway had walked up my orchard with a basket of oranges and put his thumb precisely on that wound, because guilt had made him fluent in other people's griefs, and fluency is a weapon too.

I stayed at the wall until his banner went back down the valley road.

Then I went to find Ivo, because there was a week now instead of a fortnight, and because Halloway had told me true in at least one thing: cellars get found.

And because I had realized, standing there with the wind coming off the drowned valley, that I already had my answer to the provost's offer — I'd simply need to build it out of something heavier than silence.

Down in the yard, Ivo looked up from harnessing the mule, read my face in a single glance, and said, "How much time?"

"A week," I said. "Maybe less. He bought his conscience an orange's worth of delay."

Ivo nodded slowly, and reached for the lanterns.
