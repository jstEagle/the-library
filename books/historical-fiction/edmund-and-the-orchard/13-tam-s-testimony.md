## 13. Tam's Testimony

The morning after the storm, the orchard looked like a battlefield that had lost interest halfway through. Half of the upper terrace was down — trunks twisted, roots torn from the soil in pale fists, apples everywhere, rolling in the mud like the world's most patient artillery. We spent the whole day hauling what could be saved into baskets and what couldn't into piles for the press, and nobody said a word about the mark on the cellar door, because forty people can agree to be silent faster than three people can agree on anything else.

I know silence. I grew up inside it. I am, if you want the truth of it, its most loyal product.

My name is Tam Wrenn. I am twenty-three years old, I keep the west terraces and the bees, and for six years I have been committing a crime so small that even I had learned to stop seeing it.

That night, when Sera Voss finally sent everyone to their beds with a look that promised the matter was not finished, only postponed, I did not go to my bed. I went to find Paloma Whitlock, because she was the only person in the orchard who hadn't known me long enough to forgive me, and I found I couldn't bear it any longer.

She was in the drying shed, working by a lamp with a cracked chimney, surrounded by paper. She looked up when I came in, and I watched her do the thing I'd seen her do since she arrived: measure me. Cartographers do it to everything. You can see them deciding where your edges are.

"I need to tell you something," I said, "and then I need you to decide whether I'm allowed to still live here."

"That's not how we do things," she said. "But go on."

So I went on. And because I have never been able to say a difficult thing without circling it first like a dog before lying down, I circled.

* * *

"You should understand something about the beginning," I said. "Of the orchard. Nobody talks about the beginning here. They talk about Merrow Fen constantly — the streets, the eel boats, whose grandmother made the best plum wine — but never about the last night. It's like a house where one room is kept locked and everyone walks past it whistling."

"Except you," Paloma said.

"Except me," I agreed. "Because I wasn't born yet. The locked room is just a room to me. So when I was seventeen, I did the unthinkable thing. I asked about it out loud."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain had softened to that steady businesslike drizzle that follows a storm around like an apology.

"Sera sat me down at her table and told me the parts she thought I needed. The fog Master Aldous invented on his own charts. The families walking out through marshland the Duke's soldiers believed was impassable, because their mapmaker had told them so. How he let himself be taken afterward so the pursuit would end with him." I shrugged. "It's a good story. It's the story this whole place stands on. But there's a hole in it, and I fell into the hole, and I've been down there six years."

"The hole being?"

"My brother," I said. "Corvan."

I watched her face for the flicker. She'd heard the name; everyone here had heard it by now, whispered through the terraces all day like weather coming. Tam's brother serves the Duke. Tam's brother wears the red coat. Tam's brother —

"Corvan stayed," I said. "When the others went out through the fog. He was fourteen and he panicked, or he didn't believe the fog story, or — I don't actually know, because I wasn't there either, I was a year from being born. What we know is he survived the burning. A soldier's widow took him in, and then the army took him, the way the army takes boys without anyone else. And he rose. He's a corporal now. He has a ribbon. He writes a very tidy hand."

Paloma had gone very still, in the way I was learning meant her mind was already drawing.

"When I was seventeen," I said, "it occurred to me — and I swear to you, it occurred to me as the most innocent idea I have ever had — that somewhere in the world there existed exactly one other person who shared my blood and my dead town, and that he was reachable by post. That's all. Not intelligence. Not betrayal. News. I wanted to know if he remembered the smell of the cider press. I wanted to know if he was all right. Sera would have told me no. Everyone would have told me no. So I didn't ask."

"How many letters?" Paloma asked quietly.

"Eleven. Over six years. I never wrote from here. I walked four miles off the mountain and posted them from the market town under another name, and I gave the orchard as nowhere, and I said I was a trader who'd once passed through Merrow Fen before the war and had heard an old man there had a son in the service. Every letter was a fiction wrapped around one true question: *are you well.* And every answer came back the same way — careful, cold, correct, a soldier writing back to a stranger. Until the last one." My hands had gone cold around nothing. "Until the last one, which said: *Stop writing to me. Your letters are read. They have been read since the second one. Whoever you are, whoever they are, they now know someone out here remembers Fen well enough to grieve it.*"

The shed was very quiet. Somewhere outside a branch shifted, settling into its new fallen geometry.

"He was warning me," I said. "My brother, in the Duke's uniform, used the only channel he had to warn whoever I might be connected to. And I got the letter, and I understood two things at once. One: I had been a courier for the enemy without knowing it, carrying proof of our existence straight into the offices of the men who erased us. Two: I could never tell anyone here, because the truth would do exactly what it is doing right now, which is turn every kind face on this mountain away from me."

"But you marked the cellar," Paloma said. It wasn't an accusation. Her voice had that terrible cartographic gentleness, the tone of someone confirming coordinates.

"No," I said. "But I know who did."

* * *

Here is what I told her, and later told the whole orchard gathered under the great roof of the press house with rain drumming above us like applause from an audience we couldn't see.

The surveyor's annotation on the cellar lintel — grid references, precise, professional, rubbed out too late — was in a hand I knew. Because for six years I had studied the hand on eleven envelopes, the tidy soldier's script of the man who warned me. Corvan's hand. My brother had come to the valley ahead of the commission. Not with the commission — before it, alone or nearly alone, in the storm. He had measured the cellar entrance the way he'd been trained to measure things, and then he had stood in the rain and tried to erase himself, and failed, and left.

"Why?" Paloma had asked me in the shed, and I could only give her the answer I trusted, which was the same instinct that had written eleven foolish letters: "Because he's been reading my letters for six years and composing his replies like a man passing notes under a guard's nose. Because whatever's left of the boy from the fen is buried deep in that red coat, but it isn't dead. He measured us so the commission wouldn't have to guess. And then he rubbed it out so that — maybe — they'd arrive and find nothing worth measuring twice."

Or he had marked us properly and repented in the rain. Or the rubbing-out itself was theater. I said all of this to the press house myself, standing up, because I refused to let forty frightened people build theories in the dark when I could hand them the worst version of the truth in daylight.

Sera Voss sat at the head table with her hands folded, and she let me speak, which was its own kind of verdict deferred.

"So there it is," I finished. "Six years of letters. Eleven small betrayals I dressed up as love. If the commission finds this place, some portion of the road leads back through me. I'm sorry doesn't cover it. I know sorry doesn't cover it, because I've been drafting a better word for six years and never found it. What I can offer is this: my brother reads his post carefully, and he answers carefully, and he is attached to the commission's escort. Let me write him a twelfth letter. Let me tell him a lie — a good one, a detailed one — about what we are and where, and let me be the one who carries false word while the rest of you carry true word somewhere else. I owe the orchard a debt of misdirection. It turns out misdirection is the family trade."

Nobody spoke. Rain, rain, rain.

Old Marta, who had carried two children out of the fog on the night of the burning and one of whom had not lived to see morning, stood up. I braced.

"You were a boy asking after his brother," she said. "That's not treachery. Treachery requires you to know what you're selling. You didn't." She looked around the room. "But hear me, all of you, because I'm old and entitled: if fear starts deciding who belongs here, then the soldiers don't need to burn us again. We'll have done it ourselves, from the inside, room by room. We will not become the people who burned us. Not tonight, not over this boy."

And Sera Voss, who had not looked at me once during the whole confession, finally turned her head, and said: "The vote isn't whether Tam stays. Tam stays. The vote is the one we've been avoiding for a week, and this boy just made it unavoidable. Plates or fire, friends. Publish or perish quietly. Decide."

* * *

They decided. Badly split, as I'd been told such votes always run in this place — the young and the angry saying burn everything, hide forever; the old and the tired saying the hiding was never meant to be permanent; and in the middle, slowly gathering weight like water behind a weir, the argument that Paloma Whitlock had apparently been making for days: that a truth which exists only in one cellar is a truth with a single point of failure, and that point of failure is currently annotated in grid references.

What tipped it — what actually moved the count — was me, which I intend to enjoy for approximately the rest of my life.

"If it goes out," Sera said, when the room had settled toward Paloma's position but stalled on the practical horror of *how*, "the commission sits between us and every road south. Forty people cannot outrun a column of soldiers carrying a crate of vellum."

"They don't have to," I said. "The commission isn't looking for a crate of vellum. They're looking for a *story*. They've been sent to certify that Merrow Fen never existed, or existed wrongly, or existed in a manner convenient to the Duke. Give them a story to seize. Give them a book."

Paloma turned toward me so fast her lamp swung shadows across the whole press house.

"There are plates in that cellar," I said, "that Master Aldous wants burned. Beautiful work, I'm told, and wrong — wrong on purpose, or wrong by age, I don't care. Don't burn them. Copy them, alter them, make them *convincing*. Make an atlas that looks like exactly what a guilty village would die protecting. Let them take it. Let Halloway open it in front of his commissioners and find a treasure — and then let him find, slowly, over weeks, that it's poisoned. Wrong rivers. Wrong charters. Wrong enough that if the Duke's historians ever canonize it, they've canonized a lie *we* wrote, and the moment anyone checks it against reality, the whole official edifice cracks." I spread my hands. "I've spent six years learning how far a careful lie can travel in an envelope. Trust me: it goes much farther than the truth. The truth has to stop and prove itself at every gate."

Silence again, but a different species of it. Paloma was looking at me the way I imagine she looks at a coastline that has just resolved into something mappable.

"That's monstrous," said old Marta, with obvious delight.

"It's penance," I said. "You wanted me to pay a debt. Pay it in the enemy's currency."

Sera Voss struck the table once with the flat of her hand, and the vote was called again, and this time it did not split badly. This time it barely split at all.

Afterward, in the mud outside the press house, Paloma caught my sleeve.

"The letter to your brother," she said. "If you write it, I'm writing it with you. Every word. And Tam—" She hesitated, and for a moment she looked less like a guild cartographer and more like someone's apprentice, long ago, missing somebody. "Your brother warned you. In his own hand, at real risk. Whatever we forge, whatever we feed them — we should remember that the enemy, tonight, in the rain, tried to rub us out."

"I've remembered it every day for six years," I said. "It's the only reason I'm still standing here."

She nodded and went back inside, and I stayed out in the drizzle a while longer, looking up the mountain at the broken terrace, thinking about letters. About how many words it takes to build a lie solid enough to carry a person's weight, and whether my brother would read mine and know it for forgery — or know it for the message it really was, the one I'd been trying to send him since I was seventeen:

*Someone out here remembers Fen well enough to fight for it.*

In the morning, I would need ink, good paper, and Paloma's help composing the most beautiful lie of my life.

What I got instead, before first light, was Ivo Reyes shaking me awake with a lantern in his fist and a look on his face like a man who has just seen the clock start ticking.

"Riders on the valley road," he said. "Three of them. One carries a white flag and a guild satchel."
