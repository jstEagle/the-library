## 14. Copying the Truth

The root cellar smelled of earth and apples going soft in the dark, and by the third night it also smelled of hot tallow and my own stubbornness. We had moved the work below ground because light above ground was a confession. Somewhere past the terraces, a Royal Historical Commission was sharpening its quills, and there is no instrument on this earth more dangerous than a quill held by a man paid by the word.

Aldous Whitlock sat across the worktable from me, half-blind, his face lit from beneath like something raised out of a well, dictating corrections to surveys he had drawn forty years ago and could no longer draw again. His eyes had gone the color of watered milk. His hands, though — his hands were still the steadiest things I had ever known, resting flat on the table like two animals asleep.

"Third meridian," he said. "You've flattered it."

"I've *corrected* it."

"You've flattered it. I did the same at your age. The line wants to curve west, half a degree, where the fen ground gives way. It doesn't want to, mind — it does. Maps are full of lines that behave as they ought instead of as they must." He tapped the table once. "Take out the flattery."

I took out the flattery. He was right; he was almost always right about the land, which was precisely why they had hanged him, or hadn't hanged him, depending on which version of history you had been issued. Twenty years of my life I had spent grieving a man who turned out to be alive, old, and sitting close enough that I could smell the burnt linseed oil still living in his skin after all these years. Grief, it turns out, is a poor cartographer. It had mapped him entirely wrong.

* * *

We worked the way an old marriage works, or so I imagine: mostly in silence, punctuated by corrections neither of us enjoyed but both of us trusted. The true plates of the Merrow Fen atlas lay between us in their oilcloth wrapping, forty-one sheets of vellum bearing surveys that proved, in the patient grammar of measurement, that the Duke's charter claimed empty land that had never been empty. A town of three hundred souls. A church with a bell. Boundary stones cut with the Voss mark. All of it surveyed by Aldous before the war, when he was the army's chief cartographer and still believed that precision was a form of honesty rather than a weapon waiting for a buyer.

My task was to copy them. Not once — twice. One set to go out into the world, one set to hide, because Aldous had survived twenty years by never trusting any single basket with all his eggs, and he had passed the habit on to me along with the letterforms.

"The ink's mixing too thin," he said, without looking up. I hadn't shown him the inkpot.

"How do you—"

"Your brush strokes are drying pale at the tails. You always thinned your ink too much when you were tired, and you're tired now, girl. Sleep."

"It's barely dusk."

"Dusk is a suggestion down here." He leaned back, and the lamplight caught the ruin of his left eye, the one that saw only weather and shadow now. "I kept you up late enough as it was, twenty years ago. I owe Halloway's God a debt of rest on your behalf."

I should explain what the work actually was, because it was stranger than copying. Aldous's failing eyes could no longer hold a rule line steady, but his memory had become, if anything, ferocious with disuse — the way a man locked in a room memorizes its cracks. So I drew, and he corrected, and between us we produced something neither could have made alone: my hand, his certainty. There were moments — I will be honest, since I have decided to be honest about everything now, at whatever cost — there were moments when his hand came over mine on the vellum to show me how a boundary stone's hatching should fall away from the light, and I felt twenty years collapse like wet paper. His fingers over mine. The same correction, the same grip, the same dry voice saying *lighter here* — I was eleven years old again in the guild workshop, being taught that a map is a promise made to strangers.

Except now I knew what the promise had cost him.

"You're crying onto the plate," Aldous observed mildly.

"I am not."

"Saltwater distorts vellum. Ask any coastal surveyor." He passed me his sleeve-cloth without ceremony, the way you'd pass salt. That was the whole of his comfort, then and ever: practical, precise, and somehow, against all arithmetic, enough.

* * *

Somewhere in the second watch of that night — cellar nights have no honest hours, only the lamp's slow consumption of itself — he began to talk, which he almost never did. I have learned since that men like Aldous ration their words the way misers ration coin, and when they finally spend, they spend everything at once.

"Your first lesson," he said. "Do you remember it?"

"You made me draw the same harbor nine times."

"Eleven. You were worse than you remember. And on the eleventh, do you recall what I said?"

I did not, and told him so.

"I said: *now draw what isn't there.*" His good eye found me across the lamplight. "Tidal flats at low water. The sandbar that drowns in spring. Every harbor master knows the chart lies by omission, and every drowning is an omission made flesh. That was the day I decided you'd understand Merrow Fen someday, if you lived long enough and I didn't have to tell you."

"So the whole apprenticeship—" I set down the pen. My hands had gone cold, and not from the cellar damp.

"The salt routes I taught you from rumor instead of survey. The cipher alphabet disguised as ornamental capitals. The lesson about drawing fog onto a chart where no fog existed." He counted them off on scarred fingers, unhurried. "I wasn't teaching you geography, Paloma. I was burying a message in the only vault I knew would never be searched. Your memory. They can raze archives. They razed mine, all of it — Halloway showed me the gaps himself, in the end, almost apologetically. But nobody has ever invented a method for searching what a person remembers without asking her."

"And you couldn't just ask me."

"I couldn't just ask you," he agreed. "Asking makes you guilty of knowing. Ignorance was the only shield I could give you, and I gave it badly, and it cost you—" he gestured, a small motion that took in the cellar, the orchard overhead, the whole crooked shape of my life, "—cost you grief you'd earned no right to. I know what my death did to you. Sera wrote me about the funeral. About the girl who wouldn't leave the guild steps."

"She said I was making a scene."

"She said you were the only person in that crowd who understood what had been lost." He picked his pen back up, which for Aldous was the equivalent of another man wiping his eyes. "I chose it, you understand. Every day for twenty years, I chose it. Don't forgive me too quickly. Quick forgiveness lets the forgiver off easy."

"I wasn't planning to forgive you quickly."

"Good," he said, and there was, unmistakably, warmth under the dryness — the sun under a slate sky. "Then we understand each other. Pass the ink. And thin it properly this time."

* * *

Near dawn — or what passes for dawn in a root cellar, which is to say the lamp running low and my spine filing formal complaints — I began the second project, the one I had not announced. While Aldous slept in his chair with his chin on his chest, breathing in the shallow careful rhythm of a man who has spent decades practicing not being noticed, I took from my satchel my apprentice atlas.

Every guild cartographer keeps one. Mine was the book I had bound myself at fifteen: clumsy sewn signatures, a cover of cheap calfskin I had stained unevenly brown, filled with practice charts and exercises and one page where fourteen-year-old Paloma had drawn, in secret and with tremendous dignity, a portrait of Master Whitlock's profile that made him look like a disappointed hawk. It had survived the war, the purge, two moves of lodging, and my own periodic resolutions to burn it. Sentiment, I find, is best preserved in ugly bindings; nobody loots a book that embarrasses its owner.

Now I cut the boards. It felt like surgery on a living relative. Along the inner edges, where the covers met the spine, I pared channels with my knife — deep, clean, sized exactly to the folded sheets — and into those channels I laid the second complete set of plates, folded to the width of two fingers. Forty-one truths, hidden inside the exercises of a dead girl who had believed maps were honest. Then paste, then the facing paper laid back down and burnished until the seam vanished into the old staining. I rubbed ash and tallow into the join until even my own eyes lost it.

When Aldous woke, I had the book open innocently on my knee.

"What's that?"

"Homework," I said. "From a strict teacher."

He held out his hand, and because I had never once in my life refused him a manuscript, I gave it over. He turned the pages slowly, his thumb reading the paper the way other men read print, and when he reached the hawk portrait he went very still.

"You captured my disapproval admirably," he said at last. "I had hoped I was subtler."

"You weren't. Everyone said so."

A sound escaped him that in a lesser man would have been called a laugh. He closed the book and held it a moment longer than necessary, both hands flat on the cover, as if taking a bearing. Then he handed it back.

"You know what you've done, putting them in there."

"I've put the truth inside my own apprenticeship," I said. "Where it always was. You buried your message in my memory; I'll bury mine in yours. If they take the loose plates, they take the copies meant to be taken. This—" I touched the warped calfskin "—no commission seizes a dead girl's schoolbook."

"They seized plenty of dead girls' schoolbooks in the purges."

"Then I'll be the first to disappoint them."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched something move behind the milk-white eye and the working one both — pride, probably, or the closest thing his face permitted, which might have been the expression of a man checking his figures and finding them correct.

"Sera wanted to name you honorary kin last night," he said. "I told her you'd refuse the ceremony and accept the obligations."

"Sera knows me already?"

"Aldous talks in his sleep," said a voice from the stair, and there was Sera Voss with a jug of small beer, descending into our lamplight like a verdict. She was seventy if she was a day, straight as a boundary stone, with eyes that had watched a town burn and had opinions about it. "Talks, mutters, corrects measurements in his sleep. Twenty years I've heard more about an apprentice named Paloma than about my own grandchildren. 'She thins her ink,' he says, at three in the morning. 'She flatters coastlines.' We felt we knew you before we ever saw you, girl. It's a strange thing, missing someone you've never met."

"I'm given to understand," I said, "that you knew me by a different name once."

"Palla," she said, and the childhood syllable went through me like a needle finding an old scar. "He wrote to us — letters carried out by smugglers, months late, read aloud by firelight. *Palla has finished the northern routes. Palla draws better than I did at her age.* Do you know what that meant to people who had lost every child and grandchild? It meant the world outside the burning still had young hands in it. It meant somebody was carrying the craft forward." She set the jug down hard enough to slosh. "So drink your beer, both of you, and then sleep, because Tam came down from the lookout at moonset, and there's dust on the west road."

The words dropped into the cellar like a stone into a well. Dust on the west road. The commission was not due through the valley for days yet — we had counted on days.

"Soldiers?" Aldous asked, already reaching for his stick.

"Too few for soldiers," Sera said. "Two riders, flying no colors. But one of them, Tam says, rides like a scholar and carries his saddlebags like a man carrying books."

I looked at Aldous. Aldous looked at the apprentice atlas under my arm, and then at me, and his ruined face arranged itself into what he perhaps imagined was reassurance.

"Halloway travels with no escort," he said quietly, "when he comes to bargain."

* * *

They let him through the terraces at first light, alone, leading his horse, a white flag of scholarship flying in the shape of an open folio held up in one bony hand. Provost Halloway looked older than his years and better fed than his conscience, and when his eyes found me among the apple trees he stopped as if the ground had addressed him personally.

"Miss Whitlock," he said. "What a remarkable — and yet, on reflection, entirely predictable — surprise."

"Provost." I kept my voice at guild temperature: cool, correct, capable of citing regulations. Behind me, unseen among the rows, Aldous withdrew toward the cellar with the slow dignity of a man who had practiced vanishing for two decades. "You've come a long way from the archive."

"Archives travel with one, eventually." He smiled the smile of a man offering restoration of my standing, my rooms, my whole safe architecture of rules — and I stood in a burned town's surviving orchard, with forty-one truths bound into a schoolbook against my ribs, and prepared, against every instinct I had ever been taught, to listen.

I only wished I knew whether he had come to bargain — or to count us.
