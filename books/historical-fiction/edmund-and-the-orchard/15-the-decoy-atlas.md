## 15. The Decoy Atlas

There is an art to lying with a straight face, and it turns out the same art applies to lying with a straightedge.

I learned this in the third week of my apprenticeship to no one, when I was still a boy in a market town selling forged lead seals to pilgrims who wanted proof they'd touched a saint. The trick, I told my customers then, is not to make the thing perfect. Perfection invites inspection. You make it *nearly* perfect, and you let one flaw show — a flaw just plausible enough that a suspicious man finds it, feels clever for finding it, and stops looking. The flaw is the gift you give the examiner. It's how you tell him: *you win, go home.*

Tam Voss's nephew-by-marriage — actually his cousin, but Tam had stopped correcting anyone years ago — had been a guild-trained bookbinder before the Duke's men decided binders were suspiciously literate. He'd fled east with his tools wrapped in oilcloth, and those tools now sat on Sera Voss's kitchen table beside a stack of vellum Paloma had ruled herself, because apparently even a forgery deserved honest linework.

"Again," I said.

Tam pressed the copperplate down and pulled the sheet through the roller press Aldous had kept buried under sacking for twenty years like a corpse he couldn't decide whether to bury or feed. The impression came up pale and clean: a coastline, wrong by four miles, and a river that had never existed bending politely around land that had always belonged to the Duke.

"That's the third one," Tam said.

"I know. I'm checking for consistency. Forgeries fail from inconsistency more than from skill."

"You're enjoying this."

"I'm enjoying *not being hanged*," I said. "The enjoyment is incidental."

He almost smiled. Tam was seventeen and carried guilt the way other boys carried slingshots — always in reach, always weighing down one pocket. Three days ago he'd stood in front of the whole orchard and confessed that his letters to his brother had been read by the Duke's clerks, that the commission was coming to our valley partly because of him. Most people would have been sent away after that. Instead Sera Voss had put him to work. In Merrow Fen, apparently, penance looked like ink under your fingernails.

* * *

The forgery had a name, which I thought was going too far until Paloma insisted on it. She called it the *Halloway Atlas*, because — she said — if you build a trap, you should at least address the envelope.

Its beauty was the point. That was what I hadn't understood at first, back when my plan for the commission had been simpler: hide the real plates, deny everything, outlast them. But Paloma sat me down over the root-cellar lamp and explained it the way her master must have taught her, patiently, as if I were vellum to be stretched.

"The commission doesn't want truth," she said. "It wants confirmation. They arrive expecting to find evidence of the Duke's title, and if they find nothing they'll have to manufacture it, which takes time and paperwork and invites questions. But if we hand them something beautiful — a survey, dated, sealed, gorgeously executed — that places Merrow Fen exactly where their history needs it to be *not to have been*, then they don't search. They celebrate. They write their report standing in our ruins and thanking God for the efficiency of erasure."

"And the damning part?"

"If it's ever seized and examined by anyone honest," she said, "the flaws are deliberate and documented. Wrong rivers, wrong charter dates, a surveyor's mark belonging to a man who provably died eleven years before the 'survey.' Every error points back at the commission itself. They can't display it without displaying their own fraud."

"So it's beautiful, wrong, and poisoned."

"Like everything the Duke has ever built," Paloma said, and went back to ruling her lines.

* * *

We worked four days. I handled the aging — tea-staining the edges, cracking the spine with a warm knife, rubbing ash into the gutters so the book smelled of the grave it had never visited. Tam did the fine lettering, because it turned out the boy could write a capital H that would fool the Provost himself, which felt either fitting or blasphemous, depending on your view of Provost Halloway.

On the fourth evening Paloma came to check the work. She picked the book up, turned it in the lamplight, ran her thumb along the false coastline, and I watched her do the thing she does — the thing that frightens people who don't know her — which is to look at a lie with total professional tenderness.

"It's good," she said.

"It's gorgeous," I corrected. "Gorgeous is the point."

"It's better than the atlas they'll bring." She set it down. "Which should worry us more than it does."

When Tam drifted off to check the press, it was just the two of us and the lamp and the smell of old tea masquerading as old paper. I should have gone to sleep. Instead I heard myself say:

"When I started looking for Whitlock, I wasn't looking for a cartographer."

Paloma didn't answer. She had the habit of waiting out silences rather than filling them, which I'd decided early on was either wisdom or a weapon.

"My father died at Merrow Fen," I said. "Not in the massacre — he was already gone by then, sold off or fled, nobody in my family agrees. But his mother was in the town. My great-grandmother. She burned with it. And my father spent his whole life being angry about a place he'd never seen, and he passed the anger to me like an heirloom, and I carried it into every room I ever entered." I turned the decoy atlas ninety degrees, for no reason. "For six years I followed rumors of Aldous Whitlock. Everyone said he was dead; everyone said it too smoothly, the way people say things they've been paid to say. I wanted to find him alive so I could ask him why the army's own chief mapmaker led those families out and then let the world call him a hero for dying. I wanted—" I shrugged. "Revenge sounds cleaner than what it was. It was mostly wanting someone to hurt the way we'd been hurt."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a farmhouse helping the same man's apprentice forge a beautiful lie to save forty people who were supposed to be dead." I looked at her. "So it became something else. I still don't have a word for it. It's heavier than hope and less useful than a knife."

Paloma considered me across the table. "You could stop," she said. "After the plates are out. Take your share of copies and disappear south. No one would follow you. No one would even know to."

"That's the offer, is it? Disappear."

"It's not an offer. It's a question I'm asking myself out loud because I can't answer it." She laughed, short and dry, nothing you'd mistake for joy. "Aldous asked me once why I stayed in the guild workshop inking coastlines I knew were lies. I said it was steady work. What I meant was that the silence was warm and I didn't know how to live outside it. Now the record exists — or will exist — and I keep catching myself wondering what happens the day it's finished."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean," she said, and there was something raw under the wryness now, some grief finally speaking its native language, "what will you do when the record exists and no one may be allowed to read it? We can publish abroad. Fine. The Duchy bans it within the month. The free ports print it; the universities buy it and lock it in cases. Truth on paper is still just paper, Ivo. It doesn't walk. It doesn't testify. Eleven villagers can swear oaths in a foreign port and the men who burned their town will die honored, and the book will sit somewhere being right, uselessly, forever." She pressed her palm flat against the decoy atlas. "This thing will be seized and praised and locked in a ducal cabinet, and the true one will gather dust in exile, and twenty years from now some clerk will misfile both and the whole story will be two objects nobody opens."

The lamp guttered. Neither of us moved to fix it.

"Then we misfile louder," I said.

She looked up.

"Somebody reads books, Paloma. Somebody always does. A student, a smuggler, a widow with a grudge and a literacy. My father's anger survived thirty years on nothing but word of mouth and spite — imagine what it'll do with plates and sworn testimony attached. The record existing isn't the end of anything. It's the beginning of the long part." I tapped the forged cover. "And the long part is where I live. I've got nowhere else."

Something in her shoulders came down half an inch — the half inch by which Paloma Almeida admitted things.

"The long part," she repeated. "That's the most pessimistic hopeful sentence I've ever heard."

"I'm a complex man."

"You're a forger."

"A forger with ideals. There's no lower form of life."

She smiled then — briefly, crookedly, the first full smile I'd gotten from her, and I understood suddenly why Aldous Whitlock had written to an orchard full of strangers about a girl in a guild workshop. Some people you describe to other people because describing them is the only way to believe they're real.

* * *

We finished the decoy two nights later. Tam's last task was the dedication inscription inside the front board, copied faithfully in the style of ducal commissions: *Surveyed and Certified for His Grace, by Authority of the Crown.* Underneath, invisible unless you held the page to firelight, Paloma had me burn in a single line of Aldous's cipher — the same cipher Ivo Reyes had spent six years learning to read, spelled out for me by a half-blind old man who'd chuckled the whole time he dictated it.

It read: *Ask the river that isn't there.*

Any honest scholar who ever examined the book under proper light would find it. Whether any honest scholar ever would — that was the bet the whole orchard had placed, unknowing, when they voted to let the truth travel instead of burn.

We wrapped the decoy in waxed canvas and I stowed it in the bottom of the grain chest where the commission's captain would be *invited* to find it, because the best hiding place for a trap is a place you seem careless about. Tam labeled the chest with a fictitious farmer's name. His hands shook doing it, and I pretended not to see, because pretending not to see is also a craft, and I've had longer to practice it than he has.

That night the watch boy came down from the ridge terrace at a run.

Riders on the north road. Six of them. Flying no colors — which meant soldiers, because only soldiers traveled incognito and called it diplomacy.

They'd come early. Days early. Whatever stall Aldous had built for us was about to be tested before the mortar set, and somewhere behind those riders, I knew, walked a scholarly man in a scholar's coat carrying twenty years of guilt like a lantern he couldn't put down.

I woke Paloma with a hand on her shoulder and three words:

"They're here early."
