## 16. The Commission Comes

They came up the valley road in the manner of all official parties: too slowly, and with more banners than sense. I had watched from the upper terrace since dawn, an apple I had no intention of eating going soft in my hand, counting pennants the way other people count the beats between lightning and thunder.

Six soldiers on bony horses, a wagon with the Royal Historical Commission's arms painted fresh over what was plainly an older coat of arms — someone had gilded over the Duke's boar rather than repaint the shield, which struck me as the most honest piece of heraldry I'd seen in years — and riding at the front of it all, in a scholar's traveling cloak he had no business still fitting into, Provost Halloway.

Beside him, a captain. Young. Clean gloves. The kind of face that has never once been contradicted and resents the possibility.

"They're early," Ivo said at my shoulder. He said it the way other men say *the roof is on fire.*

"Scholars are always early," I said. "It's the one military virtue we have."

Below, Sera Voss was already moving among the trees with a basket on her arm, directing her people into the choreography we had rehearsed for six days: the old ones seated in doorways looking harmless and slightly deaf, the children sent to the far terraces where they could be heard but not easily counted, Tam stationed by the press house with his face arranged into peasant stupidity — which, I will note for the record, required no arrangement whatsoever. He is simply like that.

I put down the apple. I smoothed my guild sash. And I gave myself the advice I give apprentices before any examination: *you are not lying if you are only choosing what to show.*

Then I walked down to greet the men who had come to certify a massacre out of existence.

* * *

"Master Almeida." Halloway's voice carried the same warm dustiness it had carried across forty years of lecture halls. "The Guild of Cartographers sends its regards, though I confess I did not expect its escort to arrive ahead of us."

"The guild teaches thoroughness, Provost," I said, and bowed exactly deep enough. "I rode north the moment your letter cleared the archive."

Which was true, in the sense that all the best lies are true. There had been a letter. It had not cleared anything; Ivo had forged the seal while I dictated the phrasing, and between us we produced a document so dutifully dull that Halloway read it twice without suspicion. The guild's great weakness, I have found, is that it cannot imagine its own instruments being used against it.

"And this is Captain Brase," Halloway said. "He'll want to walk the ground. The commission's charge is to survey what remains of Merrow Fen and confirm the disposition of the land."

*Confirm the disposition.* Four words, and in them a whole village died again, tidily, in committee.

"Captain," I said, and offered him the map tube under my arm like a bouquet. "You've brought me an audience. I've brought you twenty years of erasure. Shall we begin?"

Brase looked at me the way young officers look at women who speak first — a flicker of recalculation, quickly suppressed. "You know this ground?"

"I mapped it," I said. Which was also true. Just not for them.

* * *

Here is what Master Aldous Whitlock taught me about ruins, in the years before I understood why he insisted: *a ruin is a text, Paloma, and whoever reads it aloud controls the story.* He made me practice on the old Roman kiln sites along the coast, narrating their decay to him until I could make a crumbling wall say whatever the listener needed it to say.

I had never imagined the exercise would keep people alive.

We started at the valley floor, where Merrow Fen had stood. There is almost nothing there now — the victors had been thorough even by the standards of thoroughness — and thoroughness, I have learned, is a gift to the performer, because absence can be interpreted freely.

"The town occupied roughly this bend," I said, gesturing with the easy sweep of a woman describing something she had never loved. "Charter records place thirty-one hearths. The commission will find the foundations consistent with that figure, or with any figure, really — the stone was robbed out within a decade for the Duke's new roads. What survives—" I led them to a sunken rectangle of shadow in the grass, "—is the cellar of the parish house, and these postholes, which I would counsel against interpreting as defensive earthworks unless the Duke wishes to imply the village resisted."

Brase wrote that down. I watched his pencil hesitate over *resisted* and knew I had him. A man who writes a word becomes responsible for it. He would now spend the rest of the inspection quietly hoping the villagers had *not* resisted, because if they had, his report became a different kind of document entirely.

"You surveyed this yourself?" he asked.

"In part. My master held the original commission for the region." I let that sit a moment. "Aldous Whitlock."

Halloway's step did not falter. That was the remarkable thing — a cartographer of his quality would have felt the small tremor go through the party like a fault line reading a boot-heel, but his stride stayed smooth, his expression settled into scholarly melancholy.

"A tragedy," he said mildly. "A fine mapmaker lost to treason."

"A fine mapmaker," I agreed, "lost." Let him chew on the difference between those two statements. Let him wonder, tonight, whether the second word had been a courtesy or a blade.

* * *

We climbed through the morning. This was the part I had prepared hardest, because this was the part where the truth kept trying to get in.

There were things on that hillside that should not exist after twenty years of official emptiness. A well with a carved lintel bearing the fen-folk's names. Terrace walls laid in a pattern — herringbone, Aldous called it once, drunk on surveyor's brandy — that belonged to no tradition in the Duchy. An apple variety growing wild from the orchard rows above, unknown to any nursery catalog in the capital, because its mother trees had been carried out of a burning town on shoulders and in arms.

Every one of them, I had spent nights re-drawing, re-labeling, re-narrating. The well became a later shepherd's improvement. The herringbone became "regional technique, common to river valleys" — a statement so blandly false that no honest scholar would question it and no dishonest one would bother. The apples I saved for last.

"These trees," Brase said, when we reached the terraces. He had stopped walking. His clean glove rested on a trunk older than he was. "These are productive. Fruit-bearing. Someone maintains this orchard."

"Someone does," I said pleasantly. "Forty souls, Captain, give or take. They came here generations back as woodsmen and tenants of the old charter lands. When the town below was destroyed in the disturbances, some of the rural population remained. Your commission's writ covers the town site and the contested ploughlands. The terraces fall outside the disputed boundary." I unrolled my map against an easel Ivo had positioned that morning at precisely the right terrace, and pointed to a line I had drawn myself, in ink mixed for the occasion, establishing a boundary that existed nowhere on earth except in the commission's own documents — which, conveniently, none of them had memorized.

Brase studied the line. Halloway studied me.

That was the danger of the day, and I want to be honest about it, because honesty is cheap in retrospect and expensive in the living: Halloway did not fully believe me. I could see the arithmetic running behind his spectacles. The guild escort who arrives early. The perfect narrative. The boundary drawn with a confidence that comes only from invention. He had taught me half of what I was using against him, and teachers recognize their own exercises.

But Halloway had come to this valley carrying twenty years of guilt like a stone sewn into his cloak, and guilt makes a strange ally, because it wants two opposite things at once. It wants the secret safe. And it wants, desperately, to be caught.

"Master Almeida," he said, while Brase paced off distances he did not care about, "your command of this ground is extraordinary. One might think you mourned it."

"One might think I'm competent," I said. "Mourning is above my pay grade, Provost. The guild prefers precision. Grief introduces error." I looked at him steadily, and then — because sometimes the performance must include a wink folded inside itself — added, quietly enough that only he could hear: "Though I understand errors of that kind run through the archive like a bad watermark."

Something moved behind his face. Not fear. Recognition. He knew now that I knew, and I watched him decide — visibly, the way a man decides not to report a colleague's fudged bearings — that knowing was survivable.

"Watermarks," he murmured, "can be difficult to prove."

"Everything difficult is," I said, and turned back to the captain before either of us could say something unforgivable.

* * *

They lunched at midday on bread and hard cheese Sera's people provided with the flat generosity of the very poor hosting the powerful, and I used the hour to move among the soldiers the way a good hostess moves among guests: collecting names, commenting on horses, being precisely useless. A lieutenant told me they'd ridden from the garrison at Cold Harbour. A corporal complained about the mud. None of them mentioned why a land-title survey required six mounted men and a wagon, and I did not ask, because the answer was standing in the press house pretending to mend a barrel, and asking would only have pointed at him.

Tam, to his enormous credit, was magnificent. Brase's sergeant stopped to question him — standard procedure, I suspect, for anyone within arm's reach — and Tam delivered a performance of stammering incomprehension so complete that the sergeant eventually thanked him, which may be the first time anyone has thanked Tam Voss for anything. I made a note to tell him so, and then made a second note never to tell him so, because his head is already the largest object on the property.

At the third bell, the inspection resumed, and turned toward the one place I could not narrate away.

"The cellars," Brase said, consulting his orders. "The commission is instructed to examine all sub-surface structures. Cellars, wells, cisterns. For... records of prior occupation."

*Records of prior occupation.* Meaning: anything written. Anything sealed. Anything buried by people who expected someday to come back and dig it up.

"The parish cellar we've seen," I said. "The others collapsed decades ago, or were filled deliberately by the army garrison that burned the town — standard practice, to prevent shelter for irregulars. I can show you the fill sites. The soil stratification is quite clear." I smiled at him with all the authority of the guild behind my teeth. "Unless the captain doubts the thoroughness of His Grace's own engineers?"

And there it was — the trap I had spent six days building, baited with loyalty. A young officer cannot doubt the Duke's engineers. Doubting them is doubting the campaign, the victory, the peace. Brase stood at the lip of the filled cellar of the Merrow Fen meetinghouse, twenty feet from the root cellar door hidden behind ivy and stacked barrels and the whole living truth of the atlas, and chose, as I had known he would choose, to be loyal.

"No," he said. "The fill is evident." He wrote it in his book himself, in his own careful hand: *sub-surface structures filled, c. aftermath of disturbances.* And thus certified, in ink, the burial of everything he had been sent to find.

Halloway watched me do it. He said nothing. But as we walked back down toward the ruins for the closing formalities, he fell into step beside me and spoke to the landscape rather than to me.

"When you were my apprentice," he said — and the *my* was deliberate, the old possessive vanity doing its work, "you argued with me about margins. You said a map that shows only what the state owns is not a map but a wish."

"You remember better than I do, Provost."

"I remember everything," Halloway said. "That is my affliction." He adjusted his spectacles, and for a moment the provost fell away and I saw only an old tired man standing on the grave of a town he had helped erase, in the company of the woman who was about to un-erase it. "I will sign the commission's findings tomorrow. The Duke's title will be certified. That is beyond either of us now."

"Of course," I said.

"But certification," he went on, softly, "is not seizure. The commission carries a seizure order for documents relating to prior occupation. Blank, until I fill in the name of the party from whom documents are to be taken." He looked at me then, directly, and gave me the smallest nod I have ever been given by a man dismantling his own life's work. "I have not yet filled in a name. I find I am... undecided. Scholarship requires deliberation."

"Deliberation," I agreed, "can take days."

"Days," said Halloway. "Perhaps." And he walked on toward the soldiers, already composing his remarks for the record, leaving me standing in the grass of a dead town with my heart going like a hammer on wet vellum.

* * *

They made camp that evening at the valley mouth, close enough that we could see their cookfires, far enough — I prayed — that they could not count our faces twice. Ivo found me on the upper terrace in the last grey light, checking the false wall of the root cellar for the fourth time, which is three times more than checking accomplishes.

"Well?" he said.

"He signed nothing yet. He's holding the seizure order blank. He knows, Ivo. He's known since the well, maybe since the moment I opened my mouth. And he's letting us run."

"That's not mercy," Ivo said. He had a soldier's read of camps; he'd been watching the pickets all evening. "That's a man deciding which way to jump when the floor goes. And floors always go." He was quiet a moment. "There's something else. Tam heard the sergeant talking at the water barrel. They didn't come up here just for land title."

"Documents. Halloway told me as much."

"Not only documents." Ivo's voice dropped to nearly nothing, and the valley suddenly felt colder than the hour deserved. "They came up with a warrant for a man. Someone the captain's orders describe — old soldier's shorthand, Tam couldn't catch all of it, but the sergeant said *the cartographer* twice. Said he'd be old now, if alive. Said the captain had instructions about what to do if anyone in this valley matched the description."

The wind came up the drowned valley the way it always did at dusk, smelling of salt and old water and apples, and somewhere below us Sera Voss was lighting lamps in windows that appeared on no map in the world, and I stood very still with my hand flat against the terrace wall and thought: *they are not finished with him. Twenty years was never going to be enough.*

Tomorrow the commission would sign away the land. But tonight, somewhere in that tidy camp of canvas and correct paperwork, a description was already written, and a captain with clean gloves had orders to match faces to it.

And at breakfast, I realized with a sick lurch I would carry the rest of my life, Aldous Whitlock intended to be there.
