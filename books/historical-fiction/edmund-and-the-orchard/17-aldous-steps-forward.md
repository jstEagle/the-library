## 17. Aldous Steps Forward

I had spent twenty years practicing the art of being nobody, and I flattered myself that I had grown good at it. A half-blind old man on a terrace, pruning shears in his lap, listening to the wind argue with the apple branches — I could pass through a room of strangers without raising an eyebrow. I had done it. I had been furniture, fence post, and fool for two decades.

Then a soldier held up a piece of paper with my face on it and said my name aloud, and twenty years of careful erasure came apart in the space of one breath.

* * *

The commission arrived the way weather arrives in the valley: you saw it coming long before it came, and there was nothing to be done but watch. From the upper terrace I could see the column winding up the switchback road — soldiers first, in the Duke's grey-blue, then the wagons, then the commissioners on horseback, looking like men who had dressed for a funeral and were disappointed to find no corpse.

Paloma had gone down to meet them as guild escort, wearing her guild sash like armor. I watched her through Sera's spyglass until my bad eye watered, and I will confess something here that I confessed to no one at the time: I was proud of her. Terrified for her, furious with Ivo for ever fetching her north, proud of her — all three at once, which is roughly what it feels like to be a teacher whose student has outgrown every lesson you gave her except the ones that would keep her safe.

"They'll want to see the ruins," Sera Voss said beside me. She had come up the terraces without seeming to climb them, the way she did everything, appearing where she was needed the way rain appears under a door. "Halloway will perform. He'll measure stones and read from his folio and say *ah* in that way scholars say *ah* when they've already decided."

"And the captain won't perform," I said.

"No. Captains don't perform. They search." She looked at me with those flat grey eyes that had watched Merrow Fen burn and had never once, in twenty years, looked away from anything since. "Aldous. If it comes to it—"

"It won't come to it."

"Liar," she said, almost fondly. "You've been lying badly since the fog night."

That is the other thing about being old: everyone around you has known you too long to let you lie well. I turned back to the glass. Below, the column had halted at the foot of the terraces, and Paloma was gesturing — toward the ruins of the fen, toward the drowned valley, away from the orchard. Buying our attention the way she'd been taught to buy a reader's attention on a chart: with something true placed exactly where you wanted the eye to fall.

She had learned that from me. God forgive me, she had learned everything from me.

* * *

They gave us an hour. Then two officers came up the terraces with a folded paper between them, and a sergeant knocked at Sera's door with a courtesy so thin you could see the boot-prints through it, and we were assembled in the yard — forty-some souls who did not officially exist, standing in the light of day before men whose whole trade was deciding who existed.

The captain's name was Brann. I remember because he wore it on his collar and because I have a cartographer's habit of recording names; also because he had the kind of face that has already decided the ending of the story and is merely waiting for the middle to finish. He walked the line of us slowly. He looked at calloused hands and downcast eyes and he found nothing, because there was nothing to find — we were farmers, keepers, an old woman, children kept back at the cider house.

Then he stopped in front of me, and he unfolded the paper.

"Aldous Whitlock," he read, "formerly chief cartographer of the Army of the Marches. Convicted of treason in the matter of Merrow Fen. Sentence commuted to death. Executed—" he paused, savoring it, or perhaps puzzled by it, "—executed in the capital, this day, twenty years ago."

"Ah," I said. "Then I'm sorry to disappoint you. I'm Cullen. Orchard keeper. Bad eyes, worse knees."

He turned the paper toward me. My own face looked back — younger by twenty years, full-haired, the jaw of a man who had not yet learned what waiting does to a face. The likeness was good. It was very good. It had been drawn from life by someone who hated me, which is how you get the best likenesses.

"That," said Brann, "is the poster's problem, not mine. Look again, grandfather."

And I made the mistake every old man makes, which is to look.

Behind him, one of the soldiers shifted. A young man, red-knuckled, the sort of soldier who is mostly feet and appetite — and he made a small sound, the sound a man makes when a name falls into place in his head, and he said, not loudly, not wanting the credit or the blame of it:

"Sergeant — that's the man from the academy courtyard. Before the — sir, I stood guard. That's him. That's the cartographer."

The yard went very quiet. Somewhere up the slope a wind shook the trees, and a few early apples came down and thumped the grass like slow applause.

Brann smiled. It was not a cruel smile, particularly. It was the smile of a bookkeeper finding the ledger balances.

"Well," he said. "Grandfather Cullen."

* * *

I want to record what happened inside me in those few seconds, because Paloma will tell the rest of it better than I, and because a man ought to account honestly for the moment he spends his last coin.

What happened inside me was arithmetic.

Brann would search. That was certain now; suspicion had a shape and a witness, and captains do not leave shapes unexamined. He would search the houses first, then the cellars, then the terraces, and he would find the root cellar within the hour, because Tam's marking — whatever had been chalked or cut near its entrance — would show itself to a man looking for marks. And in the cellar, if we were unlucky, plates. Forty sets of hands that did not exist, attached to forty throats that could be made not to exist. A village erased twice — once by fire, once by paperwork, and the second time no one would ever write a ballad about.

Against that: one old man. Expendable. Already dead on paper these twenty years — and there is a strange freedom in being executed; the enemy can only kill you once more, and after that they are simply being wasteful.

There was a version of the next hour where I ran. I want it recorded that I considered it, briefly and without shame, because Ivo was watching me from the barn wall with his hand moving toward something in his coat, and Sera Voss had gone still as a drawn bow, and Paloma — Paloma was down among the commissioners with her heart in her throat and her face smooth as fresh vellum, and running would spend all of them.

Instead I laughed. It surprised everyone, including me.

"Well spotted, lad," I said to the young soldier, who had the grace to look as though he wished the ground would take him back. "Twenty years, and I'm recognized by a boy who was in short trousers when I was hanged. There's gratitude." I spread my arms to Brann. "Yes. I'm Aldous Whitlock. Chief cartographer, Army of the Marches. Convicted of treason for refusing to aim artillery at a town full of bakers. Executed, apparently, though I must say the rope work was sloppy."

"Search the houses," Brann said to his sergeant, not taking his eyes off me.

"No need," I said, and I put every year of my voice into it, the whole weight of an old man's certainty, the tone I had used on generals. "Captain, you are going to waste your afternoon, and your Duke's daylight, and I am going to save you from it, because I am, whatever else I am, still the best mapmaker your side ever condemned. Listen to me. The plates you're hunting — the true surveys of Merrow Fen, the charter evidence, the lot of it — exist. One set. Mine. I made them alone, over years, and I hid them where only I know, because everyone who helped me is either dead or standing in this yard, and I would not hang them for paper."

"Convenient," said Brann.

"It is, rather," I agreed. "It's the most convenient thing about me. Here is the shape of it: I will take you to them. Dig them up with my own hands, in front of witnesses, Halloway included — he'll want to authenticate them for his commission, and he knows my hand better than anyone alive. You get your seizure, your proof, your promotion, whatever a man gets for catching a dead traitor's life's work. And this village—" I gestured at the frightened, silent line of them, at Sera with her chin up and her eyes like winter, "—these people are apple farmers. They can't read a survey plate any more than you can read Latin backwards. They stayed quiet because I told them staying quiet was survival, and they've paid for my silence with twenty years of their lives. Take me. Leave them. They are worth nothing to your report except a footnote, and footnotes bore your masters."

It was a good speech. I had composed most of it in the nights since the commission was sighted, the way I compose everything — in private, in the dark, revising. I had only left out the part that mattered, which was that the plates were already gone, carried down a flooded salt road by my student and a boy with a grudge, and that every word I now said was bought with the only currency I had left: my own useless, twice-condemned self.

Time. That was the transaction. Every minute Brann spent watching me talk was a minute the salt road carried my truth south. Every hour of the fabricated dig site I would have to invent, the false burial, the theatrical spade-work — hours. Perhaps a day. Perhaps, with luck and Halloway's conscience, longer.

I was buying time with the last thing I owned, and I have never in my life made a purchase with such satisfaction.

* * *

Brann did not believe me. I respect him for that; belief is for chaplains. But he wanted the plates, and wanting is a lever, and I have spent my whole life among levers.

"Halloway," he called down the terrace path.

The Provost came up slowly, hat in hand, and when he saw me his face did something complicated — grief and guilt and relief all arriving at once like carts at a market gate, none of them willing to go first. Twenty years ago that man helped stage my death out of a conscience he couldn't afford to follow further. We had never spoken of it. We were about to speak of it constantly, in the language of documents and glances, for the rest of this wretched afternoon.

"You look well, Aldous," he said, which was a lie delivered kindly, which is the national language of scholars.

"I look executed," I said. "Provost, tell the captain about my hand. Tell him whether anyone else in this valley could forge it."

Halloway looked at me for a long moment. Behind his eyes I watched the same arithmetic run that had run in mine — except his ledger had twenty years of guilt written in it, in a hand very much like mine. He turned to Brann.

"No one," he said quietly. "No one alive could counterfeit those plates. Only Whitlock could have made them. Which means—" and here he paused, delicately, the way you pause over a folio you are choosing not to damage, "—which means the captain is quite right to secure them personally. And equally right, I should think, that the seizure be conducted properly. Methodically. With the prisoner's full cooperation." His eyes met mine. "We would not want anything... rushed. Ruins rush badly."

He knew. Of course he knew — not where the plates were, but that there were no plates to find where I would lead them, that I was building a stage, and that every hour of methodical scholarship he imposed on Brann was an hour purchased on his behalf by his own buried conscience. He had razored pages from the archive to protect a secret; now he would slow a captain to protect a bigger one. A man spends his whole life being consistent, mostly without noticing.

Brann frowned at the delay, and I watched him weigh ambition against procedure, and procedure lost by the width of a warrant.

"Bind him," he said. "Whitlock, you will show us where these plates are buried at first light. If you're lying—"

"I'm always lying," I said pleasantly, "but not about this."

As they led me past the line of my people, Sera Voss stepped out of it — one step, no more, just enough to break regulation distance — and pressed something into my bound hands. Small, hard, round. An apple. From the oldest tree, the ones carried out of the burning town in a child's arms the night I invented a fog.

"For the road, Master Aldous," she said clearly, so the soldiers would hear nothing in it but an old woman's courtesy. "You always did forget to eat."

I held that apple all night in the cell behind the commission's wagon-line, and I did not eat it. Some things are not provisions. They are instructions — reminders of what the road is for and whom you are carrying.

At first light they would come for me with spades, and I would lead them to a hillside where I would dig, with great ceremony, toward nothing, and improvise, and stall, and lie magnificently, and I could hold them thus for a day, perhaps two. What I could not know — what no arithmetic of mine had accounted — was what was happening that same night in the Provost's tent, where a seized atlas sat in a strongbox, and a burned order sat in a brazier, and a key was changing owners.

Nor could I know that the salt road had a culvert on it, and that culverts fail, and that the truth I had spent twenty years and one final morning protecting was at that moment underwater.

But that is the trouble with maps, even for a mapmaker. You chart the road. You cannot chart the river rising.
