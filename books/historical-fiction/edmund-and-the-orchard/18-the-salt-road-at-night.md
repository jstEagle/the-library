## 18. The Salt Road at Night

**Paloma**

There is a particular kind of silence that comes over a person when she is strapping forty pounds of vellum truth to her own back in the dark. It is not the silence of the guild workshop, which I had mistaken for peace for twenty years. It is the silence of a woman who has finally stopped asking permission.

The plates were wrapped in oilcloth and bound to a pack frame Sera Voss had made from apple-wood — *apple-wood*, because even our escape had to be sentimental — and I had divided them as we'd agreed. One set rode against my spine. The second set went into a satchel that Ivo would carry by a different road, so that no single soldier's lucky night could end the whole argument at once. Aldous had called this redundancy. Ivo called it not putting all your dead men in one grave. They were going to get along terribly, those two, if either of them lived long enough for it.

Aldous did not come down to see us off. He was already up on the ridge with a lantern and two orchard boys, staging his miracle: a "dig site," freshly turned earth, survey stakes, a false trail of papers leading to a pit that would take a commission three days to excavate and find nothing but Aldous's own forged disappointment. He had insisted on doing it himself. *I have spent twenty years learning how to be invisible,* he'd said. *Let me spend one night being loudly visible instead.* His hands had shaken when he said it. Not from fear. From relief, I think, which frightened me more.

Sera Voss pressed her forehead to mine at the orchard gate. She smelled of woodsmoke and bruised fruit.

"Salt road," she said. "Past the third marker it floods. The culvert under the old toll arch has been groaning since spring."

"You've walked it?"

"I've buried people in it." She said it the way she said everything, like a woman setting out dishes. "Go before the moon sets. The water rises faster than regret."

Ivo was waiting where the terraces gave way to scree, and he looked at my pack, then at me, with the particular suspicion he reserved for things he hadn't yet found a reason to distrust.

"You're sure about the split?" he asked. Not *are you sure you can do this*. He'd stopped doubting my legs somewhere around the second mile of the first night. He doubted my judgment, which was fair; I doubted his manners.

"One capture shouldn't kill the truth twice," I said. "That's the whole arithmetic."

"And if yours is the capture?"

"Then yours had better be the publication."

He almost smiled. On Ivo's face an almost-smile is a full ceremony, trumpets and all. We went down into the dark.

* * *

**Ivo**

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying your father's murder on your back through flooded country: the weight changes. For six years the weight had been a name — Merrow Fen, my father's name, the names of the hanged — and it had sat where all grief sits, behind the sternum, immovable. Tonight it was forty pounds of copperplate and vellum, and it shifted with every step, and I confess I preferred it. A burden you can adjust a strap on is a mercy.

Paloma walked ahead of me on the salt road, and I watched her the way I'd watched her since the day she opened her door and went white at the sight of a letter. She moved like a draughtsman. Every step placed, every handhold tested before trusted, her body reading the ground the way her eyes read a chart. Aldous Whitlock had built that into her without her knowing, the same way he'd built the salt routes into her memory disguised as exercises. I used to think the guild had stolen her childhood and trained it into a tool. Walking behind her, I revised the opinion: he'd hidden a key inside her, and tonight the lock was finally turning.

The road itself was an obscenity. Salt roads are built on bedrock and arrogance, and this one had been the Duchy's pride once — the toll arches still stood, carved with mermaids holding scales, though the tollhouse was a roofless jaw and the mermaids wore twenty years of moss like mourning veils. Where the valley had been drowned to feed the Duke's new mills, the road ran causeway-style between black water on both sides, close enough to touch.

At the second arch the water had come up over the paving. Ankle-deep, then knee-deep, cold enough to make my teeth file complaints.

"Third marker next," Paloma said. Her voice was steady. Her breath wasn't. "Sera says the culvert's bad."

"Sera says a lot of things."

"Sera's never been wrong yet."

"She told me I had honest eyes." I let that sit. "First time anyone's been wrong."

Paloma laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, as if laughter were another thing the guild had regulations about. But it stayed in the air between us longer than it should have, and I carried it along with everything else.

We came to the third marker at the hour when the moon had gone behind the hills and the world was lit only by whatever light water remembers. The flood here was waist-deep and moving. You could feel the current leaning on you, patient, enormous, like a creditor.

"The culvert's under the toll arch ahead," Paloma said. "If it holds, we're through to the goat path in a quarter mile."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we learn to swim with copperplates."

She went first. Of course she went first. I had learned by now that arguing with Paloma Whitlock about precedence was like arguing with a tide table.

* * *

**Paloma**

I want to record, for whoever reads this someday — and someone will, that is the entire obscene point of me — that I was afraid. Not the clean fear of the guild examination hall, where terror wears a collar and sits up straight. This was animal fear, the kind that lives in the gut and speaks in the voice of everyone who ever told me to be careful, be quiet, be correct. My mother's voice. Halloway's voice. Twenty years of *a map is only as good as its discretion.*

The water at the arch came to my ribs. I turned sideways, pushing the pack ahead of me on its frame, feeling the current try to peel me off the causeway wall. Under my palms the stones were slick with something older than moss. And beneath my feet — beneath the water, beneath the paving — I felt it before I heard it: a shudder, deep and structural, the sound a bridge makes when it decides.

"Culvert's going," I shouted, and the words barely survived the wind funnelling through the arch.

Ivo was four feet behind me. Four feet. I could see him in the last grey light, one arm hooked through the arch's iron ring — some ancient toll-keeper's courtesy to late travellers — and I saw him look down through the churning water at the sound rising out of the earth, and I saw him understand exactly how much distance there was between us and exactly how little it mattered.

The culvert collapsed the way old things die: all at once, after pretending for years.

The road didn't open so much as exhale. A section of paving the size of a hay cart tilted, slid, and was gone, and the water that had been leaning on us fell into the gap with a roar like a crowd. I was thrown flat against the causeway wall. The pack frame caught on a jutting stone and held — held, held, *held*, I could have kissed that stone — while the flood tried to take my legs out from under me like a pickpocket working a crowd.

"Ivo!"

Nothing. Water. Then, downstream, a cough, and a hand breaking the surface, and my whole life compressed into the space between seeing that hand and reaching it.

I got a wrist. He got my forearm. The current argued. The current was winning the argument when his other hand found the drowned rootstock of some tree the flooding had killed years ago, and he hung there, half in the black water, and shouted at me with the fury of a man who refuses to drown politely:

"The satchel — is it —"

"Your satchel's gone, you idiot, I've got *you*—"

"That's not the arithmetic!" Which was, I will note for the historical record, the first time Ivo Reyes had ever objected to my arithmetic, and he chose drowning to do it.

I braced both boots against the causeway wall and hauled. He was heavier than he looked — grief usually is — and the rootstock tore loose by inches, and then all at once, and we came up the wall together like two drowned things being reeled in by a fisherman with no patience left. I got him over the lip onto the surviving paving, and he lay there vomiting floodwater and rage in equal measure.

"Plates," he rasped.

"Gone. Down the culvert, into the fen, feeding the eels."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them again they were wet, and not from the river. "Six years," he said. "Six years I carried that set. Through three towns. I slept with it under my head like a pillow."

"I know."

"You don't know. That was my father's — that was the copy I swore on—"

"Ivo." I knelt in the floodwater beside him and took his face in both hands, which surprised us both. "Listen to me. The set on my back is complete. Every plate. I copied the critical ones twice precisely so that losing one set would hurt and not kill. That was the arithmetic. *This* was always the arithmetic." I tapped my own spine, where the apple-wood frame pressed through the oilcloth. "You didn't lose the truth. You lost a duplicate. Grieve the duplicate."

He stared at me. Somewhere in the dark the fen settled around its new hole in the world, water finding its level with a long sigh.

"Aldous taught you that," he said finally.

"Aldous taught me everything except how to say goodbye to him. I'm improvising that part."

* * *

**Ivo**

We sat on the causeway until my hands stopped shaking, which took longer than my pride approved of. The goat path, when we found it, climbed away from the drowned road through gorse and old drystone walls, and Paloma led, because of course she did, reading the slope by touch and memory, murmuring route-notes under her breath like prayers: *wall corner, sheep-crawl, the oak with the lightning scar, bear left where the land falls.*

Somewhere on that climb I understood something I'd been circling for weeks. I had started looking for Aldous Whitlock to make someone pay. Revenge is a simple cargo; it packs light and rides easy. But somewhere between the orchard and the flood it had changed freight without my noticing, and now what I carried — besides nothing, now, literally nothing, my arms absurdly empty — was the wish that the record survive. Not my vengeance. The *record*. Eleven testimonies and a dead man's surveys and a girl's perfect copperplates. I wanted strangers in a free port I'd never see to open a book and know my father's town had existed.

"What will you do," I asked her, "when it's done? When it's printed and sworn and real?"

Paloma didn't break stride. "Sleep for a week. Then start the next map."

"There won't be a next map. Not for you. You'll be a fugitive with excellent penmanship."

"Then I'll be a fugitive with excellent penmanship and a finished atlas." She glanced back at me, and in the dark her eyes caught what little light there was. "What will *you* do, Ivo? Your revenge walked into a culvert tonight. What's left in the satchel?"

I thought about lying. I'm good at it; the road teaches you. But she'd just pulled me out of a fen by one arm, and there are debts even irony can't service.

"Whatever you're doing," I said. "Apparently."

She faced forward again. But I saw her shoulders, and shoulders don't lie the way mouths do, and hers were laughing.

* * *

**Paloma**

We came down to the coast road at grey dawn, two soaked ghosts with one set of plates between us and the whole weight of a duchy's patience behind us. The sea was the colour of uninked vellum. Somewhere north of us, Aldous was performing his dig for the commission, buying us hours with a shovel and a lie. Somewhere south, Halloway was deciding — or failing to decide — what kind of man he was. I have since learned how that night ended for him. At dawn I knew only that every hour Aldous bought was an hour we spent walking, and that walking was the only prayer I had ever been any good at.

At the crossroads above the fishing village, where the smugglers' path met the king's road, we stopped. The plan required it: separate roads, separate boats, separate risks. Ivo would go south to the free port with the sworn testimonies, which Sera had sewn into his coat lining. I would carry the plates north to the second harbour, where a herring boat owed Sera a favour older than the war.

He held out his hand. I shook it, because we were that kind of people, formal even at the edge of the world.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your master built you well."

"He didn't build me. He *left* me material and instructions." I tightened the straps on the apple-wood frame. "Like everything else in that atlas, I finished it myself."

He almost smiled again — the full ceremony — and then he was gone down the southern road, and I stood alone at the crossroads with forty pounds of truth on my back and the tide going out.

Behind me, far off across the fens, I saw it: a smudge of smoke rising from the direction of the valley, too much smoke, the wrong colour for a cookfire or a kiln.

I told myself it was Aldous burning his false papers at the dig site. I told myself it was nothing. I adjusted the straps, and I walked north, and I did not look back again — because I had learned that much from him, at least: a mapmaker keeps her eyes on the road ahead, and lets the past render itself in ink.
