## 20. The Burning Orchard

I was born in a burning town, which is to say I have always known the sound fire makes when it finds a home. It is not the roar they put in songs. It is smaller than that. It is the sound of a roof settling into itself like an old man into a chair he knows will be his last. It is patient. Fire has all night and no conscience.

So when the bell began ringing over Merrow Fen's orchard — our orchard, twenty years grown from trees carried out of another man's ruin — I did not run toward it the way the young ones did. I finished buttoning my coat. I put the kettle off the flame. And then I walked out under my own apple tree, the one Aldous had grafted from a cutting of my mother's tree back in the fen, and I looked up the terraces at the orange light climbing through the leaves, and I said, aloud, to no one: "Well. They've come to read the margins."

An old woman is allowed to talk to herself. It is one of the few perquisites of the office.

* * *

They had come up the drover's path on the north side, maybe thirty soldiers, maybe more, with torches and that particular economy of movement men have when they have been ordered to do a thing they will later claim they were ordered to do. That is the arithmetic of atrocity, children, write it down: nobody ever did it. It was always done.

The bell was Tam's work. He'd been on watch, bless his guilty heart, and whatever else the boy had done with his letters and his brother, he rang that bell fast enough to wake every soul on the terraces before the first torch touched the first bough. I have made my peace with many things in this long life, but I am not sure I will ever make peace fully with how much of survival comes down to somebody being awake at the wrong hour.

Aldous was already gone — down the mountain with his guards two days prior, spending himself like a coin so the rest of us could keep our shapes. Paloma and the Reyes boy were somewhere below with the true plates, God and the salt road willing. Which meant the orchard held no maps, no plates, nothing worth seizing. What it held was forty people the Duke's commission could not find on any chart, and a captain humiliated by an old man with a fabricated dig site, and a night that needed an answer for its failure.

Fire is how failed expeditions write their reports.

"Voss!" That was Marta Ellery, sixty years old and faster than me across the upper terrace, herding her grandchildren ahead of her like a woman who had done this before. She had. We both had. That was the whole obscene joke of us: we were practiced. "The lower paths?"

"Flooded," I said. "Paloma's routes. The west stairs and the sheep track behind the cistern. Everyone goes the way she drew it or not at all."

Marta looked at me in the torchlight, and I watched her understand what I was saying — that the girl cartographer had spent her weeks among us not just copying plates but drawing our escape into the hillside itself, every goat path and drainage run marked in that neat hand of hers, routes for exactly this evening, though none of us had had the courage to say the word *evening* out loud.

"She mapped us out," Marta said.

"She mapped us out," I agreed. "That's what love looks like when it's been trained by the guild. It doesn't weep. It measures."

* * *

Here is what I remember of the next hour, and I set it down honestly because memory is the only archive they cannot burn, though they will certainly try:

I remember the smoke smelling like harvest. That is the cruelty I was not prepared for, even the second time. Apples burning smell like cider, like kitchens, like everything the trees were for. The valley filled with the sweetness of our own abundance destroying itself, and I thought: some clerk in the capital will smell woodsmoke tonight and think pleasantly of home.

I remember Ansel Dore refusing to leave the press house until he had smashed the type face-down into the mud, letter by letter, so the soldiers would find no alphabet among the ashes to hang anyone with. He came out with ink to his elbows and his face wet, and I took his arm and told him the truth, which is that letters are easier to cast than men are to unhang, and he let me lead him down the west stairs.

I remember the children being quiet. Children know. There is a look they get — I saw it in Merrow Fen twenty years ago on faces now grown and carrying children of their own — a look of terrible competence, small people folding themselves into the work of disappearing. Yanna Ellery, seven years old, carried a kitten down a flooded sheep track in the dark without one cry, and when they reached the rendezvous rocks she handed the animal to her grandmother and asked, in a whisper, whether they would be allowed to stop being quiet soon. I did not hear the answer. I was already walking back up.

Because here is the thing about Sera Voss, and I have had twenty years and one previous catastrophe to become acquainted with the thing: I do not leave last because I am brave. I leave last because someone must count. A village is not its houses. It is its census. Somebody has to stand where everyone can see her and be counted leaving, so that the ones already gone can believe, in the cold hours ahead, that they were not left. Fear is a fog, and a familiar figure is a lighthouse, and if the lighthouse must burn, let it burn visibly, on schedule, after the ships are out.

I went terrace to terrace by the light of the thing destroying them, calling the low hoot that has meant *all clear, moving on* among our people since before Paloma Whitlock was born. Empty terrace. Hoot. Empty cellar — the root cellar stripped bare days ago, the true plates long gone, thank God and Aldous's paranoia. Hoot. The meeting lawn with its long table where we had argued and voted and split and stayed anyway. Empty, chairs overturned, one sandal, one doll. Hoot.

At the third terrace I found old Piet Rennick sitting against his trunk with his hands in his lap, watching his own orchard go.

"Piet." I crouched. His eyes were working fine; it was his legs that had retired years since, and I had known this night would ask a question I did not want to answer. "Up you get. I'll take your weight."

"Sera." He said it the way you say a name at a funeral. "The Gravenstein's burning."

The Gravenstein was the oldest tree on the terraces — older than our exile, a cutting of a cutting of the great tree in the fen's common ground, the one the charter named, the one that proved in its own rings that Merrow Fen had stood and planted and prospered where the Duke's maps said marsh and emptiness. Aldous had tended it like a relic. In a sense it was one. The oldest witness we had, and witnesses, we had learned that week, were being taken seriously at last — just not by the right people.

"The Gravenstein will come back," I told him, "if any piece of it gets out. That's apples for you. Stubborn as historians." And I got my shoulder under his arm and hauled, and he rose, swearing gently at his knees in the old fen dialect, which made me laugh, which made him laugh, which is how you carry a man past the burning of everything he owns: you make him laugh at the weight instead of the loss.

We went down the west stairs together, slow as a funeral, while above us the orchard roared its terrible kitchen-sweetness into the drowned valley's fog.

* * *

I want to be fair to the soldiers, in the way that fairness is sometimes the sharpest weapon available. They did not hunt us. They stood in the lower field with their torches and let the dark do the work, because the order had been to destroy the orchard, and destruction was achieved, and pursuing forty civilians up flooded goat tracks in fog was not in anyone's orders. That is the banality I need you to record, whoever you are, reading this in whatever future purchased with our night: no one had to be a monster. It was sufficient that everyone follow instructions. The fire was administrative.

One young soldier did see us — Piet and me, coming off the last stair like a pair of ancient bookends. He raised his halberd and then did not use it, and looked at us with a face that was trying very hard to have no expression at all, and stepped aside. I met his eye as I passed. I did not thank him. Thanks would have made him complicit in something he will spend decades deciding whether he witnessed, and I would not put that weight on a boy who hadn't asked for it. But I did pause, one breath, long enough for him to see that I saw him. Witness acknowledges witness. That is the whole of my religion.

Then the fog took us, and behind us the orchard light went diffuse and enormous, staining the whole underside of the clouds the color of the inside of an apple, and I heard Piet praying — not for the dead, not for ourselves, but giving thanks, item by item, in the manner of old fen folk, for specific trees.

* * *

The rendezvous was a shelf of rock above the salt road where the fog pooled thick as wool, and by midnight it held perhaps thirty of us, pressed together for warmth, listening to the valley burn below. Families whispered roll calls. Somewhere a baby, God preserve its mother, slept through the end of the world.

Tam Verrin found me there. He was soaked, ash-streaked, and wearing the expression of a young man rehearsing a confession he intended to deliver whether anyone wanted it or not.

"I rang the bell in time," he said. "But Sera, the marker on the cellar — that was read by someone before the storm ever came. My brother—"

"Hush." I put my hand on the back of his neck, the way I used to when he was small and the fens were whole and the worst thing in the world was a broken graft. "Listen to me, Tam of the orchard. You wrote to your brother because a heart needs news, and hearts that are denied news find worse ways. Your letters were read — yes. And out of that reading came a marker, and out of the marker came fire. That is a chain. But chains do not tell you which link to blame; they only tell you the smith. The smith sits in a palace. Now." I turned him by the collar to face the crowd on the rock, the scattered remnant of Merrow Fen, wet and ash-grey and alive. "Look at them. Every soul on this stone got off these terraces tonight along lines drawn on paper by a woman you helped convince, and you rang the bell that woke them. If your guilt wants employment, it may have the job it was made for: testimony. You know the other side of this wall better than any of us. When the record asks — and it will ask, Tam, it is asking already, out on a salt road in a satchel — you will answer. That is your penance and your post, and I am assigning it to you as the oldest surviving officer of a town that officially does not exist. Do you accept?"

He stood straighter than I had seen him stand since his brother's uniform first appeared in his stories. "I accept."

"Good. Then help me count."

We counted. Thirty-one souls on the rock, with nine more unaccounted for — the Reyes party below, and eight who had scattered early toward the coast route as contingency demanded. Not one lost, so far as fog and darkness allowed knowing. I sat down on the wet stone amid my census and permitted myself, briefly, the luxury of shaking.

Piet lowered himself beside me. Above the fog, the sky had begun, obscenely, to pale toward morning.

"You'll want to go back up," he said.

It was not a question, because Piet had known me through two lives.

"There's a cutting I mean to take," I said.

"They'll still be up there. Soldiers."

"Soldiers guard ruins, Piet. Nobody guards ash. Ash has no strategic value." I pulled my coat tighter. "Besides. Someone from Merrow Fen should walk out of Merrow Fen on her own feet, in daylight, awake, unhurried, carrying something. Both times. Let the pattern belong to us and not only to them."

* * *

Dawn found me climbing the west stairs alone, which I will concede was the act of a foolish old woman, and I have been called worse by better.

The orchard was a field of standing trunks and fallen fire, black lace against a grey-gold sky, steam rising off the burnt terraces where the fog had come in overnight to do its small penance. The soldiers were gone — withdrawn at first light, their work certified, their captain's report presumably concluding that the site now presented no further historical interest. I walked among the dead trees the way you walk through a cathedral after a flood, stepping over beams, touching char, saying nothing because there was nothing left to say that the silence wasn't already saying better.

And there, on the third terrace, at the corner where it had sheltered a century of wind, lay the Gravenstein. Felled, not merely burned — the trunk split, the crown down, the great limbs sprawled like a dropped marionette. But near the root, low on the split bole where the fire had licked and passed, the bark was scorched and the wood beneath was alive. I put my palm flat against it and felt, or believed I felt, the slow insistence of sap under ruin, which is either botany or hope depending on the hour.

I had brought the knife, the wax cloth, and the moss, because I had told Piet the truth: apples are stubborn. I cut three scions from the living wood, each the length of my forearm, wrapped them as Aldous had taught the orchard keepers to wrap them twenty years ago — damp moss, waxed cloth, tied with the patience the craft requires. One for me. One for Tam, whose penance needed something to grow toward. One for whichever child on that cold rock would live longest and need most badly, fifty years hence, to hold a piece of a tree older than the lie.

Then, because the scions were cuttings and not proof, I dug with my knife and my two hands at the root crown until I could free a sucker — a self-rooted shoot, small as a riding whip, its own tree entire, wanting nothing but soil and time. I got it out with most of its roots, cradled it in the waxed cloth and the crook of my arm, and stood.

Below me the valley lay white with fog, and the salt road ran invisible under it, going south toward the strait and the free ports and whatever Paloma Whitlock was doing with the truth at that exact moment. I could not know — this is the honest ledger, I could not know — whether the plates lived, whether Aldous lived, whether the commission had swallowed the decoy or the decoy had swallowed the commission. I knew only what I carried: three scions, one sapling, one census of thirty-one names held word-perfect in an old head, and eleven households' worth of sworn testimony waiting in the mouths of the scattered.

They thought they had burned a village. They had performed a distribution. Every family on that rock had gone out along mapped lines carrying the same cargo: the story, whole, duplicated beyond seizure. You cannot confiscate a diaspora, Captain. You cannot requisition memory. We were the atlas now, all forty of us, walking copies of the truth, and every road out of this valley was a page.

I shifted the sapling in my arm, took one last look at the black lace of the terraces — my home for twenty years, the second life of a murdered town, ash to the skyline — and I did not weep, because the accounting is not finished and I do not pay grief before the bill is presented.

Then Sera Voss, last officer of Merrow Fen, walked down through the fog onto the salt road, carrying a tree.

Behind me, far off to the south, a gull cried once over the drowned valley — and under the gull, fainter, stranger, carried up the mist like something out of a dream of better mornings, I thought I heard wheels. Cart wheels, heavy-laden, moving away south at speed, and a voice urging the horse on that I could almost, almost have named.

I walked faster.
