## 21. On the Record

The free port of Callais smells of tar, oranges, and other people's laws — or rather, the blessed absence of ours. I noticed it the first morning, standing on the quay with salt wind pulling at my coat and thinking: *no one here has ever heard of the Guild of Cartographers, and if they have, they don't care.* Twenty years in the Duchy had taught me that a seal on a document was a kind of weather; in Callais, the only weather was the sea's, and it signed nothing.

We arrived in the last week of autumn, eleven days out from the coast, on a hull whose captain accepted payment in coin and asked no questions about the oilcloth case I would not let out of my hands even to sleep, which is why I slept sitting up with it across my knees like a child I'd stolen.

Ivo slept like a man who had finally put something down. Not the plates — those were mine, split between us as agreed, his half in a second case wrapped in sailcloth and disguised as charts of herring grounds, which in a sense they were not. He put down something else. I watched him do it on the third night out, when the fear left his face all at once, like a tide going out, and what remained underneath looked younger and more tired than anything I'd seen on him since the day he knocked on my door carrying a dead man's handwriting.

He did not sleep so easily once we made landfall. Grief keeps its own schedule, and Aldous's death reached us in installments.

But that comes later. First: ink.

* * *

The printing house of Vosk & Daughter stood on a street too narrow for its sign, run by a widow named Maren Vosk who had hands like a dockworker and read the first proof sheet in silence for the better part of an hour while Ivo and I sat among her apprentices doing our best impression of people with money and patience.

She set the sheet down at last.

"This is a confession," she said. "Not an atlas."

"It is both," I said. "The maps are sworn evidence. The evidence is mapped. That was rather the point."

"The Duke's advocates will say the surveys are forged."

"The Duke's advocates can come to Callais and say it to the faces of eleven witnesses who will swear otherwise, under oath, before notaries who keep their own records and do not razored pages out of them when the wind shifts." I heard my own voice climb and made myself stop. Wry is a discipline. Rage is easy; wit is work. "Forged or not, Madame Vosk, it will exist. That is all we came to buy from you. Existence."

She looked at me a long moment, then at Ivo, then at the plates fanned across her bench — Aldous's true survey of Merrow Fen, the drowned valley drawn as it *was*, the charter boundaries laid over it in his careful hand, and in the margins, small as prayers, the names of every household the official maps had eaten.

"I printed pamphlets against the Regent of Ostrand," she said. "They exiled me. I took my press with me." A shrug that moved more than her shoulders. "Three hundred crowns. Half now. And you two learn to set type, because I'll not waste my people's hours on your corrections."

So for six weeks I was an apprentice again, at thirty-nine, ink to the elbow, learning a trade adjacent to but humbler than my own. There is a particular humiliation in being corrected on kerning by a girl of sixteen, and a particular grace in accepting it. I think Aldous would have laughed. I thought about him laughing, some evenings, and had to go stand in the alley until I could hold the composing stick steady again.

The testimonies were the harder labor. We took them properly — a notary, oaths, signatures — because a truth without procedure is only a rumor with good posture. Eleven villagers of Merrow Fen gave their accounts. Sera Voss went first, because she had appointed herself first at everything since the fire, and hers ran forty pages: the massacre night, the fog that appeared on no chart but Aldous's, the weeks in the hills, the terraces cut into the valley wall, twenty years of apples grown from scions carried out of a burning town in a woman's apron.

Tam wrote his own. He insisted. His spelling was inventive and his hand was terrible and I would not correct a word of it, because it began: *My name is Tam Voss, I am nineteen years old, and for six years I sent letters to my brother in the Duke's service, and every letter was read, and this is what I know about being watched.* It was, I confess, better testimony than mine.

And then there was Aldous's confession.

Ivo had found it folded inside the master folio, in the false bottom where Aldous kept everything he could not bear to look at. Dated three days before the commission came. In it he set down, in that flourished capitals hand I would have known at the bottom of the sea — and nearly did know at the bottom of a flooded culvert, but that chapter belongs to another telling — the whole architecture of his survival: the execution staged with Halloway's help, the order falsified, the twenty years of silence bought with the lives and land of everyone who trusted him to be dead.

He did not excuse himself. That was the thing I turned over at night. He had a craftsman's opportunity to shade a document — he was the finest cartographer of his generation, shading was his religion — and he declined it. He wrote: *I did not lead them out because I loved justice. I led them out because I loved the town more than my career, which is a smaller virtue than history will want, and history will get what it gets.* And lower down, the sentence I have not yet learned to carry: *I never sent for Paloma because her ignorance was the only armor I could give her. I am told armor is cold. So was the alternative.*

Ivo wanted the confession printed entire. I wanted — God help me — one passage struck. The part about the armor. Because it was addressed, unmistakably, to me, and I did not see why the whole world should be handed my father-figure's apology along with his geography.

"You're editing a dead man's soul," Ivo said, "to spare your feelings."

"Yes," I said. "That is exactly what I'm doing. Isn't it magnificent that you noticed."

We fought about it for two days, there in the alley behind Vosk & Daughter, quietly, the way people fight who have shared a rowboat. On the third day I set the passage in type myself, every word, and pulled the proof, and stood looking at it upside down, which is how compositors read, and thought: *there. Now it is true in every orientation.*

Aldous taught me that. Never draw a coastline you wouldn't defend from either direction.

* * *

He died in the water, or just after it. I have told the escape badly and briefly to the notary and I will tell it badly and briefly here, because some things do not improve with narration. The culvert collapsed. The salt road flooded faster than any chart predicted — a chart, incidentally, that Aldous himself drew, and which he had annotated in a margin I found later: *this figure assumes the sluice holds; if it does not, do not take Paloma this way.* We took it anyway. The water took him instead. Ivo held him and lost him and came up alone, and we dug the case out of the silt the next morning, and the plates inside were dry, because Aldous waterproofed everything he ever loved.

His last letter was not found in the folio. Sera Voss handed it to me the morning we scattered — she had been carrying it since the orchard burned, waiting, she said, for me to be somewhere with walls. He had written it during the copying nights, when we worked hand over hand on the same vellum and he dictated corrections his blind eyes remembered but could no longer draw. I thought those nights were reconciliation. They were also, it turns out, dictation of a different kind.

*The atlas was always yours to finish,* he wrote. *I stole twenty years from you and called it protection; you may bill me. Finish the coasts. Sign them yourself. An unsigned map is a rumor; a signed one is a promise. Do not sign it Whitlock. Sign it Almeida. You earned the name honestly, which neither of us can claim.*

I had spent the whole voyage assuming grief would arrive as collapse. It arrived instead as a kind of terrible competence. I woke each morning in Callais and knew precisely what to do next, hour by hour, plate by plate, oath by oath, and understood that this clarity was itself the mourning — that he had trained me so thoroughly that even bereavement came to me as a task list. Wry, I told myself. Stay wry. But some mornings the composing stick shook anyway.

* * *

The atlas published on the feast of Saint Ambrose, patron of — I am not inventing this — beekeepers and candlemakers, which Maren Vosk declared fitting, since we had burned through both.

Two hundred copies. Rag paper. The title page read: *The Merrow Fen Atlas: Surveys, Charters, and Sworn Testimony Concerning Lands Purported Empty, Being a True Description Compiled from the Plates of Aldous Whitlock and Finished by Paloma Almeida, with the Attestations of Eleven Survivors.* Underneath, in smaller type, the sentence the Duke's lawyers would spend a decade trying to drown: *No portion of the within lands was unpopulated at the date of the Duke's grant, as shown by the surveys herein, which surveys the compiler signs under her own name and takes full responsibility for.*

Full responsibility. I have been asked since whether I hesitated over that line. I did not. A signature is a coastline you draw around yourself.

The eleven testimonies were bound into the same volume, so that no advocate could argue the maps were forgeries without calling the witnesses liars to their faces, and no one could dismiss the witnesses as sentimental rustics without attacking documents certified by Callais notaries. Aldous's confession closed the book. The armor-passage included. Every orientation defended.

We sold the first copy to a shipping merchant who bought it, he said, because his grandfather had been born in a village whose name he could never find on any map. Ivo stood in the corner of the shop while the man paid, and afterward he walked out to the harbor wall and was sick over the side, quietly, with dignity, the way he does everything that costs him.

I went and stood beside him, not too close. We had gotten good at distances.

"My father's name is in the margins," he said, when he could. "Household twelve. Reyes. Just a name in a list of households, like a coordinate." He wiped his mouth. "Twenty years I've been trying to kill somebody for that margin. Turns out all I needed was a printer."

"Revenge and publication," I said, "are both ways of making the past answer correspondence. Only one of them gets replies."

He almost smiled. From Ivo that was a toast.

Here is what nobody warns you about catharsis: it does not feel like triumph wearing grief's clothes. It feels like both at once, at full volume, permanently, and you simply learn to walk while carrying them. That evening the whole Merrow Fen remnant — all eleven witnesses plus children plus Sera Voss plus a sapling wrapped in sacking that rode in the boat like a passenger — crowded into Maren Vosk's narrow street, and someone produced a fiddle, and we ate fried fish off paper, and Sera Voss got drunk on cherry brandy with a printer's widow and told the story of the invented fog for the fourth time, louder each time, and I stood in the doorway of the press house with wet eyes and a laughing face and thought: *this is what victory looks like when it arrives twenty years late and missing one man. It looks like a party you can't quite attend.*

At midnight Sera found me anyway. She pressed something into my hand — a dried apple, wrinkled as a thumb, from the oldest tree, the mother tree, carried out of the fire in her apron those twenty years ago.

"For planting," she said. "Trees and truths are the same craft. You've proven it. Both need poor soil and stubbornness."

"Sera, I live above a print shop."

"So plant it on the roof. Truths have managed in worse conditions." She patted my cheek with a hand like a mooring rope and went back to her brandy.

* * *

The first copies crossed the strait within the fortnight. By midwinter, word came back in the way word comes back to free ports: banned by decree in the Duchy — which, Maren observed, was worth fifty copies of sales — seized at two customs houses and immediately reprinted by the seizing officers' own clerks, who could apparently read; denounced from three pulpits; quoted, carefully and without attribution, in a university lecture in Ostrand; and demanded, by name and number, by a bookseller in the capital of some inland kingdom I could not have placed on a chart five years ago and can now draw from memory, because Aldous taught me all of them, the ones that matter and the erased ones alike.

And then, in the gray last week of winter, a letter came to Vosk & Daughter bearing the Duchy's seal.

Ivo brought it up from the counting house and set it on my bench without a word, and we looked at it together the way you look at a tide coming in over ground you thought was high. Not the Duke's seal. Not the guild's. Something older — the wax stamped with the sigil of the Royal Historical Commission itself, the body that had come up the valley to certify erasure and left with nothing but a decoy atlas and a prisoner who slipped its fingers.

I broke it. Inside, one page, in a hand I knew almost as well as Aldous's — the small, precise, guilt-ridden script of Provost Halloway, the man who razored the archive and unlocked the cell and chose, at the hinge of his life, to fall on the side of the record.

It began: *Madam Almeida — the inquiry you have forced cannot be smothered, though men are trying. I write to tell you what they are trying, because I owe the commission's secrets to no one now. I am dismissed, indicted, and content. Come home, if you dare call it that. The hearing is set for spring. They have summoned me as a witness against you.*

*I intend to testify differently. Bring your plates. And bring, if you can find it in whatever remains of your forgiveness —*

The rest I read twice, and then I put the letter down on the bench among the type trays, next to a half-set page of Sera Voss's testimony awaiting its fourth printing, and I looked out the window at the harbor, where the gulls were arguing over something, and I said aloud, to no one, to him:

"Well, Master. The coastline's finished after all. Now they want to redraw it."

Ivo picked up the letter, read it through, and set it down again.

"Spring," he said. "That's not much time to prepare a witness."

"No," I agreed, reaching for my pen. "But then, we've never once had enough time. We just stopped waiting for it."

I drew a line across the top of a fresh sheet of vellum and began, in a hand I signed as my own, to draft our answer.
