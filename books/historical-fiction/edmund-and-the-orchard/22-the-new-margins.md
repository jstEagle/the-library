## 22. The New Margins

The sapling came up through the frost like a question no one had authorized.

I found it on a morning in late February, my second winter across the strait, when I went out to sweep the schoolroom step and there it was — Sera Voss's apple tree, the one she'd carried out of the burning orchard on her shoulder like a sleeping child, planted crooked in the frozen ground outside my door. No note. No footprints but her own, and those already half-filled with snow. She had not knocked. She had not said goodbye the autumn before, either, when she left for the fishing villages up the coast where the other scattered families had gone. That was Sera. She believed that some things should be given without witnesses, or they stopped being gifts and became transactions.

The tree leaned east, toward the sea and, beyond it, home. I decided to take this as instruction rather than accident.

By then the atlas had been banned for eight months, which is to say it had been read by everyone who mattered and most people who didn't. The Duchy's censors had done us the great favor of condemning it publicly and at length — four separate proclamations, each more florid than the last, one of which described my work as "a forgery of sentimental geography assembled by persons of disordered loyalty." Ivo had that proclamation framed. It hangs in our press house between the window and the stove, where visitors can admire it while warming their hands. He says no advertisement we could buy would have sold half so many copies. He is probably right. The third printing sold out before the ink was dry, and a bookseller in Vellport told me, with the delicacy of his trade, that demand ran highest among clergy, schoolmistresses, and junior officers — the three professions most likely to be disappointed by their governments.

But a book can be burned, and copies were. What could not be burned was the inquiry.

It began, as such things do, with a single inconvenient document: Aldous's confession of the falsified execution order, sworn before a notary of the free port, witnessed by eleven living villagers of Merrow Fen whose names the Duchy's own records insisted did not exist. A dead man confessing to a death that never happened, attested by people who had never been born. The Commission's historians called it impossible. Then a merchant captain produced a second copy. Then a clerk in the Duke's own chancery — a young man whose grandfather, it emerged, had come from the Fen — produced a third, along with a charter dated sixty years before the Duke's claim, bearing seals no one disputed because no one dared.

They convened an inquiry in the spring. They called it the Commission of Verification, which Ivo said was like calling an arsonist the Commission of Warmth. Three historians, two lawyers, and a soldier, sent to determine whether the land had been empty when the Duke took it. We knew what they would find, because we had made certain of what they would find: the true plates, the testimonies, the survey marks Aldous had cut into standing stones twenty years ago with a mason's chisel and a conspirator's patience. The stones were still there. Stones are stubborn that way. It is why I love them better than people, most days.

The inquiry has not yet reported. It may never report honestly. But it has been forced to sit in a room for eleven weeks and listen to farmers from a drowned valley describe their orchards row by row, tree by tree, and every word of it entered into a record that cannot be razored out, because too many copies exist, because Ivo Reyes learned his lesson about single sets of plates the way he learned everything — expensively, and forever.

* * *

My schoolroom occupies the ground floor of a converted salt warehouse, which pleases me more than I can explain. There is a rightness to teaching mapmaking in a building that once stored the means of preserving things. Nine apprentices, ages eleven to nineteen, drawn from the exile community and a few locals besides — fishermen's daughters mostly, and one boy, Piet, whose father died on the salt road the night of the culvert collapse, though Piet does not know I was there when it happened, and I have not told him. Some knowledge is a weight you choose your moment to set down.

I teach them the way Aldous taught me, which is to say I teach them the way he taught me and then I teach them the parts he left out.

"Rule one," I say on the first morning of every term, and nine faces look up at me with varying degrees of suspicion, which I count as a healthy sign. "A map is an argument. Anyone who tells you a map is neutral is either lying or selling something, and often both."

Piet raised his hand the first week. "Then how do we know if a map is true?"

"You don't," I said. "You ask who paid for it, who was erased to make room for it, and whether the person who drew it ever walked the ground. If the answers satisfy you, trust it until Thursday. After Thursday, ask again."

They laughed. Then I showed them the first exercise, the one I have made famous in certain circles and infamous in others: I give them a street map of this town, accurate in every particular, and I ask them to add what is missing. Where do the poor live? Where does the water actually go when it rains? Which houses have two families in them and one name on the door? Where did the plague dead get buried, and why isn't it marked?

The first year, they gave me maps full of careful guesses. This year, they went out and asked. Marta, who is fourteen and frightening, interviewed the harbor widow who remembers every hull that ever sank off the point, and drew me a chart of the drowned boats with names and dates going back forty years. The guild would call it unsurveyed. The guild, I am pleased to report, can call things whatever it likes from its side of the strait.

Because that is the other news, the small news, the news I carry in the pocket of my coat like a stone I keep meaning to throw into the sea: the Guild of Cartographers has struck me from its rolls. Formal notice arrived in October, sealed in wax of a red so aggressive it looked embarrassed. *For conduct unbecoming a member and publication prejudicial to the guild's charter.* Provost Halloway's signature was not on it. His successor's was — a brisk, ambitious man named Corvel whom I remember as a third-year apprentice who once wept over a mislabeled river. I hope the notice cost him something to sign. I suspect it did not. That is the difference between him and Halloway, and it is the whole difference.

Halloway himself wrote separately. One page, unsigned by any office, signed only with his name, in a hand gone shakier than I remembered. He thanked me. He said the inquiry had reached documents he had spent twenty years protecting and found them intact, and that he did not know whether that was his doing or mine, and had concluded it did not matter. He said Aldous had forgiven him, or claimed to, and that he had never learned to tell when Aldous was being generous and when he was being cruel, and suspected Aldous had engineered matters so that no one ever would. And then the last line, which I have read perhaps two hundred times:

*You asked me once why the archive pages were cut. I cut them because I could not bear to be the man who filed them. I am an old coward, Paloma, but even cowards know which drawer the truth belongs in. You put it there. Keep the key.*

He died in January. Ivo heard it from a shipping clerk and told me gently, the way you hand someone something hot. I did not cry, or rather I cried the way you cry for weather — for a thing vast and impersonal that shaped your life anyway. He razored the pages. He unlocked the cell. Both of those were Halloway, and I have stopped trying to make them fit in one man, because they don't, and pretending they do is exactly the kind of cartography I have sworn off.

* * *

In March, when the frost let go, I transplanted the sapling. It had wintered where Sera put it, leaning east, refusing to die, and I thought it deserved soil it might actually root in rather than the packed clay of the warehouse yard. Piet helped me dig. We moved it to the south corner by the schoolroom window, where the light comes longest, and I staked it and tied it with the same kind of cord Sera uses, the tarred twine of the fishing villages, and stood back feeling foolish and enormous feelings I had no intention of examining in front of an apprentice.

"It'll fruit in five years," Piet said. "Maybe six. Apples are slow."

"So are inquiries," I said.

He grinned. He has his father's grin, though he doesn't know that either, and I keep the ledger of what he doesn't know balanced against what he will someday need to, and some mornings the arithmetic is very hard.

Sera came to see it in April. She arrived unannounced, as is her custom and possibly her religion, carrying a basket of dried apples and a jar of honey from hives kept by the Ferrow family, who survived the Fen and now keep bees on cliffs the Duchy's maps describe as uninhabited. The bees, I feel obliged to note, also believe the maps.

She stood looking at the sapling for a long time. "It's crooked," she said.

"It grew crooked."

"It grew leaning." She crouched, touched the bark with two fingers, the way she touches everything — as if checking whether the world is keeping its promises. "Leaning trees fruit heavier. Everyone plants them straight and wonders why the branches break under the load."

"Sera," I said, "you cannot simply say things like that and then change the subject to honey."

"I brought honey." She handed me the jar. Then, quieter, looking at the schoolroom windows where nine heads were bent over drafting tables: "Aldous wrote to us about you. Twenty years, he wrote. Every letter, he said, *she'll finish it.* Not the atlas. I used to think he meant the atlas." She straightened up, joints cracking like green wood. "He meant you."

She stayed for supper, corrected my apple-drying technique without being asked, insulted Ivo's beard, kissed the top of my head on her way out the door — she is the only person alive permitted to do this — and walked back down to the harbor to take the night boat north, alone, the way she does everything, carrying cuttings from the sapling wrapped in wet cloth to plant in whatever ground the scattered families have managed to hold.

There are orchards now. That is the sentence I never expected to write. Small ones, secret ones, terraces above drowned valleys and windbreaks behind dunes, from here to the northern capes — thirty-one of them by Ivo's last count, each grown from stock carried out of Merrow Fen, each a copy of a copy of a copy of trees that were supposed to have burned. The Duchy can ban a book. It has not yet worked out how to ban a tree.

* * *

The new map sits on the great table in the back room of the press house, half-finished, and I work on it in the evenings after the apprentices go home, when the light goes long and gold through the salt-stained windows.

It has no title yet. Provisionally, in pencil, in the corner where a title goes: *An Atlas of Silences.*

The conceit is simple, which is how you know it will cause trouble. Where other maps leave blank space — the blank spaces that let dukes claim emptiness, that let commissions certify vacancy, that let whole towns burn without leaving a mark on the vellum — I draw the silence itself. A town erased gets not absence but presence: a symbol of my own devising, a small ring broken open, and beneath it, in letters smaller than any guild standard permits, the truth. *Merrow Fen. Population before: 340. Recorded afterward: 0. Actual afterward: 40, unrecorded, alive.* The fog Aldous invented on his chart to lead the families out — I have drawn it, from Sera's telling, a rendered haze along the valley mouth, labeled *fog, deliberate, merciful.* The routes of the scattering. The graves of the march. The orchards, all thirty-one, marked with the broken ring and the words *growing, unrecorded.*

Ivo watched me ink the first of these symbols last month, standing over my shoulder the way he does, breathing on my neck like a weather system.

"People will say it isn't a map," he said. "It's a testimony wearing a map's clothes."

"Yes," I said.

"They'll say the silences aren't surveyable. You can't triangulate grief."

"No," I agreed. "But you can locate it. That's most of the work. Everything else is decoration."

He was quiet a while. Then he said, "When I started looking for Aldous, I wanted the men who burned Merrow Fen to have their names in a record they couldn't burn back. That was the whole of it. Revenge with footnotes." He touched the edge of the table, not the map; he almost never touches the map, as if it were sleeping. "And now there's a girl in the Fen country teaching other girls to read, using your banned atlas, and a clerk in the chancery asking questions his superiors can't answer, and Piet's mother says her son comes home talking about broken rings. I don't know what this is anymore. It got bigger than the revenge."

"That's usually the sign it turned into justice instead," I said. "Or trouble. In my experience they're distinguished retroactively, by whoever writes the history."

"And who writes this history?"

I looked down at the map, at the little broken rings marching across the drowned valley like a constellation someone had tried to erase and failed. "We do," I said. "That was always the entire point."

* * *

Tonight I worked late. The press house is quiet except for the tick of Ivo sorting type in the front room and the sound of the sea doing what the sea does, which is insist. On the table, the Atlas of Silences has reached its northern margin — the border country where this whole affair began, where Aldous taught me coastlines I'd never seen and salt roads he'd stored in my memory like letters waiting twenty years to be opened.

At the bottom of the sheet, where the cartographer signs, I have ruled a line and left the space beneath it blank.

Apprentices always ask about that space. Marta asked just yesterday, frowning at it with the intensity she brings to everything, including breakfast. "Who signs it? You? Or Master Whitlock's memory?"

"Neither," I told her. "That line is for whoever finishes it. An atlas of silences can't be finished by one hand. That's how silences happen — one hand deciding it's finished, everyone else's excluded."

She considered this with the gravity of a judge. "So it stays unfinished?"

"It stays *open,*" I said. "There's a difference. Unfinished means abandoned. Open means listening."

She went back to her drafting table, and I stood at the window a while, watching the sapling lean east in the evening wind, its first blossoms just breaking — white, five-petaled, absurdly hopeful for a tree that has been burned, moved, frozen, and transplanted inside a single year. Sera says leaning trees fruit heavier. Below in the harbor, a boat was coming in past the point, running lights low, the way boats run when they're carrying something worth hiding, and Ivo appeared at my elbow with the particular stillness he has developed for moments when the world is about to hand us the next page.

"Courier from the Fen country," he said. "Wouldn't give a name. Says she's carrying a message for the woman who draws the broken rings." He paused. "Paloma — she's maybe fifteen. And she's got a guild seal on her satchel. The new provost's seal."

I looked at the blank line at the bottom of the map, and then at the girl waiting in the doorway below, rain-wet and resolute, holding out a letter sealed in aggressive red wax — and I thought: *open means listening.*

"Well," I said, reaching for the lamp. "Let's hear what the margins have to say."
