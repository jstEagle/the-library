## 1. The Ledger of Omissions

The winter Sable Lockridge came to the Quill & Anchor, they had one rule and held to it the way a drowning man holds a plank: *nothing attaches to you that you cannot put down.*

It was a sensible rule. Sable had learned it in the field hospitals at Salamanca and Badajoz, where attachments were the first thing the war took from you, usually by way of a fever or a cannonball, and where the second thing it took was your belief that any single life mattered to history. History, Sable had concluded somewhere between the amputation tent and the dysentery tents, happened to other people. It happened to colonels and cardinals and men whose names got printed afterward. It did not happen to nurses with ink-stained fingers and a cough that would not entirely leave. A person who understood this could survive anything. A person who forgot it buried friends.

So when the work at the riverfront workshop was offered — copying fair hands for a master printer named Garrow, mending his ledgers, keeping his accounts straight through the winter — Sable took it the way one takes a coat off a hook. Warm enough. Nothing in it.

"You'll want to be careful with the muster rolls," Garrow said on the first morning, setting a stack of printed sheets on the counter between them like a man laying down cards he hoped nobody would count. "They come from the War Office. They go back corrected. That's all you need know."

"All I need know," Sable repeated, and meant it as agreement, and mostly did mean it.

The Quill & Anchor sat at the edge of the Thames ice like an old dog at the end of its chain, half timber, half brick, smelling always of lampblack, tallow, and the river's cold iron breath. In the front room stood the press — a great wooden beast with iron teeth that Garrow spoke to more tenderly than he spoke to his own daughter. Behind it, racks of type in wooden cases, drawers of paper, and above everything, reached by a ladder that groaned like a ship in weather, the type loft, where finished plates were stored in oiled cloth like relics in a crypt.

Garrow's daughter, Marigold — a name she plainly despised and answered to anyway, with the air of someone doing the world a favor — worked the composing stick beside her father. She was nineteen, quick-eyed, and had taken Sable's measure inside the first hour.

"You were a nurse," she said. Not a question.

"Was."

"Army?"

"Does it matter?"

"No." She handed him a rag soaked in turpentine. "Only that you'll have seen worse than our ink. Father worries about offending people. I don't see the point of worrying about it before they're actually offended."

Sable decided, cautiously, that he liked her, and then immediately decided to stop liking anyone, per the rule.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble usually does, with arithmetic.

Sable's task each week was simple: the regimental agents sent down their muster rolls to be printed — names, ranks, pay due, pay rendered — and Sable checked the printer's proofs against the agents' manuscripts before the press ran them. It was dull, exacting work, and Sable was good at it, because after three years of field service there was very little left in the world that frightened Sable Lockridge except inaccuracy.

By the second week, the numbers had begun to itch.

A private named Hollis appeared on the manuscript but not on the proof. Then a corporal named Finch. Then, in the same roll, two whole lines of the manuscript had been quietly omitted — not struck through, not marked as dead or deserted, simply *gone*, as though the compositor's eye had slid over them, though compositors' eyes did not slide; that was rather the entire point of compositors.

"Mr. Garrow," Sable said, carrying the proof to the press, "these two names are missing."

"Are they?" Garrow did not look up from his inking ball.

"They are. Hollis and Finch. And last week there was a private called Denny who vanished between manuscript and plate."

"Compositor's error, likely. Happens."

"It doesn't," said Sable mildly. "Not twice. Not three times. And never only ever downward — never a man added, always a man subtracted."

That made Garrow look up. He had a face like a wet sack of flour, gray and sagging, but his eyes were quick and unhappy. "Miss Lockridge. Nurse Lockridge. Whatever you were. Your work is the sums. The sums balance because I make them balance. What goes on the roll is what the agent pays me to put on the roll."

"And what the agent pays you to *leave off*?"

"I'm a printer, not a magistrate."

"No," Sable agreed. "You're a man who prints things, which makes you the closest thing to a magistrate most soldiers will ever meet. Nobody reads the War Office. Everybody reads the roll. If a man isn't on it, his widow can't prove he lived."

The workshop went quiet. Even the press seemed to hold its breath. Marigold had stopped mid-composition, a piece of type pinched in her tweezers, watching them both with the frank enjoyment of a girl at a play.

Garrow wiped his hands slowly on his apron. "You've been in the wars," he said at last. "You'll know, then, that wars run on paper the way mills run on water. And you'll know better than most what happens to people who dam the wrong stream." He turned back to his press. "Correct the proofs as given. That's the wage. Take it or leave it."

Sable took it. Of course Sable took it. There was a room above the cooper's next door, coal enough, and a cough that argued against sleeping rough in January. The rule held. Nothing attached.

But that night, alone in the cold room with a candle stub and the workshop's spare account book — bought honestly, at a stationer's, with honest wages — Sable found himself writing at the top of the first page, in a hand much steadier than the hand felt:

*A ledger of omissions.*

And beneath it:

*Hollis, J., private, 44th Foot. On agent's manuscript, 3rd inst. Absent from printed roll. No record of death, discharge, or desertion filed.*

*Finch, T., corporal, same regiment. As above.*

*Denny, W., private. As above.*

It was, Sable told himself firmly, nothing. A habit of accuracy. A nurse's reflex, the same instinct that made one chart a fever even when no doctor would come. Writing a thing down was not the same as *doing* something about it. Writing a thing down was how a person kept from doing something foolish. The page was a wall between Sable and the world, not a door into it.

History happened to other people. This was merely a list of the other people.

* * *

The weeks turned, and the list grew.

It grew the way ivy grows — slowly, then all at once. Sable learned to read the omissions the way other people read weather. A name missing near the bottom of a page, where the eye tired. A name missing in a batch printed just after the agent's quarterly visit, when Garrow's manner went stiff and his pipe went unlit. Once, a whole company's pay column reduced by a third between manuscript and proof, so smoothly done that only someone who had spent years counting out doses and bandages and coffins would ever catch it.

Sable caught it. Sable caught all of it, and wrote it all down, and told himself every night that the writing-down was the end of the matter.

Some nights the telling worked better than others.

There was a night in early February when the wind came down the river hard enough to rattle the shutters, and Sable sat in the workshop late, finishing a proof by lamplight while Garrow and Marigold were at supper upstairs. The roll open before Sable belonged to a line regiment just returned from the Peninsula, and the manuscript listed forty-one men under the heading *Wounded, remaining on strength.* The proof listed thirty-four.

Seven men, wounded, alive, erased.

Sable sat for a long time with the pen dry in his hand. He thought of tents, of the particular smell of a wound going bad, of men who had asked him, again and again, in the voice men used when they were afraid of the answer: *Nurse — will my name go home? Will they write? Will they know?*

He had lied to some of those men, kindly, because the truth was that nobody writes home about a man who has been subtracted. The army does not strike a bell for the erased. They simply stop appearing, the way footprints stop at the edge of a tide.

Sable dipped the pen. Wrote the seven names. Wrote the date, the regiment, the discrepancy, the sum of pay that would now never reach seven households in seven towns where seven women would wait by seven doors until waiting became its own occupation.

Then he closed the ledger, and blew out the candle, and sat in the dark listening to the ice groan on the river like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and admitted — privately, in the dark, where admissions cost nothing — that the wall had become a door. That he had been walking through it nightly for two months. That a person who keeps a secret ledger of stolen men is no longer a person to whom history happens elsewhere.

He was still sitting there when Marigold came down with a covered dish of supper she pretended was leftover and plainly was not.

"You're doing it again," she said, nodding at the closed book as she set the dish down. "Father thinks I don't know. I compose half those plates; you think I don't notice what comes out of my own stick?" She pulled a stool to the counter and sat, elbows on wood, chin on fists. "Three years he's been paying for the quiet. Since before Mother died. I used to think it was shame. Now I think it's fear, and I think somebody holds the leash, and I think whoever it is pays better than printing pays."

"Marigold—"

"Don't say it's nothing. You keep a book. People who think it's nothing don't keep books." She looked at him across the lamplight, and her quickness dropped away for a moment, and underneath it she was young and frightened and furious about being frightened. "Whoever they are, nurse — do you think they'd stop at seven names?"

Sable did not answer. Outside, the wind shoved the river ice against the pilings, and somewhere below the floorboards the dark water moved under it, patient, going wherever it was going regardless of what anyone wrote down.

"We print what we're paid to print," Sable said at last, which was Garrow's sentence, and tasted like it.

"Yes," said Marigold. "And you write down what they paid us not to." She stood, gathered the empty dish, and paused at the stair. "One day somebody's going to ask you for that book. I'd think, before then, about which side of it you want to be standing on."

She went up. Sable stayed.

Later — much later, past midnight, with the candle burned to a coin of wax — Sable opened the ledger one final time and added a line at the bottom of the February page, small, almost apologetic, the way one speaks a thing aloud in an empty church:

*Nineteen entries to date. All omissions favor the payer. None favor the man.*

And then, because the night was long and the river was loud and the rule had already broken without permission:

*I was wrong to think this happens to other people. It is happening here. It has been happening here for years, in this room, under this roof, in my own handwriting's neighborhood — and I am the only one writing it down.*

Sable closed the book and slept badly, and woke to a knock at the workshop door before dawn — too early for agents, too urgent for post — and a sound underneath the knocking that Sable would remember, all the rest of his life, as the exact moment the ledger stopped being a list of other people:

a child, crying in the snow.
