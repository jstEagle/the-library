## 2. A Child at the Door

The knocking had the wrong rhythm for a bill collector and the wrong hour for a friend, which left only trouble, and trouble in my experience always arrived wet.

I came down the stairs with the candle held high like something that might defend me — it has never once defended anyone, but the gesture steadies the arm — and put my eye to the peephole of the Quill & Anchor's street door. What I saw there made no sense at first: a hat. Just a hat, bobbing at the level of the letterplate, as though the door had grown a small black mushroom. Then the hat moved down, and under it was a boy.

He could not have been more than seven. He was not dressed for snow so much as *arranged* for it — a coat too fine to be his own, sleeves rolled three times apiece, and boots that had never met a puddle before tonight and were clearly losing the argument. His face was blotched red from crying, which did not explain why he had stopped crying the moment my shadow crossed the frosted glass. Children know when they are being looked at through doors. They learn it early, God help them, because the looking is so often followed by the shutting.

Behind him, on the step, the snow was filling in footprints. Small ones. And larger ones already going away up the lane toward the coach road.

I opened the door, because I am weak, and because the alternative was watching a child freeze while I debated philosophy with a bolt.

"No," said Mr. Petch, from the top of the stairs.

"I haven't done anything yet."

"You've opened the door. That's how it starts." Petch is the workshop's compositor, a narrow man assembled entirely of opinions and knuckles, and he descended now with the air of someone preparing to lose an argument and wanting witnesses. "It's the parish matter all over again. You remember the widow's dog."

"The widow's dog was not a child."

"It had the same eyes."

The boy stood in the doorway and dripped. Under one arm he clutched a canvas satchel nearly as big as he was, the kind printers use to carry type, and he held it the way soldiers hold their packs in retreat — not because it is comfortable but because putting it down would mean the retreat was over.

"Are you the nurse?" he asked.

"I was. In another life."

"Then you can't die of cold, can you? That's what nurses are for."

"That is not precisely the training," I said, "but you've grasped the spirit of it. Come in before your boots finish what the French couldn't."

* * *

We got him to the kitchen behind the press room, where the stove lives and therefore where all decisions at the Quill & Anchor are made, whether or not they concern stoves. Mrs. Harkaway, the master printer's daughter, arrived wrapped in a blanket with her hair down and her sleeves up, which is her state of readiness for any crisis, and took one look at the boy and began heating milk without asking anyone's permission. This is why the workshop functions. Not the press. The milk.

"What's your name?" she asked him.

"Edmund."

"Just Edmund?"

"Yes," he said, in a tone that suggested this had been rehearsed and we were not to pry further, an instruction delivered by a person the height of the flour barrel carries a certain comedy, but nobody laughed.

Petch sat opposite and conducted the interrogation he imagined was subtle. "And who sent you here, Master Edmund?"

"A man in a coach."

"What sort of man?"

"A frightened one," said Edmund, with terrible simplicity. "He kept looking out the window backward. He gave me sixpence and said knock loud. Then he said—" The boy stopped. He looked into his milk as though the rest of the sentence might be floating in it.

"Said what?" I asked.

"He said forget the face." Edmund frowned. "But I don't know whose face. There wasn't one. There was just this." And he pulled from inside his coat a folded paper, damp at the corners, and pushed it across the table.

Four words, in a good hand gone slightly ragged, as if written fast or drunk or both:

*Forget the face.*

No name. No direction beyond what the boy himself had supplied. A note that instructed us to forget something while accomplishing, with great efficiency, the exact opposite. Whoever wrote it understood many things about documents and nothing whatsoever about children, who are incapable of forgetting a face and incapable of resisting one either. Edmund had already memorized the frightened man in the coach, I could see it happening behind his eyes, filed away among the grand house and the letters and whatever else he carried in that satchel of his.

"We send him to the parish in the morning," said Petch. "That's the whole of it. Parish overseer, workhouse ward, done by dinner. It's not cruelty, it's procedure."

"How comforting," said Mrs. Harkaway, "that cruelty has a procedure now. Does it come with forms?"

"You know what I mean. We're a print shop, Hark. We set type. We're not equipped—"

"For children?"

"For *consequences.*" Petch's voice dropped, and he glanced at the door to the stairwell, where old Master Corben slept above his plates like a dragon of very modest hoard. "You know what comes through this shop. You know what some people would pay to know what else comes through it. A child left on our step with a note like that isn't lost, he's *discarded*, and whoever discards things like this comes back for them. I've seen it."

"So have I," I said, and everyone remembered at once that I had, and in worse places than a snowy doorstep, and the argument lost some of its footing.

Because here is the truth of me, set down plainly since this narrative insists upon it: I had spent four years as a nurse to line regiments, and I had learned the one discipline that keeps a person sane in a field hospital. You do not learn names. You do not ask about homes. You bandage the wound in front of you and you do not wonder whose mother embroidered the collar you're cutting away, because the moment you wonder, you are undone, and an undone nurse is worse than useless — she is a second patient. I came to the Quill & Anchor with that rule sewn into me like a seam. No attachments. Tend the body, close the ledger, walk on.

And here was the flaw in the rule, the crack I'd been pretending not to see since the war: it works beautifully right up until the body in front of you is small enough to fit inside your coat.

Edmund finished his milk. He looked around the kitchen with the frank inventory-taking stare of the very young — the stove, the hanging proofs, the cat (who permitted him), me — and then he yawned, an enormous, jaw-cracking yawn that no amount of fear could suppress, and leaned sideways until his head found my arm, the way iron filings find the magnet, without policy or deliberation.

I sat very still.

"Don't," I said, to no one in particular.

"Wouldn't dream of it," said Mrs. Harkaway, smiling at the stove.

"This is exactly the thing. This is precisely the—"

"Mm," she said.

The boy's breathing slowed and evened. His fist stayed closed around the strap of the satchel even in sleep, and I noticed that his other hand had found the hem of my sleeve and held two inches of it between finger and thumb, lightly, the way you hold something you have been told will be taken away.

I thought of the muster rolls. I thought of the names struck through in a clerk's careful hand, men reduced to omissions, erased by somebody's convenient pen — and I thought how easy it was, how *procedure-shaped*, to look at a child and see a problem to be routed elsewhere by morning. The parish. The overseer. Forget the face.

I am a nurse. I know every trick the mind uses to make a person into a task.

"Well," I said at last, and my voice came out rougher than I intended, "he sleeps in the proof room. Near the stove duct. And somebody — not me — finds him dry stockings before morning."

"Somebody is already doing it," said Mrs. Harkaway.

Petch threw up his hands, which in him constitutes surrender, appeal, and prophecy all at once. "Mark me," he said, taking his candle toward the stairs. "A thing left on a doorstep with instructions to forget it is a thing somebody means to collect. And collectors don't knock twice."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone up, I sat by the stove with my private ledger open on my knee and did not write anything for a long time. The rule about attachments sat in my chest like a swallowed coin. I examined it. I turned it over. I noted, with clinical honesty, that it had been cracked for years and I had simply been carrying the pieces.

In the end I wrote only this, beneath the struck names and the missing men:

*A boy called Edmund, age about seven. Left at this door before dawn, 14th January, in snow. Note in a gentleman's hand: "forget the face." Fed. Warmed. Not forgotten.*

I blotted the page and looked over at him — asleep in the nest of sacking Mrs. Harkaway had rigged by the warm duct, the satchel still gripped to his chest like a drum at a rout — and I admitted the thing I had known since I saw the larger footprints leaving up the lane.

Whoever brought him had walked away quickly. But nobody walks away from a colonel's-looking coat and a satchel carried like orders, and leaves a note about faces, unless the face matters to someone powerful enough to want it gone.

The boy stirred and murmured in his sleep, and the murmur had words in it, and I bent close to catch them.

"Papa said the papers would keep me safe," he said, to nobody, to the stove, to the dark. "*Where are the papers?*"

I closed the ledger quietly, and did not sleep at all.
