## 3. What Edmund Remembers

The boy slept for two days in the corner behind the drying racks, where the warmth of the press-room gathered like a held breath. Sable had told themselves this was triage, nothing more — a nurse's reflex, the body remembering its trade even after the mind had sworn off caring. He was half-frozen and undersized; you fed him; you let him sleep. Any sensible person would do the same.

Sable was not entirely sure they were a sensible person anymore.

On the third morning, Edmund woke properly, ate three eggs and most of a fourth, and looked around the workshop with the frank appraisal of a small general surveying new territory.

"This is where they make the paper soldiers," he said.

Sable set down their cup. "I beg your pardon?"

He pointed at a stack of muster rolls waiting by the door, each sheet dense with names in tidy columns. "Paper soldiers." A pause, and then, with the gravity of a child correcting a foolish adult: "They're not real ones."

"No," Sable said slowly. "They're lists of them."

"Lists are not people," Edmund said, and went back to his eggs, leaving Sable to sit very still with that sentence, because it was almost exactly what Sable had been telling themselves for six months, and hearing it from a seven-year-old's mouth made it sound suddenly like an excuse.

* * *

Edmund liked the type loft best. It smelled of ink and oil and the cedar blocks the plates were stored in, and it was full of small metal things arranged in rows, which suited him the way a stable suits a horse. He would sit on the top step while the printers worked below, and he did not talk much, but when he talked, things came out of him in pieces, like a dropped plate broken into sorts.

The house came first.

"It had a stair like a snake," he told Sable, drawing in the frost on the window with one blunt fingernail. "All twisty. And windows that went all the way up." He drew a tall rectangle, then stabbed at it. "But I wasn't allowed in the tall rooms. I lived in the little room past the kitchen. With the sloped roof."

"Were there other children?"

"A big brother and another big brother." He said *brother* the way other children said *weather* — flatly, a fact without warmth. "They called me the foundling. That's not a nice word. I looked it up in the colonel's dictionary."

"The colonel's dictionary," Sable repeated.

Edmund nodded, and something shifted in his face, some careful thing put down for a moment. "He taught me letters. Every morning before prayers, when the house was still quiet. He had hands like this—" Edmund held up his own small hands and spread them wide, "—and he'd say, *Now then, Master Edmund, a letter is a small thing, but enough letters are a fortress.*" The boy recited it exactly, with the cadence of a phrase heard hundreds of times, and Sable felt the first cold thread of recognition slide down their spine. Not the words. The cadence. Officers spoke like that. Men used to being obeyed spoke like that.

"What else did he teach you?"

"That I was to have my name written down properly. In a book. He said—" Edmund frowned, reaching for it, the fragment just out of grasp. "He said, *When the ice goes out, we'll do it.* But then the men came and argued."

"Argued about what?"

"Papers." His finger traced the window again, hard, so the frost squeaked. "I heard them through the wall. Loud voices. One of them was crying, which is strange, because he was the biggest one. And then the kind man went away somewhere and didn't come back, and the tall brother said I was to go on a journey, and the coach stopped, and the man with the lantern said—" Edmund's voice flattened into perfect imitation, empty as a struck bell: "*Forget the face.*"

"And did you forget the face?" Sable asked quietly.

Edmund looked at them with terrible directness. "No."

* * *

That night Sable could not sleep, which was nothing new; field service had left them with a mind that woke at three and began listing everything that could go wrong. But tonight the list had only one item, wearing a boy's face, sitting in their kitchen eating eggs.

Before dawn they took a lamp down into the plate room, where the copper stock was kept — portraits of local dignitaries, trade signs, the commemorative plates the workshop printed for funerals and weddings and civic dinners. If a man taught Edmund his letters, if a household called him *the foundling* and yet gave him a colonel's dictionary, then the man had money and standing, and men with money and standing sat for portrait plates.

Sable went through the drawers by lamplight. Aldermen. A bishop. Two MPs. A shipping magnate with an unfortunate nose.

And then: a colonel in full dress, square-jawed, gray at the temples, with hands — Sable saw it now, absurdly, in engraved lines no wider than a hair — with hands that looked like they could cup a child's whole head.

Beneath the plate, in the master printer's inventory hand: *Col. H. Ashworth, Ashworth Hall. Funeral plate, ordered 14th November.*

Ordered. Which meant dead. Everyone within thirty miles knew the name Ashworth; the great house upriver had dominated the county's business and most of its elections for a generation. Sable had seen the funeral notices roll off the press weeks ago, black-bordered, the family's grief rendered in handsome typography.

Sable stood in the cold room holding the plate and understood, with a dread that arrived all at once and completely, what *forget the face* meant. Not shame. Not embarrassment over a servant's by-blow. Erasure. A boy who could inherit, or block an inheritance, or simply testify — a boy whose existence was a document someone wanted burned.

And the papers. The argument behind the wall. The colonel gone away and not come back.

"Oh, you poor little fortress," Sable said to the empty room, and heard how their voice had changed. Six months of telling themselves that history happened to other people, and here it was, sleeping behind their stove, calling muster rolls *paper soldiers* with more honesty than any officer alive.

* * *

Edmund, upstairs, was not asleep either.

He lay under the quilt Sable had given him — it smelled of lamp oil and lavender, which was a strange combination, but he had decided he liked strange combinations now, because this whole place was made of them — and he thought about the kind man's hands.

He was trying to keep the face. That was the important thing, though he couldn't have explained why. The tall brother had said the journey would be an adventure, and adventures were good, and when he asked when he'd come home nobody had answered, and then the man with the lantern had said *forget the face*, and Edmund had understood, in the way children understand things whole and instantly, that forgetting was the price of surviving.

So every night he practiced. He closed his eyes and built the face out of pieces: the gray eyebrows like two caterpillars arguing. The deep line between them that smoothed away when Edmund got his letters right. The smell of snuff and cedar. *A letter is a small thing, but enough letters are a fortress,* and Edmund had decided that he himself would be the fortress, and he would hide the kind man inside himself where nobody could strike him from any list.

Because that was what the grown-ups did, he'd realized, watching them here in the paper place. They wrote names down and scratched them out. The big brothers at the great house had tried to scratch out *foundling* and write nothing at all.

Well. He was Edmund. He knew his letters now. He was keeping count.

* * *

In the morning Sable watched Edmund help Marta sweep the press-room floor — badly, cheerfully, getting in everyone's way — and made a private decision that violated every rule they had built since the war ended. Rule one: no attachments. Rule two: history happens elsewhere. Rule three, learned over too many cots in too many tents: you cannot save everyone, so learn to look away or it will hollow you out.

Three rules, and a seven-year-old had already broken all of them before breakfast, simply by existing.

"Marta," Sable said, low, catching the printer's daughter by the elbow near the stone sink. "The Ashworth commission. The funeral plates. Who handled it?"

Marta glanced toward her father's office, where old Herr Voss could be seen through the glass, hunched over his ledger, and something flickered across her face — quick, guarded, gone. "Father saw to it personally. Why?"

"The colonel ever sit for anything else? Anything... not printed?"

The flicker again, longer this time. "Sable. What's the boy to you?"

"Nothing," Sable said, too quickly, and Marta's eyebrows rose with devastating gentleness, and Sable heard their own lie hang in the steam above the sink and knew it was the worst lie they'd told since the war. "That's rather the problem," they admitted. "I think he may be everything to somebody else. Somebody who wants him gone."

Marta dried her hands very slowly. Through the office glass, old Voss turned a page, and did not look up — but Sable would swear afterward that his shoulders had stiffened, that he had heard, that he had known something all along and was sitting there in his office carrying it the way he carried everything: silently, and badly, and close.

"Sable," Marta said, barely above the sound of the water, "last autumn, before the colonel died, Father rode up to the Hall three times in one week. Three. He never rides once." She folded her cloth with unnecessary care. "Whatever you're going to ask me next — don't ask it in front of the boy."

* * *

Edmund, meanwhile, had discovered the drawer of misprints, and was sorting the ruined sheets into piles with tremendous authority, and had decided — privately, in the fortress of himself — that he was going to show Sable the face.

Not today. Today the tall brother might still be looking. But soon, when the ice started making its cracking noises at night the way it had begun to, when the river woke up — the kind man had always said things would happen *when the ice goes out*, and Edmund had learned to trust what the kind man said.

He pulled the top misprint from the pile and studied it: a list of names, one column of them struck through in red ink by some impatient officer's hand. Names crossed out. Paper soldiers, unwritten.

Edmund smoothed the sheet flat, very carefully, the way the kind man had taught him to handle a book, and set it on the top of the *keep* pile instead of the *throw away* pile.

Somebody ought to keep count. He was going to be somebody.

And in the office above, old Voss dipped his pen, opened his ledger to a page near the back — a page with a single line on it, dated last November, payment received and services rendered, the ink brown with age — and sat for a long time without writing anything, while downstairs a dead man's son swept the floor of the shop that had been paid to erase him.
