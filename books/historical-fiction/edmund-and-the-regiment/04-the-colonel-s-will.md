## 4. The Colonel's Will

There is a particular silence a workshop makes when it is keeping something from you. I had learned to hear it the way a sailor hears weather. The Quill & Anchor was never truly quiet — the press ticked as it cooled, the stove popped, Master Hollis hummed off-key through his nose like a man being paid to annoy the angels — but that February it developed a new quality of hush, a held-breath quality, and it centered entirely on the locked drawer in the proofing room.

I did not go looking for secrets. I want that on the record, since so much of what followed was people accusing me of going looking for secrets. The truth is that Edmund went looking for them for me, in the way of small children, which is to say by accident, upside down, and while eating something he shouldn't.

He had taken to following me on my rounds of the shop the way a duckling takes to a boot. He knew the names of everything now — "chase," "tympan," "quoins" — though his pronunciation turned *quoins* into something like *coins*, which delighted him so much that we let him believe the type was locked into place with shillings. On this particular morning he was sitting under the composing stone eating a heel of bread Hollis's daughter Marguerite had buttered with a generosity she did not extend to anyone else at table, and I was setting a page of the parish burial notices, when Edmund said:

"That's the paper."

"What paper, love?"

"The paper the men were shouting about." He pointed his bread at the stack of old plates leaning against the wall — surplus stock, misprints, plates awaiting melting down. "That one."

The plate he meant was face-down, as most of them were, stacked like grim slate tiles. I nearly told him to finish his bread and stop inventing stories. I am glad, in the way one is glad after falling and finding the ground was not there, that I did not.

* * *

We turned the plate together, because he asked, and because I had learned that when Edmund asked twice, there was generally a reason wearing ordinary clothes.

It was a portrait plate. Not an unusual thing in a shop that printed everything from hymnals to auction bills. But I knew this face. I had set text beneath it not three weeks past — a memorial notice, dignified, brief, paid for promptly by a family that wanted the whole business over quickly. Colonel Ashworth, of Ashworth Hall. Dead of a fever, said the notice, mourned by all.

Edmund touched the engraved cheek with one finger, gently, the way you touch something asleep.

"That's the kind man," he said. "The letters man. Only he looks crosser here."

"You knew him?" My voice came out very level. I have been told my level voice is worse than my sharp one. Edmund didn't flinch; he was still looking at the colonel.

"He taught me letters. In the long room with the windows." He frowned at the effort of retrieval, the way he always did, as if memory were a cupboard that someone kept rearranging. "And then the loud men came and argued about papers, and the kind man was already gone to bed forever, and Mama — Mrs. Hedges, I mean, not my mama, my mama is —" He stopped. He looked at me with those grave eyes that had no business on a seven-year-old face. "You're going to write it in your book, aren't you. The book of names."

I hadn't shown him the ledger. Children see everything; that is their whole occupation.

"Yes," I said. "If it wants writing."

* * *

Here is what I knew, laid out plain: the memorial notice had come to us with instructions to print five hundred copies and distribute none. That alone should have woken me earlier. A notice no one reads is a candle lit in a cellar. And here was the portrait plate, not melted down as finished work ought to be, but shelved among the discard stock — deliberately, I now understood, shelved where it could be found by someone who might someday need it, or lost by someone who hoped nobody ever would.

I carried it to the proofing room, inked it lightly, and pulled a proof. The colonel stared up out of the paper with the mild impatience of a man who has been made to sit still for an engraver.

Under the lamp, I finally let myself think the thought I had been refusing for a month: *forget the face.* A note left with a child. Not *feed him*, not *find his mother*. Forget the face. You write that only when the face is dangerous. When seeing it again would cost somebody something.

And the somebody was a dead colonel whose family had paid for a funeral notice they never intended anyone to read.

I sat back, and the chair creaked, and behind me the door opened and Marguerite came in with an armful of rag and stopped dead.

"You've found it, then," she said.

Not *what are you doing*. Not *put that down*. Found *it*, with the definite article, the way one speaks of a thing long expected.

* * *

Marguerite Hollis is twenty-six, built along the lines of a well-stocked pantry, and possesses the specific courage of a person who has spent her life being underestimated. She shut the door, took the proof from my hands, looked at her father's future in it for a long moment, and then sat down across from me as if settling in for the longest story of her life, which it was.

"Papa took the commission in November," she said. "Before the colonel died. It came by hand — a lawyer, London-trained, very fine gloves. Two documents. One was the memorial notice. The other was sealed, and Papa was to print fifty copies of it upon receiving word of the colonel's death, and hold them for collection by a Mr. Prosser, who would distribute them as instructed."

"And?"

"And word came of the death. And before Mr. Prosser could collect, another gentleman came. Younger. The son." Her mouth went flat. "Mr. Ashworth. He offered Papa three times what the job had paid to destroy the second document unprinted. And Papa —" She stopped. She rubbed her hands on her apron the way she did when the words hurt coming out. "Papa took the money. Or he says he took the money. He says he burned the copies."

"But."

"But he's my father," Marguerite said, "and I have watched him lie to excise men and bishops for thirty years, and he is a *bad* liar. He didn't burn anything. He couldn't. He put it in the locked drawer, and he has been walking past that drawer every day for two months like a man walking past a dog he's kicked."

"Why keep it? If he took the money, why not destroy it and be done?"

"Because it's a will, Sable." She said it patiently, as if explaining rain to me. "Fifty signed copies of a man's last will, commissioned by himself, entrusted to a printer because printers are the only people alive who can make a truth too numerous to smother. The colonel knew what he was doing. He'd seen what happened to single copies of inconvenient papers. So he had it printed in quantity, to be scattered like seed once he was dead. And Papa has been sitting on a field of seed for a winter, being paid to keep it in the dark."

My head was doing arithmetic without consulting me. "A will naming whom?"

"I haven't read it. Papa keeps the key around his neck." She met my eyes. "But I know what I heard through that door in November. I heard the gloves-man say the words *natural son*, and I heard Papa say *surely not the boy*, and then I heard nothing more because I made a great deal of noise dropping a tray outside, which is a skill our family practices like violin."

The room was very warm and very far away. Seven years old. Left at a printshop's door in the deepest cold of winter with a note saying *forget the face* — delivered, I realized, not abandoned. Someone had brought him to the one place in the county where a suppressed document slept, the one place the colonel trusted, and left him there like a living seal on an envelope. *Forget the face* — written by someone who hoped the household would keep him quietly rather than send him back to the great house, where faces could be recognized and erased properly.

"They'll come for it," I said. "When the thaw comes. Papers move when roads open. Someone will ask whether the job was done."

"Papa thinks so too. That's why he hums." She almost smiled. "Papa hums the way other men drink."

* * *

I want to say, in my own defense and before the record proceeds, that I did not storm into the master's office with accusations. I had eaten at that man's table for four months. He had taught me the difference between a good ink and a stubborn one, had wrapped my coughing chest in a mustard plaster when the winter fever went through, had called me *our Sable* in front of the vicar with evident pride. Whatever betrayal was in that drawer, it was not simple, and simple things are the only ones you may charge at.

So instead I knocked, and asked to learn about locked-job work — commissions held for clients, sealed printing, the etiquette of it. Hollis brightened the way he always did when teaching, drew out diagrams, explained the holding of plates, the signing of affidavits, the sacredness of a printer's custody. And then, mid-lecture, with the ease of a man talking himself round a corner he has been circling for weeks, he said:

"It's why I couldn't melt that portrait plate, you know. Custody. Everything belonging to a commission stays whole until released or lawfully discharged. Even the bits nobody thinks matter." His eyes came up to mine, and I watched him realize how much I already knew, and I watched something in him sag with relief, which surprised me more than defiance would have. "You've been to the proofing room."

"This morning."

"Marguerite."

"She didn't tell me anything," I said, which was true in spirit and false in every particular. "But the boy knew the colonel's face, Hollis. From life. Taught him letters."

The old man closed his eyes. His hands lay flat on the desk as if pressing something down. For a while there was only the stove ticking and the press cooling and, faintly, Edmund upstairs singing to himself a mangled version of a hymn about sheep.

"There's a will in that drawer," Hollis said at last, quietly, "that names that boy heir to Ashworth Hall and half the Ashworth rents, ahead of two grown sons who will inherit nothing but debts otherwise, and who know it, and whose grandfather's money is in my strongbox for the sin of knowing it. I took the money, Sable. I tell myself I took it to protect the shop — forty souls fed off this business, yours included. I tell myself a great many things at three in the morning." He opened his eyes, and they were red-rimmed and terribly tired. "The question that's been eating me since November isn't whether the will is right. It's whether a man who prints ten thousand pages of other people's truths gets to bury one true page because burying it is cheaper."

"And have you answered yourself?"

"No," he said. "But you have. I've watched you for months, our Sable. Writing your little book. Thinking I didn't notice." He fumbled at his collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord a key, small and heavy and black with handling. He set it on the desk between us and did not push it toward me and did not take it back. "Whatever happens to this shop happens partly because of what's in that drawer. I'd sooner it happened to me than to you. But I'm a coward with a bad conscience, and bad consciences make poor guardians. So."

I looked at the key for a long time. Outside the window the river lay white and silent, holding its breath, holding everything, the way it had all winter.

"Hollis," I said, "if I open that drawer, I don't open it alone. Whatever's in there, Marguerite sees it, and it stops being a secret in this house tonight."

"That," said the master printer, with the ghost of his old humor surfacing like a fish, "is the first sensible demand anyone has made of me since November."

* * *

The drawer smelled of oilcloth and iron. Inside, wrapped in three layers against damp, lay a packet of printed leaves, fifty in number, crisp as the day they left the press, each headed with the Ashworth arms and each bearing — my breath caught — the same signature, witnessed twice, in a hand I had seen on the memorial notice instructions.

*Last Will and Testament of Sir Edmund Ashworth, Colonel, of Ashworth Hall.*

*Edmund,* I read. The boy's own name, in the possessive clause of a dead man's intent. *my natural son, Edmund, known to me and acknowledged by me in these presents…*

Hollis had never destroyed it. The Ashworths' gold sat in his strongbox like a stone in a shoe, and the truth had slept fifty deep under oilcloth all winter, two floors above the boy it named.

Marguerite read over my shoulder, and her breath went out slow. Then, from the top of the stairs, where small boys are not supposed to be and always are, came a voice asking the question that would occupy us all until the ice broke:

"Is that my papa's name? It's got my name in it."
