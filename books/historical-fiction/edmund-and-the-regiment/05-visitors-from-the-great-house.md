## 5. Visitors from the Great House

The knock came on a Tuesday, which I have since decided is an untrustworthy day for knocking. Tuesdays are for deliveries and dull errands; nobody of consequence arrives at a printing workshop on a Tuesday before noon, and so when Master Hobbes opened the door with flour still — no, ink still on his hands, he was entirely unprepared for the man on our step.

I heard it from the composing room, where I was teaching Edmund to sort type without dropping it, a lesson that had so far produced two dropped letters, one bruised shin (mine), and a declaration from Edmund that the letter *Q* "ought to be ashamed of itself." At the knock I felt him go still beside me, the way small animals go still, and I knew before anyone said a word.

"Boy," I said, keeping my voice level as a shelf, "do you remember where the ladder to the loft is?"

He nodded.

"Then go up it now, quickly, and don't sneeze, don't hum, and don't have opinions about the letter Q until I come for you."

He went. He was quick about it, quicker than I liked, because quickness meant he'd been practicing escape in his head, and a seven-year-old who has practiced escape has practiced it for reasons no seven-year-old should need. The loft above the casting bench held the old type trays, the broken matrices, and enough dust to write one's memoirs in. It also held, if Edmund kept his head down and his Q-related grievances private, nothing visible at all.

* * *

Mr. Ashworth — the eldest, as I would learn; there were two brothers and they divided the world between them the way weather divides a sky, one thundering and one cold — came into the workshop like a man visiting his own property. He did not look at things. He *inventoryed* them. His eyes moved along the presses, the paper stock, the racks of plates, and I watched him find the portrait plate of his father among the stock and linger there half a heartbeat too long.

He was perhaps five-and-thirty, dressed in a greatcoat worth more than the workshop's yearly rent, and he smiled the way a knife smiles: all edge and no warmth, though very polished about it.

"Master Hobbes," he said, extending a gloved hand. "You will not remember me. I passed through years ago, with my father, when your establishment took on some commissions for the regiment."

Hobbes took the hand. Hobbes's own hand, I noticed, had gone the color of good cheddar.

"Sir. Yes. An honor. We — we do fine work. Muster rolls. Notices. Very regular work."

"So I understand." Mr. Ashworth turned, surveying us all: Hobbes; his daughter Marigold, who stood at the press with her apron wiped and her chin up and her eyes doing arithmetic; old Fenwick by the window, pretending to read proof he had already memorized; and me, at the back, being furniture. Furniture is a useful profession. I recommend it.

"I'll come directly to it," said Ashworth pleasantly. "My father died in December, as you may know."

"We knew," said Hobbes, and then, catastrophically, added: "We printed the funeral notices."

"Did you? Good. Good." The smile widened by a precise fraction. "In settling his affairs we've found certain papers unaccounted for. My father, you understand, was a careful man — meticulous — but he had habits of secrecy. He liked to keep matters out of the house. Out of solicitors' hands. He trusted tradesmen, oddly enough. Printers especially. He said a printer knows how to make a thing permanent or make it vanish, and respects both powers."

Nobody breathed. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked, and Ashworth's eyes flicked to the ceiling like a hound's nose finding scent. Marigold coughed — loudly, deliberately, a cough with real professional commitment — and said something about the damp making the beams noisy in February, and Ashworth let himself be redirected, which told me he was a patient hunter. Patient hunters do not lunge at the first creak.

"So," he continued, "we are making inquiries. A stray child was seen on the towpath some weeks back, headed this direction. A servant girl swears she saw the colonel's coach stop near this quarter one night in January. And my father is known to have left papers in trust somewhere along this street." He produced, from an inner pocket, a purse that clinked with the confidence of a cathedral bell. "Gold for information. Generous gold. For the child, if such a child exists, and for any papers — any papers at all — left in his care. No questions asked. We wish only to settle Father's affairs decently, as families do."

"As families do," repeated Hobbes, in the voice of a man agreeing to be robbed.

* * *

Now, here is what I learned about lying that morning, watching Hobbes attempt it: honest people lie badly because they tell the truth in the wrong order and apologize while doing it. Hobbes said, "No, sir, no child here, no papers either, none that I know of, that is, we print everything we're given, we wouldn't hold anything back, not that anything was given to hold" — and each sentence dug the hole a foot deeper until I expected him to surface in China. Ashworth watched him with the mild interest of a man watching a dog try to climb a ladder.

"You're certain," Ashworth said at last.

"Certain, sir. Quite."

"Because my father's correspondence suggests otherwise. He wrote — forgive me, I have it nearly by heart — 'the Quill & Anchor holds what must not burn until it cannot be unsaid.'" Ashworth smiled apologetically, as though quoting scripture at a funeral. "Poetic, wasn't he, my father. Soldiers often are. It's the waiting that does it."

The silence afterward had weight and temperature. It pressed down on the room. And it was Marigold — bless her forever, whatever comes — who broke it by saying, briskly, "Well, if papers turn up, sir, we'll know where to send word," in exactly the tone she used with tardy customers collecting ballads, and somehow that ordinary briskness did more than all of Hobbes's stammering, because it treated the whole visit as commerce rather than catastrophe.

Ashworth considered her. Then me, briefly — furniture, I reminded myself, dusty, unremarkable furniture — and then the ceiling again, one long slow look.

"If the child is here," he said, gently, terribly, "you should know he is not well. There is sickness in his history. Fever. My family feels a responsibility — we should like to see him cared for properly." He set a single coin on the counter. Not gold. Silver. A token, a down payment on our consciences. "For your trouble, whether or not you recall anything. People rarely recall things immediately. Memory is a funny animal. It comes home when it's hungry."

At the door he paused, half-turned, and delivered the sentence I would carry up the loft ladder with me an hour later:

"My father taught a boy his letters, I'm told. Near the end. Sentimental old man. If such a boy exists, he is not a bastard to us, do you understand — he is a *witness*. Witnesses to a man's last arrangements are legally inconvenient, and morally dangerous, and historically short-lived." A pleasant nod, taking in the room. "Good day. Mind the ice on your step; it's treacherous this year."

* * *

The coach wheels had barely faded before Hobbes sat down on a stool as if his strings were cut. "God in heaven," he said. "God in heaven."

"He knows," said Marigold. Her voice was steady but her hands had gone white on the press bar. "Father, he *knows*. That business about witnesses —"

"He suspects," I corrected, and heard my own voice come out harder than I intended, harder than the nurse's voice I usually kept. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything. If he knew, he wouldn't have knocked. He'd have brought men. He came to see which of us would flinch, and now he knows it's Hobbes, and he'll be back for the flinching."

"That's my *father* you're discussing like a bad joint of meat," Marigold began, and then stopped, because we both looked at Hobbes, and Hobbes looked at the floor.

"I never destroyed anything," he said quietly, to the floorboards. "I want that said plainly, whatever comes. I only *kept* what I was paid to destroy, and told no one, and called it prudence every night for four months." He raised his head, and his eyes were wet and old. "Sable. You knew. You've known since you found the sealed packet in the plate drawer. Why didn't you say?"

"Because saying it made it real," I said. "And I have spent my whole life believing that things that happen to other people stay other people's. It's a comfortable creed. It's also a lie, and there's a boy in my loft who is the living proof of it."

I went up the ladder then. Not quickly — slowly, giving him warning, the way you approach anything frightened.

* * *

Edmund was wedged behind the type trays with his knees drawn up, and he had done exactly as instructed regarding noise, having instead occupied himself by silently arranging loose letters on a tray lid into words. I looked at them upside down and made out *HE* and *WAS* and, alone at the corner, *Q*, presumably in exile.

"You heard," I said.

"Some." His chin was up, which was how I knew he'd been crying and lost the argument about it. "He said I was sick. I'm not sick."

"No. You're not."

"He said I was a witness. I don't know that word."

"It means someone who saw a thing, and can say it truly."

He thought about that. "I saw the men arguing over Papa's papers. The night the coach came. Two men, and one had Papa's ring on the wrong finger, and they put papers in a box and the box went to the house and I was sent to bed but I didn't go, I went under the stairs, and I heard Papa say *it must be printed, printed things can't be unsaid*." He recited it flatly, the way children recite things too heavy to carry any way but flat. "Was that wrong of me? Listening?"

"No, Edmund. It may be the most right thing anyone in this story has done."

I sat down beside him in the dust, and I confess I held his shoulder, attachment and rule-breaking and all, and looked out through the loft window at the river, grey and locked, holding its ice the way we had all been holding our secrets.

Because here is what fear does, once it has finished shaking you: if you are built a certain way — and apparently I am built that way, God help me — it hardens into something with edges. All winter I had written names in a ledger and told myself it was bookkeeping. But Ashworth had stood in our workshop and priced a child in silver and threatened him with adjectives, *witness*, *inconvenient*, *short-lived*, and somewhere between his smile and his leaving, my ledger stopped being a habit and became an intention.

They would come back, and next time not alone. We had days, perhaps less. We had a suppressed will, a guilty printer, a brave daughter, a stolen boy, and a list of cheated soldiers whose pay had been struck from the rolls like their names from memory.

It occurred to me, sitting in the dust with Edmund's exile-Q gleaming at the corner of the tray, that we also had a press.

"Hobbes," I called down the ladder, in a voice that reached every corner of the shop, "how soon could you set forty pages?"
