## 6. Names in the Ice

The trouble with a discovery, Sable Lockridge had learned, was that it never arrived all at once. It arrived in installments, like a bill from a tailor, and each installment was worse than the last.

The first installment had come three nights after the visit from the great house. The workshop had settled into its January rhythms — Master Quennel grinding ink with the exaggerated patience of a man pretending his hands were steady, Marta humming off-key at the composing stone, Edmund asleep upstairs with one boot still on because he refused to be parted from it — when Sable found the second key.

It had been hiding inside the first. That was the trick of it. The small brass key that opened the drawer where the suppressed papers lived had a hollow shank, and Sable, who had spent two years in field hospitals learning that everything worth finding was hidden inside something else — a fever inside a chill, a wound inside a uniform, a lie inside a compliment — had thought to look down the barrel of it by candlelight.

Paper. Rolled tight as a wick.

Sable should have woken Quennel. Sable did not wake Quennel. There are loyalties and there are loyalties, and the ledger had its own claims.

* * *

The reading took most of the night, and Sable took it slowly, because the document was written in the colonel's own hand — a soldier's hand, blunt and slanted, the letters of a man who had learned to write in a saddle — and because Sable kept having to stop and press a fist against their mouth.

*Item: I leave to the boy Edward, called Edmund, my son begotten in honesty if not in ceremony, the whole of Ashworth lands not otherwise disposed of, he being the only one of my children who ever asked me where the money went.*

There it was, then. The thing they had all half-known since the boy arrived with his orders to forget the face. But wills name heirs; wills do not usually explain themselves. This one explained itself at length, because Colonel Ashworth had apparently been the sort of man who saved his honesty for documents nobody was meant to read.

And further down, in a clause so plain it might have been carved:

*Item: I require that restitution be made from my estate to the undernamed soldiers of the regiment, whose names were struck from the muster rolls in the winters past, and whose pay was thereby withheld, which withholding I authorized through ignorance and my stewards converted through greed. Their names follow. Let them be read aloud in the parish church, every one, before any stone is cut for me.*

Then came the list.

Forty-one names.

Sable sat very still in the cold counting-room with the candle guttering and the river muttering under its ice, and felt something move in their chest that they recognized, after a moment, as anger — but anger of an unfamiliar make, anger with no floor to it.

Because Sable knew those names.

Not all of them. Not even half. But eleven of them — eleven — were already written in the ledger, in Sable's own careful hand, recorded over two years of noticing what the official rolls declined to mention. Corporal Whitlow, struck in December of '11, who had a wife in Millbrook and a limp he claimed was gout but everyone knew was shrapnel. Private Osei, struck the same winter, who had taught half the camp to whistle in parts. The Fenn brothers, struck together, which no honest clerk would ever do by accident, because clerks knew better than to orphan a mother twice on one page.

Sable had spent two years telling themselves a comfortable story. Mistakes, they had called them. Clerical error, fog of war, ink spilled on the wrong line. History happens to other people; the ledger is just tidying up around the edges of it. A nurse's habit of keeping track of the ones nobody else would count.

They were not mistakes.

The omissions had been arithmetic. Someone — several someones, with tidy handwriting and warm offices — had worked out that a dead-on-paper soldier draws no pay, and that forty-one such soldiers, over three winters, comes to a very handsome sum indeed. The colonel had caught it too late to stop it and too angry to bury it, and so he had done the only thing left to a man being robbed by his own household: he had written it all down and smuggled it to a printer, trusting paper the way other men trust God.

And the printer — good old Quennel, who ground ink like a monk and wept at sad ballads — had taken the family's gold and buried the truth in a drawer, and the drawer had sat ten feet from Sable's bed for two years while Sable wrote their little ledger and congratulated themselves on their detachment.

"Well," Sable said aloud, to the candle, "damn."

The candle did not editorialize. Somewhere upstairs, Edmund turned over in his sleep, and the floorboard creaked, and Sable blew out the light and sat in the dark for a long while doing sums of a different kind.

* * *

Morning came up grey over the river, and Marta found Sable asleep at the counting-room table with both documents under their cheek like a pillow stuffed with dynamite.

"You've the look of a woman who's been arguing with angels," Marta said, setting down tea. Marta said *woman* or *man* or *person* according to some private system she never explained, and today Sable apparently rated *woman*, which felt about right; women, in Sable's experience, were permitted a specific quality of fury.

"I've been arguing with arithmetic," Sable said. "Sit down."

Marta sat. Marta was twenty-four and had her father's broad hands and none of his talent for looking the other way, and when Sable laid out the will and the ledger side by side and showed her the eleven matching names, she read each one twice without speaking. Then she said, quietly, "Whitlow. I set his type once. Last year, a commemorative roll for the parish. His name wasn't in the official copy and Father told me not to ask why."

"Your father knew."

"Everyone knew something." Marta's jaw set in a way that was going to cause trouble in this family, Sable thought, and not small trouble. "I used to think it was shame. That the men had deserted, and the family hushed it."

"They didn't desert. They were deleted." Sable tapped the colonel's blunt clause. "Deleted is a word I learned from printers. You taught me, actually — you said a name isn't removed from history until the last copy burns, and even then the ice remembers." It was something Marta had said once, drunk on midsummer ale, laughing; she looked stricken to hear it returned to her in earnest. "Well. Here's the ice remembering."

Outside, the river groaned — a long, tectonic complaint, the sound of the freeze shifting in its sleep. Everyone in town swore the ice would break early this year. Everyone in town was always swearing that.

"What do you mean to do?" Marta asked.

"What the colonel ordered done. Print it. All of it — the will, the restitution, the forty-one names, read aloud in the parish church as required, and I'll stand in the pew and check them off." Sable heard their own voice and was startled by it: level, unhurried, the voice they'd used for triage. Underneath, less level: *they had a wife in Millbrook. He whistled in parts.* "And then the Ashworths ruin us."

"Probably."

"And Edmund becomes a bargaining chip, or worse."

"Also probably."

"And yet," Sable said, and found they were almost smiling, which frightened them more than the great house did. "Funny thing about probables. I've been dodging them my whole life. Kept the ledger precisely so the probables couldn't touch me — record everything, attach to nothing, let history happen to other people and write down what they wore when it did." They pushed the two documents together, will and ledger, edge to edge, like closing a wound. "Turns out I was never detached. I was armed. I just hadn't loaded it."

Marta studied them for a long moment. "You know what frightens me? Not the son. Not the gold. Father." She lowered her voice. "He paid the price of his silence years ago and he's been paying interest ever since. Men who've made one bargain with the devil hate being reminded of the rate."

"He'll come round."

"He'll come round," Marta agreed. "The question is whether it'll be toward the press or toward the door."

* * *

Quennel came round, eventually, toward neither. He came round toward the window, where he stood for the better part of an hour that afternoon with his back to them, watching the ice, while Sable read the relevant clauses aloud — calmly, fairly, giving the old man the courtesy of hearing his own guilt described in full.

When Sable finished, the silence had a texture to it, like wet wool.

"I told myself it was protection," Quennel said at last, not turning. "The family came to me before the funeral. Not the son — the steward, which is worse; a son sins from passion, a steward from habit. They paid for destruction of the plates. I destroyed plates, yes. Copies of copies, ones I made to satisfy them." His shoulders moved — something between a shrug and a shudder. "The originals I hid. I told myself that was courage. Keeping the truth safe for a better season."

"It was," Sable began.

"It was cowardice wearing courage's coat," Quennel said, "and you know tailoring, Nurse Lockridge, so you know a borrowed coat when you see one. A truth locked in a drawer protects no one. It doesn't protect the boy. It didn't protect the boy's father from being buried under lies. And it has sat there," his voice cracked, "for two years, ten feet from my daughter, while I recited the psalms about the poor and oppressed of the earth on Sundays with particular feeling."

He turned then. He was an old man and he looked older, but his eyes, when they fixed on Sable, had the stubbornness of type metal — the stuff doesn't bend, it either holds or it cracks.

"The press runs slow in winter," he said. "Cold type, cold ink, stiff paper. To print a will properly — enough true copies to survive burning, witnessed, dated, distributed before anyone can stop us — that is four days' work if nothing goes wrong, and everything will go wrong."

"Four days," Sable said.

"Three, if Marta sets the names and I work the press and you keep watch." He looked at the ledger, open on the table, forty-one names in another hand and eleven in Sable's. "The colonel asked for the names read aloud in church. Read aloud. Not printed." A ghost of the old craftsman's smile. "A man after my own heart. Ink can be burned. A room full of people who have *heard* their neighbors' names — that cannot be unprinted from their ears."

"Then we do both," Sable said.

"We do both." Quennel nodded slowly, and then, because he was who he was, he crossed to the composing stone and began sorting type with hands that had stopped pretending to shake. "But understand what you've lit, Lockridge. This stops being about the boy the moment the first sheet dries. There are men in this county whose whole fortune is built on those missing names — stewards, paymasters, half the commission of the peace. When we print, we don't embarrass one family. We audit an entire war."

The words settled over the workshop like the weather coming in.

Sable thought of the ledger's first page, written two years ago in a hospital cot with a stolen pencil: *Names omitted from the December roll, being noted here so that someone, someday, might account for them.* They had believed it then. Tidying. Bookkeeping for ghosts.

Now the account had come due, with interest, and the debtors were coming to collect it from them.

Above them, a floorboard creaked — the third step from the top, the one that announced Edmund's midnight wanderings to the whole house. Small feet padded to the landing. Small breath held, listening.

Sable looked up at the ceiling, and the ceiling offered no opinion, but the river did — a sharp crack, loud as a pistol shot, somewhere out under the greying ice.

Four days, Quennel had said.

Sable privately doubted they'd be given three.
