## 7. The Split

Edmund had learned where the best listening place in the Quill & Anchor was: the little cupboard under the stairs where damp proof-sheets were stored. It smelled of ink and wet wool, and if he pulled his knees up just so, he could see through the gap between the boards all the way to the long table where the grown-ups argued.

And tonight they were arguing.

He hadn't meant to hide there. He had been hiding from supper, because it was barley soup again and the barley had a way of looking at him reproachfully, and then the door had banged and everyone had come in stamping snow off their boots, and by then it was too late to come out without being seen. So he stayed. He was getting rather good at staying.

Sable came in last, shaking sleet from their coat, and set something down on the table with a click that made even Edmund's stomach tighten. He knew that sound now. Papers. Papers were never good news in this house, however much Sable loved them.

"Say it again," said Master Hollis, the old printer. His voice had a crackle in it, like paper itself about to tear. "Say the whole of it slowly, so I can be certain I've gone deaf as well as old."

"The will is real," Sable said. "It's been sitting in plate form in your back room for two years. Colonel Ashworth paid for three hundred copies to be distributed — to the magistrate, the solicitor, the regimental agents, every name on this list. And instead it went into the drawer, and the drawer went into the dark, and we printed his muster rolls with half the pay-lines struck out." A pause. Edmund could see Sable's hands flat on the table, very still, the way they went still when they were angriest. "The omissions weren't mistakes, Master Hollis. They were wages. Stolen wages, signed into theft by a dead man who is currently paying you to keep quiet about it."

Nobody spoke. In the cupboard, Edmund held his breath. He knew the word *Ashworth*. It lived inside him somewhere near the word *Father* and the word *the kind man with the good pen*, and hearing it out loud made all those words shift around uncomfortably, like furniture being moved in a dark room.

"The money," began Tice, the youngest compositor, whose job seemed to consist mostly of having opinions.

"There is no money yet," snapped Marren, Hollis's daughter. She was tall and had ink permanently under her fingernails and Edmund loved her fiercely, second only to Sable, though he would have died before saying so. "There is a *promise* of money. There is a difference the width of the whole world."

"They offered forty pounds," said Tice. "Forty! That's four years of—"

"Of looking over our shoulders," Marren finished. "Forty pounds to spend the rest of our lives waiting for the day the Ashworths decide loose ends are cheaper than promises."

Master Hollis had not sat down. That was how Edmund knew it was bad. The old man always sat when things were merely tiring; he stayed standing only when they frightened him. He stood at the head of the table now with his hands behind his back like a man at a funeral he wasn't sure he was welcome at.

"You don't understand what they are," he said at last. "You're young. Ashworth Park has stood above this town for two hundred years. When the family wants a man ruined, they don't send ruffians. They send *clerks*. And the clerks are worse."

"Then we print it anyway," said Marren.

"And by Friday this workshop is a chimney!"

* * *

From the cupboard, Edmund watched Sable's face do something complicated. He had begun, in these weeks, to think of faces as books — Sable's most of all, because Sable tried hardest to keep theirs shut, which somehow made it easier to read. Right now the pages were turning fast: worry, guilt, something fierce and stubborn underneath.

"I took an oath once," Sable said quietly. "When I came off the campaign service. Not a grand one. I swore it to myself over a grave I dug with my own hands, that I would stop pretending other people's history was none of my business. I've broken that oath every day since, in small ways, because breaking it keeps a roof over—" Sable stopped. Looked at the ceiling. Started again. "I am not going to tell any of you what to do. I'm the newest hand here. But I will tell you what I know. I know that ledger downstairs has eleven names in it. Eleven men who fought and bled and came home to find themselves never existed. And I know that boy upstairs"—Edmund's heart slammed—"*is not a stray.* He's the thing all this machinery was built to erase. If we take the forty pounds, we're not surviving the winter. We're just dying more slowly, and in worse company."

"That's easy for you," Tice muttered. "You arrived with nothing. We have something to lose."

"Yes," Sable agreed, and there was no anger in it at all, which somehow made it land harder. "That's exactly what makes it hard. I'm sorry for that. I'm sorrier than you know."

Master Hollis sank into his chair. For a long moment he looked older than the building.

"I held the colonel's hand when they gave me the plates," he said. "Right here in this room. He was dying and he gripped my wrist like a vice and he said, *Hollis, print it true, print it everywhere, let them read my name in every corner of the county.* And I took his money and I put his truth in a drawer." His voice wavered. "Two years I've told myself a dead man can wait. Now there's a living child in the balance and I find the excuse doesn't fit anymore. God forgive me. God forgive me, but I'm afraid."

"Being afraid isn't the sin," said Marren gently. She put her hand over her father's. "Printing it anyway while you're afraid — that's the whole trick of courage, Da. Nobody's ever found a better one."

* * *

Edmund's legs had gone to sleep entirely. He shifted, trying to restore them, and his boot heel knocked against the board.

The knock was very small. He was nearly certain it was very small.

But Sable's head turned toward the staircase the way a bird dog turns, and Edmund understood, with the special clarity available only to children who have just been extremely foolish, that the argument was about to acquire an audience.

"It's me," he said, deciding honesty was the lesser humiliation, and backed out of the cupboard into the light, blinking, hair full of dust, one foot entirely numb so that he listed to port like a small ship taking water.

"Edmund." Sable crossed the room and crouched to his level. Their voice was careful. "How much of that did you hear?"

Edmund considered lying, rejected it as beyond his current acting abilities, and settled on: "The part about me. And the part about the chimney. Is the workshop going to be a chimney?"

"No, love." Sable glanced up at the others. Something passed among the adults, quick and silent, the way weather passes. "No. Nobody's decided anything yet."

"Well, I've decided," Edmund announced. His numb foot chose that moment to wake up, filling with pins, but he pressed on valiantly. "If printing it makes us a chimney, then we should print it anyway, because chimneys are how smoke gets *out*, and maybe the smoke is the lie, and—" He lost the thread of his own metaphor but not its conviction. "Anyway, the kind man with the good pen said I mattered. You all say it too, only quieter. So do the loud version."

There was a terrible pause, and then Marren laughed — a real laugh, wet-eyed, helpless — and even Tice cracked a smile, and Master Hollis rubbed his face with both hands and made a sound that might have been a sob or might have been the world's oldest man laughing.

"Out of the mouths," the old man murmured. "Out of the mouths of babes, indeed." He looked around the table, and Edmund, watching from the floor, saw something settle in him like type being locked into its chase. "Vote. All of us. No proxies, no cowards, no half-measures. Print the colonel's will and the restored rolls, and hang the cost — or take the Ashworth gold, and may we choke on it slowly. Hands for printing."

Marren's hand went up first. Then Sable's. Then, after a heartbeat in which Edmund forgot to breathe, Tice's — "Well, I'm not choking on anything slowly, thank you" — and then, trembling, rising like a man lifting something far heavier than an arm, Master Hollis's own.

"Carried," he said hoarsely. "God help us all. Carried."

* * *

Later — much later, after the candles had been pinched and the house had gone blue and quiet with the small hours — Edmund lay in his trundle bed listening to the press below begin to move. Thump, slide, thump. A heartbeat made of iron. Sable had come up to tuck the blanket and thought him asleep; he had kept his breathing slow and childlike, another skill lately acquired, and heard them whisper, "Sleep, Edmund. Whatever happens, you're real. I've got it written down."

Now the press beat on beneath him like a great slow heart, and he was drifting toward sleep, warm at last, when he heard the sounds that did not belong.

Boots in the yard. More than one pair. And the low, carrying voice of Mr. Prosser, the steward from Ashworth Park, saying pleasantly, terribly, to someone at the door:

"Wake the household, if you'd be so kind. We've come about the child."
