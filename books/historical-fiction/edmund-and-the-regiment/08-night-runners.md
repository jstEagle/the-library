## 8. Night Runners

Sable had learned, in two years of field hospitals, to sleep the way a sentry sleeps — one ear above the covers, listening for the sound that does not belong. So when the knock came at a quarter past four in the morning, they were already halfway down the stairs with their boots in their hand.

Three raps. Paused. Three more. Not the pattern of the coal man, not the pattern of anyone friendly. Friendly people did not arrive before dawn with their knuckles and their patience.

From the loft above came a small creak — Edmund waking, or Edmund already awake, doing the clever still thing he did when he sensed weather coming. Sable paused at the foot of the stairs and put one finger to their lips at the dark, as if the whole house could see them.

Master Hollis answered the door himself, candle held high, and Sable stood in the shadow of the stairwell where they could hear everything and be nothing.

"Gentlemen. It is," said Hollis, with the doomed precision of a man consulting his conscience by clock, "a very early hour."

"It is," agreed a voice Sable did not know. Level, unhurried, the voice of someone paid by the day rather than the task. "We're looking for a child. Seven, perhaps eight years. Dark-haired. We understand he was left here."

"Left here? Good heavens. No."

Sable closed their eyes. Hollis lied the way a cart went over ice — with enormous effort and no control of direction whatsoever.

"There's a note," said the voice. "In a clerk's hand. *Forget the face.* Ring any bells?"

"None whatsoever."

A pause, long enough for Sable to count the second man's breathing by the door. Then: "May we come in and look? The family is generous to cooperative households. Very generous. There's gold in it. There's also," and here the pleasantness thinned like broth, "the alternative, which we haven't discussed yet because it's early, and we're all friends."

* * *

They had voted on this, in a manner of speaking, three nights ago around the kitchen table — which is to say Marigold had declared it, her father had objected, and Edmund, who everyone believed asleep, had sat on the top step with his knees hugged to his chest and heard every word. Sable had found him there afterward and carried him back to bed, and he had said, into their shoulder, with terrible calm:

"If they come, I'll go out the window. Marigold showed me. I'm light."

"You are not going out any windows," Sable had whispered. "If they come, you will be exactly where I put you, and you will stay there until I come for you, even if I don't come quickly."

"Even if you never come?"

The question had gone through Sable like a splinter working toward the bone. They were the person who did not keep attachments. They had written that rule themselves, in effect, in the ledger's margin, on the night they'd resolved to record other people's histories and have none of their own. And here was a seven-year-old asking them to promise the impossible, and here was Sable's mouth, entirely without authorization, saying: "Even then. But I will come. That's how ledgers work, Edmund. Every entry gets its answer."

So now, while Hollis fumbled his candle and his dignity in the front room, Sable took the stairs two at a time, lifted the loose board under the type cases, and got Edmund into the gap between joists where the paper stock kept warm.

"Not the window?" Edmund whispered.

"Not the window. Marigold will come for you. You'll know her knock." Sable pressed the boy's own boots into his arms and — absurdly, tenderly — smoothed his hair flat. "Count your breaths. When you reach five hundred, you'll be somewhere else and this will be a story you tell."

"Stories are your job," Edmund said. "Not mine."

"They're everybody's job," Sable said. "Some of us just get paid worse."

Then they went down to lie badly on Hollis's behalf.

* * *

Marigold Hollis came to Edmund's window from the outside the way she did everything — efficiently, crossly, and without being asked twice. She had a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, a boatman's coat that swallowed the boy whole, and a plan she had evidently rehearsed against her father's objections, since it involved details like *the third lock gate is iced over but the towpath beneath is swept clear by the wind.*

"The men are at the door," she told him, latching the casement. "Two at the door. There may be more watching the lane, which is why we do not use the lane. Can you run on ice?"

"I can slide on ice."

"That'll do. Hold my sleeve and don't talk. If anyone asks, you're my cousin's boy, half-deaf, half-witted, and fully my problem."

They went out through the paper-store and down the water-stair, where the river breathed under its lid of ice like something patient and enormous. The cold hit Edmund's lungs and made his eyes stream, and he decided — in the way he decided things now, having learned that grown-ups' decisions could erase a face — that he would remember this cold exactly, so that no one could ever take it from him either.

The towpath ran black and silver under a sky going grey at the edges. Marigold moved fast, the hem of her skirt stiff with frost, and Edmund ran in the pocket of her shadow, counting breaths the way Sable had told him. At forty he heard the horse on the road above. At ninety he heard it stop.

"Don't," breathed Marigold, and stopped too, folding them both into the black seam between a barge's hull and the lock wall.

Hooves on the road. A voice, bored and unpleasant: "Anything?"

And another voice, from further up the path — a man walking his dog, apparently, though no dog barked — saying something about eels, about the season for them, about how his cousin dealt in them, drawing the riders' attention up and away with the magnificent dullness of a born liar.

Marigold's grip tightened on Edmund's shoulder. He felt her heart going like a rabbit's against his ear, and understood that the eel man was hers — some cousin, some favor called in at four in the morning — and that courage, which he had thought was a large loud thing in stories, was mostly small and cold and quiet, and smelled like river mud.

They ran again. Behind them the ice sang — a long groaning note, like a struck string, as the river shifted in its sleep.

* * *

They took Sable at the paper-store door.

It was, in fairness, the plan. Misdirect: that had been the word agreed upon, though Sable noted, as hands took their arms with professional impersonality, that *misdirect* had sounded considerably safer by lamplight than it felt by starlight, with one's cheek being introduced to a stable wall.

"The child was never here," Sable said, which was true, and said it with the flat certainty of a nurse announcing a death, which unsettled the men more than a lie would have.

"Where is he, then?"

"Gone. Weeks ago. The master turned him over to the parish." Sable let weariness bleed into their voice, the honest exhaustion of a convalescent dragged from bed. "You've woken the wrong invalid. I keep accounts here, not children."

"Accounts," repeated the level-voiced man, and something changed in his face — the first genuine interest Sable had seen in him. "Yes. I heard there were accounts." He smiled, and it was almost friendly, which was the worst thing yet. "Mr. Ashworth would very much like to meet whoever keeps accounts. Wrap up warm, Nurse. The house has fires, but the road doesn't."

They put Sable in a cart, of all things — indignity stacked upon indignity — and the cart jolted east through the blue pre-dawn while Sable did sums. How long for Marigold and Edmund to reach the ferry cut: an hour, if the ice held. How long before the press could begin: dependent entirely on whether old Hollis, terrified and alone, chose his guilt or his skin. How long Sable could keep six angry inheritors talking with a ledger full of dead men's names.

The answer to the last one, Sable knew, was: exactly as long as the truth was worth more to them than the ledger was dangerous. Which meant Sable's job was not to hide the truth but to make it glitter — to hold up those struck-out names like a lamp and say *burn this and you burn the proof that your father cheated his own soldiers, and magistrates love a widow with a grievance far more than they love a colonel's son.*

Terror, Sable found, was survivable if you treated it as arithmetic. Sacrifice was harder, because sacrifice meant trusting other people — Marigold's cold hands, Edmund's counting breaths, a trembling old printer and a press older than all of them — and Sable had spent years ensuring they needed no one.

The great house rose out of the mist like something drawn by a child who had been told what wealth looked like. Candles in every window, burning money at four in the morning out of pure habit.

"Welcome," said the level-voiced man, handing Sable down like a guest. "He's awake. He's always awake, poor devil, since his father died. He wants to know what you know."

"Then he wants a great deal," said Sable, clutching the ledger through their coat, "because I know every name his family paid to lose."

* * *

Edmund reached the ferry cut as the sky turned the color of whey.

Marigold hauled him down the frozen slipway to where a flat-bottomed boat waited, iced to its mooring, and a woman — the eel man's wife, presumably, wrapped in three shawls — was breaking the ice from the gunwale with a boathook.

"Across and up the church lane," Marigold told him, kneeling to look him in the eye. Her breath came in white flags. "Mrs. Petley keeps the vestry warm. You say nothing to nobody except *I'm expected.* Can you do that?"

Edmund nodded. Then, because stories were his job now too: "Are you coming?"

"I'm going back," Marigold said, and her chin did the stubborn thing her father's did. "There's a press that needs starting, and my father needs telling, and neither of them will move for anyone but me. You cross that river, boy, and you get witnessed. That's your work this morning. Mine's the ink."

She kissed the top of his head, briskly, as if administering medicine, and pushed him toward the boat.

Edmund looked back only once, from midstream, when the ice sang again — that long groaning note underneath the hull, the river turning in its sleep toward spring. On the far bank, small in the grey, Marigold was already a running figure headed home to the workshop, where the lamps, he could see even from the water, had begun to glow in the print room window.

Someone was lighting the press.

And ahead of him, on the near bank, past the church lane, past Mrs. Petley's warm vestry, stood the great house — every candle burning — where Sable was, at that moment, opening a ledger in front of people who had paid to forget.

Edmund counted his breaths. At five hundred, he stepped onto solid ground, and started walking toward whatever came next.

The ice began to crack.
