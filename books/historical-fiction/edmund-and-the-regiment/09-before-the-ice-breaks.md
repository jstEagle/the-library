## 9. Before the Ice Breaks

The great house smelled of beeswax and cold stone, the way churches do when they have given up on being warm. Sable stood in the long gallery with their hands bound loosely before them — a courtesy binding, as if Mr. Ashworth's men had been taught manners along with their violence — and counted the portraits. Eleven ancestors. Not one of them Edmund.

"You might have spared yourself the journey," said Reginald Ashworth, pouring two glasses of wine he did not offer to share. "My man could simply have driven you back at dawn with an apology and five pounds. That is generally how these evenings end."

"I've had better offers," Sable said. "Worse wine, though. Usually."

He smiled without warmth and set down the decanter. Behind him, in the corner of the gallery, a fire burned in a hearth large enough to roast an ox, and Sable thought — with a clarity that surprised them — that this was where all the missing pay had gone. Into marble floors. Into wine nobody shared. Into eleven painted men looking down at a nurse from the riverfront who had made the mistake of writing things down.

"The ledger," Ashworth said, nodding at the book tucked under Sable's arm — they had refused to surrender it, and his men, uncertain whether a ledger counted as a weapon, had let it be. "I'm told you keep accounts of my father's regiment. Names. Dates."

"Names that were removed," Sable said. "Forty-one of them. Men who served and were struck from the rolls so their wages could stay in your family's pocket. I imagine your father told himself it was tidiness. The dead are so much easier to manage on paper when there are fewer of them."

"My father was a careful man."

"He was a thief with excellent handwriting. And he knew it." Sable opened the ledger, and the sound of the spine cracking was very loud in the gallery. "That's why he wrote the will the way he did. Restitution first, inheritance second. He wanted the names restored and the money returned before anyone inherited so much as a teaspoon. Your brother the lawyer will confirm it — the document is properly witnessed, properly sealed, and it has been sitting in a printer's workshop for two years because you paid a frightened old man to burn it."

Ashworth moved then — quickly, for a courteous man — and closed the distance between them. Up close he smelled of brandy and something sharper. Fear, perhaps. Sable had smelled fear on dying soldiers often enough to recognize it dressed up in good wool.

"Here is what happens now," he said quietly. "You will give me the ledger. Then you will tell me where the child is. Then you will forget his face, which I believe was the original instruction, and which would have spared everyone considerable trouble."

"Everyone except forty-one men and one boy."

"Everyone who matters."

And that was the sentence, Sable thought, that history always turned on. Everyone who matters. They had said it themselves once, in a field hospital at Salamanca, closing their heart like a door: history happens to other people. It had taken a workshop full of ink-stained fools and a boy who slept with his boots on to teach them otherwise.

"No," Sable said.

* * *

At the Quill & Anchor, the press did not stop.

Edmund sat on the stairs above the printing floor, wrapped in Mrs. Hartnell's second-best shawl, watching the old machine thunder like a heart that had finally decided to beat honestly. His friend the master printer — Mr. Hartnell, whose guilt had made him silent for two years and whose daughter's fury had made him brave for one night — stood at the stone pulling proofs by candlelight, checking each sheet against the colonel's will with tears running freely into his beard.

"Mind the forme," he kept muttering. "Mind the forme. Two years I let this sit and now I mind the forme like a drunkard minds his feet."

"It's straight, Papa." Tabitha Hartnell stood at the composing stick, setting the restored muster names in metal, letter by letter, her fingers black to the wrist. She read each name aloud as she set it — *Corporal Whitlock. Private Amos Dunn. Private Elias Ferris* — the way other people say prayers.

Edmund knew some of those names. They were in Sable's book, and Sable had let him hold the book sometimes, at night, when the house creaked and the river muttered under its ice. Forty-one names. He had practiced reading them until Sable said he read like a clerk of ten years' standing, and Edmund had decided this was the finest thing anyone had ever said to him.

Now, downstairs, the sheets came off the press damp and gleaming. *The last will and testament of Colonel Edmund Ashworth.* Named for him too, Tabitha had explained. The colonel had named his son after himself and then given everything to the boy instead. Edmund wasn't sure whether this made the great house more or less his family, and had decided not to decide until morning.

The knock came at half past three.

Not a knock, really — a pounding, fists on the doorframe, and Tabitha blew out half the candles in one breath and pointed Edmund up the ladder to the type loft while her father went to the door making the terrible face of a man rehearsing innocence.

"We know the child is here," said a voice from the dark street. "We know the press is running. Open the door, Hartnell, or we open it for you, and then we open *you*."

Edmund, in the loft, lay among the trays of type and did not breathe. Below, he heard Tabitha unbar the door herself — her father apparently struck dumb — and heard her say, in a voice far steadier than Edmund could have managed:

"The press is running, yes. You're welcome to come in and read what it's printing. There's a magistrate already on his way to do exactly that. He should arrive around dawn, when the ice goes out. You may wait if you like. There's tea."

There was a silence in which Edmund was quite certain grown men were being defeated by a girl of nineteen.

Then someone said, low: "Burn it. Burn it now, before—"

And Edmund smelled smoke.

* * *

In the gallery of the great house, Sable heard the mantel clock strike four and understood, with a soldier's instinct for time, that somewhere across town a press was running and every hour they could steal here was an hour of honest paper.

"You're waiting for dawn," Ashworth observed. He had not touched his wine. "Why?"

"Because dawn is when the ice breaks," Sable said. "It's been groaning all week — you'd hear it too if your windows weren't so expensive. When it goes, the whole valley hears it. And my friends arranged for a certain gentleman with a certain warrant to be standing at your gate when it does. Magistrates love an audience. It saves on explanations."

"You're bluffing."

"Probably," Sable agreed pleasantly. "But you can't afford to find out, can you? Because if I'm lying, you lose nothing by letting me stand here. And if I'm telling the truth, then every minute you delay turns a family embarrassment into a criminal proceeding. Your father, whatever else he was, understood arithmetic. Try it."

For a long moment Reginald Ashworth looked at the nurse from the riverfront the way a man looks at a card he cannot afford to call. Outside, faint but unmistakable, came a sound like a giant turning over in bed — the ice on the river, shifting, complaining, preparing to speak.

Sable turned to the window. "Listen. Even the river's testifying."

* * *

The fire started small, which saved them.

A rag, oil-soaked, thrust through the window of the paper store. But paper stores want nothing to do with fire — paper smothers it, starves it, makes it work for every inch — and Tabitha Hartnell had fought a press-room fire once before, at fourteen, and had not screamed either time. She had sand buckets at every corner of the shop because her father was a coward about fire but a fanatic about preparation, and cowardly fanatics, she had often said, make the best survivors.

Edmund came down the ladder before anyone could stop him, seized a bucket, and threw sand with the grim concentration of a boy doing arithmetic. The smoke bit at his eyes. Somewhere behind him Mr. Hartnell was beating at the sill with a wet blanket and shouting scripture at it, none of it appropriate, and Tabitha was everywhere at once, and the fire hissed, guttered, considered its options, and died in the corner of the store with only four reams of paper lost and one scorch mark on the floorboards that Edmund would point out to visitors for the rest of his life.

"There," Tabitha panted, sitting down hard on the sand bucket. "There. That's — that's all of it."

Mr. Hartnell lowered the blanket. He looked at the scorched boards, at the press still warm and waiting, at his daughter, at the boy. Then he laughed — a cracked, astonished laugh — and wiped his face with an inky hand, achieving nothing but redistribution.

"They'll hang for this," he said wonderingly. "Arson. In front of witnesses. They've handed the magistrate the whole affair gift-wrapped." He looked at Edmund. "Your father chose well, lad, even if it took us all a scandalous age to catch up to him."

Outside, far off across the sleeping town, the river spoke.

Not a groan this time. A crack — vast, splitting, joyous — the sound of a winter admitting defeat, followed by a rush and grind like the world's largest drawer being opened. The ice was going out.

Edmund ran to the window. Down the dark street he could see lanterns bobbing: the magistrate, early, drawn by the fire-glow and the river's roar, coming at a pace that suggested he already knew everything. Behind him, further off, came the sound of hoofbeats retreating fast toward the great house — men who had suddenly remembered urgent business elsewhere.

"Tabitha," Edmund said. "The ice broke. That means—" He did sums on his fingers, hastily. "It means Sable!"

"It means Sable," she agreed, and pulled the bar off the door.

* * *

They came for Sable at the great house just as the eastern sky went grey — the magistrate's men, brisk and embarrassed, with a warrant that named Reginald Ashworth, his steward, and three others for conspiracy, theft of regimental funds, and arson. Sable heard the charges read aloud in the front hall while Ashworth stood rigid among his eleven ancestors, and if the nurse felt any triumph, it was the tired kind — the kind that comes after a battle where everybody bled.

"You'll testify, I suppose," Ashworth said as they led him past. His voice had gone flat, spent.

"I'll read," Sable said. "Aloud. All forty-one names. It takes eleven minutes; I've timed it. You're welcome to listen."

Then the door burst open and Edmund came through it like weather — small, filthy with soot, trailing a shawl and both Hartnells — and hit Sable at the knees hard enough to stagger a soldier.

"You're all right," Edmund said into their coat, in the voice of a boy conducting an inspection. "You're all right. The press ran all night. I helped with the fire. I threw *sand*. And the river broke, and it sounded like — it sounded like the whole world clearing its throat."

Sable held on to him and looked over his head at the grey light strengthening in the windows, at the magistrate already asking for the printed sheets, at Tabitha carrying in the first damp copies like a midwife with twins.

"Well then," Sable said, when they could speak steadily again. "Since the whole world has cleared its throat — I suppose somebody had better start reading names."

Edmund looked up. "Can I do Corporal Whitlock? I've been practicing."

"You," said Sable, "can do Corporal Whitlock."

They went out together into the loud bright morning, the river running free at last behind them, carrying winter away in pieces.
