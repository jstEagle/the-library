## 10. Every Name Read Aloud

Spring came up the river the way forgiveness does — slowly, and then all at once. One morning in April Sable Lockridge opened the workshop door and found the towpath mud brown instead of white, and a boat on the water, and a boy standing ankle-deep in melt-puddle with his sleeves rolled up as though winter had been a coat he'd finally outgrown.

"You're letting the warmth out," Edmund said, which was not true of a doorway eight feet wide, but had become one of his governing principles anyway.

Sable let the warmth out. They stood a moment in it together, listening to the river run loud and free where three months ago men had walked across with lanterns, and beneath their boots the floor still carried its scorch-mark from the fire — a great dark tongue that licked halfway to the press and stopped, as if even flame had lost its nerve that night.

"Master Hollis says we print today," Edmund said.

"I heard him. He's said it four times this morning, each time more frightened."

"He's not frightened. He's proud and pretending otherwise. It's a family trait." Edmund delivered this with the serene authority of a boy who had spent a winter learning everyone's tells, and who now owned, by magistrate's signature and colonel's blood both, half the guilt in the room he was describing. "Come on. I can't ink the rollers alone, and you promised."

Sable had promised nothing of the kind. But they went.

* * *

The Quill & Anchor wore its wounds the way an old soldier does: openly, and with a certain stubborn dignity. The east wall was patched with new brick that hadn't yet learned to match its elders. The loft stair bore a black handprint — the daughter Ruth's, they said, though she claimed it was smoke — where she'd gripped the rail hauling buckets. The type cases nearest the window smelled faintly of char no amount of oil could drown, and Master Hollis complained weekly that every em-dash set near the front of the shop printed with a ghost of soot about it, so that the whole spring's correspondence read like elegies.

But the press stood. That was the miracle nobody said aloud anymore; they'd all agreed silently to stop remarking on it, the way families agree not to mention the uncle who survived the war by methods never discussed.

The first honest roll of the season lay locked in its chase on the stone: the muster roll of Colonel Ashworth's late regiment, printed complete for the first time since the colonel's death, with every struck name restored in full — not appended, not annotated, but *restored*, set in the same body size as the others, in the same lines, so that a man reading it would never know there had ever been an omission at all. Which, Hollis argued over breakfast, was the entire point. "A correction that apologizes for itself," he said, "isn't a correction. It's a confession wearing its hat indoors."

Ruth had added a line to the colophon, small but present: *Printed from the original copy, nothing withheld.* She'd fought Sable for an hour over whether even that much was too bold, and won by simply setting it while Sable was fetching tea.

Now Sable checked the forme a last time, running a fingertip along the type the way they'd once checked a soldier's pulse — by habit, gently, dreading what might be absent. Nothing was absent. Two hundred and eleven names. They knew because they'd counted them four times, and because Sable had known most of them once already, in worse circumstances, by lamplight, in a ledger written in a hand that shook less now than it used to.

Edmund worked the tympan with the concentration of a boy performing surgery. The press took the sheet. The screw turned. Somewhere in the iron guts of the thing a sound like a long-held breath released, and Ruth pulled the impression and held it to the light.

"Well?" said Hollis, who could not bring himself to look first.

"Well," said Ruth. "It's honest."

They printed forty sheets before the yard filled up.

* * *

The reading had been Edmund's idea, which surprised no one who knew him and would have surprised everyone who didn't. He had announced it at supper some weeks prior with the offhand cruelty of children proposing large things: *"We should read the names. Out loud. In the yard. Everyone should hear them, that's the whole point of names."* And then he'd gone back to his pudding, leaving four adults to sit in the wreckage of their own reluctance.

So now the yard. Now the tables dragged out and the press rolled to the doorway so visitors could watch it work between readings. Now the widows from the cloth-row, and the two old sergeants who'd walked eleven miles from the coast because they'd heard — word travels strangely fast when it has been suppressed for years — and the magistrate himself, uncomfortable in his good coat, and a scattering of printers from neighboring towns who came ostensibly to inspect the equipment and stayed, every one of them, with their hats off.

Hollis began, because it was his shop and his penance. His voice cracked on the fourth name and Ruth took over without ceremony, and she read thirty before her father quietly resumed, steadier now, the way a man is steady the second time he confesses.

Then it was Sable's turn.

They had thought, all week, about how to do this. In the field they had buried men with a line apiece and no one watching. Here were two hundred and eleven names and a whole yard watching, and Sable had planned to be brisk about it — names are names, read them plainly, don't make theatre of grief. But then Edmund appeared at their elbow with the ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and pressed it into their hands, and looked up with an expression that was seven years old and seventy simultaneously.

"Yours should come from yours," he said. He had learned the phrase from somewhere; Sable suspected Ruth.

Sable unwrapped the ledger. Its cover had warped slightly from a night in a wet pocket during the worst of February, and the pages bore the small tide-marks of that season, but the ink had held. Ink holds. That was the whole faith of the trade, wasn't it — that ink holds when everything else lets go.

They read. Not quickly. Some names they knew the manner of: Private Corliss, struck after Bautersem, owed nine months' pay, dead of fever before anyone corrected the books. Sergeant Amble, struck twice, once by each clerk who found him inconvenient, restored twice in the new roll because Ruth had insisted errors deserve emphasis. The Whitcombe brothers, listed under one name because the second brother had been entered as "attached, see prior roll," and there was no prior roll, there never had been.

Halfway through, Sable's voice went rough, and they paused, and the yard waited without shuffling, which is the finest courtesy strangers can extend. Edmund reached up and took the ledger's edge, sharing its weight without being asked, and Sable continued.

When they finished, the silence lasted exactly as long as it needed to and not a moment more, because a sergeant at the back said, in parade-ground voice, "Three cheers for Quill and Anchor," and the yard obliged, and Hollis turned the colour of a man being applauded at his own trial.

* * *

The magistrate came inside afterward, alone, holding his hat like a man in church. He asked to see the plates.

Sable took him down to the stone room where the colonel's will had slept through two winters in copper, hidden in plain sight among portrait plates and almanac stock. The plate was mounted now in a plain frame Ruth had made, hung where every apprentice would pass it daily.

He studied it a long while. "You understand," he said at last, "what a thing like this costs a county. Every clerk who signed a false roll. Every officer who looked away. Half the commission in the district will be answering letters till Michaelmas."

"Yes," said Sable.

"And you did it anyway."

"The alternative," Sable said, "was knowing. There's a difference between knowing a thing and doing nothing, sir, and I've spent enough years on the wrong side of that difference."

The magistrate nodded slowly, and then did something Sable had not expected: he held out his hand for the ledger. "The county archive will take it," he said. "Entered properly. Catalogued. Anyone may read it, forever. That's the only protection left for it — daylight and many readers." He hesitated. "It means giving it up."

Sable looked at the ledger. At the tide-marked pages, the cramped furious entries, the little marginal notes they'd written in the field without ever imagining an audience — *owed six months, has a sister in Norwich, verify* — private instructions to a future self who had not existed yet, who did now.

History happened elsewhere; Sable had believed that once, had built a whole life on the belief, walls and roof and a locked door against attachments. But history had come to the door in the shape of a freezing boy and a note that said *forget the face*, and it had moved in, and eaten their porridge, and learned the trade.

"Take it," Sable said, and was surprised to find the words cost almost nothing. "Copy anything you need first. But it goes as it is — margins and all. The margins are the truest part."

* * *

Edmund found them at dusk on the loading dock, feet dangling over the water, and sat down beside them without speaking, which was his way of opening negotiations. Below, the river ran brown and muscular, carrying ice-out debris seaward — branches, a fence rail, somebody's lost hat, all of winter evicted at last.

"Hollis says I can be apprenticed proper," Edmund said. "Composition first. He says my hands are too small for the press but perfect for setting type." He wiggled said hands, displaying them. "And Ruth says I'll ruin my eyes, and Father Ashworth's solicitor says an heir shouldn't work a trade, and I told them all—" he paused, savoring it— "I told them an heir who can't set his own name in type doesn't really have one."

Sable laughed despite themselves. "Where did you learn to argue like that?"

"From you. You argue with everything. You just do it very quietly so people don't notice." He swung his feet. Then, quieter: "Will you stay? Now the ledger's gone to the archive. There's nothing keeping you."

The question hung over the water. Sable considered it honestly, because the boy deserved honesty and had paid for it in frozen nights and burned staircases. Nothing kept them, technically. Their field pay had ended; their trunk contained two shirts and a compass; their attachments, per the old rule, numbered zero and had done for fifteen years.

Except the rule had been dead since winter and nobody had informed them until just now.

"Someone has to teach you proofing," Sable said. "You misspell 'regiment' when you're pleased with yourself. I've seen the galleys."

"I do not."

"'Regemint.' Tuesday's sheet. Third line."

Edmund went pink, and leaned his head briefly against Sable's arm — a collision, really, brief as a spark — and then pretended it hadn't happened, and Sable pretended likewise, and the river ran on.

From inside came Hollis's voice, raised in the complaint that had replaced his cough: someone had left the ink fountain uncovered again, and there would be consequences, and the consequences would be Ruth ignoring him. A lamp bloomed in the window, and another, and the smell of supper — Ruth's bread, which was famous within a quarter mile and unknown beyond it, like everything else worth knowing here.

"One more thing," Edmund said, digging in his pocket. He produced a folded sheet, printer's waste from the day's run, and unfolded it with ceremony. On it, in fresh black type, a single line:

**SABLE LOCKRIDGE — NURSE — KEEPER OF THE LEDGER — QUILL AND ANCHOR**

"I set it myself," he said. "Every name ought to be somewhere, you said. Even ones nobody official bothered to write down."

Sable read it twice. The type was well-set — even, square, the leading generous. Only the spelling of *Lockridge* wavered, the *d* set upside down, so that it printed as a small black flag.

"It's perfect," Sable said.

"It's got a letter backwards."

"It's perfect anyway." They folded it carefully and put it inside their coat, over the heart, where ledgers used to live. "That's the thing nobody tells you about records, Edmund. The honest ones always have something backwards in them. It's how you know a person made them."

Inside, Ruth called supper, and the lamp in the pressroom guttered and was trimmed, and tomorrow there would be formes to lock and names to keep — because the county archive had sent word that afternoon, a clerk's neat hand on cheap paper, requesting one further document: a certain muster roll from a different regiment, twelve years old, whose omissions a certain nurse might perhaps also know something about.

Sable read the letter twice, and smiled, and put the kettle on.
