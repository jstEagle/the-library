## 1. The Weight of Quiet

There were three ways to survive the Meridian Trading Company, and Orsen Coldharbour had learned all of them by the end of his first winter.

The first was speed. The second was invisibility. The third — the one that mattered, the one that kept a boy breathing when the other two failed — was silence.

He was twenty-three now, and he had been silent for seven years, which was long enough that silence had stopped being a thing he did and become a thing he was. It sat in his chest like ballast, keeping him steady in rough water. Most mornings he barely noticed it. Most mornings.

Greyhaven woke the way it always did: reluctantly, and in fog. The harbor mist came off the water before dawn and swallowed the masts of the herring fleet whole, so that from the warehouse steps the ships looked like they were being slowly erased, one rope and one spar at a time. Gulls argued over something dead on the pilings. The tide smelled of salt and tar and, faintly, of the fish-rendering works upwind, which no amount of fog could ever quite forgive.

Orsen stood at the loading door of Meridian Warehouse Three with a tally-slate under his arm and watched the fog do its work. He had arrived before the fog did. He always arrived before the fog did. That was not ambition; it was arithmetic. A tally-clerk who arrived late was noticed. A tally-clerk who arrived early was furniture, and furniture was never blamed for anything.

"Coldharbour." The voice came from the shadow of the salt barrels, where a man could stand for an hour and be warm and unseen. "You're early."

"I'm on time, Mr. Petch."

Petch, the night foreman, emerged looking like something the tide had returned against its better judgment — a squat man with a beard full of yesterday's porridge and the general air of a person who had been awake so long that sleep had become a rumor he'd stopped believing. He squinted at the gray light, then at Orsen, then at the slate.

"Barque out of Saltmere's due on the morning tide. Forty crates of dyewood, twelve of indigo, and whatever the captain's smuggling in his own cabin, which you'll count as rope, because that's what the manifest will say." He scratched under his arm. "You'll be careful with the counting."

"I'm always careful with the counting."

"You're careful with the *writing down* of the counting." Petch grinned, showing a tooth like a fence post. "There's a difference, and the difference is why you're still here and young Tallow's not."

Orsen said nothing, because that was the correct answer, and because it was also the true one. Young Tallow had counted out loud. Young Tallow had counted out loud exactly once.

Petch clapped him on the shoulder with a hand like a mooring cleat and wandered off toward the office to sleep standing up, which was a skill he'd perfected over twenty years and would demonstrate, as usual, by mid-morning.

* * *

The barque *Kindly Fortune* came in on the morning tide, which was a joke of a name, because nothing that sailed out of Saltmere was kindly and the only fortune involved belonged to the Company. Orsen stood at the dock edge with his slate and chalk and counted crates as the stevedores swung them ashore.

Forty of dyewood. Twelve of indigo. And one crate, marked ROPE, that clinked.

He wrote *rope*.

Here is what Orsen had learned, and learned the hard way, and learned at a cost he did not let himself think about on working days: the manifest was not a record of what was in the crates. The manifest was a record of what the Company had decided to say was in the crates, and those two things were related only in the way that a portrait is related to a man — in likeness, and only when it flattered him.

He chalked the numbers. He did not chalk the clink. He had stopped hearing clinks years ago, in the same way a man living beside a waterfall stops hearing water. It was not deafness. It was architecture. You built the wall, and then you lived inside it, and after a while you forgot the wall was there at all, except on nights when the wind came off the harbor in a certain way and you remembered that walls have a weight, and that you were the one carrying it.

The morning proceeded. Fog burned off into the flat gray light that Greyhaven offered instead of sunshine, the way a miser offers a farthing instead of a crown. Orsen tallied. He signed. He initialed. He carried his slate to the office and set it on the desk of the day supervisor, Corvane Dull, who was not dull at all, despite the name, and who was the reason Orsen's silence had been polished to such a high shine these past three years.

Dull was a narrow man with narrow hands and a smile that arrived precisely on schedule and never a moment before, like a ferry. He wore his Company pin polished to a small brass sun on his lapel. He was, by the standards of the Meridian Trading Company, a rising man. By Orsen's standards — which he kept, like everything else, strictly to himself — he was a knife that had learned to wear a collar and answer to a name.

"Coldharbour." Dull did not look up from the slate. "The Saltmere cargo. Any irregularities?"

"No, sir."

"No clinks?"

The question was a hook, baited with casualness. Dull liked to ask it. Orsen had never determined whether Dull was testing him or simply enjoying the taste of the fear it produced. Perhaps both. Perhaps, with Dull, enjoyment and testing were the same activity, the way a cat's play and a cat's hunting were the same activity, and the mouse's opinion was not solicited.

"I wouldn't know, sir. The manifest said rope."

"The manifest," Dull agreed warmly, "said rope." He made a small approving mark with his pen. "You have a tidy hand, Coldharbour. Tidy hand, tidy mind. The Company values tidiness."

"Yes, sir."

"Dismissed."

Orsen went. Behind him he heard Dull humming — Dull hummed when he was pleased, a little tuneless hymn of satisfaction, the sound of a man balancing his books against his soul and finding the soul came out ahead, which told you everything about the state of Dull's soul and nothing good about his arithmetic.

* * *

The midday meal was taken in the long shadow of Warehouse Three, on upturned crates, in the company of the other indentured men. There were eleven of them at Warehouse Three, ranging from Tam Brickle, who was nineteen and had the attention span of a gull, to old Hesk, who was fifty-eight and had been indentured so long that his contract had become less a document than a diagnosis.

They ate salt fish and bread and, on Thursdays, if the herring boats had done well, a soup that Hesk insisted on calling *stew* out of what everyone recognized as stubborn optimism.

"Did you hear," Tam said, around a mouthful of bread, "about the *Gannet*? Run aground on the Teeth, off the island station. Whole crew drowned."

"Where'd you hear that?" asked Hesk.

"Dock talk. Fishermen saying the wreck's been sitting out there three days and nobody's gone to salvage. Company's got a boat sitting on it. Won't let nobody near."

Orsen chewed. The island station. The pearling station. The name went through him the way cold water goes through a boot — quickly, and leaving everything it touched colder. He kept chewing. He kept his face arranged in the mild, vacant interest of a man listening to dock gossip about boats, which was the safest expression in Greyhaven and the one he wore the way other men wore hats.

"Fever station, that was," Hesk said, with the authority of a man who had never been within a hundred miles of it. "Lost to fever, years back. Whole place. Terrible thing."

"That's what the Company said," Tam agreed.

"That's what happened, then," said Hesk, and that was that. That was how it worked in Greyhaven. The Company said a thing, and the thing became true, and the truth became a thing nobody said twice.

Orsen looked at his bread. He thought — briefly, the way you touch a stove to confirm it's hot, only when you've forgotten, only once every few years — of his father, who had been a diver, who had gone to the station as *volunteer labor*, which is what the Company's papers called men whose alternatives were the Company's papers or the Company's debt-collectors, and who had come back to Greyhaven in a box with a Company form attached to the lid explaining, in tidy administrative prose, that he had been lost to fever.

His father had never had a fever in his life. His father had been built like a bollard and had once carried a piano up three flights of stairs for a bet. But the form said fever, and the form was signed by a Company man with a tidy hand, and so his father had died of fever, officially, and would go on dying of it for as long as the Company's records endured.

Orsen finished his bread. He did not say anything about the *Gannet*, or the island, or the salvage boat sitting on the wreck like a gull on a corpse.

He had rules about this. He had made them himself, early, and carved them into himself the way the Company carved its name into the warehouse lintels, and they had kept him alive for seven years, and he recited them now, silently, the way a pious man recites his evening prayers:

*One. Count what you're told to count. Two. Write what you're told to write. Three. Hear nothing that isn't said to you. Four. See nothing that isn't shown to you. Five. If a question is asked, the answer is no, sir. Six. If a kindness is offered, look for the hook before the bait. Seven. Never — never — be interesting.*

Seven rules. Seven years. It had the neatness of a ledger entry, and like most neat ledger entries, it concealed a great deal of mess.

* * *

The afternoon brought rain, because Greyhaven's afternoons generally brought something, and rain was the default. Orsen spent it in the counting room, copying figures into the master ledger in his tidy hand, which Dull had praised, and which was tidy the way a well-made cage is tidy — everything in its place, everything accounted for, nothing able to leave.

He was halfway through a column of salt-beef weights when the light in the room changed, and he looked up, and Corvane Dull was standing at the window with the day's manifests spread on the sill, and Dull was altering them.

He did it the way he did everything: neatly, unhurriedly, with the small humming contentment of a man doing a job he was good at. A figure here, adjusted. A date there, shifted. A crate of indigo became, with two strokes of the pen, a crate of rope. Orsen watched him do it. Dull knew he was watching. That was, Orsen understood with a clarity that felt like cold water, part of the point. Dull was not hiding it. Dull was *demonstrating* it — demonstrating that he could do it in the open, in front of a witness, and the witness would see nothing, because the witness had been built, over seven careful years, into a man who saw nothing.

Dull finished. He blotted the page. He turned, and his smile arrived, right on schedule, like the ferry.

"Tidy work today, Coldharbour."

"Thank you, sir."

"The *Kindly Fortune*'s cargo. All accounted for?"

"Every crate, sir."

"Rope and all."

"Rope and all, sir."

Dull looked at him for a moment longer than the exchange required. There was nothing in the look — that was the terrible skill of the man, there was genuinely nothing in it, no threat, no suspicion, no curiosity, only a pleasant blankness, like the surface of deep water — and then he gathered his manifests and went out into the rain, humming his little hymn.

Orsen sat with his pen above the ledger and did not move for a long time.

Here was the thing about the wall he had built. Here was the thing he did not let himself think about on working days, and thought about, instead, on nights like this was going to be, when the wind came off the harbor with rain in its teeth and the warehouse creaked like an old ship and sleep was a country he couldn't get a visa for.

The wall kept things out. That was its purpose, and it was good at its purpose. It kept out Dull's looks and Petch's hooks and the clink in the rope crates and the word *fever* and the memory of his father's hands, which had been scarred in patterns no fever could explain, pearl-callused and split from years of cold diving, hands he had last seen — no. The wall kept things out.

But walls, he had learned, are not selective. The same stones that kept out the cold kept in the prisoner. He had spent seven years being safe, and somewhere in the middle of those seven years, without noticing the exact day it happened, *safe* had quietly become the same thing as *buried*. He was alive the way a coin at the bottom of a well is intact. Nothing had touched him in years. Nothing had reached him in years. He could not have said, if asked, what his own voice sounded like at any volume above a murmur, because he had not used one.

The rain thickened. The lamps in the yard came on, one by one, halos of orange drowning in the wet dark. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy clanged its slow, patient count, and the fog was coming back in off the harbor for the evening shift, erasing the masts again, erasing the ships, erasing everything it could reach.

Orsen blotted his last column, squared his ledger, and set it precisely at the corner of the desk where it was always set. Tidy. Everything in its place. Nothing able to leave.

That was when the storm arrived — properly arrived, not the usual Greyhaven drizzle-with-ambitions but a real one, a harbor-shaking, door-banging brute of a thing that came off the sea like it had a grudge. The lamps went out. The yard turned to a single roaring blackness. And through the din, over the wind, Orsen heard — or thought he heard, and the difference would matter more than he could yet know — a sound from the loading steps.

Small. Thin. Furious, in the way that only one kind of small creature in this world is ever truly furious.

He stood in the dark counting room with his rules arrayed around him like a fence, all seven of them, each one a stone in the wall that had kept him alive and kept him buried, and he listened to the storm, and he listened to the sound, and the wall, for the first time in seven years, did not feel like armor.

It felt like a cage with something on the other side of it, crying.
