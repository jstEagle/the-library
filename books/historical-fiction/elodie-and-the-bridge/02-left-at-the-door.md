## 2. Left at the Door

The sound did not stop.

Orsen stood in the counting room with his lamp turned low and told himself, with the practiced calm of a man who had talked himself out of better judgment before, that it was a cat. Cats got into warehouses. Cats cried at doors. Cats had no respect for the rules of a man who needed his rules the way other men needed food.

The sound cried again, and it was not a cat. Cats did not pause between cries the way this thing paused — as though gathering itself, as though deciding whether the effort of being heard was worth it. Cats were honest about their noise. This was the cry of something that had learned, in whatever short life it had lived, that nobody came anyway, and was trying all the same.

"Damn," Orsen said quietly, which was as close to a prayer as he ever got.

He took the lamp because he would not go anywhere without it — Rule Three: *never be found in the dark without a reason to be there* — and he went out through the counting room's side door into the long spine of the warehouse, past the bales and crates stacked in their patient towers, past the tarpaulins that breathed in the wind like sleeping animals, to the great loading door at the water-facing end. The storm had worked itself into a proper fury by then. The whole building groaned around him, timber and iron complaining the way old men complain about weather: constantly, and with genuine grievance.

He put his hand on the bar and hesitated.

Here is what a sensible man does, Orsen thought. A sensible man stands very still until the crying stops, or until the watch bell rings, whichever comes first. A sensible man does not open a door in a storm at midnight to see what the sea has left him, because the sea leaves nothing but trouble and driftwood, and trouble floats in on its own schedule without needing an invitation.

But the crying came again, thinner now, and Orsen discovered that seven years of rules had not managed to kill everything in him after all, which was annoying, and also — he would realize later — the beginning of everything else.

He lifted the bar.

* * *

The wind hit him like a wet wall. Rain came sideways off the harbor, hard enough to sting, and the lamp guttered and nearly died and then thought better of it and burned on, stubborn as its keeper. Orsen stepped out onto the loading platform, one hand on the doorframe, and held the light up against the dark.

The steps ran down from the platform to the cobbled apron where the Company's carts turned in from Quay Lane. They were stone steps, old ones, worn into shallow bowls by a century of boots, and they were empty — empty except for one thing, set neatly on the second step from the top, tucked back against the wall where the rain could not quite reach it.

An oilskin bundle. About the length of a loaf of good bread. It moved.

Orsen's stomach dropped somewhere below the warehouse floor and kept going.

"No," he said, to the bundle, to the storm, to himself. "No, no."

But he was already down the steps, crouched, sheltering the lamp with his body, and his hands — traitorous hands, hands that had not done an unapproved thing in seven years — were working at the oilskin's knots before his mind had finished objecting.

The oilskin was good quality, he noticed even then, because noticing things was the one habit he had never managed to break. Sailcloth treated with tar and wax, folded with the double tuck of someone who knew canvas, who had wrapped cargo or nets or sails in her time. Someone had wrapped this bundle the way you wrap something you intend to survive weather. Someone had cared.

Inside the oilskin was wool. Inside the wool was a face.

It was red and furious and screwed up tight, a fist of a face, with a damp wisp of dark hair plastered across its brow, and when the lamplight touched it, the face opened its eyes — dark eyes, too serious for a thing that small — looked directly at Orsen Coldharbour, and stopped crying.

That was worse somehow. Much worse.

"Don't look at me like that," Orsen said. His voice came out rough. "I'm nobody. I'm the wrong person entirely. You've made an error of delivery."

The baby considered this. The baby appeared to find it unconvincing. The baby's chin began to wobble, telegraphing catastrophe, and Orsen did the only thing he could think of, which was to gather the whole bundle up against his chest, oilskin and all, and stand there on the steps in the hammering rain while something small and warm and impossibly alive settled against him with a sigh that seemed, absurdly, satisfied.

The wind screamed down the quay. Somewhere out on the water a ship's mooring line cracked like a gunshot. And Orsen Coldharbour, who had spent seven years learning to feel nothing on purpose, stood holding an abandoned child in the doorway of the Meridian Trading Company and felt his careful heart crack straight down the middle like river ice in thaw.

* * *

There was a paper. He found it when he shifted the bundle, half-tucked under the wool where it would stay dry: a square of cheap paper, folded once, ink already threatening to run at the edges.

He should not have read it on the steps. He knew that even as he unfolded it. But there was no version of the world where Orsen left a note unread; notes were information, information was survival, and besides, the note was addressed in the general direction of whoever found the child, and tonight that was him, like it or not.

Four words, written in a quick, slanted, confident hand:

*Forget this face.*

No name. No instruction beyond that. Nothing about feeding or keeping or where she'd come from — nothing, in fact, that acknowledged the child existed at all, except for the word *this*, which pointed at her like a finger. Whoever wrote it had wanted the finder to know two things: that there was a child, and that the child must vanish. Not given away. Not brought to the parish. Forgotten.

And Orsen stood in the rain with the paper running soft in his fingers and felt the bottom fall out of the night, because he knew the hand.

Seven years in a warehouse teaches you many useless things and a few essential ones, and among the essential ones, for a man who handled manifests and waybills and contracts by the hundredweight, was handwriting. You learned the clerks' fists. You learned which supervisors pressed hard and which signed in haste. You learned to spot forgery because your own skin depended on spotting forgery. Orsen had spent so much of his life reading other people's writing that he read it the way sailors read water — without thinking, instantly, bodily.

This hand was not a clerk's hand. It was a working hand, a hand used to salt and rope and doing things quickly in bad light. The letters leaned forward like runners. The *F* was made in one impatient stroke. The *g* dropped below the line with a little hook, and there was a small tremor in the final word, in the *ce*, as if the writer's hand had shaken at the end and she had not gone back to correct it.

He knew it because he had seen it once, three years ago, on a petition.

The divers' wives had come to the warehouse in the autumn of the year the station closed — a delegation of six women, quiet as church mice, standing in the receiving hall with a paper asking the Company to confirm the death payments owed for men lost at the pearling station. Lost to fever, the official story went. Terrible fever. Whole boats of men. Orsen had been carrying ledgers through the hall that day, invisible as furniture, the way he made himself invisible, and one of the women had pressed the petition into the duty clerk's reluctant hands, and Orsen had glanced at it — because glancing was free and looking away was suspicious — and he had seen that handwriting, leaning forward like runners, and he had heard the woman say, in a low island voice roughened by salt: *"My husband dove eleven seasons. Eleven. And they will tell me he died of a cough."*

Sera Voss. Widow of Edric Voss, pearl diver, listed lost at the Cinder Shoal station in the spring of the fever year. Orsen had never spoken to her in his life. He had heard exactly four sentences from her mouth across a receiving hall. He had seen her signature for the space of ten seconds.

And he knew her hand the way you know a voice from a dream.

Because there was more. Because the mind, once it has decided to horrify itself, does not stop halfway. Sera Voss was not merely a widow. Sera Voss was *dead*. Drowned — reported drowned, the winter after the station closed, along with two other widows who had asked too many questions about the same payment ledger, in the same stretch of grey water east of the harbor bar. The Company record said accident. The harbor folk said nothing at all, in the particular loud silence that Greyhaven reserved for such entries. Orsen had read the incident report himself, filed and stamped in the course of his duties, and he had read it and felt the cold slide through him that he always felt, and he had filed the feeling away with everything else, under silence, where it belonged.

A drowned woman had left a baby on the Company's steps in a storm, with a note in her living hand.

Orsen looked down at the child. She had fallen asleep against his chest — just like that, mid-catastrophe, trusting the shape that held her — and her small fist had worked loose from the wool and lay against the oilskin like a claim.

"You," he whispered, "are the most dangerous thing that has ever happened to me."

The child slept on, unconvinced of her own danger, unaware that she was, at that moment, the single most flammable object in the port of Greyhaven. Evidence walked on two legs — well, no legs yet, actually, evidence mostly slept and drooled — and she was currently drooling gently against the chest of a man whose entire strategy for staying alive consisted of never, ever being near anything like her.

* * *

He stood on the steps for a long moment longer, because the next thing he did would be the first real choice he'd made in years, and some part of him wanted to savor the last seconds of having no choices at all.

Option one: take the child inside, raise the alarm, report the abandonment per regulation, let the Company handle it. The Company would handle it. That was precisely the problem. The Company handled inconvenient things the way a tide handles sandcastles. A note saying *forget this face*, left on the Meridian's own doorstep — that was not an orphan's tragedy, that was a message, and messages sent to the Company were usually sent *by* the Company, and either way the child was at the center of something with edges. Hand her over and she disappears into the parish rolls, or onto a westbound nurse-cart, or simply off the face of the record, and Orsen goes back to his bunk and his rules and his silence, and someday, maybe, in a year or five, a memory of dark eyes wakes him at night and he tells himself it was only a dream.

Option two was everything else, and everything else was death by degrees, slow or fast, depending on how careless he proved.

Rule One, the oldest rule, carved deepest: *What the Company does not see, cannot be laid at your door.* An infant on his chest was the most visible thing in the world. Rule Four: *Never carry anything across a threshold that isn't on a manifest.* He was standing on a threshold. Rule Six: *Grief is a luxury purchased at someone else's expense* — and he didn't even know where that one had come from anymore, only that it was true, only that grief cost, and the currency was always somebody's blood.

The wind drove rain against his back like a hand shoving him toward the door. Inside was warmth and dryness and the counting room and the fence of his seven rules. Inside was safety.

The child sighed in her sleep, a tiny sound, barely anything, and her fingers curled shut around a fold of the oilskin, and Orsen understood — suddenly, completely, with the terrible clarity that arrives only when it is least welcome — that safety was exactly what this child had been denied, deliberately, by people willing to drown widows to get it, and that every rule he owned had been built by a boy who chose to survive instead of choose at all.

"My father," he said to the storm, quietly, "would have picked her up."

His father had been a diver too, once, before Greyhaven, before the Company, before the drowning that had made Orsen an indentured orphan with a debt. But that was another night's arithmetic. Tonight there was only this: a bundle going rapidly heavier against his chest, a note going rapidly softer in his pocket, and a door standing open behind him like a question.

Orsen Coldharbour broke Rule Four.

He carried her inside.

* * *

He did not take her to the counting room. He took her to the sail-loft, the high narrow room at the warehouse's east end where the repair crews stitched canvas in winter and nobody worked at night, because if anyone found him, better to be found somewhere explicable than somewhere incriminating — though he was aware, distantly, that he had crossed some internal line where "explicable" and "incriminating" had begun trading places.

He lit the loft's stub of candle, hung his wet coat by the stove that hadn't been lit since spring, and unwrapped the child properly for the first time.

She was perhaps two months old. Small, but not starved; someone had fed her recently, and recently meant hours, not days, which meant whoever left her had been close, tonight, in this storm. The wool swaddling was fine stuff, island-made. And at its edge, where the fold fell open, Orsen saw the stitching — dense little rows of them, ridged and shiny, the kind of scarring that comes only from years of hauling shell-weight lines barehanded.

Pearl callus. Diver's cloth, mended by a diver's hands, on a baby left by a drowned woman's writing.

The Cinder Shoal station. The fever that wasn't. The widows who drowned. The petition in the hall. All of it, which he had folded away under silence seven years deep, sat up in its grave and looked at him.

"Well," Orsen said to the baby, and his voice was not steady, and for once he did not care, "it appears you and I are both supposed to forget something."

She opened her eyes at the sound of his voice — those dark, too-serious eyes — and studied him with the gravity of a magistrate, and Orsen Coldharbour sat down slowly on a coil of rope in a freezing sail-loft, holding the most forbidden bundle in Greyhaven, and laughed once, helplessly, at absolutely nothing funny at all.

Then he got up, because laughing changed nothing and candles burned down and storms ended, and a storm's ending was a deadline. Someone had left her here in the worst weather of the season, which meant urgency, which meant pursuit, which meant that come morning there might be questions, searches, a supervisor's eye moving along the docks.

He needed a fire in the stove. He needed milk, though God alone knew where a warehouse man found milk at midnight. And above all he needed to move her — soon, quietly, tonight — to the one person in Greyhaven who asked no questions, owed no allegiance to the Company, and knew exactly how to keep a fragile thing breathing.

Whether she'd forgive him for bringing the asking of questions to her door was another matter entirely.

Orsen looked down at Wren-that-was-not-yet-Wren, sleeping in pearl-mended wool on a dead man's coat, and made her a promise he had no right to make and every intention of keeping.

"I don't know who told this city to forget you," he said. "But I am spectacularly bad at forgetting. Ask anyone. Well — ask anyone who'll admit to knowing me."

Outside, the storm began, at last, to lose its temper. And somewhere down in the black harbor, under the rain, the tide was turning.
