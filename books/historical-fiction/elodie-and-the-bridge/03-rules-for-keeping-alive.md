## 3. Rules for Keeping Alive

The storm blew itself out sometime before dawn, and by the grey hour when the warehouse lamps guttered low, Greyhaven lay steaming under a sky the color of old dishwater. Orsen Coldharbour stood in the shadow of the salt shed with an infant asleep against his chest, and he was, by his own careful accounting, in the worst trouble of his life.

She — and he was fairly sure it was a she, though he had checked only once, briefly, with his eyes fixed respectfully on the far wall as if the bricks might object — slept with the unreasonable serenity of someone who had never once read a Meridian Trading Company manifest. Her small fist was knotted in the oilskin wrapping. The note was folded in his pocket, and every few minutes he became aware of it again the way you become aware of a stone in your boot.

*Forget this face.*

Sera Voss's handwriting. He'd have known it anywhere. She wrote her letters leaning forward, urgent, like each one was trying to arrive before the rest of her. He had seen that hand write three lines to the Company office asking after her husband's pay, back when Tam Voss was still counted among the living divers at Saltmere Station, and Orsen had been the boy who carried the ledger out to her and did not meet her eyes because meeting eyes meant questions and questions meant the overseer's list.

Now she was drowned. Officially. The harbor records said so in Fenwick's neat archivist's hand, or a hand very much like it, two years gone. And yet here was her baby on the Company steps during the worst storm of the season, with instructions to be forgotten.

Orsen pressed his palm flat against the warehouse wall and breathed the way he had taught himself to breathe: slow, shallow, invisible.

He had rules. Seven years of indenture had burned them into him like brands, and they were good rules, and men who kept them lived long enough to buy their way free at thirty-one and walk out of Greyhaven a nobody with a full purse and no history. Rule one: see nothing. Rule two: say less than nothing. Rule three: if you must be seen seeing something, be seen seeing it stupidly.

Rule four, unwritten but understood: children found on doorsteps belong to the sea, the parish, or God, and none of those three can punish you for walking away.

He looked down at the small warm weight against his chest.

"Oh," he said quietly, "you are a problem."

Wren-sized, he thought later. Though he didn't know the word yet, and wouldn't for weeks, and when Maren Quill suggested it he would pretend he had thought of it first. But that was ahead of him. Right now there was only the present difficulty, which was that rule four had developed a heartbeat and a smell like milk and rain, and was gripping his shirt with the fierce, entitled strength of someone who had decided he was furniture.

* * *

He should go back to thinking about his father.

That sounds backwards, but Orsen's mind worked in circles, like a dog settling. His father was where all his worst decisions had been decided for him in advance, and this decision felt exactly like that one coming around again.

Jorin Coldharbour had been a big man with small careful hands, which is an unusual combination in a diver and a useful one. He had signed with the Meridian Company the year Orsen turned seven, out of the same cheerful desperation that signed everyone: debts after the blockade winters, a wife with a bad lung, a son who ate like a growing wharf. Three-year contract, pearl shares, renewal bonus. The Company's pamphlets had woodcuts of smiling divers on them, which Orsen did not remember noticing at the time and would think about constantly later, the way you think about a joke you failed to laugh at.

For two years it went as these things go. Letters came, written by the station clerk because Jorin couldn't shape letters well. They were short and mostly about weather and mostly lies about money, because the clerk rounded everything downward and Jorin, reading over the man's shoulder, could not stop him. Orsen's mother kept the letters in a tin and read them until they softened at the folds.

Then came the third year, and the petition.

Orsen learned about the petition years afterward, from a man in a tavern who had been too deep too often and spoke slowly now, like his thoughts still had to come up from a long way down. There had been a petition, signed by forty-one divers, asking the Company to honor the contract terms on pearl shares — the actual terms, not the clerk's terms. Forty-one names. Jorin's name was fourth, because Jorin Coldharbour did not believe in hanging back when he believed a thing, and this was the flaw in him that Orsen loved most and that killed him, in roughly that order.

The divers who asked questions at Saltmere stopped appearing on the manifests. That was how the Company did it — not with violence, violence leaves paperwork, but with transfers, reassignments, boats that went out in weather other boats stayed ashore for. The tavern man said the word for it, slowly, from down deep: *thinning*. The station wasn't purged, it was thinned, like stock.

And in the spring of that third year, one boat came back without Jorin Coldharbour, and the report said drowning, and drowning was legal, drowning required no inquiry, drowning was the Company's favorite word because it blamed the sea, and the sea could not be sued.

Orsen was nine. His mother lasted eighteen months more, coughing, and then it was the workhouse, and then it was the Company taking indenture applications from boys twelve and up, and Orsen standing in front of the same company that had drowned his father, signing himself into its care with a mark he was ashamed of because he could write his name perfectly well and pretended he couldn't, so that the clerk wouldn't look closely at the surname.

Coldharbour. A name the Company had already used once, to its satisfaction.

* * *

So he knew what happened to people who asked questions, was the thing. He hadn't just heard it. He was made of the evidence.

And the arithmetic, therefore, was simple, and he ran through it anyway, twice, standing in the steam-wet yard while gulls argued overhead about ownership of something dead, because simple arithmetic deserved a fair hearing even when you already hated the result.

Option one: report the child. Take her to Corvane Dull, the supervisor, first bell, say *found her on the steps*, collect whatever small credit attaches to diligence. The Company would handle her. The word *handle* sat in that sentence like a hook sits in a fish's mouth. A child left with a note saying *forget this face*, a child connected to Sera Voss, a child who was — he was increasingly sure — evidence of something, would be handled the way Saltmere had been handled. She would become a line in a ledger, and then she would become no line at all, and there would be no inquiry because there would be no body and no name and no one who remembered her face, per instructions.

Option two: hide her. Keep her alive. Which meant conspiring against the Meridian Trading Company, an activity with a documented survival rate that Orsen could recite from personal grief, and which would end, when discovered — not if, *when*, he was a realist — with Orsen Coldharbour losing the seven years he had invested in staying alive, the four remaining years he needed to buy his freedom, and quite possibly his skin into the bargain.

Silence was armor. Silence had kept him breathing through seven years of watching manifests get falsified, watching men get transferred onto unlucky boats, watching his own reflection in the warehouse glass learn to keep its face smooth as water. Silence was the whole of his estate. And silence said: put her down somewhere warm, walk away, forget this face. It was even, he noted with a kind of bitter humor, the same instruction the note gave. The Company and his own survival instinct, in rare agreement.

Against all of that: one fist, knotted in his shirt. Sleeping like the weather didn't matter. Breathing that little breath, in, out, with the absolute confidence of someone who had placed herself, deliberately, blindly, entirely, in the keeping of whoever found her — and the note said *forget*, which meant whoever left her had expected the finder to be Company, had expected erasure, and had left her anyway, because the alternative was worse.

Sera Voss had trusted the wrongness of the world to trip somewhere between the sea and the steps. Or she had trusted one particular person. He turned the thought over carefully, the way you turn over a coin you suspect might be clipped. *Forget this face.* If you wanted a child forgotten by the Company but remembered by a friend, you left the note legible enough for both.

He had carried ledgers past her door once a week for two years, before Tam Voss died and before she drowned, and she had fed him honey bread on the step once, unasked, because he was a thin boy carrying heavy books, and she had said, "You've got your father's shoulders," which was the kindest thing anyone had said to him that decade, and also the most dangerous, because it meant she had known who he was. Known, and spoken kindly anyway, to an indentured boy whose father's name was on a petition.

People remember who was kind to them. It is one of the few reliable laws of the world. Orsen had built his entire life on the other laws, the cold ones, and here was this one, warm and inconvenient, sitting on his chest and drooling slightly.

"Right," he told her. "Here is what happens now. I take you somewhere safe today, I find someone who knows what to do with babies, and then we never speak of it again, either of us, and you grow up tall and anonymous somewhere far from the Meridian Trading Company. That is the plan. It is a good plan. Plans do not come better than this."

The baby opened one eye, considered him, and closed it again, apparently satisfied. He chose to take it as a signature.

* * *

The trouble with plans is the witnesses. By full light the yard filled up: stevedores, tally-clerks, Dull himself sweeping through in his good coat with the collar up, inspecting the storm's damage like a general reviewing casualties. Orsen arranged himself into his working posture — spine bowed, eyes down, expression of a man holding exactly one thought at a time and holding it loosely — and joined the line assessing the flooded lower stores.

"Storm took a bite out of the west doors," Dull announced, to no one and everyone. "Coldharbour. The bilge run in the old annex is stopped. You'll clear it."

Clearing a bilge run meant waist-deep in cold water and worse, and everyone knew it, and Dull assigning it at random meant it wasn't random; Dull had a memory like a debt collector. Orsen bowed his head at the correct depth of humility and said, "Aye, sir," in the correct tone of a man with no opinions, and thought, with a calm that surprised him: *he doesn't know yet. Nobody knows yet. For today, it's only mine.*

There is a strange liberty in a punishment, if you're carrying a secret: while Dull is watching you stand in filth, he isn't wondering where you've been. Orsen spent the morning in the annex bilge, arms deep in the dark water hauling weed and rope-ends, and let the cold make his thinking simple.

Simple thinking produced this: the note was evidence. Evidence needed keeping, and keeping was a trade, and there were exactly two kinds of people in Greyhaven who knew about keeping things the Company wanted lost. One kind was archivists. The other kind was smugglers, because anything that can be hidden can be moved, and anything that can be moved can be kept moving.

He knew of one of each. Tobias Fenwick, disgraced, drinking somewhere above the dockside taverns. And Maren Quill, midwife and mover of unstated goods, who kept a barge at the tar-yards and delivered half the harbor's babies regardless of what the parish register would prefer to admit.

Both choices were dangerous. Both were better than the parish, which reported to the Company in all matters that mattered. He hauled another armful of weed and let himself imagine, for one luxurious moment, the alternative life: hand her to Dull, keep his head down, four more years, freedom, anonymity, done. He examined that life the way you examine a room you're leaving. It was a good room. Warm. Quiet. Empty.

In the water's reflection, distorted by ripples, his own face looked briefly like his father's — the set of the jaw, the stubbornness of it — and he looked away from it, and finished the job, and made his decision in the practical way he made everything: not with thunder, but with a list.

One: the child eats. Two: the child goes somewhere warm. Three: the note stays hidden. Four: nobody learns anything, ever, ideally including him.

The baby, when he retrieved her from her nest behind the salt barrels — fed on soaked bread mashed with a little milk, which she had regarded with visible editorial comment — was asleep again, having concluded that her new arrangement was acceptable for the time being. He tucked her inside his coat, wrapped in the oilskin, close against the warmth of him, and pulled his jacket over the lot, and stood for a moment in the yard like any man pausing before a hard afternoon.

If you had stopped him and asked what he was doing, he would have said, honestly, "Nothing. Just walking."

It was the truest thing he'd said in seven years, and it cost him everything he had saved, though he didn't know it yet, and wouldn't for weeks — and by then, he would find, he minded less than he expected.

He walked toward the tar-yards, whistling — badly, tunelessly, the way a man whistles when he is practicing being nobody — with a sleeping child's breath fogging the inside of his coat, and the note folded over his heart like a debt he had finally decided to pay.

Behind him the warehouse rose grey and enormous, patient as a ledger. Ahead, down at the water, on a barge hung with drying herbs and suspiciously good netting, a lamp was already lit.

Maren Quill, he had heard, counted fingers.

He hoped she'd approve of his.
