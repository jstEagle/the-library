## 4. The Midwife Who Counts Fingers

The rain had stopped by the time Orsen slipped out of the warehouse with the baby under his coat, but Greyhaven's fog had merely changed its mind about how to be miserable. It hung in the alleys like wet wool, swallowing lamplight, muffling footsteps — which was good for smuggling and bad for nerves.

He had spent two hours deciding how to carry her. Under the arm was too obvious. In the cargo satchel was too cruel, and besides, she'd cried at the zipper of his thoughts alone, as if she could hear him planning. What he'd settled on was the oldest trick in the dockhand's book: carry something heavy-looking against your chest, walk like you're late, and no one looks twice. Dockhands carried things constantly. A man carrying nothing drew more eyes than a man carrying everything.

So he carried her the way he'd carry a bolt of canvas, grumbling internally about the weight, and she — mercifully, miraculously — slept against his heartbeat as if she'd been told this was the arrangement now and had agreed to its terms.

*Left at the third lamp. Turn where the fish smell changes.* Maren Quill's directions, given to him once, three years ago, when he'd paid her to stitch up a stevedore who'd argued with a winch cable and lost. *You'll know my barge by the herbs dying in the windows. I keep them alive out of spite.*

He turned where the fish smell changed — from herring to something older and saltier, the smell of the harbor remembering itself — and there she was: a barge moored between a gutting shed and a rotting pier, listing gently to port like a tired woman leaning on a doorframe. Herbs in the windows, half dead, defiantly green at the roots. Lantern light the color of weak tea.

Orsen stood on the pier a long moment, fog beading on his eyelashes, and made himself say the true thing to himself, because lying to himself was a habit he couldn't afford tonight:

*I don't know anyone else.*

That was the whole of it. Seven years in Greyhaven and he knew hundreds of faces, dozens of names, and could count on one hand — with fingers left over — the people who would take a risk for him that cost more than coin. Maren Quill was one. Possibly the only one.

He knocked on the barge door the way she'd told him to: two, pause, one. Like a bird with a stutter.

"Go away," said a voice inside, warm as a hearth.

"It's Orsen."

"I know. Go away anyway. I'm eating."

"Maren."

A sigh, floorboards, the scrape of a bolt. The door opened on a woman built like a barrel with opinions — short, broad, silver-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be a fishhook, sleeves rolled over forearms that had delivered more babies than most priests had baptized. She looked at him, then at the lump under his coat, and her eyebrows performed an entire conversation without her mouth joining in.

"Well," she said. "Either you've gotten very strange about stealing bread, or you'd better come in."

* * *

Inside, the barge was warm and cluttered in the way of a place where everything had been put somewhere on purpose, even if the purpose was chaos. Bundles of dried herbs hung from the ceiling beams. Jars lined every shelf, labeled in a hand so fierce it seemed to argue with itself. A kettle sat on a stove that burned driftwood and, apparently, grudges.

"Lay her on the table," Maren said, before he'd said anything at all.

"How did you—"

"You walk like a man carrying a baby, boy. Everyone walks like that carrying a baby. They think they're being subtle. They're not." She swept a pile of mending aside and folded a blanket into a nest with brisk, practiced motions. "Coat off. Out. Let me see her."

He unwrapped the oilskin with hands that shook more than he liked. The baby woke during the transfer, considered the situation, and decided — for reasons known only to infants and cats — not to cry. She blinked up at Maren with the grave, ancient expression all newborns wear, as if they've just resigned from an important position and are still deciding whether to mention it.

Maren counted fingers first. Orsen watched her do it, puzzled, as she took each tiny hand in turn and pressed each finger individually, murmuring under her breath. Ten. Then toes. Ten again. Then she turned the child gently, checked her ears, her spine, the soft spot on her skull, pressing here and listening there with the focus of a woman reading a very small, very important document.

"Well?" Orsen asked.

"She's perfect, which is suspicious," Maren said. "Babies born right are either loved or lied about." She looked up at him then, really looked, and her voice dropped into something gentler than he'd ever heard from her. "Whose is she? And don't tell me you found her, because you have the face of a man who found her and wishes he hadn't."

"I found her," Orsen said. "On the warehouse steps. Last night. In the storm."

"And?"

"And there was a note." He hesitated. Telling Maren meant telling someone, and telling someone meant it was real, and real things could be taken away. But he'd already crossed the harbor with her under his coat. There was no un-crossing. "It said *forget this face*. And the handwriting—" His throat closed. He tried again. "I know the handwriting. Sera Voss. Pearl diver's widow. They said she drowned two years back, same season as the fever took the island station."

Maren had gone very still. Stillness, on Maren Quill, was louder than shouting.

Then she picked the swaddling cloth up off the table — the inner layer beneath the oilskin, the one the baby had been wrapped in before someone added the waterproof shell — and held it to the lantern light.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you foolish, brave woman."

"What?"

Maren didn't answer right away. She ran the cloth between finger and thumb, stretched it, sniffed it, held it close to her eye the way an archivist might hold a page. Her jaw worked.

"This isn't Greyhaven weaving," she said. "Feel it. Go on, feel it."

He did. The cloth was soft but dense, dyed a deep sea-green gone slightly uneven, and when he rubbed it between his own fingers he felt them — tiny ridges, hard little knots scattered through the weave like grains of sand sewn in on purpose.

"Grit in the cloth?"

"Not grit." Maren's voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go around dangerous facts. "Pearl nacre. Ground shell, worked into the dye. Island divers' wives used to do it — said it kept the sea's luck in the cloth and the sharks' luck out. Every diver's family on Saltmere wove like that. You could tell which household a wrapping came from by the pattern of the flecks." She laid the cloth down with exaggerated care, as if it might bite. "This is Saltmere work. Station work."

"The station burned with fever," Orsen said. "Two years ago. Everyone knows."

"Everyone knows what they were told." Maren poured tea — three cups, though neither of them had asked, and pushed one into his hands like a weapon she expected him to need. "I'll tell you what *I* know, and you can decide what to do with it, because I'm too old to decide other people's business for them anymore, though I keep trying."

She sat down across from him, and the baby, sensing narrative gravity, chose that moment to yawn enormously.

"Two winters past, before the 'fever,'" Maren said, making the word sound like something she'd scraped off her boot, "a boat came through Greyhaven carrying eleven women off Saltmere. Divers' wives, sisters, a mother or two. Said the Company was moving families to the mainland 'for their health.' I attended three births that winter for those women — here, on this barge, because they had no money and I have no sense. They talked. Women talk when they're pushing out babies, boy, they talk about everything, and what they talked about was contracts. Pearl contracts. The divers had signed seven-year terms, same as any indenture, and the terms were coming due, and the Company owed out — by the women's counting, and islanders count pearls the way misers count gold — the Company owed out a fortune in shares and bonuses."

Orsen's tea had stopped tasting like anything. He wasn't sure he was breathing.

"And then the fever came," he said.

"And then the fever came," Maren agreed. "And the station was 'lost,' and the ledgers were 'lost,' and the debt was 'lost,' all conveniently lost together, like socks in a wash. Eleven women came through my hands that winter, and I buried four of them since — fever, they said, again, always fever, Greyhaven's fever is a very hardworking disease, it kills whoever the Company needs dead." She drank her tea. Her hand was steady. Her eyes were not. "And now a Saltmere-wrapped baby turns up on your Company's doorstep with a note from a drowned woman, telling you to forget her face."

The stove popped. Somewhere outside, a gull complained about the economy.

"So the question," Maren said, "is not whether that child is from Saltmere. She is. The question is whether Sera Voss sent you that baby because she wanted her hidden—" she tapped the table once, "—or because she wanted her *found*."

Orsen looked down at the baby. She had fallen asleep again mid-yawn, mouth open, utterly trusting, in the middle of a conversation that was deciding the shape of her whole life. Something in his chest went tight and hot.

"Forget this face," he repeated quietly. "That's what the note said. But why write a note at all? Why leave her anywhere? If you want a child forgotten, there are quieter doors than a Company warehouse."

"There are," Maren agreed. "Church steps. Foundling wheels. Any doorstep in the Low Quarter, and no questions asked by morning." She leaned forward. "She left that baby at the one place in Greyhaven where forgetting is a profession. Either she's the biggest fool ever to swim out of Saltmere—" 

"—or she knew exactly whose hands she was putting her into," Orsen finished.

They looked at each other across the table, across the sleeping child, and Orsen felt something shift in his chest — the strange, frightening loosening of a man realizing he has just become part of a plot. He had spent seven years cultivating the fine art of not being part of anything.

"I should give her to the parish," he said, without meaning it, testing the words like rotten boards. "Report it. Say I found her, let the Church sort it out. That's the safe thing."

"Mm," said Maren.

"No one would ever connect me to her. No one would ask questions. I'd go back to stacking crates and keeping my head down and—"

"Mm," said Maren again, with the eloquence of a woman who has heard men talk themselves out of courage a thousand times and learned that silence works faster.

"—and she'd grow up in a foundling house with a number instead of a name," Orsen said, "and if anyone from the Company ever wondered what happened to the last piece of Saltmere, they'd know exactly which number to come asking after." He set the cup down. His hand had stopped shaking somewhere in the last minute, and he noted it the way you note weather changing. "There's no version of this where she's safe and quiet. There's only versions where she's safe and loud, or quiet and lost."

"Now you're thinking," Maren said. She reached over and adjusted the blanket around the baby with two fingers, a gesture so tender it seemed embarrassed to be seen on such a formidable woman. "Here's what I know about you, Orsen Coldharbour. Three years ago you brought me a man bleeding from a winch accident and you paid me with money you didn't have, and when I asked how you'd eat that month you said 'carefully.' You count fingers too. You just pretend you're counting coins."

He hadn't known she remembered that. It went through him warmer than the tea.

"So," Maren said, rubbing her hands together. "You can't keep her at the warehouse. You can't keep her at your bunk — I assume you sleep in a coffin with forty other coffins, all Company men. Which means, much as it pains my reputation, she stays here."

Orsen stared. "Maren. If anyone finds out—"

"If anyone finds out, I'm an old widow midwife who took in a foundling, which is half the women on this waterfront, and none of them have been arrested yet. Whereas *you*" — she pointed the fishhook from her hair at him, having apparently removed it for emphasis — "are indentured property. If they catch you with her, they don't fine you. They *own* the consequences. So we do this properly. You visit like a man visiting a midwife about a sick friend. You bring food, because visitors bring food, that's the custom and also I'm poor. And you start thinking about who else knows things."

"Who else—"

"Fenwick," Maren said.

The name landed like a dropped crate. Tobias Fenwick. Once the Meridian Company's chief archivist, the man who had stood up at a shareholders' meeting six years ago and declared — loudly, drunkenly, and correctly — that the Company's death tallies were lies. Now he lived above the Broken Keel and drank his disgrace like it was on a ration and he intended to make it last.

"He won't help," Orsen said. "Everyone says he's finished. Pickled. He threw away his whole life on the truth and the truth didn't save anybody, and men like that don't get up twice."

"Men like that don't get up twice on their own," Maren corrected. "But they get up when someone puts something in front of them worth standing for. And you, boy, are currently holding the only thing in Greyhaven worth standing for." She nodded at the baby. "Besides. Fenwick kept records like a priest keeps sins. If there's paper proving what happened at Saltmere — real paper, dated, signed — it passed through his hands. And men like Fenwick never trust the Company's copies of anything."

Outside, the fog pressed against the windows and the tide muttered against the hull. The baby slept on, ten fingers, ten toes, wrapped in the luck of a drowned island, breathing in and out with the absolute confidence of someone who does not yet know what she costs the people who love her.

Orsen looked at her small, furious, perfect face and felt the last of his old rules — *stay quiet, stay small, stay alive* — loosen another notch. Not break. Not yet. But loosen, like a knot someone patient was working at from the other end.

"All right," he said. "Tomorrow I'll go see Fenwick."

"Tonight," Maren said, gathering the oilskin and the swaddling cloth and folding them away into a jar-lined cabinet with the reverence of a woman hiding relics. "Because tomorrow, boy, you'll find a reason to wait until the day after. That's what fear does — it sells you tomorrows. Buy it back now while it's cheap."

She was right, and he hated it, and he stood, and she pressed a parcel of bread and cold eel pie into his arms — *"visitors bring food, and you'll be visiting again tomorrow"* — and at the door she caught his sleeve.

"One more thing." Her voice had gone quiet and serious. "The woman who wrote that note. Sera Voss. If she's alive — and a drowned woman doesn't embroider notes, boy, drowning doesn't leave you the use of your hands — then she's alive *on purpose*, and scared *for a reason*, and whatever drove her to give up that baby is still out there driving." Her grip tightened. "Whatever you do next, do it like you're being watched. Because in Greyhaven, you always are."

He walked back through the fog with eel pie under his arm and a name in his mouth — *Fenwick, Fenwick* — rehearsing arguments to a drunk archivist who had every reason to hate hope.

Behind him, on a barge full of dying herbs kept alive out of spite, a Saltmere child slept in the smell of tea and dried mint, and the tide came in, and the record of the world — though no one aboard knew it yet — had just grown one page heavier.
