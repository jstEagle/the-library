## 5. Ledgers Do Not Lie, Men Do

The garret above the Rusted Compass smelled of lamp oil, mildew, and the particular sourness of a man who had stopped expecting company.

Orsen knew this because he stood outside its door for the better part of a quarter hour, eel pie long gone cold under his arm, listening to the sounds within: the scrape of a chair, the slosh of something poured, and — this was what made him stay rather than turn back — the scratch of a pen. Drunks who had given up did not write at four in the afternoon. Drunks who had given up stared at walls and accused them of crimes. This one was still arguing with paper.

He knocked.

The scratching stopped. A long silence followed, of the sort Orsen had learned to read the way other men read weather. It was not the silence of someone deciding whether to answer. It was the silence of someone deciding whether answering would be worth the trouble of standing up.

"It's locked," said a voice like gravel left out in the rain.

"I can hear you writing," Orsen said. "Locked doors don't usually come with clerks."

Another silence. Then footsteps, uneven, and the door opened perhaps eight inches on a chain that was, in Orsen's professional assessment, purely ceremonial — anyone with a shoulder and a grievance could have had it. What showed through the gap was half a face: grey stubble, a nose that had been broken and set by an optimist, and one eye that was disappointingly clear. He had expected the eye to be bleared. It was not. That was either very good news or very bad news, and Orsen suspected the latter.

"You've got pie," Fenwick said, looking down at the bundle under Orsen's arm.

"You've got penmanship," Orsen said. "I thought we might trade."

The eye considered him for a long moment. "You're Company."

It wasn't a question, and there was no use denying it; seven years of Meridian livery wore its own accent. "Warehouse," Orsen said. "Third tier. Orsen Coldharbour."

"Coldharbour." Fenwick pronounced it the way a man pronounces the name of a fish he has already decided not to buy. "I know every indent on the warehouse rolls from nine years back. There's no Coldharbour."

"There's an O. Coldwater," Orsen said quietly. "Entered at eleven. Father listed as deceased, cause 'misadventure, marine.'"

Something moved behind the clear eye — quick, quickly smothered, like a match struck and cupped out. Fenwick shut the door.

Orsen counted to ten. The chain rattled; the door opened all the way.

"Well," Fenwick said, stepping back into his own mess, "you'd better come in before someone sees me being hospitable. I have a reputation."

* * *

The reputation, as far as Orsen could see through the gloom, consisted chiefly of empty bottles arranged along the windowsill like a fleet at anchor, and papers everywhere. Not scattered — *arranged*. Stacked in towers that leaned against each other with the weary trust of old drunks holding each other upright. Some stacks were labeled in a cramped hand: *Petitions. Payroll (forged). Correspondence, burned (copies).* One tower near the single window was labeled simply *GHOSTS*, and Fenwick caught him looking and turned it slightly so the label faced the wall.

"Sit anywhere that isn't load-bearing," Fenwick said. He cleared a chair by lifting an entire stratum of documents onto the floor, where they landed with a sigh. "Pie."

Orsen handed it over. Fenwick unwrapped it, ate three bites with sudden startling efficiency, then put the rest down as though he'd remembered food was also a negotiation. He poured two glasses of something amber and terrible and pushed one across.

"That's not a drink," Orsen said, sniffing it. "That's a threat."

"Everything in this room is a threat. Drink it anyway. It helps the conversation go faster, and I want it over."

Orsen drank. It went down like a disagreement and settled in his stomach like a grudge. "They said you were drunk."

"They say I'm a lot of things. Mostly they say nothing, which is cheaper." Fenwick sat back, lacing his fingers over his knee — clerk's hands, ink-stained at the second knuckle even now, even after everything. "So. O. Coldwater, entered at eleven, father misadventure marine. You found something in the ledgers, or you found a baby, and now you're here. Which?"

Orsen kept his face still. Seven years of practice held it still. But Fenwick watched it hold, and laughed — a short, rusted sound with no pleasure in it.

"There it is," he said. "That little nothing you just did with your mouth. I used to do interviews for the Claims Office, boy. I've watched forty men decide whether to lie to me. You're better than most. You're still lying."

"The station at Saltmere didn't die of fever," Orsen said.

The room changed. Not dramatically — Fenwick didn't move, didn't spill anything, didn't do any of the theatrical things a man does in a story. He simply went *still*, the way water goes still over something deep, and the amber glass in his hand stopped halfway to his mouth and stayed there, forgotten, while the lamplight moved slowly in it.

"Say that again," he said.

"You know it's true. That's why your hand stopped."

Fenwick set the glass down with exaggerated care, as if it were suddenly full of nitroglycerine, which in this city it might as well have been. He leaned forward, and up close Orsen could see the fine tremor in him, the constant low vibration of a man who had spent years being afraid and had built his whole life around the shaking.

"How much do you know?" Fenwick asked. "Actually know. Not rumor. Not dock talk. Know."

Orsen laid it out plainly: the child on the steps, Sera Voss's hand on the note, Maren's reading of the swaddling cloth, the pearl-callus of island work, the names of divers officially lost to fever whose widows had never once received the standard condolence letter because — Maren had noticed this, and told him on the barge with her arms folded — the Company only sends condolence letters when there is a payroll record to attach them to, and there was no payroll record, because the divers of Saltmere had been struck from the rolls six weeks *before* the fever was supposed to have killed them.

He had rehearsed none of this on the walk over and regretted it now; it came out of him in the order grief stores things, not the order argument wants them. But Fenwick listened the way Orsen imagined he had once listened to testimony — utterly, hungrily, his eyes closed for part of it, mouthing dates.

When Orsen finished, Fenwick opened his eyes and said, "Six weeks. You're certain about the six weeks."

"Maren Quill counted the condolence letters. She buries people for a living. She doesn't lose count."

"No," Fenwick said softly. "No, she wouldn't." He got up, crossed to the tower labeled *GHOSTS*, and after a moment's visible struggle with himself — Orsen watched a man argue with his own past and lose — pulled a thin folder from the middle of it and dropped it on the table. "Look."

Inside were copies. Orsen recognized the copying hand immediately — it was the same cramped script that labeled the towers, pressed hard, a man writing things twice so they couldn't be unwritten once. Petitions from Saltmere divers requesting contract renegotiation. A requisition order for medical supplies to the station, dated, stamped *FULFILLED* — and beneath it, in the Company's own internal annotation, a note in another hand: *Do not fulfill. Station scheduled for closure Q3.*

"Scheduled for closure," Orsen read aloud. "In the same quarter it drowned in fever."

"With full medical supplies ordered, paid for, and never sent." Fenwick's voice had gone flat and careful, a man walking on ice he already knows is thin. "I filed the discrepancy. Twice. The first time I was told it was a clerical error. The second time I was told my services were no longer required at the Claims Office, and offered a position counting bolts in the Saltmarsh depot, which is where careers go to be buried alive. I declined. I wrote instead — a full report, thirty-one pages, with exhibits. Sent it up through channels. Channels being, as I learned somewhat later, a euphemism."

"What happened to the report?"

"You're drinking it," Fenwick said, and nodded at the bottle. "Metaphorically. Vane came himself, which I took at the time as an honor. He brought a fire bucket and a very reasonable tone. He explained to me — reasonably — that the report contained errors, and that a man of my talents shouldn't endanger his pension over errors, and that my landlady's son had recently applied to the maritime conscription lottery, and how fortunate that the Company had influence in such matters. Then he waited while I burned it. All thirty-one pages. He was very patient. He even held the bucket." Fenwick smiled, and it was the worst thing Orsen had seen in some time, worse than Dull's smile, because it wasn't aimed at anyone. "There's a kind of courtesy, boy, that's worse than cruelty. Cruelty at least admits it's fighting you."

Orsen sat with that. Outside, somewhere below, a gull screamed at another gull about something neither of them would get.

"I'm not asking you to write a report," Orsen said finally.

"Good, because—"

"I'm asking you to tell me where the numbers live."

Fenwick blinked. "What?"

"You tried the honest road. Up through channels. Thirty-one pages, exhibits, sealed with your name." Orsen leaned forward. "It went into a fire and you went into a bottle, and the Company went on doing whatever companies do. Fine. I understand why you'd never take that road again, and I'm not going to insult you by asking. But you were Chief Archivist's second for nine years, and men like you don't burn everything. You *copy*. You copied these petitions. You copied the requisition stamp. You kept a folder called GHOSTS on a windowsill in a garret where anyone with a shoulder and a grievance could kick in your chain." He nodded at the door. "You kept duplicates, Mr. Fenwick. Everyone who's ever been wronged by a ledger keeps duplicates. Where are the rest?"

For a moment Orsen thought the man would throw him out. The tremor came up Fenwick's arms and into his jaw and he gripped the edge of the table to stop it and the wood creaked faintly under his hands, and Orsen understood suddenly that underneath the ruin, underneath the rusted laugh and the ceremonial chain, Tobias Fenwick was strong the way old rope is strong — frayed everywhere and unbroken where it mattered.

"You think I haven't thought about it?" Fenwick said quietly. "Every day for six years? I wake up and I think: today I'll send it. Today I'll take the copies to the *Courier*, to a delegate, to anyone, and today it ends, one way or the other. And then I think about the fire bucket. And I think about my landlady's boy hauling nets somewhere north of here because of me. And I pour a drink." He picked up the bottle, looked at it with something close to hatred, and put it down without pouring. Small victory. Orsen noted it and gave it weight. "And then I sleep, which is the whole point of the drink. It isn't cowardice, exactly. It's arithmetic. One drunk archivist against the Meridian Trading Company, and the answer comes out the same every morning."

"It's different arithmetic now."

"Is it." Flat. Unmoved.

"There's a child," Orsen said. "Eight weeks old. She has nobody. Her mother is hiding in a laudanum den with her face ruined because she carried evidence out of Saltmere instead of drowning quietly like she was supposed to. The Company wants this baby erased before the Accord is signed, and in six weeks the delegates will sit down in Greyhaven and write the official history, and if we do nothing, the history will say fever, and it will always say fever, forever, because victors' ink doesn't fade." His voice stayed level. He had practiced that too. "You asked what I know. Here's what I know that isn't in your folders. My father dove at a Company station for eleven years and they wrote *misadventure, marine* on his death and *volunteer labor* on his memorial wall, and I have spent seven years learning to keep my mouth shut so well that I nearly taught myself to believe it. I am done believing it. I came here because everyone says Tobias Fenwick was the last honest clerk the Company ever employed, and I hoped it was still true under the bottles." He stood. "It evidently isn't. Thank you for the drink. It was terrible."

He got two steps to the door.

"Wait." Fenwick's voice cracked on it. "Boy. *Wait.*"

Orsen turned.

Fenwick stood among his towers of paper with the lamplight behind him, and his face had come apart somehow — the cynical armor slipped sideways, and underneath was something older and rawer, a man looking at a door he had nailed shut years ago and finding it standing open. "Even if — *if* — I still had anything worth having," he said carefully, "what would you do? You're an indentured warehouseman. I'm a disgraced drunk. Your allies are a smuggler midwife and a widow in hiding and a baby. Against Vane. Against Dull. Against the entire weight of the Company and its lawyers and its fixers and its fire buckets. Tell me the sum, Coldharbour. Show me the arithmetic that doesn't end with all of us in the harbor."

"The sum ends with us in the harbor either way," Orsen said. "The only question the ledger asks is whether we're in it silent or on the record. I know which line I'd rather appear on."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Downstairs, in the Rusted Compass, somebody began badly singing a song about a girl from Saltpans, and the song was so cheerfully awful that it seemed to break something loose in the room, because Fenwick laughed again — really laughed this time, helplessly, wiping his eyes with an ink-stained thumb — and then stopped as abruptly as he'd started, and looked at Orsen with the expression of a gambler pushing his last coin to the center of a table he knows is rigged.

"If I told you," he said slowly, "that before my disgrace — before the bucket — I removed certain materials from the archive and placed them somewhere the Company would never look for them... somewhere damp, and consecrated, and full of dead people who owe me favors..." His hand was shaking openly now and he let it. "If I told you there exists a complete duplicate of the Saltmere station ledger, payroll through closure, in the Provost-Marsh's own hand ordering the rolls struck — would that change your evening plans?"

Orsen found he could not speak for a moment. When he did, his voice came out low and steady, which surprised him.

"Yes," he said. "And yours."

Fenwick looked at the bottle. He looked at the folder marked GHOSTS. He looked, last, at the window, where the fog was coming in off the harbor the way it always did, indifferent and grey, covering everything equally, the honest ships and the dishonest ones alike.

"St. Brannock's chapel," he said. "The crypt floods every spring tide. I put them in the third niche, behind the Voss family vault, because—" his voice failed briefly, and he steadied it "—because it seemed right that they should rest with divers. Six years, and I never told a soul. Not even sober." He picked up his coat, and it was astonishing how much the simple act of putting it on changed him — squared the shoulders, dried the eyes, put a spine back into the stoop. "Well. Apparently I've told one now."

He reached the door before Orsen did and stopped, hand on the latch, not looking back.

"One condition, Coldharbour. If those ledgers come out of that crypt, they go somewhere permanent. Not to a reporter who can be sued into silence. Not up channels. Somewhere they cannot be burned, bought, or misplaced. You swear me that first, or I'll pour the drink and forget your name by morning."

"I swear it," Orsen said. "On my father's name. The real one."

Fenwick nodded once, opened the door, and then paused again, and glanced back over his shoulder with the ghost of the rusted smile.

"You should know," he said, "that Vane holds the fire bucket personally. If he finds out about tonight — and he finds out about everything eventually, it's his one genuine talent — he won't offer me the bucket this time." He stepped out into the fog ahead of Orsen, coat collar up, a disgraced archivist walking toward his own ghosts at last. "He'll offer it to the child."
