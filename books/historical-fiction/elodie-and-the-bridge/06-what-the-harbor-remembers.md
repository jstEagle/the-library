## 6. What the Harbor Remembers

Maren Quill had delivered four hundred and eleven babies in her life, and she had learned one thing above all others from the work: everything a body needed to know, it told you early, if you were willing to look.

The cloth was talking already. She'd seen it the moment Orsen laid the bundle on her table and unwound the first fold — that particular weave, tight as a closed fist, dyed the brownish-red that only came from ochre pits on the pearling islands. Mainland folk didn't make it. Mainland folk didn't know how. She had turned the swaddling over twice under her lamp while Orsen hovered like a nervous gull, and she had said nothing, because some things you say out loud and some things you carry until you're sure of their weight.

The baby, at least, was thriving. She had a good set of lungs, an imperious way of demanding her supper, and — Maren counted them again that morning, out of pure superstition — ten fingers and ten toes, every one perfect.

"There," Maren told her, tickling a heel the size of a walnut. "You're the healthiest thing to come off those docks in twenty years, and I include the cargo."

Wren — for so Maren had privately begun calling her, though no one had agreed on it yet; the girl made a small clacking sound in her throat when she slept, exactly like the birds that nested under the barge, and Maren did not think names should wait on committees — Wren blinked up at her with the profound, unimpressed dignity of the very young.

Outside, Greyhaven did what Greyhaven always did: rained sideways, smelled of fish and tar, and went about its business pretending the fog was weather rather than a habit. The tide was low. Low tide was when the harbor gave things back.

Maren wrapped the swaddling cloth in her good shawl, put Wren in the sling against her chest — the baby approved of this arrangement enormously, being carried, and kicked her approval — and went out onto the deck where Brannigan kept his boat.

Brannigan was seventy if he was a day, missing two fingers and most of his teeth, and had been salvaging wrecks in Greyhaven Harbor since before the war, which made him either the luckiest or the least observant man alive. He owed Maren for delivering his granddaughter, and he paid his debts the old way: grudgingly, and in kind.

"I need to know where oilskin comes ashore," Maren said.

Brannigan spat thoughtfully over the rail. "That's a wide question."

"It's a wide harbor."

"Oilskin comes off courier boats mostly." He squinted at her. "Off *wrecked* courier boats, if it's coming ashore torn and salt-stiff and smelling of three weeks' drowning. You got a specific piece in mind?"

She unwrapped the corner of the shawl enough to show him. His eyebrows climbed — both of them, a rare event — and he looked from the cloth to her face and back down, and something behind his eyes went careful.

"That's island work," he said.

"It is."

"And you're holding it on my deck."

"I am."

A long pause. Somewhere across the water a bell buoy clanged its idiot head off, as it did every hour of every day, faithfully reporting nothing to no one.

"The *Merrow's Lament*," Brannigan said at last, very quietly. "Courier packet, Company-chartered. Went down off the spit six weeks back — they called it ballast shift. Came apart on the shallows and everything loose washed into the eel-grass flats by the old powder jetty. Salvage crew went through twice." He rubbed his remaining fingers together. "Third time's usually the charm, but I'm an old man and I move slow."

"Brannigan."

"I never heard nothing," he said firmly. "I'm saying if a man *were* to walk the flats tomorrow at first light, before the mud dries, he might find whatever the salvage crews judged not worth carrying. And if that man happened to be a woman with a baby, well. Babies are lucky. Everyone knows it."

* * *

First light on the eel-grass flats was grey and cold and beautiful in the way that Greyhaven managed perhaps nine days a year, when the fog lifted high enough to show the horizon and the whole city seemed surprised by it. The flats stretched out gleaming, ribbed with sand and pocked with crab holes, littered with the harbor's leavings: rope, bottle glass, a boot, half a crate stenciled MERIDIAN TRA in flaking paint.

Wren rode the sling, asleep, her breath making warm damp spots against Maren's collarbone. Maren walked with her skirts knotted to the knee and her boots sinking with every step, and she worked the way she'd worked oyster beds in her girlhood: systematically, patiently, letting the ground tell her where to look.

The harbor remembers. That was something her mother used to say, back when Maren was small and the islands still had names anyone spoke aloud. The sea takes and the sea keeps, but the harbor remembers. Everything that floats eventually comes home to the shallows.

She found the first piece within the hour: a strip of oilskin, same weave, same stiffening, torn clean as if cut. Then a brass buckle of the pattern the Company issued to its couriers. Then, wedged deep under a mat of eel-grass with the roots grown right through its folds, a satchel.

Her heart did something complicated. She knelt in the mud, checked that Wren still slept, and worked the satchel free with both hands.

It was courier-issue, lead-lined — meant to keep papers dry past all decency — and it was locked, but the lock had been underwater for six weeks and locks are honest about such things. It gave to a firm twist. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper that had done its job perfectly, she found:

One shipping log, water-spotted but legible, recording the *Merrow's Lament*'s final run.

And one note, folded twice, in a hand she recognized even before she opened it — because she had seen it before, two years ago, on petitions and letters and once on a child's naming record, in a neat, educated, desperate script. Sera Voss. The diver's widow who was supposed to be drowned.

Maren's hands were steady. Midwives' hands learn steadiness young; you do not shake while you work or people die. But something behind the steadiness had gone very cold and very quiet.

She opened the note standing hip-deep in the freezing mud with her dead friend's handwriting in front of her, and she read it, and then she sat down in the mud, because her legs had opinions of their own.

It wasn't a letter. It was a list.

Nine names. Nine names in Sera Voss's hand, each with a date beside it, each date falling in the autumn two years past, each name followed by a single word in smaller script: *confirmed.*

Denna Marsh. Old Tam. The brothers Kell. Sweet foolish Anselm who sang to the oyster beds. Little Pip Okafor, who everyone had laughed at for diving at his age, and who had waved at Maren from the water the last morning she ever saw the station.

Every name on it was a name she knew. Every name on it was a name she had buried.

* * *

Here is what Maren Quill had done two years ago, and here is why she had not slept properly since:

The fever story had come to Greyhaven ahead of the station's closing — sickness on the pearl beds, quarantine, tragic losses, the Company's sincere condolences printed in the *Courier* in a tasteful border. And then, weeks later, the bodies had started washing north with the currents, and someone — the Company, through intermediaries, through money passed to men who didn't ask questions — had arranged for them to be handled. Quietly. Without ceremony. Without, above all, without records.

The work had come to Maren because she was known on the waterfront for doing necessary things cleanly, and because she could certify a death in a way that satisfied the harbormaster's office without inviting the coroner. She had been paid well. She had told herself at the time that it was mercy — that these drowned men and women deserved graves, or the nearest thing Greyhaven offered to graves, instead of the lime pit — and that telling herself so had been true, mostly, in the way that most comfortable lies are true.

But here was the thing she had never said aloud, the thing that had lived in her chest like a swallowed hook for two years:

They hadn't looked drowned.

Oh, they were wet. They were bloated and sea-touched and terrible, and a person who didn't know bodies might have believed it. But Maren Quill had pulled more than four hundred children into the world and closed the eyes of a fair number of people leaving it, and she knew what drowning did and what it didn't. Drowning left weed in the fists. It tore fingernails. These bodies had none of it — clean hands, unmarked, several with wrists showing the bruise-patterns of cord. And one of them, little Pip Okafor, had had a wound under his ear that she had recognized at the time and named to no one, because the moment she named it she would have to act on it, and acting on it would get her killed and change nothing.

A well. She remembered, later, hearing secondhand about a well at the station — how during the fever, supposedly, the sick had been lowered into the cistern to cool. A well that people came out of with cord-marks on their wrists and wounds behind their ears and no water-weed anywhere on them.

She had sewn them into canvas with her own hands, given each one a stone and a prayer and the only dignity available, and signed certificates that said *drowned, recovered from the sea*, and taken the money, and gone home, and poured herself very drunk, and woken the next morning and delivered twins in Salt Row, because the world does not pause for grief it hasn't officially noticed.

Now she sat in the mud of the eel-grass flats with a list of those same names in the hand of a woman the Company said was dead, and every name had the word *confirmed* after it, and Maren understood, with the awful clarity that arrives too late and exactly on time, what she had been part of.

Not graves. Disposal. Evidence management.

And Sera Voss — who must have been on that courier boat, who must have been gathering proof, who must have written this list as a tally of her husband's fellows — Sera Voss had survived whatever sank the *Merrow's Lament*, crawled out of the water with her evidence, borne a child, and left the child on a warehouse step with three words scratched in oilskin: *Forget this face.*

Forget. As if forgetting were a kindness. As if it were possible.

Wren stirred against her chest and made her small bird-clack, and Maren put her hand over the tiny back and felt the quick flutter of a heartbeat going about its innocent business, and the cold thing inside her stopped being cold and became something else entirely.

"You," Maren said softly, to the sleeping baby, to the harbor, to the Company in general, "have made a serious error. You sent her to a midwife."

* * *

Back at the barge, she dried the log and the list by the stove, laid them flat under a board and three bricks, fed Wren until the girl achieved the boneless milk-drunk state that Maren considered the eighth wonder of the world, and then sat down to think with a cup of chicory going cold at her elbow.

Think, Quill. You've spent thirty years keeping your head below the windowsill. What's changed?

Everything, she supposed. Nothing. She ran the arithmetic the way she ran her accounts, unsentimentally.

Item: a living witness to the massacre existed, hidden somewhere in Greyhaven, hunted, and Maren now knew her handwriting and her desperation. Item: a courier boat had been sunk — *sunk*, ballast-shift be damned; she'd seen the manifest weight in that satchel and knew a scuttling when she held one — specifically to stop this evidence, which meant the Company considered the evidence worth killing couriers over, which meant the evidence was real. Item: she personally held proof that the Company had murdered nine people she had, unknowingly, helped bury — which meant that whether she lifted a finger or not, she was already in it. Her certificates existed in the harbormaster's files. Sooner or later, someone tidy on the Company side would notice that a midwife had certified nine drownings with no inquests, and would wonder what else the midwife knew, and would come asking in the polite manner that got people fitted for lime-pit canvas.

So the choice she'd told herself she was weighing — stay out of it or step in — was no choice at all. She was in it. She had been in it for two years. The only question left was whether she stood in it as an accomplice or an answer.

"Well," she told Wren, who was gnawing on her own fist with scholarly focus, "your Aunt Maren has been a coward on layaway, and today she's paying it off."

There was a knock at the hull — the particular knock Brannigan used, two-and-one — and she let him in long enough to hand him the shipping log.

"Salvage scrap," she said. "Metal fittings are worth money. Paper rots. Do what you like with it, only lose it thoroughly, and lose any memory of walking the flats."

"Never walked any flats," Brannigan said, taking the log without looking at it, in the manner of men who have survived long wars by seeing nothing. He paused at the door. "The mother. The little one. She'll need wet-nurse milk soon if she's not on it already, and goat's milk boiled won't do forever. Widow Coby on the coal barge is drying off from her own, and she's honest, and she's quiet."

"She's also Company kin," Maren said sharply.

"Distant. And poor enough to hate them proper." Brannigan shrugged. "Your call. But babies don't keep, Maren. That's the one rule older than the Company."

He left. Maren stood a while in the doorway watching the fog roll back in over the water, folding the horizon away again like a sheet drawn over furniture in a closed house.

Two years ago the harbor had handed her nine bodies and she had buried the truth along with them. Today it had handed her a list and a name and a child, and the harbor, it turned out, was not finished. The harbor remembered, and it was passing its memory along to anyone willing to walk the flats at low tide, and she suspected — with the professional instinct that had never once been wrong about a labor, reading the signs before the mother felt the first pain — that it was only beginning to deliver.

Because there was one more thing, the thing she kept turning over like a coin she couldn't identify. Why now? The massacre was two years old. The courier was six weeks sunk. The baby was mere days old. Whatever game was being played, it was being played *urgently*, and urgent games in Greyhaven meant politics, and politics this season meant only one thing.

She'd heard the dockhands shouting about it that very morning, giddy with it: the peace delegates were coming. Six weeks out. The Greyhaven Accord, signed here, in this fog-bound backwater, chosen precisely because nothing important was supposed to ever happen here.

Six weeks. Nine dead divers. One widow writing lists. One baby nobody was meant to remember.

Maren picked up the list of names, folded it small, and sewed it into the hem of her winter skirt with sailmaker's thread — where it would sit, in perpetuity, next to her left knee.

Then she went to check on Wren, found her awake and furious about it, scooped her up, and danced her around the cramped cabin to a tuneless hum, and when the baby finally grinned at her — a gummy, ruthless, entire grin — Maren laughed out loud for the first time in longer than she cared to count.

"Oh, you're trouble," she said fondly. "Good. We're going to need it. Now let's go find out what your Orsen knows about archives, because I've a feeling we're about to need a man who reads."
