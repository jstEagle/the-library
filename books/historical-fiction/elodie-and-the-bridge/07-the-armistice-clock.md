## 7. The Armistice Clock

The news came to Greyhaven the way most news did: on paper first, then in shouts, then in the particular silence of men doing arithmetic they didn't like.

Orsen heard it third-hand from a stevedore named Brakka, who heard it from a customs clerk, who heard it from a man whose cousin swept floors at the Harbormaster's Hall. By midmorning it was everywhere — chalked on warehouse doors, argued over in fish queues, sung badly by drunks outside the Saltcellar.

*Delegates come in six weeks. Six weeks, and Greyhaven signs.*

"The Greyhaven Accord," Brakka said, leaning against a bollard with the satisfaction of a man who had been saying something important all morning and intended to keep at it. "Peace papers, signed right here. In our harbor. You believe that? All them great men coming to *our* fog."

"Someone has to sign it somewhere," Orsen said, coiling rope because his hands wanted occupation and his head did not want conversation.

"Not *somewhere*. *Here*." Brakka spread his arms at the docks — the cranes, the tar-smell, the gulls wheeling over fish guts with the entitlement of minor nobility. "They could've signed it in the capital, all marble and wine. Instead they come here. Know why?"

Orsen knew why, or suspected he did. The war had ended the way wars involving the Meridian Trading Company tended to end — with the Company's ledgers heavier and everyone else's lighter — and the victors needed a stage where the spoils would look like settlement rather than plunder. Greyhaven was Company country, top to bottom. A treaty signed in its shadow would read as graciousness.

"No," he said. "Why?"

"Because we're neutral ground now, ain't we." Brakka tapped his nose. "That's what my cousin says. Neutral. Like a church."

Like a church, Orsen thought, watching a Meridian tally-clerk cross the yard with a ledger under his arm and two guards behind him. Yes. Neutral like a church, with the Company holding the collection plate.

He said none of this. Seven years had taught him the exact weight of things not worth saying aloud, and criticism of the Company while standing on Company stone topped the list by several hundredweight.

But the number stuck to him anyway, the way tar stuck to everything in Greyhaven.

*Six weeks.*

---

It should have been good news. That was the strange part. Peace was good news; even the gulls seemed to sense it, screaming their approval at nothing. The war had bled the coast for eleven years — taken ships, sons, and half the pearling fleet's protection — and now it was ending on paper instead of ending nowhere, which was how most things in Greyhaven ended.

And yet by evening Orsen found himself unable to shake a feeling he couldn't name until he'd carried Wren's memory of that oilskin bundle around inside him for long enough to understand what it was.

It was the feeling of watching a door begin to close.

---

He saw the proof of it three days later, and he saw it because of his rules.

Rule four: *Be where you're told, doing what you're told, looking bored.* Bored men are invisible. Interested men are remembered. So when Supervisor Corvane Dull summoned the warehouse crew into the main hall one gray morning — an event so unprecedented that half the men assumed they were about to be conscripted — Orsen arranged his face into the flat patience of a man expecting nothing, and stood in the second row, slightly left of center, where supervisors' eyes slid off people like rain off oilskin.

Dull stood on a crate at the front, hands clasped behind his back, wearing the expression he reserved for occasions he considered beneath him but profitable. Behind him, two clerks unrolled a banner across the far wall. It took them a moment, wrestling with the cloth, and then it hung there in letters of gilt paint:

**GREYHAVEN ACCORD — CEREMONY OF RECONCILIATION AND REMEMBRANCE**

Below it, smaller:

*Honoring Those Lost in Service of Coastal Prosperity*

Someone in the back row snorted. Dull's head turned with the slow menace of a lighthouse, and the snort died without issue.

"Gentlemen," Dull began, in the voice of a man addressing gentlemen rather than dockhands out of pure administrative necessity. "In six weeks, delegates of the Armistice Commission will arrive in Greyhaven to sign the Accord. The Meridian Trading Company has been granted the honor of hosting the commemorative ceremony."

*Granted,* Orsen noted. Not purchased. The word choice alone was a small masterpiece.

"There will be repairs to this hall. There will be cleaning. Some of you will be assigned to these preparations." Dull let his gaze travel the rows, and Orsen felt it pass over him like a cold draft — pause, almost, at his face, then move on. "You will conduct yourselves with dignity. The eyes of the coast will be on Greyhaven."

He paused. He was fond of pauses; they let him enjoy his own importance twice.

"And you will speak well of what is remembered here," Dull said. "There have been... rumors. Stories. Old men's tales about the island station, about fever, about numbers." His voice cooled several degrees. "The official history will be read aloud at the ceremony. It will be the *only* history. Any man found spreading sedition during the delegation's visit will answer to the Company magistrates, and after them to the Commission's own security. Am I understood?"

Murmurs of yes, sir, variously pitched between sincerity and survival.

"You may return to work."

The crew dispersed. Orsen stayed exactly as long as invisibility required, then drifted toward his station — but his eyes caught, on the clerk's table near the banner, a stack of pamphlets. Freshly printed. The top one lay face-up, and the engraving showed the pearling station at Saltmere Cay rendered in loving detail: white buildings, blue water, divers wading cheerfully into the shallows, and beneath it a caption in careful type:

*Saltmere Station, where three hundred souls served faithfully until taken by the coastal fever. Their sacrifice built the prosperity we now share.*

Three hundred souls.

Orsen had stood in front of that number before, in other forms, in other years, and each time it had been wrong in the same direction. Too round. Too tidy. Numbers that came out of grief were never that neat; grief counted in sevens and thirteens, in *almosts* and *one more*. Three hundred was a number someone had chosen because it sounded complete.

And he had held, in his arms, in the rain, the child of a woman listed among those three hundred — a woman the Company said had drowned, whom Maren Quill swore she'd seen alive and walking two years past the fever that supposedly emptied the island.

He picked up a pamphlet. No one stopped him; pamphlets were meant to be taken, that was the entire point of pamphlets. He folded it into his shirt, next to his skin, where it felt less like paper than like a coal.

---

That night he sat in the loft above Maren's barge — he'd earned the habit of evenings there honestly enough, hauling water and mending netting, and no one questioned a man who worked for his supper — and turned the pamphlet over and over in the lamplight while Maren darned socks with the ferocity of a woman stabbing enemies through wool.

"They're going to say it happened just so," he said finally. "Fever. Three hundred. Sacrifice. They'll read it out loud with the delegates standing there, and everyone'll clap, and it'll go into the treaty record, and that'll be the end of it. Forever."

"Mm." Stab, stab.

"Maren."

"I'm listening." Stab. "I always listen. It's why I know everything."

"The ceremony's the seal. Once they read that history in front of the Commission — once it's part of the *Accord* — it stops being a lie and becomes the record. And records don't get argued with. Records get cited." He set the pamphlet down. His hands weren't quite steady, and he watched them as though they belonged to someone braver. "Six weeks. After that, Sera Voss drowned forever, and every diver on that island died of a fever nobody ever caught a name for, and Wren grows up the daughter of no one."

The needle stopped.

Maren looked at him across the lamp — a long look, the kind she usually saved for newborns and liars, since both required close reading.

"Well," she said. "Look at that."

"Look at what?"

"You've gone and grown a spine in my kitchen. I've seen it happen before. Usually takes years. You did it in a fortnight." She resumed darning, but her voice had lost its tease. "Go on, then. Say the rest. You've been circling it for three days like a gull over a fish cart."

Orsen took a breath. Saying it aloud made it real, and real things could fail, and failing things could hurt you — but he had learned, lately, that some hurts were the price of admission.

"We put it on record first. Before the signing. Before the ink. We find the proof — the true count, the names, whatever Sera knew — and we get it somewhere the delegates can't ignore it. Into the annex, into a delegate's hands, onto paper that can't be quietly burned afterward." He heard his own voice speed up and made himself slow down. "I don't know how yet. I don't know who. But if truth arrives *before* their version does, theirs has to fight it. And lies fight badly in daylight. Even Company lies."

Silence, except the harbor lapping at the hull and the lamp hissing.

"That's a plan the way a wish is a plan," Maren said at last. But she said it gently, and she'd already put down the sock. "You want to walk up to the Meridian Trading Company's own party and tell them they got the story wrong. With six weeks' notice and—" she gestured at him, at the whole of him, "—that."

"Yes."

"And if they catch you?"

Orsen thought about rule one — *never be interesting* — and rule seven — *the Company forgives nothing it notices* — and all the other rules he'd carved into himself over seven years, the way prisoners carve calendars into walls.

"Then I'll have been interesting," he said. "Once."

Maren studied him. Then she laughed — short and surprised, a bark of a laugh she immediately looked annoyed at herself for producing.

"God help me, I like you," she said. "That's going to be a problem." She stood, joints announcing themselves, and went to the little cabinet above her stove where she kept things she considered valuable: needles, brandy, and information. From behind a false panel she drew out a scrap of paper worn soft at the edges. "If we're doing this — and I haven't agreed, mind, I'm merely talking — there's a man you need. Name of Fenwick. Tobias Fenwick."

Orsen frowned. The name rang a distant bell — a bad one.

"He was Company archivist. Head of records, before your time. Clever as a knife and twice as likely to cut his holder." She dropped the paper on the table between them. "Eleven years ago he tried to tell anyone who'd listen that the Company's death counts didn't add up. Island counts, coastal counts, all of them. He got dismissed for 'irregularities in his keeping,' which is what they call it when they ruin a man for telling the truth. Now he drinks in a garret on Cordwainer Lane and hates everybody equally."

"Why would he help?"

"He wouldn't," Maren said comfortably. "Not at first. Men like Fenwick don't help; they argue. But an argument needs a opponent with facts, and you've got facts now — that pamphlet, the widow's hand, the swaddling cloth. Go lose an argument to him. See what shakes loose." She fixed him with a look. "And Orsen. If you go poking a disgraced archivist about dead pearl divers, you do it quiet. Rule of mine, free of charge: the Company doesn't watch drunk men. It watches who *visits* them."

Orsen pocketed the paper. It was soft as cloth, that scrap, handled by worry.

Outside, the fog had come in off the water the way it always did, folding the harbor into itself, hiding everything beyond arm's reach. Somewhere under that fog lay the wrecked courier boat, and somewhere under the city lay whatever truth Tobias Fenwick had tried and failed to make permanent. Six weeks to dig it out and hold it up to the light before the Company nailed its lid shut with a ribbon and a speech.

He walked home along the quay with his collar up and the pamphlet against his chest, and for the first time in seven years, Orsen Coldharbour noticed that his silence had changed shape. It wasn't armor anymore.

It was a held breath.

And held breaths, sooner or later, have to break.
