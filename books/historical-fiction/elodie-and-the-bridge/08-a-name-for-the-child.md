## 8. A Name for the Child

The storm had passed three days ago, but the barge still smelled of wet rope and river. Orsen had learned the smell quickly, in the way he learned everything — silently, thoroughly, as though understanding might be confiscated if anyone noticed him acquiring it.

Maren Quill's home was a coal barge in its previous life, beached now against the pilings at Tarrow's Reach, where the harbor silt grew thick enough to hold a hull forever. She'd hung lanterns from every hook and nail, and the light turned the low cabin gold, which seemed like a small act of defiance given how hard Greyhaven worked at being gray.

The baby slept in a fruit crate lined with flannel, and Orsen found he could not stop looking at her.

He'd been doing this for three days. Standing watch over an infant who was not his responsibility by any law of the Company or common sense, guarding her with the same careful blankness he wore on the warehouse floor, and failing utterly, because babies did not care about masks. Babies required you to make faces. He had discovered this the hard way, at two in the morning, when she'd wailed and Maren — without opening her eyes — had said, "She's not going to respond to silence, lad. Try smiling. Badly, if you must."

He had tried badly. It had worked. This troubled him more than anything Corvane Dull had ever done.

"She needs a name," Maren said now, not looking up from her mending. Her needle moved with the economy of a woman who had delivered four hundred children and buried too many of them. "I can't keep calling her *the cargo*. It's making my hands feel like smuggler's hands instead of midwife's hands, and there's already a fine line."

"I hadn't thought about it," Orsen said, which was true, because thinking about names meant thinking about futures, and futures were not something indentured men were encouraged to stockpile.

"You've thought about nothing else," Maren said. "You look at her like a man reading a ledger trying to find the entry where she belongs."

* * *

Fenwick arrived at dusk, smelling of gin and river fog, carrying a bottle wrapped in brown paper as if it were contraband rather than merely cheap. Tobias Fenwick had once been Keeper of Records for the Meridian Trading Company's entire western division; now he was a drunk in a garret above a chandlery, and the descent between those two states had been swift and public and seven years old.

He'd refused to help them. That had been two days ago, in his garret, when Orsen had climbed the stairs and named himself and been shown the door. But Maren had sent word anyway — *come see the child, if you remember what you were before you were this* — and something had dragged him down off the hill.

He stood in the doorway of the cabin now, hat in hand, looking at the fruit crate the way a man looks at a grave he dug himself.

"Well," he said. "She has your chin, Quill's blanket, and no name at all. In archivists' terms, she is an unfiled document. The worst kind. Anything unfiled can be lost without anyone noticing."

"That's why we're naming her," Maren said. "Sit down, Toby. You're dripping on my floor."

"There is no floor. There is deck." But he sat, folding his long frame onto a stool built for smaller men, and set the bottle on the crate beside the baby without drinking from it. Orsen noticed that. Filed it away, in the place where he filed things.

"Names have weight," Fenwick said, apparently to the lantern. "A name entered into a record is a fact. Facts are the only immortality I know how to administer. So it should be chosen properly. Not sentimentally."

"Properly," Maren repeated, in a tone that suggested she would enjoy watching him fail to define the word.

"Something short," Fenwick went on, warming despite himself, the archivist surfacing through the gin like a fish through ice. "Something that won't attract notice. Not a family name — family names are traceable, and hers is presumably Voss, which is precisely the name we cannot put within a mile of any document. Something common enough to pass, uncommon enough that she knows it was chosen. A name that says *ordinary* on paper and says something else entirely in the mouth."

Orsen looked at the baby. She had woken at some point during this speech and was watching the lantern flame with total absorption, one fist curled near her ear, pearl-callused swaddling cloth tucked around her like armor.

"Wren," he said.

He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It had been sitting in him for days — the little bird that lived in the eaves of the warehouse, that sang at dawn before the work bells, that nobody fed and nobody owned and nobody had ever managed to stop singing.

"A wren," Maren said slowly, testing it. "Small bird. Loud for its size."

"Common enough to pass," Orsen said. "Uncommon enough that she'll know it was chosen."

Fenwick was quiet for a moment. Then he said, softly, in the voice of a man reading an inscription: "*Wren.* One word, no lineage, deniable in court and unmistakable at a christening." He cleared his throat. "It will do. It will more than do. It's — "

He stopped. His eyes had gone bright, and he turned his face toward the bottle with great dignity.

"It's a good filing decision," he finished.

"Yes," Maren said gently. "That's exactly what it is."

* * *

They ate afterward — Maren produced a stew of fish and river onions that tasted like someone had decided Orsen deserved feeding, which was an experience so foreign he ate slowly, suspiciously, waiting for the invoice. Nobody presented one. Fenwick drank two cups and then, somewhere between the third and fourth, began to talk, which Orsen suspected was the actual purpose of the stew.

"The ledgers," Fenwick said abruptly. "You should know, since you've decided to stand next to me in public, that I am worse than a disgrace. I am an incomplete one."

Orsen set down his cup. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning that when they broke me, they didn't get everything." Fenwick's hands were flat on the table now, the way a man lays out cards. "Seven years ago I ran the death registers for the pearling station. Saltmere, it was called — you'll find it on no current chart, which should tell you something. The official record says fever took forty-one divers in the winter of '41. Forty-one, tidy, closed, signed by me." His jaw worked. "The real number was higher. And it wasn't fever."

Maren had gone very still across the table.

"I kept duplicates," Fenwick said. "Every register, every requisition, every order that passed my desk that contradicted the story the Company wanted told. Payroll records showing contracts bought out rather than honored. Supply manifests showing medicine diverted *away* from the station while the 'fever' burned. Copies of petitions from the divers themselves — good hands, decent people, begging in writing for what they were owed." He laughed, a dry sound with no floor under it. "I told myself I was building a case. Then the case was taken out of my hands, my testimony was burned in front of me, my career followed it into the fire, and the duplicates stayed where I hid them because I never had the courage to go back for them."

"Where?" Orsen asked quietly.

"The flooded chapel, down in the Old Quarter crypts. Saint Aldric's Half-Drowned, the locals call it — the tide takes the lower vault twice a day. I put them in a sealed tin in the memorial niche behind the font, paid the sexton to forget me, and told myself I'd collect them when it was safe." He looked up, and for the first time Orsen saw the man he must have been before, the Keeper of Records, the one who believed numbers could testify. "It has never once been safe. That is the entire history of my life in a sentence."

The lantern hissed. In the fruit crate, Wren made a small sleeping sound, and all three adults turned toward it with the same instinctive motion, and then looked slightly embarrassed at each other.

"You kept them," Maren said. "All these years, you kept them."

"I kept them the way a coward keeps a knife under his mattress. For comfort. Never for use." Fenwick rubbed his face. "But tonight there is a child in a fruit crate whose mother is officially drowned, whose island is officially empty, and whose whole existence is a clerical error the Company means to correct. And I find, sitting here eating Quill's excellent onions, that the duplicates are no longer comfortable. They are heavy. There is a difference, and I am too old not to know it."

Orsen thought of his father — of the gray water off Saltmere's pier, of seven years of swallowing questions until they dissolved like salt. He thought of the note in Sera Voss's hand. *Forget this face.*

"We'll go for them," he said.

"*We*," Fenwick repeated. He said it the way a starving man repeats the word bread.

"After the tide's out tomorrow," Maren said practically, already calculating. "Lower vault drains from midnight to four. I've run cargo through those crypts; I know the sexton's dog by name."

And that was how, Orsen thought, it happened. Not with oaths or banners. With stew, and a name, and a drunk archivist remembering what his spine was for. Somewhere across the city, the Company was writing its clean history for the peace delegates, and here on a stranded coal barge, three people and an unfiled document had just begun the counter-entry.

Fenwick raised his cup. "To Wren," he said. "May every ledger she appears in be honest."

"To Wren," said Maren.

"To Wren," said Orsen — and realized, saying it, that it was the first time in seven years he had spoken aloud in company without first weighing whether it was safe.

* * *

He stayed late, taking the night watch again, though Maren had stopped pretending to believe he came for the baby's sake alone. The harbor breathed outside, fog rolling off the water in slow gray lengths. Wren woke once, fretted, and settled when he picked her up, gripping one of his fingers with a strength absurd for her size.

"They gave you no name," he whispered to her. "Just an order to forget you. Well. You're filed now. First entry, witnessed."

She watched him with dark, serious eyes, entirely unconvinced of her own danger, and eventually slept.

It was nearly dawn when he heard the footsteps on the dock — unhurried, deliberate, the walk of someone who knew exactly which plank creaked and avoided it anyway. Orsen eased Wren back into the crate and slid to the cabin window.

On the fog-bound pier below, a figure stood at the head of Maren's gangplank. Not walking off. Not approaching. Just standing there, in the gray half-light, facing the barge.

Watching.
