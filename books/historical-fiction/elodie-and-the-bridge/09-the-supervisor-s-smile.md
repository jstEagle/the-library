## 9. The Supervisor's Smile

There were three kinds of mornings at the Meridian warehouse in Greyhaven, and Orsen Coldharbour had long ago learned to tell them apart before his boots finished hitting the floor.

There were ordinary mornings: fog thick enough to chew, the cranes complaining like old men made to rise before dawn, tally-clerks barking numbers that were only sometimes lies. There were bad mornings — a hold that wouldn't balance, a crate that had leaked something red down the dock planks, a Company man from upriver walking the floors with his hands clasped behind his back the way priests and auditors did.

And then there was this morning, which was neither, because Corvane Dull was standing at Orsen's work station when he arrived, smiling.

Dull never smiled before noon. He was a man who treated pleasure the way misers treated coin — hoarded it, counted it, spent it only where it bought something. He was tall in a stooped way, like a door left half-shut, and he had small neat hands that were always clean no matter what came through the warehouse. His smile now was thin as paper and just as easy to damage.

"Coldharbour," he said. "You look well rested."

Orsen set down his coat. Folded it. Hung it on the peg, because his hands wanted something orderly to do while his mind ran ahead of them like a rat along a beam. "I sleep well, sir."

"So I hear." Dull consulted a slate he hadn't needed to consult. "You slept especially well last Thursday night. And again on Sunday. The night watchman mentions you've been leaving your bunk."

The fog outside the high windows was grey-gold, almost pretty. Orsen thought, absurdly, of Wren asleep in Maren's arms on the barge, her whole fist wrapped around one of Maren's fingers, and then he put that picture away somewhere deep and locked the little door on it.

"I walk," Orsen said. "Bad dreams otherwise. My father drowned, sir. Walking settles me."

It was true, which was the best kind of lie. Seven years in the Company's employ had taught him that the truth made excellent raw material; you just carved it to shape afterward.

Dull's smile didn't move, but something behind it adjusted, like a man shifting weight in a chair. "Grief," he said, tasting the word. "Well. The Company respects grief. It's in our charter, more or less." He turned the slate around. "Which is why I've assigned you lighter duties today. Consider it a kindness."

The duties were not light. The duties, Orsen saw at a glance, had been designed by a man with time on his hands and a grudge he hadn't yet confessed to himself. Scrubbing the brine vats. Then re-tallying the salt-cured hides from Bay Six, which every tally-man in Greyhaven knew had been entered wrong twice already and would be wrong again. Then mucking out the clerk's privy, which was not technically within an indentured laborer's scope of duties but which nobody below the rank of God would bother to contest.

"Very good, sir," said Orsen.

That was the first rule of silence, the one his seven years had worn into him like a groove in stone: *agree quickly*. Agreement cost nothing and bought everything. A man who argued gave you information about himself. A man who nodded gave you nothing, and nothing, in Greyhaven, was the most valuable thing a person could own.

He took the bucket and went to find the brine vats.

* * *

Scrubbing brine vats was the kind of work that freed the mind embarrassingly much. The body learned the rhythm — brush, rinse, brush, haul — and the brain, unguarded, wandered off to do exactly what it shouldn't.

He thought about Sera Voss's handwriting, the way the *f* in *forget* crossed low, hurried, the ink dragged where her hand had shaken. He thought about the oilskin, folded sailor-fashion, corners tucked the way divers wrapped things they meant to carry down through water. He thought about Maren Quill counting Wren's ten small fingers aloud like a woman settling accounts with heaven, and about Fenwick, who had stared at the swaddling cloth's pearl-callus weave for four full minutes and then drunk himself under his own table without saying a word.

He thought, most dangerously, about six weeks. About delegates and treaties and a ceremony where the Company would stand at a podium and call the pearling station a tragedy of fever, an act of the sea, nobody's hand upon it.

Brush. Rinse. Brush.

"You missed a spot," said Dull's voice behind him, pleasantly.

Orsen hadn't heard him come in. Nobody ever heard Dull come in; the man walked like spilled sand. He turned, dripping to the elbow, and found the supervisor crouched at the vat rim, peering down into the murk with genuine interest, as though inspecting pearls rather than pickling residue.

"Where, sir?"

"Everywhere." Dull straightened, dusted his knees, though there was no dust. "You know what I admire about you, Coldharbour? Most men scrubbing a brine vat think about how much they hate the vat. You think about nothing at all. It's remarkable. Like watching a machine." He smiled his paper smile. "Machines don't have bad dreams, though. Do they?"

"My father drowned, sir," Orsen said, for the second time that day, and discovered that saying a true thing three times in one morning began to wear it smooth, like a pebble, until it slid out of you without catching on anything.

"Mm," said Dull. "So you've said. Twice." He patted the vat rim once, twice, like a man bidding a horse good evening. "Bay Six after this. The hides won't count themselves."

He left. Orsen stood with the brush dripping in his hand and realized his heart was going harder than the work could explain.

*Here it comes*, he thought. Not accusation — accusation was loud, and loud things got reported upward, and the Company hated noise. This was quieter. This was a man laying out pieces on a board and watching which ones Orsen flinched from.

Fine. Let him lay out pieces. Orsen had been playing this game since he was eleven years old, when a Meridian clerk had first explained to him, kindly, that his father's debt was now his debt, and that the arithmetic of the world was not negotiable. He knew how to be a machine. Machines survived.

He went to Bay Six.

* * *

The hides were, as promised, wrong twice over. Re-tallying them meant sitting alone in a lamp-lit bay with a ledger, a stylus, and several hundred cured skins that smelled like a funeral for a cow. Somewhere around the fourth hour, Orsen made a discovery that interested him enormously and professionally: the errors in the Bay Six manifest weren't sloppiness. They were patterned. Forty hides received, thirty-two recorded, eight vanished into the fog between ship and shelf — and the same eight, week over week, always routed past the same signature.

Corvane Dull's signature. Small and neat, like his hands.

Orsen sat back and looked at that signature for a long moment, feeling something complicated happen in his chest that was half fear and half — there was no other word for it — delight.

The supervisor who was hunting him was himself a thief. Of course he was. In Orsen's experience, the men who probed hardest at other people's small secrets were always men guarding large ones. A man with clean books slept well; a man like Dull stayed up nights inventing reasons to search everyone else's bunk.

Orsen copied nothing. That was important; he made himself very still about it, made his face a machine's face, finished the tally correctly, signed it, and filed it where filings went. But he kept the numbers in his head, because a head was the one place the Company had never taught itself to search, and heads, unlike ledgers, could not be seized as evidence.

He was beginning, he realized with some unease, to enjoy this. That was the crack in the old mask, he supposed — not fear, but appetite. For seven years his survival had consisted entirely of *noticing and doing nothing*, and now noticing had become a currency he intended to spend, and some starving part of him had woken up and wanted more.

* * *

The privy came last, as the privy must. He was on his knees at the threshold of that fragrant little chamber, sleeves rolled, when he heard voices from the loading floor — Dull's, and another he didn't recognize, lower and smoother, with the particular unhurried quality of a man being paid by the hour rather than by the task.

"—the child, if there is one," the smooth voice said. "Not confirmed. That's why we use soft hands first. Your soft hands, Dull."

"If my laborers are hiding infants, Vane, it's news to—"

"It's news to everyone. That's rather the point." A pause; boots on boards. "Keep him close. Keep him tired. Tired men talk, frightened men confess, and grateful men do anything at all. Pick which one he is and cultivate it."

They passed the privy. Orsen kept scrubbing, head down, breath steady, the very portrait of a man with no interior whatsoever. Through the gap under the door he watched two pairs of boots go by — Dull's polished ones, and a stranger's, cracked leather, salt-stained, the boots of a man who worked docks all over the harbor and owned none of them.

Then they were gone, and Orsen knelt alone in the stink, and let himself understand the size of what stood outside that door.

Vane. He'd heard the name once or twice over the years, the way sailors hear weather warnings — always thirdhand, always attached to someone who subsequently stopped appearing anywhere. The Company didn't send clerks after problems. It sent Vane.

*Tired men talk, frightened men confess, grateful men do anything.*

Orsen looked at his own hands in the lamplight, wrinkled from water, gray to the knuckle with lye. Which one was he, in their taxonomy? He'd been all three at various times. Gratitude was the dangerous one — he'd seen men ruined by engineered gratitude, promoted just past their wits until they owed their souls for the rope they were hanged with.

But there was a fourth category, and Dull and Vane didn't have a column for it, because it wasn't supposed to exist among indentured men: *a man with somewhere to be.* Orsen had a barge, a midwife, a drunk archivist, a newspaper he hadn't visited yet, a widow's sworn account waiting in a laudanum den, and a child who would grow up and someday ask what he had done when it mattered. He was not tired enough to forget that, not frightened enough to trade it, and not nearly grateful enough.

He rinsed the brush. He hung it neatly, because a machine hung its tools neatly.

* * *

At the end of shift, Dull was waiting by the coat pegs, holding out a small envelope with both hands, ceremonially, like a man presenting a medal.

"For your diligence," he said. "A token. The Company notices effort, Coldharbour. Especially lately."

Inside was a half-crown. Real silver. More than a day's wage for a man whose wage was mostly bed and gruel.

"Why, thank you, sir," Orsen said, and let his face do something it had done perhaps five hundred times in seven years: the wide, artless, slightly stupid grin of a laborer delighted by unexpected coin. It was, he reflected, the deepest insult he could safely offer — a man so transparent that even a gift from his hunter read to him as simple kindness.

Dull watched the grin and, for the first time all day, seemed faintly disappointed. "Walk home safe," he said. "Fog's thick tonight. Men have been known to lose their way entirely."

"Yes, sir," said Orsen, meaning *I hear you*, and Dull heard him mean it, and something like satisfaction settled between them, mutual and cold as the harbor.

Outside, the fog was indeed thick, and Orsen walked into it with the half-crown warm in his fist and his heart going like a drum. Three streets from the warehouse he stopped, opened his hand, and looked at the coin a long moment.

It occurred to him — not for the first time, but for the first time with force — that he could not keep it and could not spend it. It was marked money in every sense. It would sit in his pocket like a coal.

There was a flooded chapel on Chandler's Lane, defunct these twenty years, its crypt sealed since the parish moved inland. Fenwick had mentioned it once, drunk, in the oblique way he mentioned everything that mattered: *"Some things a man keeps in wet places, Coldharbour. Water remembers longer than men do. There's a chapel off Chandler's Lane, if you ever want to see what devotion looks like when it drowns."*

At the time Orsen had taken it for drunkenness. Now, turning the supervisor's silver over in his fingers, hearing Vane's soft voice recite its little catechism of tired and frightened and grateful, he understood that it had been an address. Perhaps the only honest sentence Fenwick had spoken in a month.

Orsen flipped the half-crown once, caught it, and pocketed it anyway. Evidence of his own performance, if nothing else — proof that the mask held, that the fool's grin still fit, that Corvane Dull had paid real money tonight for the privilege of believing himself unobserved.

Eight hides a week, vanishing between ship and shelf. A thief hunting a witness. Well, then. Two could play at knowing things.

He turned toward Chandler's Lane, and the fog closed behind him like a page.
