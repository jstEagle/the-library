## 10. The Hidden Ledger

Tobias Fenwick had spent thirty-one years among documents and had come to believe, on reasonably solid evidentiary grounds, that he was a man who did not frighten easily. Paper did not bite. Ink did not scream. A misfiled folio could ruin an afternoon but never a life — or so he had believed until the day he burned one ledger to save his own skin and learned precisely how much a book could weigh.

The crypt beneath the drowned chapel of Saint Aldhelm weighed considerably more.

He stood in the shallows at the chapel's threshold at low tide, boots already soaked past the ankle because his waterproof boots had been sold four winters ago for laudanum money, and regarded the black water filling the nave like a ledger entry he did not wish to audit.

"You're certain," he said, "that it is down there."

"I'm certain you told me it was down there," Maren Quill said from the boat. She sat in the stern with her arms crossed and an expression that suggested she had rowed him out here under protest and intended to invoice him for every minute. "Twice. Once when you were drunk and once when you were sober, and the two accounts matched, which surprised us both."

"They matched," Fenwick said, "because I am a professional."

"Because you only have one story left."

He chose not to dignify that with a reply, mostly because it was true. He adjusted the oilskin satchel across his chest — inside it, wrapped in waxed cloth, were dry blankets, a lantern, flint, and the last clean handkerchief he owned — and waded in.

The water came to his knees by the second pew, to his thighs by the altar rail. The chapel had gone under in the flood of '09, when the Company re-dredged the eastern channel and the sea, offended, took back a neighborhood nobody rich enough to matter lived in. Saint Aldhelm's congregation had scattered; the building had remained, roofless, its stone going soft as old cheese, its crypt below the waterline. Nobody came here. Nobody wanted anything from a drowned church.

Which was, of course, exactly why he had chosen it.

* * *

The duplicating of ledgers was, Fenwick would want it understood, standard archival practice. Any competent records-keeper made copies against fire, flood, rats, and incompetence. That he had made his copies secretly, at night, in his own hand over three years, and hidden them in a crypt rather than the Company strongroom — that part was less standard practice and more treason, depending entirely upon which side of the desk one sat.

The Meridian Pearling Station at Callowmere Reef had employed one hundred and forty divers. Its contracts ran seven years, same as any indenture, payable in coin upon completion. In the spring of the year the armistice delegations now prepared to descend upon Greyhaven like well-fed crows, the Company had discovered that paying out one hundred and forty completed contracts would cost eleven thousand crowns — and that the war's end meant no auditor would ever look closely at a station being quietly wound down.

Fenwick had seen the order first as a routing slip: *Station Callowmere to be cleared of personnel ahead of schedule. Contractual obligations resolved per attached memorandum.* The attached memorandum, which he copied before it vanished into Supervisor Harrow's safe, contained a single sentence he had never managed to forget:

*Resolution by discharge of population is authorized where payment is deemed impracticable.*

Discharge of population. As though the divers were ballast. As though Sera Voss's husband and Orsen Coldharbour's father and one hundred and thirty-eight others were so much water taken on and let off.

He had copied everything. The requisitions for quicklime that no fever required. The boat manifests showing two trips out and one trip back. The payroll page where one hundred forty names ended in the same week, each marked *deceased — fever,* in handwriting that grew tired toward the bottom, because even the clerk falsifying the record had eventually found it boring.

Then Harrow had come to his office with two men and a brazier, and asked him, very courteously, whether the archive was in perfect order, and put the Callowmere file into the fire page by page while Fenwick watched, and asked whether he had any objection to perfect order.

He said no. He watched the family petitions burn — the wives asking where their husbands' pay had gone — and he said no, sir, the archive is in perfect order, and he was thinking, even then, of the crypt.

Three weeks later a widow named Pell had come asking questions, and been arrested for trespass, and deported to the northern works, and her questions had died somewhere in transit. Fenwick had understood then that his copies were not evidence. Evidence requires someone willing to read it aloud. He had copies and no reader, and so he had done what careful men do with unbearable things: he had sealed them in lead sheeting, tarred them twice, buried them beneath the floor of a drowned chapel, and drunk himself steadily upward toward forgetfulness.

Until a young man with careful eyes had appeared at his garret door holding a baby and a note, and asked him about Callowmere, and Fenwick had told him to go to the devil.

The devil, apparently, had other plans, because here Fenwick was, up to his ribs in seawater in a dead church, hunting his own buried conscience.

* * *

The crypt steps were under the altar, which had seemed poetic when he'd chosen them and merely inconvenient now. He felt for the third step with his boot, found the hollow, and ducked beneath the water.

Cold. Cold like a fact. It closed over his head and pressed at his ears and for one absurd moment he was thirty years old again, diving off his uncle's boat at Callowmere to prove he wasn't afraid of the deep, coming up laughing with a shell in his fist. Then his hands found the step, counted along it to the loose flagstone, worried it up — it moved more easily than he expected, which frightened him more than resistance would have — and his fingers closed on the lead package.

It was heavier than he remembered. Everything was.

He surfaced gasping, hauled himself and the package up onto the altar slab, and lay there a moment, sixty-one years old and dripping, listening to Maren laugh at him from the doorway.

"There's a sight," she called. "The pride of the Meridian archives, communion with the fishes."

"The pride of the archives resigned some years ago," he wheezed. "You're addressing the remainder."

"Open it, then. Let's see if the remainder was worth a swim."

His hands shook — cold, he told himself, only cold — as he cut the tarred wrapping with his pocketknife. Inside the lead sheet, nested in wool gone gray and damp at the edges, the oilcloth inner bundle had held. He unwound it with the tenderness of a midwife, and there they were: forty-one pages in his own neat archivist's hand, plus the original memorandum he had stolen outright and never confessed to stealing, ink only slightly bloomed at one corner, every figure legible.

One hundred and forty names. One hundred and forty *resolved.*

"Well," Maren said, behind him. He hadn't heard her get out of the boat. She was reading over his shoulder, and her voice had changed entirely, gone flat and hard as a cleat. "Well. There it is."

"There it is," Fenwick agreed.

He had imagined this moment, in the good hours of his drinking, as vindication. He would hold the pages and feel righteous; the truth would warm him like brandy. What he actually felt was terror, arriving promptly and completely, the way the tide arrived under the chapel door. Because the pages proved everything — and proving everything meant that everyone who touched them became a target. Harrow was gone, promoted, but the men who had signed the orders still sat on the Company board, and the Company had six weeks in which its future depended on history staying quiet, and Fenwick knew better than anyone alive what such institutions did to inconvenient paper.

They burned it. Or they burned the people near it. Usually both, in that order.

"We should take them to Coldharbour tonight," Maren said.

"No copies exist of these. If we carry all forty-one pages through the streets and something happens—" He couldn't finish it, so he finished it mathematically. "One accident, and it's as though none of it ever happened. Again."

"So we copy them."

"Copying takes days. Days are exposed." He looked at the pages, at his own handwriting, younger and steadier than he was. "We split them. Half stays hidden here, re-wrapped, in case. Half comes out. And tomorrow we begin copying, page by page, in three different hands, in three different places, until there are too many truths to drown."

Maren was quiet a moment. Then: "You've thought about this."

"I've had seven years to think about it," Fenwick said. "I simply lacked the courage to do any of it." He began, carefully, dividing the stack. "Courage, I find, is largely a matter of arriving late."

She snorted, and helped him fold the oilcloth, and that small domestic act — the two of them kneeling on a drowned altar, folding papers together — did more to unman him than the water had. He had spent seven years telling himself he was alone in knowing. He was not alone anymore. That was the terror and the cure of it in a single bundle.

* * *

They reburied half beneath the flagstone — Maren insisted on doing the diving herself, since "you swim like a filing cabinet" — and Fenwick sat on the altar ledge drying his feet with the last clean handkerchief while she worked, and thought about names.

Specifically, he thought about the name on line ninety-seven of the payroll page. *J. Coldharbour, diver, resolved.* He had never known the man. But he knew the son now — knew the way Orsen stood slightly outside doorways, always leaving himself an exit; knew the boy had held a stranger's baby in a storm and let it rearrange his whole life rather than file it under *foundling, resolved.* Fenwick had watched Orsen say nothing at their first meeting, and recognized the silence immediately, because it was his own silence, worn by a younger man who still had time to take it off.

That was why he had said yes, eventually. Not conscience. Conscience had failed him thoroughly in Supervisor Harrow's office. It was recognition — the unbearable experience of watching your own cowardice performed by somebody with better posture.

"Tide's turning," Maren said, hauling herself up the steps, dripping, satisfied. "Move it, archivist."

They rowed back toward the harbor in a gray drizzle, the satchel between them like a third passenger. Fenwick kept his hand on it the whole way. At the mouth of the channel the lamps of Greyhaven were coming alight, one by one, the way they did every evening, indifferent and lovely, the whole shining apparatus of the port that ran on ledgers like his.

"Do you know what strikes me," he said, mostly to hear something besides the oars.

"Probably nothing useful."

"What strikes me is that we are now conspirators. Proper ones. I conspired alone for years, which is merely melancholy. This is the genuine article." He found, to his surprise, that he was smiling. "I haven't been frightened like this since Harrow's brazier. It's almost restorative."

"That," Maren said, "is the laudanum talking."

"The laudanum is at home in a drawer. This is gin, and clarity, and possibly joy." He pulled his collar up. "I had forgotten that fear and hope feel nearly identical in the chest. It makes diagnosis difficult."

She laughed at him, warmly this time, and shipped the oars as the current took them, and for half a minute they simply drifted, two middle-aged criminals in a borrowed boat with half a massacre under the seat, and Fenwick thought: *if they take me tomorrow, at least the pages are in two places, and the boy knows, and the woman knows, and the child will grow up in a world where somebody wrote it down.* It was more than he'd had yesterday. It was, he suspected, more than most men got.

Then the harbor bell began to ring out of pattern — the quick triple stroke that meant Company business, watchmen summoned — and both of them turned, and saw lanterns clustered thick around the quay nearest Maren's berth, moving fast, organized.

"Lanterns don't gather like that for smugglers," Maren said slowly. "Smugglers they send two men for. That's a search."

Fenwick's hand tightened on the satchel. Somewhere in the crowd of lights, he was certain, someone was asking questions about a barge, and a baby, and a disgraced archivist — and the certainty settled over him colder than the crypt water, because searches that begin at night, in Company hands, have a way of finding exactly the person the Company needs found.

Someone had already given them a name.

"Row," Fenwick said quietly. "Row as though the record depends on it. Because I am increasingly afraid it does."
