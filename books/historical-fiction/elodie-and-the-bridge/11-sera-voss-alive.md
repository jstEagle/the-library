## 11. Sera Voss, Alive

The tip came from a boy who smelled of fish glue and lies.

He was one of the runners who haunted the warehouse district like gulls after a gutting boat, doing small errands for smaller coins, and I had bought information from his kind before — mostly harmless things, which crane was free on Thursday, which foreman took bribes in tobacco rather than coin. He sidled up to me while I was pretending to check rope coils, and he said, in the flat voice of someone reciting a memorized line, "The woman you're asking after. The diver's widow. Salt-Lane, the blue door with the knocker missing."

I hadn't asked anyone about Sera Voss. That was the first thing wrong with it.

I had asked Maren to ask her cousins of cousins, in that sideways way smugglers have, and apparently word had traveled sideways all the way around and come back wearing a different coat. Either the boy genuinely didn't know who'd sent him — possible — or someone wanted me walking through a particular door at a particular hour so they could note my face going in and coming out.

I went anyway.

I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the whole point of everything that followed. I knew it might be a trap, and I went anyway, because somewhere between the warehouse steps and Maren's barge I had stopped being a man who could leave a question unanswered. It is a weakness. It has cost me nearly everything. I recommend against acquiring it if you can manage.

Salt-Lane was not a lane so much as a disagreement between two buildings, a slot of shadow and standing water behind the tanneries, where the fog came to die and couldn't quite manage it. The blue door was there. The knocker was missing, leaving a rusty circle on the wood like an old scar. Above it, painted over what might once have been a shop sign, were the words THE MERMAID'S REST, though the mermaid had faded to a suggestion of fins and the rest looked like anything but.

A woman with a face like a closed ledger let me in for two coppers and didn't look at me again, which in Greyhaven passes for hospitality.

* * *

Inside, the Mermaid's Rest was dimness arranged into rooms. Lamps burned low behind smoked glass. Men lay on pallets and chaises in various postures of surrender, pipes trailing sweet gray smoke, and the air was so thick with laudanum and tallow that I could feel it settling on my coat like a second garment. A clerk I half-recognized from the shipping office was weeping quietly into a cushion, and nobody was paying him any attention, which struck me as the most honest service this establishment provided: a place where a man could fall apart on schedule and no one would think it newsworthy.

"The back room," said the door-woman, holding out her palm again. "You're expected."

"I'm expected," I repeated, because repeating things gave me time to decide how afraid to be. "By whom?"

"By whoever's back there." She wiggled her fingers. I paid. Somewhere in Greyhaven there is a person whose entire fortune is built on second doors, and I respect them enormously.

The back room was smaller, cleaner, and held a single lamp turned down to a coal-red whisper. There was a bed, a chair, a basin, and a window cracked open to the tannery stink because apparently even the drowning prefer fresh air.

And there was Sera Voss.

She was sitting upright in the chair as if she'd been holding the posture for hours, which she probably had, because waiting does that to people. The reports had called her drowned, and drowned people are supposed to come back soft and bloated and blue, but she had come back thin instead, carved down to wire and decision. Her left cheek and the whole left side of her throat carried the burn scars — old ones, silvered now, pulling slightly when she spoke so that her words came out with a faint lisp of effort, like wind through a half-shut shutter.

She was mending a sock. That detail undid me more than the scars did. Something about a woman hunted by a trading empire sitting in a laudanum den darning wool made the whole horror of it suddenly domestic, suddenly real.

"You got my message," she said.

"That depends which message you mean," I said. "If it's the one about the blue door, then yes. If it's the one wrapped around a baby on the worst night of the winter, then I've been carrying it for weeks and I'd like a word about your handwriting."

Something moved across her ruined cheek that I slowly understood was a smile. "Orsen Coldharbour." She said my name carefully, tasting each part of it. "Sera Voss. Though I suppose you worked that out already, or you wouldn't be here."

"I work at the Meridian warehouse. Recognizing handwriting is one of perhaps three skills they've never thought to pay me for."

"And the other two?"

"Sneaking, and not laughing at supervisors." I nodded at the chair opposite her. When she gestured permission, I sat, and the chair received me with the groan of furniture that has heard too many confessions.

For a moment neither of us spoke. The lamp breathed. Somewhere beyond the wall, the weeping clerk had graduated to snoring.

"You've been seen," she said finally. "Asking. You and the midwife and — people." Her eyes, quick and dark and unscarred, fixed on mine. "Do you know what that means? Do you understand what happens when people start asking about Kesh Voss and the others?"

"I have an idea," I said. "It involves the phrase 'lost to fever,' and it makes me want to put my fist through the Company's front windows."

"It involves you being pulled out of the harbor with your pockets full of stones," she said, "and it involves a baby of eleven months learning to say 'warden' before she learns her mother's face. Which is why I did what I did. Before you ask. Before you look at me the way you're starting to look at me."

"I wasn't going to judge—"

"Yes, you were." No heat in it. Just fact, the way water is wet. "Everyone judges. I judged myself first and fastest, so everyone else can form a queue." She set down the sock. Her hands, I noticed, were steady — diver's hands, pearl-callused even now, hands that had learned to work in cold and pressure and dark. "You know what the station was. You know what they grew there, besides pearls."

"Grief," I said. "And then silence."

"My husband dove from twelve years old. Forty feet on a breath, cold water, sharks, the bends — he called them 'the pinch' and laughed about them." Her lisping voice found a rhythm, the rhythm of testimony, and I sat very still because I recognized it: this was a story she had told herself every night to keep it from dissolving. "Kesh signed a three-year contract with Meridian. Good money, they said. Enough to buy our own boat, they said. Then the contracts came due, all of them at once, forty divers and their families, and the Company looked at what it owed and decided arithmetic was more flexible than law."

"So they cleared the station."

"They cleared the station." The scar tissue pulled; her voice flattened. "First they burned the barracks — 'a cooking fire,' the report says, in the middle of a drought, on an island without a cook. Then the fever. Anyone who'd signed a petition, anyone who'd counted the dead, anyone who'd written letters home. My Kesh wrote letters. Beautiful ones. He wrote to me about the color of the water at dawn, and every letter ended with *if anything happens, don't believe them.*"

She was quiet a moment.

"He meant the Company. He meant exactly this. And when the boats came for the last of us — the wives, the children, the ones who could still testify — I understood that Wren's only chance was to not exist. To be forgotten before they finished forgetting everyone else. So I wrote the note. I chose you."

"Why me?" It came out rougher than I intended. "I was nobody. I'm still nobody. An indentured clerk with a talent for silence."

"Because you're the son of Aldric Coldharbour, and you were on the memorial wall before you were ten years old, and the Company thinks that makes you safe." She leaned forward. "But I'd seen you. At the harbor office, carrying manifests. You used to look at the lists of the dead like you were reading names, not numbers. Nobody else did that. Nobody else even saw that there were names." A pause, and something almost gentle entered the lisp. "Also, you were the only person connected to that warehouse who ever returned a dropped parcel without stealing from it. I asked around. People notice that sort of thing, in the end."

* * *

I should explain, perhaps, what happened to my composure during all this, which is that it left entirely and did not return until well after dawn.

Seven years I had kept myself like a sealed jar. Rules for keeping alive: speak when spoken to, agree when contradicted, watch everything, record nothing, feel less. And here was a burned woman in a poppy-smoke room dismantling the seal with a darned sock and a dead man's letters, telling me that my small stubborn decencies had been witnessed and weighed and found sufficient to trust with a child's life. I did not deserve it. I have never entirely convinced myself that I deserved it. But guilt, I have since learned, is just gratitude that arrived too late and doesn't know where to put itself down.

"I hid her," I said. "Your daughter. She's with a midwife named Maren Quill, on a barge — you may know of it, it's technically a smuggling operation and spiritually a bakery. She's healthy. She has four teeth and opinions about porridge. She laughs like—" My voice did something embarrassing, and I let it. "She laughs."

Sera Voss closed her eyes. For perhaps ten seconds she was not a hunted woman in a hiding place; she was just a mother hearing good news, and the lamp light made the scars shine like tide marks.

Then she opened her eyes and was hunted again.

"I can't go to her," she said, before I could offer. "Don't ask me. If they're watching you — and they are, or will be — then any place I stand becomes a place they search. She's safe as long as I'm a ghost. Ghosts don't lead soldiers to cradles."

"Maren would protect her."

"Maren Quill is one extraordinary woman with one barge and a network of cousins. Vane is the Company's fixer, and he has the harbor watch, the constables, and patience." She pronounced the name *Vane* the way sailors pronounce a storm warning. "You've met him yet? You will. He doesn't threaten. He *inquires*. It's much worse."

* * *

What happened next, I planned beforehand, which is why it worked, and I mention this because for most of my life I had survived by improvisation and it was the first time I ever built something deliberately.

From inside my coat I brought out paper, the little traveling ink pot Fenwick had grudgingly donated ("archival ink, Coldharbour, do not spill it on your shirt like a barbarian"), and a board to write on. I laid them on the table between us like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"I need it in your hand," I said. "Everything you told me. Names, dates, the petitions, the burning, the boats, all of it. Sworn. Signed with your true name."

"No." Flat, immediate, the way you refuse a second helping of poison.

"Not to publish. Not yet. Not to show anyone but the people already in this with us. But paper survives, Mrs. Voss. Paper is the only thing I've ever known to survive. Men get drowned and disgraced and drugged in back rooms, but a page with a name on it, hidden properly — that outlives everyone's courage."

She looked at the blank sheets for a long time. The poppy smoke drifted under the door from the outer room, sweet and thick, and I watched her fight it the way some people fight a current — not with panic but with slow, relentless strokes.

"If I write it," she said at last, "it exists. Things that exist can be seized. Things that can be seized can be traced. Back to me. Back to her."

"Things that don't exist are already erased," I said. "That's what they're counting on. That's the whole design, Mrs. Voss — that everyone who knows will be dead or silent by signing day, and the treaty will record a fever, and forty families will be a rounding error. They don't need to find us. They just need us to stay quiet long enough."

"*They* don't need to find her," she said sharply. "*You're* the one leading them to questions."

"Yes," I said. "I've noticed. I'm trying to make it expensive for them to follow."

That earned me another of the almost-smiles, crooked along the scar line. "Kesh would have liked you," she said, and then, quieter, as if the admission cost blood: "He'd have hated all of this. He'd have said, *Sera, love, you write it down, and I'll hold the door.*" Her jaw set. "So. I'll write it. I'll swear it. But hear me clearly, Orsen Coldharbour, because this is the price: I will never stand in a courtroom. Never a witness box, never a public hall, never in front of delegates or journalists or any gathering where a man with a notebook might learn my face. I'll give you the truth on paper. I won't give them me."

"Paper persuades," I admitted. "Names persuade better. Adela Brine at the Courier told me that herself — anonymous accounts are rumors, named witnesses are news."

"Then find another name," said Sera Voss. "You have one yourself, I'm told."

I hadn't told her. Word travels sideways in Greyhaven, all the way around and back wearing different coats. I stared at her, and she stared back, and there was nothing cruel in it — only the terrible fairness of a woman who had already paid the highest price on offer and declined to pretend the menu had other items.

"I haven't decided," I said.

"You decided the night you picked up my daughter instead of walking away," she said. "The rest is just scheduling."

* * *

She wrote for three hours.

I will spare the reader the full text — it appears elsewhere in this account, in its proper place, in her own words — and record instead only what it looked like to watch. She wrote the way she must once have mended nets: steadily, without flourish, pulling each knot tight. Twice she stopped, and I thought she was done, and both times she drank water and continued. When she came to the list of the dead — the petitioners, the letter-writers, the forty names the Company recorded as fever — her pen slowed, and I realized she was writing each one from memory, checking nothing, because there was nothing left to check.

At the bottom she signed *Sera Voss, widow of Kesh Voss, of the pearling station at Callowmere Isle,* and blew on the ink, and pushed the pages across the table as if setting down something heavy that was no longer hers to carry.

"There," she said. "Now it's yours. God help you."

"Amen," I said, and folded the pages into the oilskin lining of my coat, where they lay against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

She made me drink tea before I left. It was terrible — tannery-water tea, stewed to the color of varnish — and she watched me drink the whole cup with grim satisfaction, as if keeping a witness hydrated were the last maternal duty available to her, and she was going to perform it thoroughly.

At the door I paused. There are things you say at doors because they're easier at doors than across tables.

"She has your chin," I said. "And Maren claims she'll walk within the month, and when she walks she goes straight for trouble, so she has your judgment too."

Sera Voss stood in the lamplight with her ruined cheek and her steady diver's hands, and for a moment she was so beautiful with grief that I had to look at the floor.

"Don't bring her to me," she said. "Whatever happens. Promise me. Even if I send for her — especially then. Check twice. Trust Maren's nose over my signature." Because, her look said, signatures can be forged, and mothers can be broken, and the kindest thing a hunted woman can give her child is distance.

"I promise," I said. And because I am, at bottom, a clerk, I added: "It's in writing."

The laugh she produced was small and rusted and real, and I carried it out into the fog with me like contraband.

* * *

Salt-Lane at midnight was emptier than Salt-Lane at nine, which I told myself was good, and then told myself that empty lanes are also easy lanes to be followed down, and then made a circuit of four streets and doubled back twice before heading for the river, per the rules Maren had drilled into me. *Never leave a place by the door you care about.*

I was three streets from the water when I saw the lantern.

Not moving. Just standing, at the mouth of Cooper's Row, hung from a hook outside a chandler's shutters — except chandlers do not hang lit lanterns outside closed shops at midnight, and this one had been freshly trimmed, its glass clean, its flame steady. Someone had placed it there recently. Someone who wanted a well-lit stretch of street between themselves and anyone watching.

Or someone who wanted *me* to see how visible I was.

I walked on, unhurried, a tired clerk going home, past the lantern, past the dark windows, and did not run until I reached the water stairs. Only then did it land, fully and coldly, what Sera had written at the top of her third page, in the same careful hand as the note on the warehouse steps:

*They are not looking for a baby anymore,* she had written. *As of last week they are looking for a witness. I do not know who told them one exists. Be careful of anyone who asks you questions you have already answered.*

I had answers for that. Uncomfortable ones. Because there was exactly one person who had known about the baby, about me, and about the questions we were asking, and who had lately gone quiet in a garret full of empty bottles and older regrets —

and I turned toward Fenwick's dockside lodging with the oilskin crackling against my ribs and dread arriving ahead of me like weather.
