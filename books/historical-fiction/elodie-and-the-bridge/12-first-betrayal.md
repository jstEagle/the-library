## 12. First Betrayal

Maren Quill had delivered enough babies to know that the worst trouble always arrived quietly. Screaming was honest. Screaming meant someone wanted you to know exactly where they stood. It was silence — the held breath before a birth went wrong, the too-calm voice of a husband waiting outside the door — that you had to fear.

Which was why she noticed Pip.

Pip was a barge-hand, nineteen or twenty, all elbows and appetite, the sort of boy who ate like each meal might be his last and grinned like he knew something the rest of the world had forgotten. He'd worked Maren's barge on and off for two years, mostly hauling crates of smuggled brandy and occasionally holding a frightened woman's hand when his own were steadier than anyone expected. He was, by every measure Maren used, a good boy.

And for three days, he had been quiet.

Not sulking-quiet, which she could have fixed with a bowl of stew and a straight question. This was a different quality of silence. A listening quiet. Twice she'd caught him at the barge's stern window, watching the alley where Orsen came and went with his collar up, and twice he'd turned away too fast, whistling a tuneless little thing that fooled no one.

"You're costing me money," she told him on the third evening, setting down two bowls of fish stew heavier on the potatoes than she'd ever have given herself. "Standing at that window all day instead of working. Either tell me what you're looking at or start scrubbing."

He took the bowl. His hands were steady. That was the first wrong thing — Pip's hands always trembled a little after dark, ever since the winter he'd come off the deep-water boats.

"Just watching the weather," he said.

"It's been fair for a week."

"Fair weather turns." He smiled up at her, and it was his usual grin, warm and crooked, and somehow that made it worse. "You taught me that, Maren. Everything turns."

She let him eat. She even refilled his bowl, because whatever was coming, she wasn't going to have it said that Maren Quill starved a boy on his way out the door. But after he'd gone below to his bunk, she sat at the galley table with Wren asleep against her shoulder — the baby smelled of salt and milk and something faintly floral from the soap Sera Voss must have washed the oilskin in, once, in another life — and she did sums.

Maren Quill was not an educated woman, but she could count faster than any clerk on the docks, and she counted the things that didn't add up. The way Pip had stopped asking about wages. The new boots he wore, good ones, stitched leather, not the kind a barge-hand bought. The way he'd asked — casual as anything, three nights ago over cards — how often the archivist came aboard, and whether Fenwick still kept rooms in Salt Row, and hadn't touched his cards while he asked.

She looked down at Wren's small face, slack with sleep, one fist curled under her chin like a boxer guarding.

"Well," Maren said softly. "I think our boy's been bought."

* * *

She didn't confront him. Confrontation was for people who wanted drama; Maren wanted distance. Instead she spent the night doing what she'd done during two epidemics and one naval blockade: moving supplies before anyone thought to stop her.

Wren went into the market basket first — the false bottom, lined with wool, that had carried more contraband infants than the Company would ever care to know about. Not that Wren was contraband. That was the whole cursed point of her. She was evidence, which was worse, because contraband only had to be hidden while it was worth something.

"Right," Maren murmured, tucking a blanket around the little body. "We're going for a walk, my girl, and you're going to be the best-behaved cargo of your entire career. No fussing. No singing. Save your opinions for people who can appreciate them."

Wren opened one eye, considered this, and closed it again. Maren chose to interpret it as agreement.

The safehouse was Tansy Hobbe's rooming house on Candlemaker's Lane — three stories of leaning brick where the rent was paid partly in coin and partly in discretion. Tansy had buried two husbands and one Company inspector, though only the first death had ever been explained to anyone's satisfaction, and she owed Maren for a delivery twelve years back that had cost Maren a week in a fever-ward and gained Tansy a granddaughter.

Tansy answered the door with a candle stub and no surprise whatsoever, which was one of the many things Maren loved about her.

"You look like a woman carrying something," Tansy said.

"I'm carrying a baby."

"Besides the obvious." Tansy peered into the basket, and her whole craggy face changed — softened around the eyes the way old stone softens near water. "Oh. Oh, look at that. Look at her hands."

"Everyone looks at her hands," Maren said. Wren had pearl-calluses even now, tiny ridges on her fingers that spoke of an island everyone said was empty. "It's going to give her a complex."

"What's it called?"

"Wren. As of last week. There was a committee."

"A committee." Tansy stood aside to let them in. "How many people does it take to name one infant?"

"Three. And it nearly took six hours, and the drunk one won, which tells you everything about committees." Maren set the basket on Tansy's kitchen table and gave the old woman the shape of it without the details — a child in danger, questions being asked somewhere she couldn't see, a favor called in that Maren would repay however Tansy named. Tansy listened the way she listened to confessions: without blinking and without judgment.

"And the boy?" Tansy asked, when Maren mentioned Pip's new boots. "The elbows-and-appetite one?"

"He hasn't done anything yet."

"No," Tansy agreed. "But he's been *paid* to do something, and there's no un-paying that. You know what my second husband used to say. A man who sells you once has sold you already; the rest is just scheduling."

"Your second husband sounds like he'd have got on well with me."

"He did. That's why there's a granddaughter nobody asks about." Tansy lifted the candle and gestured toward the stairs. "Third floor, back room. Window looks out on the chimney-stack, so no light shows, and the stairs creak so you'll hear anyone coming long before they arrive. I've put Mrs. Aldery in the front room and she disapproves of everything, including me, so nobody will bother visiting her."

Maren hesitated at the foot of the stairs, basket heavy against her hip.

"Tansy. If men come asking—"

"If men come asking," Tansy said, "I'm an old woman with lodgers and a bad memory, which I have been for thirty years, and it has never once failed me. Go up. Sleep if you can. And Maren—" she caught her arm, grip surprisingly strong, "—whatever this is, whoever that child belongs to, you did right bringing her here. Now go, before you stand there long enough for me to change my mind about sentiment."

* * *

Orsen came at dawn, pale and hollow-eyed, taking the creaking stairs two at a time and then stopping dead in the doorway of the back room when he saw Maren sitting up in the chair with Wren in her arms.

"She's fine," Maren said. "Before you ask. She slept four hours straight, ate like a dockworker, and produced a smell that would fell a horse. She's thriving. It's the rest of us I'd worry about."

"What happened?" He stayed in the doorway, coiled, the way he always stood — like a man who'd learned doors were things other people came through.

"Pip."

His face did something complicated. "No."

"Yes."

"He wouldn't. He—" Orsen stopped himself, and Maren watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done, watch it come out the same. Seven years in a Company warehouse taught a man that *wouldn't* was a word for children's stories. "What did you see?"

"New boots. New quiet. Questions about Fenwick dressed up as card talk. And yesterday morning, before first bell, he left the barge and didn't come back until noon, smelling faintly of tobacco he doesn't smoke. Vane's people chew a particular black kind. Cheap. You can smell it across a room." She shifted Wren to her other arm. "I moved her last night. Before he could sell anything worth selling."

Orsen sat down slowly on the edge of the cot, as if his legs had submitted a resignation letter his pride had only just processed.

"How long?" he asked.

"Since the questions started, I'd guess. Which means since we started asking ours. We woke something up, love. That's the arithmetic none of us wanted to do, but there it is. Every person we talk to is a door we open, and somebody's standing behind at least one of them with his hand on the latch."

"The ledgers are still—"

"In the chapel crypt? Then they're safe for now, but 'for now' just shrank." She leaned forward. "Listen to me, Orsen Coldharbour. I've been in this city through a blockade, two fires, and a marriage, and I'm telling you what I know. Being hunted feels like the world's ending, but it isn't. It's just the world getting specific. They don't know yet what we have. If they knew — truly knew — we'd all be in the harbor by now wearing new chains. What they know is that strangers are asking questions about a lost station, and that frightens them, and frightened companies make mistakes."

"Frightened companies also make widows," Orsen said quietly.

"Yes," Maren admitted. "They do both. I never promised you this was safe. I promised you it was possible. Those are different animals."

Wren woke then — not crying, never quite crying, just making a small interrogative sound, like a question mark with lungs — and both adults froze in the universal posture of people hoping a baby would settle herself. She did not. She fixed Orsen with a stare of enormous, ancient reproach, and waved one callused fist at him as though filing a formal complaint.

"She wants feeding," Maren said, "and I've about run out of patience as a substitute, so either you take her for a turn about the room while I warm the milk, or you develop opinions fast."

To her lasting surprise, he held out his arms. He was clumsy at it — held Wren the way a man holds a live grenade, elbows locked, breathing carefully — but he held her. And Wren, having lodged her complaint, studied his face with that grave attention infants pay to things they intend to remember, and gripped his finger with her whole small hand.

"There," Maren said, gentler than she meant to. "That's the trick of it. That's the whole secret. Somebody holds on to you, and you find out you can't put them down either."

Orsen didn't answer. He was looking at Wren the way Fenwick looked at his surviving ledger pages — like something that proved the past had happened, and demanded a future.

* * *

Fenwick arrived at midmorning, smelling of gin attempting to disguise itself as sobriety, and heard the news standing by the chimney-breast with his arms folded tight.

"Pip," he repeated. "The elbows boy."

"You remember him?"

"I remember everyone. It's my curse. I remember the name of every clerk who ever falsified a line under my supervision, and every diver whose contract I countersigned, and every—" He stopped, pressed his lips together, restarted. "Yes. I remember Pip. He held Mrs. Corrie's hand through her lying-in, winter before last. Sat up all night. Good boy."

"They're all good boys," Maren said. "That's rather the point. Nobody recruits the wicked ones; they're unreliable."

"So the question becomes," Fenwick said, pacing the narrow room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, "what precisely has been sold. If Vane knows only that a warehouseman, a midwife, and a disgraced archivist have been seen in company — that's suspicion, and suspicion they could have gathered a dozen ways. But if the boy described the child, or mentioned the oilskin, or the name Voss—"

"Then it stops being suspicion," Orsen finished.

"Then it stops being suspicion." Fenwick halted at the window, angling his head to look down past the chimney-stack at the lane below. His shoulders went rigid. "Orsen. How many men does a coal merchant need to count his own cart?"

"That depends entirely on how much he's had to drink."

"There are two across the street. Been there since before I arrived — I took them for deliveries. One hasn't moved in forty minutes. He's not delivering. He's *stationed*."

Maren rose, careful not to crowd the window. She didn't need to look. The pattern was older than the war: watchers at the approaches, patient as tide. Not raiding — watching. Counting who came and went, building their picture one visitor at a time, the way you assembled a case the way you assembled a ship, plank by plank, and launched it only when it would float.

"They followed someone here," she said. "Not Pip — Pip's raw material, they wouldn't waste him on tail-work. Someone else led them to Tansy's door."

"Then whoever it was," Orsen said slowly, "doesn't know they were followed. Which means it's someone inside the circle of people who know where Wren is."

The room went very quiet. Wren, oblivious, gnawed on Orsen's knuckle with toothless determination.

"Not necessarily inside," Fenwick said at last, but his voice lacked conviction. "Sera's den. Adela's office. The chapel sexton. Any thread pulled far enough reaches a hand." He rubbed his eyes. "God help me, I spent eleven years believing that records were the dangerous things in this city. Paper trails. Sealed annexes. And here we are, betrayed by something no archivist can file: a boy with new boots."

"We can't stay," Maren said. It wasn't a question and nobody treated it as one. "Tansy's covered for me, but she shouldn't have to cover for watchers too. We move her tonight, and we move her somewhere nobody in this circle knows about except the three of us — and we don't tell even each other all of it. Each keeps one piece. Address split three ways, like a cipher."

"That's madness," Fenwick said. "If anything happens to any one of us—"

"Then two pieces survive. That's rather the point." Maren took Wren back from Orsen, who released her with visible reluctance. "Welcome to paranoia, gentlemen. It's not a mood, it's a discipline. From today: no names written down, no meetings repeated in the same place, no kindnesses we can't afford. We test our trust the way you test ice — one foot, weight slow, and never all together."

Fenwick laughed — short, bitter, real. "Seven years sober-adjacent and now you want operational security. I once ran the Meridian's entire eastern annex, you know. Sealed rooms. Sworn clerks. Iron cabinets. And do you know how the Company learned about my first inquiry into the station? A laundress. A woman who washed our collars noticed which ones came back stained with island ink, and sold the observation for two silver bits. All my iron cabinets, defeated by laundry." He shook his head. "Vane will have a dozen threads on us by now. Threads we haven't even thought to pull ourselves."

"Then we cut the ones we can see," Orsen said, "and assume the rest."

Below, in Candlemaker's Lane, the stationary coal merchant finally moved — walked ten paces, bought nothing from the pie stall, and resumed his post. Maren watched him from the window's shadow and felt the old cold settle into her bones, familiar as an ache in an old break.

They were watched. They were divided. Somewhere behind Pip's crooked grin, a price had been agreed, and none of them knew the figure.

But Wren, riding Maren's hip, had discovered her own fist and was gnawing it with entire contentment, utterly unaware that three adults had just rearranged their lives around her safety for the second time in a fortnight.

"Hold that thought, my girl," Maren whispered into the downy top of her head. "You're worth every watcher in Greyhaven."

Then she turned to the men, and her voice came out flint.

"Now. Who among us, besides us three, knows Tansy's address — and think hard before you answer, because the next words out of your mouth decide whether we spend tonight running from shadows or from soldiers."

Fenwick opened his mouth. Closed it. Went the color of old paper.

"Maren," he said. "There's something about the chapel crypt you should know."
