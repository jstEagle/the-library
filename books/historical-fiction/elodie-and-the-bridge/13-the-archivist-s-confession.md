## 13. The Archivist's Confession

Tobias Fenwick had a theory about hangovers, developed over eleven years of field research: they were the body's honest archivists. Every drink was an entry in a ledger, and the morning's headache was simply the account coming due, itemized and stamped, with interest. He lay on his garret cot — it barely deserved the word *bed* — and listened to his skull conduct its audit while rain ticked against the single window like a patient clerk.

There were voices downstairs. That was unusual. Voices in the stairwell of the Salt & Anchor were usually just sailors arguing about rope, but these voices were arguing about *him*, or at least one of them kept saying "Fenwick" the way you'd say a word you didn't trust to stay still.

"He's not a morning person," Maren Quill was saying, in the tone of a woman who had delivered enough babies to know that some things could not be hurried and some things absolutely must be. "But he is a morning person eventually, if you put food near him."

"I brought bread," said Orsen Coldharbour, from the landing. The boy's voice always sounded half-swallowed, like he was used to being told to hold it in.

"See? Strategy." A pause, then Maren's knuckles rapped the door — three times, businesslike. "Tobias. We know you're awake. Dead men don't snore."

Fenwick considered pretending otherwise, but he had, in fact, snored. He'd heard himself do it. He hauled himself upright, wrapped a blanket around his shoulders with what dignity remained to him — perhaps a third of a dignity, measured in the old Company standard units — and opened the door.

They stood there in the dim corridor: the smuggler midwife with her arms crossed and her eyes doing arithmetic; the indentured warehouse clerk holding a loaf of bread like it was a diplomatic credential; and, bundled against Maren's chest, the baby. Wren, they'd taken to calling her. Small, solemn, with the gravity-defying wisps of dark hair that all infants seemed issued by the same quartermaster.

"You look terrible," Fenwick told them.

"You look worse," Maren said cheerfully, "and I've seen you after two days on the brandy. This is only one morning's worth. You're improving."

"We need to talk," Orsen said quietly.

"There's a sentence I've been dreading for six years." Fenwick stepped back and let them in, which required moving a stack of ledgers — his own salvaged papers, mostly shipping manifests annotated in a hand that had once been praised as *the finest penmanship in the Meridian archive* before it became *the most dangerous handwriting in Greyhaven* — and clearing a chair of its coat. The room smelled of tallow, damp plaster, and old ink. He thought it smelled like honesty, personally. Nobody else had ever agreed.

Maren settled onto the chair without ceremony and began unswaddling Wren with the brisk tenderness of routine. Orsen stood by the door, then sat on the edge of the cot, then stood again, a man who had not yet decided where his body belonged. Fenwick put the kettle on because his hands wanted employment and his mouth wanted time.

"You came about the ledgers," he said, when the water began to mutter. "The duplicates. I told you Tuesday I don't have them, which was true. And I told you Thursday that even if I did have them, I wouldn't give them to you, which was also true. And now you've brought bread, which suggests you think I can be bought for the price of a loaf. I want you to know I'm mildly offended, and mostly flattered, and we should discuss the quality of this bread first—"

"Tobias," Maren said.

He shut up. She rarely used his name. It landed like a stamp on wet paper.

"Maren found names," Orsen said. He spoke carefully, each word set down like a coin on a counter. "On the oilskin that wrapped Wren. A list of divers the Company reported lost to fever at Saltmere Station. Maren knew some of them. Personally."

"I buried two of them myself," Maren said. Her voice stayed level but something underneath it had gone very flat and very hard, like reef rock under shallow water. "Alive-well, Tobias. Not fevered. Shot, or drowned, or whatever the overseers did to men who asked why their contracts weren't being paid. I stitched one of them up on my barge two years ago and he was dead within the month anyway, but he was *alive* when the Company wrote him into the ledger as a corpse. They killed him twice. Once in the flesh and once in the record."

Fenwick turned back to the kettle. It was boiling. He let it boil. There was a particular skill to looking busy, and after six years of practice he was very good at it.

"And you want me to help you prove it," he said. "With documents I claim not to have."

"I want you to tell us why you won't," said Orsen.

That, at least, was new. People had asked Fenwick for many things over six years — for drinks, for money, for the archive expertise that had made him briefly famous and permanently ruined — but nobody had asked him for his reasons. Reasons were the one inventory he never put on display.

"The kettle," Maren observed, "is done."

So it was. He poured four cups of tea — three for them, one large one for himself — and distributed them, and sat down on his trunk with his blanket around him like a man presiding over the world's smallest and saddest court, and looked at the baby, who was watching him with the round, unblinking appraisal of someone who has recently arrived in the world and is still deciding whether to stay.

"All right," Fenwick said. "You want the confession. Let me get the phrasing right; I've rehearsed it in the bath for years, though the bath here is a bucket, so the rehearsals lack grandeur."

Nobody laughed. He hadn't expected them to. But he took a breath, and the breath tasted like rain and old ink, and he began.

* * *

"In the spring of the year the armistice talks were first proposed — you'll remember, everyone remembers, it was the year of the fish shortage — I was Chief Second Archivist of the Meridian Trading Company, Greyhaven branch. Third-highest ranking record-keeper on the coast. I had keys to the deep stacks, a salary that embarrassed me pleasantly, and a wife named Isolde who believed, with reason, that I was a good man."

He sipped his tea. It was too hot. He drank it anyway; punishment was familiar territory.

"My portfolio included the outstation returns. Pearling, salt, timber. Saltmere Station sent quarterly reports through a junior clerk who couldn't spell 'diving,' which should have been my first warning — stations that are well don't send their paperwork illiterate. Their numbers were wrong. Not fraudulently wrong, at first. Just *tired*. Diver counts that didn't reconcile with wage disbursements. Deaths recorded as desertions. Desertions recorded as contract transfers, as if a man could transfer himself to a station that didn't exist. I flagged it. Quietly, correctly, through channels, the way the manual instructs. Do you know what happens when you flag an irregularity through channels, Mr. Coldharbour?"

"I expect," Orsen said, "nothing happens."

"*Nothing* happens. Precisely so. You have described the entire mechanism. My flags went down a channel and disappeared like coins in a well. So I stopped using channels. I started keeping copies. Wage books, muster rolls, the medical returns from the station surgeon — a frightened little man named Pell who wrote 'pulmonary complaint' forty-one times in eighteen months, always in the same shaking hand. Forty-one divers, Mr. Coldharbour. In a healthy year, Saltmere lost three. Four, in a bad one."

Wren made a small sound, a kind of hiccoughing sigh, and Maren adjusted her without looking down, the way a helmsman corrects course by instinct. Fenwick found he could not look away from the baby while he talked. It was easier, somehow, to confess to someone who couldn't understand him yet. She'd understand later. Someone would tell her. Perhaps he would.

"I built a case," he said. "A beautiful case. Cross-referenced, footnoted, indexed. I was going to send it to the Harbor Commission, and if that failed, to the delegate courts, and if that failed—" he smiled, and felt how crooked the smile was, "—I confess I hadn't thought past failure. Arrogant men never do. I assumed the truth was a weapon and I had merely to swing it.

"What I did not account for was Corvane Dull."

Orsen went very still. Fenwick noted it, filed it. The boy knew Dull. Of course he did; Dull was his supervisor. Everyone in that warehouse knew Dull the way livestock knows the drover.

"Dull was then what he is now: a man whose ambition had outpaced his talent and decided to solve the problem by removing anyone talented from the road. He'd been passed over twice for my position. When the irregularities in my section started drawing attention from above — attention, mind you, not inquiry; the Company distinguishes carefully between those — Dull saw weather coming and chose to become the storm.

"They summoned me to the upper office on a Tuesday. I remember it was Tuesday because Tuesdays were when Isolde and I had supper at the chowder house on Rope Walk, and I missed it, and she waited, and that is the smallest of the things I will never forgive. The office contained Dull, two men from the Company's legal division whom I never learned to tell apart, and a brazier."

Fenwick set down his cup. His hand was steady. Six years ago it had not been.

"They showed me a letter. An anonymous denunciation — Dull's composition, I'd swear to the phrasing on my deathbed; the man uses 'furthermore' the way other men use breathing — accusing me of theft of Company property, falsification of records, and sedition. War-time sedition, no less; the accusation was timed to catch the loyalty committees at full sail. And then they showed me the brazier, and one of the interchangeable lawyers held out my case file — my beautiful cross-referenced, footnoted, indexed case — and told me that I had a choice.

"I could burn it myself, resign citing health, and receive a settlement and a character reference. Or they would burn it for me, and I would receive a tribunal, and my wife would receive the social consequences of being married to a seditious archivist awaiting trial. They were very courteous. That's what I try to make people understand. No one shouted. There was no villain twirling anything. The Company does not roar, Mr. Coldharbour. It *files*."

"So you burned it," Maren said. It wasn't a question, and there was no contempt in it, which somehow made it worse.

"I burned it," Fenwick said. "Page by page. It took eleven minutes. I know because the lawyer checked his watch aloud, twice, as though I might be dawdling. And I told myself, as the smoke went up, that I was being wise. Pragmatic. That Isolde and I would move inland, that I would take a position with a grain merchant, that the truth would out someday through some braver instrument than me. That is the sentence every coward composes in the firelight, and I composed mine fluently, because I was, after all, a fine writer of official prose.

"But here is the part I have never said aloud, and the reason your bread will not buy it, and the reason I am telling you at all."

He made himself look at Orsen. The boy's face gave back nothing — that warehouse stillness, the silence worn like armor — except in his eyes, where something watched and waited and, Fenwick realized with a lurch, understood. Understood in the bones. Whatever this boy had survived to keep quiet for seven years, it had taught him the exact weight of a burning ledger.

"The muster roll I burned listed a diver named Eron Sault," Fenwick said. "Saltmere, senior line, twelve years' service, petitioned three times for his contract payout — he'd earned his freedom clause and the Company owed him wages and passage home. His last petition was in the file. I'd read it a dozen times. He wrote that his wife had given birth that autumn, and he said — I can recite it, God help me — *'I ask only what was written and sealed. My daughter is born. I would see her walk.'*"

The rain filled the pause. Downstairs, faintly, someone dropped a barrel and swore about it with maritime fluency.

"Two weeks after I burned that file," Fenwick said, "Eron Sault was entered in the replacement ledger as deceased of pulmonary complaint. Along with nine others from his line, the same week. Witnesses — later witnesses, other survivors, the ones who scattered — said the men of his line had been called to a meeting about their payouts. A meeting on the water. There was no meeting. There was a boat that went out heavy and came back light.

"His widow got nothing. No wages, no pension, no passage. She was on the Company rolls as the dependent of a deserter, which meant she was owed less than nothing; she was owed *disappearance*. And the daughter — the daughter who was born, the daughter he wanted to watch walk — she grew up on charity alms on an island that the Company then closed, emptied, and renamed in the reports as 'lost to fever,' so that even the place where she learned to walk would stop officially existing."

Fenwick spread his hands. They were ink-stained and old and they shook slightly, and he let them.

"I did not kill Eron Sault," he said. "Dull's masters did. The Company did. But I burned the only document in the world that proved what he was owed, and I burned it eleven minutes before a tribunal would have forced the question into daylight, and the delay — my delay, my eleven minutes, my *wisdom* — was exactly long enough. So when you come to me with bread, asking me to stand up again, to raise the flag, to swing the truth like a weapon — you are asking a man who swung it once and hit a child."

* * *

Silence, then. Fenwick drank his tea because his hands needed orders. Maren didn't fill the silence, which he respected; midwives knew which silences were laboring and mustn't be disturbed.

It was the baby who broke it. Wren, having conducted her appraisal, reached one small hand out from the swaddling and gripped Maren's thumb, and made a noise that was not quite a word and not quite a complaint but carried, unmistakably, an opinion.

And Fenwick — tired, hungover, confessed — started laughing. It came out cracked and rusted, a laugh that hadn't been serviced in six years, but it came.

"Look at her," he said. "Look at her. She can't say a word and she's already filing objections. She's better at advocacy than the whole legal division."

"She gets it from her father, maybe," Orsen said quietly. "If Sera Voss's husband was a diver—"

"Eron Sault," Fenwick said.

The room changed temperature. Orsen turned his head slowly, and Maren's hands stilled on the swaddling.

"The widow on your list," Fenwick said, "the one who wrapped this child in pearl-work cloth and left her on the steps with instructions to forget her face. Think, Mr. Coldharbour. Sera Voss, née Sault. The widow of a 'fever' death from Saltmere's senior line. The woman the Company erased twice — once as a deserter's dependent, once as a drowned diver's widow — is the same woman who trusted a stranger on a warehouse step with the one thing left of her family. She didn't choose your steps by accident, I'd wager. She chose the one place in Greyhaven where a man famously keeps silent, because she needed silence kept."

Orsen had gone pale in the particular way of a man rearranging everything he knows. "Her husband petitioned for payout," he said. "The note — the handwriting — she wrote *forget this face* because remembering it is a death warrant. Because they killed the men who asked for what they were owed, and they'll kill the proof."

"They killed the proof already," Fenwick said. "I fed it to a brazier myself." He stood, blanket sliding off his shoulders, and crossed to the corner where the salvage-paper ledgers were stacked, and stood before them without touching them. His heart was behaving disgracefully. Six years of careful, cask-bottom living, and a baby gripping her mother's-thumb-in-law had knocked it loose in a quarter hour.

"I told you I don't have the duplicates. That was true, as far as it went. What I have—" his voice caught, and he pushed through it, an old man hauling on a rope, "—is not the case I built. That case died in eleven minutes of smoke. What I have is what I did afterward, in the worst months, drunk and disgraced and unable to sleep for hearing pages crinkle in the fire. I re-copied what I could remember. From memory, Mr. Coldharbour. Names, dates, entries, Pell's forty-one 'pulmonary complaints' — the ones seared deepest I restored. It is not evidence. It is a scar in the shape of a ledger. A court would laugh at it."

"A newspaper wouldn't have to," Orsen said.

"No," Fenwick agreed. "A newspaper wouldn't have to. Nor would a delegate with a conscience and a staff attorney willing to work from corroboration. Memory-testimony plus original documents would be another matter entirely. Which brings us to the difficult part, and the reason I have never spoken of this, and the reason I need another drink before I say it."

Maren was already pouring; she kept a flask, of course, because she kept everything. He took it neat.

"The originals may yet exist," he said. "When I resigned, Dull's people inventoried my office and found nothing, because I had moved certain materials beforehand — the true originals, the surgeon's returns, the second muster book. I did not burn everything that night. Only the case file. The raw records I hid, in the one place I judged beyond even the Company's tidiness." He paused, and found, to his astonishment, that he was smiling — a real one, crooked but warm. "I hid Company property in consecrated ground. The flooded chapel crypt under the old Seamen's Chapel on Lantern Row. Waterproof oilcloth, lead-lined box, sealed against tide-tables. Six years of high springs and low ebb, so God alone knows what the damp has done to them. But paper is stubborn, gentlemen and ladies. Paper is the most obstinate material ever milled. It wants, more than anything, to be read."

"You're telling us," Maren said slowly, "that the actual, original, hanging-grade evidence of a massacre has been sitting in a crypt for six years while you drank."

"When you phrase it that way, yes, it sounds bad."

"It sounds *biblical*, Tobias. It sounds like the parable of the idiot and the talents."

"I buried it," Fenwick said. "Literally. With the best of intentions, which I am assured pave certain roads. I buried it because I could not bear to burn it and could not bear to look at it, and I told myself the sea would decide — that if it rotted, then the truth had drowned fair and square, and if it survived, then perhaps someday..." He gestured vaguely at the baby, the rain, the absurd smallness of the room. "Someday someone would come asking. With bread, apparently. Bread is a poor bribe, by the way. Add cheese next time."

Orsen stood. For the first time since Fenwick had known him, the boy's stillness looked less like armor and more like a drawn bow. "Tomorrow," Orsen said. "Low tide is at ten. I'll get leave from the warehouse till noon — Dull's short-handed at the coal count and won't question a volunteer for the foulest job on the dock."

"Eager," Fenwick murmured. "Dangerous, eagerness. It was eagerness that got me into archives in the first place." He looked at the window, where Greyhaven's rain was writing its endless grey draft across the glass, and felt something in his chest shift its weight for the first time in six years — not healing, nothing so kind, but turning over, like a ledger opening.

"One condition," he said.

Orsen waited.

"If we go to that crypt, and that box opens, and those pages are legible — then there is no version of events after that in which I remain a drunk in a garret. Dull will smell it inside a week; the man's nose for trouble is his only competent organ. They will come for the papers, and then for the witnesses, and then for anyone who ever held this child." He met Maren's eyes, then Orsen's, and finally — longest — the baby's. "So my condition is this. Whatever we build from what's down there, this child's name goes in it. Not as evidence. As *purpose*. Eron Sault petitioned to see his daughter walk. Well. She'll walk. Over the Company's signature if we do this right. That's my fee, Mr. Coldharbour. Not money. A walking girl."

Orsen put out his hand. Fenwick took it. The boy's grip was calloused from cargo hooks and surprisingly firm, and it occurred to Fenwick, shaking it, that he had just shaken hands with the second bravest coward he had ever known, and that together they might yet amount to one brave man.

"Ten o'clock," Orsen said. "Lantern Row. Bring the flask."

"Bring the bread," said Fenwick. "And for heaven's sake, bring cheese."
