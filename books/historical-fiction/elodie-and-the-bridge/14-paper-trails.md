## 14. Paper Trails

Maren Quill's barge had a table meant for two, but by midnight it held four adults, one sleeping infant, a stub of tallow candle, and every scrap of paper we owned in the world.

We had laid them out like a surgeon lays out instruments: Fenwick's duplicate ledger pages, water-stained at the corners but otherwise whole; Maren's second note from the courier boat, the one with names; Sera Voss's sworn account, written in a shaking hand that steadied itself by the third line and did not waver after; Adela Brine's first draft of nothing, which was to say, three sheets of her questions with our answers penciled between them like a confession waiting for its crimes.

And in the middle, weighted down with Maren's salt cellar, a map of Greyhaven.

"Right," Fenwick said. He had pushed his spectacles up onto his forehead without noticing, which he did whenever he stopped being a broken man and started being an archivist again. It was becoming a habit I liked watching. "Let us proceed as though the Company is competent."

"That's generous of you," Maren said.

"It's not generosity, it's method." He tapped the table. "Assume they are competent, assume they are watching, assume that any single copy we possess can be found and burned within a week. Vane has already proven he can find barges and burn them. Now. What do we actually have?"

"Paper," Maren said. "A great deal of very damning paper."

"Paper is not a record." Fenwick said it the way a priest says *the Lord is my shepherd*. "Paper is a possibility of a record. Paper becomes a record only when it sits somewhere that other people can reach — somewhere with rules, somewhere with a keeper, somewhere where burning it requires more than a match and a dark night. Do you understand? A ledger in this room is a story we tell each other. A ledger in an archive is a fact the world has agreed to keep."

Wren stirred in her basket on the sideboard, and all four of us stopped talking until she settled again. It was remarkable how quickly we had learned that reflex. One week old as a family, and already we moved like soldiers around her sleep.

"So," I said, when she was breathing softly again. "Where does paper become fact?"

Fenwick drew a stub of pencil from behind his ear — I never saw him fetch it; it simply appeared, the way gulls appear over fish — and made three marks on the map.

* * *

"The first," he said, "is obvious, and it's the hardest. The Company's own archive, under Meridian House. Sub-level two, annex registry, where documents go once catalogued and stamped. Once something enters the permanent registry, removing it requires a writ signed by two directors. Not one. Two. I built that rule myself, eleven years ago, after the Corvess shipping scandal, when a director tried to quietly disappear some inconvenient insurance claims." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "They called me tiresome for a decade afterward. Tiresome is the finest compliment an archivist can receive."

"And now they'll change the rule," Maren said. "One director, no writ, done."

"They could. But changing the rule takes a day, and changing it looks terrible, because everyone in Greyhaven who knows anything about archives knows why there are two signatures. The moment they amend it, anyone paying attention smells smoke before there's fire." He looked around the table. "The Company's greatest protection has always been that nobody outside its walls understands what happens inside them. That cuts both ways. If we get the ledgers stamped into the registry, their own machinery protects them."

"If," Maren said.

"Yes. If. It's a large if. It requires credentials, access on the right day, and probably a miracle." He said this without any apparent distress, as if miracles were simply a category of paperwork filed under difficult. "Which is why we don't stop there. Second mark." He tapped the harbor district, near the delegate quarters going up along the Esplanade for the armistice. "The neutral delegation. Madame Ostrava's staff will arrive with an attaché whose entire job is documentation — every claim, every counter-claim, every petition submitted during negotiations goes into the delegation's treaty file. Treaty files travel with the delegates when they leave. They end up in four capitals, in four national archives, in languages the Company doesn't speak and clerks it can't bribe."

"You know this how?" Maren asked.

"I was nearly hired as such an attaché in '39, before—" He waved a hand, indicating the general wreckage of his life. "I know the shape of the work. An attaché files what he's given, properly receipted, and a receipt is a small immortal thing. If testimony reaches Ostrava's attaché through proper channels before the signing, then even if the Company burns every page in Greyhaven, the massacre exists in Lirreth, in Ossory, in the Vell archives, translated into three languages and bound in government calfskin."

"Proper channels," I said. "Meaning the Company controls the intake."

Fenwick's pencil paused. "Yes. That's the difficulty. Their man will be sitting at the door deciding which petitions are genuine and which are crank correspondence. We'd need to go around him or through him, and I don't yet know how. But it's a door, and doors are better than walls."

"And the third?" I asked.

He set the pencil down and, for once, looked less certain than I had ever seen him. "Adela Brine."

"The newspaper woman." Maren snorted. "She told Orsen to his face she wouldn't print a word without a named witness, and now she's a fortress?"

"A newspaper morgue," Fenwick said. "That's what we call it — the archive of past editions, clippings, correspondence, drafts. And here is the thing about newspapers that no one appreciates until they need it: a newspaper is the only archive in this city the Company cannot enter. Meridian House owns warehouses, wharves, judges' debts, and half the aldermen. It does not own the *Courier*. Brine's father kept that paper independent through the blockade years by being too stubborn to sell and too loud to strangle. The morgue holds every edition ever printed, plus the working papers behind them. If the story runs — even hedged, even partial — then the raw material lives forever in a room full of ink smell and bad coffee, guarded by a woman who sues people for looking at her wrong."

"So three places," I said slowly, seeing the shape of it. "The Company's own registry, so erasure looks like guilt. The delegates' file, so erasure doesn't matter. And the newspaper, so erasure isn't believed."

Fenwick sat back, looking mildly astonished, the way he always did when someone else completed his thought. "Yes. Precisely. Any one copy can be destroyed. Any two can be denied. All three together—" He spread his hands. "All three together mean that fifty years from now, some historian pulls a thread, and the thread doesn't stop. It keeps going. From archive to treaty file to newsprint and back again. That's what a record *is*, in the end. Not paper. Redundancy."

Maren laughed, short and surprised. "Listen to him. Eleven months ago this man couldn't redundancy himself out of a wet paper bag."

"I was drinking," Fenwick said with dignity, "in a dry paper garret, and there is a difference."

Wren woke then, properly, with the full-throated conviction of the very young, and Maren went to fetch her, and for a few minutes the great strategy of our lives was suspended while a midwife counted fingers she had counted forty times before and reported, as she always did, "Ten. Still ten. She's hoarding them against the future." And Fenwick, who pretended to be embarrassed by this ritual and was not, turned his face toward the window so we wouldn't see him smiling.

* * *

We divided the work the way careful people divide dangerous work: into pieces small enough that no one piece could hang us alone.

Fenwick took the Company registry. Of course he did. It was his cathedral and his prison both, and when he spoke about sub-level two his voice took on the hushed tone men use for churches and for places they were dragged out of. He would need his old credentials reactivated or forged, and a reason to be inside on a day when the sealing clerk was present, and he would not tell us yet how he meant to manage it. When Maren pressed him, he said only, "Leave the impossible part to me. Impossible is a scheduling problem."

Maren took the delegation. Her reasoning was pure Maren: "I've delivered half the servants' babies on the Esplanade and buried the other half's mothers. Attachés are just clerks with better coats, and clerks eat. Whoever feeds the delegates' household controls the gossip that reaches the attaché, and I can cook circles around any chef the Company sends." She said it lightly, but I had seen her hands while she talked about feeding people — hands that had held newborns and dug graves — and I knew she understood exactly what she was volunteering to carry through that door.

Which left the newspaper, and the newspaper wanted a name.

I walked home from the barge that night by the long way, along the sea wall, because I needed the fog and didn't want to admit why. Three copies in three fortresses. It was good strategy. It was the first night since I'd pulled that oilskin bundle off the warehouse steps that strategy felt larger than fear, and I kept turning the feeling over like a coin I didn't recognize.

But every road on Fenwick's map ran eventually through the same tollgate: credibility. Ledgers could be dismissed as forgery. Sworn statements could be dismissed as coercion — Vane would see to that, would produce witnesses to swear poor Sera had been paid or threatened. Even Fenwick himself, disgraced archivist, drunk of the dockside garret, was a gift-wrapped target for any Company lawyer: *a man with a grudge, gentlemen, a man who falsified records once before.* Adela Brine had seen all of it in the space of one conversation, which is why she had asked for the one thing none of us could cheaply give.

A named witness. Someone clean enough, plain enough, present enough that the story stopped being a rumor and became a man.

I stood at the sea wall a long time, listening to the water work underneath, thinking about my father's name cut into Company stone under the words *volunteer labor*. Seven years I had worn my silence like a coat fitted to me in childhood, and it had kept me warm through every winter, and I had never once noticed until recently that it was also the shape of a shroud.

There was a version of this where I stayed unnamed. Where Fenwick's ledgers and Maren's cooking and Sera's sworn statement carried the truth into all three fortresses, and Orsen Coldharbour, indentured man, warehouse drudge, went on being nobody, safe in his nobodyhood, until the treaty was signed and Wren grew up and someday, maybe, someone told her.

And there was the other arithmetic. Because a ledger proves numbers, but it cannot prove that the numbers were people. Names persuade. My father had been reduced to a number by men whose whole art was reducing people to numbers, and the only currency I possessed that they could not counterfeit was the same coin: a name, spoken plainly, by a person standing in the light.

I got to the warehouse before the dawn bell, as always. Corvane Dull was already there, as always, checking manifests with his little smile, and I put on my silence like a coat, and it fit worse than it used to.

* * *

It was two days later, at the barge, that the plan got its first crack in it — the kind of crack you only notice when the light comes through at the right angle.

We had drawn everything up fair: Fenwick's routes and schedules in his cramped beautiful hand, Maren's list of Esplanade contacts, a draft of Brine's story with a blank space where the witness's name would go, blank on purpose, blank as an argument. We were feeling, I think, almost pleased with ourselves. Maren had produced a plum duff, on the grounds that conspirators work better fed, and Fenwick had eaten two servings while insisting that sugar impairs archival judgment.

That was when Maren, wiping Wren's chin, said it casually, the way she said most of the truest things.

"You know they're going to come at you through your supervisor."

I looked up. "Dull?"

"Dull, the fixer Vane, whoever holds the leash. They've watched you for weeks — the absences, the questions. They won't arrest you; arrests make noise. They'll *invite* you somewhere." She shrugged. "Rich men never recruit in alleys. They recruit over dinner."

Fenwick frowned. "You think Dull would sit across a table from a warehouse drudge and make him an offer?"

"I think," Maren said, "that Corvane Dull has spent twenty years learning exactly what a frightened indentured man wants most, and that the moment Orsen stops being predictable is the moment Dull starts being charming." She looked at me over Wren's head, and there was nothing casual left in her face. "When that dinner comes — and it's coming, Orsen, men like Dull always send the invitation before they send the knife — you'll sit at that table knowing things he mustn't learn. Our routes. Wren. The ledger. You'll have to lie to a professional liar over soup."

The candle guttered, and nobody spoke, and down at the waterline a hull knocked gently against its pilings like a knocker on a door.

Fenwick cleared his throat and reached for the pencil behind his ear. "Then we'd best rehearse," he said. "I once spent six hours being questioned by the Company's inquiry board, and I lied to them magnificently right up until they offered me tea, at which point I confessed to everything. The lesson, which I share freely, is: beware the courtesy. Men lie well to wolves and badly to waiters."

Maren snorted. Wren, sensing the room's attention shifting, chose that moment to sneeze with tremendous force, and we all laughed longer than the sneeze deserved, because laughter is also a kind of provisioning, laid in before the storm.

But walking home that night, I turned Maren's warning over, and by the time I reached the warehouse gates I knew she was right in a way none of us had planned for. We had mapped the fortresses, counted the copies, built our redundancy brick by brick. We had planned for matches and dark nights and locked doors.

We had not yet planned for a man smiling at me across a table, offering me the one thing I wanted most in this world, at the exact price of everything else.

The invitation came three days later, on cream-colored card stock, hand-delivered, smelling faintly of bay rum.

*Dinner. My rooms. Friday. No business — only gratitude between colleagues.*

Corvane Dull had written the word *colleagues* without a flicker, and beneath it, in smaller script, almost tender:

*I understand you've had concerns lately. Bring them.*
