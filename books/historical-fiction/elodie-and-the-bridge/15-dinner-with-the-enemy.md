## 15. Dinner with the Enemy

The invitation arrived on a Wednesday, which was suspicious in itself. Nothing good had ever come to Orsen Coldharbour on a Wednesday.

It came folded inside his wage packet — a small card of heavy cream stock, written in a hand so elegant it looked embarrassed to be found in a warehouse. *Mr. Coldharbour. Supper at my residence, Friday, eight o'clock. Dress as you are. — C. Dull.*

Dress as you are. Orsen read the card four times behind the shelter of a cargo manifest, and each reading made the thing worse. Supervisors did not invite dockmen to supper. Supervisors docked wages, assigned night shifts in the rain, and on rare festive occasions threw a half-rotten orange into the crowd at Midwinter. They did not send cream-colored cards.

He considered the possibilities while he coiled rope with hands that knew their work and left his mind free. One: Dull knew about Wren and this was a trap dressed in gravy. Two: Dull suspected something but didn't know what, and meant to look at Orsen closely over candlelight, the way you look at a coin you think might be shaved. Three — the least likely and most unsettling — Dull was simply lonely.

Orsen had survived seven years by being uninteresting. He had cultivated dullness the way other men cultivated gardens: patiently, daily, weeding out anything that might flower. His opinions were mild to the point of invisibility. His ambitions were stated as none. When asked how he was, he said *kept on*, which was true and invited no follow-up questions.

A man like that could not decline a supervisor's supper without becoming interesting. That was the whole cruelty of it. The invitation was a door with no handle on the inside; the only way through was forward.

So on Friday evening he put on his second-best shirt — the one without the burn hole — and presented himself at Corvane Dull's lodgings above the counting offices on Chandler's Row, carrying a bottle of plum wine he could not afford and had purchased anyway, because a grateful fool always brings a gift.

* * *

Dull answered the door himself, which told Orsen the servants had been sent away. He was smaller than he seemed across a warehouse floor — most men were — with soft hands and a face built for disappointment, long and pale, like a fish that had heard bad news early and never recovered. But his eyes were quick. The eyes of a man who counted things for a living, including people.

"Coldharbour." He took the wine, examined the label, and smiled. "You shouldn't have. Well — you should have, obviously, or I'd wonder about your upbringing. Come in."

The rooms were warm and close, lit generously, smelling of roast fowl and beeswax. A table set for two. A fire already sunk to embers, the better to keep a guest from sitting near it and feeling comfortable. Orsen noted all of it the way he noted everything now: exits first. Back stair down to the yard. Window over the drainpipe, painted shut, probably. Front door past Dull's study, where a lamp still burned.

"You'll take wine," Dull said. It wasn't a question. He poured two glasses of something far better than plum wine and gestured Orsen into a chair. "I've been meaning to do this for some time. Seven years you've been under my authority, isn't it? And I don't believe I've exchanged ten words with you that weren't about tar."

"Eleven, sir," Orsen said. "You once asked if I'd seen your glove."

Dull laughed — a genuine bark of surprise, quickly smoothed away. "Did I find it?"

"No, sir."

"I never do. There's a metaphor in there somewhere." He settled across the table, steepled his fingers, and studied Orsen over them. "You're doing well, aren't you? Better than well. Cargo doesn't spoil under your watch. Manifests balance when you're on shift. Do you know how rare that is? I have men who've worked the Meridian twenty years who can't make numbers sit still."

Orsen shrugged modestly and reached for the bread. "Numbers either balance or they don't, sir. I just write down what happened."

"Mm," said Dull, in the tone of a man who had just learned something and filed it somewhere damp. "Yes. Write down what happened. What a novel approach."

The soup arrived. It was excellent. Orsen ate it with the uncomplicated pleasure of a man who had spent seven years eating standing up, and let that pleasure show, because showing honest appetite was safe. Grateful fools enjoyed their food.

"The Company," Dull said, carving the fowl, "is entering a delicate season. You'll have heard the talk. Delegates coming. The Accord. Six weeks, and Greyhaven becomes the center of the civilized world for one shining afternoon." A slice of meat landed on Orsen's plate, then another, unasked. "There are men upstairs — real men, men whose names you'd only learn from a scandal — who want the ceremony to go perfectly. No loose threads. No awkward stories. History, Coldharbour, is like a manifest. It balances or it doesn't. And someone has been trying very hard lately to unbalance ours."

Orsen buttered his bread. Slowly. "Bad business, sir. Unbalanced manifests."

"Isn't it." Dull sat back with his glass. "A child went missing recently. An infant, in fact. Left somewhere it oughtn't to have been left, and then — gone. Vanished like smoke. There are people very anxious to find her. Not out of cruelty," he added smoothly, watching Orsen's face the way an auditor watches a ledger. "Out of tidiness. She's a discrepancy. And discrepancies, left uncorrected, have a way of growing. You'd know that better than anyone — you're the man who writes down what happened."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobbles and faded. Orsen discovered his heart was going hard and steady, like pile-driving, and made himself chew, swallow, and smile around it.

"A baby, sir?" He shook his head with the bafflement of a simple man. "That's a strange sort of cargo to go missing."

"Strange indeed." Dull's smile didn't move but changed quality entirely, the way lamplight changes when a cloud crosses. "Though here's a curious thing. The steps where she was left — my steps, the warehouse steps — belong to a watchman's round. Your round. Yet somehow you saw nothing, reported nothing, wrote down nothing." He leaned forward an inch. "And now you're eating my chicken."

For one long moment Orsen let himself feel the full shape of the trap, admiring its joinery the way a drowning man might admire the craftsmanship of the anchor dragging him down. Then he set down his knife, sighed — a small, honest, weary sound — and said:

"Truth between us, Mr. Dull?"

"Please."

"That night scared me half to death." He kept his voice low, confessional, ashamed. "Found a baby on the steps in that storm. First thing I thought was — trouble. Second thing I thought was — whose trouble? Third thing I thought was, get it off Company ground before it becomes *my* trouble. So I wrapped it up and carried it to the charity house on Salter's Lane and rang the bell and walked away, and I didn't write it down because — forgive me, sir — writing it down means signing my name next to it, and I've learned not to put my name next to anything that isn't mine."

Silence. Dull turned his glass by the stem, one slow revolution.

"Salter's Lane," he said. "Which charity house?"

"The sisters'. Saint Ferren's." Orsen had rehearsed this in the bathhouse, saying it to the tiles until it came out smooth. Maren had approved the shape of it — *give them a place they can check, and make sure the place checks out.* The sisters at Saint Ferren's took in foundlings and asked no questions; more usefully, they were chronically disorganized, and a wet-nursed infant passed through their registers weekly without ceremony. If Dull sent someone, he'd find a busy orphanage, a crowded ward, and no memory of any particular face.

"Hm," said Dull. He refilled Orsen's glass without being asked, which was either courtesy or a test of how much a grateful fool would drink. Orsen drank exactly enough to seem companionable and stopped, because his survival required all of his wits and at least half of his liver.

"You understand my position," Dull said gently. "I'm asked to account for discrepancies. I'd rather account for them kindly. There's a version of this evening where I report that the child was taken to Saint Ferren's by a diligent watchman, the matter closes, everyone breathes." He paused, delicately. "And there's another version. I'd hate us to end up in that one, Coldharbour. Truly. You're the best man on my rounds, and I mean to keep you."

Then he opened a drawer and laid a document on the table between the candlesticks, turned it around, and slid it across.

Orsen looked at it and felt the room tilt very slightly, though he kept his hands still and his face pleasant.

An indenture release. Drawn up proper, stamped pending, his own name printed in the space for it — *Orsen Coldharbour, indentured, term remaining three years, two months, eleven days* — and beneath it, in the clause reserved for early termination: *services rendered to the satisfaction of the Company.*

Three years. Three years of his life, sitting on a dinner table next to the salt cellar.

"Eat," Dull said warmly. "It's getting cold. The paper keeps."

* * *

Orsen ate. It was, annoyingly, delicious.

He talked too — that was the performance, and he gave it his whole craft. Asked about the fowl's seasoning. Admired a print of the harbor on the wall. Laughed at Dull's anecdote about a junior clerk and a misdelivered crate of ladies' corsets, and laughed in the right places, and added nothing clever of his own. A grateful fool, he reminded himself, was not a stupid fool. Stupidity got you dismissed. Foolishness — warm, bumbling, harmless foolishness — got you underestimated, and underestimation was the finest armor ever issued to a poor man.

But underneath the performance, cold and clear as harbor water in January, he was doing arithmetic.

The offer was real. That was what frightened him. Dull had power to spend and was spending it — three years of a man's life, freely given back, in exchange for one child's whereabouts. And the terrible thing, the seductive, sickening thing, was how reasonable it sounded served with good wine. Who would blame him? Who would even know? Hand over a name, a street, a barge — walk out free tomorrow with stamped papers in his pocket. Seven years of silence, rewarded at last. Wasn't that what all his rules had been building toward? Keep quiet, keep alive, buy your way out.

Except the rules had been written by a younger man who hadn't yet held Wren asleep against his shoulder at three in the morning, her fist closed around his finger with the absolute confidence of someone who had decided he was hers.

*Forget this face,* the note had said. Sera Voss's handwriting, sharp and hurried. Well. He had failed at forgetting once already. He was not going to succeed now at the price of a stamped piece of paper.

"So," Dull said at last, folding his hands. "If you were to hear something — anything — about our missing discrepancy. Where would you bring it?"

"To you, sir," Orsen said promptly. "Directly."

"And if bringing it to me were... inconvenient? If you feared someone else was listening?" Dull's voice dropped to something almost kind. "There's a man named Vane who may approach you. If he does, you'll tell me the same day. That's not a request, Coldharbour. That's the price of my continued goodwill."

Vane. Orsen kept his expression at grateful-idle, but somewhere behind his ribs, a door he hadn't known was unlocked swung open. Maren had spoken the name once, spitting into the bilge. *The fixer. The one who finds things for the Company. Mostly people.*

"I don't know any Vanes, sir," he said. "But if one finds me, you'll hear of it before my boots are dry."

"Good lad." Dull rose, signaling the evening's close, and clapped him on the shoulder with those soft, counting hands. At the door, holding Orsen's coat for him — a courtesy so unnatural it raised every hair on Orsen's neck — he added, lightly:

"Oh — one more thing. Purely domestic. My housekeeper's cousin works at Saint Ferren's, you know. Small world. She says the sisters turned away no infants at all last month. Quiet month, apparently." He helped Orsen into his sleeve, unhurried, pleasant. "Funny thing, memory. Everyone remembers something different. Well. Goodnight, Coldharbour. Mind the fog on your walk home."

The door closed. Orsen stood on the landing a moment, breathing fog, hearing behind him the cheerful clink of Dull clearing dishes.

*She says the sisters turned away no infants at all.*

Which meant either the cousin was lazy, or Dull had invented the detail to see Orsen flinch. Either way: the clock on the lie had started ticking. He had days, perhaps a week, before someone stood in Saint Ferren's ward with a list of dates and a good memory.

* * *

He did not go straight home. Rule of the trade: never lead trouble to your door. He walked the long way, along the sea wall where the fog came off the water thick as wool, and let his thoughts finally take off their company manners.

Three years. He turned the number over like a coin bitten for gold. Three years, two months, eleven days — that was what his freedom cost retail tonight, and Dull had offered to waive the fee for the price of a baby. And the worst of it, the part that sat in his stomach like swallowed anchor-chain, was that for one whole heartbeat — one — he had wanted it. Wanted it with a hunger that shamed him. Sleep without counting exits. Work without bowing. A name in his own mouth instead of a number in a Company book.

One heartbeat. Then he'd thought of Wren's face when Maren blew raspberries on her belly, and of Fenwick saying quietly, over the duplicate ledgers, *"They wrote 'fever' forty-one times, Coldharbour. Forty-one lies, in my handwriting, and I signed every one."*

Safety bought with silence was just silence with better food. He'd eaten well tonight. He felt no fuller for it.

At the corner of Rope Walk he checked his shadow twice, doubled back through the cooperage yard, and only then let himself knock — twice, pause, once — at Maren's current safehouse, a sailmaker's loft that smelled of canvas and cat.

Maren opened the door with a boat-hook before she opened it with her hand, saw it was him, and lowered both. "Well? You look like a man who's been dined and digested."

"Dull knows the charity house story has a hole in it. Days, maybe less. And he's put a leash on me — I'm to report if a fixer named Vane comes asking." He unwound his scarf, and his voice went flat and quiet. "Maren, he offered me my indenture papers. Signed. For word of where Wren sleeps."

Maren was quiet for a moment. She was not a woman who offered comfort cheaply, and when she spoke it was practical as rigging: "And you're telling me instead of taking them. That's the whole sermon, that is. Now sit down and eat something that isn't chicken, because you've got a guilty look and Fenwick's been up since dawn copying ledger pages, and—" 

She stopped. From the loft's inner room came a sound that stopped them both: Wren, awake, crying — not the cross, hungry cry they'd catalogued between them, but something thinner, hoarse, wrong. Maren's face changed, and Orsen felt the floor drop out of the evening.

"She's been hot since noon," Maren said, already moving. "I thought it was teeth."

Orsen crossed the loft in four strides, and by the time he reached the little bed and gathered her up — burning, flushed, whimpering at the light — every thought of cream-colored cards and stamped releases had burned off him like morning fog.

Whatever came next, it would come fast. And Wren needed a doctor's tonic, tonight, from shops where asking after infant fever powders in a nervous voice could be remembered by the wrong clerk.

"Get your coat," Maren said grimly, reaching for her bag. "We're going out through the yards, and pray the checkpoints are sleepy."
