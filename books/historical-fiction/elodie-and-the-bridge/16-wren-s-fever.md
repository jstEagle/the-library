## 16. Wren's Fever

The trouble with moving a baby through a city that doesn't want her to exist, Maren thought, was that babies had no talent for subtlety.

She'd smuggled brandy past customs cutters, laudanum under fish crates, and once — a story she told only when very drunk and only to people who owed her money — an entire fugitive alderman rolled in a sailcloth and labeled as pickled herring. All of those things could be kept quiet. A baby, on the other hand, considered silence a personal insult. Wren had spent the first hour of the journey asleep against Maren's chest, angelic as a painting of somebody else's child, and then woken at precisely the wrong moment and announced herself to the whole of Salt Row with a wail that could have stripped barnacles.

"There now," Maren murmured, bouncing her along the narrow lane between the fish-stinking houses. "None of that. We're being nobody, remember? Nobody's don't scream."

Wren screamed.

An old woman leaned out of a window above them, took in the scene — broad-shouldered midwife, bundled infant, pre-dawn dark — and withdrew without comment. In Greyhaven, that was courtesy. Everyone knew what it cost to move quietly through the world, and everyone knew better than to ask what was being moved.

It wasn't supposed to be like this. The plan — Orsen's plan, and he drew plans the way other men drew breath, careful and ruled and double-checked — had been simple. The landlady at the Rope Walk safehouse had grown nervous; her eyes went to the window whenever boots passed in the street, and nervousness was contagious. So they would move Wren before the nervousness could turn into something worse. Fenwick would meet them at the salt arches with papers. They would walk her across town in daylight, plain as pilgrims, because the boldest disguise was ordinariness. New safehouse by noon. Done by supper.

Except Wren had been hot when Maren lifted her from the cot. Not warm — Maren knew the difference the way she knew her own pulse. She'd pressed her lips to the small forehead and felt the fever sitting there like a coal under cloth, and by the second street their bundle of nobody had gone from screaming to a thin, exhausted mewing that frightened Maren considerably more than the screaming had.

Babies burned fast. That was the cruelty of it. A grown body argued with sickness over days; an infant's body lost the argument by teatime.

"Right," Maren said to her, to herself, to the fog rolling in off the harbor. "Change of plans."

There were no plans. There was only the knowledge every midwife carried in her spine: fever in a babe under one year meant medicine within a day or a funeral within the week, and willow bark and prayer were for grown bodies. Wren needed willowin — the bitter blue tincture, the expensive kind, kept behind counters by apothecaries who asked questions of women carrying sick infants at dawn in districts where sick infants at dawn were usually a coroner's business.

Maren turned down a crooked alley, ducked a line of drying nets, and came out on the steps of the Ropewalk Chapel, which hadn't been a chapel in twenty years and was currently a chandler's, but whose squat bell tower still kept the only clock in Salt Row that worked. Seven bells. Fenwick would be waiting at the salt arches in two hours with his forged papers and his trembling dignity, and if she didn't come he would assume the worst, and if he assumed the worst he would do something brave and stupid, because that was what Fenwick did now — he'd discovered bravery late in life, the way some men discover drink, and had no tolerance built up for it.

She found a sheltered nook beneath the old chapel stair, wrapped Wren tight against her own heat, and did the arithmetic she hated most: leave the child to fetch the medicine, or carry the child into the lion's den of questions.

Neither was good. Both beat doing nothing.

Above her, the bell rang out the quarter hour, and Wren stirred, and her small fist found the edge of Maren's collar and gripped it with the astonishing strength of the very young and the very ill, and Maren Quill, who had delivered four hundred children and buried eleven mothers and never once wept in front of any of them, sat in the dark under a chapel stair and let exactly one tear go, quickly, like a thief.

* * *

Fenwick found them at half past seven, which meant he had left his garret an hour early, which meant worry had beaten him there.

He came around the corner clutching his satchel of papers to his chest like a breviary, and stopped dead when he saw Maren's face.

"She's ill," Maren said, before he could ask. "Fever, high and climbing. I need willowin tincture and a cool place and neither of those things is in this district."

"Ill." Fenwick repeated it the way men repeat words they're refusing to understand. Then he crouched, pulled back the edge of the blanket, and looked at Wren's flushed face with an expression Maren had not seen on him before. Tobias Fenwick's face was built for regret; it had practiced regret so long it could do nothing else. But this was different. This was fear wearing tenderness's clothes.

"How high?" he asked, and touched the baby's brow with two fingers, gently, as though asking permission.

"You tell me. You're the man of numbers."

"Frightening," he said quietly. "Where do we get the tincture?"

"Hobbes & Vair, on Cordage Street. Hobbes knows me. Hobbes also knows the Company pays him to report unusual purchases, which is why I've never bought anything unusual from Hobbes in my life."

"So we can't buy it."

"We can't buy it *as ourselves.*" Maren stood, knees cracking, and sized him up. "But you, Master Archivist. Look at you. Coat buttoned to the chin, spectacles, papers, that face like a man apologizing to furniture. You look like exactly what you are — a clerk. And clerks buy medicines for their employers' households all the livelong day. Sick children of Company factors die of fevers every week; half the tincture in Greyhaven goes down the gullets of Company brats."

"I don't have a household," Fenwick said. "Or an employer. Or, strictly speaking, a reason."

"You have papers." She nodded at the satchel. "You have *forged* papers, which is better than real ones, because real ones tell the truth and forged ones tell stories. Tell Hobbes a story. Factor's household, sick child, master's orders, here's the chit. He'll sell you the tincture and be grateful you didn't haggle."

"And while I am committing fraud at an apothecary counter," Fenwick said, with the particular dryness that Maren had learned meant he was terrified, "what will you be doing?"

"Standing at the corner looking like a woman waiting for someone. Because I am. And because if it goes wrong, someone has to run with her."

They looked down at the bundle together. Wren had gone quiet again, which was worse than the crying, her breath coming quick and shallow, her lashes dark against burning cheeks.

"She'll be fine," Maren said, in the voice she used on laboring mothers, the voice that made it true by sheer authority. "Fevers break. It's what they do. This one just needs help breaking."

Fenwick adjusted his spectacles, squared his satchel, and said, "I was exiled from the archive for telling one truth too many. It seems only fitting that I return to respectability by way of a lie."

"That's the spirit."

"It is not spirit. It is irony. Spirit comes later, if we survive."

* * *

Hobbes & Vair smelled of camphor and dried lavender and forty years of spilled iodine, and Hobbes himself sat behind the counter like a toad that had achieved tenure. He was reading a broadsheet and did not look up until the little bell over the door had finished its whole conversation.

Fenwick approached the counter with the papers held slightly wrong — Maren could see it from the corner through the shop glass, the chit presented at an angle instead of flat, the fingers too white around it — and she breathed a silent instruction across thirty feet of apothecary air: *flat, fool, lay it flat, you're not serving a writ.*

Lay it flat, he did. And then, blessedly, the training of a lifetime took over. Maren watched Tobias Fenwick, disgraced archivist, become a different animal entirely: shoulders easing into a subordinate's stoop, voice dropping into the bored, precise murmur of a man reciting someone else's errand. She couldn't hear the words, but she could read the shape of them. *Factor Aldery's household. The little one took a chill. The mistress wants the blue tincture, the good stock, none of your watered stuff.* And Hobbes, who had spent decades being talked down to by clerks, responded to the tone the way a lock responds to its key. He grumbled. He shuffled into the back room. He returned with a brown bottle and wrapped it in paper with the officious slowness of a man who believed speed was for the lower orders.

Then Hobbes asked a question. Maren saw it land — saw Fenwick's back stiffen one degree.

Probably it was nothing. Probably *how old is the child*, or *has the physician seen her*. But Maren's heart went into her throat anyway, and she shifted her weight toward the door, ready to walk in and be a factor's cook with a grievance about being kept waiting.

Fenwick answered without hesitation. He even produced, God help them all, a small sympathetic smile. Hobbes laughed — a short, wet laugh — wrote something in a ledger, and took Fenwick's coin.

A minute later Fenwick emerged onto Cordage Street holding the parcel against his chest, walking with the studied calm of a man crossing a frozen pond, and did not permit himself to hurry until they had turned three corners.

"He asked the child's age," he reported, breathless. "I said ten months. I have no idea if that's true."

"Close enough. She's nine. Nobody checks."

"He also offered his condolences on the child's illness and remarked that his own granddaughter had recently survived a similar fever, and I expressed relief at this, and we discussed, for nearly a full minute, the resilience of grandchildren." Fenwick stared into the middle distance. "I have made a friend, Maren. Under false pretenses. I feel I should send him a note."

"Buy from him again next week and you can apologize properly. Give it here."

The dose was a matter of drops — six, no more, folded into a spoon of cooled barley water she'd brought in a flask — and Wren fought it the way drowning things fight rescue, weakly and with great moral conviction, most of it ending up on Maren's sleeve. But some went down, and after a while the burning forehead seemed, if not cooler, at least less like something that might crack.

"Now the cool place," Maren said. "And the papers."

"The papers," Fenwick said, and produced them with the flourish of a magician revealing a trick that had nearly killed him.

They were good. Of course they were good — Fenwick had spent a miserable week on them, complaining throughout that forgery was merely archival work conducted in reverse and that his conscience objected less to the crime than to the sloppy penmanship of the original documents he was imitating. A widow's passage chit for the ferry at Millstone Stair. A letter of introduction from a midwife's guild that did not technically exist. And the centerpiece: an internal Company movement order authorizing one T. Fenwick, contracted clerk, to escort one nurse and one ward to a convalescent cottage upriver, signed with a supervisor's name that Fenwick swore belonged to a real and suitably obscure man.

"It's Dull's second cousin's name," he'd explained. "Nobody has ever met the second cousin. It's the safest signature in Greyhaven."

At Millstone Stair the checkpoint was exactly as bad as they'd feared, which is to say it was manned by two Company porters, bored, cold, and hungry for someone to bother.

Maren hung back with Wren under the arcade, letting Fenwick go ahead alone — the plan, again, was ordinariness, and a clerk traveling with a nurse and an infant was ordinary, but only if the sequence was right. Papers first. Then the party assembled. Never approach a checkpoint already assembled; assembled parties look like conspiracies.

Fenwick presented himself. Papers out. The elder porter read them with the slow suspicion of a man who could read but preferred not to advertise it.

From the arcade, Maren watched Fenwick do the bravest thing she'd ever seen him do, and it was nothing. He simply waited. He stood in the wind off the harbor, holding his hat, watching his own fate being decided by a bored man with bad literacy, and he did not fidget, did not babble, did not sweat — or if he sweated, he sweated like a gentleman. Somewhere in that ruined man was the spine of the person who had once tried to blow open the Company's ledgers, and it turned out the spine had been there all along, waiting for a cause worth the risk of bending.

The porter handed the papers back. Said something. Fenwick nodded, beckoned, and Maren walked out into the open with the bundle, and the younger porter glanced at her, glanced at the blanket, and visibly lost interest in favor of a pie cart that had just rounded the corner.

They were through.

On the far side of the stair, at the foot of the ferry ramp, Fenwick's composure finally failed him — not dramatically, just a slight loosening at the seams, his breath going ragged, his hand finding the rail.

"They didn't even look at her," he said. "A child, moving through a checkpoint, in this climate, in this city, and no one looked. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means the paperwork was good."

"It means," Fenwick said, and his voice cracked clean down the middle, "that she could have vanished a hundred times over and no one would ever have written it down. No record. No name. Nothing. What we are doing — the ledgers, the testimony, all of it — it isn't abstract for me anymore. I have carried her through a checkpoint. She weighs almost nothing. She weighs everything."

Maren looked at him. Below them the gray water slapped the pilings; above, the fog was beginning to lift into a pale, reluctant morning.

"Yes," she said. "That's how it works. You carry them, and then you can't stop."

* * *

The convalescent cottage was a fiction — a fisherman's bothy upriver that Maren knew from her smuggling days, one room, a hearth, a roof that mostly agreed with itself. But it had cool stone floors, clean air, and silence, and by afternoon Wren's fever broke the way fevers do: all at once, in a sweat, between one of Maren's anxious checks and the next, so that the baby woke clear-eyed and furious and ravenously hungry, as though the illness had been an unfortunate scheduling error.

Fenwick, who had refused to sit down for six hours and had spent them feeding the fire, boiling water, and reading aloud — from what, Maren could not determine, since the only book present was a tide almanac three years out of date — heard the change in the cry and came over so fast he nearly upset the kettle.

"She's—"

"She's fine. Fever's broken. Sit down before you fall down."

He sat. He sat rather suddenly, in fact, on the floor, with his back against the hearthstone, and put a hand over his eyes, and Maren pretended to be very busy with the baby so that a man of letters could have his moment privately.

Wren, restored, celebrated her survival by gripping Fenwick's proffered finger and attempting to eat it.

"Ten months," Fenwick said thickly, from behind his hand. "I told the apothecary you were ten months old. You ought to know that your medical records contain a fabrication."

"Don't listen to him," Maren told the baby. "Everything else about you is true."

Later, with Wren fed and sleeping properly — the deep, boneless sleep of the recovered — and the fire low, and the almanac abandoned, Fenwick spoke into the dark.

"When I burned the ledger," he said, "there was a family. The Okellos. I've never said their names aloud to anyone but myself. I thought perhaps... if this succeeds. If the names go into the sealed record. I would like theirs entered first. Before mine. Before anyone's."

"They'll be there," Maren said. "All of them. Every name we can find, and the ones we can't, written as 'one unknown, beloved.'"

"Is that proper archival practice?"

"It's proper midwife practice. Better than yours."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been something else, and settled against the wall, and within minutes was asleep sitting up, an old man's sleep, sudden and total.

Maren stayed awake a while longer, as she always did, listening to the river and the fire and the small wet sounds of a sleeping child who was, tonight, alive and cooling and safe. Love, she thought, wasn't the feeling. The feeling was cheap and everywhere. Love was the tincture bought under a false name. It was forged papers and a checkpoint crossed. It was a disgraced man reading a tide almanac to a feverish baby for six hours. It was concrete, or it was nothing.

She was nearly asleep herself when the knock came — soft, twice, in the pattern Orsen used — and her heart performed its small nightly ritual of terror and relief.

But when she opened the door, the fog spilling in around a familiar shape, she knew at once that something was wrong. Orsen stood on the step with his collar up and his face pale under the lamplight, and behind him, at the edge of the dark, the lane was not empty.

"Maren," he said quietly. "We have a problem. Someone sold us. And I need to get inside before they see me knocking."
