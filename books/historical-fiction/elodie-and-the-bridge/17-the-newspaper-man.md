## 17. The Newspaper Man

The Greyhaven Courier occupied a narrow building wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold nothing but rope, which Orsen thought fitting. A newspaper, in his experience, was mostly rope — something to bind things with, or to hang from, depending on who held the end.

The sign above the door had lost its C years ago and now read THE GREYHAVEN OURIER, which Maren said was an omen and Fenwick said was a maintenance budget problem. They had argued about it the whole way down Rope Street, in the low, hissing way they argued about everything now, like two cats forced to share a warm windowsill.

"You're certain about this woman?" Fenwick muttered, tugging his coat straight for the third time. He had shaved for the occasion, badly, and there was a stripe of blood at his jaw that he refused to acknowledge existed.

"I'm certain she writes things the Company hates," Orsen said. "That's more certainty than I've had about anything in seven years."

"She also once wrote that my resignation from the archives was 'a coward's retreat dressed as principle.'"

"Was it?"

"It was *neither*, which is worse." Fenwick squared his shoulders. "I want it noted that I am here under protest."

"Noted," said Maren, who had left Wren sleeping at the safehouse with the landlady — a decision that had cost her twenty minutes of pacing and one full recitation of the landlady's virtues before she could walk out the door. "Noted by all of us. Now stop preening and let's go ruin someone's morning."

* * *

Adela Brine turned out to be nothing like Orsen had imagined, which was to say he had imagined a hard-bitten man with ink-stained fingers and a voice like gravel, and instead got a small, quick woman of perhaps forty, perched on a desk stacked so high with paper it resembled a fortification. She was eating an apple and reading a proof sheet at the same time, and she did not look up when they entered.

"Compositor's out sick," she said, by way of greeting. "So if you're here to sell me rope, chandlery goods, or your immortal soul, you'll have to come back Thursday."

"We're here about the pearling station," Orsen said.

The apple stopped halfway to her mouth.

She set it down. She looked at him — really looked, the way he imagined she looked at proofs, hunting the error hiding in plain type — and then at Fenwick, and then at Maren, and something in her face shifted from irritation to attention, sharp as a blade being drawn.

"Close the door," she said.

* * *

Her office — the whole building, really — smelled of ink, cold tea, and the particular staleness of rooms where people argue for a living. She cleared a chair by moving an entire season's worth of back issues onto the floor, listened to them for the better part of an hour without interrupting once, and asked questions the way a surgeon asks: precisely, without mercy, and only where it mattered.

Fenwick did most of the talking, because Fenwick had spent three decades among ledgers and could not help himself. He laid out the duplicate books' contents in the dry cadence of a man reading a shipping list, and somehow that made it worse — the numbers marching across the table like soldiers. Two hundred and eleven divers contracted. Ninety-four officially returned. The rest, in the Company's own hand, *cleared*.

"Cleared," Adela repeated. She had gone very still. "Their word."

"Their word," Fenwick confirmed. "In the overseer's quarterly report, dated four days after the last petition from the divers' council. I have seen the original. I copied it myself, before—" He stopped. Everyone let him stop.

"And the widow," Adela said, turning to Orsen. "Sera Voss. Her sworn statement. You have it where?"

"Safely."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I can afford," Orsen said, and held her gaze until she looked away first, which surprised him. He had expected her to push harder. Instead she nodded slowly, as though he'd passed some examination she hadn't announced.

Maren spoke next, about the oilskin, the courier boat, the names of men she had personally pulled half-drowned from the water and buried alive-well two years before the fever supposedly took them. She told it plainly, hands folded, the way she must have told mothers hard news a hundred times. Adela wrote nothing during any of it. Orsen noticed that. She just listened, and her stillness was its own kind of note-taking.

When they finished, the silence went on long enough that Orsen heard the presses below shift and settle, like a ship at anchor.

"All right," Adela said finally. "Here's my problem."

"I imagine you have several," Fenwick said.

"One big one." She stood and began to pace between the paper forts. "Everything you've told me is true. I believe that. I've covered this harbor for fifteen years and I know what the Meridian Company smells like when it's lying — it smells like lilies over a fish market. But belief isn't print. If I run this story on anonymous sources and documents nobody will let me show, their lawyers will eat this paper alive inside a week. And not just the paper. Me. My compositor. The boy who sweeps the floor. The Company doesn't sue people, Mr. Coldharbour. It *dissolves* them."

"So we find another way," Maren said.

"There is another way. There's exactly one." Adela stopped pacing and faced them. "A named witness. On the record. In print. Someone the delegates can subpoena, someone whose name makes the story too heavy to bury. Anonymous sources whisper. Named witnesses *testify*. The difference is the difference between gossip and history."

The room went quiet in a new way.

"And if no one gives you a name?" Orsen asked.

"Then I don't print. Or I print something so hedged and careful it changes nothing, and the Accord gets signed over those divers' graves, and we all get to live with it." She picked up her apple again but didn't eat it. Just held it, like something to grip. "I didn't make the rules. I just know how the game is scored. Credibility is arithmetic, and anonymity divides by zero."

* * *

They walked back along the harbor in a silence with edges on it.

It was Fenwick who broke first, because Fenwick always broke first; it was practically his function in the group, like a keel breaking water. "She's right, you know. That's the worst part. I spent thirty years learning that a document without provenance is worth less than the paper it's written on. A sworn statement from 'a source known to this newspaper' is a candle in a gale. But a name—"

"A name is a target," Maren said flatly.

"Yes," Fenwick admitted. "Both things are true. That's rather the difficulty."

Orsen said nothing. He was watching the water, gray-green and patient, doing what it always did — taking things and keeping them. His father was down there somewhere, or what the sea had left of him, in the same waters where the Company had once weighed the price of a diver's contract against the cost of silence and found the silence cheaper.

He knew what they were all thinking. He knew because he was thinking it too, and had been since Adela said *named witness*, the words landing in his chest like coins into a well.

Anonymity protects. Names persuade. And somebody had to be the persuasion.

* * *

That evening, at the safehouse, Maren fed Wren mashed pear while Fenwick sat by the fire pretending to read a pamphlet about drainage improvements and fooling absolutely no one. The landlady's cat had adopted the room and lay across Fenwick's feet like a sentry, and every time he moved, it opened one eye with an expression of official disapproval.

"She wants a name," Maren said, without preamble. She was watching Orsen over Wren's head. "You heard her."

"I heard her."

"And you've been wearing that face since the Courier steps. The one where you've already decided something and are waiting to see if anyone talks you out of it."

"Do I have such a face?"

"You've had it since I met you. It's why I trusted you with her, actually. A man who decides quietly and doesn't announce it — that's a man who keeps children safe." She scraped the bowl. "But it's also a man who walks into fires because he's already counted himself as kindling."

Wren chose that moment to bang her spoon on the table with the authority of a magistrate, and the solemnity of the room cracked despite itself. Fenwick jumped. The cat fled. Wren laughed — a wet, delighted, entirely unearned laugh — and for a moment they were just three adults and a baby in a warm room, and the world outside was a rumor.

"She's asleep by eight," Maren said softly, when the laughter settled. "And then you're going to tell us what you decided. Because I can see it sitting on you like a coat."

* * *

She was asleep by eight. Of course she was. Babies keep better discipline than conspirators.

Orsen stood at the window, looking down at the street, where a lamplighter was working his way along with his pole, lighting the dark one flame at a time. Seven years he had survived by being no one. No name spoken above a whisper. No debts, no attachments, no record worth finding. Silence as armor, silence as cage, and never once — not once in seven years — had he been asked to choose between them so directly.

Until now.

He thought about his father's hands. Diver's hands, scarred white in patterns like frost, hands that had taught him to tie knots before he could write his own name. The Company's ledger called those hands *volunteer labor*. Somewhere in a sealed annex, someday, if the world was lucky, someone would write down what they actually were. What they loved. Whose boy they'd carried on their shoulders along this same harbor wall.

Names persuade. And there was one name in that room that the Company already suspected, one name that would buy the others time, one name that had been waiting seven years to be either useful or safe, and could not be both.

He turned around. Maren and Fenwick were both watching him, having the grace to pretend they hadn't been.

"Tomorrow," Orsen said, "I'm going back to the Courier. Alone."

Maren set down her mending. Fenwick lowered his pamphlet. Neither of them spoke, which was its own kind of argument, and he let it stand for a moment before he finished it.

"My name goes in her column. Orsen Coldharbour, indentured man of the Meridian Company, witness to the manifest falsifications, custodian of Sera Voss's sworn account. Let the Company chew on that." He almost smiled. "They can't dissolve what they don't own, and they own me. Which means for once, the arithmetic works in our favor — a named witness they can't pretend doesn't exist, standing right inside their own warehouse."

"That's not courage, that's madness," Fenwick said, but his voice shook, and Orsen knew it was the other thing wearing madness's coat.

"It's arithmetic," Orsen said. "Anonymity protects. Names persuade. And I've been protected long enough."

Outside, the lamplighter reached the corner below their window and lit the last lamp on the street, and the dark gave ground, one flame at a time.
