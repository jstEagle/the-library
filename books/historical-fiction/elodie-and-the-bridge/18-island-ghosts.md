## 18. Island Ghosts

Six years before the storm that brought a child to a warehouse door — and some months before he ever learned what it was to be disgraced — Tobias Fenwick stood on the deck of the *Meridian Rose* and was violently, thoroughly seasick over the railing.

"You'll get your sea legs," said the bosun, without sympathy. "Or you won't. Either way the island's the same distance."

Fenwick wiped his mouth with a handkerchief monogrammed by his mother and reflected that he had made three grave errors in accepting this assignment. The first was going. The second was going by sea. The third, he would only understand later, was going at all.

He was then Senior Archivist of the Meridian Trading Company, Greyhaven division, a title that sounded grander than it paid and heavier than either. He had come aboard carrying two trunks of ledgers, one valise of clean shirts, and a letter from the Company's comptroller instructing him to "audit the station's accounts ahead of contract renewal, with discretion." He had been twenty-nine years old and still believed that an account, honestly kept, could protect people. It is a belief he has since misplaced, the way one misplaces an umbrella — not lost, exactly, but no longer where he left it.

* * *

Saltreach Station did not look like the scene of anything. That was the first thing he noticed, and it would bother him for years: how ordinary it looked. A crescent of white sand. A long shed for drying nets. Rows of low stone houses with blue shutters, a cookhouse, a chapel hardly bigger than a garden shed, and beyond them all the lagoon, so pale a green it seemed the water had been watered down for company.

The divers came down to the beach to meet the boat, and Fenwick counted them out of habit — thirty-one, plus children, plus a handful of old men too stiff in the shoulders to dive anymore. They were lean and brown and squinting, and their hands were wrong. He saw that immediately and did not know what he was seeing: knuckles swollen hard as river stones, palms ridged and pale, fingers that moved stiffly, like gloves that had been worn too long. Pearl-callus, the overseer would tell him later. The sea builds its own armor into you if you let it, and the divers of Saltreach had been letting it, generation after generation.

The overseer was a man named Halloran, broad and pink and cheerful in the way of men who have never once been contradicted on their own island. He shook Fenwick's hand hard enough to reset something.

"The archivist! Good. We'll have you fed, and then we'll talk numbers, and then we'll get you back on a boat before the rains." He laughed. Fenwick smiled the small professional smile he kept for supervisors, which cost nothing and committed him to nothing, and followed Halloran up the sand.

That evening, at the cookhouse, an old woman set a bowl of fish stew in front of him and watched him eat the way a magistrate watches a defendant. When he finished she took the bowl and said, in the tone of a woman remarking on weather:

"Tell them at the Company the contracts ended in spring. Tell them we're still diving on dead paper."

"I'll make a note," Fenwick said, because he always made notes.

She looked at him a long moment, this soft-bodied clerk from the mainland with his ink-stained cuffs, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod once, curtly, and go. Her name, he would learn, was Sera Voss's mother-in-law, though he did not know then what that would come to mean. The widow herself was there that night, young, laughing at something a young diver said, her hair tied back with red cloth. Fenwick remembers her laugh. He has spent years wishing he didn't.

* * *

The ledgers were kept in a room behind Halloran's office, and they were, on their face, immaculate. Halloran wrote a fair round hand and ruled his columns true. Divers entered, tons of shell weighed, pearls graded, wages credited against contract advances. Fenwick audited for four days and found the arithmetic flawless.

It was the fifth day that he found the other thing.

He was cross-checking contract dates against payment dates, a tedious business, when he noticed that every single diver's contract had expired — not recently, but in a cluster, two and three years past. Under the standing rules of indenture, expired contracts meant choice: sign anew or take passage home with wages owed. And wages owed, he discovered, ran to sums that made his eyes water. Saltreach's divers were, by any honest reading, among the wealthiest laborers in the Meridian's employ. Their money simply had never been given to them.

Instead there were entries. Neat ones. *Advance against renewal. Provision credit. Medical fee.* Every expiring contract was followed within days by fresh deductions large enough to zero the balance and force a new signing. Year after year. A machine for making free men unfree, running quietly on an island so beautiful nobody thought to look at it.

Fenwick sat with the ledger open on his knees for a long time while geckos chirped in the wall and the lagoon went gold and then violet outside the window. He thought about the old woman's stew and her weather-voice. He thought: *I will write this up. The comptroller will be horrified. It will be corrected.*

He was thirty years old in six weeks. He believed things.

* * *

The petitions began arriving the next morning, and he understood at once — with the sick lurch of a man who realizes the letter he found was written for him to find — that the divers knew exactly who he was and what an audit might mean.

The first came under the chapel door, folded and sealed with a dab of candle wax: fourteen names, requesting confirmation of contract expiry and settlement of balances owed. The second arrived tucked into his audit trunk, wrapped around a dried seahorse some child thought was funny. Twenty-two names. By the third day there were petitions from nearly every dive-family on the island, some signed, some marked with thumbprints in squid-ink, all saying variations of the same thing in careful, formal words clearly rehearsed: *We ask the Company's auditor to confirm what we are owed.*

Halloran found one of them — Fenwick never learned how — and brought it into the office and laid it on the desk between them with two fingers, as though it were something dead.

"Folk here are happy," Halloran said pleasantly. "You'd wonder at that, maybe, coming from the mainland, where everyone's got a grievance and a lawyer. But island folk are simple. They dive, they eat, they pray. Paper like this—" he tapped it— "this is mainland trouble blowing in on a mainland wind. You follow me?"

"I follow that these men are owed eleven years of wages," Fenwick said. His voice surprised him; it came out level, though his heart was going like a rabbit's.

"And I follow," Halloran said, still pleasant, "that contracts renew, fees accrue, and the ledger balances. Which you've checked yourself, haven't you? Balances beautifully." He leaned back. "Here's my advice, lad, free. Finish your count, write your tidy report, catch the next boat. There's fever comes through sometimes, wet season. Bad luck to linger."

There are moments in a life that divide it cleanly, and Fenwick's came then, sitting in a pink-cheeked tyrant's office with a petition between them and paradise visible through the window. He could feel, distinctly, the shape of the trap closing — not on him yet, but on everyone whose names sat in his trunk — and he felt also, shamefully, vividly, his own fear, and beneath the fear something harder and colder: the arithmetic instinct. *If I leave now, I can carry the ledgers out. If I stay, I can prove more.* He told himself the second thing. He has never stopped telling himself the second thing. It did not make the first thing untrue.

* * *

He stayed nine more days. In those days he did the bravest and most foolish work of his life: he copied everything. Duplicate ledgers, in his own fair hand, nights in his room by candlelight with the shutters barred — contract dates, deduction entries, the petitions themselves copied name by name, thumbprint by thumbprint. He hid the originals in the false bottom of his audit trunk, beneath shirts. He slept four hours a night. During the days he performed mildness so perfectly that Halloran sent a pigeon to Greyhaven praising his cooperation.

And during those days he came to know the divers a little, which was, God help him, the worst part.

He drank palm wine with Brann Voss, Sera's husband, who had hands like driftwood and a habit of laughing just before he said serious things. Brann explained the pearl beds the way another man explains his garden. He showed Fenwick the well — the deep stone well behind the drying shed where divers soaked to steady their ears for depth, cold salt water, forty feet down. "Best training in the Meridians," Brann said proudly. Then, quieter, laughing first: "Course, it's also where they say the fever folk got put, two seasons back. Old Toma and his boy. Company man said drowned, but Toma dove like a seal. You don't drown in a well you dug yourself." He laughed again, watching Fenwick's face. "Anyway. Stew's good tonight. Come on."

Fenwick lay awake most of that night doing sums that had nothing to do with columns: how many boats, how many witnesses, how much proof was enough, how long before Halloran's pigeons finished their journey and whatever was coming finished coming. He was a man trying to count his way out of drowning, and he knew it even then, and the knowing did not help.

On the last night, the old woman — Sera's mother-in-law — came to his window with bread wrapped in cloth, and inside the bread, flat and ironical, a key. "Chapel crypt," she said. "Dry as a sermon. If you can't carry it all out, leave some where Halloran won't look and God will." She pressed his hand with fingers as callused as her son's. "You're the first paper-man who ever counted us right, Tobias Fenwick. Remember that whatever happens."

He has tried. Heaven knows he has tried.

* * *

He sailed on the ninth morning with both trunks heavy as sins. From the stern he watched Saltreach shrink to a white comma on the green water, and he raised his hat, feeling ridiculous and hopeful and brave, a clerk with a trunk full of justice.

The fever was announced six weeks later.

Not fever as country folk mean it — not a bad season, not a hard winter — but a total fever: the Company's notice used the word eleven times in two paragraphs until it wore smooth of meaning. *The station regrettably lost to epidemic. All personnel deceased or dispersed. Operations concluded. Assets written off.* Thirty-one divers, twenty-six family members. Gone between one quarterly report and the next, on paper faster than plague could possibly travel.

Fenwick read the notice in the Greyhaven archive on a Tuesday, standing up, and sat down slowly on a stool built for ledgers, not for men.

He did what he could. He truly did, and this is the part he clings to at three in the morning: he filed his report. Full duplicate ledgers attached. Petitions appended. Recommendations for immediate investigation, wage restitution, and the suspension of Overseer Halloran pending inquiry. He addressed it to the comptroller, who forwarded it upward, where it vanished — not rejected, which could be fought, but vanished, the way documents vanish when someone with a key decides a drawer should be empty.

Then came the meeting. Two men he had never seen before, in coats too fine for the archive, sitting in the comptroller's chair uninvited. They were very kind. They explained that the Saltreach matter was closed for reasons of the armistice, that certain historical sensitivities outweighed certain accounting irregularities, and that a man of Fenwick's talents had a long career ahead of him if his drawers stayed tidy. They left a copy of the official history pamphlet on his desk. *Meridian at Peace: The Company and the Islands.*

They also left a list. Not of threats — nothing so vulgar — a transfer roster. Names of Saltreach petitioners' mainland relatives, with addresses. Cousins in Greyhaven. An aunt in Harrow Point. A brother-in-law, a fisherman, expected on any tide.

Fenwick understood. He wants it recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of his own ruined conscience: *he understood*, and he was afraid, and he delayed. One week, then two, deciding, drafting letters he did not send, waiting for courage to arrive by post like everything else. On the seventeenth day the fisherman's boat was found swamped off the point, and the fisherman was found with it, and Fenwick received a note in a fine copperplate hand: *We trust the matter requires no further attention.*

The matter required no further attention. That was the sentence his whole life afterward was built around.

* * *

Now, six years later, in a garret above a chandlery that smelled of tar and regret, Fenwick set down the cup Maren Quill had bullied him into drinking and looked at the oilskin packet Orsen Coldharbour had laid on his table, and heard again the old woman's voice: *Remember that whatever happens.*

The child slept in Maren's arms, impossibly small, wearing a swaddling cloth with pearl-callus worked into its weave. Island work. Saltreach work.

"There were petitions," Fenwick said. His voice cracked on the second word and he pretended it hadn't. "Fourteen names, then twenty-two. I copied every one. I have them still, or — some of what I have still. The duplicates went into the chapel crypt on Saltreach before I sailed. Whatever the Company burned, burned. Crypts are patient."

Maren and Orsen exchanged a glance over the baby's head, the particular glance of practical people realizing a drunk might be useful after all.

"A crypt," Orsen said carefully, "on an island the Company says doesn't exist anymore."

"Islands don't stop existing because reports say so. Neither do ledgers." Fenwick looked at his own trembling hands, soft as ever, no sea in them at all, and thought of Brann Voss laughing before he said serious things, and the well behind the drying shed, and the seventeen days.

Once, he had delayed, and the delay had a body count. Once was a thing a man carried forever. Twice —

"Well," he said, and reached for the bottle instead of the cup. "It seems I'm being haunted by competent people. Give me a week and a very good reason to believe we won't all drown in that well too."

But even as he said it, he was already, secretly, hopelessly, counting.
