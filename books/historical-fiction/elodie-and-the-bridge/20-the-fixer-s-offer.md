## 20. The Fixer's Offer

Orsen did not go straight home.

He walked the long way instead — along the seawall where the fog came in thick enough to drink, past the fish-stalls being hosed down by boys too tired to complain, past the chapel with its crooked bell that rang wrong twice a day and nobody had ever fixed because fixing things was not Greyhaven's way. He walked and he folded Dull's note smaller and smaller in his pocket until it was a hard little square, like a stone he meant to skip across the harbor.

*A cradle.* Drawn neat and patient, the way Dull drew everything. The man sketched a cradle the way another man might sketch a flower. That was the horror of Corvane Dull: there was nothing in him that found a threat distasteful. To Dull, a threat was simply correspondence.

By the time Orsen reached the door of his rented room above the chandlery, his hands had stopped shaking. Seven years of practice will do that. Fear arrives like weather; you let it pass through you and get on with hauling rope. But sleep did not come, and dawn did come, and dawn was Dull's office, so Orsen washed his face in cold water, put on his most obedient expression — shoulders slightly apologetic, eyes pleasantly empty — and went to be educated.

* * *

Dull's office smelled of lamp oil and ink and, faintly, of the pickled herring he ate at his desk every noon without ever staining a page. He was not eating herring at this hour. He was standing at his window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the dockhands below sort crates, and he did not turn around when Orsen entered. He let Orsen stand there. Of course he did. Making a man wait was the cheapest form of power ever invented, and Dull had never once paid full price for anything.

"Orsen," he said at last, warmly, as if they were old friends who'd shared a boat. "Sit."

Orsen sat. There was one chair, positioned lower than the desk, which was a detail Dull had surely thought about more than once.

"I've been reviewing your file." Dull turned now, smiling his small precise smile, and tapped a folder that Orsen knew contained almost nothing — seven years of obedient silence condensed to a page and a half. "Remarkably clean. No drinking. No debts. No woman. No questions asked in all this time." He sat down opposite, folding his fingers on the blotter. "You understand that a clean file is itself interesting, don't you? Most men accumulate something. A fine here, a warning there. You've accumulated nothing. It's as though you've been holding your breath for seven years."

"Just careful, sir," Orsen said, and gave the little shrug of a man whose ambitions extend to dry socks. "My father drowned working Company water. Carefulness is what I've got of him."

It was a good lie because it was nearly true, and Dull nodded as if tasting it and finding the seasoning correct.

"Well. Careful men do well here, when they're noticed properly." He opened the folder. "There are opportunities coming with the Accord. Ceremony work. Livery, wages doubled, your name read aloud at the dedication. The Company remembers men who help it present itself well to history."

"I'd be honored," Orsen said.

"Yes," said Dull. "You would." He slid a single sheet across the desk. On it was a list of dates and locations — taverns, a barge berth, a laudanum house called the Pale Lamp whose name made something behind Orsen's ribs go very still. "We believe a child has been moved through these places. Illegally. Possibly by persons connected to sedition against the armistice process. If such a child were found by the wrong people first — smugglers, foreign agents — it could embarrass the Company at a delicate moment. If she were found by us, quietly, everyone involved might find their situations… resolved."

He said *she* without emphasis, but Orsen heard it land like a dropped cleat on a wooden deck.

"And if I see her?" Orsen asked, keeping his voice dull with curiosity rather than dull with guilt.

"Then you report to me before you finish your next breath." Dull leaned back, satisfied, a man closing a ledger. "That's all, Orsen. Loyalty is simple. It only looks complicated from inside."

Orsen took the list, thanked him in the flat tone of a man who has just been handed a chore, and left with his heart beating so evenly through sheer force of discipline that he suspected bruising.

The Pale Lamp was fourth on the list. Sera Voss had been moved twice since. Which meant someone had already been asking questions in the places between.

* * *

Maren Quill's barge sat low in the gray water off Tanner's Slip, and Maren herself sat low in a chair on its deck, mending a net with the ferocity of a woman strangling an old enemy. When Orsen came up the gangplank she looked at his face for exactly one second and put down the net.

"Wren's asleep," she said. "And Fenwick's in the hold pretending to organize papers, which means he's panicking productively. Sit. Eat. Then tell me."

She put a bowl of fish stew in front of him that was better than it had any right to be, given Greyhaven, and Orsen told them everything: the note, the office, the list, the word *she*.

Fenwick emerged from the hold looking like a man who had been arguing with a filing cabinet and losing. His hands shook as he poured tea, three cups, spilling a little of each — but when he spoke his voice was steady, and Orsen remembered that courage and fear were not opposites but neighbors who shared a wall.

"They're close," Fenwick said. "The list isn't a search pattern. It's a map of everywhere we've already been. Someone is feeding them."

"The landlady on Cordwain Street," Maren said darkly. "I've said it since the second move. She counts our comings and goings like a moneylender."

"Or the boy who runs errands at the Slip," said Fenwick. "Or—"

"Stop." Orsen set down his spoon. "Chasing the leak today gets us nowhere. What matters is what Dull told me, because it tells us what they know and what they don't. They know a child exists. They know she's been moved. They don't have her location — if they did, Dull wouldn't need me, he'd have sent Vane and a wagon." He looked at the list again. "They also don't have Sera. The Pale Lamp is on it, but she left there eleven days ago. So we are ahead of them. Barely. Ahead is ahead."

"Ahead of them," Maren repeated, unimpressed. "That's a fine place to stand on a dock with the tide coming in. What happens when you tell Dull you haven't seen anything?"

"I tell him nothing yet. Silence buys days. A false sighting buys more, but it burns me the moment they check it, and I can't burn yet — not until the annex plan is ready."

"You're playing cards with a man who owns the table," Maren said.

"Yes," said Orsen. "But he thinks I don't know the game. That's worth a few tricks."

From the deck below came a small sound — Wren waking, a grumble building toward complaint — and Maren went down to fetch her, and came back up with the baby propped on her hip, gnawing on a knot of clean rag with tremendous seriousness. Wren regarded Orsen with her grave dark eyes, then hit him gently on the arm with the rag, apparently by way of greeting.

"Hullo, Wren," Orsen said, and felt the strange loosening in his chest that had been happening lately whenever he saw her, like a knot he hadn't known was tied.

"She's grown," Fenwick observed.

"Babies do that," said Maren. "It's how they taunt us. Every inch is a week less we get to keep them small." She shifted Wren higher and her face, usually set like a harbor wall, went briefly soft and briefly afraid. "So. Days. How many do you think you can actually buy, Orsen Coldharbour?"

Orsen watched Wren worry her rag, utterly unaware that supervisors drew pictures of cradles for men like him.

"As many as I can," he said. "Then some more."

* * *

Vane came four days later, and he did not come through Dull.

Orsen was walking back from the evening shift, taking the route along the fish-market that he always took, because a man under watch should be boringly predictable — and there was a carriage waiting at the corner by the oyster barrows. Not a Company carriage. Something quieter, black, well-hung, with curtains. A man stood beside its door as though he had merely paused to admire the smell of dead fish.

He was ordinary-looking, which was the first frightening thing about him. Medium height, middle years, a good coat worn comfortably, a face built to be forgotten. He could have been a clerk, a customs man, a wealthy widow's nephew. Only his eyes were wrong — not cruel, exactly. *Patient*. Eyes that had never once needed to raise their voice.

"Master Coldharbour." He pronounced it perfectly, first try, which was the second frightening thing. "May I steal five minutes? I'm Vane. I do… tidying. For the Company."

Orsen's whole body wanted to run and his training held it still, and somewhere beneath both of those he registered, absurdly, Maren's voice: *warm bread, keep your hands visible, and never sign anything.*

"Five minutes," Orsen said.

"There's a bench." Vane indicated the seawall with a small courteous gesture, as if offering the best seat in a restaurant.

They sat. The tide muttered below. Somewhere behind them a gull screamed at another gull about an oyster shell, with more passion than either had shown for anything else in its life.

"You've had an interesting week," Vane said pleasantly. "Supervisor Dull believes you're simply slow to report. I suspect otherwise. I suspect you've seen the child, or know who has, and you're waiting — weighing. That's not disloyalty, Master Coldharbour. That's prudence. I respect prudence. So let me save you the trouble of weighing."

He did not produce papers. He produced nothing at all. He simply spoke, in the mild tone of a man discussing freight rates.

"The Meridian Trading Company holds your indenture. Fourteen years total, seven served. Seven remain — hard years, wet years, and at the end of them, what? A discharge paper and a room like the one you have now, only older. Now consider the alternative." He turned slightly, including Orsen in some pleasant shared arithmetic. "Two hundred crowns. Passage on a legitimate vessel — I'd suggest south, the wine coasts; a working man does well there and no one asks questions in sunlight. Papers in a new name, properly made, real seals. And your indenture dissolved, bought out, stamped, gone. You would walk out of Greyhaven a free man with money in his boot within the week."

The words landed in Orsen like coins down a well. Two hundred crowns. He had spent seven years learning not to want things, and Vane had found the wanting anyway and picked it up and turned it over in his hands, showing Orsen its shine.

"That's generous," Orsen managed.

"It's tidy," Vane corrected gently. "Generosity implies sentiment. This is tidiness. A loose thread, resolved without noise, before the delegates arrive. Everyone prefers tidiness. Even you." He paused, letting the sea speak for a moment. "Now the untidy version, which I mention only so you understand the full ledger. If the thread is not resolved, it must be pulled. Children are discoverable, Master Coldharbour. Harbors are large, records are flexible, and people who ask after lost infants tend themselves to become… findable. I would regret that. Genuinely. I've met women like the midwife — salt of the earth. But my regrets and the Company's requirements are kept in separate accounts, and only one of them gets audited."

There it was. Not shouted. Not even emphasized. Offered like a second course.

Orsen looked out at the water. Below the seawall, at low tide, you could see the old dive-ladders, iron and furred with weed, going down into the dark where men had once gone down into the dark for pearls and come up, mostly, except when they didn't. His father had climbed one of those ladders for the last time on an October morning. Everything the Company had ever done to anyone, Orsen thought, it had done in exactly this tone of voice.

"You're asking me to sell a baby," he said, and made his voice as flat as Dull's manifest ink.

"I'm asking you not to drown for her," Vane said. "There's a difference, and it's larger than people think. The child will be found either way — that is not a threat, it's logistics. The only question on the table is whether you and your friends arrive at the same finding. Tidily." He rose, brushed nothing from his coat, and inclined his head with genuine courtesy. "I'll give you three days. Not because you need them — because a man should feel he chose. Choice makes the decision sit better afterward. You'll find me, or I'll find you. Either suits."

He walked to the carriage and got in, and the carriage rolled away, and the gulls went back to their oyster war, and Orsen sat on the bench with two hundred crowns and a drowned father and a baby named after a bird, doing the terrible arithmetic.

Three days.

* * *

They gathered that night in the hold of the barge, among Fenwick's crates of genuinely organized papers, because Maren said the hold had one advantage over every room in Greyhaven: no walls anyone else owned. Fenwick had brought candles and his hidden ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, and he laid them out with the reverence of a priest handling relics, which Orsen supposed they were — the bodies of dead men, in ink.

"He offered you money," Maren said, when Orsen finished. "How much?"

"Two hundred crowns. Plus passage. Plus freedom."

Maren whistled softly. "That's real money. That's start-a-life money. He wants you to know it's real. That's the point of the number."

"The number is theater," Fenwick said sharply. He had gone pale in the candlelight, and his fingers kept moving over the ledger edges as though checking they were still there. "The offer is theater. All of it. Vane doesn't intend to pay two hundred crowns; he intends to establish that payment was possible, so that refusal reads as choice. It's how the Company defends itself later — 'we offered the man an honorable exit.' They'll write it down somewhere. There's always a version written down somewhere." He looked up, and his eyes were bright and terrible. "Don't you see? They're already building the record, the clean record, the Accord record. Every offer they make us is a page in advance. We have to make sure the other pages exist too."

"So we refuse," Maren said.

"We don't refuse," said Orsen slowly, and they both turned to look at him. "Refusing tells him where we stand, and then the three days stop being ours and start being his. We stall. I go back to Vane and I bargain like a frightened man — because he expects frightened, and frightened men haggle. More money. Guarantees. My mother's grave moved somewhere respectable, whatever nonsense occurs to me. Every hour I spend negotiating is an hour he isn't searching, because Vane doesn't waste assets mid-bargain. Frightened men who negotiate are men who intend to say yes eventually. He'll wait for yes."

"And while he waits?" Maren said.

"While he waits," Orsen said, "Fenwick finishes mapping the archive routes, Adela gets her corroboration, and we move Wren one more time — somewhere no landlady counts."

Wren herself was asleep above them in the cabin, in a hammock Maren had rigged from an old sail, and it occurred to Orsen that the whole plan of his life had changed shape without his noticing — that once he had planned his days around avoiding notice, and now he planned them around a sleeping child's breathing, and that both plans required silence, but only one of them was worth the cost of it.

"Three days," Maren said grimly. "You'll give him an answer in three days?"

"I'll give him a counter-offer," said Orsen. "What happens after that depends on whether we're fast enough."

Fenwick snuffed one of the candles to make it last, and the hold got darker, and the ledgers lay in the shrinking light like sleeping animals.

It was two nights later, with one day of Vane's patience remaining and the counter-offer half-composed in Orsen's head, that Maren shook him awake before dawn with a hand like iron and a smell of smoke on her, pointing out the porthole toward Tanner's Slip — where the sky over the water glowed orange, and her barge, and everything aboard it, was burning.
