## 21. Midpoint: The Burning Barge

### MAREN

Maren Quill had delivered two hundred and eleven babies in her life, and she could count every one of them the way a miser counts coins. She had also, over forty-odd years on the water, developed a superstition she never voiced aloud: that fire and birth were cousins, both of them arrivals, both of them demanding you decide in a heartbeat what you would run toward and what you would let burn.

She was mending a net on the barge's stern at half past midnight when she smelled it.

Not smoke — not yet. Smoke came later, rude and obvious. What she smelled first was lamp oil, sweet and greasy, the smell of a hundred dockside suppers, wrong only because it was three in the morning and nobody on the Tallow Eel fried anything at three in the morning except her, and she wasn't frying anything now.

She set down the net. She did not hurry. Hurrying was for people who hadn't spent decades learning that panic made you stupid. She walked below, lifted Wren out of her crate-bed — the girl slept like a small warm stone, bless her — wrapped her in the good blanket, the pearl-callused one, because if everything else went into the harbor tonight then that cloth was going too, and she climbed back up.

The light under the cabin door was orange.

"Well," Maren said, to nobody, to the barge, to the river. "Well, then."

She got Wren onto the gangplank before the flames found the oilcloth. Behind her the fire made its entrance the way fires do — no subtlety at all after that first whisper, just a great roaring appetite, and the Tallow Eel, her home of nineteen years, began to go up like a festival lantern built by someone who hated festivals.

It was only halfway down the plank, wind tearing at her shawl, Wren beginning to wake and fuss against her shoulder, that Maren remembered the ledgers.

*They're in the cabin,* said the part of her brain that kept accounts. *The duplicate ledgers, wrapped in oilskin, under the loose board by the stove, because you thought — you actually thought — a boat full of midwife's clutter was safer than a chapel.*

"No," she told the baby. "No, no, no."

And then, because the universe occasionally arranged itself around the incompetent, she saw Fenwick coming down the quay at a dead run, nightshirt flapping, hatless, looking like an owl that had been robbed.

"FENWICK!" she bellowed. "THE STOVEBOARD! THE OILSKIN!"

He didn't ask questions. That was the thing about Tobias Fenwick that she'd been slowly, grudgingly revising her opinion of: he was a coward about people, but he was absolutely fearless about paper. He went up the far mooring line hand over hand like a man thirty years younger, vanished into the smoke, and Maren stood on the quay with a baby in her arms and watched her whole life cook, counting seconds the way she counted fingers.

Ten. Fifteen. Twenty.

At thirty she started composing what she'd say at his funeral, and it was not going to be kind.

At forty-three he came out through the window, which was no longer entirely where the window had been, clutching an oilskin packet to his chest, his sleeve smoldering, and fell eight feet into the river with a splash that scattered gulls from Gull Point to the customs house.

Maren hauled him out by the collar. He coughed half the harbor up onto the stones.

"Half," he wheezed. "I got half. The other half —" He gestured weakly at the inferno, where nineteen years of Maren's life were becoming weather.

"Which half?" Maren demanded.

"I — what?"

"Which half of the ledgers, man?"

"The..." He peeled back the soaked oilskin with shaking fingers, counted by touch in the firelight. "The death tallies. The station manifests. The orders." A wet, ragged breath. "The payroll records burned."

Maren looked at the fire. The Company's money trail, gone. But the names — the names of the divers listed as lost to fever, the numbers that didn't match the official story, the written instructions to clear the station — those were here, dripping on the quay, clutched by a disgraced archivist who smelled like burnt owl.

"The money we can prove another way," Maren heard herself say. "Money always leaves footprints. Men who steal are vain about something." She shifted Wren higher on her shoulder. "Names are harder. You saved the hard half."

Fenwick looked up at her, streaming, singed, ridiculous, and for one unguarded moment he looked young. "You're not going to shout at me?"

"I am absolutely going to shout at you. Later. When you've stopped dripping." She turned to look at the fire properly.

The Tallow Eel was gone. Her still, her herbs, her mother's chest, four decades of a life arranged exactly as she liked it. Somewhere in there, her good delivery forceps were melting.

Someone inside their circle had talked. There was no other explanation. Nobody knew she kept the ledgers aboard except their own five — six with Sera, seven with Adela Brine, though Adela only knew of them, not where.

Maren stood in the firelight with a baby and half a conspiracy and felt the warmth on her face and understood, with a midwife's certainty, that this pain was the labor pain of something trying to be born, and that it was going to get much worse before anything arrived.

"All right," she said. "First question. Who knew I kept lamp oil in the forward locker?"

### FENWICK

Tobias Fenwick had a theory, formed over years of archival work, that disasters were orderly. They proceeded according to catalog. First the shock, then the inventory, then the blame, then — if one survived all three — the plan. He had watched institutions burn from the inside often enough to know the sequence. What he had never appreciated until now was how much louder it was on the outside.

His hands would not stop shaking. He kept them busy by wringing out his sleeves while Maren interrogated the survivors in the lee of a fish-curing shed, and he tried to think like an archivist about the fire instead of like a man who had very nearly died in it.

Cause: ignition point forward, near the oil locker. Accelerant: lamp oil, generous quantity. Timing: deep night, when the barge-hand Pell would have been ashore drinking and Maren awake, because everyone in Greyhaven's underworld knew Maren Quill slept four hours a night and worked the rest.

Conclusion: someone who knew the barge, knew Maren's habits, and wanted the fire to be survivable-but-ruinous. Not murder. Message.

"You're doing the face," Maren said. He looked up. She had deposited Wren in his arms without ceremony, apparently deciding that a man who had just run into fire for her ledgers could be trusted with an infant, and the weight of the child was doing something disorganizing to his heart rate that he chose not to examine.

"What face?"

"The one where you file the crime instead of feeling it."

"It's a legitimate coping strategy."

"Uh-huh. Say your conclusion."

"Someone who talked knows us but doesn't want us dead," Fenwick said. "If they wanted us dead, they'd have barred the cabin door. This was arson as punctuation. Which means whoever it is isn't Vane or Dull — they don't punctuate, they execute." He bounced Wren slightly; she gripped his thumb with the fierce conviction of the very young. "It means someone smaller. Someone frightened. Someone who was caught and squeezed."

"Pell," said Orsen's voice from the dark.

They all turned. Orsen Coldharbour came along the quay the way he always did, quiet as spilt ink, and crouched down beside them. His face was unreadable in the way that Fenwick had learned meant extremely readable, if you knew the code. Right now the code said: furious.

"Pell drinks at the Anchor Chain," Orsen said. "The Anchor Chain is where Vane's fixers hold court. Last week Pell asked me — casually, he thought — whether Maren had family abroad. Whether she'd ever talked about leaving Greyhaven." He paused. "I told myself it was idle talk. Sailors gossip. I filed it and moved on."

"You filed it," Maren repeated dangerously.

"I'm a warehouse clerk, Maren. Filing is what I'm for." His jaw tightened. "I should have said something. I'm saying it now."

Fenwick watched the two of them look at each other across the dying fire, and understood that this — this exact moment, accusation offered honestly and accepted honestly — was the difference between a group that shattered and one that scarred over. He had seen organizations fail on this hinge. He had personally caused one to.

"So it wasn't betrayal," Maren said at last, slowly. "It was coercion with extra steps."

"Pell has a brother conscripted on the Company hulks," Orsen said. "I checked tonight. On my way here. His transfer papers came through three days after we started asking questions at the Anchor Chain."

Silence. Wren, sensing the mood was insufficiently about her, began to whimper.

"We move the ledgers tonight," Fenwick said. Standing required effort; everything hurt; his left hand was blistered in the shape of his own courage. "Not tomorrow. Now. And we stop assuming any place is safe and start assuming every place is watched, and we build our plans accordingly." He heard his own voice and was startled to find it steady. "I spent six years being careful and it bought me nothing but gin. Careful failed. Let's try loud."

"That's the fire talking," Maren said.

"The fire makes excellent points."

Orsen rose, a shadow unfolding. "There's a coal cellar under the chapel of Saint Aldhelm. Dry, forgotten, and the Company considers religion beneath its notice." A ghost of a smile. "Fenwick. You'll want to see what's carved on the crypt steps."

### ORSEN

Orsen walked rearguard the whole way to Saint Aldhelm's with Wren strapped against his chest in Maren's spare sling, because Maren carried the ledgers and Fenwick carried, in his arms, wrapped in Maren's second-good blanket, the entire surviving contents of her herb still, which he had refused to let burn on principle and was now visibly regretting.

"Why," Fenwick panted, "did I insist on the valerian root."

"Because you're a good man having a bad night," Orsen said.

"I'm a foolish man having a catastrophic one."

"Those are frequently the same thing."

The city slid past them in fog and ember-light. Behind them, the harbor still glowed where the Tallow Eel had been, and already the dockside philosophers were gathering to explain it: insurance fraud, a lamp knocked over, the Quill woman's still finally catching up with her. By morning there would be nine theories and none of them true, and the Company would encourage all nine, because a mystery was cheaper than a message investigated.

Orsen had spent seven years learning the exact weight of silence. Tonight he learned the weight of noise. It was heavier. It cost more to carry. But it moved differently — silence sat on your shoulders; noise swung in your hands like a tool.

Wren stirred against his chest and made her small complaint-sound, the one that meant *cold* or *hungry* or *existentially dissatisfied*, and he murmured to her without thinking, some nonsense about gulls, and felt her settle. Seven weeks ago he had picked her up off the steps because putting her down again had been unthinkable. Now he was carrying her through a sleeping city away from a fire lit to destroy the record of a massacre, and somewhere out in the dark, men paid by the Meridian Trading Company were writing the next line of a story in which none of them existed.

Let them write, he thought. We're writing faster.

The chapel of Saint Aldhelm crouched between two warehouses like a beggar who had wandered into the wrong neighborhood and decided to stay. Fenwick muttered the countersign Orsen had taught him, and the sexton — a vast, silent woman named Mother Kettil who owed Maren three favors dating back a plague winter — let them into the crypt stairwell with a lantern and a look that asked no questions because questions were above her pay grade and below her interest.

The coal cellar was dry, cold, and smelled of old stone. Orsen set the ledgers on a shelf niche while Maren fed a scrap of tallow candle to a saucer, and the light came up yellow and small, and four people and one baby stood in a hole in the earth surrounded by the truth.

"Half," Fenwick said quietly, touching the oilskin. "We have half the proof and no home and a leak in our walls."

"And they have a burnt barge," Maren said, "and no idea where we sleep tonight, and come dawn they'll discover that burning my still has deprived Greyhaven of the only person who can deliver babies at three in the morning, which means within the week every merchant wife in this city will be complaining at dinner parties, and complaints travel upward." She allowed herself one thin, savage smile. "Vane thinks fire ends things. Fire starts things. I've attended enough births to know."

Fenwick sat down heavily on the crypt step, still cradling the herbs, and looked up at the carving Orsen had mentioned: old letters in the stone, a diver's mark, cut by some hand generations back. *For those the sea took unpaid.*

"They knew," Fenwick murmured. "Even then. It's all one long story, isn't it. We're not starting anything. We're finishing it."

Above them, faint through stone and fog, the harbor bell rang the half-hour. Somewhere out there, someone who had sold their fear to the Company was lying awake wondering if they'd done the right thing, and Orsen found he couldn't hate them. He'd been that person for seven years. He knew exactly how warm the cage was.

"Maren," he said. "Tomorrow, when the sun's up — we need to talk about Sera. Because if they burned your barge over the ledgers, they haven't found her yet. And that means the search hasn't really started."

Maren looked at him across the candle flame, and the smile went out of her face entirely.

"No," she agreed. "It means it starts tomorrow."

In his sling, Wren slept on, breathing the small even breaths of someone who did not yet know she was at war, and Orsen stood watch in the dark and counted them, one by one by one, like a miser counting coins.
