## 22. Who Talked

The barge was still smoking when we gathered in Maren's cousin's net-loft above the salt sheds, and the smell of it had followed us there — tar, char, and river water — so that everything we said that night seemed to come out tasting of ash.

We sat in a rough circle on upturned fish crates because the loft had no furniture worth the name. Wren slept in a drawer lined with sacking that Maren's cousin swore had once held an admiral's boots, which Maren said was the most aristocratic thing the child would ever own. Fenwick sat apart from the rest of us, hunched over his half-saved ledgers like a man guarding a wound. Half. He'd pulled exactly half the pages out of the fire, and you could read the arithmetic of his failure in what was missing: whole months gone, names gone, the left side of the record burned to lace.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The lamp guttered. Somewhere below us a dog was barking at the smoke, or at the general injustice of the world, and I found I sympathized with it entirely.

It was Maren who broke first, and she broke loud.

"Somebody talked." She said it the way she delivered babies — flatly, without decoration, because decoration cost breath and breath was for the work. "Four years I've run that barge. Four years. Revenue men have boarded her twice and found nothing but pickled eel and my lawful face. And last night somebody knew exactly where the ledgers were, exactly when Fenwick would be elsewhere, and exactly how to set a fire that would look like a drunkard's lamp instead of what it was."

"Perhaps it *was* a drunkard's lamp," Fenwick said. His voice came out sanded down to almost nothing.

"A drunkard's lamp," Maren repeated, "that caught in the hold, under lock, where you'd hidden dry paper wrapped in oilcloth, in the one berth nobody slept in. Yes. The drunkards of Greyhaven are famously methodical."

"Maren—"

"Don't you *Maren* me." She stood. In the low loft she looked enormous, though she was a small woman; she had that trick, midwives do, of filling whatever room their hands are needed in. "You've been drinking in the Anchor & Almanac since before the fire. You've been telling stories about the ledgers to anyone who'd buy you the second cup. I've seen you do it. You get three cups into that great ruined engine of yours and you start narrating like it's a play and everyone around is your audience."

Fenwick's face did something terrible and quiet. "I don't talk about the ledgers."

"You talk about *around* them. You talk about the archive, the annex, the sealing laws. You recite, Tobias. I've heard you. A man doesn't need to say the secret out loud if he says everything touching the secret loudly enough for someone clever to assemble it."

"And who," Fenwick said, rising now too, and I noticed his hands weren't steady, "assembled it? Your barge-hand? The one with the new boots? The one whose gambling debts you paid off twice and called it charity? I told you at the time — charity on the docks is just a longer word for leverage. Someone buys that boy's debts outright and he'll sell you the fillings from his teeth. Which he appears to have done, minus the teeth."

"That boy has birthed lambs with me in snowstorms."

"Irrelevant. So have I, metaphorically, and I'd still inform on myself for a warm room."

There it was. The ugly sentence, hanging in the lamplight. Maren stared at him as if he'd slapped a patient.

I should have said something then. That's what I thought afterward, anyway, lying awake: *you should have spoken sooner, Coldharbour.* But seven years in a warehouse teaches you that the first minutes of an argument are just the two parties loading weapons, and nothing you say while they're loading will help. So I did what I do. I kept quiet, and I watched, and the quiet that had always been my armor sat on me suddenly like a cage, because this time the silence wasn't keeping me alive. It was letting my family tear itself apart in front of me.

"I'll go," Fenwick said at last. Very dignified. He began gathering the scorched ledgers into his coat like a man packing away his own organs.

"No one is going anywhere," Maren said.

"I'm clearly the risk—"

"You are not going anywhere," she said again, louder, and then, in a completely different voice, small and furious and frightened: "Wren is asleep four feet from you, you impossible man. If you leave through that door with your coat full of paper and your pride full of holes, where do you go? Back to the garret? Vane knows the garret. Everyone knows the garret. You'd be delivering yourself *and* the ledgers gift-wrapped."

That stopped him. It stopped all of us, actually, because it was true, and because it was the first thing either of them had said that acknowledged what the fire actually meant: not that someone among us had betrayed us — that someone out there could find any of us whenever they wanted.

I cleared my throat. Three faces turned to me, and I had the distinct sensation of a man stepping off a dock into water he has not yet measured.

* * *

"Let's be careful," I said. "Because we're doing the Company's work for free right now."

Maren frowned. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning a fire on a barge is worth nothing to Vane unless it breaks us. And the cheapest way to break four people is to let them break each other." I looked between them. "So let's do it properly. Not who's angry. Who *could*."

Fenwick sat back down slowly, which I took for agreement. Maren crossed her arms, which with her was also agreement, just noisier.

"Start anywhere," I said. "The barge-hand. Name?"

"Tolly," Maren said, tight. "He's nineteen. He's not bright, but he's not cruel."

"Bright enough isn't the question. Could someone reach him? Debt, family, fear?"

She was silent a moment. "His mother lives on Coriander Row. Company housing." She said it as if the words hurt on the way out. "But Orsen — he didn't know about the ledgers' berth. I never told him where anything was. He doesn't even know what the ledgers *are*. He thinks Fenwick is my eccentric uncle."

"I am frequently mistaken for worse," Fenwick murmured.

"So Tolly could have sold *us*," I said. "That we existed. That questions were being asked from the barge. But not where the paper slept." I turned. "Your contacts, Fenwick. The ones Maren's worried about. Anyone you've seen since we started?"

"One," Fenwick admitted, and the admission seemed to cost him a rib. "Hessel. Old archive clerk, dismissed the same season I was. I met him at the Anchor & Almanac, once, three weeks ago. I bought him dinner. I shouldn't have. He asked why I was sober and cheerful, and no one has ever accused me of either, and I lied badly. I said I'd found religion."

"Did he believe you?"

"He believed I'd found something," Fenwick said. "Hessel was always good at reading the gap between a record and its cover. But Orsen — Hessel is a coward of the purest kind. He wouldn't inform. Informing requires initiative. He would, however, mention over drinks that Tobias Fenwick seemed suspiciously alive. And drink leads downhill to Vane faster than anything in this city except gravity."

Maren snorted despite herself. Then her jaw set again. "And the laudanum den."

Fenwick's eyebrows went up. "What about it?"

"You've been going back."

"To sit with Sera, yes—" He caught himself. "To keep her supplied. She won't come to us, she won't meet anyone else, and she's in pain. Somebody has to carry the poppy syrup and sit with her while she talks or doesn't. I go Tuesdays."

"Alone."

"Alone."

"Through streets where Vane has watchers on every den door, because dens are where the Company finds people, Tobias. That's what dens are *for*."

"She trusts me," Fenwick said, with sudden heat, and I heard underneath it: *someone trusts me.* After the disgrace, after the burned ledger, after years of drinking alone in a garret — someone trusted him and he would not hand that over, not even to Maren, not even tonight. I understood it better than I wanted to.

"The den isn't the leak," I said.

They both looked at me.

"Sera is hiding from the people who want her dead. If Vane found her through the den, she'd already be in irons, and we'd know, because Fenwick would have arrived Tuesday to an empty room and a broken door." I paused. "You did arrive to an empty room and a broken door once?"

"No. She was there. She's always there." He said it almost defensively, then heard himself. "All right. Not the den."

"Then not Tolly either, probably, and not Hessel, probably," I said, "because none of them could have known the one thing the fire proved someone knew."

"Which is?" Maren asked.

"Which berth."

The lamp flickered. Downstairs the dog had given up and gone somewhere to think about its grievances.

"Fenwick hid the ledgers in the empty berth under lock, wrapped in oilcloth," I went on, and my voice sounded far away to me, the way it does when I finally see something I've been looking at for days. "Only — how many people knew that? Fenwick. Me. You, Maren, after he moved them aboard. Sera, maybe, if he told her. Nobody else. Tolly couldn't have sold it. Hessel couldn't have guessed it. So whoever planned that fire didn't learn it from gossip at all. They learned it by *watching*."

"Watching the barge," Maren said slowly.

"Watching someone carry oilcloth-wrapped bundles aboard and lock them away. Which means they followed someone home. Followed one of us." I looked at Fenwick. "Tuesdays, you walk from the den to the barge. Do you vary your route?"

"...No," Fenwick said. "I take Harrow Lane. It's shortest. I tell myself it's because of my knee, but my knee is a lie I invented to avoid walking past the archive, so really I take Harrow Lane because it avoids Harrow Lane's opposite, which is sentimentality, and—"

"Tobias." Maren, gently, for once.

"Yes. They could have followed me." He sat with that. Then, quietly: "But following me gets you the ledgers' berth, agreed. Following you, Maren, gets you the same. And following Orsen gets you nothing, because Orsen carries nothing and tells no one anything, ever, it's practically his religion—"

"—and following Wren," I said, and my stomach dropped as I said it, "gets you the landlady."

Silence.

"The safehouse," Maren whispered. "Mrs. Ambrosole. Where Wren is *now*."

"Where Wren is now," I agreed. "Where we moved her after the first betrayal, chapter twelve of our misfortunes. Mrs. Ambrosole has seen all our faces. She's seen Maren bring the baby. She's seen Fenwick arrive with forged papers during the fever. She's discreet, kind, charges fair, and has one son."

"In the conscript levy," Maren said. Her voice had gone flat and cold, the professional voice she used for bad news delivered standing up. "Coastal garrison. She showed me his sketch the day we took the rooms — she shows everyone, she's proud of him, he has her chin and her terrible drawing hand. She showed me because she trusted us." She closed her eyes. "And the Company owns his enlistment papers. One word from a fixer and the boy's posting goes from coastal garrison to the island stations, where conscripts wash out in eighteen months, or onto a press-crew, or wherever else they need bodies to be spent. All she'd have to do is answer a few friendly questions about her lodgers. What could she do? Refuse? Trade her son's life for strangers?"

"No," I said. "She couldn't refuse. That's the point. That's how Vane works — he doesn't buy people, he puts them in a room with only one door and then bills them for the walk."

Fenwick had his head in his hands. "A woman who draws badly and loves her son," he said, muffled. "That's our traitor. That's the whole conspiracy of Greyhaven, reduced to a mother with a sketchbook."

"She's not a traitor," Maren snapped, and then caught herself, and laughed — a short, awful, exhausted laugh. "Listen to me. Twenty minutes ago I was ready to feed Tobias to the harbor, and now I'm defending the woman who burned my home."

"You weren't ready to feed me to the harbor," Fenwick said. "You were frightened. It looks similar from inside but the recipe's entirely different."

"Well." Maren wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she did everything. "Someone explain the difference to my heart, which is currently filing complaints."

* * *

We talked until the lamp oil ran low, and what we built in those hours was uglier and stronger than what we'd had before. Trust rebuilt on harder ground, Fenwick called it later, and he was right, though at the time it felt less like construction and more like setting bones without splints.

The conclusions, such as they were:

First, Mrs. Ambrosole was not to be blamed, confronted, or — Maren was immovable on this — endangered further. Whatever she'd been made to give Vane, she'd given under the worst coercion there is, and the debt for that sat squarely on the Company's ledger, not hers.

Second, Wren had to move. Tonight. Not to another lodging house, where the same trap waited wearing a different face, but to the one place Vane's machinery reached worst: Maren's cousin's net-loft had too many eyes, but the salt sheds' night-shift foreman owed Maren a debt from a birthing she'd attended at two in the morning for no fee, and the foreman's wife had wanted a baby around the house since theirs had grown. It wasn't safe forever. Nothing was safe forever; we had perhaps accepted that somewhere around the burning barge. But it was safe tonight, and tonight was the entire country we lived in now.

Third — and this was mine, and it came out of me with the strange calm of a thing long forming — we had to stop thinking of the leak as a disaster.

"Say it plainly," Maren said, when I told them so.

"Vane thinks he's blindfolded us. He burned the barge to teach us we're watched. Fine. We're watched. But a watcher is a channel, Orsen said to myself about six hours ago, and channels run both ways. Mrs. Ambrosole answers their questions. Which means Mrs. Ambrosole can be *given* answers. If we know she reports, we know what Vane knows. We can decide what he learns next. We can send him chasing a fever that doesn't exist while the real work happens behind him."

Fenwick lifted his head very slowly, the way a man lifts his head when he hears, for the first time in twenty years, an argument in favor of hope. "Disinformation," he said. "Archivists have a term for it. We call it *falsifying the manifest*."

"Careful," I said. "That's how supervisors get promoted."

Maren laughed — really laughed, brief and cracked but real — and something in the loft loosened by a degree. She fetched the drawer with Wren in it, and the child slept through her own rescue being planned, one fist curled against her cheek, pearl-callused cloth gone, ordinary swaddling now, ordinary child, extraordinary circumstance, asleep as only the truly unburdened sleep.

"We wake her gently," Maren murmured, gathering blankets. "Cold nights make cross babies and cross babies make noise, and noise is the one commodity we're out of stock in."

Fenwick stood to help, wincing at his knee, which he still claimed was real. At the door he paused.

"Orsen," he said, low, while Maren cooed nonsense at the drawer. "The landlady. When you deduced it. How long had you known? Before tonight?"

"Since the smell of the smoke," I said. "Maybe since before. I watched Dull falsify a manifest for seven years and said nothing, Tobias. Recognizing a coerced woman is the same skill pointed in a kinder direction. You get very good at seeing who's been put in a room with one door."

He studied me for a moment. "And when you saw we suspected each other. You stayed quiet. Watching. Why?"

It was a fair question and it deserved a fair answer, and I gave the truest one I had.

"Because quiet is a tool," I said, "and I'd forgotten tools can rust if you use them for everything. Tonight it nearly let the two of you finish what Vane started. I won't be doing that again." I picked up my coat. "From here on, when I see something, I say it. Even when saying it costs."

Fenwick nodded slowly. "Good," he said. "Because there's something I've been seeing for two days and not saying, and if we've genuinely retired the practice, then I suppose I'm obligated to say it now."

"Then say it."

He glanced toward the drawer, toward Maren, toward the dark window and the city beyond it, all its lit windows full of people in rooms with one door.

"Tuesday," he said, "when I left Sera's den, a man followed me to Harrow Lane. Young. Company coat under a plain jacket. I lost him at the tannery bridge." He swallowed. "Orsen — he wasn't following me. He was *counting*. Checking my route against a schedule. And I've seen that young man before. Years ago. He used to carry Fenwick's tea in the archive, before the disgrace. He has my old supervisor's chin and my landlady's terrible drawing hand."

The lamp went out on its own, as lamps do when the oil is done, and none of us moved to relight it.

"Mrs. Ambrosole's son," I said.

"Conscripts get leave," Fenwick said quietly. "Once a season. And someone arranged his leave to fall this week."

Down in the drawer, Wren sighed in her sleep — a small, ordinary, unbearably precious sound — and we stood in the dark, four people and a child in a net-loft above the salt sheds, understanding at last exactly how fine the mesh was that held us, and how fast we would need to move.
