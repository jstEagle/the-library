## 23. Adela Prints

The printing press on the third floor of The Greyhaven Courier had a stutter.

It was an old machine, older than Adela Brine's tenure and older than her predecessor's, and every ninth impression it hesitated—a wet, mechanical gasp—before committing ink to paper. Newsmen before her had sworn at it. One had reportedly threatened it with a hammer. Adela had made peace with it years ago, the way one makes peace with an aging relative: you learn its rhythms, you forgive its flaws, and on the night that matters most, you stand beside it anyway.

Tonight was such a night.

She stood at the compositor's bench at half past ten, sleeves rolled to the elbow, fingers gray to the second knuckle with lead dust, reading the galley proofs for the fourth time. Around her, the Courier's night staff moved with the particular hush of people who know they are doing something that will be discussed in the morning and possibly regretted by noon.

"Read me the headline again," said Tam Wicker, her printer's devil, a boy of perhaps sixteen whose entire face was freckles and whose entire opinion of journalists was unearned admiration.

"'Questions Rise Over Pearling Station Death Records,'" Adela read.

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"It sounds like a weather report."

Adela looked at him over her spectacles. "That is precisely the point, Wicker. A headline that says *Company Massacred Divers* gets us sued into the sea by breakfast. A headline that says *Questions Rise* gets us sued slowly, over months, while our lawyers earn their keep. I have chosen the slower lawsuit. It is the only kind we survive."

He considered this. "So the whole story is like that? Hedged?"

"The whole story is hedged within an inch of its life." She tapped the proof. "Every name is 'a person familiar with the matter.' Every document is 'a record believed to originate from Company archives.' Every allegation is 'unverified but corroborated in part.' If Meridian's lawyers read this, they will find nothing to sue. They will find only fog."

"And if ordinary people read it?"

Adela paused. That was the question, wasn't it. The hedging was armor against the Company, but armor also muffled. She thought of Orsen Coldharbour standing in her office three nights earlier, hands folded with the terrible stillness of a man who had spent seven years learning not to fidget, saying: *You want corroboration. I understand. But understand this too—the longer you wait for perfect certainty, the better their story gets. They have six weeks and unlimited ink.*

She had told him she'd print when she could prove. He had nodded—he was very good at nodding—and then he had put a page on her desk. Not the ledgers themselves; Fenwick would sooner part with his own teeth. A single transcribed page, a copy of a copy, listing eleven names of divers recorded as dead of fever on dates spanning eight months, each with a payout entry beside it reading *contract voided — no settlement issued.* Eleven men owed money to no one, because everyone who might collect was dead.

"Eleven names," Orsen had said. "You don't need to believe what happened out there. You only need to ask why dead men's contracts were voided without settlement, when settling with a widow costs less than a barrel of pitch. Follow the arithmetic. The arithmetic doesn't hedge."

The arithmetic, it turned out, was beautiful. Beautiful, verifiable, and damning in aggregate even when every individual fact wore its little legal raincoat. Adela had spent two days confirming payouts through harbor customs records—divers' families receiving nothing where the ledgers said contracts were voided—while telling herself she was only gathering context.

"Ordinary people," she said now, to Wicker, "will read between the hedges. People always do. Fog is only convincing from inside it."

* * *

The presses began at eleven and ran until two.

Adela stayed for all of it, partly from professional duty and partly because there is a pleasure in watching a thing become real. Words on a proof sheet are an intention. Words on four thousand circulating copies are a fact in the world, and facts, once loose in Greyhaven, behave like rats leaving a burning warehouse: everywhere at once, and impossible to call back.

By six in the morning, copies were in the coffee houses along Cordwainer Row. By seven, she was told later, a man in the Saltmarket was reading the list of eleven names aloud to a growing crowd, and at each name someone in the crowd knew a cousin, a neighbor, a widow, and said so, and the crowd grew by grief, which is the fastest-growing crowd there is.

Adela herself went home at three, slept four hours, and was back at her desk by nine, whereupon the day stopped being hers entirely.

The first caller was a delegate's attaché at ten—neutral delegation, Ostrava's office, a pale precise young woman named Hessel who did not remove her gloves and did not sit and did not accept tea, which Adela privately scored against her; refusing tea in Greyhaven was practically an accusation.

"The Madame wishes to know," Hessel said, "the provenance of your documents."

"The Madame may read the article," Adela said pleasantly, "where provenance is described as rigorously as the law permits."

"The Madame does not read newspapers. Newspapers are opinions printed quickly."

"What a flattering definition. I'd have said newspapers are evidence printed quickly, and opinions printed slowly afterward by everyone who reads them."

Hessel's left eyelid performed a movement that on a warmer face might have been a wink. "The Madame will be asking questions in committee. Committees prefer documents to articles. Should your source wish certain materials to reach certain desks through certain channels—" she let the sentence trail off like a rope thrown toward a drowning man, "—there are proper ways."

"There are," Adela agreed. "And I'll mention to my source that proper ways exist. Whether he trusts them is his affair. He has been failed by institutions rather thoroughly, you see. He's developed opinions about channels."

Hessel departed with the expression of a chess player who has discovered the other side also owns pieces. Two more delegations' runners came before lunch, and a courier from the trade commission, and a person Adela strongly suspected was a Company lawyer pretending to be an interested scholar, who asked unusually detailed questions about paper stock and watermarking and left with a copy of the article held exactly as one holds something that may later be entered as an exhibit.

At one o'clock, Tam Wicker stuck his head into her office, freckles alight. "Miss Brine. There's a crowd outside the Meridian counting house. Someone's chalked the eleven names on the wall. All eleven. In a nice hand, actually. Very legible serif work."

"God help us," Adela said, "the city is developing typography standards. Has anyone rubbed the names out?"

"Not yet. There's an old woman standing under them daring anyone to try."

* * *

The Company's response came at four, and it came the way Meridian did everything: thickly, expensively, and with an assumption of obedience.

A clerk arrived at the Courier's front office bearing a pamphlet bound in good blue cardstock, forty pages, freshly printed, titled *A True and Faithful Account of the Meridian Pearling Station at Sallow Key: Compiled from Official Records.* Copies had been delivered simultaneously, Adela later learned, to every municipal office, every guild hall, every school above fourth form, and every clergyman in the port district. The print run, by her estimate, cost more than her entire annual salary. It smelled gloriously of fresh ink, which struck her as the single honest thing about it.

She read it standing at the window, and it was, professionally speaking, a marvel.

Where the official record said fever, the pamphlet said fever, with footnotes. Where the ledgers Fenwick kept showed eleven voided contracts, the pamphlet showed a generous relief fund established for grieving families—with subscription lists, amounts, and a facsimile signature of a Company director that Adela happened to know had been in another country during the relevant months. Where the article raised questions, the pamphlet answered different ones, confidently, at length, in a tone of weary patience, as though a kindly uncle were explaining to children why the scary noise upstairs was only the house settling.

There was even a section titled *On the Unreliability of Certain Press Interests,* which managed to cast doubt on the Courier's sources, methods, motives, and presumably personal hygiene without once using a legally actionable phrase. Adela read it twice and felt, despite everything, a flicker of respect. Then anger. Then something colder and steadier than either.

"They've written a whole country," she said aloud. "Fever, relief fund, regrettable tragedy, honorable company. Forty pages of it. Footnoted."

Tam, who had come in with the post, looked up. "Is it lies, then?"

"Some of it's true. That's what makes it dangerous." She set the pamphlet down. "The fever was real, Wicker—or some sickness was; the men were dying of something before they were dying of anything else. The relief fund existed, briefly, before it was quietly closed and its remainder absorbed into general accounts. They haven't invented a world. They've taken the true world and sanded off every edge that cuts. That's harder to fight than a lie. A lie you can catch. Sandpaper you have to feel."

"So what do we do?"

"We do what sandpaper hates." She pulled a fresh sheet toward her. "We find the edges. Tonight's edition runs a follow-up: *Relief Fund Records Show Disbursements Ceased Fourteen Months Before Pamphlet's Claimed Continuation.* We quote their own pamphlet against their own customs filings. Let them argue with themselves. It's the one argument they can't win, because both sides are wearing their own uniform."

Wicker grinned, took the sheet, and paused at the door. "The old woman under the chalked names—someone gave her a copy of ours. Word is she read all eleven names out again, slow, so people could remember them properly. Said the pamphlet's lists had the wrong dates on two of them. Said she knew, because she buried them."

Adela's pen stopped.

"Which old woman?" she asked, though her heart had already begun to answer.

"Quill, they call her. Midwife, lives on a barge. Well—lived. Barge burned last week, didn't it."

"Yes," Adela said softly. "It did." She wrote in the margin of her notes: *M.Q. knows the dates. M.Q. buried them. Dates wrong in pamphlet = dates right somewhere else = get to her.* And beneath it, because she was a journalist and therefore superstitious about luck: *before they get there first.*

* * *

By the end of the week, Greyhaven had divided itself cleanly, the way a log splits along the grain.

In the taverns north of the canal, men argued the pamphlet's version, because it was comforting and because Meridian owned the mortgages on half the buildings the arguments happened in. Along the docks and in the Saltmarket, the article's eleven names circulated like coins, worn smoother and heavier with every retelling. The delegates' committees, meeting behind closed doors on the Accord's tariff schedules, found their agendas invaded by a single recurring item that no one had placed there officially: *correspondence regarding pearling station records.* Three separate delegations requested documents. The Company stonewalled two and offered the third a private briefing, which the third—Ostrava's office—declined in a note Adela would have framed if framing notes were a thing journalists did: *The neutral delegation does not accept private briefings on matters of public record. If records exist, produce them publicly. If they do not exist, explain the disbursements.*

Someone in Ostrava's office had a spine and a sense of theater. Adela sent flowers. Anonymous ones. Journalism permits this.

Orsen came to her office on Thursday evening, slipping in with the fog the way he seemed to slip in everywhere, and stood reading the week's clippings pinned to her wall with an expression she couldn't parse—pride and dread sharing a chair.

"You've stirred it," he said.

"I've stirred it," she agreed. "Now comes the part I warned you about. When you stir a pot, whatever's at the bottom rises first." She turned from the wall. "Meridian won't answer questions forever, Coldharbour. Questions are expensive. Sooner or later they'll stop answering and start producing—something. A witness, a document, a face. Something that ends the argument instead of feeding it. Companies don't defend histories. They replace them."

Orsen was quiet a moment. Seven years of quiet, compressed into a few seconds of it, and then: "They've already got one. A diver. There was a man at the counting house Tuesday, handing out pamphlets, answering questions for the clerks. I saw him across the yard. I couldn't get close." His jaw worked. "But I saw his hands, Brine. Pearl-scarred. Both palms, the way years of shucking marks a man. Whatever story he tells, those hands dove at Sallow Key. They're going to make him say fever."

Adela reached for her coat.

"Where are we going?"

"To look at his hands," she said, "before the whole city learns to stop seeing them."
