## 24. The Counter-Narrative

The Company chose the Fishmarket Square for its miracle, which told Orsen everything he needed to know about how much they feared him.

Fishmarket Square was the largest open space in Greyhaven that ordinary people entered without being paid to, and by nine in the morning it was dressed like a widow at a wedding she disapproved of. Bunting in Meridian blue and white looped between the fish stalls — still reeking faintly of yesterday's catch, because no amount of bunting had ever beaten a fish market at its own game. A stage had been raised on cargo crates, draped in canvas, and above it hung a banner so large that Orsen suspected they'd had to commission new looms for it:

**THE TRUTH OF SAINT ERREN STATION: FEVER, FORTUNE, AND THE COMPANY THAT CARED**

"The Company that cared," Maren repeated under his shoulder, in the flat tone she used for gutting eels. "That's a lovely touch. Like a knife that cares."

"Shh," Orsen said, without heat. They were wedged near a barrel of pickled herring at the square's edge, and Maren had Wren wrapped against her chest under her coat, which was either reckless or brilliant depending on whether one believed an eight-month-old was less suspicious than two adults standing together. Orsen had decided not to decide. There were limits to what a man could usefully worry about in a single morning.

Adela Brine stood apart from them, deliberately, notebook out, wearing her most professionally incurious expression — the face of a woman who attended Company ceremonies the way other people attended funerals: because it was her job and she intended to get through it without weeping.

On the stage, Supervisor Corvane Dull adjusted a microphone that didn't need adjusting. Beside him stood a man Orsen had never seen before, and in the same breath recognized completely.

* * *

He was thin, the man. Thin in the particular way of someone who had been fed enough to stay upright and not one spoonful more. His coat was new — Company grey, good wool, the kind of coat that got handed over with instructions — and beneath it his shirt was clean and buttoned to the throat. Dull introduced him as *Bram Holtz, sole survivor of the Saint Erren tragedy*, and the man stepped forward to the microphone and said, in a voice sanded down to a monotone:

"I was a diver at Saint Erren. When the fever came, I was lucky."

Orsen stopped listening to the words almost immediately, because the words were nothing — he could have recited them himself, could have written them better; whoever drafted this speech understood rhythm if nothing else about human beings. What held him was the hands.

The man gestured as he spoke, small gestures, economical, and every gesture showed his hands.

They were ruined. That was the first thing. Pearl divers' hands were always ruined — the cold and the pressure and the shell edges took their toll, split the skin along lines that healed into pale seams like rivers on a map. But these hands had something more specific. On the right palm, across the base of the thumb, there was a callus ridge — thick, yellowish, curved like a horseshoe. Every diver who had worked the Saint Erren beds developed that ridge from prying oysters off the rocks, hour after hour, breath after breath. It wasn't a scar. It was a signature. You couldn't fake it any more than you could fake a fingerprint, and you certainly couldn't grow one in the two years since the station had "closed."

And there was something else. The left hand's little finger bent wrong at the second knuckle — the old break, the kind that happened when a line snagged and the tender hauled instead of waiting. Orsen had watched a dozen men haul a dozen snags. He knew exactly what that bend cost a man on cold mornings.

*Bram Holtz* was telling three hundred people a story about a fever, and his hands were telling them the truth, and only one person in Fishmarket Square could read both languages fluently.

"He's one of ours," Orsen said quietly.

Maren's head turned a fraction. "One of whose?"

"Saint Erren. He dove the beds. Look at his right palm when he gestures. That callus doesn't come from anything else on earth." Orsen found, to his mild surprise, that his jaw ached, and that he had been clenching it. "Dull dug him up somewhere. Fed him. Clothed him. Put words in his mouth."

"Coerced?"

"Does it matter? A man who says yes under threat and a man who says yes for bread make the same sound through a microphone."

Below the stage, the crowd was behaving the way Greyhaven crowds behaved at free entertainment: attentive in the way of people who would cheer anything that delayed going back to work. A woman near the barrel of herring murmured to her neighbor, "Poor soul, survived all that," and her neighbor nodded, and Orsen watched the fever story settle into the city's memory like sediment into a harbor bed. That was the thing about lies, he thought. They didn't have to be believed with passion. They only had to be convenient. People carried convenient stories the way they carried knives — without thinking, until they needed them.

On stage, Dull stepped back to the microphone. "Mr. Holtz has generously agreed to take questions from the press," he announced, with the magnanimity of a man who already knew which questions would be permitted. "One at a time."

Adela's hand went up first. Of course it did.

Dull's smile tightened by a degree visible even from the herring barrel. "Miss Brine."

"Mr. Holtz." Adela's voice carried pleasantly across the square. "You say the fever took the station. How many died?"

"One hundred and eleven," said Bram Holtz, without hesitation, and Orsen felt the number go through him like a hook through the cheek, because it was *their* number — Fenwick's number, the duplicate ledgers' number, the true number, published last week in the Courier's second story. The Company hadn't bothered to dispute it. They had simply adopted it. It was, Orsen had to admit, elegant. You couldn't argue with a man who agreed with you.

"And your own family?" Adela asked. "Were they at the station?"

For the first time, Bram Holtz hesitated.

It was small — a half-second, the pause of a man checking a memorized page. "My wife," he said. "She was a diver also."

"She was," Adela said, not unkindly, and Orsen saw her pen not moving, saw her storing rather than recording. "What was her name?"

Another half-second. "Mira," said Holtz. "Her name was Mira."

Adela wrote something down then — one line, unhurried — and asked nothing further, and Dull closed the questions with the brisk relief of a man closing a door on weather.

The ceremony wound down. A Company choir sang something about the sea receiving its own. Bunting fluttered. The crowd dispersed toward work, carrying their convenient story with them.

Orsen stood very still by the pickled herring, doing arithmetic.

* * *

Back at Maren's — or what remained of Maren's arrangements, since the barge burned and the world had contracted to borrowed rooms above a sailmaker's loft on Tanner's Row — Orsen laid it all out on the table the way Maren laid out her birthing kit: each piece named, each piece given its place.

"They used our number," Fenwick said. He had been saying versions of this since Orsen finished the account, pacing the little room with a teacup he kept forgetting to drink from. "They took the true death toll — the number we *established*, at cost, in print — and they put it in the mouth of a living witness. Do you understand what that does? Before yesterday, the number was evidence. Now the number is *theirs*. If we attack the testimony, we appear to dispute the count. If we accept the count, we accept the witness." He set the teacup down on the mantel, which was not where teacups went, and continued pacing.

"So we don't attack the number," Orsen said. "We attack the man."

Fenwick stopped pacing.

"You can't," Maren said. She had Wren asleep against her shoulder and was patting her back in the slow metronome that meant *I am participating in this conversation while simultaneously ignoring it*. "Whatever they've got on him — debt, kin, a cell, a needle — pressing him publicly just gets him pressed harder privately. You'd be interrogating a hostage."

"I know." Orsen turned his cup slowly on the table, watching the ring of old tea stains like a tide chart. "I'm not proposing we interrogate him. I'm proposing we read him."

Fenwick lowered himself onto the stool by the window, which creaked its usual complaint. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning lies have seams, Tobias. You taught me that, though you were too drunk at the time to remember teaching it." He did remember, actually — a garret, four years ago, a night Fenwick had spent explaining manifest fraud with the eloquence of the truly ruined. "Every lie is stitched together from pieces of truth, and the stitching shows if you look. Holtz's speech had stitches everywhere. He said the fever came in the spring. The ledgers say the station's supply ship stopped coming in autumn — a year of silence before anyone announced a fever. He said the dead were buried at sea. Maren—"

"Buried two of them myself," Maren said, to Wren's back, "in a well. Alive-well, as it happens, which is a different kind of well entirely." The patting never changed tempo. It was one of the things Orsen admired most about her, this ability to speak of atrocities in the voice of a lullaby. "Go on."

"He said his wife's name was Mira. And he hesitated both times she came up — once before and once after. A grieving widower doesn't hesitate over his wife's name. A man wearing a dead man's coat hesitates." Orsen looked around the table. "So here's what I think. I think Dull didn't find a willing liar. I think he found a frightened one. And frightened men can be reached — not to recant publicly, maybe, not yet — but reached."

"With what?" Fenwick asked. "We have nothing to offer him but danger."

"We have the one thing the Company hasn't given him," Orsen said. "His own name back."

The room went quiet in the way rooms did when an idea arrived and everyone checked it for structural faults. Wren stirred, sighed, resettled.

"He called himself Bram Holtz," Orsen went on. "But he's a Saint Erren diver. Which means he's in the ledgers, Tobias. Every diver on the station roster is in your duplicate ledgers — names, contracts, next of kin, the lot. Somewhere in those pages is the name Bram Holtz used to be, and if it isn't Bram Holtz, then Dull has made his first mistake worth celebrating. Because a survivor can be argued with. A ghost—" he smiled, and was surprised to find the smile had teeth, "—a ghost can't testify at all. But a man who discovers the Company erased his name? That man has a grievance of his own."

"You want to show him his own record," Maren said.

"I want to show him that we know who he is. That we've known since the fishmarket. That the Company thinks he's a costume, and we think he's a man. Then I want to leave the door open and walk away slow." Orsen leaned back. "Fury's a fine fire, but it cooks nothing. Method cooks. So we cook."

Fenwick had gone to the shelf where the surviving half of the duplicate ledgers lived, wrapped in oilcloth inside a biscuit tin, and was already pulling volumes, his earlier agitation transmuted into the particular serenity of a man with a lookup task. "Holtz, Holtz — divers were filed by contracting year... here. Saint Erren intake, third year." His finger ran down a column. Stopped. He read the entry twice. Then he looked up, and there was something in his face that Orsen hadn't seen since before the barge burned — something almost festive.

"Well?" Maren demanded.

"Bram Holtz does not appear in the Saint Erren roster," Fenwick said. "Not as a diver, not as a tender, not as labor of any class. However." He turned the ledger around so they could see the entry under his finger. "*Miravet Osk, diver, contracted third year. Kin listed: husband, Anselm Osk, tender.* Miravet — Mira. And Anselm Osk appears again in the discharge section, marked 'transferred to mainland recovery, fees accruing.'" He let the ledger lie flat. "There's your survivor. Not a diver at all — a tender. The husband of a diver. Which explains the hesitation, and the hands—"

"Wait," Maren said. "If he was a tender, why do his hands look like that?"

"Because tenders hauled lines and pried oysters when the divers fell short, and on a failing station everyone dove eventually." Fenwick's voice had gone soft, the archivist's awe at a document behaving exactly as documents should. "The Company grabbed a man whose grief they could manage and whose hands they assumed no one could read. But we read them. We have his real name, his wife's real name, his contract, his debt — *fees accruing*, gentlemen, which means the Company owns him twice over and can ruin him with a word."

"Then we don't use the word," Orsen said. "We hold it. Gently."

Maren had stopped patting. She looked down at Wren, then up at Orsen, and her expression was complicated — approval and warning braided together. "You're talking about hunting a broken man's leash. You're sure you can pull it without choking him?"

"No," Orsen admitted. "But I'm sure the Company will choke him eventually either way. Survivors who outlive their usefulness to Corvane Dull have a short second career." He thought of the laudanum den, of Sera Voss's ruined face turning away from the light, and added silently: *he knew exactly what the Company did with inconvenient survivors.*

"There's one more seam," Adela said from the doorway.

They all turned. She had come up the stairs unnoticed, which for a woman of her dimensions and opinions was a minor miracle, and she was removing her coat with the air of someone about to deliver a punchline she'd been carrying across town.

"Mira," she said. "When I asked about his wife. He said her name was Mira — correct, incidentally, which means he knows the real Miravet Osk's fate, or believes he does, and is protecting the fiction deliberately. But here's the thing." She hung the coat on the peg, precisely. "Miravet Osk isn't in the burial lists. Any of them. Fever dead, sea dead, well dead — nowhere. I checked the morgue copies this afternoon before coming here. Which means either she walked away from Saint Erren alive—"

"—or the Company has her," Maren finished, "and Mr. Anselm Osk is reciting fever stories with his wife's life sitting in a drawer somewhere as collateral."

"*That,*" said Orsen, "is why we go gently."

The plan assembled itself over the next hour with the satisfying click of well-cut joinery. Adela would find where the Company housed its miracle witness — witnesses under management were always lodged close, cheaply, and within sight of their keepers. Fenwick would prepare a single page from the ledger, copied fair, showing Anselm Osk his own name and contract — proof of recognition, not leverage, nothing that could be called theft or blackmail by a hostile magistrate. Maren would keep Wren and the remaining ledgers moving, because if this went wrong, it would go wrong fast.

And Orsen would do the one thing he had done for seven years, except now in service of breaking silence rather than keeping it: watch, wait, and approach a man carefully.

It was nearly midnight when the room finally emptied into strategy and sleep. Orsen stood at the window looking down at Tanner's Row, where the lamps burned low and honest, and found the fury from the fishmarket still in his chest — but changed. In the morning it had been a hot, useless coal. Now it was banked, drawn, directed, a forge-fire with iron in it.

*Lies have seams,* his father had never said, because his father had been a pearling diver who spoke perhaps forty words a day and most of them about weather. But his father had shown him mended sails, once, and told him: *a patch holds better than the cloth around it, if you sew it honest. It's the lazy patches that tear.*

Dull's story was a lazy patch. Orsen intended to find the corner of it and pull.

He was reaching for the lamp when a knock came at the street door below — three quick raps, pause, one more. Adela's signal, but wrong: she'd left minutes ago and wouldn't knock like that. Maren appeared at her door, Wren in her arms, eyes already sharp.

Orsen descended the stairs, opened the door on the chain, and found not Adela but a dockside runner, one of the feral, quick children who carried messages for pennies and secrets for slightly more. The girl thrust a folded paper through the gap and was gone before he could thank her.

He unfolded it by the hall lamp. The hand was cramped, hurried, unfamiliar — but the message was clear enough, and it rearranged the entire night:

**They are moving your witness tonight. Wharf Three. If you want him before the Company finishes dressing him, come now — and bring proof of who he is. He will not believe it otherwise. He cannot remember his own name.**

Signed at the bottom, in letters that shook only slightly:

*M. Voss.*
