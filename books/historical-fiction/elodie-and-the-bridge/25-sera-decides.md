## 25. Sera Decides

The laudanum den's window was a lie of a window — painted shut, fogged with grime, and positioned so that anyone sitting in the corner chair could see the square across the street without being seen. Sera Voss had chosen that corner chair three days ago and had not surrendered it since, except to be sick twice and to sleep once, badly.

She had told Orsen she wanted nothing to do with testimony. She had meant it, mostly. Testimony was a word for people with futures. She had watched her future go down into the water at Saltmere Station wrapped around a pearl shell he'd been forbidden to keep, and what came up after was not a widow so much as a woman-shaped absence that the sea had declined to take.

But this morning the Company had put up bunting in the square.

That alone should have warned her. Greyhaven did not decorate for anything except the herring fleet and executions, and no one was being executed today — or rather, someone was, but politely, with pamphlets.

* * *

She had gone out before dawn, hood up, half her face turned to the wall out of habit. The scar ran from her left ear to her jaw, and children stared, and staring children made questions, and questions were how the Company found people. Two years of hiding taught you the geometry of walls. You learned which doorways swallowed you and which spat you out under lamplight.

The square had filled early. Dockworkers on their way to shifts who lingered instead, which told her the Company had threatened or paid or both. A platform of new pine, still bleeding sap at the joints. Banners of Meridian blue-and-silver. And at the center of it all, a man in a borrowed coat standing at a lectern too tall for him.

Sera knew him immediately, though she had never seen his face whole.

She knew his hands.

They were resting on either side of the lectern like two pale crabs, and even from thirty yards she could see the scarring — the ridged, pearl-white welts that ran across knuckles and between fingers, the marks that years of prying shells open in cold water wrote onto a diver's skin. Every diver at Saltmere had them. The island women called them *pearl-blessings* when they were feeling kind and *the sea's receipts* when they were feeling honest. Her husband Tam had traced hers once along her collarbone and said she was worth more than anything she pulled up.

This man's hands said he had been down in those waters. His mouth said otherwise.

"Friends of Greyhaven," he began, and his voice cracked on the second word, and Sera felt something cold move through her chest, because she recognized the crack too. It was the crack of a man reciting words he had been made to memorize by people who had explained, gently or not gently, what would happen to the people he loved if he improvised.

"I was stationed at Meridian's pearling station," the man said. "Saltmere. I survived the fever of the winter before last. I buried friends." He paused, and his eyes went somewhere else, and for one terrible moment Sera thought he might actually say something true. "I have come forward now because I am told the peace delegates will hear many stories about Saltmere, and I want them to hear the true one. There was sickness. It spread quickly. The Company did what it could."

A man beside her in the crowd sniffed. An old woman crossed herself in the fisher fashion, thumb to sternum, twice.

"The medicine came late," the survivor continued, and here his voice steadied, because steadiness had been drilled into him, "because of the war. The blockade. No fault of the Company's. We were isolated. Many died. I saw the graves myself."

He had seen the graves himself. That was almost certainly true, and it was the most poisonous thing about the speech, Sera realized. They hadn't picked a liar. They'd picked a witness — a real one, scarred and grieving, whose every honest detail could be welded onto a dishonest frame. The fever was real enough in the telling. What it hid was everything else: the ledgers, the contracts, the order that had come through on the mail boat in Corvane Dull's careful hand, the divers called together and told the Company would not be honoring its payouts, and then —

Then the well.

Sera had not been there when they sealed it. She had been in the infirmary tent with the other wives, held there by two overseers who wouldn't meet her eyes, and Tam had been at the well because he was always where the trouble was, always stepping between men and consequences, saying *we signed, we're owed, there are papers.* Papers. Her beautiful stupid brave Tam, who believed ink protected people.

She heard the sound anyway. Everyone heard the sound. Stone on stone, and then the quiet after, which was worse, and which she had carried in her ears for two years like water that would not drain.

"They tell me some say there was a massacre," the man on the platform was saying now, and the crowd murmured at the word, fed to them like bait. "I was there. There was no massacre. There was fever, and there was grief, and the Company has cared for its survivors ever since. Look at me. Am I not cared for?"

He spread his arms slightly, displaying the coat, and Sera saw his wrists tremble, and saw him force them still, and understood suddenly and completely that this man was dying inside at the rate of approximately one sentence per minute.

She should look away. That was survival. That had always been survival — the wall, the hood, the doorway that swallows. Two years ago she had run from Saltmere with her daughter under one arm and her own blood in her mouth, and she had learned that the dead cannot be helped, only remembered quietly, in corners, in the space between one breath and the next where no one from the Company is watching.

The man on the platform said, "In memory of the lost, let us pray for the souls taken by fever," and the crowd bowed its heads, and Sera Voss did not bow hers.

She stood in the swallowing doorway with her scarred face turned fully to the light, and she thought: *they are burying him again. In front of everyone. In ink this time.*

Tam had died believing papers protected people. She could not — she physically could not, her hands had begun shaking in a way that had nothing to do with the laudanum or its absence — she could not let the only paper that mattered be a Company pamphlet with a bought witness's trembling hands folded neatly on top of it.

Someone had to say the true thing out loud, in the square, in the record, where erasure couldn't reach.

And she knew exactly who was mad enough to help her do it.

* * *

Finding Orsen Coldharbour was not difficult, because Maren Quill had left instructions sewn into the hem of the coat she'd given Sera, in thread-code that any smuggler's wife could read: *If trouble, back of the chandlery on Rope Walk, ask for oysters, mention the tide runs strange.* Sera had memorized it and dismissed it, because instructions were for people who planned to need rescuing, and she had not planned anything in two years except staying alive until dark.

Now she walked to Rope Walk with her hood down.

It was a small rebellion and it felt enormous. Greyhaven's morning crowds parted around her the way crowds do around anything damaged, glancing and looking away, and Sera discovered that two years of hiding had not cured her of caring what faces did when they saw her — but today the stares landed differently. Today she walked through them like weather.

The chandlery's back alley smelled of tar and old fish and, faintly, of Maren Quill's pipe tobacco, which meant Maren was there before Sera even saw the barge-woman leaning against the wall with her arms crossed, watching the street with the unhurried vigilance of a woman who had delivered more babies than the harbor had ships and trusted fewer people than she had fingers.

"You're off your street corner," Maren said. "That's new."

"There was a ceremony." Sera's voice came out flatter than she intended. "In the square. The Company produced a survivor."

Maren straightened slowly. Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise exactly; more like the grim satisfaction of a bet paying off badly. "They've started the story war already, then. Who was he?"

"A diver. Real one. I knew his hands." Sera pulled her hood back entirely, letting the scar sit in full daylight, which made Maren's eyebrows climb. "Brannoc Tull. He mended nets on the north beach. His wife cooked for the infirmary tent. He has two boys, Maren. They're holding his sons over him, that's how they bought him. I could see it in his wrists. He was praying for someone to interrupt."

"And nobody interrupted."

"No." Sera looked down the alley toward the gray slap of the harbor. "Nobody interrupted."

Maren studied her for a long moment, the way she probably studied a laboring mother, reading what was coming before the person carrying it admitted it. Then she pushed off the wall. "Come inside before you finish deciding whatever it is you're deciding. Orsen's in the back arguing with Fenwick about marginalia. It's very dull. You'll hate it. It might do you good."

* * *

The room above the chandlery was small, warm, and chaotic in a way that suggested four people were living in it while pretending only two were. Maps of the harbor shared table space with teacups; a child's wooden horse — Wren's, sent ahead with Maren to the safehouse and smuggled back for comfort — stood guard on the windowsill; and Tobias Fenwick, the disgraced archivist, was red-faced over an open ledger, jabbing a finger at a column of numbers as though the numbers had personally insulted his mother.

"—and if you would simply *read* the notation instead of assuming every abbreviation means what you wish it meant—"

"It means barrels, Fenwick. Even I know it means barrels."

"It means *barriques*, you philistine, which are not barrels, and the distinction matters because the *barriques* column corresponds to the shipment dates of—"

"Fenwick." Maren dropped onto a stool with the sigh of a woman settling a quarrel between toddlers. "The man survived seven years of indenture without knowing barriques from barrels. Let him have this."

Orsen looked up then, and Sera watched him see her — really see her, hood down, scar lit — and she watched him make the decision not to flinch or pity or fuss, and thought, not for the first time, that the Company had made a serious error somewhere along the line with this one. Indenture was supposed to sand people smooth. Somebody had forgotten to check whether Orsen Coldharbour was wood at all.

"You saw it," he said quietly. Not a question.

"The whole pantomime." Sera stayed near the door, out of habit, then deliberately crossed the room and sat, which felt like stepping off a ledge. "Bunting, pine platform, a diver with pearl-hands swearing on his mother's grave that fever did it all. They gave him a coat two sizes too big. He kept touching the cuffs." Her voice caught on that detail, unexpectedly, the smallness of it. "They didn't even measure him for it. They don't think he's a person. He's a prop that can weep on command."

Fenwick had gone very still, in the manner of a man recognizing the shape of history from an unflattering angle. Orsen hadn't moved at all.

"Sera," Orsen said carefully, "you don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." She heard how it sounded — abrupt, too loud, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed in alleys and never in front of people. She made herself slower. "I told you I wouldn't testify. I told you I'd give you my account written down and my name nowhere near it, and you were kind enough not to argue. But I stood in that square this morning and listened to a good man describe my husband's murder as an act of God, and forty people bowed their heads and thanked the Company for its care, and—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Tam believed in papers. Ledgers, signatures, contracts. He thought if you just got things *written properly*, the world would have to behave. They killed him with paperwork, in the end — a false fever report, a closed station file. So." She spread her hands, scars and all, and found she wasn't ashamed of them today. "We'll fight it with paperwork. Properly written."

Silence, of the useful kind. Fenwick removed his spectacles, cleaned them on his sleeve, put them back on, and said, with the reverence of a priest being handed a relic, "You'd swear to it? On the record? Name attached?"

"On the record. Name attached. Face attached, if it comes to that." Sera touched her scar, almost idly. "They gave me this making sure I'd never want to be seen again. I've decided I'm done cooperating with their intentions."

Orsen made a sound — small, involuntary — and Sera looked at him and saw the guilt there, plain as lamplight. Guilt for surviving the den raid that flushed her out, guilt for every question he'd asked, guilt perhaps simply for being the messenger who'd brought her ruin to her own doorstep.

"Don't," she said. "Whatever apology is loading behind your teeth, swallow it. You didn't do this. They did. And the only apology I want from anybody is the kind that gets entered into a treaty annex."

"Annex," Fenwick breathed, already halfway into some archival fever dream. "Sealed documents become permanent record. If her sworn statement lodges alongside the ledgers—"

"That's the plan," Orsen said. "That's been the plan. Now it has a face." He rubbed his eyes. "It also has a problem, which is that the moment Sera testifies, she becomes the most hunted woman on the coast. Vane already tried to arrest her once on invented charges. If we put her name in play before the annex is sealed, they'll disappear her legally before the ink dries, and use the arrest to discredit everything else. Timing isn't strategy here. Timing is survival."

"I know the arithmetic," Sera said. "I've had two years to learn how little my life weighs on their scale." She looked around the room — at the archivist with his trembling reverence, at the midwife with her level gaze, at Orsen with his careful, wounded decency — and felt the strangest sensation, which was that she was not alone in a room. She'd forgotten rooms could do that. "So before any of you start protecting me from myself, I have terms. One term."

"Name it," Orsen said.

"Wren."

The word changed the temperature of the room. Even Fenwick stopped writing.

"She goes free," Sera said. "Whatever happens to the rest of us. Whatever happens to *me*. If I'm arrested, if I vanish, if the whole scheme collapses and Vane wins — my daughter ends up safe, named, provided for, and far from the Company's reach, with papers that say exactly whose child she is and no clause anywhere that gives Meridian a claim on her. You guarantee it. Not best efforts. Not 'if we win.' Guarantee it first, in writing, witnessed, before I ever set foot in front of a delegate." She leaned forward. "I watched men sign papers at Saltmere that were supposed to protect them, and I learned the difference between a promise and paper. So make it paper, Orsen Coldharbour, or find yourself another witness."

She expected argument. She expected Fenwick to explain why guarantees required legal machinery they didn't have, expected Maren to point out practicalities, expected Orsen's decent face to do the decent thing and hesitate.

Instead Orsen was already nodding, slow and certain, like a man confirming something he'd decided long ago.

"Adela Brine," he said. "The Courier will publish it — a declaration of guardianship, printed, dated, distributed. The Company can burn one copy or fifty, but they can't unprint a thing once Adela's had at it, and she'll relish nothing more than making Wren's freedom front-page news." He glanced at Maren. "And Fenwick draws up the formal instrument tonight, witnessed, lodged with the printing. Whatever happens — victory, arrest, the whole thing going down in flames — Wren is out of their reach by tomorrow noon."

"Tomorrow noon," Fenwick repeated, already pulling a fresh sheet toward him, muttering, "guardianship, wardship, we'll need Maren as foster-named party, obviously, she's the practical choice—"

"I accept the honor," Maren said dryly, "provided nobody expects me to stop cursing in front of the child."

"And me?" Orsen asked.

"You," Sera said, and here her voice finally wavered, because this was the part she'd carried up three flights of stairs and through a hostile city and she was tired of carrying it gracefully, "you're the second name on the guarantee. Because I've watched you with her. You count her fingers when you think nobody sees. All ten, every time, like you're checking the world hasn't stolen anything else." She blinked hard. "So it's Maren who keeps her and you who answers for her, and if the Company ever comes asking whose child this is, there'll be a printed page with two names on it and neither of them works for Meridian. That's my price. That's my *only* price. Everything else I'll give freely — Tam's account, the well, the orders, Dull's handwriting, all of it. But my daughter's future is not a bargaining chip, not a reward for success, not a consolation prize for failure. It's the ground the rest of it stands on. Understood?"

"Understood," Orsen said.

And Sera, who had spent two years perfecting the art of expecting nothing, found herself believing him — which was terrifying, and which she decided to do anyway.

* * *

They worked late. Fenwick drafted with the furious joy of a man given permission to be pedantic again; Maren fetched oysters and bread from somewhere and refused to disclose her methods; Orsen sat by the window with Wren's wooden horse in his hands, turning it over, counting — Sera noticed — nothing at all, just holding it.

Around midnight, Fenwick read the document aloud in full. It was, in its way, the most beautiful thing Sera had ever heard: clauses and witnesses and seals, all of it arranged around one small sleeping child in a house across the city, ensuring that the world would have to be extraordinarily determined to hurt her.

"Sign here," Fenwick said, "and initial here, and here, and — ah — forgive the smudge, the chandler's ink is barely fit for labeling herrings—"

Sera signed. Her scarred hand dragged a slight groove through the letters, pearl-white ridges catching on cheap paper, and she thought of Tam tracing those same ridges and calling her priceless, and thought: *look at us, love. Worth more than anything we ever pulled up.*

When Orsen signed beneath her, he wrote his name slowly, and Sera realized she had never actually seen it written out in full before — only fragments, only the abbreviated version indenture permitted a man to own.

"There," Fenwick said, sanding the page. "Done. Witnessed. Lodged for printing at first light." He peered at them both over his spectacles, suddenly shy about his own sentiment. "You understand what this makes us, I suppose. Legally entangled parties to a mutual instrument. Family, more or less, in the eyes of any court that cares to inquire."

"Heavens," said Maren. "Do we get a court to inquire?"

But Sera wasn't listening. Orsen had turned to the window, and the moonlight had shifted, and he was looking at the paper — at the two names stacked one above the other, a drowned woman's daughter and an indentured man's word, bound together in cheap chandler's ink — and his expression was doing something complicated and young.

"What?" she asked him.

"Nothing. Only—" He hesitated, and then, in the flat, careful voice of a man handing over something breakable: "There's news from Rope Walk. One of Maren's runners came while you were signing. Vane's men visited Adela Brine's office this evening. Officially, a routine licensing inspection." He met her eyes. "Unofficially, the Courier's presses are now being watched, day and night. Which means our guarantee gets printed tomorrow, but so does everything else — and the moment that page hits the street, the Company will know exactly who signed it and exactly where to come."

Sera looked at the signature drying under the sand, her own name, out loud in the world for the first time in two years.

"Good," she said. "Let them read it."
