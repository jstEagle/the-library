## 26. The Delegate's Door

The delegate's residence stood on Cordwainer Hill like a cake left out in the rain — three stories of pale stone with wrought-iron balconies that served no purpose except to make the fog feel welcome. Orsen had walked past it perhaps two hundred times in seven years, because the road down from Cordwainer Hill was the shortest route between the warehouse district and the fish market, and he had never once looked at it longer than it took to keep from tripping on the cobbles.

Today he looked at it the way a drowning man looks at a boat.

"You're staring," Adela said. She had dressed for the occasion, which for Adela Brine meant a coat without ink on the left sleeve and her good boots, the ones with heels, which put her nearly at his shoulder and gave her the air of a woman about to head-butt someone important. "Don't stare. Staring is what guilty people do."

"Innocent people don't get past the gate either," Orsen said. "I've checked."

"Then we don't go past the gate." Adela produced a folded card from inside her coat and held it up between two fingers, the way a card sharp shows an ace. "We go through it. I interviewed her attaché last month — a young man named Pell, ambitious, terrified of his own handwriting. He promised me the delegate reads every petition brought to her personally. Every one. His exact words were 'she insists upon it,' which in diplomatic language means 'please, gods, give me something to do that isn't filing.'"

"And you believe him?"

"I believe ambition," Adela said. "Ambition is the only honest thing in this city. Everyone wants something, so everyone tells you precisely where the doors are." She tucked the card away. "Come along. And whatever happens in there — she'll say no. Expect it. Plan around it. But when she says no, you look at her like a man who expected better, not a man who expected nothing. There's a difference, and powerful women can smell it."

Orsen considered this. Seven years in the Meridian warehouse had taught him a hundred varieties of silence, but Adela spoke a language made entirely of noise, and he was slowly learning to translate.

They climbed the steps. The door was answered by a servant with the mournful dignity of a man who had polished the same brass knob for thirty years, and Adela gave her name as though the hill itself ought to be honored, and they were shown into a morning room that smelled of lemon oil and old treaties.

* * *

Madame Ostrava was smaller than her reputation. The pamphlets and the Courier's editorial cartoons had built her into a granite monument with a widow's peak; the woman seated at the writing desk was compact, silver-haired, and mending a glove, of all things, with a needle no thicker than a whisker. She did not rise. She looked up at them over half-moon spectacles with the mild patience of someone who had been interrupted by worse.

"Miss Brine." Her voice was dry and even, gravel smoothed by years of water. "You've brought a guest to a petition call. Unusual."

"Not unusual if the guest is the petition," Adela said.

Ostrava set down the glove. She gestured to two chairs arranged before the desk with the geometric precision of everything else in the room, and Orsen sat, and found that his hands, which had learned stillness as a survival trait, would not entirely comply. They wanted to move. They had carried a note across a city; they wanted to carry something now.

He laid them flat on his knees and began.

He had rehearsed nothing. That was deliberate. Fenwick had offered to draft a statement — Fenwick could draft anything, could draft a confession for a chair — and Maren had suggested he at least decide in advance which details to lead with, but Orsen had refused all of it. He had spent seven years watching supervisors sand documents smooth until nothing true remained. If he was going to speak to the one person in Greyhaven with the power to write him into history or out of it, he would speak the way the truth actually arrived: crooked, out of order, smelling of storm water.

So he told her about the oilskin bundle on the warehouse steps during the worst rain of the spring. He told her about the note, and the handwriting, and how he knew it because he had once inventoried the personal effects of drowned divers, including letters, and Sera Voss wrote her f's with a peculiar downward hook like a gull diving. He told her about the pearling station at Saltmere — the official word was fever, the official word was always fever — and about the ledgers Fenwick had pulled from a flooded crypt, pages in the Company's own hand ordering the station cleared rather than the contracts paid. He told her about Wren, who was eight months old and had recently discovered her own feet with an astonishment he found difficult to describe and impossible to forget.

Somewhere in the middle of it, Madame Ostrava stopped mending the glove.

She let him finish. Then she was quiet for a long moment, and took off her spectacles, and rubbed the bridge of her nose in a gesture so human and so tired that it frightened him more than any of Dull's smiles had.

"The Voss woman," she said. "She is alive. In hiding. She has given sworn testimony."

"She has. Along with eleven names of divers listed as fever dead whom witnesses can place alive and well after the date of the supposed outbreak. Along with duplicate ledgers. Along with—"

"Yes." She raised a hand, not unkindly. "I believe you. That is the first thing you should understand, Mr.—"

"Coldharbour."

"Mr. Coldharbour." Something flickered across her face at the name; he couldn't read whether it was recognition or merely the fatigue of a woman who had heard ten thousand surnames in ten thousand rooms. "I believe you. Completely, and without hesitation, and it changes almost nothing, and that is the second thing you should understand."

Adela leaned forward. "Madame, with respect—"

"With respect, Miss Brine, you may want to hear this before you sharpen your pen." Ostrava rose, moved to the window. Fog pressed against the glass like a face. "Do you know how the Greyhaven Accord will actually be signed? Not the ceremony — the signing. There will be speeches, photographs, a fountain running blue and white for the delegates' pleasure. None of that is the treaty. The treaty is an annex. Forty-one schedules of claims, reparations, territorial waters, labor conventions. Everything real lives in the annexes. And each annex must be lodged through the secretariat, verified by the registry office, and sealed before the final instrument is signed. Once sealed, a document enters the permanent record. It cannot be struck, amended, or quietly lost. It becomes, in the only sense that will matter in fifty years, true."

"So seal our testimony," Orsen said. "Lodge it now. Today."

"With whose authority?" She turned from the window. "A document enters the annex through proper channels. Proper channels here mean the joint secretariat, which requires the signature of each party's accredited officer — and the Meridian Trading Company sits at this table as a party, Mr. Coldharbour, with full standing. Their accreditation predates the armistice. Their representative countersigns every schedule before sealing. Your evidence, presented through those channels, arrives on the desk of a man whose salary depends on it never being read."

"Then present it unilaterally. Neutral parties can lodge—"

"Neutral parties can lodge *observations*." Her mouth thinned. "Observations are appended, not annexed. They are not sealed. They live in a supplementary file that the Company's lawyers will spend thirty years arguing is non-binding, and they will win, because they will still exist in thirty years and the divers will not." She came back to the desk and sat, folding her hands. "Do you see it yet? It is not that anyone refuses you. No one will refuse you. You will be heard, received, sympathized with, thanked. The system does not kill inconvenient truths anymore, Miss Brine — that's crude, and crude things make headlines. The system *files* them. It gives them a number and a shelf and a form confirming receipt, and then it outlives them."

The room was very quiet. Down on the hill, a cart bell rang somewhere in the fog.

"There is one door," Ostrava said at last, slowly, as if measuring each word against some internal ledger of her own. "Testimony reaches the formal record before signing only through the channels I described. The secretariat closes to new lodgments at dusk on the eve of signature. After that, the annex is frozen. Whatever is sealed by then is history; whatever isn't never happened."

"How long?" Adela asked. Her voice had gone flat and professional, the newsroom voice. "How many days do we have?"

"Nineteen."

*Nineteen.* Orsen felt the number settle onto his chest like a sack of grain. Nineteen days, and a secretariat locked behind Company countersignature, and Sera hunted, and Vane circling, and half their ledgers charred on the bottom of the harbor.

"Madame," he said, "you called me Mr. Coldharbour. You know the name."

"I read the personnel rolls of the signatory parties," she said. "It is a habit. Indentured laborers are listed among the Company's assets, and I make it my business to know what my negotiating partners consider assets." She looked at him directly then, and there was no sympathy in it, which he somehow trusted more than sympathy. "Seven years of indenture, entered at age twelve, renewed twice under provisions you could not have consented to. Yes, Mr. Coldharbour. I know exactly what kind of man is sitting in my morning room telling me the truth while the men who own his next seven years wait for him to slip." She paused. "And I notice that you told it anyway."

"It didn't seem like there was a version where I didn't."

"No," Ostrava said softly. "There rarely is, afterward."

Adela stood. Orsen followed a heartbeat later, and understood that the audience was over, and that they had gotten precisely nothing, and that somehow the nothing had edges he could grip.

"Madame Ostrava," Adela said at the door, "when the story runs — and it runs regardless — I'd rather quote you accurately than guess at you."

"When the story runs," Ostrava replied, "quote me saying nothing at all. Accurately." Then, as Pell materialized to show them out, she added, so quietly it might have been meant only for the lemon-oil air: "Mr. Coldharbour. The registry office keeps night hours during conference weeks. Archivists work late. Documents move through that building at strange times, in strange hands. I mention it only because I am a neutral party, and neutrality obligates me to tell both sides everything I know."

* * *

Outside, the fog had thickened into something almost solid, and the walk down Cordwainer Hill felt like descending through milk. Neither of them spoke until the bottom, until the harbor sounds swallowed the possibility of being overheard.

"Well," Adela said finally. "That went catastrophically."

"That went catastrophically?"

"I got nothing quotable, you got no door, and we now have nineteen days instead of the six weeks we were lazily assuming." She pulled off her good boots' worth of confidence with a long breath. "But she told us where the lock is. Did you catch it? The whole machine — accreditation, countersignature, the registry, the sealing. She drew us the entire mechanism, free of charge, while pretending she hadn't. A sympathetic enemy is more dangerous than a hostile one, but a *conscientious* neutral—" she smiled, thin and sharp, "—a conscientious neutral will hand you the blueprint and call it procedure."

"She said the Company controls the channels."

"She said the Company controls the *front* door." Adela turned up her collar. "Fenwick used to work in a registry. Fenwick has credentials, expired or otherwise. Fenwick knows how archives breathe — when they inhale, when nobody counts the papers going in." She started toward the Courier's offices, then stopped, and looked back at him with something that was almost gentleness, delivered in her usual tone of a woman announcing bad weather. "You understand what she just did, don't you? She told a man the law forbids him to help that the archive door is unlocked at night. That's not permission. Neutrals don't give permission. But it's not nothing, either."

Orsen looked back up the hill. Through the fog, the residence's windows were pale rectangles, and he thought he could make out a small figure at one of them, mending a glove, or pretending to.

Nineteen days. A locked system with one honest flaw in it, and a disgraced archivist who knew exactly where archivists kept their keys.

"We'd better find Fenwick," he said. "Before he talks himself out of it. Before any of us do."
