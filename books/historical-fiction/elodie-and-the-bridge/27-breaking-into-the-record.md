## 27. Breaking Into the Record

Tobias Fenwick had spent the better part of thirty years believing that the most dangerous place in Greyhaven was a tavern after midnight. He had revised that opinion several times — the Company's records hall during an audit, Maren Quill's barge during a storm, any room containing Orsen Coldharbour when he'd made up his mind about something — but he now suspected he'd finally found the true answer.

The most dangerous place in Greyhaven was his own garret, at a table covered in stolen history, while a baby chewed on the corner of a massacre ledger.

"Wren," he said gently, extracting the oilcloth-wrapped pages from her fists for perhaps the ninth time that morning. "That is evidentiary material. It is not — no, we've discussed this — it is not for eating."

Wren regarded him with the profound, unblinking contempt that only infants and archivists can properly achieve, and reached for the ledger again.

He let her have it. There were, he reasoned, worse fates for the Meridian Trading Company's secrets than being gummed to death by a one-year-old.

The ledgers lay open before him in two stacks: the salvaged half of the duplicates he'd hidden eleven years ago in the chapel crypt at Saltmarsh Row, and the sworn statements Sera Voss had pressed into Orsen's hands in the laudanum den, written in a diver's widow's careful, salt-warped hand. Beside them, Adela Brine's notes from three interviews, and Orsen's own testimony, which Fenwick had transcribed himself because the man wrote like someone who expected every sentence to be confiscated.

Which, Fenwick thought, was not paranoia. It was memory.

He dipped his pen, crossed out the fourth draft of the sentence he'd been circling all morning, and started again.

*Annex Filing Protocol, Subsection Twelve: documents lodged with the Armistice Commission's annex archive prior to the sealing ceremony acquire permanent record status upon seal affixation. No party, signatory or otherwise, may withdraw, amend, or destroy sealed annex materials.*

He had written that subsection himself.

Eleven years ago, as a young second-archivist with more confidence than sense, Fenwick had been assigned to draft the archival provisions for what was then a hypothetical peace treaty between powers nobody expected to stop fighting. He had been proud of Subsection Twelve. He had thought of it as a small fortress built into the paperwork — a promise that whatever the victors wanted forgotten, the *record itself* would refuse to forget. His supervisor had praised it as "unnecessarily idealistic" and filed it anyway, because filing things was what they did.

Now Fenwick sat in a rented garret that smelled of mildew and old tea, staring at his own fortress, and realized the door only opened from one side.

His credentials. His seal-key. His registration number with the Archivists' Guild, which had never formally revoked it — merely "suspended pending rehabilitation," which was Guild-speak for *we are embarrassed by you and hope you'll die quietly*. The annex archive would admit him on signing eve, when the final lodgments were processed. Any accredited archivist could lodge documents. That was the whole architecture of the thing. You didn't need permission from the Commission; you needed to be *real*, and to arrive before the seal came down.

The problem, of course, was everything else.

* * *

Orsen arrived at dusk, letting himself in with the knock Fenwick had taught him — two quick, one slow, like a man tapping out a ledger total — and stood dripping in the doorway with the particular expression of someone who has walked through rain without noticing it was raining.

"You're drafting again," Orsen said. It wasn't a question. He could apparently smell ink on Fenwick the way sharks smelled other things.

"I am *structuring*," Fenwick corrected. "Drafting implies uncertainty. I have moved past uncertainty into the stage where I argue with myself about punctuation." He slid a chair out with his foot. "Sit. And prepare yourself emotionally. I've had an idea."

Orsen sat. He did not, notably, look afraid. Fenwick had come to know the man's fears the way a sailor knows weather — the small flickers, the long grey fronts, the sudden squalls — and this was not fear. This was attention. Orsen listened the way he did everything: completely, silently, as though silence were a currency he was hoarding against a famine he alone expected.

"The annex archive," Fenwick began, "is housed beneath the Commission Hall on Cordwainer Square. Three vault levels. Everything lodged there becomes untouchable the moment the treaty seal is affixed — which happens at noon on signing day, overhead, in the great chamber, with all the delegates present and the whole city ringing bells."

"And the lodgment window closes the evening before."

Fenwick blinked. "You've read the protocol."

"You left it on the table under the teapot for four days." Orsen's face did something that on another man would have been a smile. "I read everything you leave anywhere. Occupational habit."

"Yes, well." Fenwick cleared his throat. "The point is this. On signing eve, the annex accepts final lodgments from accredited archivists until the tenth bell. After that, the outer doors seal, the inner vault seals, and at noon the whole thing becomes — legally speaking — a stone that cannot be unturned. Ever. Not by the Company. Not by the delegates. Not by God, probably, though He'd need proper documentation."

He pushed the ledgers forward an inch, as if presenting evidence to a court that consisted of one silent man and one drooling child.

"We lodge these. The duplicate ledgers proving the station was ordered cleared rather than quarantined. Sera's sworn account. Your testimony, under your true name, witnessed and countersigned. Adela's corroboration. Everything we have, into the annex, before the tenth bell on signing eve. And then the treaty seals over it like amber over a fly, and no one — *no one* — gets to write those divers out of history. Not Dull, not Vane, not the entire board of the Meridian Trading Company wearing matching coats."

The rain filled the pause. Wren banged the ledger corner against the table twice, as if calling the session to order.

"And the cost," Orsen said at last, quietly.

"Ah." Fenwick laced his fingers together. "Yes. The cost."

There it was, sitting between them like a third guest. Fenwick had done the arithmetic already — he was an archivist; arithmetic was half the trade — but saying it aloud was different from knowing it.

"To lodge documents, I use my Guild credentials. Fine. But a lodgment of this nature — contested materials, adverse to a signatory party — requires the lodging archivist to stand as custodian of record. My name goes on the filing. My registration. My oath. If the Company challenges the annex contents afterward, they challenge *me*." He shrugged, a gesture he hoped looked philosophical and suspected looked like a man shrugging off his own skin. "They can't touch the documents once sealed. But they can certainly touch the archivist."

"They'll arrest him," Orsen said. Not cruelly. Just precisely, the way he said most true things.

"They'll try. Breach of archival privilege, theft of Company property — those ledgers were technically theirs, though I'd argue eleven years of abandonment constitutes gift — conspiracy, sedition if they're feeling poetic. I'll be detained before the bells finish ringing." Fenwick pulled the fourth draft toward him and showed it over. "Which is why the filing must be perfect. Flawless. Airtight beyond even their lawyers' appetite. Because if there is so much as a misfiled page, they'll use it to crack the seal's legitimacy, and then everything we're doing is for nothing."

Orsen studied the draft for a long moment. Then he said, "And me?"

"Ah." Fenwick had been dreading this part slightly less than he'd dreaded telling Sera, slightly more than he'd dreaded telling Maren, and immeasurably more than he'd ever dreaded anything involving punctuation. "Your testimony goes in under your true name. Which means your name enters the record as a witness against the Company. As a bonded man testifying against his bond-holder." He met Orsen's eyes. "It voids your protections. All of them. The indenture becomes — complicated. Litigable. They will come for you the moment they understand what's in the annex, and unlike me, you won't have the Guild's dusty dignity standing between you and them."

He had expected argument. He had prepared arguments back, three of them, ranked by rhetorical strength. Instead Orsen simply nodded, slowly, the way a man nods at a bridge he's decided to cross.

"That was always the price," Orsen said. "I told you. I told Sera. I'm not paying it to be safe. I'm paying it so the record knows who paid."

Fenwick looked down at his papers. Thirty years in archives, and he still hadn't learned what to do with his hands when people said things like that.

"Well," he managed. "Good. That's settled, then. Now for the genuinely difficult part."

"Which is?"

"Convincing Maren."

* * *

Maren Quill arrived two hours later carrying Wren's supper, a fresh goose for roasting, and the expression of a woman who had heard the words *I've had an idea* secondhand and come prepared to be disappointed in men.

She listened to the plan in full — Fenwick had learned not to abbreviate things for Maren; she caught every abbreviation and treated it as contraband — while she fed Wren mashed turnip with the patient efficiency of someone who had kept a hundred infants alive through worse winters than this.

When he finished, she set down the spoon.

"So," she said. "You want to walk into the belly of the beast — the actual stone belly, beneath the actual hall where the men who own this city will be drinking sherry directly above your head — carrying the only irreplaceable evidence in the world, on the one night when Vane will have every hired eye in Greyhaven watching every door. And you want to do this because of a subsection *you wrote*."

"Subsections don't lie," Fenwick said, with more defensiveness than the sentence deserved.

"No. Men do. That's the whole trouble." She wiped Wren's chin. "What's the alternative? Do nothing, and the Company writes its pretty pamphlet history, and those divers stay 'lost to fever' forever, and Wren grows up in a city that forgot how her mother's husband died. That's the alternative?" She shook her head. "No. I didn't think so. I just wanted to hear you say the risks out loud, so you'd have to sit in them a moment."

Fenwick sat in them. They were, he reflected, considerably more comfortable than the ones he'd been sitting in for eleven years.

"There's one more piece," he admitted. "The lodgment must be physically carried to the annex desk on signing eve. But the *preparation* — certification, witnessing, the formal inventory — that should happen beforehand, somewhere defensible. Ideally somewhere the Company doesn't know about." He hesitated. "Ideally somewhere with a lock."

"My barge burned down, Tobias."

"I'm aware. I was there. I still have small blisters I consult about the weather." He spread his hands. "But you also have the counting-room behind Adela's print shop. And you have the one thing no one else in this company of ours has."

"Which is?"

"A talent for making things disappear." He nodded at Wren, who had fallen asleep against Maren's shoulder, one fist still closed around a scrap of oilcloth. "You moved that child through a city full of Vane's watchers without a single soul knowing which door she slept behind. If anyone can get us — and the documents — to Cordwainer Square on signing eve without being seen until the moment we *want* to be seen, it's you."

Maren was quiet for a while. Outside, the rain eased, and somewhere down the lane a cart horse complained about the hour with the sincerity of a professional.

"Maren Quill," Fenwick said, "you are being asked to help commit the most consequential act of record-keeping in the history of this coast. I want that on the table. When they write about this — and Adela will make sure they write about it — your name belongs in the paragraph where it stops being possible to bury the truth."

"Don't flatter me, Fenwick, I'm immune and it makes you smug." But her mouth twitched. She shifted Wren higher on her shoulder and looked at the ledgers, really looked at them, the way she looked at a swaddling cloth to see whose hands had made it.

"All right," she said. "Here's how we do it. We certify everything at Adela's, three days before signing. I carry the documents to the square myself, in the fish boxes, through the morning market — nobody searches a midwife's fish, they know better. You go in through the archivists' entrance with your precious credentials. Orsen—" she paused, and her voice gentled in a way Fenwick had only ever heard her use for the dying and the very young, "—Orsen walks in beside you as named witness, in the open, where everyone can see him doing it. No hiding. That's the point of him."

"Yes," Fenwick said softly. "That's the point of him."

"And if it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong. I wrote the protocol. I've read it four hundred times since Tuesday." He gathered the ledgers, squared their edges, aligned them with the fastidious tenderness he reserved for exactly two categories of object: documents, and children. "Eleven years ago I hid these pages in a crypt because I was a coward with good handwriting. On signing eve, I'm going to put them where no coward and no hero and no Company clerk can ever take them out again. Sealed in ink. Permanent as tide tables."

Maren snorted. "You've been rehearsing that speech in the mirror."

"For three days," Fenwick admitted. "It's better aloud."

* * *

He worked through the night, because the filing had to be perfect and perfection, in his experience, was simply anxiety with good posture. Somewhere past the fourth bell he heard Orsen stir from the chair where he'd fallen asleep sitting up — the man slept like a sentry, in increments, fully dressed — and watched him cross to the cradle Maren had left by the window.

Orsen stood over Wren for a long time. He didn't touch her. He never quite touched her, as if she were a document too fragile to handle and too important to file away.

"She'll grow up," Orsen said quietly, not turning around, "in a world where the record tells the truth about her parents. Whatever else happens. However it costs. That part is already decided."

"Yes," Fenwick said. "I believe it is."

He turned back to the papers, dipped his pen, and began the final inventory — line by line, name by name, every diver the Company had written down as fever and Fenwick was writing back into the world as murdered, each name rendered in his best, clearest, most unambiguous hand.

Outside, far off toward Cordwainer Square, the Commission Hall clock struck five.

Six days to signing eve.

And somewhere across the sleeping city, Fenwick had no doubt whatsoever, a fixer named Vane was also awake — also making lists, also counting names, also preparing, in his own tidy and terrible way, for the same night.

The race, he thought, setting down the pen and reaching for fresh ink, was officially on.
