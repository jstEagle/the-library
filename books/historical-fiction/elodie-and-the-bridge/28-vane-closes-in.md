## 28. Vane Closes In

The knock came at Maren's cousin's back room before dawn, and it was not a knock so much as an announcement. Three blows, evenly spaced, the kind of knocking that had never once been turned away unsatisfied.

Orsen was awake already — he was always awake by then, seven years of warehouse bells having rewired something in him — but he lay still and listened. Wren slept against his ribs, a warm weight the size of a loaf of bread, breathing in that determined way infants breathe, as though sleep were labor they intended to be paid for.

Sera stirred on the cot across the room. Her eyes found his in the dark.

"Company?" she mouthed.

"Maybe a neighbor," he mouthed back, without conviction.

The door opened below. Boots on stairs. Not the hurried boots of a bailiff, not the shuffling boots of a drunk. Measured boots. The kind of boots that arrived because someone had written down an address, checked it twice, and filed it.

* * *

They took Sera in the grey half-light, politely, which was somehow worse than if they'd kicked the door in.

There were four of them, and over them stood Vane, who did not need four men to do anything except perhaps carry his own consequence. He wore a coat the color of wet slate and an expression of mild administrative regret, as though Sera were a clerical error he'd been sent to correct.

"Sera Voss," he said, consulting a paper he clearly did not need to consult. "Charged with theft. A silver candlestick, removed from the Harbormaster's Chapel on the ninth of the month."

"There is no ninth-of-the-month candlestick," Sera said. Her voice was steady. Orsen noticed her hands were steady too, and loved her for it, and hated that she'd had to learn steadiness like this.

"Then you'll have no difficulty at your hearing," Vane said pleasantly. "Hearings are held quickly these days. Very efficient." He folded the paper away. "Of course, witnesses sometimes complicate matters. Witnesses with their own irregularities. Indentures, for instance. Absences from post. Associations."

His gaze moved to Orsen the way a man's gaze moves across a ledger line he intends to return to later.

"Master Coldharbour," Vane said. "We'll speak soon. There's a matter of a missing child I understand you've been helpful about."

"I haven't," Orsen said.

"No," Vane agreed. "You haven't. That's what makes it interesting."

They walked Sera out between them. She looked back once — not at Vane, not at the men, at Wren, still sleeping, mercifully ignorant — and then she was gone into the fog, and the door closed, and the room felt smaller than it had any right to feel.

Maren arrived an hour later with bread and bad news, in that order.

"They didn't search the place," she said, setting the loaf down like a verdict. "Which means they don't need to search places anymore. They know where everything is. They're just choosing what to pick up."

"A candlestick," Orsen said. "They charged her with a *candlestick*."

"Fabricated charges are always small ones," Maren said. "Small charges are easy to prove when you own the proving. Nobody fights a hearing over silver. That's the design of it." She sat down heavily. "She'll be convicted by Friday and transported by the spring tide, and every piece of testimony she swore to us dies with her signature on a confession she'll sign because they'll tell her it's the only way to see daylight again."

Wren woke then and began to cry, small and furious, and Maren gathered her up with the practiced motion of a woman who had caught a thousand falling things.

"She left this baby on your steps to save her life," Maren said over the crying. "Now they're using the baby to unravel everything the mother bled for. You know what they're doing, Orsen. They're not attacking us. They're unwinding us. Thread by thread."

* * *

He went to Fenwick's garret because there was nowhere else to go, and because Fenwick, in his ruined way, was the closest thing Orsen had to a map.

The archivist was sitting among his papers looking like a man who had been embalmed alive and had made his peace with it. He listened without interrupting, which for Fenwick was an act of heroic restraint.

"So," he said at last. "Vane has Sera. Which means Vane has leverage on your testimony chain, your nerve, and your conscience, all in one cell. And you have—" He gestured at the room, the damp, himself. "This."

"I have one thing," Orsen said.

Fenwick raised an eyebrow, or attempted to; years of drink had made his eyebrows independent contractors.

"Dull," Orsen said. "Corvane Dull has been embezzling from the Meridian Company for six years. Cargo that goes onto manifests and never comes off. Short-weighted pearl shipments. Salvage fees paid to a barge that doesn't exist. I've watched him do it since my second year, because watching was all I was allowed to do, and I kept the numbers." He tapped his own temple. "Not on paper. On paper gets found. In here doesn't."

Fenwick went very still, in the manner of a man who has heard the voice of his old religion calling from across a crowded street.

"You've had that," he said slowly, "for *years*. You could have bought your freedom with it. A promotion. Passage anywhere. You could have sold it to Vane the day we met and been drinking rum in Saltmarsh by now."

"Yes," Orsen said.

"Why didn't you?"

Orsen considered the question honestly, because Fenwick deserved honesty even if the answer was ugly. "Because spending it meant becoming a man who spends things like that. And because I kept thinking I might need it more later." He looked out the fogged window. "I suppose this is later."

"It's a dangerous coin," Fenwick said. "Embezzlement knowledge cuts both ways. Dull falls, yes — but the investigation lands on everyone under his supervision. Every manifest you witnessed. Every cargo you signed for. You hand that over and you are no longer invisible, Orsen. You are *involved*."

"I was never invisible," Orsen said. "I was just quiet enough that nobody checked. There's a difference, and I'm done pretending otherwise."

Fenwick studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled — a crooked, rusted thing, like a hinge remembering its purpose.

"You sound like the man who wrote those petitions at the station," he said. "The divers' delegate. Steady fellow. Kept his accounts clean." He reached for his coat. "Well. If we're going to burn your cover, let's do it properly. I haven't drafted a formal disclosure in nine years. I'd hate to be sloppy about it."

"We're not drafting anything yet. First I need to know how Vane receives visitors."

"Oh, I know how Vane receives visitors," Fenwick said grimly. "Through the front door, seated, offered tea, and remembered forever. That's what makes him frightening. He's *courteous*."

* * *

Vane received him, as advertised, through the front door.

The Company's annex office above the counting-house smelled of ink and lamp oil and, faintly, of money being counted somewhere below, a sound like slow rain. Vane poured tea himself. Two cups. He did not ask whether Orsen wanted one; he simply placed it at his elbow, the way one places a fact.

"Master Coldharbour," he said. "Sit. Please." Orsen sat. "I want to begin by saying that I take no pleasure in any of this."

"That must be exhausting," Orsen said, "taking no pleasure in everything."

Something flickered at the corner of Vane's mouth — appreciation, brief and genuine, immediately filed away. "You're wittier than your file suggests. Your file is very thin, incidentally. Seven years and barely a mark against you. Do you know how unusual that is? Men break early here, or they break late, but they break. You've just... endured." He sat down across the desk. "It made me curious. Curiosity is my profession, really. The Company thinks I enforce its rules. Actually I collect its exceptions. And you, Master Coldharbour, are the finest exception on the docks."

"I came about Sera Voss."

"Of course you did." Vane steepled his fingers. "A tragic woman. Disfigured, addicted, hunted by creditors of her own invention. The charge against her is trivial — a candlestick, honestly, the chapel won't miss it — but trivial charges have teeth when the accused has enemies willing to testify. And she has so many enemies, Orsen. Some of them quite high up. Some of them, I suspect, are people she trusted." He let that hang. "Her hearing is Thursday. Conviction is a formality. Transport follows within the month. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless certain testimony becomes unavailable to certain delegates. Mrs. Voss's sworn account, for instance. Statements she gave to parties unknown. If those documents were returned to me — all of them, and the names of everyone who holds copies — I could find the energy to look at her case again. Courts are strange instruments. Sometimes the truth of a charge depends entirely on who's holding the lantern." He smiled thinly. "You'd be saving her, Orsen. Isn't that what you do? Save people?"

Orsen looked at his tea. He thought about his father, who had signed a Company contract believing the word *volunteer* meant something. He thought about Wren's weight against his ribs that morning, and Sera's steady hands, and the memorial wall with its lie carved in respectable stone.

And he understood, finally, completely, that Vane was not offering him a bargain. He was offering him a mirror — a chance to become the man who saved one woman by burying forty, and call it mercy.

"No," Orsen said.

Vane sighed, genuinely regretful. "Thursday, then."

"Before Thursday," Orsen said, "you should hear what I came to say. Because I didn't come to negotiate for Sera Voss. I came to make a report."

Vane's eyebrows rose a fraction. "A report?"

"About Corvane Dull." Orsen set down the cup, carefully, so it would not rattle, because his hands wanted very much to rattle. "Six years of embezzlement. Pearl shipments short-weighted at intake. Salvage fees drawn monthly against the barge *Kindly Fortune*, which I can assure you does not exist, because I helped unload its phantom cargoes for three years and never once fell in the water. I can give you dates, amounts, and the names of two harbormasters who took a cut. It will survive any audit you care to run. It will ruin him. And it will land on every subordinate who signed beneath him." He met Vane's eyes. "Including me."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the counting continued, patient as tide.

"You understand what you're doing," Vane said quietly. "You're not selling Dull. You're buying a cell. An internal inquiry touches everyone in his shadow. Your indenture protections evaporate the moment you're named in it. You'd be handing me the instrument of your own destruction and asking nothing in return except—"

"Sera Voss walks free Thursday. Charges withdrawn, not lost at hearing — *withdrawn*, with an apology if your office can manage one. And the inquiry into Dull proceeds regardless of what happens to anyone else's testimony." Orsen stood. His legs felt like borrowed things. "That's the price. Take it or spend the rest of the week wondering why a warehouse drudge knows your supervisor's books better than your auditors do."

Vane regarded him for a long time. When he spoke again, the courtesy was gone, and what remained was almost respectful.

"Do you know what the saddest part is? I would have given you passage abroad. Money. A new name. All you had to do was bring me a child." He shook his head slowly. "Instead you've chosen the hard road, and you've chosen it *in writing*, effectively, the moment I open that inquiry. There's no walking back to the warehouse after this, Coldharbour. No quiet corner to stand in. You'll be a named man in a Company war by nightfall."

"I know."

"And the girl? The child? Once you're exposed, we find her. You know that too."

"You'll try," Orsen said. "But you'll be busy. Audits are *hungry* work."

He walked out through the front door, down the stairs, past the counting-room where the money sounded like rain, and out into the street, where the fog had begun to lift and Greyhaven looked, for the first time in seven years, exactly as exposed as he felt.

* * *

Maren listened to the whole account without moving, Wren asleep against her shoulder. When he finished, she was quiet long enough that he began composing apologies.

"Well," she said at last. "You've gone and made yourself a named man."

"It seems so."

"Burned your cover inside the Company. Spent six years of saved leverage in one afternoon. For a woman who isn't even—" She stopped herself, but the shape of the sentence was visible: *who isn't even blood.*

"For a witness," Orsen said. "For a mother. For a thread they were trying to pull loose." He exhaled. "Also, technically, I may have committed career suicide, so if you know anyone hiring disgraced dockhands, I'm available Thursday."

Maren laughed — a short, startled bark, quickly smothered, but real. Then she crossed the room and did something she almost never did: she put her free arm around him, awkwardly, the hug of a woman whose arms were built for cargo and infants and had to improvise for grown men.

"You idiot," she said warmly. "Brave idiot. Fenwick owes me ten crowns. He bet you'd stall Vane another week."

"He *bet* on me?"

"We all bet on you. It's how we cope." She pulled back and looked at him, and beneath the humor her eyes were grave. "You understand what tonight is, though. There's no tomorrow where you're careful again. From here it's forward, fast, all of it. The ledgers, the statements, Adela's page, Ostrava's door. Everything, now, while Vane's buried in his audit."

"I know," Orsen said.

Above them, on the shelf, Wren slept on, breathing her small determined breaths, unaware that the world had just been rearranged around her — that a quiet man had spent his last coin of silence, and that the only direction left was straight through the record itself, with signing day five days off and every enemy in Greyhaven now awake.

Somewhere across the city, Orsen knew, an inquiry was being opened, and Corvane Dull's smile was cracking, and Vane was sharpening whatever came next.

Five days. He touched the doorframe on his way out, the way his father used to touch the boat's mast for luck, and stepped into the evening to spend what remained of his anonymity.
