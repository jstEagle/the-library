## 29. The Long Night Before Signing

### I. Maren

The barge was gone, but the river was still Maren Quill's oldest friend, and friends did not abandon you just because someone had burned your house down.

She stood at the coal wharf an hour past midnight with Wren asleep against her shoulder, wrapped in the good wool — the one without pearl-callus in the weave, the plain one, the forgettable one. A fishing smack bobbed at the end of the pier, its owner a gap-toothed woman named Hesta who owed Maren three births' worth of favors and was finally being asked to spend them.

"He'll want coin," Hesta said, jerking her chin at the sleeping bundle.

"He'll get my thanks and a lifetime of free midwifery, which is worth more than coin and he knows it, because his wife is due in spring and I'm the only one on this river who won't charge him for the screaming."

Hesta grinned. "Fair."

Wren stirred. Two years old now, or near enough — nobody knew her true birthday, so they'd chosen one, midwinter, the night of the naming, when Fenwick had drunk two glasses of sherry and wept openly about decimal systems. She woke the way she always did, slowly, one eye first, like a small suspicious owl.

"Where we going?" she mumbled.

"On a boat adventure."

"Boats sink."

Maren laughed despite everything, despite the barge being blackened timber at the bottom of the harbor, despite the fixer Vane's men having been seen twice today near the fish market, despite the whole held-breath weight of the city on signing eve. "Where do you learn these things?"

"Fenwick."

Of course. The archivist had been educating the child in maritime disasters as bedtime reading, on the theory that truth was comfort, which said everything about Fenwick and nothing about children.

Maren lifted Wren's chin. "Listen to me, little wren. Nothing bad is going to happen, and if it does, I will be standing between you and it the entire time. That's the whole job. That's what I'm for."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "And snacks?"

"And snacks."

"Then all right."

They went down the gangplank into the smell of tar and herring, and Maren did not look back at Greyhaven's lights, because looking back was for people who intended to return, and she had not decided yet whether she was that kind of person. She had packed for a week. She had also, quietly, packed for a year. Somewhere behind her, in the archive annex above the treaty hall, Orsen Coldharbour was putting on a clean shirt and walking toward the thing he'd been walking toward for seven years.

*Hold your breath, love,* she thought. *But only until dawn.*

### II. Adela

The presses of *The Greyhaven Courier* never slept, but tonight they hummed like a hive that knew a storm was coming.

Adela Brine stood over the composing stone where the front page lay in trays of cold type, and she read it for the ninth time:

**ACCORD SIGNED AT DAWN — AND WHAT THE ACCORD WILL NOT SAY**

Below the fold: a summary of the sealed annex filing, hedged in enough lawyer's language to make a contract blush — *documents lodged pursuant to archival statute, contents unverified by this newspaper, allegations denied in full by the Meridian Trading Company* — and beneath that, in type half a size larger than strictly necessary, the sentence she'd fought the editor for three hours to keep:

*A named witness has sworn testimony before delegates of the neutral powers.*

A named witness. Not anonymous sources, not whispered corroboration, not the usual fog of journalism. A man who had walked into the Company warehouse seven years ago as a number and would walk into the archive tonight as Orsen Coldharbour, and God help him, and God help the rest of them.

Her editor, Pell, came up beside her, spectacles pushed into his hair. "If the Company sues, they take the press. They take the building."

"If they sue, they have to say out loud, in court, what they're suing about. Which is the massacre. Which puts it on a second public record for free." She tapped the tray. "I've read their pamphlet, Pell. 'A Tragic Fever, Bravely Endured.' Eleven pages and not one diver's name. You know what that tells me?"

"That they're cowards?"

"That they can count. Names are dangerous. Names are the one thing you can't burn halfway."

Pell looked at the page a long moment. "And if the filing fails? If Vane gets there first?"

Adela pulled the spare copy of the front page from her bag — printed hours ago, folded against her ribs, warm as a heartbeat. "Then tomorrow's paper runs anyway, and it says *a witness attempted to testify*, and every delegate at that table spends the rest of their life knowing what was done to stop him. There are two ways to win tomorrow. We only need one of them."

She set the folded page carefully back against her ribs, where it had been keeping company with her heart all evening, and went to check the ink supply. It was going to be a long night, and she intended to be ready to print the moment anyone — anyone at all — sent word.

### III. Fenwick

Tobias Fenwick had spent the last twelve hours doing the two things he did best and the one thing he did worst.

Best: preparation. The duplicate ledgers lay in a lawyer's document case, wrapped in oilcloth (a bitter joke between him and the universe), each page flagged, each flag annotated, each annotation cross-referenced to the sworn statements — Sera Voss's trembling hand, Adela's transcription, Maren's list of the buried, and last, in a folder by itself, the deposition of one Orsen Coldharbour, indenture number 4-117, written out in the surprisingly careful hand of a man who had taught himself letters in a warehouse loft because no one else was going to teach him anything.

Worst: waiting.

He checked the case's buckles for the eleventh time. He checked the clock. Half past two. Signing at ten. Annex access granted — through a combination of Fenwick's restored credentials, Madame Ostrava's quiet intervention, and an archival statute so obscure that Fenwick had practically proposed marriage to it — from six o'clock, four hours before the delegates convened overhead in the treaty hall.

Sealed once filed. Permanent once sealed. No Company clerk, no fixer, no force short of an act of the assembled powers could open it afterward. That was the whole plan, the entire audacious architecture of it: walk in at six, file the papers, turn the seal, and let the machine of procedure — the same machine that had ground them down for months — grind in their favor for once.

Fenwick poured himself the last finger of sherry from the bottle Maren had left, then looked at it, and poured it back. Not tonight. Tonight he wanted his hands steady and his head unburned.

"You burned one ledger," he said aloud to the empty room, the old confession rehearsed one final time. "You will not fumble the other."

A knock at the door. Three slow, deliberate raps — theirs.

Orsen came in smelling of rain and lamp oil, dressed in the only good coat any of them had ever seen him wear, dark wool, slightly too large, sleeves let out by Maren's needle. Under it he wore the indenture collar-mark, the brand on his forearm he'd hidden for seven years beneath rolled cuffs. Tonight the cuffs were down. Tonight it didn't matter. By noon he would either be free or he would be something worse, and either way the arm would be bare when he swore the oath.

"You look terrible," Orsen said.

"I look dignified. You look like a scarecrow that's come into money."

"The coat was four silver."

"Robbery. Whose?"

"A dead man's. His widow said he'd have wanted it used."

They regarded each other across the little room, two men who had been, in turns, employerless and hopeless and furious and afraid together for half a year, and Fenwick found that his eyes were stinging, which was unacceptable, so he busied himself with the buckles again.

"I've arranged the sequence," he said. "Ledgers first, then statements, then the annex declaration. Once the seal turns, I signal Adela. She prints. Whatever happens after that happens in daylight, in front of witnesses."

"And if Vane's already there at six?"

"Then you tell your testimony to the delegates directly, upstairs, in front of the neutral powers, and dare them to call it disorderly." Fenwick closed the case and held it out. "Orsen. Seven years you kept silent to survive. Tomorrow morning you could stay silent one more day and keep surviving. You know that."

"I know it."

"And you're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway."

Fenwick nodded, once, the way a man nods at a sum that has finally come out right. "Then let's go make the record honest. It's been lying to everyone for far too long, and frankly it's embarrassing to the profession."

### IV. Orsen

The streets between Fenwick's garret and the treaty hall were empty the way Greyhaven was never truly empty — the emptiness of held breath, of shutters closed on listening ears. Rain had stopped an hour before dawn and left the cobbles shining like the inside of a shell. Somewhere a gull complained about the weather, the war, and everything else.

Orsen counted exits the way other men counted sheep. Treaty hall: main doors east, service alley west, the annex below reached by the archivists' stair. Two constables at the east doors, bored. One man in a gray coat at the west alley who was trying very hard to look like a lamplighter and owned no ladder. Vane's people, then, watching the wrong door, or the right door, depending on how the next four hours went.

At the corner of Saltrow he stopped, as he had promised himself he would, and looked down the long slope of the street to the harbor, where the memorial wall stood gray against the graying water. His father's name was on it somewhere, in the Company's inscription, under the words *volunteer labor*. He didn't go down. There wasn't time, and there didn't need to be. He carried the wall with him; he had for thirty years.

*Dad,* he thought. *I'm about to do the loud version of you.*

He almost smiled. Maren would have called it gallows humor; Fenwick would have corrected its grammar; Wren would have asked why the gull was cross and accepted no answer less thorough than a lecture.

Four o'clock. The annex stair. The case in his hand, the oath in his throat, the name — his real one, his whole one, the one nobody had called him since he was nine years old — resting on his tongue like a stone he had finally decided to set down.

Beside the archivists' door, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the buttress.

Gray coat. No ladder. No lantern.

"Mr. Coldharbour," said Vane, pleasantly, the way frost is pleasant to a drowning man. "What a coincidence. I was just hoping someone from the Meridian family might be attending the filing this morning."

Behind the door, Fenwick's boots were already on the stairs. Above, the treaty hall windows showed candlelight. Below, the harbor turned over in its sleep.

Orsen shifted the document case to his left hand, and did not answer — for the first time in seven years, because silence was no longer the safest thing he owned.

It was simply the beginning of what he had to say.
