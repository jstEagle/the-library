## 33. Sentences

They read the verdicts on a Tuesday, because the law apparently believed in saving its cruelties for days when the fish market was open and the whole of Greyhaven could smell brine and blood together and not know which was which.

I learned this from a turnkey with a kind face and cold hands, who brought me bread that morning and said, "They'll want you washed. The delegates like their prisoners presentable." He said it the way you'd tell a man his horse had died — gently, as if presentation were the tragedy.

"Presentable," I repeated.

"It's a hearing, not an execution." He paused at the door. "Mostly."

* * *

The courtroom had been the Meridian Company's counting hall once, back before the armistice made it government property. I knew it well. I had stood in it as a boy of eleven, signing my indenture papers with a hand that didn't yet know how to hold a pen properly, while a clerk guided my wrist the way you'd guide a child learning to write his own name — except the name he was teaching me to write belonged to the Company now, and so did everything else about me.

Seven years later they marched me into the same room in chains, and I confess my first thought was not noble. It was: *the floorboards haven't even been replaced.* The same warped plank near the third window that used to catch Dull's boot heel every morning. The same water stain on the ceiling shaped like a drowning man, which I had stared at through a hundred inventory days and privately named Aldous.

Aldous was still there. It seemed right that something should outlast all of us unchanged.

The room was full. That surprised me. I had expected officials, maybe Adela in the gallery taking notes she'd never be allowed to print uncensored. Instead: dockworkers who had no business being there, standing along the walls because every seat was taken. Women from Maren's parish of barges and tide-tables. A cluster of pearl divers' kin wearing mourning armbands that had gone gray with age but hadn't been put away. And near the front, unmistakable even with her hair pinned up and her good coat brushed, Maren Quill, sitting with Wren on her lap as though the child were a hymnbook and the courtroom a chapel.

Wren saw me before anyone could stop her. She stood up on Maren's knee, held out both arms, and announced to the entire bench of magistrates:

"Papa!"

The word went through the room like a dropped glass. Somewhere in the gallery a woman began to cry without any attempt at discretion. One of the magistrates coughed into his fist. And I — I stood there in chains in front of the assembled law of three nations and felt my face do something it had not done since my father drowned: it smiled, helplessly, in front of everyone.

Maren hauled Wren down, whispering furiously, but the damage was done. The record would show that at the opening of proceedings, the accused was greeted by an infant as *Papa*, and no amount of legal language could un-ring that particular bell.

"Order," said the chief magistrate, without conviction.

* * *

Fenwick went first.

He looked terrible, which for Fenwick meant he had shaved. His coat hung off him like a borrowed opinion. When they read the charges — theft of Company property, breach of archival covenant, conspiracy — he stood very straight, the way men stand when they have decided in advance exactly how much dignity they can afford and are spending none of it elsewhere.

Then the delegate's counsel rose — Madame Ostrava's people, neutral, precise — and said the words that changed the shape of the room:

"The annexed documents, sealed under Article Fourteen, have been reviewed by the arbitration panel. Their provenance is confirmed. Tobias Fenwick is granted full pardon as a witness for the treaty record, with restoration of his archivist credentials."

Restoration of credentials. I watched Fenwick hear those words. He didn't weep — Fenwick would sooner weep over a misfiled folio than over himself — but his hand went to his breast pocket, where for thirty years he had kept his credential seal, until the day the Company took it, and where he had kept nothing since. His fingers found the empty pocket and pressed flat against it, and I understood that some pardons are given back to you in places you thought had healed.

"Thank you," he said to the bench, in the voice of a man thanking a librarian. Then, unable to help himself: "Although I should note for the record that the Article Fourteen sealing mechanism has a design flaw in its third-stage lock, which I documented in a memorandum in—"

"Yes," said the chief magistrate. "Thank you, Mr. Fenwick."

"They can't just leave a flaw like that. Anyone with access to the sub-archive could—"

"*Mr. Fenwick.*"

They escorted him out still talking. It was the happiest I had ever seen him.

* * *

Sera's charges were dropped next, and it took less time than reading a menu.

Fabricated theft, Vane's masterwork, dissolved the moment the sealed annex made it inconvenient. The magistrate read the dismissal in a bored voice, as if dropping charges against a woman the Company had hunted across two winters were a clerical matter, like correcting a misspelled name. Sera stood in her plain dress with her scarred cheek turned to the room — she did not hide it; she had told me once that hiding it was how they won — and when the dismissal came she simply nodded, once, the way you nod at a tide coming in. Acknowledged. Not thanked. Tides don't require thanks.

But as they led her back past the gallery, she stopped beside me. The guards let her, because what harm could one more moment do.

"You gave them Dull's books," she said quietly. "To buy me out of that cell."

"I had leverage. Leverage rots. Better spent."

"That's not why men spend things." Her eyes were steady. "That's why fathers do."

She touched my chained wrist — the lightest touch, barely a fingertip, the touch of a woman who had learned that everything can be taken except what you give away first — and then she was gone down the side door, free, into a Greyhaven that owed her an apology it would never speak aloud.

* * *

Then it was my turn, and the room got quiet in the way rooms do when everyone knows the arithmetic will not come out kind.

The charges were read in full, and I will grant the prosecution this: they were accurate. Breach of indenture. Theft of Company property — the ledgers, technically Fenwick's duplicates, but the Company had never met a document it didn't claim. Aiding unauthorized disclosure to a foreign arbitration body. Conspiring to compromise the Greyhaven Accord.

Counsel for the Meridian Company — a sleek man named Pellworth whose voice sounded like invoices being shuffled — argued for the maximum. "The prisoner was entrusted," he said, "with the ordinary machinery of commerce, and he repaid that trust by—" and here he actually used the phrase "—weaponizing paper."

I rather liked that. *Weaponizing paper.* If I had a coat of arms, I'd want it on the crest.

Ostrava's counsel spoke briefly. She did not ask for mercy, exactly. She asked for proportion. "The prisoner's testimony entered the permanent record of this armistice. Thirty-one names of the dead are now acknowledged fact because of him. The panel weighs that." A pause. "The panel also notes the prisoner was indentured at age eleven, which under the new maritime conventions constitutes grounds for voiding the original contract entirely."

Voiding. My ears caught the word and held it up to the light like a coin I wasn't sure was real.

The chief magistrate removed his spectacles, which I had learned during the trial was the judicial equivalent of clearing one's throat before bad news.

"Orsen Coldharbour," he said. "Your indenture to the Meridian Trading Company is declared void ab initio. You are, as of this hour, a free man."

The gallery made a sound — half breath, half cheer — and Wren clapped her hands twice, decisively, as though she had personally arranged all of it.

"However." The spectacles went back on. "The court cannot ignore the manner of your freedom. You obtained it through acts the law defines as crimes, whatever the arbitration panel may think of their results. The sentence of this court is three years' penal labor, to be served at Kestrel Point quarry."

Kestrel Point. Everyone in Greyhaven knew Kestrel Point the way sailors know a reef — by reputation, and by the ships that didn't come back. It was north, past the salt marshes, a granite pit on a gray coast where the wind came off the sea with nothing to slow it down. Three years. I did the arithmetic the way I'd once done cargo manifests: three years was one thousand and ninety-five days, give or take leap years, and Wren would be four when I went in and seven when I came out.

Seven. She'd have lost most of her baby words by then. She'd have new ones I'd never heard.

"Prisoner," said the magistrate. "Do you understand your sentence?"

I understood something larger, actually, and perhaps I should have kept it behind my teeth, but seven years of silence had left me with a surplus.

"I understand," I said, "that the Company killed thirty-one divers and paid no penalty at all. And I understand that I helped prove it, and I'm going to a quarry for three years. So yes. I understand the sentence perfectly."

The gavel came down hard enough to wake Aldous the water stain. But in the gallery, the dockworkers did not boo or cheer. They did something worse, for the Company's comfort: they took off their caps. All along the wall, one after another, bare heads bowed — not to the bench, but to me, or maybe through me to the thirty-one names now fixed in ink forever. Pellworth wrote something furiously in his notebook, presumably a complaint about the decorum of hats.

As they turned me toward the door, Maren stood, lifted Wren high on her hip, and called out — loud, clear, in the voice of a woman who had delivered half the harbor's babies and feared no magistrate alive:

"We'll be at the gate, love! All of us! Count on it!"

"Count on it!" echoed Wren, who could count to three and considered this sufficient authority for anything.

The guards hurried me out. I went willingly. A man walking toward three years of granite walks lighter when he knows exactly what's waiting on the other end of them.

* * *

They gave me four days before transport. Some clerk's error, probably — the paperwork for Kestrel Point was, I gathered, filed under the wrong month, an irony so pure that Fenwick, when he heard of it, laughed until he had to sit down on the prison steps.

Those four days, they let my visitors come. There were many. I will not catalog them all; some conversations belong to the people who had them. But on the last evening, the cell filled past capacity: Fenwick with a parcel, Maren with a basket, Adela Brine with a notebook she pretended she wasn't writing in, Sera standing by the door where she always stood, and Wren on the cot beside me, solemnly inspecting my chains and declaring them "too many."

"Agreed," I said.

Fenwick opened the parcel. Inside was a book — a real one, bound in gray cloth, titled *A Concordance of the Greyhaven Annex: Documents, Provenance, and Testimony.* He had written it in four days. Of course he had.

"Page forty-one," he said gruffly. "Read page forty-one."

Page forty-one listed the thirty-one names of the dead. At the bottom, in smaller type, was a note: *"Testimony of Orsen Coldharbour, given under his true name, recorded and sealed, Article Fourteen. This testimony cannot be expunged."*

"You're in the book," Fenwick said. "You're in the permanent record. Whatever they do to your body for three years, they cannot do anything to that page. I've checked the law six times. I've checked it nine times. It's very clear."

"He checked it eleven times," said Adela, not looking up from her notebook.

"The eleventh was for certainty."

Maren unpacked the basket: bread, cheese, a jar of pickled onions ("for morale"), and a small knitted cap, blue, sized for a head that would be too big for it in a year. "For the road," she said. "It's not for you, obviously, your head's the wrong size. It's for keeping. That's different."

"It's for keeping," I agreed, and put it in my breast pocket, over my heart, where Fenwick's seal used to live and where, I supposed, everyone in our strange family eventually stored the things that mattered.

Sera spoke last, from the doorway. "Three years is nothing," she said. "I waited two years in a laudanum fog thinking I was dead already. You'll be doing honest work under an open sky with your name in a book. That's not a sentence, Orsen. That's a delay."

"A delay," I said, tasting the word. Seven years I had survived by treating my life as a delay between silences. She handed the word back to me remade.

* * *

On the morning of the fifth day they marched me to the transport cart at the north gate. Fog, naturally. Greyhaven does not permit departures in clear weather; it would be poor manners.

The guards were decent enough, one of them whistling. The cart was uncomfortable. The granite coast lay three days north. None of it mattered, because at the gate, in defiance of every regulation and several laws of physics, stood my family.

Fenwick in his restored archivist's coat, holding the concordance like scripture. Adela with ink on her fingers and a promise to write ("censored excerpts only, don't thank me"). Sera with her scarred cheek to the wind, unhidden. Maren with a basket that absolutely contained contraband pickled onions. And Wren, three years old and eternal, who broke from Maren's grip, ran the last ten yards despite everyone shouting, and wrapped both arms around my chained leg.

"Papa goes to rocks," she informed the guards, with grave disapproval.

"Papa goes to rocks," I agreed, and picked her up, chains and all, and held her for as long as the guards allowed, which was longer than regulations permitted, because even turnkeys have hearts, especially the ones with cold hands.

"Letters," she demanded.

"Every week."

"Long ones."

"As long as they let me."

"And then you come home?"

And there it was — the question underneath all questions, asked by someone too young to know that home is not a place you return to but a thing people refuse to stop building. I looked past her at the barge-woman, the archivist, the widow, the journalist. At the fog-bound city that had tried to make me forget a face and failed.

"Then I come home," I said.

The cart rolled north. Behind us, Wren waved until the fog took her, and ahead of us the road ran gray and long toward Kestrel Point — and somewhere past it, already waiting, a woman on a barge was making plans that had nothing to do with distance.
