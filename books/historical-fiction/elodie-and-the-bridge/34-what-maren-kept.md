## 34. What Maren Kept

The letter came on a Tuesday, because bad news and good news alike prefer Tuesdays in Greyhaven — the day the mail boat ran, the day the fish was cheapest, the day nothing worth celebrating ever happened until it happened anyway.

I knew before I broke the seal. Adela Brine wrote her envelopes the way she wrote her headlines: tight, economical, with a certain deliberate pressure in the pen that meant *sit down before you read this*. I sat down on the step of the Saltrow cottage with Wren in my lap and the wind coming off the water the way it always came off the water at Kestrel Point, sideways and full of salt and gossip.

*The hearing went the way these things go,* Adela had written. *Which is to say: badly for everyone except the truth.*

Then she told me what the truth had cost.

Fenwick pardoned. State's witness, restored name, a stipend from a government embarrassed enough to be generous with someone else's money. I laughed when I read that part, a short ugly sound that made Wren look up from the button she was trying to swallow — I fished it out of her fist by long habit — because Tobias Fenwick had spent eleven years paying for one burned ledger and now the state was going to pay *him*, as if coin could settle accounts between men and their own consciences. He'd take the money. He'd hate taking it. He'd write three letters about how he didn't deserve it and then buy something absurd and useful, like a magnifying lamp, and pretend it was for practical reasons.

Sera's charges dropped. That sentence was two words long and took me an hour to get through, because my eyes kept stopping at it and going back. *Dropped.* Like a stone off a dock. Like a weight she'd been carrying since the purge, since the fire, since the night she wrapped her daughter in oilskin and handed her to the storm and told the storm: forget this face. Dropped, just like that, by men in good coats who had never once been wet.

And then the last part. The part I'd known was coming since the day I pulled a stranger's baby out of the rain and felt my whole careful life tilt on its axis.

Orsen Coldharbour. Indenture voided — which meant nothing, voided the way you void a debt by burning the debtor — and in its place, penal labor. Three years. Kestrel Point Quarry. The paper called it *lenient*. Adela had underlined the word twice and written in the margin, in her terrible cramped hand: *Lenient means they're afraid of what he'd say if they weren't.*

Wren patted my arm with the button hand. "Sad?" she asked. She was two years old and had exactly four words for feelings — sad, mad, bed, and more — and used them all with the confidence of a physician.

"No, little bird," I said, which was true in the way most things are true, partially and from certain angles. "Not sad. Just counting."

Counting was what I did. Forty years a midwife, forty years of counting fingers and toes and heartbeats and breaths, of knowing exactly how many of anything there were and how many there ought to be. I counted the years — three, less time than Wren had been alive, less time than some of my barges had gone without dry-rotting. I counted the miles — sixty-one from Kestrel Point to Greyhaven by sea, forty-some by the coast road if the ferry at Marsh Bend was running, and the ferry was always running because the ferryman owed me for two babies and a broken arm set crooked and then straight.

And I counted the alternatives, which was the arithmetic that actually mattered.

Because there was a packet boat. There had been a packet boat waiting in the harbor for three weeks, running passengers to the Southern Isles, and Bertram Hollis — who had smuggled everything from brandy to birth control past every customs man on the coast and considered me the only honest person he knew — had come up the cliff path in his flat-soled boots specifically to tell me about it.

"Room for four," he'd said, meaning me, Wren, Sera, and however much luggage grief requires. "Room for a goat if you want a goat. South Isles, warm water, no Company, no quarry, no fog. You could open a proper practice. Women out there still have babies, last I checked, and they pay in things you can eat."

"And you'd carry us out of the goodness of your black heart," I said.

"I'd carry you for the pleasure of cheating the Meridian Company of four productive citizens." He turned his cup in his hands. Bertram always turned his cup when he was serious, which he hated being. "Maren. They took his freedom. They nearly took the ledgers, the widow, the lot of you. Take the exit while it's offered. Nobody would think worse of you."

That was the trouble with Bertram. He was right about everything except the thing that mattered.

* *

I thought about it for nine days, which for me is roughly the deliberation of a mayfly, and I thought about it mostly at night, sitting in the cottage Sera had found — two rooms above a net-mender's shed, rent paid in delivered babies and mended bones, which is the only currency I've ever fully trusted.

Sera was sleeping again. Not well — women who have been hunted don't sleep well, they sleep like soldiers, in shifts, with one ear always cocked toward the door — but she was sleeping, and that was new. In Greyhaven she had lived in that laudanum den like a crab in a cracked shell, half-poisoned, all the way dead behind the eyes, keeping herself alive out of spite and laudanum in equal measures. Here, with salt air instead of opium smoke, with Wren's bed against the wall two feet from hers, something in her had begun to knit. Slowly. The scars on her face and throat were old and cruel, but I've caught babies out of worse hands than hers, and I know healing when I see it working.

One night I found her awake at the window, mending a hem by moonlight because her hands couldn't be empty after dark. I sat with her. Neither of us said anything, which between the two of us has never once meant silence.

"The packet boat leaves Thursday," she said finally.

"I know."

"You should be on it." Her needle didn't stop. "You and Wren both. Somewhere the Company can't reach. Somewhere she can grow up without ever learning what an indenture paper looks like."

"She'll learn what it looks like," I said. "From inside a story. There's a difference between teaching a child about wolves and raising her where the wolves write the schoolbooks."

Sera looked at me over the mending. Her ruined cheek caught the moonlight; the good eye did all the expression for the pair of them, and it was doing plenty. "You're not going."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. You've got your counting face on."

"I don't have a counting face."

"Maren. You have four faces and I've named them all."

I took the mending away from her — a trick, because she'd let me rather than argue — and held it hostage. "Here's what I keep coming back to," I said. "Sixty-one miles. That's all the distance is. Sixty-one miles of water between this window and that quarry. And Bertram wants to sail me eight hundred miles south so I can be *further*?"

"That's not reasoning, that's stubbornness doing sums."

"It's both, and it's kept me alive." I looked out at the water, black and patient. "Every week, Sera, someone's going to walk out of that quarry gate or up to that gatehouse with a bundle of letters, and someone's going to bring back answers. Every week, Wren gets to give him something to hold. If we're in the Southern Isles, he gets ink and memory. If we're here, he gets *her* — growing, changing, getting new words and new teeth and new opinions about eels. Sixty-one miles versus eight hundred. I've delivered enough babies to know the shorter road when I see it."

Sera was quiet a long moment. Then she took her mending back out of my lap, which was her answer, and said, "He'll hate that you're staying somewhere cold on his account."

"He'll hate it beautifully. It'll do him good."

* *

So I told Bertram no, and he took it the way he took all refusals of his plans, which was to immediately begin profiting from them. Within the month he'd adjusted his run — "purely commercial reasons, nothing sentimental, don't look at me like that, woman" — so that his barge touched at the Kestrel Point landing every second Friday, carrying goods nobody needed to a coast nobody wanted, and carrying mail everybody needed, and charging the Company's quarry commissary scandalous prices for pepper, thereby achieving revenge, commerce, and kindness in a single business model, which I consider the finest moral achievement of his career.

We moved closer. That's what I did with the nine days of thinking, and then with the weeks after: I moved us closer, the way water moves toward the level it believes in.

There was a cottage at Halfway Cove — the name alone decided me, though the price finished the job, because the landlady was deaf in one ear, soft in the heart, and delighted to have a midwife within shouting distance of her daughter-in-law's third pregnancy. Halfway Cove sat eleven miles from the quarry gate by the cliff road. Eleven miles. I have walked farther to deliver a baby who refused to wait for the doctor, and I have walked it in worse humor.

Wren thrived the way children thrive anywhere, which is to say ferociously and without consulting anyone. She learned the names of tides before she learned the names of cousins. She developed a passion for gulls that bordered on the theological. She acquired, from the net-mender's boy, a vocabulary that made me glad her mother was out of earshot, and from Sera, a patience with quiet that no other two-year-old on the coast possessed. And every second Friday she stood on the shingle with her fists curled around whatever she'd chosen to send — a drawing, mostly of circles; a white stone; once, memorably, a dried starfish that must have alarmed the quarry censors considerably — and she'd make me lift her up to watch Bertram's barge shrink southward with her love lashed to the mast.

"What's he building?" she'd ask, about the quarry, about Orsen, about everything distant.

"Walls," I said. "Good ones, from what they tell me. He's the best wall-builder they've got."

"Why?"

"Because he knows what walls are for," I said, "and most of them don't."

She accepted this the way children accept the truths of adults, which is to file them away and produce them later at devastating moments. She produced this one at supper, apropos of turnips, announcing that walls were for keeping weather out and love in, and Sera had to leave the table abruptly to attend to something in the other room, and I had to attend very hard to my turnips.

* *

The others came and went like weather, which is what found family does when geography tries to argue with it.

Adela came down on the mail coach twice that autumn, ostensibly to cover "coastal labor conditions," actually to sit at my table and fill notebooks and eat an amount of stew that should have been physiologically impossible for a woman her size. She brought newspapers. She brought the official history pamphlet the Company had published — *Greyhaven and the Islands: A Record of Industry and Sacrifice* — and she and Sera read it aloud to each other in increasingly theatrical voices until Sera got to the page about the pearling station and stopped theatricalizing and just read it quietly, all the way through, the way you'd read the name of someone you buried. Then Adela folded the pamphlet into quarters and said, "Watch what the next edition doesn't say," and went back to her stew, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

Fenwick came at midwinter, when the coach lines thinned and a pardoned man's movements raised fewer eyebrows. He arrived carrying — I am not inventing this — a filing cabinet. Carried it. Two miles from the coach stop, on his back, down the cliff path, in December, because he "couldn't trust the carrier with the drawers." Inside the cabinet: copies of everything. Every ledger page, every sworn statement, every scrap of the annex record, transcribed in his own hand in three separate inks because "archival redundancy, Maren, is not paranoia, it is *professional ethics*."

He stayed a week. He taught Wren to sort buttons by size and declared her aptitude "promising, though her taxonomy lacks rigor." He fixed the cottage roof because he could not sit idle, badly, with instructions consulted at every stage, and the roof has leaked gently ever since, and neither Sera nor I have mentioned it, because some gifts shouldn't be audited. And on his last night, sitting by the fire with his tea going cold the way Fenwick's tea always does, he said, without preamble:

"He asked me to tell you something. At the hearing. Before they took him north. He wouldn't waste a letter on it, he said, letters are for Wren."

"Well?"

"He said—" Fenwick frowned at his tea, editing. "He said, *tell her I'm glad she counts everything.* Something like that. It sounded better when a dying archivist says it, obviously. He wasn't dying. But the principle holds."

I made some noise about sentimental men and poured him another cup, and neither of us looked at the other for a while, and the fire did its work on the silence.

Sera never left. That was the thing I hadn't planned and shouldn't have been surprised by. I'd expected her to move on eventually — south, inland, anywhere the mirrors didn't follow her. Instead she took over the schooling of Wren with terrifying competence, learned the net-mender's trade in a fortnight out of pure spite toward her own hands' tremor, and became, by degrees, the person the cove's women went to when their troubles weren't strictly medical. She never spoke of testifying. She never needed to. She had put her account in the sealed annex, sworn and signed, and the annex did not unseal, and some mornings I would see her standing at the window with the tide running and I understood that she was reading it — the water, the horizon, the whole indifferent book of the world — and finding her own name written somewhere in it at last.

* *

Winter came down hard on the cove, the honest kind of hard that makes everything smaller and warmer inside its circle. And on a raw gray Tuesday — of course a Tuesday — Bertram's barge came up from the south a day off schedule, which meant something, and Bertram himself came up the path two at a time, which meant more, and he was holding out a letter with a wet, happy, insufferable grin on his face.

"Off the quarry mail," he said. "First one. Came through my hands and I only read the outside, which is more restraint than you've ever shown me credit for."

The envelope was addressed in a hand I knew — small, careful, each letter placed like a stone in a wall. Addressed to *Maren Quill, Halfway Cove*, and beneath it, in a second line, added later, the ink slightly different:

*and Wren.*

Wren abandoned her blocks instantly and climbed into my lap with the entitlement of a duchess, and I broke the seal with fingers that had steadied at ten thousand births and shook now over one sheet of paper.

It was short. His letters always would be; he rationed words the way poor men ration candles, and made them burn longer for it. It said that the quarry was cold and the work was heavy and the men were mostly decent once you fed them. It said he'd been given the light duty of tally-keeping, which meant the overseer had noticed he could count, which meant — I could hear the dry note in it — that the Meridian Company's habit of putting its convicts to their highest use continued uninterrupted. It said Wren's starfish had caused a formal inquiry among the guards and that he had declined to explain it, and that the starfish now sat on the sill of the tally office facing the sea, "where it belongs."

And then the last paragraph. The candle he saved his longest burn for.

*Tell her the walls are coming along. Tell her I've started saying things out loud here — small things, safe things, the practice of it. Tell her I am learning to be a man who was silent and isn't anymore, and that the learning goes easier because somewhere eleven miles down the coast, everything I'm not losing is keeping itself found.*

*Tell her I'll write every week. Long ones.*

*Tell her to count the Fridays.*

Wren had gotten one small hand inside the letter's fold and was examining the paper with the gravity of an assessor. "For me?" she demanded, pointing at her own name.

"For you," I said. "Every week. That's what it says."

"Long ones?"

"The longest he's got."

She considered this, and the tide, and the whole arrangement of the universe, and then she reached up and patted my cheek with the specific authority of a foreman assigning a task. "Read it 'gain," she commanded. "All the words. Even yours."

And I gathered her close against the wind coming off the water, and looked down the gray eleven miles toward the quarry, and began at the top, and read every word — even mine.
