## 35. Letters from the Quarry

*First letter — written on the back of a ration chit, three days after sentencing*

My Wren,

They tell me you are too young to read this, which is fortunate, because my handwriting has gone to ruin already and I have only been at this place nine days. Maren will keep these until you are old enough. She says she will teach you to read using them, which means your first words may well be *quarry*, *limestone*, and *do not eat the prison soup*, and for that I apologize in advance.

Here is where I am: a quarry two hundred miles north of Greyhaven, where the stone is pale as bone and the men are mostly quiet. I cut blocks for sea walls. It is honest work, which is more than I ever did for the Meridian Company, and no one here has ever once asked me to falsify a manifest.

I will not pretend it is easy. My hands have blisters on their blisters. The bread could stop a door. But I can see the sky from the pit, all of it at once, unbroken by warehouse roofs and crane arms, and I had forgotten how much sky there was. In Greyhaven you learn to look down — at ledgers, at boots, at the ground ahead. Here there is nothing to do but look up. I am trying to learn it again.

I want you to know why I am here, though you are too small to understand it yet, and one day you will be old enough to understand and perhaps too busy to care, which is also fine. But let me say it once, plainly, so that it exists somewhere besides the sealed annex and the memory of four stubborn people:

I was silent for seven years because silence kept me alive. Then you arrived on a step in the rain, and I understood that staying alive was not the same as living. You made me choose. Everything after that was just arithmetic, and I have always been good with numbers.

Sleep well, little wren. Grow big and loud. Loudness runs in our family now.

Your Orsen

* * *

*Second letter — autumn*

Dear Wren,

A man named Pellow works the saw beside mine. He is serving six years for stealing a horse, and he maintains his innocence with such vigor that none of us have the heart to mention the horse is stabled behind his mother's house and answers to his whistle. Pellow has appointed himself my instructor in quarry wisdom. Today's lesson: "Never trust a foreman who smiles before noon." I did not tell him I spent seven years under a supervisor who smiled constantly, at every hour, including once during a funeral. Some knowledge would only trouble him.

The light here is changing. In Greyhaven, light came filtered through fog, like a secret told through a wall. Here it arrives whole. In the mornings it comes down into the pit sideways and turns the limestone gold, and for an hour the whole quarry looks like something worth carving a cathedral from, instead of sea walls for merchants. I have started keeping track of the hour. It is the closest thing I have to a church service.

Maren writes that you have two teeth and opinions about porridge. I am glad about the teeth. About the porridge opinions I can only say: hold firm, Wren. A person who knows what they will not eat will eventually learn what they will not believe, and the second kind of knowing will save your life.

Fenwick visited last month. He wore his restored archivist's coat and lectured the guard about proper document handling for so long that the guard gave up and let him hand me the parcel directly. Inside were ginger cakes from Maren and, folded beneath them, a single sheet listing the names of the divers, all forty-one, each one spelled correctly. He wrote underneath: *"So you can check my work."* That is Fenwick's way of saying he thinks about you, and me, and the rest of it, every day.

Eat a cake for me when you're old enough. I ate yours. I regret nothing.

Your Orsen

* * *

*Third letter — first frost*

Wren,

Winter is coming to the quarry, and I have learned something about cold that I never learned in Greyhaven. Cold in the city is personal. It finds the gap in your collar and settles in like a debt collector. Cold here is impersonal — it simply is, everywhere, enormous, and strangely that makes it easier. You cannot bargain with it, so you stop trying, and you put on two shirts and get on with the work.

I have been thinking about debts, since I seem to have time. The judges said I owed the Company for breach of indenture, as if indenture were a gift someone had given me instead of a rope someone had tied. They took seven years of my life and then billed me for the privilege of escaping. And yet — here is the strange thing, Wren, and I hope you will not think less of me for saying it — I feel lighter here than I ever did in the warehouse. Debt of that kind is carried in the shoulders. What I carry now is honest weight, stone on stone, and at the end of the day I set it down and it stays set down.

Sera sends letters through Maren, unsigned, full of small news. Your mother is working at the harbor infirmary now. She is good at it, the way she was good at diving — patient, exact, unafraid of deep water even when the water is only other people's suffering. She writes that she tells you about your father, the diver, and that you reach for the sea out the window when she does. Keep reaching. The sea owes your family more than it can ever pay, but it belongs to you anyway.

Here is a small kindness to report, because I promised myself I would find one in every letter: yesterday I found a wren's nest in the old yew by the quarry gate. Empty, of course, this time of year — but woven into it, unmistakable, was a scrap of oilskin cloth. Birds steal whatever is at hand. Somewhere above this pit, a bird built its home out of the same stuff that once wrapped you against the rain, and it held.

Nothing meant is ever wasted. Remember that when the world tells you otherwise, as it will.

Your Orsen

* * *

*Fourth letter — midwinter*

Little Wren,

You are walking now, according to Maren, who reports it with the smugness of a woman whose porridge strategy finally worked. You walked straight to the window and banged on it, demanding the sea. Maren says you were furious that glass exists. I have never been prouder in my life. There will be many windows between you and things you want, Wren. Be furious at them. Then find the door.

The quarry in snow is almost beautiful, if beauty can be made of white and silence. The men work slower. Even the foreman — a decent, gray man named Hobb, who has never once smiled, thank heaven — lets us take our tea inside. Pellow has organized a singing arrangement whereby everyone pretends not to sing while everyone sings. The repertoire is eleven songs, nine of them about horses, which Pellow insists is coincidence.

I will tell you about the nights, because the nights are the hard part, and I promised you truth. There is a lamp outside the barracks window and it buzzes, and some nights the buzzing sounds like the warehouse, and I wake up certain I am twenty-two years old and still bound, certain none of it happened — not you, not the bridge, not the sealing of the record — and I have to lie in the dark and wait for the certainty to wear off, the way frost wears off stone at ten o'clock.

It always wears off. That is what I hold onto. And when it does, I do not count sheep; I count names. Forty-one divers. Four friends. One child learning to bang on windows. The list gets me back to sleep every time.

One night a young guard found me awake and asked, not unkindly, whether I regretted it — whatever it was we weren't supposed to discuss. I told him: ask yourself what you would trade three years for, and if the answer is nothing, that is not peace you are living in. It is just a smaller cage. He thought about that a long time. Last week he smuggled me an orange. Small kindnesses, Wren. They are the interest paid on love, and love compounds.

Your Orsen

*Fifth letter — thaw*

Dear Wren,

Spring came to the pit the way everything comes to the pit — slowly, and from above. First the light lasted longer. Then water ran in the channels. Then, one impossible morning, a green haze appeared along the quarry rim, so faint you'd swear you imagined it, and by afternoon the whole edge of the world had decided to be alive again without consulting anyone.

Pellow says spring is when escape attempts happen. I told him I am not attempting anything; my sentence has an end date, and I intend to walk out the front gate on it, which will confuse everyone terribly. He seemed disappointed. He is planning his own attempt for May, via the lime cart, and I have agreed to know nothing, which is the kindest thing one prisoner can do for another.

I have started telling you stories in these letters the way Maren tells you bedtime ones, except mine are true, which makes them harder to write. So today, a small true thing:

Last week a block came loose in the east face and nearly took Tam Rood off his feet. I caught his arm and hauled him back, and afterward he shook for an hour and I did not shake at all, and I realized why: I have been afraid before. Worse afraid. Afraid-with-ledgers-and-supervisors afraid. Next to that, falling stone is almost friendly. It announces itself. It does not smile.

The men here think I am brave, which is funny, because bravery is just fear that has been given a job. You gave mine one, Wren. You were eight months old and could do nothing but exist loudly at the world, and it was enough. Never let anyone tell you that you were too small to matter. You mattered before you could focus your own eyes.

Adela Brine writes monthly, always on Courier letterhead, always ending with the same postscript: *"Still printing. Still free. Barely."* The paper was raided twice more and reopened twice more, which she describes as "a robust dialogue with the authorities." Historians have begun writing to her asking to see the sealed annex. Actual historians, from the university at Corvale, with ink-stained cuffs and terrible manners. The record holds, Wren. It holds.

Your Orsen

*Sixth letter — early summer*

Wren,

Two years left, and the days have found their shape: stone, tea, sky, song, sleep, names. I am thinner than I was and grayer than I should be, and my hands have grown so calloused that when I touch paper I handle it like an egg, which Fenwick would say is the correct way to handle paper anyway.

Something happened this week that I must tell you, because I suspect it is the beginning of the end of this part of the story.

A carriage came to the quarry gate on Tuesday — a real carriage, with a crest on the door, and out of it stepped two people. The first was a gray-bearded man who introduced himself as a historian from Corvale and immediately asked whether the limestone in the sea walls of Greyhaven could be traced by quarry marks, which is either a very dull question or a very important one, and I have learned those often look identical.

The second person got down more slowly.

She was small, and dark-haired, and she stood at the quarry rim looking down into the pit with the ferocious concentration of someone who has learned to walk by banging on windows, and even at this distance, even across a year and a hundred feet of pale stone, I knew her.

Maren was holding her hand. Maren waved.

Wren, you waved back. At me. Before I had written a word to warn you who I was, before anyone pointed, you looked straight down into that pit and found me among forty men in gray, and you waved.

Pellow asked who they were. I said, "My family," and my voice cracked on the second word like green wood, and Pellow, horse-thief, philosopher, friend, pretended very hard to inspect a block of limestone so I could collect myself.

The historian wants to speak with me tomorrow. He says the memorial commission has questions about the pearling station, and that a name has come up repeatedly in the sealed annex, and he would like to hear it from the source.

He wants my name, Wren. Not my number. My name.

Tomorrow I will give it to him, out loud, in the open air, under all that sky.

And apparently — if Maren's grin is anything to go by — tomorrow you intend to give me yours as well, or at least your loudest attempt at it, from your mother's arms, at the quarry gate.

I have survived worse than joy. But not by much.

Your Orsen
