## 37. Release Day

They gave me back my name on a Tuesday.

Not my whole name — that had been spent at the annex, spoken aloud in front of delegates and sealing wax and Corvane Dull's purple face — but the administrative version of it, written in the colony ledger in a clerk's careful hand: *Orsen Coldharbour, indenture voided, penal term served.* Three years of quarry dust reduced to one line and a signature, as if I were a crate of tin being struck off an inventory.

The clerk didn't look up when he did it. He'd stamped out maybe four hundred releases from that desk, I judged, and he'd learned somewhere along the way that looking up made it harder. I understood. I'd been a warehouse man for seven years before all this, and I knew every trick a man develops to keep the work from touching him.

"Sign," he said, and pushed the book across.

I signed. My hand shook more than I expected it to. Not from fear — nothing in that office frightened me anymore; fear and I had parted ways sometime in the second winter, or rather fear had left and taken most of my other feelings with it as luggage — but because the last time I had signed my name on paper that mattered, men with batons had come through the door within the hour. Some part of my body still believed that was how signing worked.

Nothing came through the door. The clerk slid a chit under the bars, told me to collect my belongings from stores, and went back to his next file.

My belongings fit in one hand: a canvas coat three years out of fashion, a wool scarf Maren had sent in my second winter that the guards had let through only because it was grey, and a paper packet containing a letter I had not been able to send.

I'll come back to the letter.

* * *

The gate of Coldrock Penal Quarry — named, in the Colony Office's infinite humor, for a family of Coldharbours who had once owned it, back before the wars made everyone poor in the same currency — opened onto a road, and the road opened onto morning, and the morning was so ordinary it nearly broke me.

That's something they don't tell you about walls. You spend three years imagining what's on the other side of them, and you build it up into a kind of paradise where the tea is hot and nobody counts you at intervals and doors are just doors. Then you walk out, and the world on the other side is a cart horse steaming in the cold, and a woman arguing about the price of herring, and a dog lifting its leg against a fence post, and none of it knows you've been away. None of it has been waiting. The ordinary world doesn't wait. It just goes on being ordinary without you, and there is a grief in that, and a mercy too, and I stood at the gate for a long minute letting both wash over me like weather.

I was thinner than I'd been. Greyer. A guard who had been decent to me in an unspoken way — extra bread sometimes, never a word about it — had told me the week prior that I looked "properly haunted now, like a schoolmaster." I thanked him. In the quarry, that passed for warmth between men, and I found I meant it.

I also found, walking down that road with everything I owned over one shoulder, that I did not know where I was going.

This deserves explanation. For three years I had known exactly where I was going: to the wall, to the face, to the bell, to the mess, in that order, every day, until the days ran out. Purpose is a terrible thing to lose even when the purpose is finished. I had testified. The record was sealed. Wren was safe. Sera was free. Fenwick was pardoned and, by his last letter, insufferably reinstated. Every thread I had pulled taut on that long night before the signing had held.

So what was I now? A man with a canvas coat and a letter he couldn't send, standing in a road outside a quarry town, discovering that freedom without a task attached feels suspiciously like falling.

I did the only thing I could think of. I walked toward the harbor. Whatever else had changed, I was still a man who felt safest with water in front of him and ships on it. Old habits of a fog-bound boy.

* * *

The packet coach to Greyhaven took two days. I rode on top, in the open, because I had spent three years underground and I would have paid double to ride on the roof in a hailstorm. It didn't hail. It did something better: it rained, gently, for the first hour, and then stopped, and the whole world smelled like wet stone and gorse and someone's breakfast onions, and I sat there getting drizzled on with what I am told was an idiotic smile on my face.

A drover across the roof from me watched this for a while.

"You right, friend?"

"I'm free," I said.

He nodded slowly, the way men nod at things they understand but wish they didn't. "How long?"

"Three years."

"My brother done two." He dug in his coat and produced a flask. "First day's the strange one. Everything's too big. Second day you remember how to be a person. Third day you're just you again, except heavier and lighter at the same time, if that makes any sense."

It made perfect sense. We shared the flask — cheap spirits that tasted like a barn fire — and he asked no questions, which is the finest courtesy one stranger can pay another, and we rode through the hills watching the light change.

Somewhere past the second ridge, Greyhaven came into view, and I gripped the rail.

The city had grown. That was the first thing. Smoke where there hadn't been smoke, rooftops spilling down hillsides that had been bare, and there — impossible to miss, spanning the harbor mouth like a held breath — the new bridge. Reparations money, the papers called it. The divers' bridge. I had read about its construction in Adela's Courier clippings, which Fenwick sent folded into envelopes alongside his letters, because Fenwick was constitutionally incapable of sending a letter that contained only feeling. Even in his kindest note, he had described the bridge's stone as "adequately sourced, thank the gods; you know I checked."

Seeing it in person was different. Seeing it in person put a lump in my throat that stayed there all the way down into the city.

* * *

I had planned my arrival like a coward, I'll admit it. I had it arranged in my head: I would come into Greyhaven quietly, find a room, wash off three years of quarry, buy a shirt that buttoned properly, and only then present myself to the people I loved, so that they wouldn't have to see the thinner, greyer, haunted-schoolmaster version of me first. I had rehearsed greetings. I had, gods help me, considered whether to grow the beard back before or after.

The plan lasted exactly as far as the harbor steps.

Because there they were. All of them. Standing at the bottom of the gangway traffic like a delegation, like a monument, like the single best thing I had ever seen in a life that had contained very few candidates.

Maren Quill saw me first. She always did have eyes like a customs officer. She didn't wave — Maren has never waved in her life; she considers waving a form of begging — but she elbowed Fenwick hard enough that I saw him fold, and then he looked up, and his hat fell off, and he didn't even notice.

I don't remember walking down the steps. I remember the middle of it: Maren reaching me first and taking my face in both hands, turning it side to side like she was inspecting a foal, saying, "Thin as a whippet and grey as her knitting, look at you, look at you," which is Maren-speak for *I have missed you beyond the reach of language*, and then crushing me against her oilskin coat, which smelled exactly as it had three years ago: salt, tar, and the ghost of her pipe.

"Hello, Maren," I managed into her shoulder.

"Don't you 'hello' me. Don't you dare. Three years of letters and not one of them mentioned you couldn't cook."

"That's true, though. I can't."

"I know it's true, that's why it's unforgivable." She held me out at arm's length again, blinked hard twice — Maren does not cry, she blinks — and shoved me toward Fenwick before anything undignified could occur.

Fenwick shook my hand. Of course he did. Tobias Fenwick, restored archivist of the Meridian records, pardoned state's witness, man who had crawled half-drowned out of a burning barge clutching half a ledger to his chest, greeted my homecoming with a handshake and damp eyes and a voice he had to clear twice.

"Coldharbour." A pause. "Orsen." Another pause, longer, during which his jaw worked like a man chewing on words to see if any of them were safe. "You look — "

"Don't say it."

"Adequate."

"Fenwick."

"You look better than adequate. You look like a man I would follow into a flooded crypt again, and I want it noted that I do not say that lightly, because the last crypt had eels." His voice cracked on *eels*, which is possibly the most Tobias Fenwick sentence ever uttered, and then he abandoned the handshake entirely and hugged me briefly and ferociously, the way one hugs something one has previously failed to save.

Adela Brine was next, notebook already out, because Adela experiences even joy as potentially printable. "Coldharbour. Give me a quote for the record."

"Adela."

"'Free man returns to Greyhaven,' anything, I've got a piece half-written and my editor wants color—"

"Your editor," I said, "can want."

She laughed — that short bark of hers — and hugged me anyway, quick and fierce, and murmured near my ear, "For whatever it's worth, the annex held. Historians keep writing. Ostrava's staff sends requests monthly. You did that." She pulled back and pointed her pencil at me. "But you're not getting out of an interview."

"And here I thought this was a happy occasion."

"It is. That's why the interview will be friendly." A gleam. "Mostly."

And then there was Sera.

She stood a little apart, as she always did — a woman who had spent years hunted learns to keep a wall at her back and space at her sides, and some habits outlive their reasons. The scars on her face and neck had faded to pale ridges in the morning light. When our eyes met, neither of us moved for a moment, because there are debts between people that hugs cannot service, and we owed each other the kind that take years.

She had given me her sworn account in a laudanum den with a pistol under her shawl. She had refused to testify, and then, watching a false survivor recite the fever story on Company platforms, she had changed her mind, and her testimony had cracked the lie open like ice. And she had made me promise, before she did any of it, that Wren would have a future whether we won or lost.

"We won," she said now. Quietly. Testing the words; she still did that.

"We won."

She touched my cheek — lightly, where the grey was — and said, "You need feeding," which from Sera Voss was practically an ode, and then she stepped aside.

And I looked down.

* * *

Wren was hiding behind Maren's skirts.

I had known she would be. I had prepared for it. Fenwick's letters had warned me — Fenwick warns me of everything, including weather, including tide tables — that a child of four who knows a man only as ink on paper might not immediately reconcile the ink with the legs standing in front of her. I had composed speeches. Gentle ones. I had practiced kneeling.

What I had not prepared for was the paper.

She was holding a letter. My handwriting. One of mine — I could tell even upside down, that cramped slant the quarry clerks mocked and Maren claimed she could read fine "if the light's good and I've had tea." She held it with both hands, the way you hold something you've been told is precious often enough that you believe it, and she peered around Maren at me, and then down at the letter, and then at me again, doing arithmetic.

Four-year-old arithmetic takes a while. Nobody rushed her. The whole harbor seemed to hold its breath — Maren's hand hovering, Fenwick frozen mid-fidget, Adela actually lowering her pencil, which I believe was a historical first.

Finally Wren looked up and said, with the devastating authority of the very small:

"You're taller than your letters."

"I know," I said. My voice came out wrong, thick. I knelt down on the wet stones anyway, knees complaining about three years of quarry stairs, so that I would be less tall than my letters. "I'm sorry about that. Paper comes in smaller sizes."

"Are you the same one?" she asked. "All of them? Or different ones?"

"The same one. Every one."

"Maren reads them out loud. She does the voices."

"She does not do the voices," said Maren, behind me, doing the voices.

"She does the storm one wrong," Wren said severely. "You wrote the storm quiet. She does it loud."

I looked at Maren. Maren examined the sky with great interest.

"She's right," I said. "The storm was quiet. That was the whole point of it. Quiet storms are the ones that get you."

Wren considered this. Then she stepped out from behind Maren's skirt, crossed the stones, and put the letter in my hands.

"You keep this one," she instructed. "I have the others."

"How many others?"

"All of them." She counted on her fingers, got somewhere between six and eleven depending on her method, and then abandoned the count in favor of a more urgent matter. "You're cold," she observed. "Your hands are like fish."

"They are a bit like fish."

She solved this by taking one of them, which closed the entire account of three years and one war and one act of testimony and one prison term in a single motion, and leading me up the harbor steps, informing me over her shoulder: "We got cake. Fenwick dropped it but Maren fixed it."

"The structural integrity of that cake," said Fenwick, behind us, "was compromised from the start. The bakery's standards have slipped terribly since—"

"Tobias," said three women in unison, and he subsided.

We went up the steps like that: a child towing a freed man, a midwife and a widow and a journalist and an archivist falling in around us without any of them arranging it, the way found families fall in, without orders and without gaps.

* * *

The cake, for the record, was excellent, even the reconstructed portion. We ate it in Maren's rooms above the new chandlery — she had given up the barge after the fire, though she still spoke of it the way one speaks of a late spouse — and there was bread and butter and strong tea, and Sera cut everyone's portions with a mother's ruthless fairness, and Fenwick produced, from his coat, a document.

"Now," he said, smoothing it on the table with the reverence he otherwise reserved for sealed annexes, "before anyone gets sentimental again. Orsen. Your assets."

"I have assets?"

"One asset. I've been holding it in trust." He turned the paper around. It was a bank draft. Underneath the figures, in the fussy hand I recognized from ten thousand ledgers, was a notation: *Proceeds, sale of duplicate ledger sets, scholarly editions, volumes one through four. Minus expenses. Minus eel-related damages.*

I stared at it. It was more money than I had ever seen, including the year I'd handled manifests worth fortunes and owned nothing but my boots.

"Fenwick. This is — historians *paid* for—"

"Scholarly presses, three governments, the Ostrava commission, and," he admitted, with dignity, "one very persistent novelist from the capital who has been writing to me weekly. There is talk of a book. About the massacre. And the—" he cleared his throat, "—the witnesses."

"There is not going to be a book," said Adela.

"There is absolutely going to be a book."

"If anyone is writing a book," said Adela, "it is the person who broke the story—"

"If anyone," said Maren, "is doing voices in this book—"

They argued. It washed over me like warm water. I sat with Wren asleep against my arm — she'd eaten her weight in cake and gone down mid-argument, mid-chew almost, with the absolute trust of a child among her own people — and I looked around that small warm room at the four loudest, stubbornest, bravest people I had ever known, quarreling about authorship over the crumbs, and I thought: *this*. Not the annex. Not the sealed ink, not the hearing, not even the moment Dull's face went the color of bad ham when the sealing mechanism engaged. This. A room with bread in it. People who argue about books because they are alive to argue about them.

Quiet joy, earned. That was all. That was everything.

Sera caught my eye across the table. She tipped her head toward the window, toward the harbor and the bridge standing over the water where her husband had died, and mouthed: *Tomorrow.*

Tomorrow. Because tomorrow, it turned out, there was more. There was always more; that was the discovery of my whole life, that the record is never actually finished.

"Tomorrow what?" I asked.

Fenwick and Adela exchanged a look — a conspiratorial look, the specific look of two people who have been managing a surprise and have just been told the surprise is due early.

"Well," said Adela, pulling out her notebook. "Now that you mention it. The memorial committee met yesterday. Stone's ordered, site approved, at the bridge foot. And there's to be a plaque with names—"

"—all the names," Fenwick said, and his voice went careful and proud at once, "every diver verified from the annex record. Including—" and here he looked at me directly, the way he had looked at me across a flooded crypt, "—including Josiah Coldharbour. Volunteer labor, indeed. We'll see what the stone says."

I looked down at my sleeping daughter-by-choice-and-letters, and out the window at the bridge, and I felt the grief and the joy meet in my chest and, instead of cancelling each other out, simply sit down together like old shipmates.

"Tomorrow, then," I said.

"And Orsen," Adela added, pencil poised, wicked gleam returning, "they'll want a speech."

"No," I said. "They won't. Because I'm not giving one."

"You have to give—"

"He's not giving one," said Maren, refilling my cup. "Look at him. Man's been making speeches for three years straight. Tomorrow we stand, and we read the names, and we let the stone do the talking."

Outside, the tide was turning in the harbor, the water sliding silver under the new bridge, going about its ancient business, carrying everything with it and keeping nothing — except what we write down.

Wren stirred in her sleep, murmured something about storms being quiet, and held on tighter to my sleeve.

I let her.
