## 38. The Bridge

The bridge was finished on a Tuesday, which felt right to me. Tuesdays were the Company's accounting days, the days when numbers got set down and made true by sheer officialness. It seemed fitting that a bridge built out of their shame should open on one.

They called it the Voss Memorial Bridge, which was its own small miracle. Not the Meridian Bridge, not the Greyhaven Accord Bridge — someone in the delegates' office had actually listened, had read the sealed annex all the way through to Sera's name, and had put it on the plaque in letters taller than my hand. Sera herself had not been consulted. When she first heard of it she went very quiet for a long while, and then said only, "Well. He always wanted his name over water." Her husband Tam, that was — drowned at the station before Wren drew her first breath, before any of us knew what a child wrapped in oilskin would do to our careful lives.

The opening day was cold and bright, the kind of Greyhaven morning where the fog burns off early and the harbor looks almost apologetic for its years of gray. The whole city turned out, or so it seemed from where I stood near the far ramp, holding a basket of seed cake Maren had made and pretending I did not know anyone important, which after four years remained my most comfortable posture.

There were speeches, of course. A delegate read from a card. The mayor cut a ribbon with scissors that had clearly been polished for the occasion and never used for anything else. Adela Brine stood at the edge of the crowd with her notebook, and when the mayor's speech wandered into phrases like *healing* and *moving forward together*, I watched her write those words down with the particular small, savage smile she reserved for quotations she intended to hang later, like trophies of other people's hypocrisy.

And then they let the people cross.

I had promised myself I would walk it last. Let the crowds have their moment; let the bridge be theirs first. But promises to myself have never held much weight where Wren was concerned.

She came barreling down the ramp like weather, four years old and dressed in the blue coat Fenwick had insisted upon because, he said, "a girl of consequence requires a coat of consequence," and she grabbed my hand with both of hers and pulled.

"Orsen. *Orsen.* They said we can go across. All the way across. Over the *water*."

"So they did."

"If I dropped something, would it fall in the sea?"

"It would."

"What if I dropped *everything*?"

"Then you'd be a very expensive sort of person, and Maren would have opinions."

Maren, arriving behind us with the unhurried gait of a woman who has delivered babies in storms and does not rush for ribbons, snorted. "I have opinions already. Walk on the inside, both of you, away from the rail. And don't dangle the child."

"I'm not a child," Wren said, dangling.

"You are precisely a child," Maren said, "and the best one I've kept alive yet, which is saying something, as I've kept several alive." She took Orsen— no. She took my other arm. She still called me by the name I'd grown up with, sometimes, when she forgot; she'd known me under it first, back on the barge, back when names were dangerous things. Now the name was mine again, legally, ink-stamped and pardoned, but four years of habit dies hard, and honestly I liked hearing it. It sounded like someone who'd known me before I decided who to be, and loved me anyway.

Fenwick came last, of course, because Fenwick came last everywhere now, having acquired in his restored position at the archives the unhurried dignity of a man whose paperwork is finally in order. He wore his good coat and carried a walking stick he did not need, and paused at the foot of the bridge to look up at the cables with an expression I recognized. It was the expression he wore reading a well-kept ledger: suspicion softening into respect.

"They've used Moravian cable anchoring," he said. "That's sound engineering. I wrote to the commission twice about the pilings."

"We know," Maren said. "You showed us the letters."

"I merely want it noted that this bridge will stand."

"Noted," I said. "Filed. Sealed, even."

He gave me a look, but there was no weight behind it. Four years had taught him that some jokes could be allowed to live.

We crossed.

It takes maybe ten minutes to walk the span if you're going anywhere, and we were not going anywhere, so it took us the better part of an hour. Wren ran ahead and back and ahead again, testing the limits of Maren's patience and the elasticity of my arm. The wind came off the harbor clean and cold. Below us the water moved in long silver sheets, and the fishing boats — the ones that used to give the diving grounds a wide berth — passed underneath without hesitation now, their crews waving up at the crowd.

Halfway across, Wren stopped.

She stopped the way children stop, completely, as if the world had pressed a button somewhere. She went to the rail — Maren opened her mouth, thought better of it, closed it — and looked down at the water for a long time.

Then she said, "This is where the divers dived. Isn't it."

Not a question, quite. But nearly.

I looked at the others. Maren was watching me with that steady gray gaze of hers, the one she used on mothers in labor: *your turn, do it properly.* Fenwick had developed a sudden scholarly interest in a mooring bollard. Cowards, both of them, and kind ones.

"Yes," I said. I crouched down beside her so we were the same height, which is the first rule of telling children true things — get below their eyeline, so the truth doesn't feel like weather coming down on them. "Right here. Underneath us. Before the war ended, and for a long time before that, there was a place out here where people dove for pearls."

"Were you a diver?"

"No. My father was."

Her face did the thing it always did when I mentioned my father — the small furrow, the searching. She knew the shape of the story the way you know the shape of furniture in a dark room. She knew there had been a station on an island. She knew people had been hurt there. She knew, in the vague and enormous way children know things, that this was why we were all five of us standing together on a bridge on a Tuesday instead of scattered the way life had tried to scatter us.

"Was he good at it?"

"He was the best," I said. "He could hold his breath so long the other divers used to time themselves against him. And he was patient. Diving's mostly patience. You go down, and you wait, and you let your hands find the shells in the dark."

"Like feeling under the bed for your boots."

"Exactly like that. Only colder, and with more chance of an octopus."

"There are *octopuses?*" This with delight, immediately filed away for future nightmares and playground leverage.

"A few. Your grandfather — " and there it was, the word, out of my mouth before I could catch it, *grandfather*, the first time I'd ever said it to her aloud. It sat in the air between us, new-minted. I let it sit. Some words you don't take back even when they frighten you, especially when they frighten you.

"Your grandfather," I said again, steadier, "taught me that. Patience. Wait in the dark and let your hands find the true thing. That's mostly what I've done my whole life, one way or another. Waiting in the dark. Feeling for what's real."

Behind me I heard Fenwick clear his throat in the manner of a man swallowing something unsuitable, probably sentiment.

Wren considered all this with the gravity she gave to everything, her chin on her fists on the rail, wind whipping the hair off her forehead. Then she asked the question. I had known for four years that it was coming. I had rehearsed answers on quarry roads, in letters, in the grey hour before waking. And when it finally arrived it came out small and practical, the way the largest questions always do:

"The lady who left me on the steps. Did she leave me because of the divers?"

From anyone else it would have been prying. From a four-year-old it is simply research. They assemble the world from whatever pieces they overhear, and they want to know where their own piece goes.

"That's part of it," I said. "Do you remember the note?"

"I'm not supposed to forget it," she recited, dutiful. "*Forget this face.* You keep it in the archive now, in the special drawer. Fenwick showed me the drawer but not the note." A pause, shrewd. "Because it's sad."

"Because it's sad," I agreed. "A woman wrote that note in the rain, four years ago, on the worst night of her life. Her name is Sera. You know Sera."

"Sera makes the honey bread."

"She makes excellent honey bread. And once, Sera was married to a diver, Tam, who worked beside my father at the pearl station. And something very bad happened there — the bad thing we've told you about, the sickness that wasn't really sickness, the people who weren't allowed to come home. Sera saw it happen. And afterwards there were powerful men who wanted nobody to ever say it out loud. They wanted everyone to forget. Even the children. *Especially* the children, because children grow up and ask questions." I tapped the rail. "You're asking one right now."

"I'm evidence," Wren said, pronouncing the word carefully, having extracted it from dinner-table talk months ago. "Sera said so at Midwinter. She said I was the most important evidence in Greyhaven and also she wished I'd eat more cabbage."

"That sounds like Sera." I laughed, and it came out rougher than I meant. I looked out at the water a moment. The tide was turning beneath us; you could see the current shift, the whole harbor slowly changing its mind. "Here's the truth of that night, and you can keep it as long as you like. Sera's husband died at the station. She survived it, barely, and she was hunted, and she was frightened, and she had you. She couldn't keep you safe and stay alive herself. So she wrapped you in oilskin — that's waterproof cloth, divers use it — and she carried you through a storm to the warehouse steps, and she wrote that note so that whoever found you wouldn't look too closely. Because looking closely gets people killed. I knew that better than anyone."

"But you looked anyway."

"I did."

"Why?"

And there it was. The whole book of it, in one word from a four-year-old on a bridge.

I thought about lying — about giving her the tidy version, the version where I simply knew right from wrong like a coat I owned. But she'd been told the face on the note must be forgotten, and instead she was standing on a bridge named for a dead man she'd never met, asking questions, wearing a coat of consequence. Tidy versions had done enough damage in this city to last several lifetimes.

"Because I knew her handwriting," I said. "Isn't that strange? Out of all the writing in all the world, I knew hers, because I'd seen her name in the ledgers — the lists of the lost. And when I saw it, I understood somebody was trying to make you disappear. To unperson you. And my father spent his whole life teaching me that the opposite of drowning isn't swimming." I shook my head. "It's being remembered. Somebody knowing your name, your face, your story, and refusing to let go of it. So when I picked you up, I decided to be the one who didn't let go. That's all it was. Cold hands, wet steps, and a decision."

Wren absorbed this. Then, with devastating four-year-old logic:

"So if I hadn't been evidence, would you have kept me anyway?"

The wind moved. The boats moved. Somewhere behind me Fenwick had given up pretending about the bollard and was watching us openly, and Maren had taken off her hat, which she did only when something mattered.

"Yes," I said. "Evidence or not, note or no note. Some things you pick up and you're just — theirs afterward. Ask Maren about the cat."

"The cat was *strays*," Maren called. "Plural. It's a different thing entirely."

"It's the same thing exactly."

"It is *not* — " she began, and then caught sight of Wren's grin, and gave up with the particular sigh of a woman surrounded, and came to lean on the rail beside us, which was her way of conceding and joining at once.

We stood there a while, the four of us and the child, looking down at the diving grounds through the floor of the city. Down there, under the silver, were the beds where my father's hands had learned their patience, and the channel where Tam Voss's boat had gone out for the last time, and the cold company of everyone the ledgers had once called fever losses until Fenwick's duplicates said otherwise. The bridge didn't cover any of it. Bridges don't cover; they just make it possible to cross. That, I suspect, is the entire difference between a bridge and a lie.

"Will you tell me more?" Wren asked. "About Grandfather. When I'm bigger."

"Every year," I said. "A little more each year, as much as you can carry. That's how it works. Stories are like coats that way." I touched the collar of her blue one. "You grow into them."

"And Sera's husband? Tam?"

"His name is on the plaque at the far end. We'll read every name together on the way across. All hundred and six."

"That's a lot of walking and a lot of names."

"Yes," I said. "It is. That's rather the point."

She took it seriously, then — took my hand, and Maren's, and marched us name by name down the span while Fenwick supplied dates from memory and occasionally corrected the plaque itself, quietly, to me, as though the bronze might revise. At Tam Voss's name Wren stopped and stood very straight, because Sera had taught her that much, at least. At my father's name — Aldous Coldharbour, diver, *volunteer labor* struck through and replaced by order of the commission with *murdered* — Wren squeezed my hand very hard, harder than the walk required, and did not ask anything, because some facts even four-year-olds know to hold instead of question.

At the far end, the crowd had thinned. Adela was waiting by the plaque with two honey-bread slices and a reporter's instinct for an ending, and beside her, hood back now, unhidden in daylight for the first time in years, stood Sera.

Wren broke into a run. Sera caught her and swung her up, laughing, scarred face open to the sky, and I stood at the railing with the wind coming off the water and let myself feel the whole weight and lightness of it: the note in its archive drawer, the ledgers sealed, the names in bronze, the child in her mother's arms, my father murdered and remembered, both, forever, on the record.

Maren came to stand beside me.

"Well," she said. "You waited in the dark a long time, Orsen Coldharbour."

"Long enough," I said. "But I found the true thing."

Below us the tide turned, and above us, faint but unmistakable across the water, came the sound of Adela Brine hailing a newsboy — the afternoon edition, early, with some fresh trouble in it already, because in Greyhaven there is always fresh trouble, and I would not learn until that evening just how much of it was headed for us.
