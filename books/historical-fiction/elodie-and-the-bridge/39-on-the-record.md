## 39. On the Record

The morning of the unveiling, Greyhaven did something it almost never did: it cleared.

The fog that had wrapped the city for as long as I had lived in it — seven years indentured, three years penal, and all the years before and between — lifted like a held breath finally let go. Sun came down on the harbor in long pale bars, and the water, which I had always thought of as grey, turned out to be green. Green and silver, flecked with light, as if it had been this color all along and simply never trusted us enough to show it.

I stood at the edge of the crowd gathered beneath the new bridge and tried to decide whether I was nervous. It seemed like an occasion for nerves. There were dignitaries. There was a band. There was a platform draped in bunting the color of no flag anyone had ever fought under — a deliberate choice, Fenwick had told me, since nobody could agree whose victory it had been, so they'd settled on everyone's and no one's.

"You're fidgeting," Maren said beside me. She wore her good shawl, the one with the blue border, which she claimed was for funerals and unveilings alike because both involved somebody being put in the ground.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"Your thumb is doing that thing where it rubs your fingers like you're counting coins you haven't got."

I made my thumb stop. It started again within a minute. Some habits are load-bearing.

The bridge itself rose above us, stone and iron, spanning the harbor mouth where the pearling boats used to put out. Built with reparations funds, every stone of it argued over by committees, delayed twice, over-budget once — but built. Beneath its arch, divers had once walked into the water with stones in their hands and pearls in their lungs and contracts written in ink that dissolved faster than flesh. Above it now ran carts and foot traffic and, this morning, children hanging off the railings to see the ceremony, their mothers hauling them back by the collars.

"They've given you a seat on the platform," Fenwick observed. He had appeared at my other side without sound, as he always did, a thin man dressed carefully in his restored archivist's blacks, carrying a leather folder against his chest the way other men carry infants or instruments. "A named seat. With a placard."

"I saw the placard."

"And?"

"It misspelled my name."

Fenwick considered this. "Orsen Coldharbour is not an easy name to misspell."

"They found a way. They've written 'Coldharbor.' Like a place. Like somewhere you'd ship cargo to."

"Well," said Fenwick, "in a sense—"

"Don't."

He didn't smile — Fenwick's smiles were rare enough to be catalogued events, and he kept them in the archive of himself behind glass — but something moved at the corner of his mouth and settled. Three years had greyed him further, straightened him somehow too; disgrace had bent him, and restoration had set him upright again, like a book reshelved after repair. He still carried the folder everywhere. I suspected he slept with it. I knew for a fact he had never once told me what else was in there besides today's documents, and I had decided some mysteries were gifts you let people keep.

Adela Brine found us next, moving through the crowd the way water finds cracks. She looked older too — the Courier had been raided twice more since the first story, and each raid had added a line to her face like a ring in timber — but her eyes hadn't aged a day. They were still the eyes of a woman doing arithmetic about everyone she met.

"Their pamphlet's already circulating," she said, by way of greeting, nodding toward a vendor near the platform selling commemorative booklets, three coppers apiece, with a woodcut of the bridge on the cover. "The official history. 'Tragedy at the Station: Fever, Loss, and the Company That Endured.'"

"I know. Fenwick bought six."

"For the archive," Fenwick said, with dignity. "One keeps the errors too. Otherwise how would anyone prove they were errors?"

"That's what I keep telling them at the paper," Adela said. "Nobody thanks you for keeping the mistakes. But you can't correct a record that doesn't admit it ever wrote anything wrong." She glanced up at me, quick and appraising. "You're still not going to speak? They've left you a slot. Ostrava herself requested it. Madame Delegate Ostrava, who remembers who put names in front of her when it would have been easier not to."

I had known this question was coming for weeks, from all directions. Sera had asked it gently. Wren had asked it the way four-year-olds ask things, which is to say she had asked whether I would be talking at the big bridge party and then, before I could answer, asked whether eels have ears. Maren had asked it by not asking it, which was her way.

"No," I said. "I'm not going to speak."

Adela waited. She was very good at waiting; it was half her profession.

"There are things words are for," I said, "and things paper is for, and I've spent eleven years learning the difference. Words, they can argue with. A man stands up on a platform and says a true thing, and half the crowd believes him and half thinks he wants something, and by evening both halves have changed their minds twice. Paper's different. Paper sits there. Paper doesn't want anything. You come back to it in twenty years and it says the same thing it said, and it doesn't care whether you believe it."

"That's a speech," Adela pointed out.

"It's a private one. Those don't count."

* * *

The ceremony began with the band, which played something martial until the conductor noticed the occasion and course-corrected mid-note into something mournful, causing visible distress among the trombones. Then the dignitaries spoke. There were several of them and they spoke for a long time, and I learned why hats are brimmed.

Madame Ostrava spoke last before the unveiling itself. She was smaller than I remembered, and older, and her voice carried anyway, the voice of a woman who had spent two years forcing procedure to bend toward truth and had won often enough to be tired and tired often enough to be careful.

"On this day," she said, "we acknowledge what occurred at the Meridian Company's Third Pearling Station. Not fever. Not misfortune. We acknowledge the deaths of forty-one divers and family members, deliberately caused, deliberately concealed. We acknowledge it in the treaty record, in the annex sealed on signing eve, where it cannot be amended, cannot be lost, and cannot be quietly forgotten by any government, company, or committee present or future." She paused, and her eyes found mine in the crowd, and she went off-script for exactly one sentence. "We acknowledge it because someone chose to be remembered rather than merely survive."

Then the cloth came off the memorial.

It was a wall — low, granite, curved gently like the inside of a shell — and cut into it were names. Forty-one names, and then others, added later after the historians' inquiries, divers from earlier seasons whose deaths had been recorded as accidents and never questioned until questions became fashionable. My father's name was there. *Josiah Coldharbour. Diver.* Not *volunteer labor*. The Company's original inscription — the one I had read on a winter morning years ago and decided, standing in the cold with my fists clenched, what I would do with the rest of my life — had been removed by court order. Fenwick had petitioned to preserve the removed stone itself, and it sat now in the archive, catalogued, labeled, filed under evidence.

The crowd was quiet. Somewhere behind me a child asked loudly what the wall was for, and a mother began an explanation, low and stumbling, and I thought: that's how it starts. Not with speeches. With a mother trying to answer honestly.

They called my name then. Placard and all — "*Orsen Coldharbor*, witness" — and a polite ripple of applause, and I climbed the platform steps because declining loudly would have been worse than declining quietly, and I stood at the podium, and I looked out at all those faces, and I said:

"Thank you. I'm not going to speak."

And I took the note from my coat.

It was small. Smaller than most people expected; you could see the surprise move through the front rows when I held it up, this scrap of oilskin-paper, soft-cornered with handling, water-stained at one edge. On it, in a hand gone shaky with fear and cold, five words: *Forget this face.*

"A little over four years ago," I said — because it seemed wrong to say nothing at all, even if only to introduce the silence — "this was left on the warehouse steps of the Meridian Company, wrapped around an infant, on a night of storm. Whoever wrote it wanted the child hidden. Wanted her nameless. Wanted her forgotten, because remembering her meant remembering where she came from, and where she came from was a lie forty-one people were killed to protect."

I turned the note so the crowd could see the writing. Nobody could read it at that distance, of course. That wasn't the point. The point was that they could see how small it was, how ordinary, how easily it might have gone into the harbor instead of my pocket, how easily my pocket might have gone into a stove.

"This is the beginning of the record. Everything else — the ledgers, the sworn statements, the annex, the treaty language, this wall — everything grew out of this. And a record should begin at its beginning. So it will."

I stepped back from the podium. Behind me, Fenwick opened his folder with the reverence of a man performing last rites, and brought out not just the note but the pages beneath it — my annotations, four years of them, written in the evenings at Maren's table while Wren slept in a drawer lined with sacking. Each page carried the note's image reproduced at the top, and beneath it, in dated sequence, everything that followed. Who held the child, and when. What Maren saw in the swaddling cloth. Which wreck the oilskin came from. Whose handwriting matched. What the ledgers proved. What it cost, itemized, unflinching — the barge, the arrests, the sentences, the three years of quarry stone. Every entry signed. Every entry witnessed.

Fenwick received it all into the memorial archive — a new institution, built into the base of the bridge itself, which I confess struck me as either deeply poetic or deeply strange, and which I had stopped trying to decide between. He logged each page aloud, date and seal, and the archivist's voice carried across the crowd, flat and precise and somehow, in its flatness, more moving than any of the speeches.

When it was done, he closed the cabinet. The lock turned with a small, clean sound, and the note — *forget this face* — sat behind glass in the permanent dark, next to the ledgers and the petitions and the overseer's own damning arithmetic, annotated past all forgetting.

It would still be saying the same thing in twenty years. It didn't care whether anyone believed it. It had witnesses now.

* * *

Afterward, the crowd thinned the way crowds do, reluctantly, in eddies. The band attempted the mournful piece again with better results. Someone was selling roasted chestnuts within legal distance of a solemn memorial, and doing brisk trade.

I found my family by the wall.

Sera stood at the far end, where the later names gave way to the earliest ones, her scarred cheek turned to the light without shame — four years had taught her face to hold itself differently, or taught the rest of us to stop flinching, or both. Her husband's name was third from the left end. She touched it the way you touch a doorframe you've passed ten thousand times: not ceremonially. Familiarly.

Maren had Wren up on her shoulders, which was against the memorial's posted regulations and also inevitable. Wren was four now and built entirely of questions and elbows. From the top of Maren she surveyed the crowd like a small harbormaster.

"Is that the end?" she demanded, spotting me. "Did the party finish?"

"It finished."

"Did anybody fall in the water?"

"Not this time."

"Pity," she said, with the merciless honesty of the very young, and Maren laughed and swung her down, and Wren attached herself to my hand with the ease of long practice, small fingers finding the spaces between mine as if they'd been measured for it.

We walked the wall together, slowly, because Wren set the pace and Wren's pace involved inspecting things. She liked the names' letters. She could read some now — Fenwick was teaching her, with the patience of a man defusing bombs. She stopped at her own favorites, sounding them out, and I let her lead, and the sun stayed.

At my father's name she stopped longest, because I always stopped there.

"Who's Josiah?" she asked. She knew. She asked anyway. Asking was how she confirmed the world was still where she'd left it.

"He was my father," I said. "And a diver. And a great many other things I only found out about later, from paper." I crouched down beside her, so the name was above both our heads. "You know the story."

"Some of it."

"All of it matters, so I'll tell you whatever pieces you ask for, whenever you ask for them. That's the arrangement."

Wren considered this gravely, the way she considered all contracts. Then she leaned close to the stone, and pressed her mitten against the carved letters — *Josiah Coldharbour, diver* — the way you press your hand into wet sand to leave proof you were here.

"He has the same name-ending as you," she observed.

"He does. His father had it too, and his before him. It took me a long time to want it back."

"Do you want it now?"

The tide was turning below the bridge; I could hear it in the pilings, that deep patient scrape of water rearranging the world a grain at a time. The fog was staying away. Somewhere behind us Fenwick was explaining catalogue systems to an interested stranger, and Adela was taking notes on the stranger's interest, and Maren was watching all of them with her arms folded and her blue-bordered shawl catching the wind, guarding the whole ridiculous lot of us out of pure habit.

"Yes," I said. "Now I do."

Wren nodded, satisfied, and tugged my hand, and we walked on along the wall — past the names, past the chestnut seller, past the open doors of the archive where the record lay in its permanent dark, complete, corrected, kept — out toward the light on the water, her small hand warm in mine, holding on.
