## 40. Chapter 40

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Orsen would later consider fitting, because Tuesdays were the days Greyhaven's tide came in lazy and nothing much got done until noon anyway.

He found it wedged in the door of the little house on Candlemaker's Row — the house Maren had declared too narrow for a family and then proceeded to fill with one anyway — addressed in a hand he did not recognize, sealed with wax stamped by an emblem he very much did: the crossed quills of the Historical Commission.

"Wren!" he called up the stairs. "There's a man from the government who wants to talk to us."

"Should I hide?" she called back, six years old now and missing nothing.

"No," Orsen said, turning the envelope over in his hands. "That's rather the whole point of us."

* * *

They had been three years since the memorial, two since Fenwick had been formally reinstated to the archives with his beard trimmed to something approaching dignity, and one since Adela Brine had won her lawsuit against the Meridian Trading Company's successors and spent the entire settlement on a second printing press she named *Vindication*, which everyone agreed was slightly much but no one said so to her face.

Orsen had learned, in those years, what a life looked like when it was not organized around staying alive. It was strange at first — disorienting, like walking down a familiar street and finding the fog lifted off it. He kept catching himself performing silence, holding words behind his teeth out of habit, and then discovering that nobody in the room wanted him quiet. Maren wanted his opinion on whether the roof needed patching (it did). Sera wanted him to read aloud while her hands worked wool, because she said his voice made even dull books tolerable and he chose very dull books indeed. Wren wanted everything — stories, explanations, the names of every fish on the fishmonger's slab, why the sky was the color it was today versus yesterday.

"Why is the sky gray?" she'd asked once, aged four, on a morning as gray as any Greyhaven morning ever was.

"It just is," he'd said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," he'd agreed. "It isn't." And he'd thought, with a feeling he could only describe as wealth: *I have the time to find a better one.*

He had done his three years of penal labor at the quarry inland, and they had taken the youth out of his shoulders and put stone-colored patience in their place. He had come home thinner and grayer, as promised, and Maren had taken one look at him on release day and said, "You're eating before anything else happens. Whatever else this family does today, first we feed you." And that had been the homecoming, really — not speeches, not tears, though there were tears later — but a bowl of stew and four people arranged around him like they'd been waiting years for exactly this choreography and had rehearsed it nightly.

They had, he suspected.

* * *

The Historical Commission's letter turned out to be from a junior archivist named Pell, a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the nervous energy of someone who had read about history mostly in books and was alarmed to discover it kept showing up at his desk wearing boots. He wanted permission to publish selections from the sealed annex — the divers' names, the ledgers, Sera's sworn account — in a proper scholarly edition, with annotations.

"With your name on it, if you'll allow," Pell said. He had come to the house because Fenwick had insisted that if the Commission wanted anything from this family, they could damn well climb Candlemaker's Row like everyone else. "You testified under your true name, Mr. Coldharbour. The record has you as 'witness, indentured laborer.' Most people reading it wouldn't know there was a person attached."

"There was a person attached," Orsen said mildly.

"Yes. That's — yes, sir. Precisely my point."

Wren, seated at the table pretending to do sums and doing no such thing, looked up. "Are they going to print Papa Orsen's whole testimony? All of it?"

"All of it," Pell said.

"Including the part where he talks for a very long time without stopping?"

"Wren," Orsen said.

"I'm only asking," she said, with the perfect innocence of a child who had heard the story forty times and knew precisely where the funny parts were. "Because Auntie Maren says once you started talking you've never properly stopped, and she wonders sometimes if the sealing mechanism was actually the problem or if it was you."

Pell laughed before he could stop himself, then went red and apologized to the furniture.

"He may print it," Orsen told him. "All of it. That was always the point."

* * *

After Pell left — clutching a signed permission form and a biscuit Maren had pressed on him with the air of a woman arming a soldier — Orsen walked down to the harbor.

This was a habit he had never lost and no longer needed to hide. In the old days, walking the docks had been surveillance: reading manifests, counting guards, learning which silences meant danger. Now he walked them because the light came through the harbor fog in the late afternoon the way it did nowhere else, gold shot through with grey, and because the water remembered things and he liked keeping it company.

The bridge crossed high above, its stone pale in the sun, built with reparations funds over the water where the divers had once gone down and, too many of them, not come up. Ships passed beneath it. Children fished from its railings and were, mostly, prevented from falling off by their older siblings. It was an ordinary bridge doing ordinary work, and Orsen loved it for exactly that. There was no plaque yet — the Commission argued about the wording the way committees argue, which is to say geologically — but Fenwick had assured him the eventual inscription would include every name, in full, spelled correctly, which Fenwick regarded as the highest possible tribute an archivist could pay.

At the far pilings, where the tide ran quick and green, he stopped the way he always stopped, and stood the way he always stood, and let the wind have his coat.

His father had been a diver here. Had gone into this water with a rope on his ankle and a knife at his belt and come up with pearls and cold in his bones, until the day he went down and the Company decided fever was cheaper than contracts. For seven years of indenture, Orsen had known this and said nothing. For seven years, silence had been his armor, and it had worked, and it had also been the loneliest thing he ever wore.

"You're doing the harbor stare," said Maren, coming up beside him with a basket of mending she had clearly abandoned somewhere. She was broader than she'd been on the barge, slower on the stairs, quicker to laugh. "You know, most people just look at the sea. You *argue* with it."

"I'm winning," Orsen said.

"Mm. And how's it going?"

"We've reached an understanding. It keeps being the sea. I keep remembering."

Maren bumped her shoulder against his — the closest she came, most days, to saying affectionate things outright, though she'd gotten more generous with it since the quarry years, since the letters, since Wren had learned her letters partly off the envelopes Orsen sent. "Good," she said. "Keep it up." And then, because she was Maren: "Sera's making fish pie and claims it's edible, which historically means we should bring bread."

* * *

Dinner at Candlemaker's Row was a crowded affair, as it was most nights, because the house that was too narrow for a family had somehow accumulated one of considerable size. Sera sat by the window where the light suited her scarred face best — a practical arrangement she had made herself and defended fiercely, so that people meeting her saw her expression before anything else, and her expression, these days, was usually dry amusement. Fenwick held forth about the Commission's annotation style with the wounded passion of a man who had waited decades to be allowed to hold forth about annotation style. Adela had come straight from the pressroom and still smelled of ink, and was telling a story involving Vindication, a jammed roller, and an apprentice who now understood why one did not oil machinery during a deadline.

"And then," Adela said, "the entire evening edition comes out reading 'the Company denies ALL CHARGES' in letters you could read from across the harbor—"

"—which," Fenwick said, "in fairness, was accurate."

"It was accurate. It was not what I wrote."

Wren, wedged between Orsen and the bread, listened with her chin in her hands the way she listened to everything, hoarding details the way other children hoarded river stones. She had been born on a warehouse step in a storm with a note pinned to her swaddling that said *Forget this face*, and she did not remember it, and that was as it should be. But she knew the story now — all of it, in pieces, given to her age-appropriately and honestly, per the treaty Orsen had made with himself the night he'd decided to testify. No lies of convenience. No comfortable omissions. If she asked, she got truth, scaled to her size.

"Papa Orsen," she said now, in the lull while Sera dealt out pie, "why did you do it? The testifying. I know about the ledgers and the delegates and everything. But why did *you* have to be the one who says his name out loud?"

The table went quiet around them, but not uncomfortably. This family had learned to make room for questions that took a long time to answer. Even Adela stopped mid-sentence, which Fenwick once claimed was a phenomenon observed roughly once per decade, like a comet.

Orsen considered. He had answered this in letters, once, for a girl too young to read them — *because someone had to, and because I was tired of being safe in the wrong way* — but she was six now, and deserved the version with the seams showing.

"When I was your age," he said, "I learned that being quiet kept me alive. And it did. For a long time. But there's a kind of quiet that isn't safe, it's just small. You fold yourself smaller and smaller so you fit inside the silence, and eventually there's not much left of you to fold." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "Your mother gave you to me because she couldn't keep you and couldn't bear for the world to pretend you didn't exist. And I realized I'd been letting the world pretend a great many people didn't exist. My father among them. So when it came time to choose between staying folded up or standing up and being seen—" he shrugged— "it turned out I'd already decided. Years before. I just hadn't noticed."

"Because of me," Wren said. Not a question. She knew, the way she knew tides and bread-rationing and which chair was hers.

"Because of you," Orsen agreed. "And because of your grandfather, and the divers, and Sera, and Fenwick, and a lot of people whose names are now in a book that can't be burned. Being remembered isn't the same as being safe. But it's better than being erased."

Wren absorbed this with the gravity of a judge. Then: "Can I have more pie, or were you going to say something philosophical about that too?"

The table broke. Sera laughed her low laugh; Maren declared the girl had her head screwed on right; Adela said she wished half her sources ended interviews that efficiently. Under the noise, Orsen passed Wren the plate, and their hands met briefly on the rim — his scarred, hers small and quick — and he felt the thing he felt on the bridge, in the archive, in the quarry writing letters by lamplight: that the price had been real, and it had been paid, and it had been worth it. Not worth it the way merchants mean it, balanced in columns. Worth it the way a harbor means it when the tide turns.

* * *

Later, when the plates were cleared and Fenwick had fallen asleep upright in the good chair (he denied doing this; the evidence snored gently), Orsen climbed to the little attic room where Wren slept, to say goodnight.

She was sitting up in bed with a piece of paper, tongue between her teeth, writing.

"What have you got there?"

"A record," she said, with tremendous dignity. "Auntie Adela says everything important should go on the record. So I'm making a record of today. 'Tuesday. Tide came in lazy. A man from the Commission came and Papa said yes. Fish pie. Fenwick asleep again.'" She looked up. "How do you spell 'asleep'?"

He spelled it for her, letter by letter, watching her careful hand copy the shapes. Above the window, the fog was rolling off the harbor and the first lamps along the bridge were coming on, small and stubborn, one after another after another.

"Will you read it back to me?" he asked. "When you're finished. I'd like to hear how the day sounds, written down."

She nodded solemnly, a historian already, guarding her draft. And outside the window the tide kept turning, and the bridge held, and the record — open, annotated, unburnable — went on.

Orsen pulled the blanket up under his daughter's chin, and stayed a while in the lamplight, unhurried, unforgotten, home.
