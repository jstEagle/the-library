## 1. The Weight of Small Names

The convent clock had stopped at eleven minutes past four on the day the army arrived, and in the two winters since, no one had managed to wind it again. Elodie Marchand had come to think of that frozen hour as the town's true time — the moment Saint-Aubin stopped being itself and became instead a place the war passed through, the way weather passes through, leaving everything damp and rearranged.

She kept her own time now, by lamplight and by the breathing of wounded men.

The ward had been the sisters' refectory once. You could still see it in the bones of the room: the long tables replaced by iron cots, the abbess's dais now a nurses' station stacked with lint and laudanum, and above it all the arched windows where someone had painted over the colored glass with whitewash so the light wouldn't show at night. The paint was flaking. On clear evenings a sliver of blue came through, and if you stood in exactly the right place — Elodie's place, third cot from the end, beside the man who would not stop coughing — the fragment caught the last of the sunset and held it like a coin.

"Third lamp," Tomas said from the doorway, holding up three fingers as though she might have forgotten how counting worked. "Père Luc's fever is climbing."

"I know." She did know. She knew the rhythm of every fever in the ward the way Marguerite knew the rhythms of her ovens: by attention, by hours, by love disguised as habit.

Tomas Duval was nineteen and built like a question mark, all elbows and apology, and he moved through the hospital with the particular grace of someone who had learned to carry things without disturbing anyone. Water basins, bedpans, the dying, the nearly dead. He had been the sacristan's apprentice before the convent became a hospital, and he still crossed himself, quickly and guiltily, whenever he passed the alcove where the altar used to be.

"Père Luc keeps asking what time it is," Tomas said, following her down the row of cots. "I keep telling him the clock's broken."

"And he keeps telling you God doesn't need a clock."

"He does tell me that." Tomas smiled despite himself. "Every hour. It's the only hour he tells me anything anymore."

Elodie set her hand on the old priest's brow. The heat there was considerable, a small stubborn furnace. He opened his eyes and found her face with the accuracy of a man who had spent fifty years finding faces in dark confessionals.

"Nurse Marchand," he whispered. "Is it four o'clock yet?"

"Not yet, mon père."

"Ah." He closed his eyes, satisfied, as though this were good news. Perhaps it was. In a town where time had stopped, there was no hurry to be anywhere else.

* * *

At midnight Marguerite appeared with the soup.

This was not a request nor an offer but a fact of the universe, like tides. Marguerite Besson had cooked for the sisters for thirty years, and when the sisters were evacuated she had simply stayed at her stoves as though they were parishioners who had fallen ill, and begun feeding whoever remained. Her kitchen occupied the ground floor's south corner, and it operated under laws stricter than any Vasseur's requisition orders. No one went hungry. No one criticized her stock. And no one — no one — touched the good knife.

Tonight's soup was mostly turnips pretending confidently to be something richer. She ladled it into tin cups and moved down the ward with a rolling gait that preceded her like weather, and the wounded men straightened in their cots the way flowers find sun.

"You," she said to Private Anselm, planting herself at the foot of his cot with the cup held out of reach, "have been letting your broth go cold. I hear about these things. The basins talk to me."

"The basins exaggerate," Anselm said.

"Everything in this hospital exaggerates except me."

He was twenty years old at most, this private, with a jaw still soft and a left shoulder that had been rebuilt by Elodie's hands and God's indifference over eleven weeks. A shell splinter at a nameless crossroads forty kilometers east. His papers, when they finally came, had come wrong — the regiment listed him under another man's name entirely, some clerical slip in a rear office, so that officially Anselm Ferrec did not exist and some unfortunate stranger named Ancel Ferrand was recorded as lying in a convent hospital in Saint-Aubin, wounded, recovering, real.

"They'll sort it out after the war," the adjutant had said, in the tone of men who say such things.

"After the war," Marguerite had sniffed, "I'll be eating soup in paradise, and I doubt their paperwork will follow me there either."

Now Anselm drank his soup obediently while Tomas sat cross-legged on the floor beside the stove — forbidden, tolerated, nightly — and Marguerite stood with her arms crossed like a general reviewing troops nobody could see.

"There was a convoy today," Tomas said quietly. "On the east road. Ambulances, going toward Verrières."

"Delegates' escort, probably," Elodie said.

"Armistice people." He turned the word over carefully, the way he handled communion vessels. "Do you think it's true, then? That they're actually going to sign?"

Marguerite made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a prayer; with her the two had always lived close together. "They've been *going* to sign since before your voice broke, boy."

"But the guns," Anselm said. He said it to his soup. "They haven't stopped."

No one answered that, because there was nothing to answer with. Outside, distant but distinct, the artillery spoke its slow sentence — one, then silence, then two more — the way it had every night for months. The men in the ward had learned to sleep inside the sound. Only the newest arrivals still flinched.

"Fourteen days," Elodie said at last. "That's what the rumor at the pharmacy says. Fourteen days, and the delegates sign at Verrières."

"And if they don't?" Tomas asked.

"Then we keep making soup," said Marguerite, and gathered the cups, and went back downstairs trailing the smell of turnips behind her like a banner.

* * *

The ward settled around two in the morning. Coughing thinned. The lamps burned low. Père Luc slept, or something near it, his lips moving through prayers too worn to need words.

And Elodie, in the nurses' station, took out the ledger.

It was an ordinary account book, bought from the stationer on rue des Tanneurs in another life, green cloth boards gone soft at the corners. The first dozen pages held legitimate entries from her early months — supplies logged, temperatures charted, the dull honest arithmetic of a hospital. Then, somewhere near page fourteen, the handwriting changed character. Smaller. Closer together. Careful in a different way.

She wrote the night's names.

The officers' reports never carried them. The reports carried regiments and numbers, casualties aggregated like sacks of grain, and when individual soldiers appeared at all they appeared with their paperwork in order — which was precisely the trouble, because in the final winter of a long war, paperwork had become the rarest supply of all. Men arrived without tags. Boys arrived without regiments. Deserters arrived shot at dawn and were buried before their names traveled further than the firing squad's consciences. And civilians — the reports did not have a column for civilians. There was no line on the form where Madame Aubry's granddaughter fit, the girl of nine who had been standing in her own doorway when the skirmish crossed her street.

So Elodie kept the column herself.

*Boyer, Marcel. Private, 204th — no, 240th; his friend insisted, twice. Died 3rd of December. Said his mother's name at the end. Odette.*

Her pen moved steadily. This was the ritual, and like all rituals it looked from outside like nothing much: a tired woman writing in a book by lamplight. But she had learned — slowly, unwillingly, the way one learns anything true in wartime — that omission is also a kind of writing. Every report that left Saint-Aubin without these names was making an argument: that these lives had weighed nothing, that the arithmetic worked better without them. Her ledger was simply the rebuttal. Small, handwritten, kept in a convent where the clock had stopped, but a rebuttal.

*Nobody famous,* she thought, dipping the pen. *Nobody decorated. Just the ones the war swallowed whole and reported as indigestible.*

There was a comfort in the work she did not fully trust and could not do without. Each entry required her to remember — the face, the last thing said, the joke Anselm's neighbor had told about the chaplain's boots even as the morphine took him — and remembering was the tax, and the record was what the tax purchased. Some nights she resented it. Most nights she understood that she would fight anyone, officer or archangel, who tried to take the pen away.

She heard the footsteps and did not startle. Tomas, come back for his coat, forgotten on the stove-warming bench hours ago. But he paused at the edge of the lamplight, looking at her with an expression she couldn't read — curiosity, or worry, or the particular tenderness of the very young for the very tired.

"Still here," he said softly.

"Someone has to be."

"Yes." He considered this. "But why does it always have to be you?"

Because someone must count what the counters refuse to count, she almost said. Because a name unwritten is a name unburied. Because when this ends — if it ends — someone will come asking what happened in Saint-Aubin in the last winter of the war, and I intend to be able to answer them properly, name by name, while the colonels answer in sums.

"Go to bed, Tomas," is what she said.

He went, pausing only to glance back once, and Elodie turned to a fresh line.

*Pelay, Sergeant. Admitted tonight, belly wound, from the clearing station. Old soldier — forty, maybe more. Vasseur's own man, they say. Won't let anyone sit with him but asks constantly whether anyone is sitting nearby. Asking after his records. Asking whether his papers came with him.*

She hesitated over the entry. Something in the old sergeant's face had stayed with her — not the pain, wards full of men wore pain like uniform, but the urgency beneath it, the way his hand had gripped her wrist with a strength his body had no business supplying and said, *My papers. Did my papers come? Find my papers.* As though among all the things a dying man might clutch at, it was the documentation he reached for. As though being counted, officially, in the proper ledger, was the last honor available to him.

*I'll write you down, Sergeant Pelay,* she thought. *Here, at least, the ink is reliable.*

She finished the entry, blotted the page, and closed the book.

Then she wrapped it in the oilcloth she kept for the purpose and carried it, as she carried it every night, down the cold corridor to the chapel.

The chapel was the one room the army hadn't repurposed, partly from superstition, partly because Colonel Vasseur had declared it "not strategically relevant," a sentence the nuns, had any remained, would have found theologically interesting. The pews stood in their rows. The dust held its positions. And in the last pew, wedged upright between two hymnals where it looked for all the world like a hymnal itself — same size, similar weight, green cloth against green cloth — sat the ledger.

Elodie slid it home between *Hymns of Consolation* and *Hymns of Morning*. She pressed it flush with its neighbors, straightened the row, and stood a moment in the dark with her hand flat on the shelf.

Somewhere below the floorboards, faint as memory, Marguerite's banked stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere beyond the walls the artillery laid down its slow fire. And somewhere east along the roads, in offices lit late, clerks were entering the war into their own ledgers — clean columns, round numbers, nothing smudged, nothing missed, nothing human.

"Goodnight," she told the books, because talking to them had become part of the ritual too, and rituals are allowed their small absurdities. "Keep each other company."

She was halfway back up the corridor when she met Tomas coming down again, coat finally located, and he stepped aside to let her pass with that odd careful look still on his face.

"Nurse Marchand," he said. "The hymnal shelf. In the chapel."

"What about it?"

"It's crooked," he said. "The middle one. I noticed it yesterday when I straightened the candles." A pause, delicately catastrophic. "I put it back myself. Didn't want you to think the chapel wasn't cared for."

The corridor was very cold and very quiet, and Elodie stood in it feeling the ground shift subtly beneath twenty months of discipline. Tomas watched her with those questioning eyebrows, and she understood — slowly, the way one understands a letter one has read twice — that he was not reporting. He was confessing. He had seen. He had said nothing then, and was saying only this oblique sliver now, and waiting to see what she would do about it.

"Tomas," she said at last, "do you often straighten other people's hymnals?"

"Only the important ones," he said.

And there it was between them, laid out plain as bread: he knew, and she knew he knew, and neither of them had betrayed the other yet. Down the hall, Père Luc murmured in his sleep. Beyond the walls, the guns kept their grim punctuation. And in the chapel behind them both, wedged between songs of consolation and songs of morning, the names waited in the dark — patient, illegitimate, and, Elodie was beginning to suspect, no longer hers alone to carry.

"Tomorrow," Tomas said quietly, "the requisition notices go up. Marguerite heard it from the fishmonger. Every document in town, they're saying. Everything with writing on it." He pulled his coat on, and his young face in the lamplight was grave and steady and entirely, dangerously willing. "Everything with writing on it, Nurse Marchand. I just thought you'd want to know tonight. Instead of tomorrow."
