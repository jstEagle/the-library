## 2. Requisition Papers

The morning the Colonel came to Saint-Aubin, Marguerite baked bread with the fury of a woman who had been personally insulted by the weather.

"You can smell it in the air," she said, punching down a mound of dough as though it owed her money. "Gunpowder and starch. That's what an army smells like when it intends to stay."

Elodie, who had come into the kitchen for hot water and stayed for warmth, leaned against the great stone hearth. "You can smell an intention?"

"I can smell a requisition. I've buried two husbands and one revolution, girl. I know the smell of men arriving to take inventory of things that aren't theirs." She shaped a loaf with brisk tenderness. "Besides, Tomas heard it from the lamplighter, who heard it from the ferryman, who heard it from his own mother, who is never wrong."

"And what did the infallible mother say?"

"That the Colonel's staff officers were at the mill measuring the bridge this morning. Nobody measures a bridge for a visit. They measure it for artillery."

Elodie felt something cold settle behind her ribs, the way frost settles on a window — quietly, then all at once. She thought of the ledger upstairs, tucked beneath a loose floorboard under her cot, forty pages of small careful handwriting that existed nowhere else in the world. Forty pages of names that the army's own reports had declined to remember.

"More bread," she said, because there was nothing else useful to say.

"Always more bread," Marguerite agreed. "A town that's baking is a town that's thinking."

* * *

Colonel Vasseur made his announcement at noon, in the square, in a voice pitched precisely between courtesy and command, like a man apologizing for a debt he fully intended to collect.

The whole town was assembled — or nearly. The sick stayed in the convent hospital, tended by Tomas, and Anselm sat propped at an upper window with his leg bandaged and his face arranged into the studied blankness of a man who has learned that faces are documents too. Elodie stood near the fountain with Marguerite, whose arms were crossed over her flour-dusted apron as if in formal protest.

Vasseur was not a cruel-looking man. That was somehow worse. He had the tidy gray competence of a schoolmaster who has never once lost a register book, and he read the proclamation from a leather folio without hurry, as though the words were old friends.

"By order of the General Staff," he said, "the town of Saint-Aubin and its appurtenances are hereby placed under military administration for the duration of operations." A murmur went through the crowd like wind through wheat. He waited for it to pass. "The convent hospital will continue its function. Its personnel are commended for their service." A small, correct pause. "Effective immediately, all records, registers, correspondence, and personal papers within the commune are subject to requisition. Every document must be surrendered to the requisition office by Friday evening. This includes — I will be clear, because clarity is a kindness — letters, diaries, account books, church registers, invoices, and any written matter whatsoever."

He closed the folio. He even smiled, faintly, at the baker's youngest, who was staring up at him with open admiration, having grasped only that a very important man had said many words.

"The war does not distinguish between the battlefield and the town behind it," Vasseur said. "Papers travel. Papers fall into hands for which they were not intended. An army that cannot count its own paper cannot trust its own memory. You will be compensated fairly for surrendered property. You have my word, and my word is a habit of long standing."

Then he saluted — correctly, crisply, to no one and everyone — and was gone into the mairie, and the square exhaled all at once, a hundred held breaths released in a ragged chorus.

"Well," said Marguerite. "Starch and gunpowder."

* * *

The walk back to the convent took four minutes. Elodie spent all of them composing and discarding speeches for God, whom she did not believe in but negotiated with professionally, the way one negotiates with a landlord one never sees.

*Let it be that he means the merchants' books. Let it be that no one counts loose papers under floorboards. Let me be brave in the ordinary way, the way that looks like nothing.*

The hospital ward was warm and dim, smelling of carbolic and lavender — Marguerite's contribution, deployed against the lavender's better judgment. Tomas was moving down the rows with a basin and a cloth, and Anselm was awake, propped on one elbow, arguing with his own bandage.

"—telling you, Sister Elodie, I could walk today if they'd let me. I could walk to the door and back. Twice."

"To the door and back once," Elodie said, "and only if you pretend it costs you nothing, which it doesn't, so we'll skip pretending." She set down the water she'd carried up and lowered her voice. "You heard the announcement?"

"I heard a man describe my diary," Anselm said. "I don't keep a diary. But I suddenly wish I'd kept one, just so I could feel properly robbed."

Tomas set the basin down. He was a thin, quick man with the permanent expression of someone listening to a conversation happening three rooms away, which was how he processed most conversations happening in front of him. "They'll want the hospital registers," he said.

"They'll want everything," Elodie said.

"Everything," Tomas agreed, and looked at her for exactly one second longer than the sentence required.

She kept her face still. She had gotten reasonably good at that, these past months; grief teaches composure the way winter teaches people to close doors quickly. But something in the look snagged on her anyway, like a burr in wool, and she turned to the window to hide it.

Outside, in the convent yard, two soldiers were already stacking crates against the wall. Measuring. Counting. The world had begun arranging itself into lists.

* * *

That night, after the last lamp was trimmed and the ward had settled into its symphony of breathing, Elodie knelt on the cold flagstones beside her cot and lifted the board.

The ledger came up wrapped in an old apron, and she held it in both hands the way one holds a sleeping child — carefully, and with the constant low astonishment that such a thing should be entrusted to you at all.

It was not a beautiful object. It had begun life as the convent's kitchen inventory, and its cover still bore, faintly, a grease shadow in the shape of a hand. But inside, page after page, in her small even hand:

*Baptiste Corail, private, 2nd Regiment. Died 14th of March. Not listed: wounded in retreat, left at the Ferme des Trois Puits. Age nineteen. His mother is called Soline. She sells eggs.*

*Unidentified boy, civilian, found at the crossroads. Approximately twelve years. No report filed. I buried him myself, with Father Aubert, who wept, which the reports also omit.*

*Aldo Brenner, lieutenant, attached engineers. Reported missing, 9th of October, sector east. Listed nowhere else.*

She stopped on that name sometimes, the way one's tongue returns to a broken tooth. She had copied it from a scrap of a field return that a dying courier had carried and that no one else had bothered to read twice. She did not know why she had recorded him with such care. Missing men were not the same as dead ones; a missing man might still come home. But she had written his name as though it mattered, because every name in the book was written as though it mattered, and that was the entire point, the whole stubborn theology of the thing.

Now the Colonel wanted every document in Saint-Aubin by Friday evening.

She sat back on her heels. Somewhere below, the convent's broken clock ticked its incomplete little life — it had stopped at eleven minutes past four on the day the occupation began, and nobody could find a craftsman willing to scale the tower in wartime, so it simply clicked uselessly toward a moment that would never arrive again. Elodie had come to think of it as the town's honest historian: recording nothing, promising nothing, stopped at the hour everything changed.

*Friday evening.* Five days.

Under the floorboard was impossible. The soldiers would go room to room; she had seen requisitions before, in other towns, other winters. They pried and probed with the cheerful thoroughness of men on a scavenger hunt with prizes.

She needed somewhere no one searches.

Her eye fell on the shelf above her cot, where she kept her few books. A prayer book. A fat volume of local history, dull enough to be invisible. And the hymnal — the big convent hymnal, black leather, brass corners, the kind of object that reads as furniture. It had been left in the ward for years; the wounded sang from it on Sundays when they were well enough, badly, with great feeling.

No one searches a hymnal. Hymns are the army's favorite kind of paper: harmless, sanctioned, full of submission.

It was a wicked thought, and she examined it from all sides to see if it would turn virtuous and fail. It did not. God, she suspected, had bigger ledgers of His own, and forgave a nurse her filing system.

She worked by candle stub, slowly, so the spine would not crack and tell tales. The hymnal's binding was sewn, not glued — old work, generous work — and where the gathered pages met the spine there was a hollow, a dark little valley running the length of the book. She loosened the endpapers at top and bottom with a thumbnail until the valley opened like a purse.

The ledger was slightly too thick. Of course it was. She pressed it flat — forty pages of names compressed by a half-inch, which felt briefly like sacrilege — wrapped it in waxed cloth, and slid it home. Then she re-pasted the endpapers with flour paste from the kitchen, borrowed from under Marguerite's strict supervision ("For books? What are you binding, a Bible?" — "A psalm," said Elodie, which was true in spirit), and weighted the covers overnight between two boards.

In the morning the hymnal looked exactly like itself. It opened to *O Sacred Feast* and rustled through the verses with perfect innocence. Only when she held it shut and shook it gently did it whisper, very faintly, like a house with someone hiding in it.

Elodie put it on the shelf, spine out, third from the left, between the prayer book and the history.

Her hands, she noticed, were perfectly steady, and her heart was going like a drum corps. It occurred to her, not for the first time, that courage was mostly a clerical problem: you simply kept doing the next small thing while terrified, and let the terror file itself elsewhere.

* * *

The week acquired a new texture after that. Obedience everywhere, dread underneath, like a fine tablecloth laid over something still moving.

On Tuesday the soldiers came to the mairie and carried out the civil registers in official-looking boxes, and old Notary Guérin watched from his doorway with the expression of a man watching his children emigrate. On Wednesday they came to the church, and Father Aubert handed over the parish books with such elaborate calm that Elodie knew he had already copied the baptismal dates onto scraps hidden in the confessional — the whole town was becoming quietly literate in smuggling without meaning to.

Marguerite burned almost nothing and confessed everything, strategically. "Take my recipes, boys, take them with my blessing," she told the young corporal who came to the kitchen, presenting a basket of handwritten cards with the generosity of a saint. "Half of them are nonsense anyway. This one here —" she tapped a card — "is how you ruin a perfectly good rabbit." The corporal, delighted, catalogued sixty recipe cards, none of which were recipes, several of which were Marguerite's accounts of every egg, hen, and sack of flour requisitioned since the first winter, written in a code of hers involving the names of saints and the phases of the moon. "Compensation," she said later, serenely, "arrives when the compensation arrives. Until then, my memory is the only honest register in Saint-Aubin."

Tomas surrendered the hospital's official log without a flicker, and Elodie watched him do it and understood, with a slow cold clarity, that the official log now contained every death she was permitted to acknowledge — and that everything else lived in the valley of a hymnal on her shelf, whispering when shaken.

Anselm, meanwhile, walked to the door and back, twice, on Thursday, and pretended it cost him nothing, and fooled no one, and glowed about it anyway.

"Friday," he said, leaning in the doorframe like a man of leisure. "Big day for paper. Are you nervous, Sister?"

"I'm never nervous," said Elodie. "I'm a nurse. Nervousness is for people who can't hold a basin steady."

"Hm," said Anselm, who had been shot at for two years and knew exactly what a person looked like when their heart was going like a drum corps. He said nothing more. But he watched her a moment longer than the joke required — the second time in a week someone had done that — and Elodie felt the first cold drop of a new fear: that the ledger might be safest from the soldiers and least safe from the people who loved her, who noticed things, who counted twice.

* * *

Friday evening came down cold and early-dark. The requisition office had been set up in the mairie, and the queue of townsfolk stretched into the square, each carrying their surrendered lives in bundles and strings and boxes — letters tied in ribbon, shop ledgers, a wedding book, someone's grandfather's almanac with thirty years of weather noted in the margins. The soldiers received it all with stamps and receipts and the gentle impersonality of men who would rather be anywhere warm.

Elodie stood in the queue with a modest bundle: the hospital's duplicate supply lists, a folder of discharge papers, her own copy of the nursing roster. All correct. All complete. All, officially, everything.

The hymnal was not with her. It lay on her shelf, third from the left, whispering to an empty room, and every hour of the day she had been seized afresh by the certainty that this was madness — that the inspection teams would go room to room tonight, that some corporal with nothing better to do would pull every book on every shelf, and that *O Sacred Feast* would open its mouth and give up forty names.

But carrying it was worse. Carrying it meant surrendering it into a box with a stamp on it. At least on the shelf it belonged to her failure alone.

"Name?" said the sergeant at the table.

"Marchand. Elodie. Convent hospital."

"Papers."

She handed them over. He counted them, licked a thumb, stamped twice, and wrote her name in *his* ledger — and there was a bitter symmetry in that which almost made her smile. Everyone keeping books of everyone. The whole world a convent kitchen inventory.

"Sick and wounded exempt," the sergeant said. "Personal effects of patients, exempt. Religious materials—" he glanced at the printed schedule pinned before him— "exempt pending review. Chaplain's inventory to be conducted Monday."

Monday. Her stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, like a stone into a well.

Religious materials exempt *pending review*. Which meant that sooner or later — Monday, or the Monday after — a chaplain, or worse, a staff officer with the chaplain in tow, would stand in her ward and run a finger along the shelf, third from the left, and shake out the hymns.

She walked home through the dark with her receipt folded small in her pocket, past the fountain, past the mairie's lit windows where Colonel Vasseur's silhouette moved behind a curtain, patient as arithmetic. In the convent yard the broken clock ticked on toward its dead quarter-hour, and the frost was coming down, quiet and thorough, taking its own inventory of the town.

Inside, she lit no lamp. She stood in the dark of her cell with her hand flat on the hymnal's cover, feeling the small secret weight of it, and thought: *Five days was always a fantasy. Now it's less than three.*

And that was when she heard Tomas's step on the stair — the light, apologetic step of a man who has decided, after a week of counting seconds-long looks, that it is finally time to say what he has seen.

"Sister Elodie," he said from the doorway, holding a stack of books under his arm for the chaplain's Monday inventory, and her hymnal, she realized with a falling heart, was on top of the pile. "I've come to help you with the shelving. May I ask you something strange about a song?"
