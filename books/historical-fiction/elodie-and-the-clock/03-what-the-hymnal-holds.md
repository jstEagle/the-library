## 3. What the Hymnal Holds

Tomas Ferreol had three talents in this world, and he counted them honestly every morning the way other men counted their money: he could carry more than he looked able to carry, he could fix a hinge with nothing but a nail and stubbornness, and he could tell when Sister Veronique's old kitchen fire wanted banking before anyone else smelled it. That was the whole inventory. He had never been a man of letters. He could read, technically — the priest who taught him had died before getting past the harder words, so Tomas read the way a mule pulls a cart, steadily and without much interest in the scenery.

Which is why it took him four days to notice that the hymnal was wrong.

* * *

The requisition order had turned the convent's back parlor into something between a warehouse and a confessional. Every house in Saint-Aubin was meant to surrender its papers by week's end — registers, letters, deeds, the baptismal book, photographs if you were feeling obedient — and since most of the town trusted the convent about as far as they could roll it uphill, they brought things here instead, wrapped in dish towels and excuses. Marguerite received them at the parlor door with the serene face she used for soldiers and drunks, murmuring *yes, we'll see it kept safe, yes, of course*, while behind her back the pile of hidden papers grew like snow against a fence.

Tomas's job was to sort the pile. He did not ask why he was sorting papers that were officially supposed to be surrendered; he had learned in forty-one years of living in an occupied town that some questions were just doors you didn't need to open to know what was behind them. He made three stacks. Books of record. Letters and photographs. And hymnals, prayer books, and other holy objects, which went on the table nearest the window because Sister Veronique had insisted holy things keep the light.

It was on the fourth day, moving hymnals from the windowsill table to make room for Madame Guillard's entire family Bible (the woman had surrendered everything including, apparently, her husband's fishing licenses from before the war), that Tomas picked up the small black hymnal wedged sideways at the back, spine toward the wall, and felt that it did not sit right in his hand.

Heavy. Not Bible-heavy. Heavy in the wrong place, like a loaf with a stone baked into it.

He opened it.

Now, Tomas was not a curious man, but neither was he a fool, and the difference between the two is mostly speed. He was not fast enough to stop himself from looking, and not slow enough to pretend afterward that he hadn't. The hymnal fell open not to a psalm but to a page sewn in from somewhere else — good paper, ruled, covered edge to edge in Elodie Marchand's small upright handwriting, the same hand that filled out the ward's milk requisitions and the burial lists and the little notes she left on the wounded men's pallets saying *broth only until Thursday.*

He read the line in front of him because there was nothing else to do with his eyes.

*Private Henri Colombe, 2nd Battalion, age seventeen according to his mother, nineteen according to his papers. Died 14th October, quarter past six in the morning. His hands were cold for two hours before anyone noticed, and I am sorry for it. The report says "died of wounds." He died of a shell, and of being seventeen, and no officer wrote down that he liked to hear about other people's mothers, so I have written it here.*

Tomas stood in the parlor with the hymnal open and the light coming through the window across the page, and he understood several things at once, the way you understand all at once that ice is thin.

He understood that this was the ledger everyone whispered about and nobody admitted existing — the book of names the officers' reports left out. He understood that Colonel Vasseur had ordered every document in Saint-Aubin surrendered by Friday, and that this was a document, and that it was not going anywhere near the surrender pile. He understood why Elodie always wrote at the night table with her body curved around the page like a wall around a garden.

And he understood, with a small cold drop in his stomach, that he was standing in the middle of the convent in broad daylight holding the most dangerous object in the town, and that Marguerite was coming down the corridor, and that Madame Guillard was right behind her asking loudly whether the Church counted fishing licenses among its sacraments.

There was no time to put it back properly. Tomas did the only thing his three talents equipped him to do: he carried it. He tucked the hymnal under his arm, picked up the family Bible with the other hand, and walked into the corridor wearing the expression he wore when hauling sacks of flour — blank, capable, mildly bored.

"Tomas." Marguerite stopped. Her eyes went to the hymnal under his arm, and something flickered across her face quick as a mouse under a cupboard. "That one goes on the sill table."

"Table's full," said Tomas. "I'll find it a place."

"Which place?"

"A good one."

Madame Guillard peered around Marguerite. "Is that a hymnal? Those are being collected too. Father Anselme said—"

"It's spoken for," said Tomas, and walked off before his mouth could get him killed.

* * *

The trouble with carrying a secret through a hospital is that hospitals are full of people whose whole profession is noticing things. Tomas made it to the linen storeroom, which was the closest thing the convent had to a vault, and put the hymnal on a shelf between the pillowcases, and then stood there breathing like a man who had run though he hadn't, working out how badly he had handled the last five minutes.

Badly, was the answer. Marguerite had looked at him the way she looked at a sauce that was about to break. She knew. Or she half-knew, which was worse, because half-knowing made people ask questions, and Marguerite asked questions the way winter came — slowly, inevitably, and settling in.

He should have put it back. That was the clean thing. Put it back exactly where it was, wedge it spine-in, forget the page about the boy who liked hearing about mothers, go back to sorting fishing licenses.

Tomas picked up the hymnal instead. Opened it again. Told himself it was to check it hadn't been damaged, which was even true, in the way that a man checks a jam jar by tasting it.

He read for a quarter of an hour, standing among the pillowcases.

There were names he knew. That was what stopped his breath — not the danger of it, but the familiarity. Old Dubreuil the cooper, shot in his own doorway in September, listed here with the date and the sentence *he raised his hands first; I have written to the battalion twice.* A woman from the mill road whose name Tomas had heard at her funeral but never seen on any paper since. Boys from regiments that had passed through, names with ranks attached, some marked *no next of kin found,* one marked simply *he called me Lucie. I do not know who Lucie is, but someone should.*

And near the back, in fresher ink: *Anselm Roche, private, reported dead 3rd August. See note.* Tomas flipped to the note. It said only, in Elodie's careful hand: *He is not.*

Tomas closed the book. He held it a moment longer, feeling its weight settle differently now — not a stone in a loaf anymore, but something more like ballast, the thing that keeps a boat upright in bad water.

Then he put it in the deepest shelf corner, folded a pillowcase over it with the tenderness he usually reserved for eggs, and went to find Elodie.

* * *

She was in the ward, changing a dressing on Private Anselm's leg while he complained about it with the cheerful stamina of a man who had been told he'd walk again and had decided to be insufferable about it.

"—and I maintain," Anselm was saying, "that Sister Veronique's broth is a punishment for something. I just don't know what. Something ancestral, maybe. Something my grandfather—"

"Tomas," said Elodie, without turning around. She had eyes in the back of her head; all nurses did. "You look like a man who has lost a fight with a cupboard."

"I've come about a hymnal," said Tomas.

Anselm perked up. "A hymnal? Are we having services? I'd attend. Anything to get out of this bed."

"This doesn't concern you."

"Everything concerns me. I'm extremely concerned by nature."

Elodie set down the dressing and looked at Tomas then — really looked, the way she did at wounds, reading what was underneath. Whatever she saw in his face, she didn't flinch. "Walk with me," she said.

They walked to the end of the ward, past men sleeping and pretending to sleep, and stopped by the tall window where the fog was doing its slow gray work on the courtyard below.

"The black hymnal," Tomas said quietly. "Small one. Wedged at the back of the sill table."

Something crossed Elodie's face that was not quite fear and not quite relief, but some third thing that lived between them, where those two had apparently been sharing lodgings for a long time.

"What about it?"

"It nearly went in the surrender pile today. My fault. I was sorting, and I didn't—" He stopped, started again. "I read some of it."

The silence that followed had texture to it. Down in the courtyard a soldier was arguing with a mule, and the mule appeared to be winning.

"You read some of it," Elodie repeated.

"The boy. Colombe. The one who liked hearing about mothers." Tomas cleared his throat, which had gone rough. "My own mother's been dead eleven years and I still can't hear about somebody's mother without—" He waved a hand, giving up on the sentence the way he gave up on most sentences.

Elodie watched him. "Are you here to tell me it's been discovered? Because if it's in the surrender pile, I need to know in the next ten minutes, and after that it won't matter what either of us knows."

"It's in the linen storeroom. Under a pillowcase."

"Why?"

"Because it seemed like the right shelf." He winced at his own answer. "No. Because I couldn't put it back and pretend I hadn't read it. That seemed worse somehow. Dishonest to you, keeping quiet. And dishonest to the book, putting it back like it was just a book again."

Elodie turned fully toward him then, and Tomas had the uncomfortable sensation of being read the way she read the ledger — carefully, line by line, checking for omissions.

"You understand what happens to whoever's caught with it," she said. "Not me alone. Whoever's caught near it."

"I've done the arithmetic."

"And yet."

"And yet." Tomas looked out at the fog. "You know, I'm not brave. I want that on the record, since apparently we're keeping records now. Marguerite's brave. You're brave. Anselm would be brave if his leg let him. Me, I get up in the night to check the fire, and I can carry heavy things, and I fix hinges. That's the whole of me."

"That's not the whole of anybody."

"It's most of me." He rubbed his jaw. "But here's the thing I keep coming back to, walking back across the ward just now. There's a name in that book — Lucie. Some boy died calling for a girl named Lucie, and nobody knows who she is, and you thought that mattered. That somebody ought to know. That's — I've been turning it over in my head for twenty minutes and I can't put it down. A person could die calling any name at all, and you wrote down the one he chose. As if it were important. As if *he* were important, which the army didn't think he was."

"He was," said Elodie simply.

"Well." Tomas blew out a breath. "So. I'm not brave, but I can be deliberate, and I'm told that counts for something in the long run. If that book needs moving — and it does, because Vasseur will have this convent turned out by Friday and we both know it — then it shouldn't just be you worrying at it alone at night. Two people hiding a thing are smarter than one. Three are smarter than two. And I know a hundred places in these old walls the army hasn't found in four years of trying."

Elodie was quiet a moment. In the ward behind them, Anselm had begun humming, tunelessly, a song that might have been a hymn or might have been about wine.

"Why?" she asked. "Truly. Give me the true reason, Tomas, because I'll build on whatever you give me and I'd rather it hold."

Tomas considered lying, decided it would be like lying to a lamp, and told the truth.

"Because when I was sixteen my brother Émile drowned in the mill race, and the miller's report said *accident, no witnesses,* and it wasn't true. There was a witness. Nobody ever wrote him down. And for thirty years I've thought — somebody should have written it. Somebody should have kept it. Even if it changed nothing. Even if nobody ever read it. The name should exist somewhere outside the heads of people who'll die carrying it." He shrugged, embarrassed. "You're the somebody. For all of them. And I'd rather carry heavy things for you than carry that."

The fog pressed at the window. Somewhere beyond it, past the mill bridge and the salt road, the war ground on without them, indifferent as weather.

"All right," Elodie said at last, and there was something in her voice Tomas hadn't heard from her in months — not softness exactly, but the loosening of a knot pulled tight too long. "All right. But if we do this, we do it properly. No half-knowing, no pillowcases on shelves where any sorter could stumble on them. You're in it or you're not."

"I'm in it."

"Then tonight, after the ward sleeps. Bring Marguerite. She already suspects — she looked at you in that corridor like a woman watching a pot about to boil over, and I'd rather have her inside the pot than outside it."

"She's going to scold us both."

"She's going to scold us both," Elodie agreed, and almost smiled, "and then she's going to be better at this than we are."

Tomas nodded and turned to go, and had reached the ward door when Elodie spoke again.

"Tomas. The page you read. About the boy and the mothers."

He stopped.

"Thank you for not putting it back," she said. "Most people would have. Most people would have put it back very carefully and never said a word, and slept fine, and I'd have lost the book on Friday anyway." She looked at him steadily. "You didn't sleep fine for twenty minutes. I noticed."

Tomas felt his ears go warm, which was ridiculous, so he covered it with a gruff nod and escaped down the corridor — and nearly walked straight into Marguerite, who was standing at the linen closet door with her arms folded and an expression that said she had heard considerably more than either of them would have preferred.

"Well," she said. "I suppose you'd better come to the kitchen, and bring the hymnal."

"The hymnal's not—"

"Tomas." Marguerite's voice was patient as rising dough. "Do you truly believe I haven't known about that book since spring? I dust around it every Tuesday. Now come along. If we're going to commit crimes together, we'll at least do it fed."

Down the corridor, in the ward, Anselm's tuneless humming stopped mid-note.

"Wait," his voice floated after them. "Crimes? What crimes? Whose crimes? *Somebody come back here and tell me about the crimes—*"
