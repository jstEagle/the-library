## 4. The Officer Who Counts Twice

He arrived on a Tuesday, which felt fitting, because Tuesdays were the day the laundry came back short and we had all learned to expect small thefts as a matter of course.

I was elbow-deep in a basin of water that had gone grey twice over when Marguerite appeared at the ward door with her particular expression — the one she wore for bad news and burnt onions, which were, in her opinion, roughly equal catastrophes.

"He's here," she said. "The counting man."

"Counting man?"

"The one from the requisition office. The one who's coming to count everything we own down to the last spoon." She wiped her hands on her apron, a gesture I had seen her perform a thousand times and never once in connection with actual dirt. "Tomas says he's already counted the church. Took him three hours. Tomas says he counted the *candles*."

"How many candles are there?"

"Forty-one," Marguerite said grimly. "There were forty-two. He made Father Anselme account for the difference."

"That's not so terrible."

"It is if you're Father Anselme, who has spent four years telling himself the missing candle was an act of God." She sniffed. "Now he knows it was an act of Corporal Dubois, who took it home to his mother in October, and he has to decide whether to say so."

This was how the occupation of Saint-Aubin announced itself: not with boots in the square but with arithmetic. The army had decided, somewhere far above us, that the town was now an asset, and assets required inventory, and inventory required a man patient enough to count candles.

Marguerite had seen him first through the kitchen window and pronounced him "young, but not foolishly young, which is worse than either extreme, because it means they've taught him something." Tomas had watched him measure the granary and reported that he measured it twice. And now here he was at our door, and Marguerite had come to warn me as though warning me against weather.

"Be normal," she advised.

"When am I not?"

"You're a woman who talks to dying men about their mothers as though you've met them. That isn't normal, Elodie, it's better than normal, but it won't help you with a man holding a clipboard." She straightened my collar with two efficient tugs. "Be dull. Dull is invisible."

I promised to be the dullest nurse in the province, and she went back to her kitchen muttering about spoons, because she had hidden the good spoons inside the flour barrel and was now trying to remember exactly where in the flour barrel.

* * *

Lieutenant Aurel Brenner turned out to be neither the fop nor the brute the town had imagined in its two days of speculation. He was perhaps thirty, tall without being imposing, with the kind of careful face that seems designed by someone who expected it to be examined closely. His uniform was immaculate in a way that struck me as almost apologetic — as though he kept it perfect precisely so no one could find fault with anything else about him.

He stood at the entrance of our converted convent ward with a ledger of his own — oh, the irony of that, though I did not yet know how sharp the joke would become — and a pencil worn down to half its length.

"Lieutenant Brenner," he said, and offered a slight bow, the kind you give an equal rather than an occupied civilian, and I noted the distinction the way one notes a bird singing out of season. "I'm to inventory the hospital. Beds, linens, instruments, stores. It should take no more than a day. I'll try to be underfoot as little as possible."

"Underfoot is unavoidable, Lieutenant. This whole hospital is underfoot."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile's cousin.

"Then I'll be unavoidable quietly," he said.

He began at the north end of the ward, and I will grant him this: I have watched men count before, quartermasters and paymasters and officers taking stock of their dead, and none of them counted like Aurel Brenner. Most men counting want to finish. They tally in their heads while their eyes are already moving to the next thing. Brenner counted as though the numbers mattered personally — as though each blanket had been woven specifically for his inspection, and it would be an insult to guess at its threadbareness rather than verify it with his fingers.

He counted twice. I saw him do it, on the crutches, on the surgical needles, on the tins of broth in Marguerite's pantry. Count once, write, pause, count again, correct or confirm. Tomas, ferrying basins past him, whispered to me in passing: "He does everything twice. I think he doesn't trust the world to stay put between one look and the next."

Which was, I would come to understand, the truest thing anyone said about Lieutenant Brenner that week.

* * *

By mid-afternoon he had worked his way down to the long row of beds nearest the windows, where our sickest men lay, and among them Private Anselm Ferrec, who was well enough now to be bored, which in a convalescent ward is nearly as dangerous as fever.

Brenner stopped at the foot of Anselm's bed and consulted his papers.

"Private Anselm Ferrec, Fourth Company, transferred from the field hospital at—" He paused. Frowned faintly at the page. "Transferred from the field hospital at Marrecourt, the eleventh of November."

"That's right," Anselm said, in the voice of a man being reasonable with a customs official.

"Your transfer paper lists your wound as a shell fragment, left thigh. But the surgeon's register here says *and right hand*, burns." Brenner looked up. "Which is it?"

"Both," Anselm said.

"Then someone's paperwork is wrong."

"Somebody's paperwork is always wrong, Lieutenant. Usually mine."

Another man might have laughed, or bristled. Brenner did neither. He wrote something down — I watched the pencil move twice over the same line — and said, quietly, "I'll have it corrected. You shouldn't be listed as less wounded than you are. That's how men lose pensions."

And then he moved on, and Anselm caught my eye across the ward with an expression of pure astonishment, and mouthed: *Who is this fellow?*

I did not yet have an answer.

But I noticed things, because noticing was my profession as much as bandaging was. I noticed that Brenner never looked at the wounded men with the flat appraisal soldiers learn, the look that measures a body for its future use. He looked at them the way a man looks into shop windows after closing — wanting something he could not ask for. I noticed that when he reached Private Lohan's bed — Lohan being nineteen and delirious and calling for a grandmother who had died the previous spring — Brenner stood a moment longer than inventory required, and his hand rose halfway toward the boy's forehead before he remembered himself and turned it into an adjustment of his own collar.

And I noticed the photograph.

It lived in the breast pocket of his tunic, and it came out perhaps forty times a day. Each time, he would glance at it quickly, the way other men check a watch — a flick of the eyes downward, a half-second, done. But near evening, as the light went gold and low through the convent windows and he sat at the little desk by the chapel door making his final tallies, the flick became a gaze. He held the photograph in both hands, this officer who counted everything twice, and he looked at it the way you look at a sum that refuses to balance.

He sensed me watching. To his credit, he did not pretend otherwise.

"Do you know many of their families?" he asked, nodding toward the ward. "The men. Do you write to them?"

"I write to some. When there's someone to write to."

"My mother writes to every family in our village that's lost a son." He said it evenly, as a fact. "She's run out of envelopes. She's begun using the backs of old calendar pages." He turned the photograph toward me, politely, the way one shows a picture of a nephew at christening. "My brother."

A young man in dress uniform, squinting against sunlight, grinning with the unguarded delight of someone who has not yet learned what armies do to grinners. There was a family resemblance in the jaw, in the careful hands. The photograph was captioned along its bottom edge in faded pencil, in a round, cheerful hand:

*Kaspar — Leipzig, before everything.*

"Kaspar Brenner," he said. "Listed missing since August. At—"

He told me the place. A crossroads village whose name I have carried ever since like a stone in the boot, and I felt the stone drop even as he spoke, because I knew that name. Not the way you know a name from a newspaper. The way you know it from ink, from your own hand, from the particular weight of having written it down at two in the morning by lamplight while a man's breathing slowed around the syllables.

Kaspar Brenner was in my ledger.

Page eleven. Between a farm boy from the Aisne and an unnamed chasseur whom no one had ever claimed. *K. Brenner, corporal, wounded at the crossroads, brought in by retreating column, died of his wounds the following morning. Buried behind the chapel at Saint-Lô-des-Prés. Gave his watch to the man in the next bed and asked him to tell his mother the calendar pages weren't her fault.*

I knew it nearly word for word. I had written it.

Here is the thing about recognition: it happens faster than decency. Decency requires seconds; recognition requires none. Between his saying *missing since August* and my face doing whatever my face did, there was no room for the composure Marguerite had prescribed. I felt my expression move — some small seismic event, a settling of features — and I watched him see it.

He was a man who counted twice. He did not miss it.

"Nurse?" he said. Carefully. The word itself was a question.

"You remind me of him," I said, which was true, and safe, and a lie in every way that mattered. "Not in the face. In the manner. We had a corporal pass through in the autumn — a Leipzig man, cheerful sort, always organizing the other patients' letters by postcode." I kept my eyes on the lint tray I was sorting. My hands, thank God, have always been steadier than the rest of me. "He reminds me of you."

"Was he treated here? Is he—"

"He was moved on," I said. Which was also true, in the sense that all of us are eventually moved on.

Brenner looked at me for a long moment. I have been looked at by doctors, by colonels, by dying men, and I will tell you that none of those looks required as much of me as that one. He was deciding something. I could feel the decision happening, the way you feel weather change — pressure shifting, some internal barometer ticking. A man who counts twice does not accept a single reading of a face. He takes a second measurement.

Whatever his second measurement returned, he folded it away with the photograph.

"Thank you," he said, "for telling me. Even a rumor of a name is more than the records give." He gathered his pencils, aligned them parallel with a precision that was somehow heartbreaking. "The records are very thorough about what was expended, Nurse Marchand. Shells, rations, telegraph wire. Very thorough." He closed his ledger. "They are remarkably silent about what was *lost*."

"There's a difference?"

"In the army's arithmetic, there is." He said it without bitterness, which frightened me more than bitterness would have. Bitterness is a fire you can see. This was ash.

He bowed again — that small, incorrect, egalitarian bow — and left through the chapel door, and I listened to his footsteps cross the cloister, even and unhurried, counting themselves.

* * *

That night I did something I had not done since the ledger began: I read myself to sleep with my own book. I took it from beneath the loose floorboard beneath the third pew — Marguerite called it my confessional, which was funnier than she knew — and I sat in the dark of the dormitory with a shawl around my shoulders and turned to page eleven.

*K. Brenner, corporal.*

I had recorded thirty-eight names by then. Deserters shot at dawn whose reports said *executed for discipline, details enclosed*, except the details never were. Civilians caught in crossfires filed as *collateral losses, regrettable*. Boys like Lohan whose paperwork hadn't caught up with their deaths. Each one entered in my cramped, steady hand with everything the official record omitted: what they said, what they asked for, whom they forgave.

It had started as routine. That is the confession I have never spoken aloud: it started as nothing nobler than habit. A name omitted is a small hole in the world, and I am a person who mends things for a living, and so I mended, night after night, in ink, in secret, and told myself it was only bookkeeping.

Sitting there in the dark with Kaspar Brenner's entry open on my knees, I understood for the first time that it was not bookkeeping. Bookkeeping can be surrendered. On Friday, Colonel Vasseur's order would require every document in Saint-Aubin to be handed over — registers, letters, ledgers, everything — and if my book went into that pile, it would not merely disappear. It would be *corrected*. Thirty-eight names would become zero names, tidily, officially, twice-counted and confirmed.

And one of those names belonged to the brother of a man who counted everything twice, who adjusted collars instead of touching fevered foreheads, who wanted so badly to believe the records could be made honest that he corrected strangers' paperwork out of sheer stubborn hope.

If he ever learned what I held — that his brother had not vanished into the clean anonymity of *missing* but had died slowly, kindly, watch-to-watch, in a chapel bed with a nurse recording his last words — he would not get it from the army. The army had already decided what happened to Kaspar Brenner, which was nothing.

He could only get it from me.

From my book. From the contraband sleeping under the floorboard of a requisitioned church, in a town the army was about to strip of every scrap of paper, guarded only by a cook, an orderly, a deserter with a burned hand, and a nurse whose face, I now understood, had already told a meticulous man that something was being kept from him.

I slid the ledger back beneath the floorboard and pressed the wood down until it seated with a soft click, and I thought: *Friday.*

Four days.

Somewhere across the cloister, in the officers' quarters, Lieutenant Aurel Brenner was presumably aligning his pencils and looking at a photograph and wondering — twice, because that was how he wondered — why a nurse's face had flinched at the name Kaspar.

I got into bed and lay listening to the wind work at the broken clock in the convent tower, which had not struck since the occupation began, and I thought about how long four days could be, and how short, and how a single flinch of a face could undo in one second what four years of dullness had built.

In the morning, Marguerite would want to know how the counting man had gone.

I would have to tell her he had counted everything twice.

Except, of course, the one thing I wished he'd never counted at all.
