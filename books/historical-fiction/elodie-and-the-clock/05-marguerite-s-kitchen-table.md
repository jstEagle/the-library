## 5. Marguerite's Kitchen Table

Marguerite Bonnet had fed generals, deserters, one bishop, and — during the first summer of the war, a story she told only when the bread was rising and nobody was in a hurry — a traveling circus whose acrobats had eaten her out of an entire week's flour. She had learned long ago that the truest conversations in any house happened at the kitchen table, usually after the third helping, when people stopped performing themselves and became simply hungry, tired human beings.

So it did not surprise her at all that the most dangerous conversation of her life began with soup.

It was Thursday, which meant white beans, because Thursday had meant white beans since before Elodie Marchand was born, and if the war was going to take everything else from Saint-Aubin it would not take her Thursdays. The big pot sat in the middle of the table like a guest of honor, breathing steam, and around it sat the strangest family she had ever collected: Tomas, her orderly, all elbows and earnestness, tearing bread he wasn't eating; Anselm, the wounded private, propped on his crutch with his bandaged shoulder angled carefully away from the wall; and Elodie, who had come down from the ward looking the way Marguerite imagined women looked when they carried something heavy inside them for too long — not bent, exactly, but arranged very precisely so nothing showed.

Marguerite ladled the soup. Waited. Cooks learn to wait.

"Vasseur's men finished the church records today," Tomas said finally, to nobody, to the steam.

"I saw," said Elodie.

"And the school registers."

"Yes."

"They took Madame Ferrand's letters. All of them. From her sons."

"I know, Tomas." Elodie turned her spoon over in her hands as though she'd never seen one before. "I surrendered my own correspondence this morning. Two letters from my aunt in Rouen. I read them one last time in line, and then I gave them to a boy who couldn't have been more than seventeen, and he stacked them in a crate like firewood."

The silence that followed had texture to it. Marguerite let it sit, the way you let dough rest.

It was Anselm who broke it, quietly, watching Elodie. "There's something else. Something that can't go in a crate."

Elodie didn't answer. But her hand moved — just slightly, just once — toward the pocket of her apron, and then away again, quick as a mouse crossing a road.

Marguerite saw it. Marguerite missed almost nothing that happened in her kitchen; it was the one territory in France still entirely under her command. And she had known about Elodie's little book for longer than anyone would have guessed, because a cook knows where things are hidden in a house the way a mother knows where children are hiding — by instinct, by the small wrongnesses in a room. The hymnal in the chapel that was always slightly fatter than hymnals ought to be. The way Elodie sometimes stood at night by the window with a pencil stub, moving her lips, counting.

She had said nothing. Some things a house keeps for its people.

"The lieutenant," Anselm said. "Brenner. He inventoried the dispensary today. He counted the morphine twice."

"He counts everything twice," Tomas said. "He counted the spoons."

"I know he did," said Marguerite, and they all looked at her, because she had complained about it loudly at the time. "Forty-one spoons. He wrote forty-one. Then he counted again and made me say it out loud." She sniffed. "I've met his kind before. Men who count because counting is the only thing standing between them and weeping."

"That's the problem," Elodie said. Very low. Her spoon went down flat on the table, deliberate, like a card played. "That's the problem, and I've been carrying it alone, and I'm tired, and I'm frightened, and I've decided I'd rather be frightened in company."

Tomas stopped tearing bread.

Elodie reached into her apron and set the ledger on the table beside the bean pot.

Now, Marguerite had imagined the book many times — a fat thing, surely, a great incriminating brick of paper. It was smaller than that. A plain exercise book, gray cover gone soft at the corners, swollen slightly from damp, tied shut with a length of surgical thread. It looked like nothing. It looked like a grocery list. That, Marguerite thought, was somehow the worst part: that all those names could look so ordinary.

"In here," Elodie said, "are two hundred and thirty-one names. Soldiers the reports left off. Civilians shot in the crossfire last spring whose deaths were recorded as 'fever.' Boys who died before their papers arrived, so officially they never existed at all. Deserters executed at dawn and entered as 'lost to enemy action.'" Her voice stayed level; her knuckles did not. "And on Friday, Vasseur's men are inventorying the convent. Every room. Every book."

* * *

For a long moment the only sound was the pot settling, that tick and sigh of cooling metal that kitchens make when they think nobody's listening.

Then Tomas, bless him forever, said: "Two hundred and thirty-one," in the voice of a man doing arithmetic he intends to act on. "That's more than the official register for the whole sector."

"It should be read," Elodie said. "At Verrières. By the armistice commission, before they sign whatever they're going to sign. Because if those names aren't in the record when the ink dries, they'll be lost. Officially. Forever." She looked down at the ledger without touching it. "I keep thinking — I'm a nurse. My whole work is keeping people alive. This is the only way I know how to keep them that way. On paper."

"You could burn it," Anselm said, but gently, testing, the way you test a bridge before you ask anyone to cross it.

"I could," Elodie agreed. "I've thought about it every night since the requisition order. Burning it would be safe." She raised her eyes. "But safe isn't why I started writing it."

Marguerite stood up. Not because she had a plan yet, but because some moments required a woman to be on her feet. She went to the window out of habit — the habit of twenty years, checking the lane, checking the light, checking whether anyone was coming up the road who would need feeding — and found the lane empty, gray with early dusk, and the fog coming off the river in that slow way it had lately, as though even the weather were trying to keep secrets.

"There are three checkpoints between here and Verrières," she said, still facing the glass. "Everyone knows it. The mill bridge, the salt road, and the chapel gate. Since the requisition, nothing moves through any of them without papers, and papers are checked against lists, and lists are kept by Brenner, who counts twice." She turned back to the table. "You cannot carry that book in your coat. You cannot carry it in a cart under turnips. You cannot post it, throw it in the river to float downstream — I see you thinking about the river, Tomas, don't pretend — because the current goes the wrong way and there's a net at the weir anyway."

"How do you know there's a net at the weir?" Tomas asked.

"Because I feed the man who maintains it. Soup travels faster than soldiers, and it carries more information." She came back to the table and laid both hands flat on the wood, one on either side of the bean pot, framing the ledger like a judge framing a verdict. "No. If that book is going to travel, it has to travel the one way no soldier wants to inspect too closely."

They waited. Even the pot seemed to wait.

"A coffin," said Marguerite.

Tomas blinked. Anselm sat up straighter despite the shoulder. Elodie said, "Whose?" — which, Marguerite thought later, was such a nurse's question, practical to the bone, that she nearly laughed right there in the middle of the treason.

"Nobody's," she said. "That's the beauty of it. Old Mother Aumont died three weeks ago and she's in the ground behind the chapel, God rest her, but her coffin was built by Dubreuil's boys, and it was built well, because Dubreuil never built anything badly in his life. More to the point —" she held up one finger — "her family has been petitioning the garrison for a fortnight to move her remains to the family plot at Ferrières, outside the lines, next to her husband. There's paperwork. Approved paperwork. Signed by a captain whose name I happen to know because he eats here every second Tuesday and complains about the onions."

"You want to exhume Mother Aumont," Tomas said slowly, "and put Elodie's book in her coffin."

"I want," said Marguerite, "to put the book beneath a decent woman who spent eighty-one years being overlooked by everyone including her own parish, and let her do one last useful thing. She'd have volunteered if we'd asked her. She was that sort of nuisance." She lifted the lid off the pot and began ladling bowls, because the argument was won and bodies needed feeding regardless. "A coffin going to a family burial outside the lines gets an escort pass. Grief is the one cargo no sergeant wants to rummage through. They'll lift the lid — maybe — look at poor old Aumont's face, cross themselves, and wave us through with their eyes watering. Nobody searches death thoroughly. Death has better lawyers than we do."

"It's brilliant," said Tomas.

"It's blasphemous," said Anselm, almost admiringly.

"It's neither, it's Thursday," said Marguerite, setting a bowl in front of him hard enough to slop. "Eat. Blasphemy gives you appetite."

* * *

Elodie hadn't touched her soup. She was looking at the ledger, and then at Marguerite, and Marguerite watched the exact moment the fear in the girl's face rearranged itself into something harder and cleaner. Resolve. Marguerite had seen it before, on young men leaving for the front, on her sister the year of the floods, on herself the day she'd buried her husband and gotten up the next morning anyway and baked sixty rolls for the neighbors because grief, unattended, turns to rot, but grief given a task turns to iron.

"The burial detail needs a mourner," Marguerite went on, businesslike now, ticking points off with her ladle. "Not too important a mourner — not the mother, that draws questions — but someone. A cousin. A cousin from out of town, who wouldn't be recognized at the checkpoints. Someone with steady hands and a good excuse to be pale."

"Cousins can't escort coffins past the lines," Anselm objected. "Family can."

"Which is why the cousin will be a cousin by marriage, twice removed, on the Aumont side, and the papers will say so, because I will write the papers myself and Captain Onion-Complaints will sign them on Tuesday because he always does when I put a little extra crust on his plate." She pointed the ladle at Elodie. "You'd wear black. You already look half-dead from the night shifts, no offense intended, dear, it's the honest truth and it will help us. You'd ride in the mule cart with the coffin and weep if anyone looks at you, which shouldn't strain you, because I suspect you're holding back an ocean as it is."

"And the other two checkpoints?" Elodie asked. "If the mill bridge is the first—"

"One thing at a time. First the mill bridge, Saturday, dawn, while the fog is thick. Fog is free and God sends it to the deserving." Marguerite finally sat down, took up her own bowl, and ate three decisive mouthfuls before speaking again. "We'll manage the salt road and the gate when the bridge is behind us. A plan that tries to solve three problems at once solves none. My husband used to say it about the harvest: get the wheat in before you worry about the apples."

"And Brenner?" The word came from Anselm, quiet and careful. "He counts twice. What happens when he counts the mourners?"

The candle guttered. Outside, somewhere across the dark square, a patrol's boots went by in rhythm, four beats and a pause, four beats and a pause, the war keeping time like a metronome nobody had asked for.

"Brenner," Marguerite said, "is a man who counts things twice because he doesn't trust what they tell him the first time. That's a fault in an officer." She looked around the table, at each of them in turn — Tomas with his flour-dusted sleeves rolled up, Anselm with his soldier's jaw set stubborn, Elodie with her hand resting on the ledger as if it were a pulse she was monitoring. "But it might also be a mercy. Because a man who doesn't trust the first count may eventually notice that the official numbers don't add up. And men who notice things, my dears, are men who can be shown things."

"You sound very sure of him," Elodie said.

"I'm sure of nobody," Marguerite answered. "I'm seventy years old. Surety is for girls and fools. But I am sure of this table, and these four chairs, and what we've decided sitting in them." She pushed back from the board and stood, and the others rose with her, awkward, half-laughing at themselves, four conspirators suddenly formal over cold beans. "Saturday, dawn. Tomas finds us a mule and a cart that doesn't squeak — grease the axle twice, I mean it, that animal you borrowed in October sang the whole way to the orchard. Anselm, you speak to Dubreuil's boys about the coffin lid needing to open and close politely. Elodie—" 

Elodie was folding the ledger into a clean cloth, unhurried, precise, a nurse wrapping something fragile.

"—you practice looking like a grieving cousin, which I suspect requires less practice than you'd hope."

"And you?" Elodie asked. "What will you be doing while I'm riding out with Mother Aumont?"

Marguerite gathered the bowls. Behind her the fire settled, and the kitchen did what her kitchen had always done: held its heat, held its people, held its tongue.

"I," she said, "will be baking. For the garrison. Extra crust on everything. Because the surest way to move a coffin through a war, my darlings, is to make certain every man guarding the road owes you bread."

She was scraping the pot when Anselm spoke again, and something in his voice made her stop with the ladle still in hand.

"There's something you should know before Saturday," he said. "About why my name might matter at a checkpoint. About a name in your book, Elodie. Mine."

He looked, Marguerite thought, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was there.

"Sit down," she said, pulling out his chair. "Whatever it is, it'll keep better with soup in you."
