## 6. Anselm's Confession

The first thing Anselm Fenn learned about walking again was that his legs had filed a formal complaint and were refusing arbitration.

He made it four steps from his cot to the window before the ward spun like Marguerite's butter churn, and he had to grip the sill and pretend, very loudly with his whole body, that he had only come over to look at the weather. The weather was rain. It was always rain. He suspected the sky of doing it out of sympathy for the town.

"You'll want to do that in stages," said Tomas, appearing at his elbow with the particular gentleness of a man who has caught someone falling many times and no longer remarks upon it. "Like a ladder. Legs first. Then the rest of you."

"I *was* doing it in stages," Anselm said. "Stage one: dignity. Stage two: collapse."

"Ah, you've done both already. You're ahead of schedule." Tomas tucked a blanket around his shoulders without asking, the way mothers and orderlies do, as though the patient were furniture that felt cold. "Sister Elodie says ten steps today. She wrote it down. That means it's law."

Everything Sister Elodie wrote down became law; Anselm had gathered this within a week of waking up in the convent hospital with a hole in his shoulder and no memory of how he'd acquired either. She kept her little ledger close and her pen closer, and when she wrote your name in it you felt *counted*, in the good way — the way a baker counts loaves, not the way a tax man counts sheep.

He did not know yet that he was in that book. But we are getting ahead of ourselves, and so was Anselm, who by noon had achieved eleven steps and considered himself a conqueror of nations.

* * *

By the following week he could walk the length of the ward and back, pausing twice — once at Private Hollar's bed to lose convincingly at cards, once at the window to watch the requisitioned town go about its requisitioned business. Soldiers in Vasseur's grey-blue moved crates through the square. The townsfolk moved past them with the smooth, empty faces of people pretending not to be home.

"You walk like a man with somewhere to be," Marguerite observed, delivering his lunch tray with the ceremony of a state banquet. The tray held soup, bread, and one small pastry that officially did not exist. "In my experience, men with somewhere to be are either in love or in trouble."

"Couldn't I be both?"

"Not on one pastry, you couldn't."

He ate the pastry slowly. It was apricot. It tasted like a version of himself he'd almost forgotten — a boy at a fair, some years and one catastrophic war ago. He set the little paper wrapper flat and folded it into quarters, then eighths, until it was a hard little square he could hide inside his fist.

That was the trouble, see. His hands still hid things automatically. They'd been doing it since the night he'd walked away.

* * *

Elodie found him at the ward's end that evening, where he'd stationed himself in the one chair with a broken leg propped on a hymnal — an irony that would become apparent to him later and make him laugh in a hollow sort of way — watching the last light fail over the chapel roof.

"You're stronger," she said. She sat across from him, ledger on her knee out of habit even now, off duty, the pen tucked behind her ear like a carpenter's pencil.

"Strong enough," he said. And then, because the light was going and cowardice needs darkness to grow, he heard himself add, "Sister. There's something you should know about me. Before it knows about you."

She waited. She was good at waiting. He'd watched her wait through fevers, through last words, through Private Hollar's forty-minute explanation of why the cards had arranged themselves unfairly.

"My name isn't Anselm Rook," he said. "It's Anselm Fenn. Rook is — Rook is what they put on the casualty list at Kessel Field. Two years ago this autumn."

Elodie's pen came down slowly from behind her ear.

"I deserted," he said. It came out smaller than he'd rehearsed. All the rehearsals, in the latrine, in the stairwell, into the rain — none of them had prepared him for the size of the word spoken aloud in a quiet room to a quiet woman. "I deserted at Kessel Field. My company was ordered up a slope that didn't have a top so much as a meat grinder, and I went twenty yards up it, and my friend Joss went thirty, and then there wasn't any difference between going and staying except that going meant living long enough to be ashamed. So I went. I ran. I walked all night and hid in a culvert and told myself I'd turn myself in in the morning, and then morning came and I discovered I was a liar with good legs."

He unfolded the pastry wrapper in his fist without noticing he was doing it, smoothing it flat on his knee like a tiny map of nowhere.

"They reported me dead," he went on. "Joss saw me fall — I did fall, that part's true, I took shrapnel in the leg — and he reported it, God bless him, probably to give my running some dignity after the fact. And the army, which can lose a colonel in a drawer, lost me entirely. Dead at Kessel Field. When I finally dragged myself to a garrison six weeks later, half-starved with a rotten leg, the clerk looked me up, found Anselm Rook deceased, and panicked. A dead man reporting for duty is paperwork nobody wants. So they shipped me to a rear hospital under the dead name, patched the leg, and when I healed they handed me a rifle and pointed me at the enemy again, same as before, except now I was a ghost. Desertion pardoned — well, *forgotten*. Which is different. Pardon means they decided you were innocent. Forgetting means they decided you were convenient."

The room had gone very still. Rain ticked at the glass.

"Why tell me now?" Elodie asked. Not unkindly. Curiously, the way she asked everything — as if the answer might be interesting rather than damning.

"Because of what you write in that book." He nodded at the ledger. "Don't look like that, Sister, Tomas talks in his sleep and Marguerite talks awake, and neither of them can keep a secret from me because I'm very likable. You keep the names. The ones the reports leave out. The deserters shot at dawn, the boys without papers, the civilians in the crossfire. Someone has to say their names eventually or the war gets to decide they never existed." He took a breath. It hurt, pleasantly, the way honest things sometimes do. "I think I'm in your book. Anselm Rook, died at Kessel Field, autumn, two years ago. I've been dead in your handwriting for two years, and it seemed time somebody corrected the record."

Elodie opened the ledger.

Anselm had imagined this moment — he'd imagined most of his moments lately, being a man with abundant guilt and limited mobility — but nothing had quite prepared him for seeing his own false name in a stranger's careful script, dated, annotated, mourned in advance by a woman who hadn't known him. *Rook, A., Pvt., Kessel Field.* Beside it, in her smaller marginal hand: *no family listed. no grave confirmed.*

"No family listed," she read aloud softly.

"That's wrong, actually," Anselm said. "There's a mother, in Marren. She got a letter saying I died. She'll have grieved me. She may be grieving me still, depending on whether the second letter ever arrived telling her I was alive — and I doubt it did, because the second letter would've required somebody to care, and caring costs ink." His voice cracked on the last of it, which embarrassed him, so he covered it with a laugh that fooled nobody, least of all himself. "So there it is. Your book has a mistake in it, Sister. One of your names is alive, sitting in your broken chair, eating contraband pastries."

"It isn't a mistake." Elodie closed the ledger gently, the way she closed eyes. "A name recorded is not a verdict. It's a question. *Who was this? What happened?* You're the answer arriving late, that's all." She looked at him for a long moment. "Why tell *me*? Tomas knew, presumably. Marguerite. They didn't turn you in."

"Tomas and Marguerite forgive easily. It's their whole vocation, practically." He leaned forward, and the chair complained, and he ignored it because some things required a man to lean. "But you're making a record. And a record deserves the truth while it can still get some. Besides—" and here the thing underneath the thing surfaced, the real engine of the confession, the part he'd been hauling uphill for two years— "I'm sick of being Rook. Rook ran. Rook is a name the army gave a corpse. My mother named me Fenn, after her father, a stonemason who built walls that are probably still standing, and I would like — before whatever happens next happens — to be him again. Out loud. To witnesses."

The rain kept its soft applause.

"Well then, Private Fenn," Elodie said, and there was warmth in it, and something else too, a kind of gravity, as if his confession had weight and she'd accepted delivery of it. "Consider yourself witnessed."

* * *

He expected relief. Everyone promises you relief, like it comes free with confession, included in the price. What arrived instead, that night, was rage — at the army, at the clerks, at Kessel Field, mostly at himself — followed in the grey hours by something quieter and more dangerous: purpose. It sat on his chest like a cat. It would not be ignored.

He was up before the ward, testing the legs, and found they answered better than usual, as if shame had been ballast he could finally jettison. He dressed. He walked — unassisted, ten steps, fifteen, a scandal — down to the kitchen, where Marguerite was committing crimes against onions and humming something liturgical.

"You look terrible," she said fondly. "Sit."

"I need to talk to all of you. Properly. About the book."

Marguerite's knife stopped mid-onion.

They assembled — Elodie, Tomas, Marguerite — in the kitchen's steam and lamplight, and Anselm stood, because sitting felt wrong for what he had to say, and told them what he'd already told Elodie, and watched their faces do the journey from surprise to sorrow to something firmer. Then he told them the new thing. The thing that had grown in him overnight like a seed in a wet coat pocket.

"The ledger has to reach Verrières. Before the signing. And it can't be you, Sister." He said it gently, because she was already shaking her head. "Vasseur's men know your face now — Brenner inventoried this hospital room by room. If the book is searched and traced to you, you lose everything, and the book with it. But me?" He spread his hands. "I'm already dead. Officially. A dead man can't be punished twice, and more importantly, a dead man isn't on anyone's lists. No duty roster. No checkpoint description. Rook doesn't exist, so Rook can walk anywhere." He paused. "And Fenn owes a debt. I walked away from Kessel Field while better men stayed. Every name in that book is a man or woman who stayed, in one way or another, and got erased for it. Let me carry them. Let me finish the walk."

"Absolutely not," said Marguerite, with the full authority of the onion.

"You can barely cross a ward," said Tomas.

"A week ago," Anselm agreed. "I've been training. Ask anyone. I'm practically cavalry." He looked at Elodie, because she was the one whose opinion actually governed, and found her studying him with that waiting expression, the pen behind her ear, the ledger hugged to her chest like a child.

"You understand," she said slowly, "that if you're caught with it, they won't shoot a dead man twice. They'll just correct the record properly this time."

"Yes."

"And that you'd be carrying other people's deaths along with yours."

"That's rather the point, Sister." He softened his voice. "You taught me that. Names aren't verdicts. Well — mine isn't either. Let me make it mean something before I hand it back."

The kettle chose that moment to sing, high and absurd, and Marguerite swore at it with deep feeling, and for a moment the kitchen was just a kitchen, warm and steaming and ordinary, and Anselm loved every splinter of it with a fierceness that frightened him a little. This was what he'd run toward that night from Kessel Field, he realized. Not away from the guns — toward *this*. A warm room. People who argued with you about your own suicide mission over breakfast. He'd been walking toward it for two years by every wrong road available.

"Three checkpoints," Elodie said at last, quietly, to the table rather than to him, and he understood that he'd won, though she hadn't said so and might never say so outright. "Mill bridge. The salt road. The chapel gate. And Vasseur's patrols between them."

"I've been dead two years," Anselm said. "I'm very hard to stop."

Tomas laughed despite himself. Marguerite threw up her hands and returned to the onions with renewed violence, muttering that she'd bake provisions for the road, since clearly no one in this convent intended to survive on sensible decisions.

Only Elodie still watched him, and there was something in her face he couldn't read — not pity, not fear. Recognition, maybe. As if she'd seen, standing in her kitchen in borrowed boots, the exact shape of the question her ledger had been asking all along: *who was this?*

Later that afternoon, she brought him the ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth, and put it in his hands like something born rather than written.

"There's a page in there you should read before you go," she said. "Kessel Field. It isn't only your name."

He unwrapped it. Found the date. Found *Rook, A., Pvt.* — and beside it, in that same careful script, a line he hadn't noticed before, because grief had been shouting too loudly the night she'd shown him:

*Ferran, J., Cpl. Kessel Field. Held the slope longer than ordered. No grave confirmed.*

Joss. Reported dead holding the ground Anselm had run from.

His hands went still around the oilcloth.

"Sister," he said, and his voice had gone somewhere flat and far away, "when exactly did Joss Ferran die? Because the report I heard — the rumor that reached the rear hospital — said he survived Kessel Field."

Elodie frowned. "Then someone's records disagree," she said, "and I'd very much like to know whose."
