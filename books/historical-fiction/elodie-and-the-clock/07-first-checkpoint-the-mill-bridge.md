## 7. First Checkpoint: The Mill Bridge

The coffin was real. That was the first mercy of the morning, and Elodie found she needed to hold on to it like a handrail.

Old Madame Roux had died on Tuesday of her heart, at eighty-one, in her bed, with a clean nightdress and a granddaughter holding her hand — a death so ordinary by the standards of the past four years that it felt almost indecent. Her family, what remained of them, had agreed to bury her in the churchyard at Fontenay, two miles past the mill bridge, where her sister lived and where, coincidentally, the road continued on toward Verrières for anyone with business further afield. The army permitted funerals outside the lines. The army was sentimental about grandmothers in a way it had never been about anyone else.

So Elodie rode in a cart beside a coffin that actually contained a beloved dead woman, wearing an actual black shawl borrowed from Marguerite's own closet — "I keep three," Marguerite had said darkly, "at my age you learn to be prepared" — and wept genuine tears when required, because half of them were not performance at all.

"You're crying for her?" Anselm had asked before they left, nodding at the coffin as he'd helped load it. He walked now with only the smallest hitch in his stride, which was either recovery or stubbornness, and in Anselm those were historically the same thing.

"I'm crying for everyone," Elodie said. "It saves time."

Marguerite had approved of this plan the way she approved of most things: loudly, and with pastry. "A widow escorting her cousin's coffin," she'd declared over the kitchen table, flour to her elbows, "is the most respectable creature in Christendom. No one searches a widow. No one searches a coffin. Grief is better than papers." She had then packed Elodie a satchel of food substantial enough to suggest she expected the journey to involve either a long wait or a war, and pressed six eggs into Anselm's hands "for the road," though Anselm was not going anywhere near the bridge; his face was too well known to one sergeant already, or would be, later, on the salt road. That part of the plan belonged to him alone, and Elodie was doing her best not to think about it.

Tomas had done the last thing. He had taken the hymnal apart at four in the morning with a penknife and a pot of glue and infinite patience, and sewn the ledger flat against the underside of the coffin lid, between the wood and the padded lining where Madame Roux's shoulders would rest. "She won't mind," Tomas said. "A woman who kept her accounts in order her whole life — she'll appreciate carrying something important." Then he had looked at Elodie with those serious brown eyes and said, quietly, so Marguerite wouldn't hear from the doorway: "If they open it —"

"They won't open it."

"If they open it," he repeated, because Tomas did not let people off hooks, "you are a cousin. You are grieving. You know nothing about any book. You've never kept a ledger in your life, you can barely add." This was untrue and he knew it was untrue and he delivered it anyway, straight-faced, and Elodie had laughed despite everything, a short ugly laugh that scared a pigeon off the convent roof, and that, more than anything, had steadied her hands.

Now the cart creaked along the mill road, and the winter morning was pale and still, and the driver — old Perrin, who had driven the town's dead to Fontenay for forty years and considered armies a vulgar interruption of his trade — sat up front making small disapproving sounds at the state of the road.

"The bridge is ahead," Perrin said, without turning around. He knew exactly why she was there. Everyone knew. The whole town had conspired around this coffin like water closing over a stone. "There'll be two soldiers, maybe three. They've been stopping carts since Thursday. Don't volunteer anything. Widows who talk too much get widows searched."

"Thank you, Perrin."

"And don't look at the mill. The mill burned in October and everyone who passes looks at it, and the soldiers have started searching carts out of boredom." He clucked to the mule. "Grief, girl. Only grief. Keep your eyes wet and your mouth sorry."

Elodie looked down at her lap, where her hands lay folded over a handkerchief embroidered with someone else's initials — Marguerite's sister, perhaps, or a husband; she hadn't asked — and practiced being a woman whose worst burden was a box of bones and a long walk home.

It should have been easy. She had spent four years being invisible. Nurses are furniture; nurses are walls; soldiers talk in front of them as though the veil and apron render a person deaf. She had built a whole life in the spaces other people didn't look at, and the ledger had grown fat in those spaces, page after page of names no report wanted: Private Delacour, shot at dawn, no offense recorded. The boy Vermand, fourteen, who lied about his age twice — once to the recruiting office, once to God. Madame Ossart, caught in the crossfire hanging her washing. Forty-one names, then fifty, then sixty-three, each one entered in her careful schoolroom hand while the officers' reports above hers counted men the way a miller counts sacks.

All of it was under her hands now, sewn into a coffin lid, riding beneath a dead grandmother's honest shoulders toward three checkpoints and a commission that could either read those names into history or sign a peace that pretended they'd never existed.

The bridge came into view through the bare trees.

The mill itself was a black ruin on the far bank, its wheel collapsed into the shallows like a dropped coin, smoke-stain fanned up the stone in a great grey wing. Elodie caught herself looking — corrected herself — looked down at the handkerchief instead. *Grief, girl.*

"Stop here," said a voice. "Cart. Stop."

Perrin reined in. Boots crunched on frost.

Elodie raised her eyes the way she imagined a tired widow would — slowly, unwillingly, as if lifting them cost money. There were indeed three soldiers, young ones, their breath standing around their heads like little ghosts queuing for confession. And behind them, standing slightly apart at the bridge's near end with a clipboard under one arm and his collar buttoned to the top of a cold morning —

Lieutenant Aurel Brenner.

Her heart did something unhelpful. She told it firmly about Madame Roux, about grief, about respectable sorrow, and made her face obey.

*He counts everything twice.* That was what the orderlies said, back at the hospital, said with a shrug that wasn't quite mockery. Brenner inventories blanket by blanket, bottle by bottle; Brenner finds the arithmetic errors nobody else bothers to make. A meticulous man. A haunted man — she had seen the photograph once, when he'd flipped his wallet open for his requisition book in the convent ward and left it open a heartbeat too long: two young men, arms around each other's necks, grinning with the awful confidence of people who do not yet know which of them the war has marked.

She knew the name in her ledger. She knew it the way she knew all sixty-eight entries, in the place where names live once written: Corporal Emil Brenner, missing, Belan ridge, October of the year before last, unit abandoned in the retreat, reported by Sergeant Pelay, who wept while he spoke it and asked her to write *he stayed behind with the wounded* in the margin, because reports would never say it and somebody should.

She had never been able to learn what happened after. That page had an empty bottom, and the lieutenant standing twenty feet away carried the same surname across his own chest.

"Business at the crossing," said the youngest soldier, a boy with chapped lips and a rifle he held like a shovel.

"Funeral," Perrin said, in the voice he probably used for weather complaints. "Madame Roux, of Saint-Aubin. Burial at Fontenay. Papers are in order, if your lieutenant wants to read them, which I expect he does, because he reads everything."

Brenner approached. Up close he had the pallor of a man who worked indoors over figures and slept badly, and eyes that went first to the coffin, then to Perrin, then to Elodie, then — this was the moment, this was the hinge of the whole morning — back to Elodie, and stopped.

She let her chin drop. Let the shawl rise around her face. Wept, gently, genuinely — thinking of the boy Vermand, thinking of Madame Ossart's washing line — and dabbed at her eyes with the wrong initials, and said, in a low ruined voice, "Forgive me. It is recent."

"When?" Brenner asked. Not harshly. Almost gently, which was worse.

"Tuesday." A true answer. Truth was the best disguise; she'd learned that long ago. Lies wobble; truth sits still.

"The papers?"

Perrin produced them with the weary ceremony of a man handing over his teeth. Brenner read them. Of course he read them thoroughly — she watched his finger travel line by line, checking dates, checking seals, checking the cross-reference between death certificate and burial permit the way other men checked the horizon for weather. The frost crackled. The mule sighed. Somewhere behind the ruined mill a bird made an optimistic noise about spring.

"The coffin," Brenner said.

Elodie's blood went thin and fast. *They won't open it,* Tomas had said, and she had said *they won't*, but Tomas had put a condition on it, and conditions have a way of coming due.

"Must you?" she said. It came out cracked and true. "She is eighty-one years old. She died in her own bed. It is the only thing —" her voice failed on purpose, or possibly not on purpose — "it is the only dignity anyone has left her. Please."

Brenner looked at her again. Too long. That was the phrase that would run through her head all the way to Fontenay and beyond: *he looked too long.* Not the soldiers — they were already drifting toward the cart's rear out of dull procedure, one of them reaching for the lid — but Brenner, whose eyes stayed on her face with an expression she couldn't file anywhere. Recognition? Suspicion? Or only that particular hunger she had seen in him at the hospital, the look of a man auditing the world for the thing that was taken out of it?

He had inventoried her ward in September. He had stood three feet from her desk. Had he seen the ledger? No — it had been in the hymnal even then, since the week of Vasseur's proclamation. But had he seen *her*? Did requisition officers file faces the way they filed bottles?

"Lift the far end only," Brenner said at last, to the soldiers. "Check for contraband beneath the body. Don't —" a pause, almost delicate — "disturb her more than necessary."

The soldiers raised the foot of the coffin. Madame Roux shifted, dignified and terrible in her Sunday dress, and underneath her hem, in the shadow, nothing was visible but good joinery and white padding, because Tomas had sewn like a boy hiding contraband from himself. One soldier peered, ran a hand awkwardly along the inner wall, withdrew it, and stepped back with the guilty relief of a man glad to be done touching a dead woman.

"All clear, Lieutenant."

Brenner nodded. Stamped the permit. Handed the papers down to Perrin without looking at him, because his eyes were still on Elodie, and he said — quietly, and in a tone she would turn over and over for days, because it wasn't suspicion at all, it was something rawer —

"Widow." A beat. "You served at the convent hospital? I think I've —" and then he stopped himself, jaw tightening, some internal clerk refusing to submit the form. "No. Forgive me. Take the road slowly; there's ice past the bend."

The cart moved. The bridge passed under its wheels plank by plank, the broken mill sliding by on the left just as Perrin had predicted, and no one looked at it, least of all Elodie, who looked at her hands.

Only when the checkpoint was a smudge of grey behind them did Perrin speak, without turning around.

"He knew you."

"He wasn't sure."

"Pah." Perrin flicked the reins. "Men like that are sure. They count things twice, remember. First time he saw your face, second time he remembered it." The old man spat thoughtfully over the wheel. "Well. You're through the first door. Two more."

"Yes," Elodie said. Her heartbeat was only now climbing down from wherever it had gone. "Two more."

They buried Madame Roux at noon, properly, with the sister weeping and the priest hurrying and Elodie standing at the graveside meaning every word of the prayers twice over — once for the old woman, once for the pages of names riding in a cart back toward Saint-Aubin with Perrin, because the plan, Marguerite's plan, had never been to carry the ledger to Verrières in a coffin. Too slow, too visible, too likely to be impounded with the funeral party. The coffin crossed the bridge as bait and cover both: a reason for a widow to be on the road at all, drawing every eye at the first checkpoint while the true cargo traveled another way entirely.

Anselm had it. Anselm, walking the long loop east with the hymnal inside his coat and six eggs in his pack, aiming for the salt road and the second checkpoint, where a sergeant once knew him by another name.

Elodie stood a moment longer at the fresh grave, until Perrin's cart disappeared toward home, and then she turned west along the frozen lane toward Verrières, alone, an unarmed woman with forged grief and genuine fear, walking at the steady pace of somebody who has promised sixty-eight people they will be counted.

Behind her, at the mill bridge, Lieutenant Brenner was presumably still counting something twice.

She hoped — she would go on hoping against considerable evidence — that whatever he was counting, it wasn't her.

But that night, sheltering in a hay barn within sight of Verrières' distant chimneys, she heard boots on the lane and a voice asking the farmer questions in that precise, thorough, unhurried cadence, and understood with a lurch that hope had filed its report and been overruled: the man who counts twice had decided to check his sums.
