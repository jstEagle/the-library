## 9. A Town That Forgets Itself

Tomas Ferreol had spent thirty-one years learning how to carry things. Sacks of flour from the mill cart to Marguerite's kitchen. Buckets of water up the convent stairs before the plumbing gave out and after it, because the plumbing gave out twice a year like a bad cough. Wounded men, when stretchers were short and the night was long and nobody asked him whether his back could bear it because nobody needed to.

He was good at carrying things. It was the putting-down that troubled him now.

The requisition order had gone up on the church door a week ago, pasted there by a corporal who licked the brush like a schoolboy finishing an unpleasant errand: ALL DOCUMENTS, REGISTERS, CORRESPONDENCE, AND PERSONAL PAPERS TO BE SURRENDERED TO THE CIVIL COMMISSARIAT BY SATURDAY EVENING. FAILURE TO COMPLY WILL BE TREATED AS AID TO THE ENEMY.

Aid to the enemy. Tomas had read it four times, standing in the square with his cap in his hands, and each time the sentence seemed less like law and more like a riddle with a cruel answer. The enemy was across the river, had been across the river for three years, shelling them politely at intervals and occasionally impolitely. The enemy did not care what was written in Madame Osselin's recipe book or in old Guillaume's letters from a son nobody had heard from since spring. But the army cared. The army wanted paper the way a fire wants air, and Saint-Aubin had very little left to burn except itself.

By Saturday evening, the commissariat's carts were full. By Sunday morning, the burning began — officially, in the yard behind the gendarmerie, where a sergeant fed ledgers into a brazier with the bored efficiency of a man stoking a stove. Unofficially, it began everywhere else.

* * *

Tomas saw the first of it on Monday, delivering bread.

Old Guillaume Pelletier sat in his doorway with a tin basin between his knees, feeding papers into it one at a time. Not throwing them — placing them, the way you'd lay flowers. Letters, by the look of them, soft-cornered and much re-read, tied once with string that he'd cut and now held coiled around two fingers like something he couldn't decide whether to keep.

"Guillaume," Tomas said. "You don't have to—"

"They're only letters." The old man didn't look up. "From my Étienne. He wrote terrible handwriting and worse jokes. I kept every one." He fed another page to the basin. "They say surrender everything. So I'm surrendering."

"You could hide them. Under a floorboard. In the—"

"Where?" And here Guillaume finally looked up, and his eyes were dry but very tired, the tiredness of a man who has done arithmetic all night. "They turned my house over for the inventory, boy. They measured my cellar. They counted my spoons. You think they missed a floorboard? They'll come back for inspection, and if they find so much as a grocery list under my bed, I hang, and maybe the neighbors hang with me for not reporting it." He dropped the coil of string into the basin last. "Better I forget them myself than let strangers do it for me."

Tomas stood in the doorway while the letters went, and the smoke rose thin and gray, smelling of ink and nothing, because paper burns into almost no smell at all, which seemed to him a profound injustice. Thirty years of a son's terrible jokes, and the sky took them without even noticing the weight.

That afternoon, passing the churchyard, he saw Madame Osselin on her knees beside her husband's grave with a stone in her hand and a rag in the other, scraping at the carved dates. Not the name — never the name — just the years, the *born* and *died*, scrubbing them back into blank rock. When she saw him watching she didn't stop, didn't blush, just said over her shoulder:

"The registers are gone. If the stones still say when everyone died, they can count us. I'd rather they couldn't." She scraped. Flakes of granite fell like gray snow. "Let them dig if they want to know. Let them work for it."

Tomas walked home the long way, past the mill, past the bridge where the sentries checked papers that no longer existed, and thought: we are erasing ourselves. Town by town, house by house, grave by grave, the whole valley was quietly unpicking its own memory and handing the threads to a brazier. Not because they'd been broken. Because they'd been *counted*, and counting, in this war, was the first step toward something worse.

He was not a clever man — he'd say so himself, cheerfully, if you gave him cause — but he knew weights and measures the way a mule knows a road. And he knew this: you can seize a piece of paper. You cannot seize what a man carries in his skull, provided the man refuses to hand it over.

Which is when the idea arrived, the way ideas arrive for Tomas — not as lightning but as a slow realization, like noticing you've been humming a tune all day without knowing where you learned it.

* * *

Marguerite's kitchen smelled, that evening, of onion and barley and the particular steam that meant soup stretched further than it should. Elodie sat at the table with her hands wrapped around a cup she wasn't drinking, wearing the look she wore after hard shifts — present but elsewhere, her eyes doing that thing where they moved over faces as though checking them off some private list. Anselm was propped in the corner chair, his leg straight out, peeling potatoes badly and defending his technique on principle.

Tomas came in, set down the bread, and stood there shifting his weight until Marguerite said, "Out with it, boy, before you wear a groove in my floor."

"They're burning everything," he said. "Letters. Registers. Madame Osselin is scraping the gravestones. Guillaume burned Étienne's letters tonight — all of them, every one." He looked around the table. "And I keep thinking: those names. Those people. Once the paper's gone, they're gone. Nobody wrote most of them anywhere except the places we just handed over or set alight. My mother's in one of those parish registers. My father. Half the town's grandmothers. We've sold our dead to be fuel."

Nobody laughed. Nobody told him he was being morbid, which was a kind of mercy, because he'd half expected to be told that.

Elodie set down her untouched cup. "You want to remember them."

"I want us to remember them. All of us. Out loud." He pulled the stool from the corner and sat, knees creaking, feeling suddenly foolish and pressing on anyway, because the alternative was letting Guillaume's string-coil go into the dark unremarked. "Here's what I know about carrying things. You can take a sack off a man. You can't take the weight out of his shoulders afterward. Paper's the same backwards. They can have every page in Saint-Aubin. But if every one of us keeps a few names in our heads — truly keeps them, says them, passes them along — then the ledger isn't paper anymore. It's people. And they can't requisition people."

Anselm stopped peeling. "You're proposing we memorize the town."

"I'm proposing we already know the town. I'm proposing we say it out loud so it sticks. Every evening. Here, or wherever there's a roof and a door worth trusting. Names, births, marriages, who died when and where and how. Not written — spoken. A thing you say aloud every evening doesn't need hiding, because it looks like talk, and no soldier alive has ever confiscated conversation."

There was a silence, the good kind, the kind where you can hear an idea being weighed.

"It would look like gossip," Marguerite said slowly, stirring the pot though it wanted no stirring. "Old people talking about the dead. Soldiers ignore old people talking about the dead. It's the one entertainment we're permitted." She pointed the spoon at Tomas. "But it has to be true. All of it. No embroidery, no leaving out the shameful bits. A record with holes is just a nicer kind of forgetting."

"No holes," Tomas agreed.

"And it spreads," Elodie said. Her voice had changed — he knew that voice; it was the ledger-voice, quiet and level, the one that meant she'd stopped being tired and started being certain. "Not just this kitchen. The Rosary circle at Sainte-Odile's. The men who play boules against the wall even in winter. Children learn names faster than anyone — make it a game, saints and their feast days, who shares a birthday with whom. Within a month there won't be a name in this parish that fewer than five people carry." She looked at Tomas with something that was almost a smile. "You've invented a human archive, Tomas Ferreol."

"I've invented nothing. My grandmother did this. During the last war, when the Prussians took the registers, she taught the whole street to recite the family trees like psalms. Called it 'saying the beads.' I thought it was just a prayer habit until I was twelve and realized half the beads weren't saints at all."

"Your grandmother," said Anselm, "sounds like she'd have made a terrifying quartermaster."

"She'd have made a terrifying anything." Tomas felt the foolishness drain out of him, replaced by something steadier. "First evening's tomorrow. I'll start with the ones I'm sure of. Marguerite corrects me where I'm wrong. Elodie—" he hesitated, glancing at her, careful, because none of them spoke directly of the hymnal, of the book, of the thing carried under floorboards and inside covers — "Elodie knows more names than any of us. Where she can share them, she does. Where she can't..." He shrugged. "We'll hold the shape of the gap. Even an empty place, remembered as empty, is a kind of witness."

Elodie looked at him for a long moment. "Yes," she said simply. "That's exactly right."

"Well then." Marguerite ladled soup with the finality of a judge closing court. "Eat. Tomorrow we begin resurrecting the entire town, and resurrection goes better on a full stomach."

* * *

It began small, as these things do, and then refused to stay small.

The first evening it was four of them in the kitchen: Tomas reciting, Marguerite correcting ("Ferreols married Baudins, not Bellands, my grandmother is spinning in her grave"), Anselm contributing the soldiers' side — units, dates, the boys who'd passed through the ward — and Elodie adding, in her careful way, the details no register had ever bothered with: that Private Corbier sang in his sleep; that the boy called Petit-Jean had been seventeen and lied about fifteen; that Madame Vasseur's cousin — no relation to the Colonel, she'd been quick to establish — died holding a photograph of a dog.

Word traveled the way word does in a town where there's nothing left to read. Within the week, the Rosary circle had adopted the practice wholesale, weaving names into their devotions so seamlessly that a casual listener heard only prayer. Old Guillaume, who had burned his son's letters, came to Tomas almost shyly and asked if he might contribute from memory — and then recited, flawlessly, forty-one letters' worth of dates and jokes and small domestic news, and wept once, briefly, with what looked like relief, as though the words had been sitting in his chest waiting for permission.

Even the children took it up. Little Camille Baudin organized her schoolmates into a game called *Who Belongs to Whom*, racing to match grandmothers to grandfathers to streets to trades, and the sentries at the bridge watched them skip past, giggling, carrying more vital intelligence than any courier, and saw only children.

There were moments of comedy, too, which Tomas treasured, because a resistance without laughter starves. Père Ancel, half-deaf and fiercely competitive, insisted on reciting his portion louder than anyone and got several names confidently wrong, requiring Marguerite to stage whispered interventions like a prompter at the opera. Monsieur Aubry, the notary's clerk, could not bear imprecision and began keeping a secret mental index of *corrections to other people's recitations*, which he delivered with the satisfaction of a man restoring order to a chaotic universe. And when the soldiers stationed at the granary complained that the townsfolk had gone strange — standing about in the evenings, murmuring in groups like conspiring geese — Sergeant Daix investigated, listened for ten minutes to old women debating whether Thérèse Marchand had been born in March (obviously) or May (she had not), and concluded that occupation had finally driven them all gently mad, and reported as much, and was believed.

Madness was excellent cover. Madness required no paperwork.

But there were harder evenings, too. The night they reached the war dead, and Tomas recited the names of the boys from the parish who hadn't come home, and the room stayed silent afterward for so long that the clock in the tower — broken, stopped, dumb since the first day of occupation — seemed loud merely by not ticking. The night Elodie added a name in a voice so quiet Tomas nearly missed it, and understood without asking that this one belonged to the hidden pages, and nodded as though she'd said it loudly, and saw her shoulders ease a fraction.

And one evening, near the end of the second week, Guillaume raised his head during the recitation and said, unexpectedly: "You know what the strangest part is? They think they took everything. They loaded their carts and drove off satisfied." He smiled with all the ancient mischief of a man whose letters were ash and whose son was nonetheless, at that moment, alive in eleven heads in that room. "We're the richest town in the province, and they rode right past the vault."

* * *

It was on such an evening — nine days after the first recitation, the kitchen full, the soup long gone, Camille Baudin mid-victory-lap over correctly pairing the entire Osselin extended family — that the knock came.

Three knocks. Measured. Official.

The room went still the way a pond goes still, every face turning toward the door, every voice already rearranging itself into innocence. Marguerite rose, wiping her hands on her apron with the unhurried dignity of a woman who had hidden contraband in dumplings and could hide it in hospitality too, and opened the door on Lieutenant Brenner.

He stood in the lamplight with his cap under his arm — he always removed it at doorways, which the town had noticed and quietly filed among the reasons to be cautiously confused about him — and behind him, barely visible in the dark, stood Anselm in civilian coat and borrowed hat, pack over his shoulder, trying very hard to look like a man arriving for supper rather than a man departing on a mission.

"Lieutenant," said Marguerite, with a warmth that fooled absolutely no one, including possibly herself. "We've finished dinner, but there's soup."

"Thank you. No." Brenner's eyes moved across the room — the children frozen mid-game, the old people arranged too casually, Elodie by the window with her hands folded — and Tomas watched him take in the whole scene and visibly decline to ask what it was. "I'm not here for an inspection. I'm here because a coffin leaves for Verrières tomorrow morning. Burial detail, outside the lines. It requires an escort." His gaze found Anselm in the shadows, held for exactly one beat too long and no longer. "Sergeant Daix has approved the escort. The escort departs before dawn, by the salt road."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in it, Tomas understood three separate things at once: that Brenner knew; that Brenner was choosing, again, not to know loudly; and that whatever fragile machinery they'd built in this kitchen had just been handed its schedule by the one man who could crush it with a signature.

"The salt road," Marguerite repeated, smooth as butter. "In winter. The poor escort will need a hot meal before setting out."

"He will," said Brenner. He put his cap back on, and at the door he paused, and said — to the room, to the game, to the murmuring geese — "Whatever it is you all do in the evenings... it sounds important. I'd see it isn't interrupted." And then he was gone into the dark, and Anselm stepped inside, and the door closed, and Camille Baudin whispered, awed, "*Was that a soldier being nice,*" and Marguerite said, "Eat your imaginary dinner, child," but her hands, when Tomas glanced at them, were not entirely steady.

Tomorrow. Before dawn. The salt road, and a sergeant out there who had known Anselm's face when that face belonged to a uniform it had run away from.

Tomas caught Anselm's eye across the kitchen, and the younger man managed a grin that convinced no one, and Tomas thought: *I am good at carrying things.* He crossed the room and put his hand on Anselm's shoulder, taking the weight in advance.

"You'll want to rehearse your story," he said. "Marguerite plays the sergeant. She's merciless."

"I heard that," said Marguerite, already pulling out the chair she used for interrogating delivery boys.
