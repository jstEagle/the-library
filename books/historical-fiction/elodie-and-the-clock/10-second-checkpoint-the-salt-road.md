## 10. Second Checkpoint: The Salt Road

The coffin weighed nothing and everything.

That was the thing about carrying a dead man's name, Anselm thought as he hauled the box up the last rise before the salt road checkpoint — the wood was light, cheap planks Marguerite had bought off the cooper with a sack of dried beans and a look that could curdle milk at forty paces. But what was inside it, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a respectable layer of sawdust and a very convincing arrangement of stones, was heavy enough to bend a man's spine if he let himself think about it.

So he didn't think about it. He thought about his boots instead. One heel had come loose that morning, and Marguerite had fixed it with three nails and a lecture about how a man going to play a grieving cousin ought at least to walk like one, not clatter along like a cart with a broken axle.

"You're not a deserter today," she'd told him on the doorstep, straightening his collar with hands that smelled of onions and lye. "You're Anselm Marchand's cousin from down the valley, come to see your poor relation into the ground because nobody else will do it. Grief is your uniform. Wear it properly."

He'd almost told her then. Almost said, *Marguerite, there is no Anselm Marchand, or rather there is, but he's standing in front of you wearing borrowed boots.* But her face had been so certain, so fierce in its certainty, that he'd swallowed it and let her believe the fiction she'd invented herself. That was the trick of it, he was learning. People believed their own inventions far more readily than they ever believed yours.

The salt road ran white and rutted toward the checkpoint, flanked by ditches crusted with frost that glittered like spilled sugar. In summer this had been a busy thoroughfare — salt carts, fish carts, farmers' wagons loaded with cabbages and complaint. Now it held only him, the coffin on the handcart Tomas had oiled until its wheels squeaked like a contented pig, and the wind coming off the flats with nothing better to do than find the gaps in a man's coat.

He could see the checkpoint now. A plank barrier across the road, a sandbagged post beside it, and two soldiers in the gray-green of the occupation army, one leaning on the barrier with the boneless patience of a man who had been bored for approximately four years, the other sitting on an upturned crate eating bread with the concentration such an act deserved.

Anselm's stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution.

Because he knew the man on the crate.

Sergeant Brochard. Of course it was Brochard. Of all the sergeants in all the checkpoints in all the mud-caked length of this miserable war, the army had posted *Brochard* to the salt road — Brochard, who had shared a mess tent with him in the third year, who had taught him to cheat at cards and lose gracefully at dice, who had once dragged him by the collar out of a flooded trench and said, laughing, gasping, "You're too stupid to die, Marchand, I refuse to allow it."

Brochard, who would remember that the man called Marchand had died at Vireloup. Officially. On paper. With a number and a date and everything.

*Turn around,* said a sensible voice in Anselm's head. *Tell them the plan changed. Let Marguerite do it.*

But Marguerite was seventy-one years old and had already given enough of herself to this town to fill a second coffin, and besides — he'd volunteered. He'd stood up in Marguerite's kitchen with his bad leg still aching from the wound that had started this whole absurd chain of events and said, "I'll carry it." Because a man who had once run away owed the world at least one journey walked straight through.

Also, Elodie had looked at him when he said it. Not gratefully — worse than gratefully. She'd looked at him like he was a name she'd written down, and writing a name down meant something in Elodie Marchand's ledger of a heart. He couldn't take that look back now without becoming smaller forever.

So he walked on.

* * *

Ten paces from the barrier, Anselm began to arrange his grief.

This was a skill he hadn't known he possessed until recently, and he suspected he possessed it precisely *because* of the desertion. A man who has spent a night deciding whether to be a soldier or a coward learns that identity is a coat you put on, and some mornings the coat doesn't fit, and some mornings it fits so well you forget you're wearing anyone else's sleeves.

He slowed the cart. He let his shoulders round. He fixed his eyes on the middle distance, where grief is supposed to live, and thought about his mother — genuinely dead these six years, which made her useful in a way she'd never have approved of — and let his face do what faces do when they carry that particular weight.

"Halt," said the bored soldier by the barrier, without any particular conviction that anything worth halting for would actually appear.

"Sergeant," Anselm said, and pitched his voice low and roughened, the voice of a man who had been crying recently or intended to soon. "I'm taking my cousin to burial. Past the lines, to the churchyard at Ferrières. The priest there knew his mother."

The bored soldier looked at the coffin. The eating soldier kept eating, but his eyes came up over the bread, and Anselm felt the look land on him like a hand on his shoulder.

"Papers," said the bored soldier.

Anselm produced them. Tomas had done beautiful work — a burial permit stamped with the requisition office's own seal, which was either forgery or irony depending on how much trouble everyone was in later. The bored soldier read it with his lips moving slightly, which was encouraging; men who moved their lips while reading were men who wanted to pass papers through quickly rather than think about them.

"Name of the deceased?"

"Marchand," Anselm said. "Anselm Marchand."

And Sergeant Brochard stopped chewing.

It wasn't dramatic. It was a small cessation, the way a clock stops — everything continuing around the silence. Anselm watched him over the coffin, watching the recognition assemble itself behind Brochard's eyes: the face first, probably, because faces are stubborn things, then the voice, then the name arriving last like a guest who'd been expected all evening.

Brochard set down the bread.

"Marchand," he repeated.

"My cousin," Anselm said. "A good man. Died of the fever in the hospital at Saint-Aubin." He gestured vaguely back down the road, toward the town, toward the convent, toward the truth, which was currently lying in the coffin between them wrapped in oilcloth. "The family's scattered. Someone had to see him buried proper."

"Vireloup," Brochard said quietly.

Anselm's blood did something complicated.

"I'm sorry?"

"Vireloup," Brochard said again, and stood up off the crate, brushing crumbs from his tunic with the unhurried movements of a man buying time, though whose time Anselm couldn't yet say. "There was a Marchand at Vireloup. Third company. I pulled him out of a flooded sap once — half-drowned, laughing about it afterward, the idiot." He came closer. Up close he was older than Anselm remembered, grayer, with the flattened look of men who had been stationed too long in one place watching too little happen. "You have his face."

For a moment — one long, ringing moment — Anselm stood entirely inside the truth. He was a deserter standing at a checkpoint holding a coffin full of contraband ledger, facing the one man in the occupation army who knew both his face and his file, and every exit from the moment was barred.

Then he did the bravest thing of his life, which was also, he reflected later, the most dishonest: he chose the smaller sin.

"Yes," he said, and let his voice crack, and found — this was the terrible gift of it — that he didn't have to pretend the cracking. "Yes. I have his face. Everyone says so. His mother couldn't look at me for a year after the news came."

Brochard's expression shifted through several weathers. Suspicion. Recognition. Something older and sorer underneath.

"You're the brother."

"Cousin," Anselm said. "But near as brothers. We grew up four houses apart." This was even true, in a way that would take a lawyer and a map and considerable goodwill to disprove. "I'm the one who has to bury him, because there's no one left who'll do it."

"The report said he died at Vireloup." Brochard's voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a man walking around the edge of something. "Killed in the shelling."

"He died of fever in Saint-Aubin hospital," Anselm said, "after they pulled what was left of him out of the line. The army reports got it wrong — they always get it wrong, don't they? Half of it wrong, anyway. Enough wrong." He shook his head slowly, and here he permitted himself a real thing, a true thing, because Elodie had taught him — without meaning to, just by being Elodie — that the best hiding place for a lie is inside a truth. "They wrote him dead in October when he lasted till February. Four months of dying and the paperwork never noticed. His mother went to her grave believing one date, and it wasn't even the right one."

Brochard stared at him.

And Anselm understood, suddenly and completely, that he had guessed correctly. That somewhere in Brochard's pocket or pack or memory there was a casualty list, an official accounting, and that the discrepancy Anselm had just handed him — *the report was wrong, the dates were wrong, the army writes its dead carelessly* — was a discrepancy Brochard had already tripped over himself, alone, at some other checkpoint, over some other name.

"That's—" Brochard began, and stopped.

"They don't mean anything by it," Anselm said gently, playing now the role of comforter, which was easier than it should have been because he meant it. "It's not cruelty. It's just — arithmetic. There's too many of them to count properly, so they count quickly, and quick counting is its own kind of lie. But we're not doing quick counting today, Sergeant. Today I'm taking my cousin to Ferrières and putting him in the ground with his mother's people, slow and correct, the way it should have been done the first time."

The wind came off the salt flats and pushed at the cart, and the coffin rocked faintly, and inside it a ledger full of names that the quick counting had lost waited in its oilcloth with the patience of the dead.

Brochard looked at the coffin for a long time.

Then he turned to the bored soldier and said, "Burial party, civilian escort, permit's in order. Open the barrier."

The bored soldier lifted the plank with the enthusiasm of a man raising a portcullis for the four-hundredth time that week.

* * *

Anselm took the handles of the cart and rolled past them, one step, two, three, each step a small miracle of ordinary motion. Five paces past the barrier. Ten. The checkpoint shrinking behind him.

"Marchand."

He stopped. He did not turn around immediately — he made himself stand in the road for one breath, two, a grieving man gathering himself — before he turned.

Brochard had come a few steps after him, past the barrier, out of earshot of the post. His hand was in his tunic pocket, and what came out of it was not a weapon. It was a folded piece of paper, soft at the edges, handled many times.

"Before you go," Brochard said. "The record. When you're at the churchyard, when the priest writes him in the register — write the February date. The true one." He held out the paper. "I copied it from the hospital roll myself, months ago, because I couldn't make the numbers sit right. Killed at Vireloup in October, but the Saint-Aubin hospital shows a Marchand admitted in November, discharged— no. Not discharged." He swallowed. "Died in February. I've been carrying it around like a stone, wondering which paper was lying."

Anselm took the paper. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he would have bet on shaking.

"Thank you, Sergeant," he said. "His mother would have wanted someone to care about the date."

"She'd have wanted more than that," Brochard said, looking past Anselm now, down the white road toward Verrières, toward whatever was happening there behind the fog and the sealed orders. "There's a lot in those lists that isn't right, cousin. A lot of names that went in wrong and never came out again. You hear things, standing at a barrier all day. Men talk." He paused. "If the armistice commission wants to know what the army forgot — well. Some of us count twice."

He said it lightly, tossing it off like a joke between card players, and then he turned and walked back to his crate and his bread, and the conversation closed behind Anselm like water over a stone.

He walked. The road unspooled white ahead of him, and the wheels of the cart hummed their small contented song, and Anselm Marchand — the true one, the whole one, the one who had deserted and been pardoned by a clerical error and carried his own death certificate around like a second spine — put one boot in front of the other and did not look back.

Only when the checkpoint was well out of sight did he stop, lean against a ditch bank, and read Brochard's copied page by the thin winter light. Then he opened the false bottom of the coffin — carefully, wincing at the nails Marguerite had insisted on, which fought him like cats — and slid the page in beside Elodie's ledger.

Because here was the thing he'd figured out somewhere on the salt road, somewhere between the barrier and the horizon: he'd sat in Marguerite's kitchen and confessed his desertion to a room full of strangers and called it honesty, and it had felt like absolution. But Brochard had just handed a total stranger proof that the army's books were rotten, unprompted, unpaid, out of nothing but a tired conscience — and *that*, Anselm thought, hauling the coffin lid back down, that was courage of a kind he hadn't managed on his best day in uniform. Brochard didn't know what he'd just helped carry. He'd done it blind, on faith, on the mere suspicion that somewhere the true count was waiting to be taken.

The least Anselm could do was make sure the suspicion was rewarded.

He took up the cart handles. Ferrières was six kilometers on; the burial was real enough, a pauper's plot the priest had agreed to, and after that the long loop east toward Verrières and the commission and whatever waited there.

Behind him, far off across the flats, a bell began to ring — not the convent's broken clock, which had been silent since the first year of the occupation, but the chapel at the crossroads, urgent and arrhythmic, the way bells ring when they're ringing for trouble rather than for prayer.

Anselm stopped. Turned. Squinted back toward Saint-Aubin, where a smudge of dark smoke was just beginning to climb above the rooftops into the gray sky, exactly where the granary stood.

"Not yet," he said aloud, to no one, to the road, to the coffin. "Not yet — we were supposed to have until tomorrow."

The smoke rose. The bell kept ringing. And sixty kilometers away, in a requisitioned office smelling of ink and cold tea, Colonel Vasseur was setting down his pen.
