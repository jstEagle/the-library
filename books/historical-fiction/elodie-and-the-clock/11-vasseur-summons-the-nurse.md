## 11. Vasseur Summons the Nurse

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, delivered by a corporal with the unfortunate habit of clearing his throat before every sentence.

"Sister Marchand." He cleared his throat. "Colonel Vasseur requests—" *hem* "—requests your presence at the town hall. At nine o'clock." He cleared his throat again, as if to punctuate himself. "That is all."

"I'm not a sister," Elodie said. "I'm a nurse. The habit was retired before the war, along with most of the convent's dignity."

"Yes. Of course." Hem. "Nine o'clock."

He left. Elodie stood in the ward doorway and watched the morning light crawl across six sleeping men, one of them Anselm, who had recovered enough to complain about the porridge, which she considered the surest sign of health a body could offer. She counted his breaths out of habit — eleven a minute, steady as a metronome — and then went to find Marguerite in the kitchen.

"Summons," Elodie said. "Town hall. Nine."

Marguerite did not look up from her dough, but her knuckles went briefly white against the flour. "Did he say why?"

"Corporal Throat-Clearing didn't say. They never say. That's rather the point of a summons, isn't it? If they told you why, you'd have time to prepare an answer, and then where would the empire be?"

"Making jokes," Marguerite said mildly, punching the dough down with unnecessary force. "That's your answer prepared, I suppose."

"It's the only weapon I've never had confiscated."

Marguerite wiped her hands and came around the table, lowering her voice even though the kitchen door was shut and Tomas was three rooms away hauling water. "Elodie. If it's the hymnal—"

"Then it's the hymnal, and there's nothing to be done about it between now and nine except eat breakfast." Elodie poured herself coffee that tasted of burnt acorns and stubbornness. "Panic before breakfast, Marguerite, and you spoil two things at once: the meal and the panic."

But her hands were not entirely steady on the cup, and she knew Marguerite saw it, and she knew Marguerite was kind enough not to mention it.

* * *

At five minutes to nine, Elodie crossed the square to the town hall, which the army had requisitioned so thoroughly that only the building's bones still belonged to Saint-Aubin. The mayor's chair stood behind the colonel's desk like a widower at someone else's wedding. The portraits of municipal ancestors had been taken down; their nail holes remained, small dark mouths in the plaster, saying nothing.

Colonel Vasseur sat at the desk with a file open before him and his hands folded on top of it, which struck Elodie as theatrical. A man who wanted you to think about what was inside the folder had already lost something, in her experience. Her mother used to do the same thing with the household accounts.

"Nurse Marchand." He did not rise. "Thank you for coming."

"You sent a corporal and a carriage would have been faster, Colonel, but I take my exercise where I can."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite amusement; more the memory of what amusement had felt like before the war filed it away. He gestured to the chair opposite. She sat. She kept her hands visible and loose in her lap, the way she did when a wounded man was delirious and might swing at her. It was, she realized, the same posture. Interesting, that the skills transferred.

"Do you know why I've asked you here?"

"Your corporal was tragically vague."

"Then let me be specific." He opened the folder. From it he drew — and her heart performed a maneuver she felt in her ears — a hymnal. Grey cloth covers, spine cracked, the property stamp of the Convent of Saint-Aubin-du-Val faded but legible.

"That's a hymnal," Elodie said.

"Observant as ever." Vasseur turned it over in his hands. "We collected forty-one of these from the chapel stores during requisition inventory. Forty-one hymnals, logged, boxed, sealed for transport. This morning there are forty." He set it on the desk between them. "This one was found in the infirmary linen cupboard. Folded into a sheet, as though someone had mislaid it among the bandages. Careless housekeeping, wouldn't you say?"

"Terrible," Elodie agreed. "I'll speak to whoever is responsible."

"The thing is," Vasseur went on, in the tone of a man laying cards down one at a time and enjoying the game far too little, "this particular hymnal is inscribed. Look at the flyleaf."

She looked. She made herself look slowly, curiously, the way a woman looks at a borrowed book she has no reason to fear. The inscription was in Father Aubry's careful hand, dating back eleven years: *Given in memory of Odile Marchand, beloved cook of this house.* Below it, smaller: *Pray for her family.*

Her mother's name. In another woman's handwriting. In a hymnal that had once lived, for four months, in the drawer of her own bedside table, holding inside its hollowed spine the truest record in the province.

"Well," Elodie said. "Small world."

"Isn't it." Vasseur leaned back. "Odile Marchand. Any relation?"

"Distant. An aunt." The lie came out smooth and warm and Elodie hated how good she'd gotten at it. "My mother always said we had a cook somewhere in the family tree. It explained our fondness for salt."

"Your mother's name?"

"Also Marchand, funnily enough. It's a common name in these parts. Like stones."

Vasseur regarded her for a long moment. Outside, a cart rattled across the cobbles, and somewhere a soldier shouted at a horse, and the town conducted its occupied business as if nothing in this room mattered. Then he closed the hymnal and slid it back into the folder, and said, quietly, in a voice stripped of all its officer:

"Forty hymnals, Nurse. One missing since October. Do you know how many times I have inventoried this town? Every sack of flour. Every blanket. Every chicken, God help me, every chicken in Saint-Aubin has a number." He rubbed his eyes. "And one hymnal walks out of a sealed box, and no report is filed, and no inquiry follows, because the man who should file it cannot decide whether it matters."

"And does it?" Elodie asked. "Matter?"

"That depends," said Vasseur, "on what else in this town has walked out of sealed boxes."

* * *

He rose then and went to the window, and Elodie understood she had been dismissed from the interrogation without being dismissed from the room, which meant he wanted to talk to her back, the way people confess to priests and horses.

"I knew your mother," he said. "Not well. She sold eggs to the garrison when I was a lieutenant stationed here, twenty years ago. She shortchanged herself every week through simple arithmetic errors and refused correction. Proud woman."

"Stubborn."

"Stubborn," he agreed. "The whole town is stubborn. It's the worst possible quality in an occupied population and I find myself, lately, admiring it." He turned. On his face was something Elodie recognized from the ward: the expression of a man who has been carrying a weight so long his shoulders have forgotten any other shape. "You know what an armistice commission does, Nurse?"

"Signs papers. Long ones. With several pens, apparently, so history can collect souvenirs."

"They are meeting at Verrières. Delegates from five capitals, arguing over lines on a map while my supply columns rot at the depots." He came back to the desk but did not sit. "There is a faction — call them the continuationists — who believe the terms currently drafted are dishonorable. Who believe one more campaign season would settle matters properly. If their arguments prevail, the signing is delayed. Weeks. Months." His jaw worked. "If the armistice collapses, this occupation does not end. It *matures*. Requisition becomes administration. My men become governors. This town stays a garrison until the last of us dies of old age or the last of you dies of anything."

Elodie felt something cold move through her chest. She thought of the ledger in its hiding place — wherever Tomas had put it now, she hadn't asked, better not to know — and of the names inside it waiting for a record that might never be read.

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you are the sort of woman who counts things." Vasseur looked at her directly, and for a moment the officer's mask slipped entirely, and underneath was simply a tired middle-aged man who had spent a war doing arithmetic. "Casualty returns cross my desk, Nurse. Yours especially. Your figures are accurate. Suspiciously accurate. More accurate than the reports that are supposed to supersede them." A pause. "When the official count of a battle comes through lower than the count kept by a nurse in a convent infirmary, a man begins to wonder whose numbers are the fiction."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly. Elodie kept her face still by an act of will she could feel in her teeth.

"Clerical errors," she said.

"Mm." Vasseur sat down at last, folding his hands again. "Here is what I know, Nurse Marchand, and I will thank you neither to confirm nor deny it, because confirmation would oblige me to act and denial would insult us both. I know a hymnal went missing in October. I know a coffin left this town for burial outside the lines, which is permitted, and returned empty, which is unusual. I know Lieutenant Brenner has been asking quiet questions about casualty ledgers that don't exist on any manifest. And I know that in four days — perhaps fewer — men at Verrières will decide whether this war ends or merely changes costume."

The clock in the hall outside struck the quarter hour. Its sound seemed to land in the room like a stone dropped in a well.

"If the signing holds," Vasseur continued, "then whatever secrets Saint-Aubin is keeping will be swept up in peace, filed under general amnesty, and no one will ever trouble themselves. But if it fails — if I am ordered to hold this town indefinitely, with a hostile population, dwindling supplies, and a requisition list that doesn't add up — then I will be obliged to make examples, Nurse. Examples are arithmetic too. And I would very much prefer not to start counting the people in this town whom I have come to respect."

"That sounds almost like a warning."

"It is a weather report," Vasseur said. "I am telling you which way the wind blows. What you build in it is your affair." He drew the folder toward him and squared its edges with two precise taps. "You may go. Take the hymnal with you — it belongs to your aunt's memory, I suppose. Property claims will be sorted after the war. Whenever that is."

* * *

Elodie walked back across the square with the hymnal under her arm and her mind running at the pace of a hemorrhage. The cold air helped. Cold air always helped; it was why she made the delirious men keep their windows cracked.

Tomas met her halfway, ostensibly carrying a basket of laundry, actually carrying worry. "Well?"

"He knows things," Elodie said. "Not everything, but enough things, arranged in the right order, that 'nothing' is no longer an available answer."

"Does he have it? Does he know about—"

"He gave me the hymnal back, Tomas. Empty. He wanted me to see it empty." She hugged the book tighter. "He suspects a ledger exists. He suspects a coffin. He suspects Brenner. What he doesn't suspect — yet — is where any of it is now, and he can't search without proof, and he hasn't got proof, so instead he gave me a speech about weather."

Tomas processed this, shifting the laundry basket. "That's either very kind or very cruel."

"With Vasseur it's usually both, in equal measure, like his soup." Elodie glanced up at the convent tower, at its broken clock face frozen at ten minutes past some afternoon two wars ago. "The part that frightens me isn't the suspicion. It's Verrières. Four days, he said. Maybe fewer. If the delegates sign, everything's forgiven by default. If they don't—"

"Then we're prisoners forever."

"Then we're prisoners forever, and the names in my book die twice — once in the flesh, once in the record." She started walking again, faster. "Where's Anselm?"

"The ward, doing his impression of a man who doesn't intend to escape anywhere."

"Wake him up gently and tell him his impression has been cancelled. And find Marguerite. Kitchen table, tonight, after supper." Elodie stopped at the gate and looked back once at the town hall, where behind a requisitioned window a requisitioning colonel was presumably watching her go, counting something. "Tell her the schedule just moved. Whatever we were going to do in a leisurely fashion, we now do in three days."

"And if the roads are watched?"

"Then we get creative." Elodie pushed through the gate. "I've spent four years being creative with other people's lives, Tomas. I'd like to try it with their dignity instead. It leaves fewer stains."

Behind her, faint across the square, the broken clock in the tower hung silent at its impossible hour, and Elodie found she could hear it anyway — ticking, ticking, ticking — as though the whole town had begun, at last, keeping count.
