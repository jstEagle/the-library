## 12. Brenner's Bargain

The arithmetic would not leave him alone.

Aurel Brenner had been a man of ledgers long before the army made it official. His father had kept accounts for a grain merchant in Wels, and Aurel had learned at that same sloped desk that numbers were the only honest things in the world — until he'd met casualty reports, which lied the way rivers lie about their depth: smoothly, and all the way down.

He sat in the requisition office that had once been the convent's sewing room, with the moon through the tall window laying pale stripes across his inventory sheets, and did the sums again.

Four hundred and eleven men had passed through Saint-Aubin's hospital since the occupation began. He knew this because he counted everything twice, an habit his superiors found tedious and his conscience found indispensable. The burial register — the one the town had surrendered under Colonel Vasseur's document order — recorded three hundred and eighty-eight interments. Twenty-three men unaccounted for. Transferred, the paperwork said. Evacuated south.

But Aurel had audited the transfer manifests too. He had spent two evenings doing it, hunched over by lamplight while the frost climbed the window glass like something trying to get in, and he could find no transfers. No ambulances leaving. No trains. Twenty-three men who had entered a hospital and then, on paper, simply ceased to exist.

Numbers did not do that. Numbers were honest. It was only ever people who made them dishonest, and only ever for reasons.

And there was the other matter. The photograph.

He took it from his breast pocket now, as he did every night, as though this time the image might have changed. Emil, twenty-two years old in the picture, squinting against summer sun at their cousin's wedding, one hand raised mid-wave as if he'd seen the camera coming and couldn't decide whether to greet it or flee it. Listed missing after the retreat from the Vire line fourteen months ago. *Missing* — which in the army's vocabulary meant one of three things, and Aurel had stopped pretending to himself that he didn't know which three.

Except. Except that when he had requested Emil's service record through channels — properly, patiently, twice — the file that came back was wrong. Not incomplete. Wrong. A different man's regiment. A different man's birthday. Someone had pasted the wrong story over the right one, and they had done it carelessly, which meant they had done it confident that no one would ever look twice.

Aurel looked twice at everything. It was practically a medical condition.

Which brought him, reluctantly, to the hymnal.

* * *

He had noticed its absence the way he noticed all absences: by counting. The convent chapel held forty-one hymnals before the document order; the surrender pile contained thirty-nine. Two missing. One he had traced to old Madame Roussel in the lane, who used hers to press flowers and had produced it from beneath her mattress with the defiance of a woman surrendering a national treasure. The second had simply vanished.

Nurse Marchand's ward held the hospital's small library shelf. He had inventoried it twice. On his first count there had been a hymnal between the tattered Dumas and the agricultural almanac. On his second count, the shelf went Dumas directly to almanac, as though the book had ascended.

People did not steal hymnals. People stole bread, candles, morphine, hope. But a hymnal — a hymnal was either sentimental or it was useful, and there was exactly one thing in Saint-Aubin worth hiding inside a book of songs.

She had flinched. That was the other thread he'd been pulling. Lieutenant Marchand — no, not Marchand, Elodie Marchand, nurse, mid-thirties, local, quiet as falling snow — she had flinched, just barely, the day he'd mentioned the document order. Most people flinched at the document order; it was a flinch-inducing document. But hers had not been the flinch of a woman worried about letters from her mother. Hers had been the flinch of a woman with something *specifically* to lose, and he had spent enough years auditing grain merchants to know the difference between general guilt and particular inventory.

So tonight, instead of his usual route back to his quarters, Aurel Brenner found himself walking the long corridor toward the ward where Nurse Marchand kept the night shift, rehearsing nothing, because he had discovered long ago that rehearsals made liars of everyone, including him.

The ward at night was a country of small sounds. Candle flames breathing. The wet rattle of lungs doing their stubborn work. Somewhere, a man murmuring a name into his pillow — his own or someone else's, impossible to say. And at the far end, by the little desk with the lamp turned low, Nurse Marchand writing.

She heard him coming — of course she did; he had never once managed to surprise anyone — and did not look up until he stood across the desk from her.

"Lieutenant." Her voice was even. "Is someone worse? I can fetch—"

"No one is worse." He glanced down the row of cots. Anselm's cot, he noticed, was empty, the blanket folded back with military tidiness. He filed that away. His filing cabinet was getting crowded. "I need to speak with you. Privately."

"We are private."

"Privately-er, then."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one. She rose, took the lamp, and led him to the linen room — a closet of a place smelling of soap and cold starch, shelves of folded sheets rising around them like snowdrifts. She set the lamp on an upturned bucket and folded her arms, and waited, in the manner of a woman who had waited out better interrogators than this.

"Nurse Marchand," Aurel said, "I'm going to tell you what I know, and then I'm going to make you an offer, and then — this is the important part — I'm going to stop talking and let you think. I've been told I have a face that makes people fill silence. I'd rather you didn't fill it."

"That," said Elodie, "is the most honest opening I've heard from your side of the war."

"I keep honest books. It's my one virtue and my entire personality."

That got the smile, briefly, before she banked it. Good. He wanted her off balance in the friendly direction rather than the frightened one. Frightened people lied well. Off-balance-but-amused people told the truth by accident.

"You keep a ledger," he said. "Paper. Hidden — I'd guess somewhere within ten meters of this ward, because you're a creature of routine and your routine is this building. In it you record the names the army's reports omit. Deserters shot at dawn. Civilians caught in crossfire. Boys who died before their papers caught up with them." He paused. "I don't know how many names. I know the number should be at least twenty-three, because that's how many men your registers show buried who appear nowhere else. But I suspect it's more."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft. Neither of them moved.

"Here is what I am not saying," Aurel continued, quietly now. "I am not saying it to threaten you. If I wanted to threaten you I would have brought Sergeant Kroll, who is enormous and has no imagination, instead of coming alone to a linen room where you could smother me with pillowcases and honestly improve the army's morale."

The ancestor-smile came all the way back this time. "You rehearsed that part."

"Only that part. The rest is sincere." He drew a breath, and found the next part harder than the sums had been, which surprised him, because he had thought the hard part was behind him. "My brother is in your book. Or if not in it, then in the hole in it. Emil Brenner. Missing after the Vire line, October of last year. His official record has been replaced with someone else's — badly, lazily, by someone who assumed no one counts twice. I count twice. And every thread I pull leads toward this town, and this hospital, and a woman who writes down what the officers won't."

Elodie had gone very still. Not the stillness of fear, he thought. The stillness of a scale finding its balance point.

"So here is my offer," he said. "I offer you my silence. Complete, permanent, professionally documented silence — I will certify your ward's inventory myself, hymnal and all, and lose two pages of my notes in a credible clerical accident. In exchange, you give me the truth about my brother. Whatever is in that ledger about Emil Brenner. However it ends."

"And if the truth is ugly?"

"Then it's ugly and I'll carry it. Ugly truth weighs less than beautiful ignorance. I've weighed both." He realized as he said it that it was true, and that he had learned it recently, and that he owed the learning to her without knowing yet how.

For a long moment she studied him — not his face but his hands, he noticed, as though hands confessed more. Then she said, "You understand what you're asking me to hand over. That ledger isn't paper to me, Lieutenant. It's the only funeral those men got. Some of them, it's the only proof they existed at all."

"I understand better than you think. My brother is currently a clerical error. I would like him to be a man again."

"Yes," she said softly. "Yes, that's exactly what it is."

They stood in the smell of soap and starch, two people on opposite sides of a table that wasn't there, negotiating across it anyway.

"If I agree," Elodie said slowly, "it cannot be tonight, and it cannot be a page torn out. What's written is written — I won't mutilate the record to serve either of us. You'd have to trust me to bring you what you ask. And you'd have to answer me one question first, honestly, because everything else depends on it."

"Ask."

"If I give you your brother's truth, and it implicates your superior officers — and I promise you, Aurel Brenner, that it will — whose side are you on?"

It was the question he had been avoiding since the frost began climbing windows, and here it was, standing in a linen room with its arms folded, waiting for an answer.

He thought of his father's desk. Of the first rule of accounting, learned at nine years old with a pencil stub in his fist: the numbers must reconcile, whatever it costs, whoever it embarrasses. He thought of Colonel Vasseur's smooth reports lying like rivers.

"I was loyal to the army," he said, "because I believed the army kept honest books. When it stops keeping them, my loyalty doesn't transfer to the lies. It goes looking for the true record." He met her eyes. "Apparently it goes looking in a linen room."

Elodie let out a breath that might have been a laugh in a stronger light. "You're a strange man, Lieutenant."

"I'm an accountant in a uniform. There are stranger."

"All right." She straightened, and something in her posture shifted — the negotiation concluded, the alliance, however fragile, formed. "Not tonight. Soon. I give you my word, and my word is the one thing in Saint-Aubin the requisition hasn't touched."

"One more condition, then mine is complete," Aurel said, gathering himself to leave. "Whatever you're planning — and you are planning something, because the folded blanket on Anselm's empty cot was too tidy and Madame Roussel's grandson bought lamp oil in quantities inconsistent with reading — whatever it is, do it quickly. I wasn't supposed to tell you this, so I'll deny it in the morning: the armistice talks at Verrières are wobbling. Delegates deadlocked, deadline moving. If it collapses, Vasseur gets extended authority, the roads close indefinitely, and every hidden thing in this town becomes evidence."

The lamp flame shivered. Elodie's face, in the small light, went through several weathers very fast.

"How long?" she asked.

"If the signing holds — days. Perhaps hours. If it fails—" He shrugged, and hated the shrug, and did it anyway, because accountants deal in probabilities, however cruel. "Then never."

He left her there among the snowdrifts of folded linen, and walked back down the corridor of small sounds with his heart doing sums of its own. Behind him, faintly, he heard her footsteps already moving — not toward the ward, but toward the chapel, quick and purposeful, a woman with hours on her clock and not days.

He had his bargain. He had, he hoped, his brother's truth coming.

What he did not yet know — what would take another day and a dying man's voice to learn — was that the page he needed most was the one she had never been able to write.
