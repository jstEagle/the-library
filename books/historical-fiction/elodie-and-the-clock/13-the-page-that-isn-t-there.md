## 13. The Page That Isn't There

Elodie had always believed her memory was a kind of warehouse — that anything she had once written down could be found again simply by walking the aisles in her mind and lifting the right box from the right shelf. It was an orderly fantasy, and she clung to it the way some people clung to religion: not because it was proven, but because the alternative was unbearable.

The alternative was this: standing at the narrow window of the linen room at four in the morning, unable to remember something she had never known.

She had gone looking for the boy Brenner. Not Aurel — the brother. *Kaspar Brenner, corporal, listed missing.* She had turned the pages of her ledger back to the autumn before last, when the fighting had come through the hills east of Saint-Aubin and the convent had filled with men whose names arrived on their lips before their paperwork did. She remembered those weeks the way one remembers a fever. But when she searched for Kaspar Brenner — truly searched, running her finger down column after column of small careful names — she found nothing.

And then she understood why, and the understanding sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She had not begun recording until the third week of her night shifts. Before that there had been too much to do and too little of herself to do it with, and the men who died in those first days had gone into the ground with only the chaplain's hurried prayers and the army's arithmetic to mark them. If Kaspar Brenner had come through the ward in that first fortnight — if he had died on a stretcher by the door while she held someone else's hand closed — then his story existed nowhere but in the memory of whoever had been beside him.

Which meant, she thought grimly, folding the ledger closed, that she had spent two years building a cathedral of names and left a gap in the foundation exactly where it mattered most.

"You've been up since three," said a voice from the doorway, and there was Marguerite in her shawl, carrying two cups of chicory coffee as though warmth were a sacrament she personally administered. "I could hear you thinking from the kitchen. It sounds like a mouse trapped in a wall."

"It may be worse than a mouse." Elodie took the cup. Her hands were steadier than she expected them to be. "Brenner's brother. I can't tell him how he died because I don't know. And I can't know, because—"

"Because you weren't here yet," Marguerite finished. Cooks always knew things; Elodie had stopped being surprised by it sometime around the second winter. The older woman lowered herself onto the linen shelf with the sigh of a woman whose knees had opinions. "You're thinking of Pelay."

Elodie blinked. "How did you—"

"Because I think of him every time anyone asks about the eastern fighting, and nobody ever does, which is why he's been lying in my ward for a month talking to the ceiling." Marguerite blew on her coffee. "Old Sergeant Pelay. He was there, Elodie. Wherever 'there' is — he was there when the retreat went wrong, and he's been carrying whatever happened inside him like a bad meal ever since. He was Vasseur's man for thirty years. If your lieutenant's brother died in the hills that autumn, Pelay watched it happen."

*Vasseur's own man.* The phrase turned over in Elodie's mind and showed its underside. To ask Pelay was to ask the enemy's own furniture for testimony. It was to trust the memory of a soldier who had spent his career making omissions tidy.

"He's dying," she said quietly.

"He's everyone's dying," Marguerite said. "The question is what he does with the last of it."

* * *

Sergeant Pelay occupied the cot nearest the ward's east window because he had refused all others, and he refused all others because from that cot he could see the road, and from the road — he seemed to believe — trouble would eventually arrive, and he intended to be facing it when it did. He was seventy if he was a day, with a mustache that predated three wars and hands that trembled now except when they were holding something, at which point they became inexplicably steady.

He was holding a wooden spoon when Elodie came to his bedside that morning. Nobody knew where he got the spoons. Tomas suspected him of an arrangement with the kitchen that bypassed Marguerite entirely, which, if true, made Pelay the only living creature who had ever out-negotiated her.

"Sister Marchand," he said. He called her Sister although she wore no habit; she had given up correcting him around the same time she'd given up correcting the officers who called her *nurse* as though it were an apology. "Or should I say Widow Lefèvre these days? I hear you've been burying cousins."

The word landed like ice down her collar. Then she saw the glint in his old eyes and understood it for what it was: not accusation, but a soldier's greeting — *I see you; I say nothing.* The whole town had learned to speak in that dialect.

"Cousins are worth burying," Elodie said. "It keeps the family honest."

"Hah." He set the spoon on the blanket, aligning it with the bed's edge as though inspecting a rank of soldiers. Then his face settled into something more careful. "You didn't come to change my dressing. You change dressings at six, after the coffee. It's half past five. So either you've developed initiative, or you want something."

"I want to ask about the autumn before last. The fighting in the eastern hills. The retreat."

The trembling came back into his hands. He looked at them with something like disgust, folded them under the blanket, and said nothing for a long moment. Down the ward, Anselm was helping Tomas fold bandages with the exaggerated diligence of a man pretending very hard not to listen, and failing completely.

"Why does a nurse care about a retreat?" Pelay asked finally. "The wounded come *after* retreats. Retreats are what make her work necessary. Asking about one is like asking the gravedigger about the wedding."

"Because someone is looking for how a Corporal Brenner died," Elodie said. "And I've learned that when the army doesn't write a thing down, it's usually because somebody decided it shouldn't be written. Somebody senior."

Pelay's eyes went to the window. To the road. For a moment Elodie thought she saw him listening for trouble coming up it specifically on his account.

"Vasseur," he said. Not a question.

"You know how this looks to me," Elodie said gently. "You served under him longer than anyone. Telling me what happened could be the worst disloyalty of your life."

"Telling you what happened," Pelay said, "would be the first loyalty of mine that ever came true." He worked his jaw, as if the words were packed somewhere difficult. "But you should understand something first, girl. Whatever I give you, you'll have to carry it carefully. A thing like this isn't gossip. It's ordnance. You drop it wrong and it goes off under the wrong person."

"That's rather the point," Elodie said. "It's meant to go off under the right one."

He studied her for a long time. Old soldiers, she had learned, did not evaluate people by their words; they evaluated them by their stillness, the way horses did. Apparently she passed, because he let out a breath that seemed to have been held for two years.

"Come back at the hour of my dressing," he said. "And bring paper. My memory works better when it thinks it's giving evidence."

* * *

He talked for nearly an hour, and Elodie wrote until her hand ached.

The shape of it came out slowly, the way a drowned thing comes up from deep water — reluctantly, in pieces, changed by the journey. The autumn before last: a forward company pushed too far into the eastern hills, resupply cut off, orders to hold a ridge that had no strategic value except to a map drawn by a man who had never seen it. Kaspar Brenner's company. When the enemy counterattacked, the relief column that should have come up the valley road did not come. Vasseur had ordered it held back — *conserving forces*, the order read — and the company on the ridge had ceased to exist as anything but a casualty figure, and then, after some further arithmetic Elodie was beginning to understand, not even that.

"He wasn't abandoned to die, that's the devil of it," Pelay said, staring at the ceiling as he spoke, his voice flat with the effort of precision. "He was abandoned to be *unknown*. There's a difference. A dead man you acknowledge costs you an inquiry. A missing man costs you nothing at all. Missing men don't have mothers who testify. They have photographs on mantelpieces and no answer, forever." His hands trembled on the blanket. "I drafted the report. My own hand. *No survivors recovered; unit records lost in action.* Lost in action — as if paper dies like a man. As if it bleeds."

"And afterward?" Elodie asked softly. "When the truth might have surfaced?"

"Afterward Sergeant Pelay kept his mouth shut and his pension, and told himself thirty years of service bought him one silence." The old man turned his head at last and looked at her directly, and his eyes were terribly clear. "It didn't buy me anything. That's the discovery of my seventieth year. Write it down exactly, nurse. Don't soften it the way you soften everything else in that book of yours — yes, I know about the book, I'm dying, not blind. Exactness is the only apology I've got left."

So she wrote it exactly. The ridge. The withheld column. The scrubbed records. The name — *Kaspar Brenner, corporal* — restored to its place in time and manner of death. And when she finished, Pelay surprised her. He held out one trembling hand toward the spoon on his blanket, and when she placed it there, his grip went steady, and he used the handle to press down hard against the page's corner, anchoring it, while she read the whole account back to him aloud, word for word.

"Sign it," he said at the end.

"With what? We have no ink at the bedside."

"Coal," said Anselm from two cots away, appearing with a lump of charcoal from the stove-room wrapped in cloth, wearing the expression of a man who had been waiting all morning for exactly this errand. "Tomas keeps some for marking the laundry. Don't look scandalized, Sergeant — half the army runs on worse."

Pelay signed with the charcoal, a slow deliberate scrawl, pressing so hard the page dented beneath. *A. Pelay, Sergent.* He looked at the signature for a long moment as though meeting himself in it.

"There," he said. "Thirty years, and that's the only order I ever gave that I'm proud of."

* * *

Elodie carried the page through the ward and out into the corridor, and there she stopped, because the corridor contained Lieutenant Aurel Brenner, standing at the window with his inventory ledger under his arm, and the sight of him hit her like cold water.

He turned. "Madame Lefèvre." His gaze dropped to the papers in her hands — loose sheets, unhidden, unmistakable — and rose again to her face with terrible patience. "You're carrying something."

"I am," Elodie said. Her heart was going like a drum, but her voice came out level, and she privately marveled at it. "And it happens to be yours."

Something crossed his face — hope arriving before permission, the way it does in young men who have taught themselves not to hope. "That's not possible."

"Nevertheless." She held the pages out across the corridor between them, and did not let go until she saw that his fingers had closed on them. "Read it. Then come find me. Not at the checkpoint, not in front of Colonel Vasseur's people — find me where there are no windows with roads in them." She hesitated, and then, because the hour and the dying man behind her had stripped away her talent for caution, she added: "Your brother held the ridge for nine hours, Lieutenant. Longer than anyone believed possible. Whoever tells you he died quickly is lying to comfort you. He was extraordinary, and the record will now say so."

Brenner looked down at the pages in his hands as if they might burn him, or save him, or both.

Behind her, faintly, through the ward's open door, Elodie heard Sergeant Pelay call out in a voice cracked but carrying:

"And tell the boy's brother — the last thing my men heard from that ridge was singing. Your lot sang louder than our guns. Make sure that's in the record too."

Elodie stood in the corridor, empty-handed at last, watching a requisition officer read the truth of his own family with coal-blackened fingers — and somewhere below them both, in the town, a bell began to ring, though not the convent's clock, not yet. But it sounded, to her ears, like a beginning.
