## 14. Pelay's Last Watch

The trouble with dying, Sergeant Pelay had decided somewhere around the third week of it, was the waiting.

A man could stand a bayonet. A man could even stand the slow rot of a lung full of shrapnel-scarred tissue, if he had to, because a soldier's whole career was a course of instruction in standing things he ought not to be able to stand. What no one had prepared him for — not forty years in the army, not two wars, not the loss of three fingers to a frostbitten winter at the Vosges — was lying flat on his back in a converted convent while the ceiling plaster above him slowly composed its own map of a country he would never see again, with nothing to do but wait for the inevitable and listen to Sister Bernadette's slippers squeak past at all hours like a mouse with opinions.

"You're scowling," said Elodie Marchand from the next cot over, where she sat with her ledger open on her knees and her pen uncapped. "The doctor says scowling is bad for the heart."

"The doctor says everything is bad for the heart. That's how he knows so much about hearts."

"He also says you've rallied. He said it in the tone he uses when he doesn't believe himself, but he said it."

Pelay turned his head — an operation that now required planning, like a field maneuver — and looked at her. Small woman, steady hands, eyes that took inventory of a man the way a quartermaster takes inventory of stores: without flinching and without mercy. He had watched her move through this ward for weeks. He had watched her write in that ledger of hers when she thought no one saw. He was an old soldier and he knew contraband when he saw it, even contraband bound in grey cloth and kept as quiet as a prayer.

"Nurse Marchand," he said. "Come sit where I can see you properly. My eyes have gone dishonest."

She came. She sat. She waited, which he counted in her favor; too many people these days filled silence the way shells filled a road.

"I'm going to tell you something," he said, "and you're going to write it down. Not in your head. On paper. With your name under it and mine on top."

Her pen stopped moving before it had started. "Sergeant—"

"I know what I know you keep in that book. Don't look shocked, girl, I've been shot at by better-hidden things than your conscience." He coughed, wet and long, and let her hold the cup to his lips while he drank. "I've got maybe a week in me. The doctor's rallies are for my benefit, not his. And there's a thing in this world worse than dying with an unclean soul, and that's dying with a clean one that never testified."

"Testified to what?"

"To arithmetic," Pelay said. "Bad arithmetic. The kind Colonel Vasseur does."

* * *

He began, as old soldiers do, with the weather.

"It rained," he said. "October before last, up on the ridge north of Ferme-Charpentier. You'll want to put the date. The fourteenth. It rained the way it only rains in that country, sideways and personally, like God had taken against the particular men on that hill. We were holding the forward line — Second Company, what was left of it, and the attached platoon out of the Ninth. Brenner's boy was with the Ninth."

"Brenner." Elodie said the name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "Aurel Brenner?"

"No. Mathis Brenner. Lieutenant. Twenty-three years old, wore his helmet like it embarrassed him, wrote letters home that made the rest of us feel like we were failing at correspondence." Pelay smiled, and found, to his surprise, that the smile didn't hurt. Most things hurt. Smiling had apparently been held in reserve. "Aurel is the younger brother. The careful one. Mathis was the kind of officer who learned every private's mother's name and then used it against them at inspection — *your mother raised you to lace your boots, Corporal Gaspard, and I have written to tell her otherwise.* They'd have followed him off a bridge. Which is nearly what happened."

Elodie's pen was moving now, fast and quiet. He heard the small scratch of it and felt something in his chest loosen that had been wound tight for fourteen months. Fourteen months of carrying a thing alone. It is a curious fact about guilt that it weighs less when spoken than when carried, though it is heavier to say than to think. No one tells you that. The chaplains don't tell you that. The chaplains mostly tell you about forgiveness, which is a different currency entirely.

"On the thirteenth, recon came back saying the enemy was massing in the woods east of the mill. Straight at our flank. Mathis sent it up the chain — written, signed, timed. I know, because I carried it. I was staff by then, too old and too broken for the line, running dispatches between the companies and headquarters. I carried Mathis Brenner's warning up the hill to the château at four in the afternoon, and I stood in the room while Colonel Vasseur read it."

Pelay stopped. Not for breath, though his breath needed managing these days. He stopped because this was the part he had rehearsed in the dark, night after night, and rehearsals always sounded different from the stage.

"What did he do?" Elodie asked. Softly. She knew, he thought. Or she guessed. Women who kept ledgers of omitted names developed an ear for omission the way sailors develop an ear for weather.

"He read it twice," Pelay said. "He did that. Read everything twice — meticulous man, careful man, the sort of officer who counts his spoons. Then he set it down on the map table and he said, 'If we pull Second Company back tonight, we weaken the ridge.' And I said, sir, if we don't, they'll be cut off by morning. And he looked at me — you'll want this part exactly, nurse, because everyone else who was in that room is dead or promoted beyond questioning — he looked at me and said, '*Sergeant, war is arithmetic. One company on a ridge is worth more than one company in a bag.*'"

"And then he ordered the retreat anyway."

"No," said Pelay. "That's the thing. That's the whole rotten thing. He ordered the retreat for everyone *except* them. He pulled First and Third Companies back to the secondary line at dusk — quietly, no signal flare, nothing the forward post would catch — and left Second Company and the Ninth detachment sitting on that ridge in the rain as the hinge, the tripwire, the bait. Deliberate. Documented afterward as a 'delaying action.' Mathis Brenner and eighty-one men spent the night discovering that their own army had walked away from them, and at dawn the enemy came out of the woods and closed the bag."

The ward was quiet around them. Somewhere down the row a young private slept with his bandaged hands outside the blanket, palms up, as if surrendering even in sleep. Sister Bernadette's slippers squeaked past. The plaster ceiling held its silent map of a country made of cracks.

"How many came back?" Elodie asked.

"Nine," Pelay said. "Mathis wasn't one of them. Officially, he was posted missing, then posted killed, then — here is where it gets interesting, girl, pay attention — then posted *never having been attached to the action at all.*"

Elodie looked up.

"After," Pelay said. "Two weeks after, when the survivors trickled back through the lines and started talking. Talking is the one weapon the army has never successfully requisitioned. Nine men, nine accounts, all matching. Too well. So the reports went in and the reports went into a drawer, and the casualty lists came back out scrubbed. Reissued. Second Company's action at Ferme-Charpentier reduced to 'a planned tactical withdrawal conducted without significant engagement.' Eighty-one names gone. Not dead — gone. Dead is a number, and numbers get counted. These weren't counted. There's a difference, and the difference is a colonel with good handwriting."

"Why?" Elodie asked. And then, because she was honest: "No. I can guess why. Tell me anyway."

"Because if the action went into the record as what it was, someone would ask who ordered the withdrawal that abandoned them, and the answer would be the man writing the report. So instead of a disaster he owned, it became a skirmish nobody had. Fewer names, fewer widows, fewer questions from Paris. He'd call it mercy, if you cornered him. He'd call it protecting the war effort. He'd probably half believe it, which is the worst of it. Evil needs liars; stupidity only needs arithmetic." Pelay coughed again, longer this time, and when it passed he felt lighter and emptier both, like a warehouse after the last cart leaves. "I stayed quiet. That's my crime, and you can write it down too. I stayed quiet because he was my colonel and the war was on and there was a German army east of me, and a man tells himself he'll speak when the shooting stops. Well. The shooting is nearly stopped, and I'll be stopped slightly ahead of schedule, so the speaking falls to today."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon.

Elodie wrote, and read back, and he corrected, and she wrote again. She had a way of reading a man's words back to him that made them sound more true than they had in his mouth — steadier, arranged, like soldiers on parade ground instead of scattered across a hillside. Twice she stopped him when his account wobbled. Once she caught him softening Vasseur out of some ancient reflex of rank.

"You said 'the colonel judged,'" she said, pen poised. "Did he judge, or did he choose?"

Pelay lay there and considered the difference, which was the difference of his whole life distilled. Forty years of service, and it came down to that question asked by a nurse with ink on her fingers.

"He chose," he said. "Judging is what you do when you don't know. He knew."

"Then that's what goes on the page."

There were interruptions. Tomas came through with the evening bread and a face like a man carrying secrets in his pockets, which, Pelay reflected, half this convent seemed to be these days; he set a heel of loaf by Pelay's elbow and exchanged with Nurse Marchand a look of such loaded meaning that Pelay nearly laughed, which turned into coughing, which ended the amusement. Marguerite appeared later with a broth that smelled, impossibly, of actual chicken, and pressed her hand to Pelay's forehead with the unsentimental tenderness of a woman who had fed three generations of this town and buried several of them.

"You look better," she pronounced.

"I'm dying, Madame Marguerite."

"Well, you look better doing it than you did Tuesday. Eat your soup."

Anselm came by at dusk, moving carefully on legs that were learning their trade again, and sat on the edge of the next cot and listened while Elodie read the day's pages back. Pelay watched the boy's face. He knew what the boy was — or rather, he knew what the boy had been reported as, which in this town amounted to the same confusion. A dead man walking around in his own boots, pardoned by clerical error. There were days Pelay thought the entire war could be explained by that boy: men reported dead who were alive, men recorded alive who were dead, and somewhere in the middle, a colonel with a pen deciding which was which.

When Elodie finished reading, Anselm said quietly, "The ninth of October. That's the day I ran."

Nobody asked him to explain. That was the other thing about this place, Pelay thought — the rare and improbable thing. Nobody asked you to explain what you weren't ready to explain. The convent had become a country with different laws, governed by a cook, defended by a nurse, populated by the officially nonexistent.

"It's all of a piece," Anselm said. "Vasseur trims the rolls to make his numbers work. My death was convenient, so I stayed dead. Mathis Brenner's survival was inconvenient, so he stopped existing. It's the same pen."

"It's the same pen," Pelay agreed.

"And the brother — Aurel Brenner — is here. In Saint-Aubin. Doing the counting." The boy looked at his hands. "He's stationed at the mill bridge checkpoint. If the testimony goes to Verrières, it goes past him."

Pelay felt the shape of that settle over the room like weather. He had wondered, these past days, why Nurse Marchand asked her questions with one ear cocked toward the door. Now he knew. Everything in this convent was already in motion — the ledger, the plan, whatever kitchen-table parliament the three of them had convened. His testimony was not going to sit in a book by a sickbed. It was going to travel.

Good, he thought. Paper should travel. Paper that sits still is just furniture.

"Then we make sure it's signed properly," he said aloud, "because a signature is the one thing in this army that outranks a colonel."

* * *

It took two more days to get the account complete. Two days of mornings and afternoons, Elodie bent over the page, Pelay dictating until his voice gave out and gesturing until his hands gave up, resting between salvos, drinking broth he had no appetite for because Marguerite stood over him until he did. He told her things he had told no one — the exact wording of the order, the time it was logged, the name of the clerk who later reissued the scrubbed casualty list (Delacour, a nervous man with ink-stained cuffs, since transferred), the location of the copy Vasseur kept locked in the château's map cabinet because meticulous men count their spoons and file their sins.

"You remember all of this," Elodie said once, wonderingly. "Word for word. Dates, times."

"Girl, I've had fourteen months and nothing to do but lie here. Memory is the one muscle a dying man can still exercise." He paused. "Also I was angry. Anger files things very neatly."

On the second evening, when the light through the tall convent windows had gone the color of weak tea, she read the whole account back to him, beginning to end. Nineteen hundred words. Every name spelled correctly. Mathis Brenner, Lieutenant, Ninth Detachment. The eighty-one. The order. The choice. The scrubbing. Her own name and the date at the bottom, and beneath it a blank line with *Witness and survivor, Sgt. Émile Pelay, 2nd Company* written above it, waiting.

She set the pen in his hand.

His hand shook. It had shaken for weeks; that was nothing new. But it shook differently now, and he took a moment to be annoyed about it, because a man wants his signature steady for the one document of his life that matters, and fate had scheduled the tremor poorly. Elodie steadied the page against a board. Tomas held the lamp. Anselm stood at the foot of the cot like an honor guard of one.

Pelay wrote his name. It came out crooked and sprawling, the E trailing off like a man leaving a room early, and he stared at it and thought it was the finest piece of penmanship of his entire career.

"There," he said, when he could speak. "Forty years I saluted things. Flags, colonels, orders, most of them foolish. This is the first thing I've ever signed." He looked up at Elodie. "You understand what you're carrying now. It isn't a book anymore. It's an indictment wearing a book's clothes."

"I understand."

"They'll try to stop you. Vasseur will seal the roads if he smells it — meticulous men guard their arithmetic. And the boy's brother—" he nodded at Anselm, meaning Brenner, meaning the whole tangle of it "—the brother will have to decide whose son he is. The army's, or Mathis's. Give him time and give him proof. Proof is kinder than accusation. It lets a man change sides without admitting he was wrong, which is the only way most men ever manage it."

Elodie folded the pages, and then — this was the part Pelay would carry with him, the image he expected to take through whatever door he was approaching — she did not hide them away in the hymnal or her bodice or any of the clever places clever smugglers use. She held them a moment in both hands, out in the lamplight, plainly, as if letting them be seen was itself part of the testimony.

"Sergeant," she said. "Why now? You could have died quiet. Nobody would have blamed you."

"Nobody would have known to blame me," Pelay corrected. "That's not the same thing, and I've had fourteen months to learn the difference." He settled back against the pillows, suddenly, enormously tired, the way a man is tired after setting down something very heavy very carefully. "Eighty-one men stood on a ridge in the rain and discovered their own side had gone home without them. Somebody wrote that down honestly, once, and somebody else unwrote it. All I've done is write it back." His eyes were closing; he fought them briefly and won, or lost, depending. "Tell the boy Brenner his brother laced his boots properly. Mothers like to know."

He slept. In the morning he woke, which surprised everyone including himself, and complained about the soup, which reassured everyone including himself.

But that evening, as Elodie moved down the ward with the lamp and the folded pages tucked flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and as Tomas slipped out the side door toward wherever the town's quiet parliament convened, and as Anselm stood in the courtyard testing his legs against the cold, Pelay lay watching the cracked ceiling and listening to the convent's broken clock NOT strike the hour — it had been silent since the occupation began, wound down, dead as anything else in this town that refused to die politely — and he thought:

Somewhere in Verrières, men are choosing whether this war ends this month or next. And somewhere in this convent, a nurse is deciding whether the ending gets to be honest. And he had the sudden, iron certainty — the last clear thought of a long soldiering life — that the clock and the ledger were racing each other now, and that everything, all of it, would come down to whichever spoke first.
