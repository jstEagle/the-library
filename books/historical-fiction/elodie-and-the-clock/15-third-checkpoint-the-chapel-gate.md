## 15. Third Checkpoint: The Chapel Gate

Marguerite Badeau had buried a husband, two stillborn daughters, and — in a sense that no priest had ever quite managed to make official — her own name. For thirty years she had been *the cook at the convent*, as though Marguerite were merely an ingredient added to the town's recipe: a spoonful of her here, a ladle of her there, flavoring every christening and funeral in Saint-Aubin without ever being the dish itself.

She found, standing now before the long mirror in the convent's linen room, that this was about to become the most useful thing about her.

The woman in the glass was sixty-three years old, broad as a flour barrel, with hands mottled like old copper and a face that had learned every expression grief could ask of it. She wore her good black dress — the one she'd sewn for Henri's funeral and had worn to eleven more since, altering the hem twice as fashion marched past their occupied town without stopping. Over her arm hung the shawl. In her pocket, folded small as a communion wafer, was the lie: a burial permit for her *nephew*, one Antonin Badeau, deceased of fever, to be interred in the churchyard at Verrières, where his mother's family lay.

The coffin itself — borrowed from Père Aubry with a handshake and a promise neither of them believed — sat in the cart outside, weighted with sandbags so it would ride like a real sorrow. The ledger rode inside Elodie's hymnal, wrapped in oilcloth, tucked beneath the false bottom Tomas had built with joinery so fine it ought to have been a sin.

"You're certain," Elodie said from the doorway. She'd said it four times already, and each time Marguerite had answered with something flippant, because if she answered honestly they'd both start crying, and crying took time, and time was the one thing the war had stopped lending.

"Certain as my gravy," Marguerite said, adjusting the shawl. "Which is to say everyone trusts it and no one knows what's in it."

"Marguerite."

"*Elodie.*" She turned and took the girl's face in her flour-soft hands. "Listen to me. I have spent forty years feeding people who were on their way to do difficult things. Soldiers, pilgrims, brides, mourners. I always thought — what a small thing I do, next to theirs. Tonight I get to find out." She smiled, and felt the smile arrange itself into something older and sadder, which was exactly right for the occasion. "Besides, no sergeant alive will look under the floor of a coffin when the woman driving it looks like she might put a curse on his grandchildren."

Tomas appeared behind Elodie, carrying the horse's harness over one arm like a vestment. He was pale. He was always pale; the boy had the complexion of a man who expected trouble and kept receiving it ahead of schedule.

"The granary's laid," he said quietly. "Anselm's checked it twice. Lamp oil in the west stall, kindling against the north wall. When the bell rings from the tower — well, when *we* ring it — he lights it, and half the garrison will go running."

"And the other half?" Marguerite asked.

"The other half," said Anselm's voice from the stairwell, "will be looking at you. Which is why we're not letting them look too closely." He came down slowly, favoring the leg the surgeons had argued over, but he came down upright, in civilian clothes that hung off him like a borrowed story. He looked at the coffin, then away from it. Men who had nearly gone into coffins themselves never could look at one comfortably.

* * *

They gathered in the kitchen, because it was the only room in Saint-Aubin where serious things had ever been decided. Wars, marriages, feuds, reconciliations — all of them settled over Marguerite's table, usually over soup. There was no soup tonight. There was a map drawn in wine on the scrubbed wood, because Marguerite refused to let good brandy be wasted on geography.

"The chapel gate is here." Her thick finger rested on the last smudge of red. "Two kilometers past the mill bridge, where our Lieutenant Brenner made eyes at Elodie—"

"He did not make *eyes*—"

"He counted your face twice, child. A man who counts everything twice is either an accountant or a danger, and Brenner is both. But he is not at the chapel gate. The gate is held by the second company, who answer to a Captain Roche, who drinks, and a Corporal Fenn, who does not drink but wishes he did. They check papers. They look inside coffins when the papers smell wrong." She tapped the map. "So the papers must smell right."

"And the diversion," Tomas said. "The fire pulls Roche and most of the sentries toward the granary road. It won't empty the gate — there'll always be someone on it — but it thins them. Gives you a window where one tired corporal is doing three jobs badly."

"One tired corporal doing three jobs badly," Marguerite repeated, "has waved through every funeral cart in this province since October. Grief is the best passport ever printed. Nobody dares inspect it."

Anselm was quiet at the end of the table, turning his cup. Then he said, in the flat careful voice he used for the things that cost him, "I should carry it. The coffin. You shouldn't—"

"No," Marguerite said, and did not soften it. "Your face is on somebody's memory at the salt road, and likely at the mill bridge by now. And you walk like a man collecting injuries, which is precisely what you are. Whereas I—" she spread her arms, presenting herself, "—I am exactly what I appear to be, for the first time in my life, because what I appear to be is nobody. A cook. A widow. A woman taking her nephew home to his mother's earth." She heard her own voice catch on that last part and let it. It wasn't performance. That was the terrible, useful truth of it: there was no acting required. Every grief she'd ever stored up was currency now, and she had been saving all her life without knowing what for.

"If it goes wrong," Elodie began.

"It won't go wrong," said Marguerite. "And if it does, it goes wrong with me, which is the correct order of things. I've had my life, dear. Most of it twice over. You lot have barely started yours." She stood, which ended arguments in her kitchen the way a dropped spoon ends dinner. "Now help me into the cart, someone, before my dignity and my knees give out together."

* * *

The night was fog-thick, the kind of fog that made Saint-Aubin feel less conquered than simply erased. Marguerite drove the cart slowly, as mourners do, and the slow pace suited both the horse and her heart, which were pounding at roughly the same rate.

She talked to the coffin as they went. She couldn't help it; she had talked to pots for forty years, and a pot never once answered back or needed comforting.

"You're doing fine, Antonin," she murmured. "Nice and heavy. Nobody suspects a heavy coffin; only light ones raise questions. There's a sermon in that somewhere." The sandbags shifted with a soft sigh, like a sleeper agreeing.

At the bend above the river she paused, as planned, and looked back at the town. From here Saint-Aubin was only a few lights and the black hump of the convent tower, where the broken clock had stood silent since the first week of the occupation — its hands frozen at ten past six, the moment, folks said, when the first shell had knocked the courage out of the mechanism along with a chunk of the roof. The townsfolk were up there now, Tomas's people, with files and oil and borrowed nerve, coaxing the old clock back to life. When it struck, the armistice was signed. When it struck, whatever she did tonight would already be history, one way or the other.

"Hurry up," she told the clock, and flicked the reins.

* * *

The chapel gate announced itself by lamplight — a yellow smear in the fog that resolved, as she rolled closer, into a barrier of crossed timbers, a sentry box, and two soldiers stamping against the cold. Two, then. Not three. The fire hadn't started yet; the thinning would come after. She would pass or fail on her own merits, in front of two cold men and God.

God, she thought, had better be paying attention.

"Papers," said the nearer soldier. Corporal Fenn, unless she missed her guess — young, freckled, with the unhappiness of a boy promoted past his appetite for responsibility. His lamp came up, and its circle of light fell on her face, and she watched him take her in: the black dress, the shawl, the age, the whole weathered architecture of her grief.

"Aunt Marguerite Badeau," she said, in the voice she used for telling brides the cake had collapsed — steady, sorry, immovable. "Saint-Aubin parish. I'm taking my nephew to Verrières. His mother's family plot." She handed over the forged permit and the requisition-pass Anselm had altered, and folded her hands in her lap, and waited the way stone waits.

Fenn read the papers. Read them again. She could see the arithmetic moving behind his eyes — *old woman, coffin, fever, paperwork in order* — and she helped it along, because a grieving aunt alone at night deserves conversation whether she wants it or not.

"Fever took him Tuesday," she said. "Nineteen years old. He was helping at the hospital, carrying stretchers. Strong as an ox and twice as willing." This was true of no actual person, but it was true of the shape of the war, and Fenn flinched at it, because every word of it might have been written about him. "His poor mother's been beside herself. Wants him in consecrated ground by his cousins. You understand."

"Yes, ma'am," Fenn said, and meant it, and stamped the pass.

But the other soldier — older, mustached, with the permanent squint of a man who had learned that the war lived in details — stepped forward and set his lamp against the coffin's side.

"We'll need to look inside."

The fog held very still around them. Somewhere behind her, miles back, Marguerite knew, Anselm was crouched in the dark of the granary with a lantern and a match, waiting for the signal, and Tomas was in the tower with his hands on the bell rope, and Elodie —

Elodie was trusting her.

"Look inside," Marguerite repeated, and let her voice crack down the middle, right on the seam she'd known was there. "Corporal. I dressed that boy myself. I washed him. I closed his eyes. He weighed nothing at the end — nothing, and I carried him to the cart on my own back like he was five years old again." She leaned forward, and sixty-three years of funerals came up through her voice like heat through a stove door. "If you open that box, you will look at a dead boy's face, and I will look at you looking at him, and neither of us will ever be rid of it. Is that what your captain wants written in his report? Opened coffins? Grieving families turned out on the road?"

The mustached soldier hesitated. Papers could be forged; faces could lie; but fury like hers, that particular exhausted righteousness — no forger alive could produce it on demand. He glanced at Fenn. Fenn, who wanted desperately to be anywhere else, gave the smallest possible shrug.

"Family plot at Verrières," Fenn said, not quite a question.

"Consecrated ground," Marguerite confirmed, "where his grandmother's been waiting eight years to scold him for growing up too fast." She took the stamped papers back with hands that shook exactly as much as they needed to. "Thank you, corporal. Your mother raised you properly. Tell her so, sometime, while you can."

The barrier lifted. The wheels crunched through the gate's shadow, past the lamp, past the squinting soldier, out onto the open road where the fog swallowed the checkpoint whole.

Marguerite drove on for a quarter mile before she let herself breathe. Then she laughed — a short, disbelieving bark, entirely unladylike — and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and said to the coffin, "Antonin, my boy, we are going to need a bigger saint."

That was when the sky behind her turned orange.

* * *

It began as a glow, a false dawn in the wrong quarter of the sky, and then the bell in the convent tower rang — wild, urgent, nothing like a clock striking — and the fog itself seemed to catch fire with reflected light. The granary. Anselm's work, Tomas's signal, the whole desperate machinery of their plan igniting on schedule.

And Marguerite's blood went cold, because the plan said the bell meant *go*, and the bell was ringing, but it was ringing *wrong*. Not the measured toll they'd agreed on. A clamor. An alarm.

Hoofbeats came up the road behind her — a rider, then two, galloping back toward the fire, paying her no attention whatsoever. Good. Fine. Keep driving, she told herself. Eyes forward. Widow to the bone.

Then, around the far bend ahead, lanterns. A mounted patrol coming *from* Verrières — recalled, rerouted, the whole countryside waking to smoke. The road between the two fires was closing like a hand.

The patrol's officer reined in across the lane and raised his lamp, and the light caught the wet rails of the cart, the black dress, the coffin, and his face beneath the helmet brim was one she knew from market days and confiscated-butter disputes and every requisition that had ever come through the convent kitchens, wearing its usual expression of a man doing sums he didn't like.

Colonel Vasseur looked at her, and then — slowly — at the coffin.

"Madame Badeau," he said. "How curious. I don't recall signing a burial pass tonight."

Behind him, the orange sky spat sparks, and somewhere in Saint-Aubin the bell kept ringing its wrong, urgent note, and the coffin with all the names in the world rode silent between them.

Marguerite folded her hands in her lap and prepared, once more, to grieve on command.
