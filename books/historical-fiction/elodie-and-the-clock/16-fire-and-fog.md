## 16. Fire and Fog

Tomas had set fire to exactly one thing in his life before tonight, and that had been his own sleeve, at fourteen, leaning too close to Marguerite's stove while she lectured him about patience. She had put the sleeve out with a wet cloth and a look. The lecture had lasted longer than the burning.

He thought of her now — *patience, boy* — as he crouched in the shadow of the requisitioned granary with a bottle of lamp oil in each hand and a heart going like a rabbit's. The fog had come off the river thick and low, which was either a blessing or a curse; it hid him, but it also made the whole world feel muffled and conspiratorial, as though the night itself were holding its finger to its lips.

The granary was guarded by two sentries, which was one more than Anselm's plan had promised. Anselm's plan, delivered at Marguerite's kitchen table in a low urgent voice three days earlier, had assumed many things: that the fog would cooperate, that the sentries would be sleepy, that a fire started in the west corner would draw every soldier in Saint-Aubin to it like moths, leaving the chapel gate on the east road thin enough for Marguerite to slip through with the coffin.

"Fire is honest," Anselm had said, and then paused, because he understood better than anyone what an odd thing that was for a deserter to say. "It doesn't lie about being dangerous. People run from it or fight it. They don't stand around asking questions."

Tomas, who had spent the entire occupation being unremarkable — the orderly who carried bedpans, fetched water, wound bandages, and smiled at everyone because it cost nothing and bought goodwill by the yard — had raised his hand like a schoolboy and volunteered. He still wasn't entirely sure why. It had something to do with the evening recitations, the names spoken aloud in the chapel every night since the papers were seized. Something about saying those names out loud, forty voices in the dark passing them back and forth like bread, had rearranged something in him. He had been unremarkable long enough. Unremarkable was a kind of hiding, and he was tired of hiding.

The first sentry wandered around the west corner at half past two, boots squelching, humming something tuneless. Tomas waited until the humming faded, counted to thirty the way Elodie counted pulses — she'd taught him once, laughing, *you count the beats between, not just the beats* — and moved.

The oil went under the door where the chaff had blown into drifts all winter. He splashed it generously, said a small apology to whatever grain remained inside — the army's grain, not the town's; the town hadn't seen its own harvest since autumn — and struck the match.

Here is what nobody had planned for: the chaff was drier than sin and the wind, invisible beneath the fog, found it instantly. The fire didn't creep up the wall the way fires did in stories. It went up like a held breath released, a soft orange roar, and within a minute the granary's western face was a sheet of light that turned the fog itself luminous, so that the whole town seemed wrapped in burning gauze.

The sentries shouted. Somewhere a whistle shrieked. Boots everywhere.

*Patience, boy,* Tomas told himself, and stayed exactly where he was, pressed against the granary's south wall, because the plan required the fire to hold attention for twenty minutes and he intended to see it done properly. Twenty minutes was how long Marguerite needed to reach the chapel gate. Fifteen minutes gone already, maybe. He counted pulses between the shouts.

That was when the second problem arrived, which was that the fire, being honest, refused to stay in the west corner. It found the stairwell and went up through the building like gossip through a small town, and now there were soldiers not only watching but hauling buckets, and among the buckets Tomas heard Colonel Vasseur's voice — not shouting, worse than shouting, calm as iced-over water, giving orders about the adjacent storehouse and the pump pressure — and Tomas understood that the colonel had not been drawn out of his quarters by the alarm but had perhaps never left them, or had left them quickly and completely, and was now standing in the fog with his hands behind his back watching his grain burn with an expression Tomas could not see and did not need to.

Fifteen minutes. Sixteen. He should go. The plan said go at fifteen.

But the pump was blocked — he could hear the soldiers swearing at it — and the bucket line was disorganized, and some small stupid civic part of Tomas, the part that had spent years carrying things for people, looked at that bucket line and saw where it was failing, and before he knew he had decided anything he had stepped out of the shadow, grabbed a bucket from a startled private, and joined it.

It seemed the natural thing. That was the trap of being useful: usefulness didn't check plans first.

He made four trips. On the fifth, a hand closed on his collar.

"You," said Sergeant Delacourt, whose face Tomas knew from the hospital, from the wounded he'd carried in, "are not a soldier."

"I'm helping," Tomas said, with the smile that cost nothing.

"Soldiers help with fires. Civilians run from them." Delacourt's grip tightened. "At two in the morning. In the fog. Near the west corner." His eyes went to the place where Tomas had been crouching, and then to the smell — Tomas could smell it himself now, the lamp oil rising off his sleeves, sweet and unmistakable as a confession.

"Ah," said Tomas.

* *

They did not beat him, which he had braced for, and which he afterward understood was its own kind of message. Instead they sat him in the requisitioned schoolhouse with its little desks still scratched with children's slates-drawn hearts and sums, and they let him wait. Waiting, he decided somewhere in the second hour, was the army's preferred instrument. A man's imagination did the work of ten fists.

The fog came in through the gaps in the shutters as thin gray ribbons. Through the window slit he could see the granary still smoldering, a red eye in the white dark, and the bucket line reduced now to a tidy handful of men who had, eventually, found their competence. The roof would go, he judged. The walls might stand. He felt a strange flicker of professional assessment — he had helped build that roof repair two summers ago, before the war ate everything — followed by a stranger flicker of satisfaction. Let the army haul its grain out of the ash. Let them count what was lost. Counting was the whole war, really. And somewhere east of here, if the fog and God and Marguerite's bad knees cooperated, a coffin was rolling toward Verrières full of paper names instead of a body, which was, when you thought about it, the most honest cargo any coffin had carried in years.

He hummed to keep his courage fed. It was the same tuneless thing the sentry had hummed, come back to him like a coin returned.

Near dawn, the door opened, and Colonel Vasseur came in alone.

Tomas had seen the colonel twice before, both times at a distance, both times in the way you see weather. Up close he was smaller than his reputation, gray and neat, with the eyes of a man who did sums in his sleep and woke disappointed when reality didn't balance.

Vasseur pulled out a child's chair, considered it, and remained standing instead. He set a single object on the teacher's desk: a hymnal.

Tomas's blood went cold and then, oddly, hot.

"Found this," Vasseur said, "in the ashes near the west door. Burned at the edge, pages fused. Do you know what I thought, when I saw it?"

"That you'd caught a churchgoer," Tomas said.

"I thought: why does a fire started with lamp oil have a hymnal beside it? Fires don't carry books. Men do." Vasseur turned the hymnal over with one finger, gently, the way Elodie turned down a blanket over a fevered man. "And then I remembered that this town surrendered a hundred and six personal documents eleven days ago, and that the inventory lists ninety-eight Bibles, four missals, and — interestingly — no hymnals. Not one. A convent without a hymnal, Sergeant Delacourt tells me, is like a kitchen without a pot. So either your sisters sing a cappella, or someone kept a book back."

Tomas said nothing, which took more effort than talking ever had.

"The nurse," Vasseur said. "Marchand. She surrendered her papers?"

"She surrendered what she had."

"Yes." Vasseur studied him with something that was almost, terribly, patience. "You know, I have read the casualty reports from this sector for three years. Every name, every date. I am very good at reports. And this town has been looking at me all winter like a room that has just been cleaned — everything in order, and something missing that I can't name." He picked the hymnal up again, weighed it. "Tonight a man sets my granary alight at precisely the hour that would best serve a journey east, and that man smells of lamp oil and carries no book, and yet I find myself thinking about books anyway. About ledgers." He let the word sit in the fog-thick air between them. "About names that are not in my reports."

"They're in somebody's," Tomas said, before he could stop himself.

Silence. Outside, the granary settled with a sigh, sending up a column of sparks that climbed into the fog and died.

"Yes," Vasseur said quietly. "I'm beginning to fear they are." And for one strange moment he looked less like a colonel and more like a tired clerk who suspected his own files were lying to him. Then the moment folded itself away, and he was the colonel again, and he gave his orders to the doorway behind him as though Tomas had stopped existing:

"Sergeant Delacourt. Roads sealed. All roads — mill bridge, salt road, chapel gate. Nothing leaves Saint-Aubin, not carts, not coffins, not chickens, until after the signing. If a coffin presents itself at any gate, open it." A pause. "Especially coffins."

The word *especially* landed on Tomas like a dropped beam. He gripped the edges of the little desk and kept his face doing what faces were supposed to do, which was nothing.

"And the prisoner?" Delacourt asked.

Vasseur looked at Tomas for a long moment. "Hold him. The infirmary will want its orderly back eventually, and I find I want to think about hymnals a little longer."

* *

They locked him in the school's supply closet, which contained a broom, three boxes of chalk dust, and a poster of the alphabet with a rooster on it, and which Tomas found, all things considered, less insulting than the little desks. He sat on the floor with his knees up and conducted an inventory of the disaster, because inventories calmed him the way counting pulses calmed other people.

Item: the fire had burned, loudly and well. Item: Marguerite had needed to pass the chapel gate roughly when the fire peaked, and the gate had surely been emptied of its sentries to fight the blaze — the plan working exactly as designed, for once. Item: the roads were now sealed, which meant the plan had worked *too* well and *too early*, or not at all, and he had no way to know which. Item: Vasseur suspected a ledger, suspected Elodie, and had ordered coffins opened *especially*.

If the coffin was already through — if Marguerite had walked past the empty gate in the smoke and fog during those golden twenty minutes — then the sealing of the roads was a lock bolted on an empty house, and Tomas could sit in this closet with the rooster alphabet until spring and it wouldn't matter.

If she hadn't. If the fire had drawn the sentries but slowed her too, if the fog had confused her old knees on the river path, if anything of the thousand things had gone even slightly wrong —

Then the coffin was still inside the walls, and every gate was watched, and Vasseur had just taught his men to look under lids.

"Oh, Marguerite," he said aloud to the rooster, "please tell me you were fast for once in your life."

The rooster declined to answer. Chalk dust tickled his nose. He thought about Elodie, who would already know he was taken, because Elodie knew everything that happened in that hospital the way rivers knew rain; thought about Anselm, who would be pacing somewhere with guilt on one shoulder and purpose on the other, arguing about which to load first; thought about the names they recited each evening, and how a list of names was a rope, and how a rope was made of threads, and how you could cut a rope but the threads were already in people's hands.

He heard boots in the corridor, unhurried. Then, worse, he heard a second set of boots fall in beside the first, lighter ones, and a voice he recognized saying, in a tone of perfect professional flatness, "Lieutenant Brenner reporting as ordered regarding the gate inventory," and the voice of Colonel Vasseur answering, "Good. You'll want to see what we're opening."

Tomas put his head back against the shelf and closed his eyes, and in the dark behind them he saw it plainly: the chapel gate at first light, the fog lifting, a cart, a coffin, and Lieutenant Aurel Brenner — who counted everything twice — standing over it with a crowbar in his hand and a question in his face that no lock had ever been built to answer.

The clock in the convent tower had not struck in three years. Nobody knew how many hours were left. But Tomas, sitting in a closet with a broom, knew with absolute certainty that they were fewer than yesterday, and that somewhere in this fog-bound town, a dead man's coffin was about to be asked to speak.
