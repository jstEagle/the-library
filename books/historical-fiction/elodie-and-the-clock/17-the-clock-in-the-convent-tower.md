## 17. The Clock in the Convent Tower

The clock had been dead longer than the war had been in Saint-Aubin.

Elodie had never thought much about it before the occupation — a convent clock is the kind of thing you only hear when it stops, like a grandmother's humming or the sound of rain on a kitchen roof. The sisters had wound it every Sunday for a hundred years, and then the sisters were gone — dispersed, requisitioned themselves, folded into the great gray machinery of the war — and the clock had stopped at eleven minutes past four on an afternoon nobody had thought to record.

Eleven minutes past four. Elodie sometimes thought that if she'd been keeping her ledger back then, she'd have written it down. *The town's time died at 4:11 p.m., cause: neglect, survived by everyone.*

Now she stood in the cloister square with her collar up against the February wind and watched old Henri Bonnefoy — who had repaired the town's clocks, boots, arguments, and marriages for fifty years — shinny up the scaffold around the tower with a coil of rope over one shoulder and the expression of a man who considered gravity a personal insult.

"Henri," Marguerite hissed from beneath her shawl, "you are seventy-three years old."

"Seventy-four," Henri called down cheerfully. "And the tower is only two hundred. I'm the younger of us."

"He shouldn't be up there," Elodie murmured. Her eyes kept moving — the corner by the granary where the scorch marks still showed from three nights ago; the checkpoint at the far end of the rue Basse, where soldiers stamped their feet and blew on their hands and looked, she thought, more cold than vigilant; the shuttered window of the guardroom where Tomas had been taken, and where he was, presumably, still sitting, being asked questions by men who did not understand that Tomas Fournier could out-stubborn a mule with a head cold.

Marguerite followed her gaze and put a hand on her arm. "He'll be released. They have nothing on him. A fire starts, they arrest the first man with soot on him — that's arithmetic, not justice. And Vasseur's arithmetic is sloppy."

"It wasn't sloppy enough to miss us."

"No," Marguerite admitted. "It wasn't." She turned back to the tower. "But look at this instead. Look what they've done while we were worrying."

Elodie looked.

For three days the townsfolk had been climbing the scaffold in shifts, quietly, the way they did everything now — no orders given aloud, everything communicated in glances over bread queues and in the particular way Marguerite banged a pot. Henri had gone up first with his tools. Then the Delacroix brothers, who between them possessed the town's entire supply of machine oil, having hoarded it through two winters on the theory — proven correct — that someday something would need greasing more than their hinges did. Then half a dozen others whose names Elodie knew only as faces that appeared when faces were needed.

And now the hands of the great clock, which had hung slack and useless at 4:11 since the first week of the occupation, were moving. Slowly. Gently. Like a swimmer testing cold water.

"It can't tell the right time," Elodie said. "Nothing in this town knows the right time anymore."

"It doesn't need to know the time," Marguerite said. "It needs to know the moment." She pulled her shawl tighter. "Henri's idea. When word comes from Verrières — when the armistice is actually signed, actually holding — the bell rings. Twelve strokes. Everyone in Saint-Aubin will know within a heartbeat, soldier and civilian alike, because you cannot unring a bell, Elodie. You cannot unring anything."

"And until it rings?"

Marguerite was quiet a moment. Below them, at the base of the tower, a small crowd had gathered — pretending, badly, to be interested in nothing at all. Old Madame Roussel had brought her knitting, which she held upside down. Père Anselme's successor, a nervous young priest named Father Aubry, was ostentatiously reading his breviary with the pages facing the wrong direction entirely. Nobody was fooling anybody. It was, Elodie thought, the worst-concealed gathering in the history of occupied France, and it was also the bravest thing she had seen in four years.

"Until it rings," Marguerite said, "every hour we don't hear is an hour Vasseur is still counting. He sealed the roads at dusk yesterday. Nothing leaves Saint-Aubin until after the signing — his words, posted on the church door like an indulgence." Her mouth went thin. "Which means the ledger either goes out tonight or sits in a coffin until the world decides whether to stop bleeding."

* * *

They climbed to Marguerite's kitchen at noon, because all decisions in Saint-Aubin were made at Marguerite's kitchen table, and because Marguerite claimed she could not think on an empty stomach, and neither could anyone she loved, and she intended therefore to keep everyone fed until thinking became unnecessary.

Anselm was already there, sitting with his bad leg propped on a stool he had dragged into position himself, in defiance of medical advice, Marguerite's advice, and the stool's own structural limits. He looked better than he had any right to. He always did. There was something about a man who had already been shot, reported dead, pardoned by clerical error, and volunteered for one more impossible errand that gave him a certain immunity to gloom.

"The roads," Elodie began.

"Are sealed. Yes. I heard the announcement." Anselm tore a piece of bread in half with great ceremony. "Sealed until after the signing. Which means our problem has become a problem of hours. The armistice talks end — one way or the other — day after tomorrow. If they succeed, the roads open and the commission disperses and there is no commission to bring anything *to*, only historians, later, who will read whatever Vasseur's tidy reports say. If they fail—" He spread his hands. "If they fail, the war continues, the occupation continues, and the ledger becomes even more contraband than it is, and Pelay's page becomes worth more than the paper it's signed on, because dead men's testimony keeps poorly."

"Don't say 'dead men' so casually," Marguerite said, setting down a pot. "Sergeant Pelay is not dead yet."

"No, madame. He's stubbornly alive. I apologize."

"You should. He taught half of you deserters your footwork, and look how you've repaid him."

"I carried his pack for six miles once," Anselm said, wounded. "In the rain."

"And complained the whole way, I don't doubt."

Elodie let them go on. This was how the kitchen worked — fear dressed up as banter, circling the real subject the way moths circled a lamp without landing. She ate her soup and listened to the tower above them, where Henri's hammer rang out in small, patient intervals, and thought about hours.

*You have hours, not days.* She had known it since Vasseur posted his notice; she had felt it in the interrogation room when he'd leaned forward and said, too softly, *A hymnal is a curious thing to steal, Nurse Marchand.* But knowing a thing and feeling its weight were different animals. Knowing was a fact on a page. The weight was this: somewhere under her floorboard — no. Not anymore. In the coffin, wrapped in oilcloth, riding in Marguerite's cellar among the pickled things — lay four years of names. Boys shot at dawn whose officers wrote *died of wounds*. Civilians buried in the register as *casualties of engagement*, as if they'd enlisted. And Anselm Marchette, private, reported killed at Ferme des Chênes, alive at this table eating bread, his true name existing nowhere in the world but her handwriting.

If the ledger stayed in Saint-Aubin past the signing, those names would simply never have happened. History would close over them like water.

"The third checkpoint," she said, and the kitchen went quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone finally lands the moth. "The chapel gate. Who's left?"

"Tomas would go," Anselm said. "Tomas would go from the guardroom if they'd unbolt the door."

"Tomas is not going anywhere," Marguerite said, "except home, once they realize arresting him was like arresting soup — it just sits there. So it isn't Tomas. It isn't me; I'm watched since the granary. It certainly isn't you, Elodie — Brenner looked at your face at the mill bridge the way a man looks at a word on the tip of his tongue."

"So it's me," Anselm said, and reached for the butter as if he'd been offered a second helping.

"You can barely walk."

"I can walk fine for one mile. How far is the chapel gate?"

"A mile and a half," Elodie admitted.

"Then I have half a mile to spare. I'll walk it slowly and arrive looking dignified. A wounded veteran escorting his aunt's coffin to consecrated ground — who searches a coffin going *toward* a funeral? Nobody likes funerals, Elodie. That's the whole reason they work."

"That was nearly the plan last time," Marguerite said darkly, "and last time the plan ended with a fire and Tomas in a cell."

"Last time the plan ended well," Anselm said. "The fire drew the guards, the diversion worked—"

"The diversion worked and my kitchen boy is eating prison bread."

"He says the prison bread is better than yours."

Marguerite picked up a wooden spoon with intent, and Anselm raised both hands in surrender, and Elodie laughed — actually laughed, a short surprised sound she hadn't made in weeks — and then the laughter caught on something in her chest and turned, briefly, into its cousin, and she had to look down at her bowl until her face agreed to behave.

Because underneath the banter, the arithmetic hadn't changed. One mile and a half. Three soldiers at a gate. A road beyond it that ran to Verrières, where men in good coats were at this very moment deciding whether the guns would stop. And hours — only hours — in which a broken clock, a dying sergeant, a deserter with a bad leg, and a nurse with ink-stained fingers had to beat an army's worth of paperwork to the table.

* * *

She went up the tower steps at dusk, against everyone's advice including her own common sense, because she wanted to see it.

The stairwell smelled of stone dust and lamp oil. Halfway up she passed the Delacroix brothers coming down, black to the elbows with grease, arguing in whispers about escapements with the passion other men reserved for football. On the platform, Henri Bonnefoy sat on an upturned crate beside the clock's opened heart, eating a sausage Marguerite had absolutely not sent up to him, and did not seem surprised to see her.

"Nurse Marchand." He wiped his hands on a rag that was filthier than his hands. "Come to inspect the work?"

"Come to see it move."

"Well." He gestured grandly at the mechanism, a wilderness of brass wheels and weights, ticking now with a low, patient heartbeat. "It moves. Whether it tells time is a theological question. We set it by guesswork — the sun, mostly, and Father Aubry's watch, which runs fast, because priests' watches always run fast, they're eager for the end of the world."

"What happens when it's time?"

Henri's face changed. The humor drained out of it the way water drains from a lock, leaving behind the serious machinery.

"When word comes," he said, "someone climbs these stairs and pulls that lever." He pointed to a simple iron handle, absurdly humble, bolted to the frame. "And the bell strikes twelve. You'll hear it in Verrières if the wind is right. You'll hear it under the ground."

"Who brings the word?"

"A rider from the commission. Or a pigeon. Or a rumor running faster than any of them — you know how news travels when people want it. Someone will come up those stairs, nurse. And when they do—" He shrugged. "Twelve strokes. Every soul in this town will know the war is over, or that it isn't, in the same heartbeat. No officer can stand between a town and its own bell."

Elodie looked out over the parapet. Saint-Aubin lay below in the blue-gray light of early evening: the rooftops furred with frost smoke, the checkpoint lamps already lit, the long straight line of the rue Basse running toward the mill bridge and, beyond it, the salt road and the chapel gate and Verrières. Somewhere out there, delegates were arguing over clauses. Somewhere out there, the eleventh hour was becoming the twelfth, in a way no clock in this town had been able to count for four years.

"Henri," she said. "How long does it take to wind?"

"Fifteen minutes, once it's warmed. Why?"

"How long until it's ready to strike? Truly ready? Not almost-ready the way your estimates are almost-accurate?"

He laughed, then saw her face, and stopped.

"Midnight," he said. "Midnight, and not a minute sooner, and I'll stake my reputation on it, such as it is." He cocked his head at her. "Why do you ask, nurse?"

Because the roads seal tighter tomorrow. Because Vasseur has connected the fire to the missing book, and a man who counts twice does not count forever without arriving at the coffin. Because Anselm's leg is bad and the gate is guarded and my heart has been beating too fast for four years and shows no sign of stopping.

"Because I need to know," she said, "how many strokes I have left."

* * *

She came down the stairs into trouble.

She knew it before she reached the bottom — the quality of silence in the courtyard had changed. Marguerite stood at the tower's base talking to Father Aubry, and both of them wore the particular expression of people who have received bad news and are deciding whether to soften it.

"What?" Elodie said.

Marguerite took her elbow and steered her toward the kitchen door before speaking. "A runner came through the checkpoint an hour ago. Army dispatch, sealed, straight to the guardhouse. Father Aubry has a friend among the clerks."

"And?"

"And it concerns the signing." Marguerite's voice was very steady, which was how Elodie knew it was very bad. "There's a clause in dispute. Property and reparations — who pays for what the war broke. The delegates are deadlocked. If they don't resolve it by tomorrow evening, the commission adjourns. Indefinitely."

"Indefinitely," Elodie repeated.

"The war continues. The occupation continues. The roads stay sealed." Marguerite pushed open the kitchen door. Warmth and the smell of onions rolled out to meet them, and for one absurd second Elodie hated the kitchen for being so ordinary while the world balanced on a clause. "And Vasseur gets exactly what he wants. A town that stays forgotten. Names that stay unwritten."

Inside, Anselm was asleep in his chair, mouth open, one hand still resting on the bread. Even exhausted, even hunted, he managed to make sleep look like a strategic decision.

"Wake him," Elodie said. "We go tonight. Before midnight. Before the deadline, before Vasseur thinks of the coffin, before—" She stopped. Listened.

Above them, faint through stone and timber, the clock ticked. Tick, tick, tick — the first heartbeat the tower had owned in four years, steady and indifferent, marking off strokes none of them could spare.

"Marguerite," she said quietly, "how quickly can you get the coffin out of the cellar?"

Marguerite was already reaching for her shawl. "Faster than you can get your coat on. Why do you think I sent the sausage upstairs — Henri needed feeding, and I needed you two floors away from this conversation until I was sure."

"You knew before the runner came."

"I'm a cook, dear. I know things before they happen. It's the fat and the onions." She tied the shawl and met Elodie's eyes, and all the humor went out of her voice at once, laid down like a knife before carving. "One thing. If the bell doesn't ring tomorrow — if the signing fails and the roads stay shut — Anselm must not come back through that gate. Whatever happens at the chapel, he keeps walking. Verrières, the coast, the next country if there is one. Promise me you'll tell him that, because he won't listen to me and he might listen to you, and if he dies carrying that book within sight of his own front door I will never forgive this war, and I am *very* creative about holding grudges."

"I'll tell him."

Elodie pulled on her coat. Her fingers found the small ink stain on the cuff that four years of washing had never fully lifted — her own private flag, flown at half-mast, or perhaps at full.

Above them the clock ticked on. Eleven minutes past four, forever, everywhere in Saint-Aubin — except now, except tonight, when the minutes had begun to move again, each tick a footstep on the stair, each hour a gate closing behind them.

Somewhere across the frozen fields, a mile and a half of bad road away, three soldiers stood at the chapel gate with orders to let no one pass.

By midnight, one of them was going to have to decide whose side he was on.
