## 19. Verrières Before the Ink Dries

The road to Verrières smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke, and Elodie walked it with a ledger against her ribs the way other women carried infants — close, protective, certain that any moment might require her to run.

Brenner had walked her through the chapel gate an hour past dawn and then simply stopped, standing aside like a door opening. He had not said goodbye. He had said, "The commission meets in the old silk exchange. Follow the men with good boots." And then, absurdly, he had straightened her collar, the way she supposed an older brother might, if she'd ever had one, and turned back toward the guns.

Now the road unspooled ahead of her through fog thinning to pearl. Carts passed her going the other way — refugees returning, astonishingly, walking backward out of their exile as though the war could be undone by simply retracing one's steps. An old man nodded to her. A boy chased a dog through a turnip field with the particular shrieking joy of a child who has recently learned that shelling is over, or nearly over, or at least paused long enough for dog-chasing.

Elodie counted her steps. It was a nurse's habit; she counted everything. Pulse, respirations, drops, hours. She had counted four hundred and eleven names into her ledger, and this morning every one of them seemed to press outward from the pages, leaning against the covers, wanting air.

*Four hundred and eleven,* she thought. *And Vasseur's arithmetic says perhaps three hundred of you never happened.*

Her feet hurt. Her hands were gray with coffin dust — Pelay's coffin, or rather the empty coffin that had carried Pelay's testimony and the ledger both, since Pelay himself lay in the convent garden under Marguerite's roses, which was, Elodie thought, a better posthumous posting than most soldiers ever received. She had buried him herself two days ago, in the rain, with Tomas reading the burial office badly and Anselm standing at attention on a leg that had no business standing. Marguerite had wept freely and also, between sobs, criticized the depth of the grave, because grief never entirely displaced practicality in Marguerite, and Elodie loved her for it.

The fog lifted as Verrières came down to meet her: a market town with a square, a fountain, and, dominating the eastern end, the old silk exchange — a building far too grand for its current purpose, all arched windows and carved lintels, now hung with flags of four nations and guarded by sentries who looked, to Elodie's professional eye, profoundly exhausted.

She stopped at the edge of the square and took inventory of herself, as she would inventory a patient before moving them.

One: she was filthy. Two: her papers had been seized weeks ago, so she could prove nothing about herself, which was either a liability or, given what she carried, a strange symmetry. Three: she had no appointment, no credentials, no standing whatsoever beyond being the sort of person armies preferred invisible. Four: she had, folded inside a hymnal in a flour sack over her shoulder, the only complete accounting of the dead of Saint-Aubin, plus a dying man's signed testimony against a colonel still in command of the district.

"Well," she said aloud, to no one, in the voice she used for patients who needed to hear confidence even when she didn't have it. "In you go."

A sentry at the steps held up a hand. "Commission business only."

"I have commission business," said Elodie.

"Name?"

And there was the question, wasn't it. The whole war in miniature. She had spent years making herself small, unlisted, unremarkable — the nurse who was furniture, the woman whose name appeared nowhere because appearing nowhere meant surviving everywhere. She could give a false name. She could turn around. She could leave the ledger in a church pew with a note and let providence do the rest, and providence, in Elodie's considerable experience, was a lazy colleague.

"Elodie Marchand," she said, and heard her own voice as if it belonged to a stranger — clear, unhurried, the voice of a woman listing names in a ledger at three in the morning. "Of Saint-Aubin. I've come to be entered into your record, since no one else has managed it."

The sentry blinked. Behind him, through the open doors, came the sound she had walked five hours to hear: raised voices. Delegates arguing over final terms, in French and English and something clipped and northern, words overlapping like weather.

"You can't just—"

"Corporal." Another officer coming down the steps, young, harried, ink on his fingers up to the second knuckle, the universal coloring of staff work. "What's the problem?"

"She wants in. No appointment."

The young officer looked at Elodie — really looked, which more people should have tried, she reflected; it was very educational — and his gaze snagged on the flour sack, then on her face, which she knew wore the expression Tomas called *the Sister's look*, the one that preceded soup being finished, bandages being changed, and arguments being lost.

"Are you a widow?" he asked, uncertainly.

"No. A witness."

"Witness to what?"

"To four hundred and eleven things your reports don't mention." She shifted the sack. "I understand the delegates are deciding how the war ends. I'd suggest they ought to know what the war was. I have it here, in writing, with signatures. Including one from Colonel Vasseur's own sergeant, dictated on his deathbed and witnessed by three people you can interview in Saint-Aubin, assuming the colonel hasn't arrested them all yet, which is possible, because he knows this exists, and he is at this moment a very frightened man with a great deal of authority."

There was a silence of the kind Elodie usually associated with the moment before a wound began bleeding.

The young officer rubbed his ink-stained fingers together, leaving gray smudges on his own jaw, and said, mostly to himself: "Chairman Delacourt has been asking for exactly this for two days. The casualty annex doesn't reconcile. He's threatening to hold the signature until it does." He looked up. "Can you make it reconcile?"

"Captain," said Elodie, "reconciling accounts is the only thing I have done since 1914."

Something in her tone made the corporal step aside without further instruction.

* * *

Inside, the silk exchange was a cathedral of paperwork.

Long tables had been dragged together into a great horseshoe, and around them sat the delegates — tired men in good cloth, surrounded by towers of folders, ashtrays overflowing like trench latrines, cold coffee in cups that had been refilled so many times they'd achieved a kind of resinous immortality. Clerks ran messages along the gaps between tables like water finding channels. At the head table sat a heavy-faced man with white hair and spectacles pushed up on his forehead — Chairman Delacourt, presumably — who was at this moment saying, in the aggrieved tone of a man who has repeated himself eleven times:

"—then explain to me again, Colonel, why your district's burial returns list ninety-one graves while your casualty returns list two hundred and six dead. Either your soldiers are being resurrected, which would be welcome news, or your arithmetic has a hole in it the size of a regiment."

And across the table, immaculate, rigid, his cap placed before him like an exhibit, sat Colonel Vasseur.

He looked smaller than Elodie remembered. That was the thing about bullies brought indoors under brighter lamps — the shadow shortened. His face was composed, his voice level, but Elodie, who had spent years reading faces across bedsheets for signs of what patients would not say aloud, saw the effort of the composition, the seams of it, the way his thumb pressed white against the table's edge.

"The discrepancy," Vasseur was saying, "is administrative. Rear-area engagements generate casualties recorded at corps level, not district—"

"Then produce the corps records."

"Some were lost in the autumn retreat."

"Conveniently, the ones with names in them."

Gentle laughter from somewhere down the horseshoe, quickly suppressed. Vasseur did not laugh. His eyes moved — and found Elodie.

She watched the recognition travel across his face like ice cracking on a pond: first blankness, then identification, then something rapid and calculating behind it, and beneath all of it, briefly visible, fear. He knew the hymnal was missing. He knew the ledger existed. He had sealed the roads for precisely this ledger, and here it was, in a flour sack, walking into his hearing on the arm of the woman he'd dismissed as furniture.

Elodie smiled at him. It was not, she acknowledged privately, a kind smile. Marguerite would have called it a kitchen smile — the one reserved for inspectors who criticized the larder.

"Who is that woman?" Vasseur asked, and his voice was admirably steady, and entirely failed.

Delacourt followed his gaze, removed his spectacles from his forehead, put them on his nose, and looked at her properly. "Madame," he said, "this session is closed."

"Then I'll wait outside," said Elodie, "for as long as it takes. Though I notice you're signing tomorrow, and I've walked since dawn, and I'm fifty-one years old, and my feet have opinions. So perhaps instead—" she set the flour sack on the nearest table, untied it, and drew out the hymnal, holding it up so the whole horseshoe could see it, "—you could hear me for ten minutes, and then decide whether I'm worth the wait."

"Colonel," said a delegate further down, dryly, "shall I have her removed? Or shall we hear what has your district commander vibrating like a tuning fork?"

Vasseur stood. "Mr. Chairman, this woman is a civilian under my administration. She has no authorization—"

"No authorization," Elodie agreed, stepping forward, "no rank, no party, no patron, no papers — you took those, Colonel, you'll remember, along with every register in Saint-Aubin, including the parish records, including the grave registries, including the school roll. Which means that as of your requisition order, the only written account of who died in Saint-Aubin and how is this book. In this bag. Which I am told makes it contraband." She laid the hymnal on the table before Delacourt, opened it, and turned to the first page. "I'd call that an indictment of your filing system before it's an indictment of anything else."

Delacourt read. His lips moved slightly. Around the horseshoe, conversation died the way fire dies when a door is opened — suddenly, completely.

"What is this?" Delacourt asked quietly.

"A ledger, Mr. Chairman. Four hundred and eleven names. Begun in the winter of '15 in a convent hospital at Saint-Aubin, continued every night since. Every name your officers' reports omit. The deserters shot at dawn — Private Lucien Aubry, who was nineteen, and whose file, per Lieutenant Brenner's own audit, was never filed. The civilians caught in crossfire — Madame Fouché, who was gathering herbs, and her grandson, whose name I had to ask the priest to spell. The boys who died before their paperwork arrived — there are thirty-one of those, and I wrote down what their friends called them, because sometimes that was all anyone had left." She turned a page. Turned another. Her hands were steady. Years of practice; she blessed them silently. "Your casualty annex doesn't reconcile, sir, because the war in my town was fought twice — once with bullets, and once with bookkeeping. This is the record of the second front."

"Where did you get these names?" one of the delegates asked. "If the registers were seized?"

"From the dying, sir. From the widows. From the man who dug the graves and counted them, and the boy who watched his brother fall and never left his side, and Sergeant Pelay — late of Colonel Vasseur's own headquarters staff — who signed this testimony four days before he died of his wounds." She laid the second document beside the hymnal. "It concerns the retreat at the Chemin des Prés, where a lieutenant named Emil Brenner and forty of his men were ordered abandoned, and where the subsequent reports were amended to say otherwise. Sergeant Pelay witnessed the amendment. He wrote his name under the truth at the end of his life, because he wished to arrive wherever soldiers arrive with clean hands."

The name Brenner went around the horseshoe in murmurs. Elodie noticed, without letting her face change, that Vasseur had gone the color of candle wax.

Delacourt took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, slowly, buying time the way statesmen do. "Madame Marchand," he said at last. "You understand what you are asking. We are hours from a signature. If I open the casualty annex tonight, the delegation may demand amendments, the amendments may demand verification, the verification may take days — and days, madame, may cost us the ceasefire entire. There are men at this table who will seize any irregularity as grounds to keep fighting. Is that a risk you're prepared to carry?"

It was the right question, and he asked it kindly, and Elodie honored it by thinking before answering. She thought of Anselm standing in the square under his true name. Of Tomas in a cell somewhere, memorizing names aloud to keep himself company. Of Marguerite feeding everyone within reach because grief without bread was just grief.

"Sir," she said, "I have spent four years watching men die politely, in the correct columns, with the correct forms, while the wrong facts were entered beside their names. I have watched a mother receive a death notice for a son who was alive, and a mother receive nothing at all for a son who was not. Your signature ends the shooting — God grant it does, and quickly; I want it more than anyone in this room, because I will personally bind the wounds of whatever happens if it fails. But a peace that is signed on top of a lie is only a truce with better manners. Read the names. All of them. Into your record, where no requisition order can ever reach them. It will take—" she calculated, nurse to the last, "—a little over three hours, read at funeral pace, which is the correct pace. And if your delegates cannot spare three hours to make the peace true, then I submit, respectfully, that they do not want peace. They want quiet. They are not the same thing, and my ledger knows the difference."

Silence. Somewhere a clock ticked — a real clock, a fat municipal thing above the doors, keeping time for a room that had nearly forgotten time existed.

Then a delegate near the end of the horseshoe — an Englishman, gray, with a face like a cliff that had seen weather — began, quietly, to laugh. Not mocking. Tired.

"Three hours," he said. "God knows we've wasted three years arguing about the color of map ink. Chairman — I'll stay for the reading. I think we all will." He looked around the table, daring objection. "Besides, if we sign tomorrow over an unreconciled casualty annex, some historian finds this woman in ten years anyway, and then we look like what we'd be. Better to be tired and honest tonight than rested and caught."

Delacourt looked down at the hymnal a long moment. Then at Vasseur, whose jaw worked and produced nothing. Then at Elodie.

"Madame Marchand," he said, "you will have your three hours. Clerk — enter the supplementary casualty record into the proceedings. Full reading. Names, ranks where known, circumstances where known." He settled his spectacles and, to Elodie's lasting surprise, his voice gentled. "Will you read them yourself? You know them best."

"Yes," said Elodie. "I know them best."

They arranged it with surprising speed once decided — clerks summoned, stenographers rubbing their eyes, a chair brought for her at the head table, which she declined in favor of standing, because she had read names at bedsides standing for four years and her voice had learned its pitch there. Vasseur was escorted from the floor — "to assist the inquiry," Delacourt said, in a tone that closed the phrase like a lid — and as he passed her, close enough that she smelled the starch of his collar, he paused.

"Mercy," he said softly. "That's all it was. Fewer names. Fewer widows. You've just made hundreds."

"A name is not a number, Colonel," Elodie said. "You never learned to count."

He went. The doors closed behind him with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

She began at seven minutes past four in the afternoon.

"Aubry, Lucien. Private, 2nd Battalion. Nineteen years old. Shot at dawn, 14 March, by his own side, for desertion under fire — except the fire was ours, misdirected, and he ran from it as any sane man would. Witnessed by Corporal Devereux, who survived, and whom I hope is alive to hear this."

The stenographer's pen scratched. The delegates sat still. Outside the tall windows the fog burned off entirely, and the light came in low and gold across the folders and the cold coffee, and Elodie read.

She read for two hours before her voice needed resting, and Marguerite — who had appeared at the doors mid-afternoon like weather off a mountain, having bullied, bribed, and bread-bartered her way past three separate sentries ("I cooked for the garrison at Verdun, young man, stand aside") — stood up in the gallery and said, "Take your water, girl. Names keep. Dead men aren't going anywhere, and neither, apparently, are committees."

Laughter — real, warm, human laughter — moved through the exhausted horseshoe, and Elodie drank her water, and laughed too, one short bark of it that surprised her out of her own chest, and then resumed.

"Fouché, Odette. Civilian. Herb-gathering. And Fouché, Henri, aged nine. Her grandson. Both killed 2 June by crossfire that the engagement report describes, quote, as regrettable. I would like the word 'regrettable' entered with them, so the record shows exactly what it covered."

Somewhere in the third hour, the English delegate began writing something of his own. In the fourth, one of the clerks — a round young man with a stutter — asked permission to add a name: his cousin, missing since '14, last seen near Saint-Aubin. Elodie checked her book, found the entry, read it aloud to him gently, and watched a young man learn, in public, in ink, the shape of a loss he'd been carrying blind. He thanked her. She said, "Now he's in the record twice. Once mine, once yours. That's how it should be. Names need carrying by more than one pair of hands."

By evening the light had gone amber, then blue. The reading ended where it had to end — with Anselm's entry, the living man recorded among the officially dead, marked now, in the margin, in fresh ink, in Elodie's own hand: *Survived. Present at this reading. See attached correction.*

"There," she said, closing the ledger at last. Her voice was worn to thread. "Four hundred and eleven. Entered, witnessed, and irrevocable. Requisition that, gentlemen."

Delacourt stood. So did the whole tired horseshoe — slowly, joints and chairs creaking in chorus — and Elodie realized, with a strange floating sensation, that they were rising for her, the invisible woman, the furniture, the nurse.

"Madame Marchand," Delacourt said, "the commission will reconvene at eight tomorrow morning to consider the corrected annex. The signing follows — God and the delegations willing." He paused. "You'll be present, of course."

"I wouldn't miss it," said Elodie, "for the whole of Colonel Vasseur's filing system."

She gathered the hymnal, the testimony, the flour sack. At the doors she stopped and looked back at the great hall — the towers of paper, the guttering candles, the stenographer asleep with his pen still in his fist — and thought of the convent clock in Saint-Aubin, repaired and waiting, holding its single struck hour like a breath.

Tomorrow the ink would dry. Tomorrow, twelve strokes.

Tonight, four hundred and eleven names slept in official record for the first time, and Elodie Marchand walked out into the dark of Verrières carrying nothing at all — and lighter than she had been in four years.

Behind her, through the closing doors, she heard the clerk begin, unprompted, to read the annex back — checking it, line against line, name against name — and the sound followed her down the steps like a bell learning its work.
