## 20. Vasseur's Arithmetic

Colonel Édouard Vasseur had always believed that the universe, if properly organized, could be made to fit on a single sheet of paper. Not a large sheet. A standard requisition form, ideally, with columns and a space at the bottom for his signature. He had spent thirty-one years in the army perfecting this belief, folding the world's chaos into neat boxes until it stopped screaming.

This morning, the world refused to fold.

The inquiry room in Verrières had once been the dining hall of a wine merchant's house — Vasseuer could tell by the tall windows, built to show off casks that were long since drunk or destroyed. Now it held a long table, three commission officers who would not look at him directly, a stenographer with cold hands, and, standing near the window like a woman waiting for a bus, Nurse Elodie Marchand of Saint-Aubin.

She had no business being there. She had every business being there. That was the whole problem.

"Colonel Vasseur," said the senior commissioner, a thin man named Delacour whose mustache seemed to be apologizing for his face, "you understand why you've been asked to appear."

"I understand that a nurse walked into your proceedings carrying an unauthorized ledger," Vasseur said. "I understand that she has made certain allegations. I understand that in the final week of a war, the commission found time to summon me over a notebook." He did not add what he thought about *that*, which was that history was written by whoever could not bear to leave things alone.

"You'll address the allegations, Colonel."

Vasseur folded his hands on the table. His hands were good hands — square, steady, the hands of a man who had never once dropped anything he was responsible for holding.

He began, as he always did, with the numbers.

* * *

"In the winter of '15, I commanded a withdrawal at Chemin des Barques," he said. "Two thousand men went in. My reports recorded one thousand six hundred and twelve casualties. The true figure, which I knew, was one thousand seven hundred and forty. I omitted one hundred and twenty-eight names."

He let that sit in the room like smoke.

"Why?" Delacour asked, because someone had to.

"Because one hundred and twenty-eight men had been blown apart in ways that made identification impossible. Listing them as dead without bodies, without effects, without graves — do you know what that does? It produces one hundred and twenty-eight widows who spend ten years writing letters to archives. Who dig up fields hoping. Who refuse to remarry because some clerk's report says their husband is *missing* rather than *dead*. I gave those women a clean number instead. One thousand six hundred and twelve. A number you can carry. A name with nothing under it is a wound that never closes. I closed it."

The stenographer's pen scratched. Somewhere outside, a lorry changed gears.

"And Saint-Aubin?" Delacour asked.

"Saint-Aubin," Vasseur said, and here his voice softened, because he genuinely believed what came next, and belief spoken sincerely is very hard to argue with. "Saint-Aubin is a town of four thousand souls sitting on a road the army needed. When we requisitioned it, my orders required me to inventory everything — every register, every document, every scrap of paper the civilians possessed. Including, I might add, parish death records listing civilians killed in the crossfire of battles they had no part in."

He looked at the commissioners.

"Do you know what those records are worth? Nothing operationally. Less than nothing strategically. But as evidence? As a list of names of people our artillery killed while retreating past their bakery? Those pages are an indictment. I ordered them surrendered and sealed — yes. And in doing so I spared this country a decade of recrimination at exactly the moment it will need to reconcile. Fewer names in the record, fewer ghosts in the streets. Fewer widows. Gentlemen, war produces enough grief to go around. My job was arithmetic. Arithmetic is mercy when it's done honestly."

He sat back. He had given versions of this speech to himself, at night, for years, and it had never once failed him. It was load-bearing. He had built his entire soul across it.

Which is why the sound of Elodie Marchand turning a page nearly unseated him.

* * *

She had brought the ledger. Of course she had brought the ledger. She stood at the table now and opened it — a fat, ordinary account book, the kind used for tracking flour and lamp oil — and turned to a page near the middle.

"May I?" she said, and did not wait for permission, which Vasseur noted was her way; he had seen it in the hospital, the quiet insolence of women who spend their nights around dying men and have lost all fear of the living.

"Private Henri Colombe," she read. "Seventeen years old. Died at Saint-Aubin on the ninth of December, two days before his enlistment papers arrived, so the army has no record he ever existed. His mother writes to the war office every month. She receives back a form saying no such soldier is listed. She thinks she invented her son." Elodie looked up. "You didn't close that wound, Colonel. You opened it and then told everyone there was no knife."

"That is an administrative gap—"

"Célestin Aubry. A deserter shot at dawn last March. Your reports list him as *killed in action, honorable*. His family received a medal and a lie in the same envelope. Marguerite Fossard, a cook, sixty-one, killed carrying water across her own street. Tomas Reyes, orderly—" she paused, and Vasseur saw her jaw tighten, "—arrested two days ago, currently in your stockade, charged with arson and theft of documents."

"He burned a granary."

"He burned *your* granary so that a book could reach this table. And it did. You're reading its consequences, Colonel. They have faces."

"Mademoiselle," Vasseur said, and he was surprised at how tired his voice came out, "you speak of faces. I command eleven hundred men. Do you know how many faces I am responsible for keeping attached to living bodies? Every decision I make trades some faces for others. I chose to trade *paper* instead of people. I scrubbed the record so that the record could not be used to grind anyone further. You call it erasure. I call it triage. Triage of memory."

"A name is not a number."

The room went very still. She hadn't raised her voice. She said it the way she presumably wrote in her ledger — plainly, in ink, meaning it to stay.

"A name is not a number," she repeated. "A number can be moved. Rounded down. Absorbed. A number has no mother. Henri Colombe had a mother, Colonel, and she has not invented him — *you* did, when you decided her grief was tidier without contents. You talk about mercy as though mercy is something you do *to* people, quietly, for their own good, without asking. Mercy requires consent. Otherwise it's only management."

Delacour cleared his throat. "Colonel, do you deny ordering the surrender and suppression of civilian records at Saint-Aubin?"

"I deny the characterization of *suppression*. I ordered custody—"

"Sergeant Pelay," Elodie said, laying another page flat, "dying, in my ward, signed a testimony stating that you ordered a retreat at Chemin des Barques that abandoned a full company — including Lieutenant Marcus Brenner — and then removed the unit from the rolls so that no inquiry would follow. He signed it. His hand shook, but he signed it."

"Pelay is dying and bitter—"

"Pelay," said a voice from the doorway, "is dying and honest. Which is more than most of us manage."

Lieutenant Aurel Brenner came into the room the way weather comes in — quietly, and changing everything. He set a document on the table: Pelay's testimony, witnessed, countersigned by a doctor. Then he set a photograph beside it, a young man in an ill-fitting uniform, and Vasseur felt the floor of his well-organized universe shift one centimeter, which was enough.

"My brother," Brenner said. "Absent from every record you kept, Colonel. Present in hers." He nodded at Elodie. "I audited your casualty returns for two years. They never matched. I told myself I was bad at arithmetic." He smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "I wasn't bad at arithmetic. I was comparing my counts against your mercy."

* * *

Later — much later, after the commissioners had conferred in murmurs behind cupped hands, after the stenographer's pen had worn through two nibs — Delacour read out the finding in a voice pitched somewhere between regret and relief.

"Colonel Édouard Vasseur, pending formal review, you are relieved of command. Your papers will be transferred to the commission. Sergeant Reyes will be released this hour. The records of Saint-Aubin will be restored to the town."

Vasseur stood. He straightened his tunic with both hands, the square steady hands, and found they were not entirely steady after all, which interested him distantly, the way one is interested in weather happening to other people.

"For the record," he said, "I want it noted that I loved this army."

"Noted," Delacour said gently. "That's rather the tragedy of it, Colonel."

* * *

They put him in a chair by the tall wine-merchant windows while the paperwork was drawn up, and to his surprise it was Elodie Marchand who stayed, sitting across from him with her ledger closed on the table between them like a guest who had overstayed but was too comfortable to leave.

"You think I'm a monster," Vasseur said.

"No," she said. "I think you're an accountant who mistook the ledger for the world. It's a gentler failing and a worse one." She poured herself water from the pitcher — his water, he noticed, though he couldn't imagine wanting it. "My grandmother kept bees. When a hive grew too large, half of it would leave, and the ones left behind would grieve — actually grieve, you could hear it in the sound of the hive, lower, sadder. My grandfather said you couldn't do the arithmetic for them. You couldn't decide fewer bees meant less grief. Grief doesn't scale down. It just goes where it goes."

"You're lecturing me with beekeeping."

"It's the gentlest lecture available," she said, and something in her mouth twitched — humor, actual humor, aimed at *him*, and he found to his horror that he almost welcomed it, the way a starving man welcomes bread thrown from a passing cart. "You've been doing sums alone in the dark for so long, Colonel, that you forgot the sums had people in them. That's all. You're not a monster. You're just lonely in a way you built yourself."

Vasseur looked out the tall windows. Beyond the rooftops of Verrières, beyond the wire and the sandbags, the sky was doing something pale and expectant, the particular gray-white of a morning that intends to become significant.

"They're going to sign today," he said. "Or it collapses, and everything we've said here becomes irrelevant for another winter." He paused. "If it holds — if the guns stop — I don't entirely know what I'm for anymore. Thirty-one years. A man organizes the universe onto a single sheet of paper and then someone takes the paper."

"The paper was never yours," Elodie said, not unkindly. "It was only ever borrowed from the names."

Outside, faintly, across the flattened distance, a single gun spoke. Then — Vasseur counted, because counting was the only prayer he knew — nothing. Ten seconds. Twenty. A bird somewhere near the window said something rude to another bird.

Thirty seconds. Forty.

The guns had gone quiet.

Elodie was already on her feet, ledger under her arm, heading for the door — of course she was, she had hours of road ahead of her and a town waiting to hear whether it could take its clock out of storage. At the threshold she turned back.

"Come to Saint-Aubin someday, Colonel," she said. "When it's safe. The town forgives slowly, but it does forgive, and Marguerite cooks for anyone who shows up hungry. Even accountants."

Then she was gone, and Vasseur sat alone in the wine merchant's dining hall with his relieved shoulders and his unsteady hands, listening to the silence where the artillery had been, and far away — impossibly far away, or perhaps only in the tower of a broken convent — he thought he heard the first stroke of a clock beginning, at last, to count.
