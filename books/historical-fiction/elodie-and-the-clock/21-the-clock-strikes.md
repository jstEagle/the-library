## 21. The Clock Strikes

### Tomas

Tomas had counted the hours of his arrest the way he once counted spoons in Marguerite's kitchen — obsessively, and with a running commentary no one asked for. Seventeen hours in a requisitioned schoolhouse with a chair, a window, and a guard named Filip who turned out, after four hours of Tomas's talking, to be from a village two valleys over and homesick enough to listen.

"You talk like the wall owes you money," Filip said at last, which was the most conversation anyone had offered Tomas since his capture.

"My mother said the same thing. Well. She didn't say *money*."

He had expected interrogation. Instead there was bread at midday and thin soup at evening, and a colonel somewhere upstairs shouting about paperwork, which Tomas took as a good sign. Armies that shout about paperwork are armies losing their grip on more important things.

At dawn on the second morning, Filip unlocked the door with an air of tremendous boredom. "Orders came through. You're released. The roads are open." He paused, then added, grudgingly, "They say it's over. The signing held."

Tomas stood so fast the chair went over backward. "It *held*? It actually—"

"Congratulations," Filip said, in the flat voice of a man who had been told not to celebrate anything, and meant it anyway, a little, behind the flatness.

Tomas was out the door before the echo finished. He ran through streets still silvered with frost, past shuttered windows that were beginning — one by one, like eyes opening — to let shutters swing wide. An old man on a step nodded at him. A dog trotted beside him for half a street as if it too had heard the news and approved of the running.

The convent tower rose above the rooftops ahead of him, and its clock face — scrubbed, oiled, resurrected by six weeks of evenings and Pépin the wheelwright's blasphemous language — showed three minutes to noon.

Tomas slowed to a walk. Some things deserved to be arrived at properly.

* * *

### Marguerite

Marguerite had decided, some years ago and never rescinded, that she did not climb towers. Her knees held opinions and her dignity held more of them, and both agreed that bell-pulling was work for younger backs. And yet here she was, halfway up the convent tower's spiral stair, gripping the rope with both hands while Pépin shouted instructions from below that the stone walls chopped into nonsense.

"*When I nod!* Not before! You'll know the nod when you see it!"

"I've known nods my whole life," Marguerite muttered. "I married one."

She had not climbed the tower for the glory of it. She had climbed it because someone ought to ring the bell who remembered what bells were for. In her first winter of the war, before the occupation, the church bells of Saint-Aubin had rung for weddings and harvests and, once, terribly, for a flood. Then the army silenced them, and the town went quiet in a way Marguerite felt in her teeth every single day, like a tongue worrying a missing tooth.

Now the rope hummed in her hands, alive, waiting.

Below her, through the narrow window, the square filled. She could pick out faces: Madame Roux with her market basket, which contained no market goods but rather a bottle Marguerite herself had provisioned; old Chevalier standing straighter than he had in four years; a scatter of soldiers who were supposed to be packing up and instead stood looking upward like everyone else, off-duty in their hearts if not yet their boots.

And near the fountain — Anselm. Standing in plain sight, hat in hand, in the middle of the square where anyone might read his face and know it. Where anyone might call his name and he would answer to it.

*There* he is, Marguerite thought, and something in her chest went warm and tight at once. A boy who had spent a year being nobody, standing in the brightest open space in town, being somebody on purpose.

Pépin appeared in the stairwell below, red-faced, and gave her a nod of such violence she nearly lost her footing.

"About time," she said, and pulled.

* * *

### Anselm

The first strike of the bell went through Anselm like weather.

It was not a beautiful sound. The old bell had a crack in it, or the crack was in the town, or in him — it was hard to tell where the flaw lived, only that the note wavered on the way down, bent slightly south of true, so that each stroke arrived perfect and left imperfect, like everything else that had survived.

Twelve times it fell over the square. People stopped in the streets. A woman dropped to her knees by the fountain and covered her face. Two soldiers removed their caps without being told. Somewhere a child began to laugh at the sheer volume of it and was not shushed.

Anselm stood in the open with his name on.

That was all. That was the whole act. He had rehearsed it in his head for days — what he would do when the news came, whether he would hide again out of habit, whether the old reflexes of erasure would take him. And then the moment arrived and found him simply standing there, Private Anselm Corvec, fourth son of a tinsmith, deserter once, pardoned by accident, survivor by worse luck than any of it — present. Counted.

A hand landed on his shoulder. He turned and it was Madame Roux, who had fed him soup in the hospital and never once asked what he'd done to earn a bed there.

"Corvec," she said, testing the name aloud, deliberately, the way you'd test ice before stepping. "Your mother's boy. The one who could whistle through his hands. I remember you now."

"Yes, madame," Anselm said, and his voice cracked on it, and he found he did not mind.

"Then stand up straight," she said. "You're being looked at."

And he was. All across the square, faces turning toward the sound of their own names being called out in ones and twos — *Corvec! Roux! Chevalier!* — the evening ritual Tomas had organized, done now in daylight, done without fear, done as greeting instead of resistance. A town remembering itself out loud, and liking the sound.

The bell's last stroke faded into the hills. In the ringing silence after, Anselm heard the fountain, and a cart on the far road, and under everything, very faint and very far, no guns at all. Four years of a low continuous dread he had mistaken for silence — and now actual silence, which was different, which was enormous.

He whistled through his hands. The sound split the square, high and rude and unmistakably alive, and half the town laughed, and Madame Roux squeezed his shoulder hard enough to bruise, approvingly.

* * *

### Elodie

Elodie watched the bell-tolling from the convent steps, the ledger's empty successor tucked under her arm out of pure habit — the new book, the blank one, though its pages were no longer quite so blank as they'd been. Word had come from Verrières at midmorning, carried by a breathless army cyclist who read the announcement in the square: signed at dawn, ratified, holding. The omitted names had been read into the record on the second day of negotiations. Forty-one of them. She knew the number because she had listened to every name read aloud in a room full of men in good coats, and had watched each name land on the table like a small stone, until the pile of stones could no longer be called gravel.

Vasseur had been relieved of command the same afternoon. There would be an inquiry. She had been asked — politely, warily, by a captain with kind exhausted eyes — whether she intended to attend.

*I intend,* she had said, and surprised them both with how little ceremony the word required.

Now the crowd in the square was beginning to move like water finding its old channels. Someone had produced an accordion. Madame Roux's basket-bottle was making the rounds with the efficiency of a field ambulance. And cutting through the crowd toward her, moving like a man who had been walking for days and had decided to stop only when geography physically prevented further progress, came Lieutenant Aurel Brenner.

Out of uniform collar, almost. Coat open, no gloves, carrying nothing but a document folder and a look she had not seen on him before — or rather, a look she had seen on him always, underneath, finally promoted to his face.

"Lieutenant," she said.

"Formerly," he said. "As of this morning I'm a civilian with excellent references and a complicated file." He held out the folder. "My brother's record. Restored. Corrected. They let me read the final version myself." His voice did something small on the word *corrected*. "Henri Aurel Brenner. Died holding the crossroads at Ferme-Desjardins. Not missing. Not abandoned on paper. Dead, and named, and mourned in ink."

"He's in my book too," Elodie said quietly. "He has been from the first week."

"I know." He looked at the new ledger under her arm. "May I?"

She handed it over. He opened it to the first page and read the heading she had written there — *For the record, and against forgetting* — and closed it again gently, as if it were sleeping.

"There's an inquiry," he said. "They'll want testimony. Yours. Pelay's page. Mine, if mine counts for anything now."

"It counts," Elodie said. "It may be the only kind they can't dismiss, a man testifying against his own side's arithmetic."

"That's flattering."

"It wasn't meant to be."

He almost smiled. Across the square, the accordion stumbled into something resembling a dance tune, and Marguerite emerged from the tower door below them, descending with the grim triumph of a general leaving a won field, and Tomas burst from the crowd at a dead run, waving both arms, shouting something about Filip and the schoolhouse and soup owed.

"The inquiry is in the spring," Brenner said. "In Verrières. They'll put us up. Officially."

"Us?"

"You didn't think," he said, "that I'd let you walk into that room alone again?"

* * *

### Tomas

Tomas reached the convent steps out of breath, which was fitting, because he had been out of breath in spirit for seventeen hours and a lifetime, and only now did the two states agree.

"You're free!" he announced to Elodie, unnecessarily, since he was the one who'd been in the schoolhouse. "I'm free! Everyone's free! Filip says the guard post is being dissolved Thursday and he wants to know if Marguerite does wedding cakes, because—" he paused, recalculating what was public information, "—because he's a young man with prospects and I promised nothing."

"Tomas," Elodie said.

"Also there's a goat loose on the salt road that I feel personally responsible for, but that can wait—"

"*Tomas.*"

He stopped. She was looking at him with an expression he had learned, over these months, to read the way sailors read weather: the small crinkle at her eyes that meant the world had, against all evidence, behaved.

"They're going to read the names," she said. "Here. Tonight. In the square. All forty-one. The commission sent a copy of the record so the town could hear its own." She pressed the new ledger into his hands. "I thought perhaps you'd want to do the honors. Since you taught half of Saint-Aubin to say them in the dark."

Tomas looked down at the blank pages, waiting to be lived in, and then up at the square — at Marguerite planting her feet before the crowd like a woman about to carve a roast, at Anselm standing tall with his cap in his hands, at Pépin arguing with the accordionist, at soldiers and civilians mixed together in the failing light with nothing between them anymore but memory, and not even that for long.

"Well," Tomas said, and cleared his throat, and found it full. "Obviously I'll need everyone's attention. Which, historically, has never been a problem of mine."

The crowd gathered. Lanterns were lit. Marguerite stood at Tomas's right hand like a fortress with an apron on, and Elodie at his left, and above them all the clock in the tower ticked onward, keeping honest time at last, striking the hour merely because hours deserve striking, needing no longer to carry messages of rescue or ruin.

Tomas opened the book and read the first name into the evening air, clear as a bell with no crack in it at all, and the town answered, as it had learned to do:

*"Present."*

One voice first. Then ten. Then all of them, for every name, the whole square answering *present, present, present* into the dark, until the list was done and the accordion started up again and the bottle made another round, and Saint-Aubin stood in the noise of its own survival, counting itself, all present, none missing, at least for tonight —

though tomorrow, Elodie thought, watching Brenner light a lantern at the square's edge and turn toward Verrières and the spring, toward the inquiry and the room full of men with good coats and bad ledgers, tomorrow there would still be arithmetic to answer for, and she intended to be there, book in hand, when it was.
