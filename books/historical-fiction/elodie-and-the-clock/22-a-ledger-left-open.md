## 22. A Ledger Left Open

The tribunal room in Verrières had once been a wedding hall. Elodie knew this because the caretaker told her so while she waited on the bench outside, her ledger — the new one, the second one, though she thought of it as the first honest one — balanced on her knees. The ceiling still bore painted garlands of roses, faded to the color of weak tea, and someone had hung a Republic's banner over a nail hole where a chandelier used to live.

"Weddings," the caretaker said again, wistful. "Forty years of them. Then four years of nothing but paperwork."

"Some paperwork matters," Elodie said.

He looked at her ledger and seemed to understand without being told what it was. Word had traveled ahead of her, as word does in small towns, even towns that belong to no one in particular during an armistice commission. *The nurse from Saint-Aubin. The one with the book.*

When they called her name — her full name, spoken aloud into a room of strangers as if it were a fact worth announcing — she stood, smoothed her skirt, and did not hide it under her coat this time. She carried it in plain view, both hands, the way one carries bread to a table or a child to be christened.

The commissioners sat at a long table beneath the painted roses. Three of them: a thin man with a general's posture and a civilian's coat, a woman with sharp spectacles who had been introduced only as *Madame Ozenne, records division*, and a third who spent most of his time writing small notes to himself and underlining things. They had heard testimony all morning. A quartermaster. Two sergeants. A priest who had buried more men than his registers could account for.

Elodie opened the ledger at the first page and read the first name aloud.

*Henri Colle, private, seventeen, died 14th of March before his enlistment papers reached the depot.*

Her voice did not shake. She had expected it to shake. She had rehearsed trembling the way one rehearses a speech, privately, in Marguerite's kitchen, and Marguerite had said, "You'll not need it, girl. You've said these names every evening for a year. Your mouth knows the road."

She was right, as she usually was, and wrong about nearly everything else, as she occasionally was, and Elodie loved her for both.

She read the next name. And the next. She explained, when Madame Ozenne asked, that these names came from her own observation, from ward lists the officers never filed, from the accounts of orderlies and priests and one dying sergeant whose signed page was appended at the back. She explained that Colonel Vasseur had ordered documents surrendered, that she had hidden a hymnal inside a ledger rather than a ledger inside a hymnal — the commissioners' clerk got this confused twice, and the second time Elodie corrected him gently, and the thin man with the general's posture almost smiled.

"You understood these omissions to be deliberate?" Madame Ozenne asked.

"I understood them to be convenient," Elodie said. "Which in my experience is worse than deliberate. Deliberate men can repent. Convenient men just keep counting."

Silence. The underlining commissioner underlined something very hard.

It took four hours. When it ended, the thin man stood, which apparently he had not done for any prior witness — the caretaker would tell her this later, delightedly, as though Elodie had won a prize at the fair — and said, "These names will be entered into the record, Mademoiselle Marchand. All of them. Read aloud, in full, before the signing is ratified."

"In full," Elodie repeated. It wasn't a question. It was a condition she was checking like a latch.

"In full," he agreed. "It will take some time."

"Good," said Elodie. "It should."

* * *

Spring came late to Saint-Aubin that year, but when it came, it came extravagantly, the way Marguerite cooked — everything at once, too much of everything, and to hell with moderation. The orchard behind the convent blossomed so heavily that petals drifted into the open ward windows and settled on blankets like a polite snow. Tomas claimed the pear tree had waited four years out of spite and was now making up for lost time; he said this while sweeping petals out the door, and he swept them toward the light rather than away from it, so they'd blow back in, which was either laziness or poetry depending on whether you asked Marguerite.

The hospital was a hospital again — properly, officially, with a red cross on the door instead of a requisition number. Half the wounded were gone home. Anselm was one of them, though "home" for Anselm had turned out to mean Saint-Aubin, because he had gone back to his village for three days and reported, over Marguerite's stew, that there was nothing left there but an aunt who didn't remember him and a dog that remembered him too well and bit him.

"So you came back for the nursing," Marguerite said.

"I came back for the stew," Anselm said. "The nursing is incidental."

He was working in the orchard now, and walking without the limp, and every evening in the square he answered to his true name when Tomas called the roll of names — the ritual they'd kept up through the occupation, memorizing aloud what the papers refused to hold. Anselm's name was on the town's new memorial stone, carved by the stonemason free of charge, though the mason had grumbled the whole time about how many names there were and how little space, and had finally solved the problem by carving smaller letters at the bottom, which everyone agreed was exactly the kind of compromise the whole war had been built on.

Elodie kept the new ledger in the convent office now, openly, on a shelf, beside the inventories and the ward lists. It was a different sort of book. The first pages held the testimony record — certified copies, stamped, official, the omitted names now written into the national archive in a clerk's careful hand as well as her own. But past those pages, the book went blank, and she had told no one why she'd bought a ledger two-thirds empty except perhaps Marguerite, who had watched her wrap it in brown paper at the stationer's and said, "Ah," in the particular tone she reserved for understanding something before Elodie did.

* * *

Lieutenant Aurel Brenner arrived on a Thursday, in a civilian coat that fit him badly, as though he had bought it in a hurry to stop being a lieutenant and hadn't yet learned how to stand in it.

Elodie found him in the convent garden, standing at the gate like a man who had rehearsed knocking and then forgotten. He looked thinner than he had at the checkpoints. His hands, those meticulous counting hands, were folded behind his back.

"Lieutenant," she said.

"Not anymore. Aurel." He said his own name as if trying it on. "I resigned the commission. There's an inquiry ongoing, and I gave my testimony, and after the testimony I found I had no further use for the uniform. It kept telling me what to count."

"And now?"

"Now I count for myself." He produced a folder — a real folder, government-stamped, soft at the corners from much carrying. "The records division finished last month. My brother." He opened it with the care of a man unwrapping something breakable. "Restored. Full record. Date of death, place, circumstances, witness statement — Sergeant Pelay's account, transcribed, signed by three commissioners. He's listed as fallen in the line of duty, abandoned by a withdrawal ordered against counsel. Not missing. Not scrubbed. *Fallen.*"

"Aurel," Elodie said quietly, using his chosen name back at him.

"There's a grave now. Well — a marker. The village where the retreat happened. The people there knew the unit. They buried them in the churchyard and wrote the names on a wooden cross and nobody ever told the army, because nobody ever asked." He laughed, a short unsteady sound. "Four years I audited reports. Four years of arithmetic. And the answer was a carpenter in a village churchyard with good handwriting."

"The truth keeps poor books," Elodie said, "but it keeps them honestly."

"That's exactly what Pelay said." Brenner looked up sharply, and then softened. "You were there for that. For his last watch."

"I was there. He signed the page with a hand that shook and his signature came out steadier than mine does on a good day."

They stood in the garden a while. Blossoms came down around them. Somewhere in the orchard, Anselm was arguing with the pear tree about a ladder.

"I owe you a debt," Brenner said finally.

"You don't. You walked me through the chapel gate. We're square."

"We are not square. I sealed roads for that man for three years. I inventoried a town down to its spoons while its people burned their own letters. I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I would like to not be square. I would like to owe it, and pay it, slowly, in installments, for the rest of my life. It's the only arithmetic I trust anymore."

Elodie considered him. Then she walked to the gate, opened it fully, and stepped aside.

"The ward needs an orderly on Thursdays," she said. "Tomas will work you mercilessly and Marguerite will feed you as though you're under interrogation. Anselm will tell you desertion stories until you beg for mercy. We are a terrible household and you will hate Thursdays and you will come anyway."

Brenner looked at the open gate for a long moment. Then he stepped through it, and Elodie noticed that his shoulders came down from around his ears for the first time since she'd known him, as though a body could be counted out of its posture and back into peace.

* * *

Marguerite declared a feast for the anniversary of the armistice, which was also — by her own unsolicited proclamation — the anniversary of the granary fire, the sealing of the roads, the clock striking twelve, and several other events she had survived and intended to go on surviving annually, with pastry.

The whole town came. The mason brought his smaller-letters chisel as a joke and was made to carve the roast instead. Father Lucan said grace that mentioned, by name, six people the old registers had missed, and the room said their names back, which was the roll call now, the permanent one, the one Tomas had started with a handful of stubborn voices in a frightened kitchen and which had grown until calling it took twenty minutes and everyone stayed for supper afterward.

Anselm carved the roast. Brenner, on his fourth Thursday, was bullied into washing dishes and discovered he liked it — finite tasks, visible progress, a stack that diminished instead of multiplying. Tomas taught the schoolchildren the roll call, shortened to the ones born in Saint-Aubin, and the children recited it with the solemnity of children everywhere, which is to say they fidgeted magnificently and got every name right.

And Elodie sat at the end of the table with her ledger open on her knees, and near midnight, when the noise had gone warm and low, Marguerite dropped into the chair beside her and nodded at it.

"Well? Show me the famous blank pages."

"They're not famous. Nobody knows about them."

"Everyone knows about them. Tomas saw you wrapping the paper at the stationer's and told me, and I told Father Lucan, and now it's practically scripture." Marguerite leaned over. Page after blank page, clean lines, waiting. She frowned. "You've not written a thing. Months, girl. Not one entry?"

"One," Elodie said, and turned to the first blank leaf.

At the top, in her careful hand:

*Aurel Brenner, restored to his brother's record, spring.*

And below it:

*Anselm Ferriol, planted six apple trees in the convent orchard, autumn. Survived frost. Expected to thrive.*

"That's not how ledgers work," Marguerite said, but her voice had gone thick.

"It's how this one works. The last one recorded what the war subtracted. This one records what we add back. Names yet to be lived, not lost." Elodie ran her thumb along the blank pages. "There's room for a great deal, Marguerite. Births. Weddings — this was a convent, mind, but God has shown a sense of humor lately and I'm willing to test it. Harvest counts. That ridiculous pear tree's yield. Whoever Brenner becomes when he stops apologizing. Whatever Anselm grows."

"And you? What do you write for yourself?"

Elodie looked down the table — Tomas laughing with the mason, Anselm defending his trees against three contradictory pieces of advice, Brenner drying a dish towel with the focus of a man defusing ordnance, Father Lucan asleep sitting up with dignity — and she thought about the woman she had been in the occupied winter, invisible, essential, recording the dead so the dead would not become arithmetic.

"I'll write myself in last," she said. "That's the rule of the book. Witnesses sign at the end."

Marguerite snorted, wiped her eyes with her apron in a motion disguised as dabbing at flour, and stood to refill glasses.

* * *

Summer, and the pear tree delivered on its extravagant promises. The commission published its final report; the names were in it, all of them, printed in a column so long the typesetter complained for a week and then framed a copy for his shop window. Vasseur's inquiry closed in the spring with quiet finality; Elodie never learned the particulars beyond what the newspapers said, and found, somewhat to her surprise, that she did not need to. A name was not a number. Neither was a verdict. Some things you handed over and walked away from lighter.

On an evening in July, Elodie sat in the orchard with the ledger, adding the day's entry: the pear harvest estimate (Tomas's figure, doubled for optimism), Anselm's first apple blossom (he had checked the trees eleven times), and a note that Father Lucan had been seen laughing, cause unknown, investigation ongoing.

Footsteps on the grass. Brenner, out of uniform permanently now, working three days a week at the records division in Verrières — re-auditing, at his own insistence, four years of casualty reports across two armies, finding the wooden crosses and good handwriting wherever they'd been overlooked. He'd been passed over for promotion twice for it and spoke of the demotions the way other men spoke of medals.

"You're writing in daylight," he observed. "You used to hide this thing under hymnals."

"Hymnal season's over." She tilted the page so he could read. "There's a line for you, when you're ready. First entry's free — your brother's restoration. After that you have to earn them."

Brenner sat down heavily in the grass beside her, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was the kind of conversation they'd gotten best at.

Then he cleared his throat, took out his own notebook — small, black, meticulously kept, because some habits survive revolutions — and said, "Actually, I've been keeping a list of my own. Reports that still don't reconcile. Names still absent from official record, other departments, other winters. Forty-one so far." He glanced sideways at her, and there was something in his face she recognized from the mill bridge checkpoint — the look of a man about to risk everything on a single honest count. "I can't read them into any commission. But I thought... if your book has room, and your voice doesn't shake..."

Elodie looked at the long stretch of blank pages ahead, and at the man beside her offering her forty-one more reasons to keep it open, and she picked up her pen.

"Read me the first one," she said.
