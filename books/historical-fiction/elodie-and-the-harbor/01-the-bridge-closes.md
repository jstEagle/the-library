## 1. The Bridge Closes

The road into Vasseur smelled of coal smoke and frozen mud, and Elodie Pemberton walked it with her hand pressed flat against her coat as though she could hold the lining together by will alone. The stitches were small — she had sewn them herself by candlelight in a farmhouse loft three nights ago, unpicking the seam beneath her left arm and laying the oilcloth packet against her ribs where a heartbeat might disguise its presence. Eleven pages. Four signatures. Enough to hang three generals of the republic she had spent the war serving.

She had told herself the hard part was over. The roads behind her had been full of checkpoints and rumor; the roads ahead, she had believed, would be merely cold.

Then she crested the last hill above the river town and saw the bridge.

It stood where it had always stood, iron-boned and black against the white river, spanning the water from Vasseur's crooked quay to the far bank where the north road climbed toward the capital. But this morning it wore soldiers the way a fence wears frost. She counted them from the ridge — a dozen at least, gray coats and bayonets, a supply wagon drawn crosswise at the near end like a cork in a bottle. As she watched, two men in officer's greatcoats unrolled something long and official-looking across the planked roadway, and even at this distance she knew what it was. A proclamation. A seal. A season's worth of *no*.

Elodie stood in the wind and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do since the farmhouse. The river froze solid by the first week of December — everyone said so, the farmers, the ferryman's widow at Marle, the boy who had driven her the last ten miles in a cart of sugar beets. When the ice came, the bridge closed for maintenance and the ferries hauled out, and Vasseur became an island of stone and silence until the thaw. No one crossed. No one was meant to.

She had eleven pages against her ribs and perhaps five days before the river locked like a fist.

"Last chance to turn around," the beet-cart boy had joked at the crossroads, and she had smiled and paid him too much, and now the joke sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She went down into the town.

* * *

Vasseur in winter was a town holding its breath. Smoke rose straight from the chimneys because there was no wind off the water, only that peculiar stillness rivers make when they are thinking about freezing. The market square was half-shuttered. A woman beat a rug from an upstairs window and stopped beating it to watch Elodie pass, and Elodie lowered her eyes and became, by effort of will, nobody: a tired traveler in a good coat gone shabby, a woman with chapped hands and no business anyone needed to know about.

But the coat was the problem. The coat was always going to be the problem. It hung on her now with a weight no tailor would have found, and every soldier's eye that slid across her felt it too, or she imagined it did, which amounted to the same thing.

She told herself she would go first to the harbor office, confirm what she'd seen from the ridge, and then decide. The harbor office was a low stone building at the foot of the quay, and outside it a corporal was nailing a printed notice to the door while a knot of townsfolk read over his shoulder and muttered.

"*By order of the Provisional Garrison,*" the notice began, and Elodie read the rest without moving her lips, the way her father had taught her, so that no one watching could follow her eyes. *The bridge at Vasseur is closed to all traffic for seasonal repair until such time as the river permits safe passage. All crossings, by bridge or ferry or otherwise, require written authority of the garrison commander. Signed, Colonel Armand Deverell.*

Deverell. She turned the name over and found nothing — a stranger, then, or new enough not to matter. What mattered was the third line: *all crossings... require written authority.* Not the bridge alone. The river itself. They had closed the water.

An old man beside her spat thoughtfully into the gutter. "Same as '12," he said to no one. "Ice comes, we're prisoners till spring."

"Not prisoners," said his companion. "Just forgotten. There's a difference."

"There isn't," said the old man, "when you're hungry."

Elodie stepped back from the crowd. Her breath came out in a thin white ribbon and hung there. Five days, maybe less. And if the garrison was closing the river, someone above Colonel Deverell wanted the town sealed — wanted whatever moved through Vasseur to stop moving. She could not know whether the dispatches were the reason or merely an accident caught in the net, and not knowing was its own kind of torture. Paranoia was a luxury she couldn't afford and couldn't put down.

There was one more thing the harbor office reminded her of, and it took her a moment, standing in the cold, to understand why her feet had already begun to carry her along the quay toward the point.

The lighthouse.

* * *

It was smaller than she remembered, or perhaps everything from before the war was smaller than she remembered. The tower rose from the rocks at the harbor mouth, whitewashed once but weathered now to the color of old bone, its lantern gallery dark against the pale sky. Beside it squatted the keeper's cottage, smoke rising from its chimney in a straight gray thread, and a rowboat pulled up on the shingle, and stacks of split wood under oilcloth, and a life lived in miniature against the enormous indifference of the river.

She had told herself she would not come here. She had told herself many things on the road from Marle.

Her knuckles were on the cottage door before she had finished deciding.

Footsteps. The latch lifted, and Tomas Reyes stood in the doorway with a hatchet in one hand and kindling under his arm, and for three full seconds neither of them moved.

He had filled out. That was the first thing — absurdly, the first thing. The boy who had left for the artillery at nineteen had been all wrists and collarbones; the man in the doorway was broad through the shoulders, dark-bearded, with a scar splitting one eyebrow that hadn't been there before. His eyes were the same, though. Dark and level and unhurried, eyes that had once looked at her across a summer of stolen evenings on this same quay, and she watched them change — surprise, then something warmer, then something guarded sliding over the warmth like ice forming on still water.

"Elodie Pemberton," he said.

"Tomas." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "They told me you'd taken the light."

"They told you true." He looked past her shoulder at the empty quay, then back, and set the hatchet down inside the door with deliberate care. "Come in before the whole town sees you standing here. Cold's coming in off the water."

The cottage was one room and a loft, scrubbed floor, iron stove, books stacked on every horizontal surface — he had always been a reader; that hadn't changed either. He fed the stove without asking if she wanted warmth, which was its own kind of answer, and put water on to boil, and only then turned to face her across the small bright room.

"You're thinner," he said. "You're walking like your boots hurt. And you flinched when the stove door banged." He folded his arms. "I spent four years learning to read soldiers, Elodie. You've got a soldier's walk and a courier's eyes. So before I ask anything else — why does the sight of you make me feel like the roof's about to come in?"

Outside, far off across the water, a bugle sounded from the bridgehead, thin and clear in the frozen air. The sound of a garrison settling in for a long winter.

"I need to cross the river," she said. "Before the ice takes it. And I think you're the only man in Vasseur who can get me across."

Tomas didn't answer at once. He poured the water over the leaves in the pot, slow and exact, and when he finally looked up there was something in his face she couldn't name — regret, maybe, or warning — and it frightened her more than the soldiers at the bridge had.

"The ice isn't what should worry you," he said quietly. "Sit down, Elodie. There are things you need to know about this town before you trust me with anything."
