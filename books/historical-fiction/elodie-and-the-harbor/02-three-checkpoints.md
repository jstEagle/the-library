## 2. Three Checkpoints

Tomas laid it out for her the way he must once have charted shipping lanes — with a pencil, a stub of charcoal, and the back of an old tide table pinned to the wall of the lighthouse's small kitchen. Three marks in ink along the river's southern curve, each one a door she would have to pass through, each one held by a man.

"The customs house first," he said. "Old Ferenc keeps the ledgers there. He was your father's clerk, wasn't he?"

"Eleven years." Elodie touched the paper where his pencil had circled the customs house. "He used to bring me candied ginger when I sat under the desk and pretended to read dispatches. He called me *mademoiselle la petite ministre*."

"Ferenc is not what he was. The republic pensioned him off with a title and no work when your father fell. He drinks now. He talks when he drinks." Tomas's voice was careful, stripped of anything that could be mistaken for judgment. "But he still keeps the keys to the weighbridge records, and the weighbridge records are how you'd explain yourself at the second checkpoint."

"And the garrison gate?"

"Captain Reisz." The name came out flat as a coin on stone.

Elodie closed her eyes. She saw the courtroom without wanting to: the winter light through tall windows, the smell of wet wool and lamp oil, her own voice — nineteen years old, coached by her father, certain of its own virtue — reading testimony that ended a soldier's career and very nearly his life. Reisz had taken bribes from the harbor contractors; she had sworn to it; her word had been believed because her family's word had always been believed.

"He was cleared of the worst of it," she said. "After the review board. They reduced his sentence."

"They gave him Vasseur," Tomas said. "Which for a man like Reisz is worse than prison. A garrison of forty men and nothing to guard but ice. He knows who put him here. When he sees your face—" Tomas stopped himself, then finished anyway. "He'll know you. You were famous once, Elodie. Everyone in this town remembers whose daughter testified against him."

She looked down at her hands. They did not shake. She had trained them not to, years ago, in corridors where shaking meant weakness and weakness meant everything her father had built would come apart. But beneath the steadiness her pulse ticked like a clock in an empty house.

"And the ferry landing," she said. "Who holds the last door?"

"No one who loves you or hates you. Which may be safer than either." Tomas tapped the third circle. "Aurel Danek. Smuggler, ferryman, keeper of whatever the garrison prefers not to see. He owes nothing to your name and less to the republic. He'll take anyone across for the right price — except now the price isn't money. The ice is going soft in the middle channel. No boat crosses until it breaks, and no one walks the ferry road past the landing without Danek's say-so. He answers to Reisz on paper and to himself in truth."

Elodie studied the three circles until they blurred. Customs house. Garrison gate. Ferry landing. Her father's clerk who might weep or might talk. The ruined captain who had every reason to hate her. And a stranger who owed her nothing at all.

"Three checkpoints," she said, "and every one of them knows my family."

"That's why you came to me." Tomas set down the pencil. For a moment his hand stayed on the paper, near hers but not touching, and the distance between their fingers seemed to hold all the years since they had last stood in the same room. "Because names open doors here. Yours more than most."

* * *

That night she lay in the lighthouse's narrow guest bed — his bed, he insisted, though he wouldn't be moved on it — and listened to the wind worry the gallery rail above her head. The dispatches lay against her ribs where they had lain for eleven days, sewn into the coat lining in packets thin as communion wafers. Paper and thread and treason, warm from her body.

She thought of her father, two years dead, who had told her in his last lucid week that the generals had sold the western approaches and that someone would have to carry the proof across the river before spring made the waterway useful to warships again. She thought of the men who had died holding a line those same generals had quietly withdrawn from. She thought, too, of Reisz in the dock, his face gray, his wife weeping into her gloves, and wondered for the thousandth time whether truth was a thing you carried or a thing that carried you, whether it mattered that she had been right if being right had wrecked a household and stranded a man in a frozen town with nothing to command but his grievances.

Sleep came late and shallow, and left early.

In the gray of morning she rose and went down through the tower — past the great lens sleeping under its canvas shroud, up the iron stairs whose cold bit through her stockings — to stand in the lantern room and look at the river. It lay white and still from bank to bank, a road of bone. Somewhere beneath it ran the channel that would open in six weeks, perhaps four, and when it opened, warships would come up it carrying whatever government the generals' friends managed to build. Unless the dispatches crossed first.

From this height she could see the town arranged along the south shore like items on a ledger: the customs house with its green shutters, smoke rising from the garrison chimney behind the church, the ferry landing's dark pilings jutting out over the ice. Three doors. She counted them twice, the way her father had taught her to count exits in any room she entered.

It was coming back down the stairs that she passed the small table outside the kitchen where Tomas kept his journal, and saw it lying open.

She hadn't meant to look. She told herself afterward that she hadn't meant to. But the page was turned toward the stairwell, as if left deliberately — and perhaps it was — and the handwriting was his, upright and unhurried even upside down, and the date at the top was yesterday's.

*A courier arrived on the evening coach. Female, traveling alone, coat too heavy for the season. Reported arrival to the garrison duty officer by telephone at 21:40 as instructed. Awaiting further instruction.*

Below that, in fresher ink:

*She has not yet confided her errand. I have not yet pressed. There is still time to choose which report I write next.*

The stove ticked in the kitchen below. The kettle she'd left on had begun, faintly, to sing. Elodie stood on the stairs with her hand pressed flat against the cold iron of the rail and read the entry a third time, because the third time was the one where the words stopped swimming.

*As instructed.*

He had reported her. Before she arrived at his door, before she said a single word about the bridge or the river or the things she needed him to know — he had already telephoned the garrison, already given her description, already opened the file that would travel up the chain from Reisz to whoever Reisz answered to. *Awaiting further instruction.* The trap had been built around her before she ever walked into it, and she had slept eight feet below the man who had sprung it.

And yet — *there is still time to choose which report I write next.*

She heard him moving below, the scrape of a chair, the pour of water. Any moment he would come looking for her, or she would go down to him, and between them lay a journal and a telephone line and eleven days of stitched paper riding against her heart.

Elodie did not retreat up the stairs. She smoothed her coat, felt the hard little ridges of the packets through the lining, and descended to the kitchen, where Tomas stood with his back to her at the stove, and where the journal — she noted it now with a strange, calm clarity — had been closed.

"I read your book," she said.

His shoulders went still. He didn't turn around. Steam rose from the kettle and bent in the draft from the stairwell door, and outside, far off across the white river, something boomed — a crack of shifting ice, deep as artillery, the sound of the season beginning to end.

"You left it out," she said. "Or you wanted me to find it. Either way, Tomas — we should talk about what you're going to tell them tomorrow."
