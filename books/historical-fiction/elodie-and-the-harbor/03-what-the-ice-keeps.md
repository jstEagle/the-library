## 3. What the Ice Keeps

The knocking began at the customs house first, then moved down the river street like a slow contagion — door by door, the fist of the garrison announcing itself in the blue hour before dawn. Elodie heard it through the floorboards of the lighthouse keeper's cottage, each impact traveling up through the joists and into the soles of her feet as though the town itself were being interrogated. She was already dressed. She had been dressed since midnight, since Tomas had come in with snow in his hair and a look on his face she had learned, years ago, to read faster than any dispatch.

"They found a courier's trail," he said now, standing in the doorway with his cap in his hands like a schoolboy called before a magistrate. "A rider crossed at Brissac two weeks ago. The garrison has decided the dispatches came this way. They're searching every house between the bridge and the ferry landing."

"Then they'll search this one."

"Yes."

She looked at him across the small room — at the man who had once taught her to read the water, who knew where the ice thinned and where it held, who had reported her arrival to his superiors with the dutiful precision of a soldier and then said nothing else, given nothing else away, not even when the lieutenant's questions turned specific.

"You told them I was here."

"I told them a courier arrived." His voice did not waver, and she hated him a little for that, for the steadiness she had once loved. "I didn't tell them your name. There's a difference, Elodie, even if it doesn't feel like one tonight."

"It feels like none at all."

* * *

Tomas

The journal again, after she turned from him. He wrote because writing was the only confession available to a man who could not afford to make one.

*The courier is here. I have done what my oath requires. I have done less than my heart asks. Between these two failures I will spend whatever winter allows me.*

He set down the pencil. She was at the window, watching the search party move along the shore road, lanterns swinging like slow pendulums, and he understood — with the terrible clarity that comes only at night, only in winter, only when there is nothing left to do but be honest — that he had already chosen. He had chosen the moment he saw her coat hanging by the stove, heavier than a coat should be, the lining puckered where something flat and folded lay against the wool. He had chosen when he lied to Lieutenant Marchand about the tides, telling him the crossing ice would not hold a man's weight until well into February, buying days he had no right to buy.

His duty sat in him like swallowed stone. His love sat beside it, no lighter.

He thought of his brother, buried under the frozen ground outside Vasseur with a court-martial's lie for an epitaph — the same tribunal, the same signatures, that Elodie's testimony had once fed and that her father's name had once shielded. That was the arithmetic neither of them could escape: the dead did not care which side of a river they were mourned from. They only asked to be counted honestly.

"Elodie," he said. "If they come here, they will find you or they will not. But they will not find you because of me. Whatever else I've done, that much is finished."

She turned. In the lamplight her face was older than the war had made it, older than grief, and he saw that she had been doing the same arithmetic, and arriving somewhere colder.

"Then we'd better decide," she said, "what they find instead."

* * *

Elodie

They planned it the way lovers plan nothing — over the table, with the lamp turned low and the map of the town drawn from memory in pencil on the back of a supply manifest. Three checkpoints. The customs house where old Perrin, her father's clerk, still kept his ledgers and, she prayed, some fragment of his loyalty. The garrison gate where Captain Rousset remembered her testimony the way a man remembers the scar from a blade. The ferry landing, held by men who owed her family nothing at all.

"The ice breaks within the week," Tomas said. "Marchand knows it too. When the river opens, the gunboats come up from the delta, and after that no dispatches matter — only ships. You have until then."

"And if I burn them instead?"

He was quiet long enough that the fire popped in the stove, and she knew his answer before he gave it, because it was her own answer, and it shamed them both.

"You'd be safe," he said. "And the generals would hang someone else's brother instead of their own reputations. You didn't carry those papers four hundred miles to keep warm."

"No," she said softly. "I carried them because the dead keep asking."

Outside, the searchers' lanterns reached the end of the shore road and turned back toward town. Not yet, then. A day, perhaps two. She felt the weight against her ribs — the sewn packet, warm now from her body as though it were alive — and understood that it had stopped being paper. It was a debt, and debts of that kind are paid in names.

"Tomas. If I go through the checkpoints, someone pays. If not me, then whoever helped me. That's how it works; you taught me that, in another life." Her voice caught and steadied. "So we choose who. Deliberately. That's the only mercy left in it."

"There's only one name left to choose," he said, "and it's mine."

"Don't."

"Listen to me." He took her hands, and his were cold from the window draft, and familiar, and unbearable. "Marchand already believes a courier passed through. What he needs is a keeper who let her. Give him that and he closes the file. Your name never enters it. My post, my freedom — it buys three checkpoints' worth of luck, and it's the cheapest price on the table."

"And what do I owe you, then? What do I owe the man who reported me and sheltered me in the same breath?"

"The truth," he said. "Delivered. That's all any of us ever owed anyone."

* * *

Tomas

They did not sleep, or they slept in the way of people who know the night is borrowed — dozing in chairs drawn close, waking at every sound, her head eventually against his shoulder as though the years between had been a misfiled page. He memorized the weight of it. He had spent six years learning to live without that weight and understood now that he had only been practicing.

Near dawn she stirred and said, against his coat, "Tell me something true. One thing, before we spend everything."

"That I reported you to save myself the trouble of choosing," he said, "and that it didn't work. That I have loved one woman across two wars and a river, and I would report her again tomorrow if reporting her kept her alive, and I would hate myself identically both times. That is all the truth I have, and it is yours."

The light came up gray over the harbor. The ice groaned out beyond the point — a long, deep sound, like a door opening in the floor of the world.

Below the headland, moving fast along the shore road, came a column of soldiers, and at their head a lantern that did not turn back this time.
