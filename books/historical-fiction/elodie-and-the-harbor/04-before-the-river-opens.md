## 4. Before the River Opens

The ferry landing at Vasseur was a crooked finger of gray timber pointing into a river gone white and silent, and Elodie walked its length with the dispatches no longer against her heart but in her open hands, wrapped in oilcloth, offered up like something she meant to trade. Which, she thought, was exactly what they were. Everything she had carried since autumn had been carried for this moment: not delivery but exchange.

Commander Armand Rousseau waited at the far end of the pier beneath a lantern that swung in the river wind, throwing his shadow long across the ice. He was younger than his rank suggested, with a clerk's neat hands and the tired eyes of a man who had signed too many papers he did not read. Around him stood six soldiers with rifles slung loose, their breath rising in white columns, and behind them all, held between two gendarmes who did not need to hold him at all, stood Tomas.

They had dressed him in his lighthouse coat, the heavy one with the brass buttons, as if he were going out into the cold on business rather than into custody. Someone had let him shave. Someone had given him coffee; she could smell it on the air between them, bitter and ordinary, and it broke her a little more than blood would have.

"Mademoiselle Pemberton." Rousseau inclined his head without warmth. "Or should I say — the daughter of Henri Pemberton. We opened the customs house register last night. Your father's name appears in it forty-one times. Loyalties are written in such ledgers, I find."

"My father is dead," Elodie said. "His name is not."

She set the oilcloth parcel on the bollard between them, and the soldiers shifted, and somewhere downriver the ice groaned — a low, tectonic sound, like a door opening in the floor of the world.

"Do you know what you're looking at, Commander?" She did not wait for permission. "Those are dispatches from General Marchette's own staff. Orders for the ammunition convoy that never reached the Fourth Division. Requisitions signed by men whose signatures your quartermasters have been chasing for two years. The names of every officer who sold the winter campaign — and the proof that Marchette knew. Your garrison has spent three years answering for a defeat that was purchased in advance. This is the receipt."

Rousseau's hand hovered over the parcel. He did not touch it. A careful man, then. "And you carry this why? Out of patriotism?"

"Out of arithmetic," she said. "My brother is in the Fourth Division's graveyard outside Saint-Aubin. I have counted what these papers cost, and I intend the count to be made public."

"And the keeper?" His eyes went past her to Tomas. "He reported a courier's arrival to this garrison eleven days ago. By his own confession, written and signed. He knew what you carried and he told us. That makes him either a loyal subject or a very poor friend."

"He is both," Elodie said. "That is the price of knowing someone through a war."

* * *

The wind came off the ice then, carrying with it the smell the river gave up only in thaw — mud and iron and the green ghost of spring underneath. Elodie pulled Tomas's letter from inside her glove, the one he had pressed on her an hour before the gendarmes came to the lighthouse, and she did not read it now but felt its weight, folded small against her palm like a second pulse.

"Here is my offer," she said. "You cannot let me cross with these papers and survive the inquiry that follows. You cannot destroy them, because Marchette's people will ask what happened to them, and you will be the man who lost the evidence of his own commanders' treason. You are standing on the last honest ground available to you, Commander, and it is shrinking." The ice chose that moment to speak again, a long cracking sigh running from shore to shore, and several of the soldiers glanced toward the water. "So take my offer instead."

"Which is?"

"The Pemberton name. My family's standing, my father's friendships in the ministry, whatever protection four generations of careful marriage and careful debt have built in this republic — I give it to you. When the inquiry comes, you will say the courier was sheltered by the Pemberton interest, that the family delivered the evidence voluntarily to secure its own position. My name becomes your shield, and it will hold, because my name is the only thing Marchette fears more than these papers." She heard her own voice go quiet. "And you will enter into the record the confession Tomas Vasseur has already signed — that he reported the courier, that he served the garrison faithfully, that any assistance he rendered was done under orders. Let him be the loyal instrument. Let him walk back to his lighthouse."

"And what do you walk away with?" Rousseau asked.

"A seat on the morning ferry," Elodie said. "And the right to deliver these to the ministry myself, in the capital, where they can do some good before someone burns them."

The lantern swung. Downriver, invisible in the dark, something vast shifted against the ice, and the whole white field of the river shivered like a struck bell.

* * *

They let her speak to him alone at the end of the pier, which surprised her until she understood that even Rousseau needed the fiction of mercy to make the transaction clean.

"I wrote it twice," Tomas said. His voice was steady; his hands, buried in his coat pockets, were not. "The confession. The first time I wrote that I loved you and betrayed you for it, and I burned that one, because it wasn't true. I never betrayed you, Elodie. I reported you because reporting you was the only way to keep you alive long enough to be bargained for. Every step after that — the searches I misdirected, the names I gave them wrong, tonight — it was all one long confession wearing loyalty's coat."

"I know," she said. "I've known since the customs house."

"You didn't say."

"There wasn't time to say anything true." She looked at the river, at the black seam of open water widening near the middle channel. "There still isn't. That's the whole shape of our lives, isn't it? The war took the years when people say things, and what's left is this — transactions at the water's edge."

Tomas drew a breath that seemed to come from somewhere below the pier, below the ice, below everything. "Keep the letter. Read it on the other side. And when they ask you, in the capital, what the harbor cost — tell them the truth if you can bear it. Tell them justice outside the law always costs someone, and this time it cost a lighthouse keeper his good name, and he gave it gladly, because the light was always yours to steer by, not mine."

"Tomas—"

"The horn," he said quietly. "Listen."

* * *

It came across the water like the opening of a long-sealed vault — the first warship's horn, deep and patient, announcing that the channel was clear enough for steel. The ice answered all at once: not a groan now but a shattering chorus, plates tilting, grinding, the whole white road downstream breaking into drifting continents. Rousseau shouted an order; the ferryman emerged from his hut, gray-faced, and cast off the mooring line while the soldiers formed a corridor they pretended was honor rather than haste.

Elodie took up the oilcloth parcel and did not look at Rousseau, did not thank him. Some trades soil both parties equally, and gratitude would only flatter one of them.

At the gangway she turned once. Tomas stood at the pier's end with the lantern light on his face, and he raised his hand — not waving, she thought, but saluting, the way one salutes a ship leaving a harbor one has kept safe all night from the darkness. She raised hers in answer, and the ferryman pushed off, and the current took them sideways into the broken water.

The crossing took forty minutes and lasted, she was certain, the rest of her life. Ice ground along the hull like slow applause. The warship's horn sounded twice more. On the far shore the capital's lights were coming up along the ridge, and against her chest the dispatches felt lighter than paper, heavier than her brother's name.

When the pier scraped home she did not open the letter. She tucked it inside her coat where the dispatches had lived, over her heart, and walked up into the city to finish what memory demanded.

*Vasseur Lighthouse, the same night*

*Elodie —*

*I am writing this by the lamp itself, so if the words waver, blame the light and not my hand.*

*By the time you read this the river will be open, and I will be what I agreed to become: the man who did his duty, in the record, forever. It is a strange freedom, being officially a traitor to everything but the truth.*

*Keep the lamp lit on your side of the water. I will keep it here. And on the nights when the ice goes out and the horns come up the channel, remember that two people once stood on opposite shores of the same river, keeping faith with the same dead, and neither of them surrendered.*

*The light is trimmed. The glass is clean. Go and be terrible to them, in the gentlest possible way.*

*Ever,*
*T.*
