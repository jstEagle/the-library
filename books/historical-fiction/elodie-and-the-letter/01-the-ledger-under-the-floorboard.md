## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The wound on Tam Weller's hand was the third I had dressed that morning and the only one earned honestly. Old Marlow's gout was a debt of long standing; the Henderson girl had scraped her arm falling out of the apple tree she'd been forbidden to climb; but Tam had caught his palm on a nail at the cooper's yard, and there was something almost refreshing about a wound with a clear conscience.

"Hold still," I said, drawing the needle through. "You flinch every time and it takes twice as long."

"I'm not flinching," Tam said, flinching. "I'm bracing. It's a different thing entirely. Soldiers brace."

"You were a stable boy for four months before you ran off to the regiment. You never saw a day of fighting."

"I saw the aftermath of a day of fighting," he said, with the gravity of a man twice his nineteen years. "That counts for something."

It did. That was precisely the trouble. It counted for more than the boy knew how to carry, and so he carried it here, to the apothecary's back room, where Mr. Prewitt the apothecary pretended not to notice how often Tam found reasons to have his hand seen to, his shoulder listened to, his silence sat with.

I tied off the last stitch and dressed the wound in clean linen. "Keep it dry. Come back Friday."

"Friday," Tam agreed, and then didn't leave. He stood turning his cap in his hands, looking at the jars on the shelf—comfrey, yarrow, the blue glass bottle of laudanum that Mr. Prewitt counted twice nightly—and worked up to whatever it was. "There's a thing I see sometimes," he said finally. "When I'm dropping off. The mud at Vellmore. Not the fighting. Just the mud, after. How quiet it went."

"Yes," I said.

"That's all. I only wanted to say it out loud once today."

"Then you've said it." I met his eyes and held them, because that was the whole of the medicine. "Say it again Friday if it wants saying."

He nodded, put his cap on, and went out into the grey morning whistling something tuneless, which is the best sound a hurt man can make.

* * *

Mr. Prewitt's shop smelled of camphor and dried lavender and the particular dustiness of things kept past their usefulness, which is why I liked it. Six years since the Vellmore Campaign ended and I came home with two working hands and a head that would not stop making lists, and I had found that grinding powders and labeling bottles settled the lists better than anything else. Measured quantities. Exact weights. A world where a grain too much of foxglove could kill and everyone understood that numbers mattered.

I was weighing out cascara for the vicar's chronic complaint—of the bowel, though the vicar would tell you it was the curate—when the bell over the door rang and Mrs. Sorrel came in with rain in her hem and grief in her shoulders, six years settled into both like an old coat.

"Kestrel," she said. "I hoped it would be you behind the counter."

"Mrs. Sorrel." I set down the scales. "Sit you down. Tea's in the back and Prewitt won't grudge us the good leaves, he's at the market till noon."

She sat. She was forty perhaps, though the war had aged her the way it aged everyone who waited through it, and her hands were a gardener's hands, cracked at the knuckles. Her husband, Will Sorrel, had been a private in the Ninth, a badger-baiter turned bugler, a man who could make laughter happen in a trench the way other men made fire. He had died at the ford below Harrow Field on the fourth day of the retreat, and according to the official record—the slim, tidy, printed official record—he had died of "fever contracted in the line of duty," at a date eleven days after the ford, at a field hospital sixty miles away that he never reached, because the ford did not appear in the record at all.

"Will's mother," Mrs. Sorrel began, and stopped, and started again. "She's dying. She wants the date. For the stone. She has it in her head from the church roll—September the nineteenth—but Tom Wexford's mother has the eleventh, from a letter Tom wrote her before—" She pressed her lips together. "And I don't know which is true, and I can't bear to carve a lie over my husband."

"The eleventh," I said quietly.

She looked up. "How do you know?"

Because I had been there. Because I had knelt in the shallows of the ford with my kit dissolving around me in the water and held the hand of a bugler who joked about the cold even as the life left him, and because I had written the hour in the margin of my field notebook in a hand gone shaky, 4:40 in the afternoon, and because the dispatch that came afterward had smoothed the whole business away like a thumb smoothing wet clay.

But what I said was: "A man in the Ninth told me. Years ago. He was particular about dates."

Which was true, in a fashion. The man in the Ninth had been me.

Mrs. Sorrel studied me the way women of her kind study things—with patience and without mercy. Then she nodded once, and something in her unclenched, and I went to make the tea because my hands wanted employment.

* * *

Here is the thing I did not tell her, the thing I have not told anyone, the thing that lives under the floorboard beneath my cot in the room behind the apothecary, three nails' width of pine between it and the world:

I keep a ledger.

It is nothing grand. A quartermaster's ledger, half-ruined by damp, rescued from a burning supply wagon in the last month of the war because I needed paper and paper was scarce. Four hundred and eleven names now, each one written small and square, each one with its particulars: unit, date, place, manner of death where I knew it, and—most important—the words *not recorded*, written beside every name the official dispatches omitted.

Four hundred and eleven men who fought and bled and died for a victory the histories describe in three handsome volumes, and who appear in none of them.

I did not set out to be a keeper of names. I set out to be a nurse, and I was a fair one, and then Vellmore happened, and the weeks after Vellmore happened, and I learned that the difference between the war as it was fought and the war as it was reported was wider than the sea. Officers wrote clean reports. Clean reports won honors. And the men who fell in the cracks between the report and the truth—well. Paper is patient. Somebody had to be patient with them.

So I wrote. In hospital tents by guttering candle. On the march, balanced on my knee. After, in this village, in the evenings, working backward from letters and parish rolls and the memories of men who came home hollow-eyed and willing, if asked gently, to say what they saw.

Four hundred and eleven. I know the number the way a father knows the count of his children, and some mornings I wake with the weight of it on my chest like a cat that will not be moved.

I brought the tea back and we drank it while the rain tried the windows.

"He'd have laughed at this weather," Mrs. Sorrel said, looking out. "Will hated rain. Said a man couldn't hear himself think in it, and he liked hearing himself think, God help him."

"He liked hearing himself talk," I said. "Thinking was incidental."

She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and then her face crumpled and she wept for a while, not loudly, into her gardening hands, and I sat with her and refilled her cup when it was time, and neither of us pretended this was unusual or shameful. Grief comes to the counter of an apothecary's shop like any other customer. It gets the good chairs.

"Six years," she said at last, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. "You'd think it would sit quieter by now."

"It sits differently," I said. "That's all the quiet anyone gets."

She looked at me then, really looked, and I felt the old instinct—the shutter coming down, the pleasant blankness settling over my face like a mask of soap. Six years of practice. A man who asks no questions and answers none directly makes few friends but keeps the ones he has safe.

"You were there," she said. It wasn't quite a question. "At the end. Wexford's letter mentioned a nurse. Dark-haired fellow, soft voice, wouldn't let the chaplain hurry the dying ones. Tom said he'd remember that man to his last day."

"There were many nurses," I said.

"But only one who wrote everything down. Tom said that too. Said this nurse had a little book and wrote in it even when the shells came near, like a parson at prayer." Her eyes were steady on mine, and kind, which made it worse. "Was that you, Kestrel?"

The clock on the shelf ticked. Somewhere out in the street a cart went by, splashing.

"If it was," I said carefully, "it was only notes. Doses and dressings. Nurses keep records."

"Mm," said Mrs. Sorrel, in the tone of a woman filing something away. She finished her tea and rose, and took my hand in both of hers, and her grip was stronger than her sorrow. "The eleventh of September, then. For the stone. Thank you. Whatever it is you keep under your floorboards"—and here my heart stopped dead in my chest, and she watched it stop, and smiled faintly—"you keep it well. I'll say no more. But Will's mother will die easier, and I'll sleep tonight, and that's your doing."

She went out. The bell rang. I stood a long moment among the bottles with my pulse banging in my ears.

She could not have known about the floorboard. She had been joking—or guessing, the way the grieving guess, throwing lines into dark water. But the line had come back with something on it, and I resolved, as I had resolved a hundred times before, that the ledger must someday be dealt with. Burned, or buried, or—

Or. There was always the *or*. It sat in me like a swallowed coal.

* * *

That evening I closed the shop, ate bread and cheese standing up, and climbed the stairs to my room with the lamp. The ritual was always the same. Bolt the door. Check the window latch. Listen at the wall for the lodger, Mr. Dunmore, whose snore arrived punctually at nine like a train into a station.

Nine o'clock came. The snore followed.

I knelt, worked loose the board with the flat of a knife—it came up silent; I had waxed the joints years ago—and lifted out the ledger, and set it on my knees, and opened it to September.

*Private William Sorrel, 9th Regt. of Foot. Ford below Harrow Field, 11th September, 4:40 o'clock in the afternoon. Wounds received at the ford. Died in the presence of K. Coldharbour, field nurse. Not recorded.*

Not recorded. Four hundred and eleven times, give or take the variations, the same quiet sentence, the same refusal to let the sentence be the last word.

I read the September pages through, as I do on certain evenings—not all of them, just the ones the day calls up—and I spoke a few of the names aloud, very low, because names want air sometimes the way plants want turning toward the sun. Sorrel. Dace. The Pell brothers, who had enlisted within a week of each other and died within an hour. Young Hobb, whose real name I had learned only last spring from a cousin in Netherfold, and whose entry I had amended accordingly, feeling like a man restoring a knocked-out tooth.

When I came to the bottom of the page I sat a while with the pencil in my hand. There was nothing to add. Some nights there is nothing to add and that is its own peculiar ache, like reaching for a stair that isn't there.

Instead I did what I sometimes did: I thought about the future of the thing. What would become of it when I died? Mr. Prewitt would inventory the room and find a soldier's ledger full of accusations against decorated men, and he would burn it in confusion, or worse, bring it to the magistrate. Four hundred and eleven names erased twice over.

And yet I could not burn it myself. I had tried, early on, in the second year, alone in this room with the stove lit. I had held page after page over the flames until my hand shook, and then I had shut the stove unlit and sat on the floor and been quietly, thoroughly sick with shame at having even tried. They were not my secrets to destroy. They belonged to the men. To the widows. To Mrs. Sorrel carving a true date over her husband's bones.

Someone, someday, would have to want the truth publicly. And until that someone came, the ledger would stay under the floorboard, patient as paper, doing the one thing paper does best.

Waiting.

I slid it home, pressed the board down, and blew out the lamp, and lay listening to the rain and to Mr. Dunmore's faithful snore, and I had nearly gone under when a stray thought surfaced, the kind the mind saves for the moment of least defense.

The census.

They were talking of it at market—every household to be counted by spring, every name, age, and occupation entered in the government's book, taken door to door by some appointed person with forms and authority and, presumably, questions. Questions. My name in another government ledger, my trade, my history, my war service, laid out in official columns for any clerk to read.

I was still telling myself it meant nothing—a census is a census, a head is a head—when I finally slept, and dreamed, as I had not dreamed in months, of mud going quiet after the guns.

The census taker arrived on Thursday.

Her name was Ferris.
