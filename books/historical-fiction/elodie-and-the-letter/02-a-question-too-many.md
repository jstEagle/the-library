## 2. A Question Too Many

The census taker arrived on a Thursday, which I later decided was fitting, because Thursdays were the days Mrs. Penhale from the mill came in for her drops and would have heard everything.

She did not arrive on a Thursday. That was my first mistake—expecting her when it suited me. She arrived instead on the eleventh of October, a grey morning smelling of wet stone and coming frost, while I was elbow-deep in a mortar grinding willow bark for the Hendersons' rheumatism tincture. The bell above the shop door gave its small cheerful cry, entirely out of keeping with what followed, and a woman stepped in carrying a leather satchel, an umbrella beaded with rain, and the expression of somebody who intended to leave with more than she came with.

"Good morning," she said. "Miss Ferris. Census enumeration, district nine." She produced a leather folder stamped with the Crown, opened it on my counter as though she owned the counter, and uncapped a pen with her teeth. "Name?"

"Kestrel Coldharbour."

"Occupation?"

"Apothecary's assistant."

"For Mr. Tarnow." She said it without looking up. "How is his hip this autumn?"

I blinked at her. "You know Mr. Tarnow?"

"I know everyone in district nine by now," she said. "It's rather the whole point of the exercise. He told me I'd find you here, and that you'd be polite, and that you'd try to hurry me along. Was he right?"

"He was right about the hurrying."

Her mouth curved—not quite a smile, more the acknowledgment that one had been attempted. "Then let's not waste each other's time. Household residents?"

"Just myself. The shop belongs to Mr. Tarnow; I lodge above it."

"Age?"

"Thirty-six."

"Born where?"

"Coldharbour Hollow, down the valley." I ground the pestle another turn. The willow bark didn't need it. My hands needed something to do, was all. "My mother fancied naming children after landmarks. My sister is called Ford."

That earned me a genuine smile, brief as a coin flashed in a market crowd. "Ford Coldharbour. She teaches at the dame school in Marleigh, doesn't she? Two rows over from your section. I've been through Marleigh twice."

"You've a memory like a ledger," I said, and felt the words land somewhere in my chest with a dull private thud, because *ledger* was not a word I used lightly anymore, even in idle metaphor. Especially in idle metaphor.

"Mm," said Miss Ferris, and wrote something down. I could not see what. That struck me as unfair. She could see everything of mine—my name, my age, my bed, my trade—and I could see nothing of hers but a neat cuff and a pen that scratched like a small animal in dry grass.

* * *

A census, I want to say in fairness, is a reasonable thing. The Crown wishes to know how many souls it contains, presumably so it can tax them efficiently, and some clerk somewhere must count us the way a shepherd counts sheep—with no malice toward any individual ewe. In six years, census takers had come and gone from this district without troubling me further than the spelling of my surname. There had been three. One whistled while he worked. None of them had looked at me the way this one did, which was to say: as though I were a document she was checking against another document.

"Marital status?" she asked.

"Unmarried."

"Widowed?"

"No."

She waited. It took me a moment to understand that she'd asked a complete question and I had answered it completely, and that the silence afterward was mine to fill or not. Most people filled silences. I had spent six years learning not to. But there was something in the way she held her pen poised—not impatient, not curious exactly, just *present*—that made the silence feel less like empty air and more like a door left open on purpose.

"My parents are dead," I offered. "No wife. No dependents."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's adjacent to what you asked."

"It usually is," said Miss Ferris, "with men who live alone above apothecary shops," and she wrote something else down, and I began, quietly and without drama, to develop a headache behind my right eye.

She worked through the rest with brisk efficiency—date of residence, whether we kept livestock (we did not; Mr. Tarnow had opinions about goats), whether any member of the household was deaf, dumb, blind, lunatic, imbecile, or a pauper. I answered *no* to all five and thought, not for the first time, how much the form left out. Whether any member of the household woke at night hearing rain on canvas and mistaking it for something worse. Whether he counted things. Whether he kept a light burning because darkness had a particular smell he could not abide. The Crown, apparently, did not wish to know.

"And that's your household done," she said, bloting the page. "Except—" and here she turned back a leaf with one finger, unhurried as a barrister, "—the schedule asks after former service. Any resident formerly a soldier or sailor in Her Majesty's forces?"

The pestle stopped.

I am not proud of the pause. I want to state plainly that I noticed it myself, in real time, the way one notices oneself slipping on ice—a full half-second of watching it happen with no power to prevent it. Six years of practice had made my face a well-managed thing. But practice assumes warning, and she had asked the question the way you ask a man his name, and so my pause happened in full daylight, between one breath and the next, and she saw it. I knew she saw it the way you know a fish has seen your fly.

"No," I said.

"No, you were never a soldier?"

"That's correct."

She nodded slowly and wrote *no*, and then, maddeningly, kept writing. More than four words' worth. I watched the pen travel and could not for the life of me imagine what required such length.

"Interesting work, census-taking," I said, to fill the space. "All those questions."

"It has its moments." She blotted again. "Most people are simple. Household of six, two can read, one's a fool. But every district has someone interesting." She looked up. "Mr. Tarnow tells me you came here six years ago. Spring of '71. Straight from—where was it? He wasn't sure. He said you never spoke of before."

"Mr. Tarnow," I said, "is a dear man who talks too much."

"He also says you're the finest dresser of wounds in three valleys, that soldiers passing through on the mail coach ask after you by name, and that once, in '74, a gentleman came asking for you and you went white as flour and hid in the dispensary until he left." She recited it flatly, taking no pleasure in it, which somehow made it worse. "Was he right about that?"

"He's old," I said. "His memory invents things."

"Mm." She closed the folder. I felt relief begin, warm and foolish, rising in me like yeast—

"The census," she said, "also serves as the basis for pension rolls. Widows' pensions especially. So the schedules cross-check against regimental muster books, discharge papers, that sort of thing." She tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear. Rain still ticked softly against the window. "It's surprising, Mr. Coldharbour, how often the muster books disagree with what people tell me. A man will say he was never a soldier, and there his name sits in the rolls, plain as porridge. Or a widow swears her husband served in the Fourteenth, and the Fourteenth has no record of him at all. No record anywhere." Her pen tapped once against the folder. "Dozens of them, in fact, just in this district. Men who fought and died and left no paper trail. Their widows get nothing. The Crown says they don't exist."

"They should apply to the Crown for imagination," I said.

The words came out sharper than I intended—sharper than wit, landing nearer the bone—and Miss Ferris went very still, the way a heron goes still before it strikes the water. For perhaps three seconds neither of us moved. The willow bark sat in its mortar, ground far finer than any rheumatism required. Somewhere out in the street a cart passed, wheels hissing on wet stone.

"There was a battle," she said at last, conversationally, as though we were discussing weather. "Vellmore. You'll have read of it—the whole country read of it. Six years ago this autumn, which is, now I think of it, precisely when you arrived in this district, walking, according to Mr. Tarnow, though he says your boots were ruined beyond saving and you arrived looking, in his words, *like something dug up*." She tilted her head. "You'll forgive the questions. I ask everyone whose timeline interests me. It's a habit."

"I never saw Vellmore," I said.

I have told lies before. Good ones, even—lies delivered to provost officers with rope nearby, lies told to frightened men about their chances, merciful lies and expedient ones, and I had always supposed myself reasonably accomplished. But I heard my own voice say *I never saw Vellmore* and it sounded exactly like what it was: a man reading from a script he had written too quickly. My hands had gone flat on the counter. When had that happened? My pulse was audible in my own ears.

Miss Ferris regarded me for a long moment.

"All right," she said mildly.

"*All right?*"

"You don't have to tell me anything, Mr. Coldharbour. It isn't my business and it isn't the census's business either, past the question of service, which you've answered." She gathered her umbrella and folder and pen with quick economical movements, and I understood suddenly that she had gotten everything she came for, and that what she had come for was not data. "I'll mark your household down. Single male, thirty-six, apothecary's assistant, literate, no infirmities." At the door she paused, hand on the bell, and turned back with that almost-smile again, and said:

"Oh—one small thing. Nearly forgot. Widow Alder down in Netherby—you know her? Her husband was at Vellmore with the Rifles. She's applied for a pension four times and been refused four times, because Private Josiah Alder appears on no roll, no muster, no memorial. As if he walked onto that field out of thin air and the field swallowed him whole." She opened the door. Cold air came in smelling of rain and October. "Funny thing, though. I've been gathering those cases. Men the papers lost. I keep a list. And a friend of mine in the archive office found me a hospital requisition from October of '71—signed, would you believe, by a nursing orderly with beautiful handwriting. Requisitioning lint and laudanum for *forty-one wounded*, gentlemen, from a battle the official record says produced twenty."

She stepped out onto the wet stones, put up her umbrella, and looked back at me once.

"Twenty wounded," she said, "or forty-one. Somebody's arithmetic is wrong, Mr. Coldharbour. I do intend to find out whose. Good day."

The bell gave its small cheerful cry as the door swung shut behind her.

I stood a long while among the bottles and the mortars and the neat labeled drawers, listening to the rain, feeling something I had not felt since the war itself: the cold, unmistakable sensation of being *seen*. Not glanced at. Not pitied. Seen—down through six years of careful plaster, to whatever lay beneath, waiting to be read.

Behind my right eye, the headache bloomed into something magnificent.

And under the floorboard beneath my bed, patient as a seed, the ledger lay where it had lain for six years—four hundred and eleven names, and now, apparently, a witness.
