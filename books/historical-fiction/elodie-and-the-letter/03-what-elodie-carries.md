## 3. What Elodie Carries

Elodie Ferris had been a census taker for four years, and in that time she had learned that every household was a locked room and every householder held at least one key they would rather swallow than use. The farmer's wife who claimed six children when the school register showed five. The miller who swore he lived alone while two women's hats hung in his hallway. People lied on official forms the way rivers lied about their depth—smoothly, constantly, and always in the direction of self-preservation.

Kestrel Coldharbour had lied worse than any of them.

She turned the thought over on her walk back through the village that evening, past the bakery exhaling its last warm breath of the day, past the smith's boy pumping water with the solemnity of a boy who believed pumping water was the most important thing happening in England. Kestrel Coldharbour—apothecary's assistant, quiet man, careful hands—had told her he had never seen battle, and his voice had done the thing voices did when they carried something too heavy: it had dipped in the middle, like a shelf bowing under weight.

She had gone home. She had eaten bread and cheese standing up, as her brother Tom used to say was the only honest way to eat. And then she had taken a box down from behind the loose brick in the chimney of her lodgings—the brick that had been loose since before she rented the room, though her landlady did not know that—and she had sat by the fire and decided what she was going to do.

It was not, strictly speaking, within her duties.

But Elodie had discovered some time ago that the phrase *not strictly speaking within one's duties* was where nearly all the important work of the world got done.

* * *

She came to the apothecary shop the next morning at the hour when villages were least defended: just after breakfast, when shops were open but not yet busy, when the keeper of any establishment was trapped behind his counter by politeness. She brought a basket as pretext. In the basket were plums. Nobody could turn away a woman carrying plums; it was against nature.

The bell above the door rang, and Kestrel looked up from behind the counter, where he had been grinding something fragrant and gray-green, and Elodie watched his face perform a small, doomed stillness. He knew. Good. It saved time.

"Miss Ferris," he said. "If you've come about the census forms, I believe I answered—"

"You said your household was one," Elodie said, setting the basket on the counter between them like a small edible siege engine. "You said you had never served in His Majesty's forces. You said—" she consulted no notes at all, because she did not need notes; she had spent an entire evening with the words arranged in her memory like pressed flowers "—that you had never been anywhere near Vellmore."

"I said the campaign was before my time here."

"You did. That was the cleverest lie of the three. The other two were frankly rushed." She took off her gloves, finger by finger, and laid them on the counter. It was, she had found, a gesture that made men nervous, though she had never understood why. Perhaps because it suggested she intended to stay. Perhaps because it suggested she intended to *work*. "Mr. Coldharbour, I'd like to buy something. Do you have anything for sleeplessness?"

He blinked. Whatever he had braced for, it was not this. "There's—a tincture of valerian. Or hops, if you'd rather not taste it."

"The valerian, please. I've been sleeping badly." This was true, which she found useful; lies were so much easier to carry when they were wrapped around truths. "I dream about a field."

The grinding stopped.

"It's a field near a river," Elodie went on, quietly now, watching his hands. "There are poplars along the water. In my dream the field is empty, and I walk it looking for something, and I know—I always know, even in the dream—that there are things under the grass that should have been found and weren't." She set three coins beside the gloves. "My brother Tom is buried somewhere near that river. Somewhere, or nowhere. The War Office says he fell at Vellmore on the fourth day. There is no grave with his name on it. There is no memorial with his name on it. There is nothing at all with his name on it, except me."

The mortar sat motionless under Kestrel's hand. He was not a tall man, she had noticed yesterday; he had the kind of stillness that made height irrelevant anyway. When he finally spoke, his voice was very level, the way voices go level when the alternative is not.

"Ferris," he said. "Was that your name at the time? At Vellmore?"

"No. I married young and badly and briefly. Ferris stayed, though. It fit better than the first one." She looked around the little shop—at the drawers labeled in a neat hand, at the bundles of dried things hanging from the beams, at the jars glowing amber in the window light. A shop kept by someone who believed order was a form of kindness. "I took the census position because it put doors in front of me. Doors open, Mr. Coldharbour. People answer questions at their own thresholds they would never answer in a letter. For four years I've been asking my way across three counties, and everywhere I ask, I find the same shape of silence. Widows who were told their husbands died of fever when the fever started, conveniently, on the day the regiment needed its rolls tidy. Mothers whose sons' names appear on no roll at all. Men who came home and will not say what happened on the fourth day at Vellmore, and who flinch at the word 'poplar.'"

"They don't all flinch at poplars," Kestrel said. Then he closed his eyes briefly, as if he could gather the words back out of the air, and Elodie felt something in her chest turn over—not triumph, nothing so clean as triumph. More like the click of a lock she had been picking for years finally giving way.

"You were there," she said. Not a question. She had promised herself she wouldn't make it a question; questions gave him somewhere to retreat to.

"I was there," Kestrel said.

* * *

He did not invite her into the back room. He simply stopped being behind the counter and started walking, and she followed him through the curtain of beads—beads, of all things, blue and clouded glass, clattering like cold rain—and into a low room that smelled of camphor and soap, where a stove ticked and two chairs faced each other with the studied casualness of furniture that had been moved recently.

He gestured her into one chair and took the other himself, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and Elodie let the silence do its work. Silence, properly handled, was the best question ever devised.

"There were four days of fighting at Vellmore proper," Kestrel said at last, to the stove rather than to her. "And then a fifth day that appears in no dispatch, because the fifth day was not fighting. The fifth day was the rear guard deciding which wounded could be carried and which could not, and the road being what it was, and the enemy being closer than anyone liked. The officers wrote their reports from the road." His jaw worked. "I was with the medical column. We were supposed to be the ones carried. Instead we were the ones left."

"How many?"

"That number exists," Kestrel said. "I'm the only person alive who has it written down, and I'll thank you not to ask me to produce it before I've finished my tea, because I find I need rituals today."

Elodie had not seen him pour tea. She looked around and found the pot already sitting on the stove's flat top, already warm, two cups beside it—one cup dusty with disuse, one not. He had made two cups of tea before she arrived. Which meant he had heard her knock yesterday and lain awake doing arithmetic about her, and had concluded this conversation was coming whether he wanted it or not, and had prepared for it the way he apparently prepared for everything: quietly, thoroughly, and with chamomile.

She accepted the cup. It was good tea. They drank it mostly in silence, and the silence was different from the silences she usually harvested from reluctant witnesses—this one wasn't resistance. It was more like the pause at a graveside before the first spade of earth.

"I keep a record," Kestrel said finally.

"I know."

His head came up sharply. "You *know*."

"I guessed," Elodie said, "and then I confirmed it, in the sense that a woman who has spent six years confirming things develops an eye for the shape of them. You have ink on your left cuff that never quite washes out—you scrub it, but you scrub it gently, because whatever you write matters enough that you don't want to damage the pages getting the evidence off your sleeves. Your ledger paper, if you order any, would come wrapped in plain brown—there's brown paper and string in your waste bin, which a man selling herbs has very little honest use for. And when I said the word 'omitted' yesterday, purely in passing, your hand went to your waistcoat pocket. Not your watch chain. Your *pocket*. Men keep watches in watch pockets, Mr. Coldharbour. They keep other things in waistcoat pockets, close to the heart, where a pickpocket would have to touch them to take them."

Kestrel stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—one short, helpless syllable of it, quickly suppressed, as if laughter were also something he kept under the floorboards.

"You should have been the investigator," he said. "Instead of—" he gestured vaguely at the idea of her "—the form-filler."

"I am extremely good at forms," Elodie said with dignity. "Forms are how I get in the door. Now. Before we go one step further, you're going to look at something, and I need you to understand that I am not threatening you, and I am not accusing you, and I am not here to catch you at anything. May I?"

He hesitated. Then he nodded once, the nod of a man stepping off a ledge because the alternative was standing on it forever.

Elodie reached into her basket—past the plums, which had performed their office admirably—and drew out a notebook. Not an official notebook. A cheap one, cloth-bound, the cover worn soft at the corners the way only books that live in coat pockets and under pillows wear soft. She put it on the small table between the chairs.

"This is six years of my life," she said. "It started as a list of one name. Then a widow in Marleigh mentioned her son had been at Vellmore and the memorial at Ashcombe didn't carry him, and I wrote his name down too, because it seemed wrong for him to exist only twice in the world—in her memory and mine—when three times is the traditional minimum for making a thing true. And then it became a habit. And then it became the reason I get up in the morning." She opened the book. "Names, Mr. Coldharbour. Where they were born. What unit the mothers believe they served in, which is sometimes different from what the records claim. Who grieves for them, and where the grieving people can be found. One hundred and sixty-three names as of Sunday. All omitted. All Vellmore."

She turned the pages and let him look. She watched his face while he did it, and what she saw there was not fear, exactly, though there was fear in it. It was the expression of a man who has kept a fire alive alone in a cellar for six years and opens a door to discover a whole street of houses with lit windows.

"You count the grieving," he said quietly. "I never thought to count the grieving."

"Somebody ought to. Grief is the only casualty figure the War Office never revises downward." She closed the book but kept her hand flat upon it. "Now. Here is what I am not going to do. I am not going to demand to see yours. I am not going to tell you what to do with it. I have stood in enough kitchens across three counties to know that the people who hold these lists are holding live coals, and it is a cruelty to tell a man to open his hands before he's ready."

"But," Kestrel said.

"But," Elodie agreed, "you should know that you are not the only one, and you never were. Every name on my list was given to me by somebody keeping their own version of your ledger—a scrap of letters, a mark on a map, a memory rehearsed nightly like prayers. We are scattered, all of us keepers. And scattered things can be ignored. Gathered things cannot." She took a breath, and made herself say the rest plainly, because plainly was a kindness too. "There's talk in the newspapers, Mr. Coldharbour. Talk of a Royal Commission on War Records, sitting through the winter, to correct—listen to this—the *clerical errors* of the late campaign. Clerical errors. As though four hundred men were mislaid like receipts. If that commission writes the official history, and the official history says clerical error, then in fifty years nobody will remember there was ever anything to doubt. The names will be gone, Tom's among them, not by malice but by tidiness. I find I can forgive malice more easily than tidiness."

Kestrel had gone very still again. But it was a different stillness than yesterday's—the stillness of a man listening to his own thoughts arrange themselves into something with edges.

"Four hundred?" he said.

"I don't know the true number. That's the point. Nobody knows it. Except—" she tipped her head toward him, toward his waistcoat pocket, toward whatever waited beneath the floorboard of his life "—possibly one man in this county."

For a long moment he looked at her, and Elodie let herself be looked at. She was aware, distantly, that she had rehearsed this encounter a dozen times and none of her rehearsals had included the plums actually working, or the tea, or the strange gentleness of the room with its bead curtain and its ticking stove. She had expected to fight. She had not expected to feel as though she'd come home to a house she'd never entered.

"My brother's name is Thomas Ferris," she said. "Fourth Day, the river crossing, second company. He was twenty-two and he whistled badly and he owed me half a crown, and there is not a stone in England that says he lived." Her voice caught on the last of it, annoying her; she had meant to be composed. "Sixty-three of the names in my book, I found myself. A hundred of them, other people handed me at their doors with their eyes wet. People want to give them up, Mr. Coldharbour. They've been waiting six years for someone to ask."

Kestrel rose. He crossed to the hearth, stood with his back to her a moment, and then knelt and did something at the floorboards—she heard the small wooden sound of a panel lifting—and when he turned around he was holding a ledger bound in dark cloth, held against his chest with both arms, the way one holds either a child or a bomb.

"Not yet," he said. "I'm not ready to show you yet. But I find—" and here his composure wobbled, wonderfully, humanly, and Elodie felt her heart go out to him entirely and without her permission "—I find I wanted you to see that I'm holding it. So that when I do show you, you'll know it isn't the first time I chose to. Choosing twice is easier than choosing once."

"Twice is always easier," Elodie said. "That's why the second cup of tea tastes better."

He almost smiled. It was, she suspected, a currency he dealt in rarely, and she resolved privately to become wealthy in it.

Then his gaze shifted past her shoulder, to the window, and the almost-smile died on the sill.

Through the glass, at the far end of the lane, Elodie saw a carriage—too fine for the village, lacquered and crested, moving slowly past the smithy as if its occupant were taking the air and measuring the town while he took it. On the door gleamed a crest she recognized from newspaper engravings: a tower above crossed laurels.

"Do you know that crest?" she asked.

"Yes," Kestrel said softly, and drew the ledger a fraction tighter against his chest. "That's Colonel Ashgrave's carriage. And unless I've misread the papers, Miss Ferris, he hasn't come home to be thanked."
