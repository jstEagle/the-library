## 5. The Apothecary's Cellar

### Kestrel

The cellar of the apothecary's shop smelled of dried yarrow, lamp oil, and the particular damp that lived in old stone and could not be persuaded to leave. Kestrel had spent three days pretending he wasn't preparing for anything. He had swept the floor twice. He had arranged six chairs in a circle and then, embarrassed by the arrangement, pushed them back into their usual disorder. He had set out a jug of elderflower cordial and, after a moment's reflection, a bottle of Bram Tull's preferred whisky beside it, because a man ought to be offered his own poison when you were about to ask him for something difficult.

Elodie Ferris had left three days ago, and her list was still folded in his coat pocket, where he had taken to touching it the way other men touched a locket.

*Fourteen names,* she had said. *That's only what I found in one parish register and two graveyards. There will be more. There are always more.*

He had told her he would think about it. What he had actually done was stand in the shop until midnight, holding the ledger under the loose floorboard, weighing it in both hands like something that might detonate. It weighed less than a loaf of bread. It had cost more than most loaves of bread ever would.

Tonight he would show it to them. Not all of them — never all — but the ones who had earned it without knowing they'd earned it. Bram. Mrs. Halloway. Tam. The strange little family that had assembled itself around his shop over six years the way ivy assembles itself around a wall: slowly, without permission, and impossible to remove without tearing down the wall too.

They came at seven, because Mrs. Halloway believed evenings began at seven and would brook no argument on the matter.

Bram arrived first, which surprised no one; Bram was always first through any door that might contain drink. He was fifty-one and looked sixty-five, big through the shoulders still, going soft at the middle, with a face like a collapsed tent that had once sheltered something magnificent. He nodded at the whisky bottle, then at Kestrel, and poured himself two fingers with the solemnity of a priest at communion.

"Serious face," Bram observed. "You've got your serious face on."

"I don't have a serious face."

"You have eleven faces, Kess, and ten of them are serious. The eleventh is the one you make at cats." He settled into a chair. "Out with it, then. You didn't invite me for my wit."

Mrs. Halloway came next, small and upright and dressed in black so old it had gone the color of slate, which was as close as she came to color. She had buried three sons — Aldous, Peter, and young Wren, who had been nineteen — and she carried them the way she carried her basket: firmly, close to the body, without complaint. She looked at the circle of chairs, then at Kestrel, and something in her eyes sharpened.

"This isn't about my hip," she said. Her hip was the pretext for half her visits. "Good. I'm tired of talking about my hip."

Tam came last, slipping in the door the way he did everything — quietly, apologetically, as though apologizing to the door itself. He was twenty now, though he'd arrived at seventeen looking fifteen, all wrists and terror, having walked out of the Vellmore garrison in the last month of the war and into Kestrel's surgery bleeding from a fence he'd torn himself open on climbing it. A deserter. Legally, still a deserter, though the war had ended before anyone thought to chase him. He took the chair farthest from the lamp, as always.

Kestrel stood, felt ridiculous standing, sat back down, stood again.

"Six years ago," he began, and stopped, and started over. "I was a nurse at Vellmore. Field hospital, third echelon, behind the ridge. Most of you know that. Most of you know I don't talk about it, and most of you have been kind enough not to ask."

"We're not most people," Mrs. Halloway said gently. "Get on."

He got on. He went to the floorboard by the shelving, knelt, pried up the board, and lifted out the ledger. It was a plain thing — army surplus, canvas-bound, the kind issued for supply inventories — and its pages had gone soft at the edges from handling. He laid it on the table between the cordial and the whisky and did not touch it again.

"In the official dispatches," he said, "the Vellmore engagement is recorded as having cost ninety-three lives. Ninety-three names, listed in appendix. Honored, commemorated, carved on the memorial at Ashcombe Cross." He let that sit. "There were four hundred and eleven men who died at Vellmore who appear in no dispatch, on no memorial, in no pension record. Men whose deaths were inconvenient. Rear guard actions written off as stragglers. Wounded abandoned during the retreat whose paperwork was closed before their hearts were. Conscripts listed as deserters so their families wouldn't be owed anything." His voice held. He had practiced this part against the dark. "I wrote down every name I knew. Six years. Four hundred and eleven."

The silence in the cellar was total. Even the damp seemed to hold still.

It was Tam who moved first — Tam, who reached out and touched the ledger's cover with one finger, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"That's why," he whispered. "That's why you took me in. That's why you asked me all those questions that night. About what I saw leaving the ridge."

"I needed witnesses, Tam. You were one."

"And us?" Mrs. Halloway's voice was steady but her hands, folded in her lap, had gone white at the knuckles. "Why are you showing us?"

"Because Elodie Ferris showed me her own list yesterday, and because there's a commission coming, and because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I can't carry it alone anymore, and I'm tired of pretending I'm not carrying anything at all."

### Bram

Bram looked at the ledger and saw, very clearly, the inside of a bottle.

This was how it worked: he could go weeks, sometimes a whole month, keeping the thing submerged, and then some small thing — a smell, a song, a ledger on a table — and it rose up entire, the ridge at Vellmore in the rain, the order in his hand, his own signature at the bottom of it like a wound that had learned to write.

*Withdraw rear elements. Wounded non-ambulatory to be left for follow-on retrieval.*

There had been no follow-on retrieval. Bram had known there would be no follow-on retrieval when he signed it. Colonel Ashgrave had known it too, standing there with the rain running off his immaculate collar, saying *the column cannot wait, Sergeant, and neither can the war*, and Bram — thirty pounds lighter then, and believing still that officers must know things a sergeant didn't — had signed.

Forty-one men. He'd done the arithmetic a thousand times since, drunk and sober and variously between. Forty-one men left lying on that ridge in the rain, and Bram Tull had walked away behind a marching column with his ears full of the sound of men calling out, and had kept walking for six years, through three towns and a marriage that failed and a hundred barrels of drink, and had eventually washed up in this village because Kestrel Coldharbour was here, though Bram had told himself it was coincidence right up until the day he stopped bothering to lie to himself about it.

And now the ledger sat on the table with forty-one of those names in it, probably, maybe, and Kestrel was looking at him with those calm gray eyes and saying nothing at all, which was the worst thing he could do, because Bram could resist an argument but not a silence.

"Well," Bram said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. Took a pull of whisky that was larger than two fingers and knew it. "You want us to read it? Is that it? You want witnesses?"

"I want—" Kestrel paused. "I don't rightly know what I want. I know that Elodie Ferris thinks the record can be corrected if someone will stand up and speak, and I know that speaking will ruin whoever speaks, and I know that I have been a coward about this for six years in the specific shape of being careful."

"Careful isn't cowardice," Tam said fiercely, from his corner. "Careful kept you alive."

"Careful kept everyone else's sons unburied," Mrs. Halloway said, and Bram flinched.

She hadn't understood yet. He could see it — she understood the ledger, understood the dead men, but not yet what it meant that her boys' names might be in there, or might not, and either answer was a knife. Bram watched her reach for the ledger and stop herself, hands hovering over it, asking permission with her whole body.

"May I?" she said.

"It's yours as much as mine," Kestrel said. "More."

She opened it. The lamplight caught the columns of ink — name, unit, date, circumstance, in Kestrel's neat apothecary hand — and Mrs. Halloway read the way she did everything, thoroughly and without hurry, and the cellar got colder by degrees, and Bram watched her lips move and prayed, for the first time in years, to a God he'd filed under retired, *not the third page, please God, anywhere but the third page.*

She turned to the third page.

Her breath went out of her like a door coming off its hinges.

"Aldous," she said. Just the name. Then, steadier: "Aldous Coldharbour Halloway. Private, Ninth Fusiliers. And Peter. Peter, here, the twelfth of October." Her finger trembled above the ink but did not quite touch it, as if the ink were holy. "You wrote down the time of day. You wrote down *who held his hand.*"

"Somebody did," Kestrel said quietly. "For Peter it was a volunteer named Iris Penn. For Aldous—" He hesitated. "Aldous was past speech, ma'am. But he wasn't alone. I made sure of that much. I couldn't make sure of anything else."

Mrs. Halloway closed the ledger. Held it flat against her chest with both arms, the way you'd hold a child. When she spoke, her voice was perfectly level, which in Mrs. Halloway was the sound of a woman standing at the very edge of something.

"Wren," she said. "My youngest. Wren isn't here."

"No," Kestrel said. "Wren's death was properly recorded. He died at the aid station with full honors. Whatever else failed in that war, ma'am, nothing failed for Wren."

She nodded. She sat with it. Then, to Bram's eternal astonishment, she laughed — one short, cracked, real laugh, wet-eyed and furious and glad all at once.

"Six years," she said. "Six years I've brought that man my hip. My *hip.* And all along he was keeping my boys' last hours safe under a floorboard like a dragon on treasure." She turned to Kestrel. "Young man, if you ever make me attend another appointment about my hip, I shall strike you with my cane."

### Tam

Tam could not stop looking at the ledger.

He couldn't read well — he'd never said it outright, but they all knew, and Kestrel had been teaching him letters twice a week for a year now under the guise of "sorting inventory labels," and Tam had gotten so he could manage a page of print if the print wasn't in a hurry. But he didn't need to read it. He could see what it was. It was the opposite of the thing that had happened to him.

When Tam ran, the army had written him down as lost to duty, effective immediately, no further inquiry. Two lines. He had ceased to exist as a person and become a category. He'd spent three years since waiting for someone to come collect the category, and instead Kestrel had come — had climbed the fence after him with bandages, of all things, had sat with him in a ditch and asked him questions no one had ever asked: *What did you see? What did you hear? What were the men called, the ones who didn't make the retreat?*

And Tam had answered, because nobody had ever wanted to know before, and the answers had apparently gone into this book, into these pages, into ink that stayed.

"You wrote down Corporal Vey," Tam said suddenly. "Davy Vey. He was next to me when the shelling started. Nobody ever—his mum never even got a letter, I heard, they just told her he was missing and then stopped telling her anything."

"He's on page nine," Kestrel said. "With a note that his effects were never returned. If the commission takes the ledger, that note might get his mother a pension. Or at least an answer."

Tam's eyes were burning and he blinked hard and pretended to study the ceiling beams. *Men don't cry in front of sergeants,* he reminded himself, and then corrected: *Sergeants cry all the time, actually, Bram just does it into bottles so no one counts it.*

"So what happens now?" he asked. "Do we take it to this commission? Do you testify?"

"That," said Kestrel, "depends somewhat on whether anyone helps me, and somewhat on whether helping me ruins them, which it may. Testimony means scrutiny. Scrutiny means questions about everyone near me." His eyes went, briefly and involuntarily, to Tam. Everyone in the cellar saw it. Tam's stomach dropped through the floorboards, past the ledger's hiding place, into the earth.

"There it is," Bram said heavily. He set down his glass. "Say it plain, Kess. If you stand up in front of that commission, the first thing they'll do is pull every thread hanging off you. And the longest thread in this room has sandy hair and climbed a fence in November of 'ninety-four."

Nobody said anything. The lamp hissed. Tam became extremely interested in the grain of the table.

"I won't do it," Kestrel said finally. "Not at that price. Not yet. Maybe there's a way that doesn't—"

"Stop." Mrs. Halloway's voice cut clean across him. She had set the ledger down, and her hands were folded on top of it now, deliberately, like a woman claiming territory. "I am sixty-eight years old. I have buried a husband and three sons and my patience with being protected by well-meaning men somewhere around the second funeral. You will not decide for this room what we can bear. That is our decision, each of ours, and I'll thank you to ask it."

"She's right," Bram said, to his own visible surprise. He rubbed his jaw. "God help me, she's right. Look — Kess. I've got reasons to want this quiet. Good ones. Better than mine, even." Something crossed his face, fast, like a bird past a window. "But I've also got forty-one reasons sitting on that ridge in the rain, and I hear them every night whether this book exists or not. So ask us. Properly. Ask us like we've got a choice, because we do, and we'd like to exercise it."

Kestrel looked around the cellar — at the widow with her hands on the ledger, the deserter with his chin up, the sergeant with his empty glass — and Tam saw something happen in his face, some load shifting, some weight set down and picked up differently.

"All right," Kestrel said. "Then I'm asking. Will you stand with me? Knowing what it costs?"

### Kestrel

"Yes," said Mrs. Halloway, before he finished the sentence.

"Yeah," said Bram, after a swallow that had nothing to do with whisky.

Tam opened his mouth, closed it, opened it again. "I'm scared," he said, with the terrible honesty of the young. "Proper scared. But I've been scared since I was seventeen and it turns out you can carry scared around like a coat. So. Yes. I'm in."

Kestrel found, to his considerable surprise, that he could not speak for a moment. He had prepared arguments, contingencies, appeals. He had not prepared for the thing to simply be given to him.

"Well," he managed. "That's settled, then. We'll need copies of the ledger — fair copies, verified, in case the original is seized. We'll need sworn statements from anyone who can corroborate a name. And we'll need—" 

A knock at the top of the cellar stairs interrupted him. Three raps, precise, unhurried, unfamiliar.

Everyone froze. Tam's chair scraped. Bram's hand went, absurdly and instinctively, to a sidearm he had not worn in six years.

Kestrel climbed the stairs and opened the shop door onto the night, and the cold air that came in smelled of frost and horses.

A woman stood on the step, lantern raised — Elodie Ferris, census satchel still slung across her body at half past nine at night, cheeks pink with hurrying, an expression on her face that Kestrel had learned, in their two meetings, to recognize as the look of a woman carrying news she wished she didn't have.

"Forgive the hour," she said. "I was at the coaching inn when the mail came through, and I thought you'd want to know tonight rather than tomorrow." She glanced past him, down the stairwell, at the lamplight and the faces below, and did not pretend she hadn't seen. "There's been an announcement in the London gazette. They're calling it the Royal Commission on War Records. It sits through the winter, starting the first week of December." She lowered her voice. "Kestrel — the chairman's preliminary statement is already printed. They're framing the whole business as clerical error. And the Commission's chief advisor on military records—"

She handed him a printed notice, and Kestrel read the name at the bottom of it, and the cellar behind him went very quiet indeed.

Colonel Edmund Ashgrave, C.B., decorated hero of the Vellmore Campaign.

Coming home.
