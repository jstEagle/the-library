## 6. Colonel Ashgrave Comes Home

The bunting went up in Marrowdale on a Tuesday, which was how I knew trouble had arrived before I ever saw its face.

Villages have their own language of cloth. Black crepe means a death; white means a wedding; red-and-gold streamers strung from the inn's upper windows to the smithy mean somebody Important is passing through and the landlord wants it remembered that he once served them sherry. Tam pointed at the streamers that morning while we wrapped parcels of willow bark for the Hartley farm, and asked, with the flat suspicion of a young man who has learned what uniforms and celebrations cost, "Who's dead?"

"Nobody," I said. "Worse. Somebody's being welcomed."

He didn't laugh. Neither did I, really, but I made the shape of it with my mouth, because an assistant apothecary who goes about looking like a funeral is bad for trade, and because I had spent six years practicing the shape of calm until it fit me like my own coat. That was before I knew how quickly a coat can be taken off you by someone else's hands.

* * *

I heard the name first from Mrs. Halloway, who came in Wednesday for her tincture and stayed to gossip, as was her right and custom.

"They're saying Colonel Ashgrave's come back to the district," she said, watching me measure out drops with more attention than the task deserved. "For the Commission. Advising them, they say. Him that got the star with clusters." She said *star with clusters* the way other women said *cancer of the stomach*. "You remember him, Kestrel? He'd have come through the hospital tents. Tall gentleman. Silver in his sideburns even then. Talked to everyone like they were slightly hard of hearing."

My hand did not shake. I had trained it not to, over six years of small cruelties and smaller mercies, and I was proud of that for perhaps four seconds before I realized Mrs. Halloway was watching the steadiness of my hand the way I should have been watching hers.

"I treated hundreds of men," I said. "Names blur."

"Mm," said Mrs. Halloway, who had buried three sons and could therefore smell evasion from across a counter. "Well. Blur all you like. But if I were a man with a ledger under his floorboards—and mind, I'm only speaking of a hypothetical sort of man—I'd want to know that the colonel's taken rooms at the Bell and Horn, and that he's been seen twice at the Commission offices in Thistleford, and that yesterday he bought flowers."

"Flowers," I repeated.

"For his table," said Mrs. Halloway. "White ones. He told the florist he liked things clean."

She took her tincture and left forty seconds of silence behind her that lasted me most of the afternoon.

* * *

Here is what I knew of Colonel Reginald Ashgrave, though I would have paid good money to know less.

In the third year of the war, at a place called Corven Bridge, the wounded were moved too slowly and the retreat began too early, and when the counting was done there were men on the official rolls of Corven Bridge who had never been there at all, and men who had been there—thirty-one of them, I could have recited them alphabetically—who appeared nowhere except in a ledger kept in a nurse's slanted hand. Ashgrave had signed the dispatch. I never saw him sign it. I saw the signature afterward, on a report Elodie Ferris would later tell me had been altered in three places by three different inks, and I thought, even then, that the handwriting looked like the man must look: upright, economical, entirely without waste.

Ashgrave's omissions outnumbered any other officer's. Not by accident. Omissions multiply when someone prunes them, and pruning requires a gardener.

That was the man now advising the Royal Commission on War Records on how best to correct its clerical errors. That was the man buying white flowers.

* * *

He came on Friday, at half past four, when the shop is quiet and the light through the south window turns everything the color of weak tea.

I was grinding valerian and did not hear the door at first—or rather, I heard it and assumed a customer, and said "Be with you directly" in the cheerful voice I keep hanging by the door like an apron, and received no answer, which is when I turned around.

He stood just inside the entrance, hat in hand, taking inventory of my shelves with the mild interest of a man appraising a property he already owns. He was taller than I remembered, or I had shrunk, which are often the same thing. The sideburns had gone fully silver. His coat was civilian grey, beautifully cut, and somehow managed to suggest that every uniform he had ever worn was folded away inside it.

"You'll be Coldharbour," he said. "The apothecary's assistant."

"And you'll be Colonel Ashgrave," I said, before my caution could catch up with my tongue.

Something crossed his face—not displeasure. Recognition, almost pleasure, the way a chess player is pleased when an opponent he'd underestimated produces a decent move. "Word does travel. May I?" He gestured at the stool behind the counter without waiting for permission, and sat, arranging himself as neatly as a letter folds. "I'm told this establishment has served the district faithfully since before the war. Old Mr. Penhale, was it, before you?"

"Before me, yes."

"And you came after. From the front, they say. Vellmore and points east." He said *Vellmore* the way a man says the name of a town where nothing happened. "You must have seen a great deal of the war's paperwork. Requisitions, casualty returns, that sort of thing. Nurses always end up handling paper. Half the army's clerks are men who started out carrying stretchers."

"I carried both," I said. "Paper's lighter."

"It multiplies faster, though." He smiled. It was a fine smile, well-maintained, like the rest of him. "That's why I've taken an interest in the Commission's work, Mr. Coldharbour. There's a great deal of paper from those years in a very disordered state. Contradictory returns. Names entered twice, names misspelled, names—" he examined his thumbnail "—entered where they shouldn't be, or absent where sentiment might wish otherwise. A historian's nightmare. My task is to help them separate what actually happened from what grieving people have, quite understandably, imagined happened. You'd be surprised how many widows' memories improve with distance. Every year that passes, another few hundred men turn up in the recollections of the bereaved who were never reported lost at all. Clerical ghosts. It's a kindness to correct them, really. Closure. Clean lines."

Behind my ribs, my heart was conducting itself with a discipline I did not feel. Under the floorboard in the room upstairs lay four hundred and eleven names he would call clerical ghosts. I put my hands flat on the counter so they would have something honest to do.

"What can I dispense for you, Colonel? You didn't come for valerian."

"Observation," he said pleasantly. "And, I hope, to deliver a courtesy. When I take up a post in a district, I make a point of calling on its professional people. Doctors, clergymen, magistrates. Keeps communication open. Prevents misunderstandings later." He rose, unhurried, and drifted along the shelf of labeled drawers, trailing one gloved finger an inch above the labels without touching anything—a soldier's habit, checking ground without disturbing it. "Poppy. Willow. Foxglove, careful with that one. You keep an orderly shop, Mr. Coldharbour. Everything measured. Everything recorded." He glanced back at me. "A man of your temperament must find it difficult, leaving things unrecorded."

"I keep the records I'm required to keep," I said. "No more."

"No," agreed Ashgrave. "More would be unusual. Ambitious, even. Presumptuous." He let the word sit between us like a coin on a counter. "There was a rumor reaching me—not from here, from further down the line, these rumors travel like water finding ditches—that during the campaign there was a nurse attached to the third field hospital who wrote things down. Who kept a private accounting. Names. Dates. Little judgments about decisions that were, frankly, far above his station and his understanding. Perhaps you heard of such a fellow?"

The light through the south window had gone from weak-tea brown to something darker. I became aware of sounds with terrible clarity: the tick of the clock, the creak of the settling shelves, my own breath moving through me like a man trying to cross a room quietly.

"There were a great many nurses," I said.

"There were. Most of them had the good sense to confine themselves to nursing." He completed his circuit of the shelves and came back to stand opposite me, and for the first time he leaned forward slightly, both hands on the counter, close enough that I could see his eyes were pale grey with darker rims, and absolutely patient. "But supposing such a fellow existed. Supposing he had written down names that appear nowhere in the official record. What do you suppose becomes of that document, Mr. Coldharbour, in the eyes of the law? It isn't evidence. It's forgery. A private man manufacturing casualties the Crown never acknowledged—in wartime, no less. Why, a hostile court would call it sedition dressed as sympathy." He straightened, smoothing his lapels, and the warmth returned to his voice like gas to a lit lamp. "Of course, if no such document exists—if our nurse burned it years ago, as a sensible man would—then none of this signifies, and I've wasted a pleasant quarter hour on gossip. Gossip being, in my experience, ninety percent smoke and ten percent arson."

"The Commission sits through winter, I'm told," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me; it seemed to belong to someone standing slightly behind my shoulder. "In Thistleford."

"In Thistleford," Ashgrave agreed. "Correcting errors. Tidying history. It's remarkable, Mr. Coldharbour, how much of history is tidying." He put on his hat, adjusting the angle with two fingers. "I'll say this for you: you lie worse than you think you do and better than most men manage. It's the eyes. You look at the counter instead of at the person you're deceiving. Something to work on, if you intend to go on doing it."

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and turned back with the expression of a host remembering a courtesy.

"I nearly forgot. The flowers. White ones, for my table—the florist here keeps a respectable stock, you know. As I was paying, she mentioned that young fellow who lodges above the saddlery. Quiet lad, does errands, doesn't talk much. She wondered aloud whether he might be some relation of yours, the way he comes and goes from this shop." Ashgrave's voice stayed perfectly pleasant. "I said I was sure I couldn't say. Deserter or nephew, these country districts blur all sorts of relationships, don't they? One wants to be careful about assumptions." He settled the hat a final fraction. "Good evening, Mr. Coldharbour. Do keep well. This district has lost enough of its men to carelessness."

The door closed behind him with a soft, well-bred click, and I stood in the smell of valerian and foxglove with my hands still flat on the counter, understanding that I had just been searched, catalogued, and left standing—all without a single drawer being opened.

* * *

Tam noticed within minutes. That is the thing about Tam: the war taught him to read rooms the way other boys read adventure serials, quickly and with an eye for exits. He came down from the storeroom, looked at my face, and set down the box he was carrying.

"He was here," Tam said. "Who was here?"

"A customer."

"Don't." It wasn't defiance in his voice. It was something older and wearier, borrowed from whichever cell or ditch had taught it to him. "Kestrel. Don't do the voice. I know the voice. Who was here?"

I looked at this young man whom I had sheltered, fed, taught to read prescriptions, watched grow from a trembling stray into something like a son, and I understood exactly what Ashgrave had done in my shop: he had not threatened me. He had simply shown me the shape of the gun and set it down on the table between us, pointing nowhere in particular, and left me to notice that it pointed everywhere at once. At Tam. At Mrs. Halloway and her forged pension papers. At Bram, drinking himself toward forgetting at a rate that suggested forgetting fought back. At Elodie Ferris, census taker, keeper of lists, who had shown me her own book of names three weeks ago and changed the direction of my life with a piece of government stationery.

"Colonel Ashgrave," I said.

Tam went very still, the way animals go still.

"He knows about the ledger," I said. "Not where it is. Not what's in it. But that it exists. 'The nurse who writes things down.' Those were his words." I heard my own voice drop and steady into something I did not recognize, something with edges. "He came to tell me that history belongs to the men who sign the reports. And that if anyone disagrees, the disagreement can be prosecuted."

"What do we do?" Tam whispered. "Do we burn it? Kestrel, do we burn it?"

And there it was, the question I had been avoiding since Tuesday's bunting, since Wednesday's tincture, since long before either—since the night Elodie Ferris set her list beside mine and said, *Your dead are my dead too.* Burn it, and Ashgrave won by default, and four hundred and eleven men stay ghosts forever, tidy and corrected and closed. Keep it, and every soul under this roof stands in the light of it with me.

"We don't burn it," I said. "We need Bram. Tonight. And Mrs. Halloway." I took off my apron, folded it, and found that my hands, at last, had something honest to do. "If the colonel wants to know what a nurse writes down, then it's past time somebody read it to him."
