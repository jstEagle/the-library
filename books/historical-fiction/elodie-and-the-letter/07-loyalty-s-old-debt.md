## 7. Loyalty's Old Debt

Bram Tull had learned, over six years of trying to forget things, that memory was a dog that wouldn't be put down. You could stop feeding it. You could move houses twice and change taverns three times. It would still turn up on your doorstep on a Tuesday evening, wet-eyed and loyal, wanting its walk.

This particular Tuesday it turned up as the smell of Kestrel's apothecary—camphor and dried yarrow and something bitter underneath that was probably the tincture for Mrs. Prew's stomach—and the sight of lamplight in the window, gold and steady, the way no light at Vellmore had ever been.

He stood across the lane for a long moment with his collar up against the drizzle, working up to it. He'd rehearsed what he meant to say on the walk from the King's Arms. He'd rehearsed it twice more at the bar, which was why he'd only had one pint instead of his usual four, and why Denny behind the counter had asked him if he was ill.

*You can't do this, Kes. That's all you have to say. You can't do this.*

Simple. A man could carry a sentence like that in one hand.

He crossed the lane and knocked.

"It's open," came Kestrel's voice from inside, "unless you're Colonel Ashgrave, in which case it's also open but I'll be less pleased."

Bram let himself in. The shop smelled of rain and lamp oil and forty kinds of leaves hanging upside-down from the ceiling beams like small brown bats. Kestrel was behind the counter grinding something, sleeves rolled, that look on his face he got when he was counting things in his head—which, Bram thought, was most of the time. Six years of knowing the man and Bram had never once caught him not counting something.

"Colonel Ashgrave," Bram said, shaking the rain off his coat onto the welcome mat, because even dread didn't excuse bad manners toward a floorboard. "Is that who's been calling on you, then?"

The pestle stopped. Just for a beat. Then it resumed, slower.

"You've heard," Kestrel said.

"Denny heard. Denny tells everyone. Half the county knows the Commission's sitting through winter before the Gazette's ink is dry." Bram hung his coat on the peg by the door—the same peg he'd used since the first winter after the war, when Kestrel had found him sleeping rough behind the tithe barn and said, without ceremony, *there's a room above the shop if you don't snore.* Bram snored. He stayed anyway. "They're saying it's about clerical errors."

"Mm." Kestrel set the pestle down. He looked at Bram the way he looked at a wound he'd already decided how to treat. "Sit down, Sergeant."

"Don't call me that."

"Old habit."

"Well, break it." Bram lowered himself onto the stool by the stove, because his knees were fifty-two years old and had opinions. "Nobody's been a sergeant for six years."

"That's true of a great many things," Kestrel said quietly, and poured him a cup of tea without asking, which was its own kind of argument. Kestrel always made tea before hard conversations. Bram had watched him do it with families of the dying. Sweet, strong, and given in both hands so there was nothing left for the person to do except hold on.

Bram held on.

"I'm going to testify," Kestrel said. "I wanted you to hear it from me before you heard it in the square."

There it was. The dog off the lead. Bram set the cup down carefully, because if he didn't give his hands a job they'd start doing something worse.

"No," he said.

"Bram—"

"No. Listen to me, Kes." And here was where the rehearsal was supposed to hold. Here was where he'd say it plainly and go home. But what came out instead was everything, the whole flood of it, because Bram Tull had never in his life managed to say a thing simply. "If you stand up in front of that Commission—if you read out that book of yours—they're going to want to know how you know. Names, dates, units. They'll pull every thread, and every thread leads somewhere, and some of those somewheres are people I'd die before I'd see hanged by their collars in a courtroom."

Kestrel went very still. He had a gift for stillness; Bram had seen wounded men calm just from watching it.

"Tam," Kestrel said.

"Tam." The name came out of Bram like a tooth. "Desertion's still on the books. His regiment's still mustered. He ran from Colchester with two hundred lashes waiting for him and the only reason nobody's come looking is that nobody's asked the right question yet. And you—" Bram jabbed a finger at the floor, at the boards under the counter where the ledger slept, "—you are about to become a man who asks exactly the right questions, in public, under oath, with lawyers taking notes."

"He's twenty-three years old. The war's been over six years."

"And the books stay open longer than that! Ask me how I know." Bram heard his own voice climb and dragged it back down. The shop was small; the walls were thin; Mrs. Prew lived above. He lowered it to something closer to a plea. "Kes. They'll ask Tam how he knows what he saw at the rear guard, and he'll answer honest, because he's Tam, and the third question out of their mouths will be *and which unit were you serving with, son?*"

The fire ticked in the grate. Down the lane a cart rattled past, someone late getting home.

"And that's not all," Bram pressed on, because now he'd started and God help him he couldn't stop. "Mrs. Halloway."

"What about her?"

"You know what about her." Bram rubbed his face. "Her pension papers. Three sons dead and a widow's allowance of nine shillings a week, and do you think the War Office paid that out of kindness? Her eldest signed enlistment papers at seventeen—she signed them *for* him, backdated his birth by a year, and if anyone ever looks close at that ink she's not a grieving mother anymore, she's a forger. Nine years' fraud against the Crown. She's sixty-eight, Kes. They don't hang sixty-eight-year-old women, but they do put them out of their houses."

"She signed them so her boy could follow his brothers' drum," Kestrel said. "Any commission with a drop of sense—"

"They're not sitting on drops of sense!" Bram was on his feet now, though he had no memory of standing. "They're sitting because the historians want a clean book and the generals want a quiet one, and you want to hand them four hundred reasons to start pulling threads in this village. Threads attached to Tam. To Mrs. Halloway. To half the men who come through your door on Thursdays and don't talk about why." He steadied himself on the counter. "To you."

"To me least of all," Kestrel said. "I've done nothing wrong. I wrote down names."

"You wrote down names that other men spent good money erasing. That's not nothing, that's the worst kind of something." Bram sat back down, slowly, because his knees had filed a formal complaint. "Kes. I'm not asking you to burn the book. I'm asking you to wait. Let the Commission sit and bluster and file its clerical errors and ride out of town in spring like weather. Nobody reads those reports. Nobody ever has."

"Somebody will read mine."

"Then read it to yourself! Read it every night till you're ninety!" Bram heard the thing underneath his own words then, ugly and true, and hated it: he wasn't protecting the ledger's honor or the Commission's dignity. He was protecting a twenty-three-year-old boy who ate at his table and called him *Uncle Bram* when he thought Bram was asleep, and a widow who brought him pies on the anniversary of her sons' deaths because she couldn't bear to eat them alone. He was protecting the only family he had left, and he was doing it by asking a good man to bury the truth in a hole under the floorboards.

Love, he thought bitterly, made a fine argument for anything. It had made a fine argument for following orders once, too.

Kestrel picked up the teapot and refilled Bram's cup, though Bram hadn't touched the first one. That was Kestrel all over—he noticed everything and mentioned nothing.

"You think I haven't counted them," Kestrel said at last. "The threads. Tam. Mrs. Halloway. Old Cobb, whose hearing went at Sedgemoor and who'd swear black is white if a lawyer frowned at him right. I've sat in this chair every night since the announcement doing arithmetic, Bram. I know what testimony costs. I've priced it."

"Then—"

"But I keep coming back to the widows who aren't Mrs. Halloway." Kestrel's voice was very low and very level, the voice he used for dosing. "Forty-one women on my list lost men who officially never existed. No pension. No name on any stone. One of them writes to me every Christmas—a Mrs. Anselm, up in Northgate—asking, politely, every year, as if she's afraid of being a bother, whether I've heard anything, whether there's been any mistake, whether her Thomas might yet turn up somewhere. And every year I write back and lie to her, because the truth—that her husband was left on the field by an officer who wanted tidy paperwork—isn't mine to give until it can survive daylight." He looked up. "How many Christmases do I owe her before waiting stops being prudence and starts being cruelty?"

Bram looked into his tea. There were things a man could say to that and things a man could not, and the space between them was exactly wide enough for a silence.

"You always did know where the soft ground was," he muttered.

"You taught me that, actually. At Vellmore. You knew which shell craters would hold a stretcher and which would swallow it." Kestrel almost smiled. It was a terrible almost-smile, worn thin as old coin. "I'm not asking you to like it. I'm telling you because you're my—because you matter to me, and because I won't have you learn it from Denny."

That nearly undid him. Six years, and neither of them had ever settled on the word for what they were to each other. Comrades was a soldier's word and too small. Friends was true but somehow missed the shape of it—the way a man learns another man's silences during a bombardment and keeps them forever after, like a scar in the shape of someone else's hand.

"Eat something before you go," Kestrel added. "There's pie."

"Whose pie?"

"Mrs. Halloway sent it."

"Of course she did." Bram took the plate, mostly to have something to do that wasn't arguing. The pie was mutton and onion and excellent, and he ate it in sulky silence while Kestrel tidied the shop around him, putting the jars straight, turning the label on the laudanum bottle to face front. Small ceremonies. A man who kept a secret ledger for six years learned to make ordinary things look occupied.

It was the ordinariness that finally broke Bram's resistance—not the arguments, not the widows in Northgate, but the sight of his friend labeling bottles in a lamplit shop, preparing, calmly, to walk into a room full of lawyers and be taken apart for it.

"If you do this," Bram said heavily, "and they come for Tam—"

"Then Tam comes to stay further away, and I pay for it, and we say he's gone to work the harvests."

"And if they come for Mrs. Halloway's papers—"

"Then I stand up in whatever court they drag her into and swear the ink was mine. Let them take the license of a man who's already lost everything worth losing." Kestrel said it lightly, which was how Bram knew he'd been carrying it a long time.

Bram set down his fork. Something cold walked down his spine, the old familiar cold, the rear-guard cold, the one he'd been outrunning for six years and was beginning to suspect he'd never lose.

Because here was the thing Kestrel didn't know. Couldn't know. Bram had never told anyone, and he had planned, with real dedication, to die without telling anyone, ideally in a warm bed after a large dinner.

But if Kestrel stood before that Commission and began reading names, the first question out of Ashgrave's counsel would be *who ordered the abandonment of the wounded at the ford*, and the second would be *who signed the order*, and the answer was not some distant colonel with a decoration and good manners.

The answer was sitting in this shop eating the last of Mrs. Halloway's pie.

Bram wiped his mouth. His hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, everything was the sound of shells walking up a ridge.

"Kes," he said. "There's something you ought to know about the rear guard. About who gave the order."

Kestrel turned from the shelf, jar in hand, and looked at him—and Bram watched, with a strange floating clarity, the exact moment his friend understood that the conversation had changed shape entirely.

Outside, unseen by either of them, a woman in a grey census office coat paused at the lamplit window, raised her hand as if to knock, thought better of it, and slipped a folded note beneath the door before walking quickly away into the dark.
