## 8. The Widow's Testimony

I had worn my good dress to be respectable, and I wore it now to be stubborn, which is a different thing entirely and requires better posture.

The dress was grey wool with a black collar, the one I'd had made for Bram Tull's medal ceremony six years ago, back when we all still believed medals meant something. It had held up well. Wool is honest that way. It keeps its shape even when everything around it loses its.

"Mrs. Halloway." Elodie Ferris stood beside me on the steps of the Shire Hall, her census satchel clutched against her hip like a shield. "You don't have to do this today. There will be other sessions. There will be—"

"There will not," I said. "There are four sessions scheduled and then they close the record and print their book. I've read the notice. I can read, whatever the Colonel's counsel may think of widows from the country."

"That's exactly what worries me."

"Well. Let it worry you at a distance. You'll wring your satchel into ribbons."

Kestrel said nothing at all. That was the trouble with Kestrel Coldharbour — he could fill a silence so completely you hardly noticed he hadn't spoken. He stood one step below us in his good coat, the dark one, the heavy one, and though it was a mild morning for November I noticed he did not take it off, and I noticed the way he kept his left arm pressed against his side as though the coat were heavier than wool has any right to be.

I knew what was beneath it. He'd shown us in the cellar three nights past — the ledger, the small neat book with its four hundred and eleven names, each one dated and numbered and witnessed as far as one exhausted nurse could witness anything in a field hospital lit by guttering tallow. I had found my sons' names in it. All three. Written in a hand that shook only on the last line of each page, when I imagine his own grief got ahead of him.

I had kissed that book. I am not ashamed to say it. I kissed the page where Thomas and Wilfred and young Alfie sat in ink, because no stone anywhere in this county bears them together, and a mother takes her comforts where she finds them.

"You'll speak plainly?" Kestrel asked finally. His voice was low, careful. "No names of officers? No—"

"I shall say what happened at Vellmore and who left whom in the mud, and if that embarrasses anyone it is because the truth has poor manners, not because I do."

Kestrel looked at me for a long moment. There was something in his face I couldn't name then — I can name it now, having had leisure to study it since. It was the look of a man watching someone walk out onto ice he himself has been circling for six years.

"Then God go with you, Mrs. Halloway," he said.

"He usually does," I said. "He and I have an arrangement. I do the talking; He does the forgiving."

Elodie laughed despite herself, a short startled sound, and squeezed my arm. And we went up the steps together into the hall where the Royal Commission on War Records sat in judgment upon clerical errors.

* * *

The hall had been arranged like church, which is to say like a courtroom dressed as a church: the commissioners elevated on a dais behind a long bench draped in bunting, the counsel's table below, benches for the public at the rear. A stenographer sat at a little desk with her fingers poised, a thin woman with spectacles and the patient expression of someone paid by the word. The public benches were perhaps half full — farmers, a few soldiers in civilian coats that fit them badly, two journalists from the city papers who wrote more than they listened.

At the counsel's table sat Mr. Prosser-Fane, counsel to the Commission, and beside him, quiet and courteous, Colonel Ashgrave.

I had seen the man before, of course — everyone in the district had seen his portrait in the *Gazette* after Vellmore, all jaw and decoration, the Hero of the Rear Guard. In life he was smaller than his portrait, which pleased me unreasonably. He had grey at his temples now and the sort of face that smiles easily because smiling costs it nothing. When our party entered he inclined his head toward Kestrel with the exact degree of warmth one shows a tradesman one does not wish to offend but does not intend to invite to dinner.

Kestrel inclined his back. Two men exchanging courtesies across a battlefield neither would name aloud. I saw it plain as a fence line, and I saw that Elodie saw it too, and I thought: good. Let there be witnesses to the weather before the storm.

The chairman, a Sir Somebody with a voice like a wet log splitting, called the session to order. Petitions first — a land dispute carried over from the autumn assizes, some matter of drainage rights, read out in tones of profound suffering. Then, at last: "Public testimony regarding the war records. Mrs. — ah — Halloway. Of Lower Dintock."

I rose. My knees announced themselves, as knees do past sixty, but I made them behave.

"Do you swear to tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth?"

"I have sworn that every Sunday for fifty years," I said, "and I mean to keep the habit."

Someone in the public benches snorted. One of the journalists' pens began to move.

* * *

I will set down here what I said, or near enough, because it matters that it was said plainly, whatever came after.

I told them about my sons. Three of them, and I named them slowly — Thomas Halloway, private, 2nd Battalion; Wilfred Halloway, private, same; Alfred, called Alfie, seventeen years old when he lied about his age to follow his brothers, and may God forgive me for signing the paper that let him. I told them how the official roll of the dead listed Thomas, wounded at the ford and died of it, and listed nothing at all for Wilfred or Alfie. How the parish register had no entry. How the pension office returned my claim twice, the second time with a note informing me that *no soldier of that description appears upon the rolls*, and how I had sat holding that note and thinking that I had raised three boys and buried none of them and been told, in effect, that I had raised none at all.

"I received my sons' effects in a single parcel," I told them. My voice held. I had rehearsed it against the kitchen wall and the wall is a hard audience. "One parcel for three men. A Bible with Alfie's name in it in his own writing, misspelled, because spelling was never his gift. A button. Half a letter Wilfred was writing to me that ends mid-sentence, in the middle of telling me about the weather, of all things. And a card saying *killed in action* without saying where or when or how, as if they had died of modesty."

The stenographer's fingers were moving fast. Sir Somebody leaned forward. For eleven minutes — I counted, because counting keeps a body steady — it seemed the thing might go as it should.

And then Mr. Prosser-Fane rose to examine me, and I learned what courtesy looks like when it has been sharpened.

* * *

"Mrs. Halloway." He was a soft-spoken man, round-faced, with the air of a curate about to discuss the harvest. "You must understand we mean no disrespect to your loss."

"No," I agreed. "You mean no disrespect at all. It simply arrives anyway."

A stir in the benches. Prosser-Fane smiled tolerantly, the smile of a man patting a dog.

"Let us proceed. Your son Thomas — his death is recorded. Wounded at the action at the Vellmore crossing, subsequently deceased. Correct?"

"Died of the wound in the field hospital, yes. Third day after."

"And your other two sons. You state they are absent from the rolls. Might there not be — innocent explanations? Men were moved between units constantly. Names were misheard by clerks working by lamplight. 'Halloway' might appear as 'Holloway,' 'Halloran,' any number of variants. Have you searched the rolls under such spellings?"

"I have searched under every spelling a Christian name and a mother's grief could devise. Including Holloway, Hollis, and once, in desperation, Halibut."

That got a laugh from the benches and a rap of the chairman's gavel, and Prosser-Fane let the laugh die before he turned back to me, and I saw then that the laughter had been useful to him. A laughing room is not a grieving room, and a grieving room might have started asking why the clerk's lamplight only ever went out over certain names.

"But you cannot produce evidence of their deaths," he continued gently. "No burial record. No witness affidavit. Only — forgive me — a parcel."

"A parcel, half a letter, and a mother's memory of sending three boys out a door that four hundred other mothers in this county opened the same way."

"Indeed. And yet." He folded his hands. "Mrs. Halloway, you have stated that these omissions were deliberate. That officers — you named Colonel Ashgrave in your written petition — *knowingly erased* casualties from dispatches. That is a grave accusation. What is its basis?"

"The basis is that I have eyes and a memory."

"Eyes and memory," he repeated, sorrowfully, as though reading from a diagnosis. "Madam, you were not at Vellmore."

"No. I was home burying the idea of my sons, which took considerably longer than a body would have."

Another laugh, another gavel. Prosser-Fane waited again, patient as frost.

"Were you present at the battle?"

"You know I wasn't."

"Were you present at the field hospital?"

"No."

"Have you served in any military capacity whatsoever?"

"I raised three soldiers. That is a military capacity, sir, and a longer campaign than yours."

The gavel again, harder. And now Prosser-Fane set down his notes entirely, which I understood was the signal, the way a man sets down his fork before carving.

"Mrs. Halloway. You lost three sons. The Commission acknowledges — indeed honors — that loss. But grief, however understandable, is not evidence. You have produced a theory built on absence: names absent from rolls, therefore crimes. Is it not at least possible that your sons' omission resulted from the ordinary chaos of war — clerks overwhelmed, records burned, men buried unnamed by comrades too pressed to report? Is it not possible that you have, through sorrow, constructed a villain where there is only misfortune?"

And there it was. Not shouted. Offered, almost tenderly. *You are a madwoman, madam, and we are so sorry for it.*

I heard the benches shift behind me. I heard one of the journalists stop writing, because there is nothing so delicious as a widow undone. And I heard — this above all — the silence from the third row, where Kestrel Coldharbour sat with his heavy coat and his heavy book and his heavy silence, and did not stand up.

* * *

I want to be fair to him, because I love him, and fairness is the tax love pays.

He could not stand. I know that now. If he stood, the questions would turn to him — *and how do you know, sir? What is that you keep beneath your coat? Are these entries in your hand, and by what authority?* — and the ledger, uncorroborated, undated against the dispatches, would be ruled a forgery by a hysterical nurse before the ink dried on the ruling. Elodie had explained the trap of it to us all in the cellar, drawing lines in spilled flour on the table like a general planning a campaign. One witness alone is a crank. Ten witnesses are a scandal. A hundred are history. We had one witness and a widow, and widows, per Mr. Prosser-Fane, are weather, not evidence.

But knowing a thing afterward and sitting in the hall hearing it happen are different animals entirely.

Prosser-Fane finished me off with kindness. He expressed the Commission's sympathy. He suggested, delicately, that the parish might arrange counseling through the diocese. He thanked me for my "evidently sincere convictions." And then Sir Somebody said, "Thank you, Mrs. Halloway. Next petitioner," in the tone of a man closing a window on a draft, and it was over, and I walked down from the box on legs that behaved only because I commanded them to and would hear about it later.

Colonel Ashgrave stood as I passed the counsel's table. He bowed. Actually bowed — a small, correct bow, the kind one gives an old woman at a funeral.

"Madam," he said, audibly, kindly, "your sons would have been proud of your courage."

It was perfectly done. It was the cruelest sentence ever spoken in that hall, and it was done so gently that half the room found it moving. I stopped. I looked at him — really looked, close up, at the smallness of him inside the decorations — and I said, equally audibly:

"My sons died at Vellmore, Colonel. You were there. You tell me where."

And I walked out before he could arrange his face.

* * *

They caught up with me on the steps. Elodie first, then Kestrel, then Bram Tull looming behind like weather coming over a hill.

"Mrs. Halloway—" Elodie began.

"Don't." I held up a hand. I was shaking now, properly, now that it was safe to. "Don't comfort me yet. Give me a moment to be angry first. Comfort too soon sets like concrete."

Bram made a noise that in a smaller man would have been a sob. "You were magnificent," he rumbled. "The Halibut line alone—"

"The Halibut line was true. I did search Halibut. Clerks are capable of anything."

Kestrel said nothing. He stood on the bottom step with his hat in his hands, turning it, and I watched him turn it and I understood that he was not going to apologize, because apologizing would require explaining, and he could not explain without confessing, and he could not confess without destroying the very evidence that might someday clear my sons' names. He was silent the way a man is silent carrying something breakable down stairs.

And I hated him for it. Briefly, hotly, unfairly — I hated him, standing there with four hundred and eleven names against his ribs while I had been made a fool of upstairs for mentioning three. Six years he had known. Six years he had written my boys down in secret ink and said nothing, and today, when the saying-something was wanted, he had sat in the third row being furniture.

"Mr. Coldharbour," I said.

His eyes came up. Grey eyes, tired ones. Waiting for the blow.

"Upstairs," I said, "that man offered me his condolences for sons whose deaths he ordered, and this room full of Christians found it touching. And you sat there and said nothing, and it was the wisest thing anyone did today, and I despise it, and I despise that it was wise."

"Yes, ma'am," Kestrel said quietly.

"It cannot happen again. Do you hear me? I will not be the only one bleeding in front of that bench. Whatever it costs, whoever stands — the next time one of us speaks in that hall, the rest of us rise with them. All of us. Or the whole thing is just grief performed for gentlemen's amusement, and I have performed enough for one week."

Something shifted in his face — not resolve exactly; resolve came later, I watched it arrive. First came something rawer. Shame, I think, meeting permission. A man who has spent six years hiding a book being told by a widow that hiding has become the greater sin.

"Yes, ma'am," he said again, and then, barely: "I'm sorry about your sons, Mrs. Halloway. About all of it. About today most of all."

"I know you are." I patted his cheek, because he is half my age and twice my sorrow and somebody has to. "Now come along. I'm told there's tea at the apothecary's, and if I'm going to be infamous I intend to be infamous on a full stomach."

* * *

We were halfway down the high street when Elodie Ferris caught Kestrel's sleeve and drew him aside, under the awning of the ironmonger's, and the two of them put their heads together with the urgency of conspirators in a penny dreadful. I pretended to admire a display of hinges. I am old, not deaf.

"—can't wait for the second session," Elodie was saying, low and fast. "Prosser-Fane will use today's transcript against every witness who follows. 'As established in the testimony of Mrs. Halloway—' He's building a wall out of her, Kestrel. But there's another way in. Someone inside the Commission. If the names could be entered directly into the permanent record — verified, corroborated — the public hearings stop mattering. But it means dates, units, witnesses for every entry. And it means your name on it. Legally. On paper, under oath, where Ashgrave can reach it."

A long silence. I examined hinges with great attention.

"All right," Kestrel said finally. Just that. Two syllables, and the whole shape of the winter changed.

Elodie exhaled like a woman setting down something heavy. "All right? Just — all right?"

"I've been practicing," Kestrel said dryly, "for six years."

Well. I allowed myself one small smile at the hinges, and then I turned around and announced that if there was scheming to be done it would be done somewhere with chairs, because I had testified before luncheon like a lady and I refused to conspire standing in the cold like a smuggler.

And as we turned toward the apothecary's, I saw, across the square, a carriage I recognized from the newspaper engravings draw up before the Commission offices, and Colonel Ashgrave descend from it, and pause, and look — not at me, not at Elodie, but at Kestrel Coldharbour, standing in the shadow of the ironmonger's awning with his heavy coat buttoned to the chin.

The Colonel touched his hat brim. Almost friendly.

And I thought: *he knows exactly where the ledger sleeps.*
