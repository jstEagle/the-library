## 9. Elodie's Bargain

I had rehearsed the offer eleven times on the walk from the coaching inn to Kestrel Coldharbour's door, and by the twelfth attempt I had abandoned all of them in favor of simply knocking and hoping my mouth would behave itself.

It was raining. It had been raining, on and off, for a fortnight, which meant every road in three counties had turned to soup and every errand had become an expedition. I stood under his eaves wringing out my skirt like a washerwoman and told myself that a woman who had spent two years chasing dead men through parish registers could certainly manage one conversation with a live man.

The latch lifted before I knocked a second time.

"You've been standing out there four minutes," Kestrel said. "I could hear you arguing with yourself."

"I was not arguing."

"You were. You lost, twice."

He stepped back to let me in, and I noted—as I had noted on every visit since the census—that he did this thing where his eyes went first to my hands, then to my face, then briefly over my shoulder to the lane behind me, as though cataloguing what I carried and who might have followed me carrying it. A soldier's habit that had survived six years of bandages and cough syrup. He still counted exits. I had begun to suspect he always would.

The shop smelled of camphor and dried yarrow and, beneath it, woodsmoke from the little stove in the back room where he lived. Mrs. Halloway's shawl hung drying on a peg. Two cups sat on the table already, and when I raised an eyebrow at the second one he said, without any expression at all, "Bram drinks here at seven. You're early. Or he's late. Both are usual."

"I'll take his chair anyway."

"Everyone does."

* * *

I will not pretend I came only for the ledger. I came partly because of the ledger, partly because of my brother Thomas, whose name existed nowhere but in my mother's heart and now, I suspected, in a book hidden under this man's floorboards—and partly, if I am honest with myself, which Elodie Ferris is constitutionally required to be, for reasons I had not yet found the courage to examine. But the business came first. Business always comes first with me; it is how I keep the other things from spilling.

"Kestrel," I said, "the Commission has published its sitting schedule."

He was grinding something with a mortar and pestle—valerian root, by the smell—and he did not stop, but the rhythm changed. Slower. Careful. "Has it."

"Sittings through winter. Open sessions Tuesdays, closed sessions Thursdays. And they've appointed an historical advisor." I let that land. "Colonel Ashgrave."

The pestle stopped entirely.

"He wrote the dispatches," Kestrel said quietly. Not a question. He had known it before I said it—I could see that now—but knowing a thing privately and hearing it spoken are different animals. "Of course he did. Who else would they ask to correct the record than the man who made it wrong."

"They're calling it 'clerical review.' Errors of transcription. Omissions due to the confusion of the field." I recited the language flatly, the way you'd read out a diagnosis you didn't believe. "There is a historian attached to the secretariat. A Mr. Pellworth. His preliminary paper calls Vellmore 'a regrettable but well-documented engagement.' Well-documented, Kestrel. Four hundred men unaccounted for and the documentation is *well*."

"Their ink dries faster than our grief does." He set down the pestle. "Why are you telling me this, Miss Ferris? You could have sent word. You walked nine miles in the rain to tell me a schedule."

"Because I'm not here about the schedule." I pulled my satchel onto my knees. "I'm here because I've found someone inside it."

* * *

His name was Wrenley—Mr. Josiah Wrenley, second clerk to the Commission secretariat, a small grey man with ink-stained cuffs and, as I had learned over three careful letters and one extremely nervous meeting in a Reading public house, a conscience that had been keeping him awake since the preliminary paper crossed his desk.

Wrenley had been the junior clerk who copied the original Vellmore casualty returns into the official digest. He remembered the numbers. He remembered that they hadn't matched. He had said so once, gently, to a superior, and had been told—with great politeness—that clerks were not paid to have opinions about arithmetic.

"He can't alter anything," I told Kestrel. "He can't leak the archive; they count the folios. But testimony given properly—sworn, witnessed, submitted through channels—he can make certain it gets entered into the permanent record instead of into a drawer. That's within his power. The difference between the permanent record and a drawer, Kestrel, is the entire history of everything anyone ever bothered to write down."

Kestrel had gone very still in the way I was beginning to recognize: not the stillness of a man listening but of a man standing very carefully on ice, testing whether it would hold him.

"And what does your Mr. Wrenley require," he said, "in exchange for his insomnia?"

"Corroboration." I took a breath. Here it was. Eleventh rehearsal, discarded; twelfth version, unrehearsed; mouth, behaving so far. "Every name. Dates. Unit designations. Where they fell or where they were left. And witnesses—living witnesses who can swear to individual facts. Not the whole truth at once, he says, that's impossible, but the truth in pieces small enough to verify. If a name can be tied to a date and a unit and one living person who saw it, then it goes in. Permanently. Verbatim. Under seal of the Crown, where no historian—not even Pellworth—can quietly lose it."

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That is not a small request, Miss Ferris."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

"It's asking me to hand over the ledger."

"It's asking you to make the ledger *unnecessary*." I leaned forward. "Listen to me. That book under your floor has kept those names alive the way a lamp keeps a room lit—one flame, one room, and only if nobody opens the door. Put them in the permanent record and they're daylight. They're in the stone. Your floorboard can rot, the shop can burn, you can die—which, for the record, Kestrel Coldharbour, you eventually will—and the names will still be there, printed, indexed, argued over by tedious scholars for a hundred years. Isn't that what you wanted? Isn't that why you started?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere in the shop a bottle of tincture settled against its neighbors with a small glass clink, like a patient shifting in bed.

"You say 'it' as though the ledger is a parcel," he said finally. "It's four hundred and eleven men. Some of them I held while they died. One of them asked me to tell his mother he wasn't afraid, and I lied, because he was, terribly, and she deserved better than terror as her last inheritance from him. That book is not a document to me. It's a ward round that never ended."

I had prepared for refusal. I had prepared for fear. I had not quite prepared for this—the rawness under the apothecary's calm—and for a moment I forgot my business entirely and just let him be a man holding four hundred and eleven ghosts.

"I know," I said softly. "Thomas is number sixty-three in mine."

Something moved across his face. "You keep yours numbered too."

"Habit of the trade. Census work. Everything gets a column and a figure." I smiled, and it came out crooked. "My mother thinks I'm morbid. My mother thinks a great many things about me."

* * *

"There's more," I said, because there was, and because he deserved the whole weight of it before he answered. "And this is the part I nearly didn't come to say, and then I thought: he lies badly, this man, he'll respect it worse if I soften it."

"Go on."

"If you do this—if your names go into evidence—you accept legal exposure. Real exposure." I laid it out plainly, item by item, the way Wrenley had laid it out to me. "Unofficial record-keeping during active service was contrary to standing orders. If Ashgrave's counsel wants to destroy you, the easiest route is not to dispute your facts—it's to put you in the dock as a forger and a collector of military intelligence. They won't need to prove you wrong. They only need to prove you weren't authorized to be right."

"I'd be punished for remembering."

"Precisely. Punished for accurate memory. Welcome to the Empire." I heard my own bitterness and reined it in. "Wrenley can shield the manner of submission. He cannot shield you. Once the names are sworn, they're yours forever—on the transcript, in the newspapers, in Ashgrave's crosshairs. And there's a second exposure, smaller but real: anyone who corroborates you steps forward too. Witnesses can't be anonymous in a sworn record. So whoever vouches for your facts puts their own necks out beside yours."

For the first time since I'd arrived, Kestrel laughed—a short, dry sound with no humor in it whatsoever, like a branch cracking.

"So the offer is: give up my anonymity, endanger everyone I care about, arm the man who buried their husbands, and hope that one nervous clerk in Reading is braver than the entire Royal Commission."

"When you say it like that it sounds terrible."

"How else would you say it?"

"In the way I practiced," I admitted. "Which involved considerably more emphasis on 'permanent record' and considerably less on 'crosshairs.'"

That startled a second laugh out of him—this one almost real—and I counted it a small victory, filed it away in the growing cabinet of Kestrel Coldharbour moments I appeared to be keeping without having decided to.

Then he was serious again, and he was quiet for a long time, turning his cup in circles on the table without drinking from it.

"Miss Ferris," he said at last. "Elodie." — and I confess the Christian name, used for the first time, landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there — "you understand what you're asking me to weigh. On one side: silence, safety, and the knowledge that when the last of us dies, those four hundred and eleven men become a rounding error in a well-documented engagement. On the other: truth, on the record, and the genuine possibility that I end my days branded a liar and a criminal by the very history I tried to mend."

"Yes."

"And you've made this bargain yourself already. With your list. Three counties of tracing, letters to strangers, questions that got you noticed—I've seen how you notice being noticed. You've been standing where I'm standing for two years."

"I have."

"What decided you?"

I thought about Thomas. About the letter our family received saying he had fallen honorably, and the second letter, months later, from a corporal who had actually seen it, saying he had fallen alone, in the mud past the wire, in the part of the field no one was sent to recover. About how the first letter had been composed by an officer and the second by a witness, and how only one of them had felt like truth.

"Somebody wrote him down wrong," I said. "And somebody else wrote him down right, and the right one was never filed. That's the whole of it, Kestrel. Somebody has to file it. I'd rather it were done by people who loved them than by historians who find them inconvenient."

He nodded slowly. Then he said the thing I had walked nine miles through the rain hoping, beyond hope, to hear:

"All right. On conditions."

"Name them."

"One: nothing goes in until every name is corroborated as far as living memory allows. If we can't verify a man, he waits—we don't guess. Guessing is how their history got written in the first place."

"Agreed. Wrenley insists on the same."

"Two: Bram Tull, Mrs. Halloway, and Tam are not approached, pressured, or exposed unless they choose it themselves. Their secrets are theirs. Tam especially." Something protective flickered in his voice, and I made a private note of it—of Tam, whoever Tam was—and let it pass unexamined.

"Three?" I prompted, when he paused.

"Three." He almost smiled. "When this is finished—whatever it costs—you sit down at this table and you tell me everything about your brother. Not for the record. For me."

I found, rather to my alarm, that my throat had gone tight. "That's not a condition. That's just kindness."

"I'm told it's catching among people who keep lists of the dead."

* * *

We worked until dark. He would not bring the ledger up—not yet, not tonight, and I respected it, though my fingers fairly itched—but he brought out a plain notebook, a working copy of sorts, dates and units in his neat nurse's hand, and we began matching it against my own papers by lamplight. Sixty-three times I checked a figure against Thomas's last known position, and sixty-three times the arithmetic of grief came out exact.

At some point Mrs. Halloway's shawl disappeared from its peg and reappeared around my shoulders without comment, and at some point after that the door banged open and a large weathered man filled it, dripping, smelling of the Lamb and Flag, and stopped dead at the sight of us bent together over papers.

"Bram Tull," Kestrel said calmly, "this is Miss Ferris. She's a census taker."

Bram's eyes went from me to the papers to Kestrel's face, and whatever he read there drained the color out of his own. "A census taker," he repeated. "Counting what?"

"Names," I said. "Ones the government missed."

The big sergeant looked at Kestrel for a long, long moment. Then he pulled out the second chair—the one that was always his, the one I had stolen—and sat down heavily, rainwater pooling off his coat onto the flagstones.

"Well," he said. "Somebody'd best pour three cups, then. Because I don't think either of you is going to like what happens next."

Kestrel and I exchanged a glance over the lamplight—half dread, half something warmer that I refused, firmly, to name—and outside, the rain went on falling on a village that had no idea yet what winter was going to bring it.
