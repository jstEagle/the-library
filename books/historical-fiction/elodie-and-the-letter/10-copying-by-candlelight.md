## 10. Copying by Candlelight

I never learned my letters proper. That's the truth of it, and I'll own it plain: at fourteen I could sign my name if you didn't look too hard, and read a signpost if the words were short and the weather was good, and that was the whole of my education before the army took me and gave me a rifle instead of a slate.

So when Kestrel Coldharbour sat me down in the back room of the apothecary with a candle, an inkpot, and a stack of clean paper, and said, "Tam, I need your hand," I thought he'd gone soft in the head.

"Mine?" I said. "You've seen mine. Sergeant Tull says I write like a spider drowned in the inkwell."

"I have seen yours," Kestrel said, and he had that look on him, the one he got when he was deciding something and had already decided it but wanted you to think you were part of the deciding. "And that's rather the point. A fair hand would look like a clerk's work. A ledger copied by a clerk could be dismissed as a forgery made to order—anyone with a steady pen and no conscience could produce one. But a boy who writes badly, copying night after night, making the same mistakes his own way every time—" He tapped the paper. "That looks like what it is. The truth, worked for honestly."

"You want my bad writing as evidence."

"I want your bad writing as *proof*."

Well. Nobody had ever wanted anything of mine before, let alone my worst thing turned into my best one. I sat down. I picked up the pen. It felt like holding somebody else's finger.

"What do I write?"

Kestrel opened the floorboard and took out the ledger—that old leather book, fat as a well-fed rat—and laid it between us like a body on a table.

"You write what I tell you," he said. "Name first. Then regiment. Then the date they fell, or the date they were left, which is sometimes the same day and sometimes not. Then where. Then who saw it, if anyone did. And then—" he paused, and I watched him do the thing he does when the past gets its hands round his throat, which is swallow twice and carry on— "then whatever was known of them as men. Not soldiers. Men. What they carried, what they said, what they were owed. We begin with Aldous Prewitt, private, Eleventh Foot."

"Aldous," I said, writing it slow. "Ald-ous." The letters came out lopsided, the *d* leaning against the *o* like a drunk against a wall.

"He liked bees," Kestrel said quietly. "Kept talking about his hives at home. Left them in his brother's care. Write that."

"Bees," I wrote. My *b* came out backwards. I went over it again until it faced the right way, and Kestrel waited without hurrying me, the way nobody had ever waited for me before either.

That was the first night. There were four hundred and eleven names in that book, and we did eleven of them, and when the candle guttered out near midnight my hand ached and my eyes burned and I felt prouder than the day I got my first stripe, which tells you something sad about my life, but there it is.

* * *

We settled into it, the two of us, like a pair of old horses finding each other's pace. Nights when the shop was shut and Mrs. Halloway had gone home and Bram was off drinking himself toward forgetting—which he did most evenings, regular as church—we'd light the candle in the back room, shutter the window, and begin.

Kestrel would read from the ledger, low, almost singing, and I'd copy, and somewhere in the second hour he'd stop reading like a clerk and start reading like a man at a graveside, and that's when I learned things about him no dispatch ever recorded.

He knew everything about all of them. That's what I couldn't get over, night after night. Four hundred and eleven men and he could tell you Private Dunn was afraid of thunder and Private Oakes whittled little wooden horses for children he hadn't got yet, and Corporal Fenwick sang so badly the whole mess hall paid him to stop and he saved every penny of it for his mother's roof. Six years, and he'd kept them all alive in his head like a man keeping coals banked under ash.

"How d'you carry it?" I asked him once. "All that. In one head."

He was quiet a while. The candle spat.

"You don't carry it," he said finally. "It carries you. There's a difference, though I couldn't have told you that six years ago. Six years ago I thought I was holding them up." He looked at the ledger. "Now I know better."

I wrote *Corporal Fenwick, mother's roof,* and my eyes had gone blurry, and I blamed the candle smoke, and Kestrel pretended to believe me, which was kind of him.

It wasn't all graveside. You can't sit up nights with a man without laughing some, or you'd go mad, and Kestrel knew that better than anybody. He had a dry way about him that crept up on you. One night I miswrote *Haversham* as *Havershamme*, all grand, and he looked over my shoulder and said, "Congratulations. You've knighted him." Another time I sneezed so hard I blotted a whole entry and he said, deadpan, "Private Ludd died bravely and then exploded," and I laughed till I had to put the pen down, and he almost smiled, which for Kestrel was like a full parade salute.

Little by little, without either of us saying it, the room stopped feeling like a back room and started feeling like a kitchen. He corrected my grip on the pen the way a father would. I started bringing him bread from the baker before he asked. Once I called him "sir" out of habit and he flinched, actual flinch, and said, "Tam. I spent six years being addressed by rank by frightened boys in tents. Call me Kestrel or don't call me anything."

So I called him Kestrel. But inside my head, where a man keeps his private accounts, I called him something else, and I'm not ashamed of it now.

* * *

The lodger came in October.

His name was Mr. Pell, and he rented the front room upstairs from the widow who owned the corner house next door, and he was one of those men who are friendly the way nets are friendly—everything about them designed to catch. He was a clerk of some kind, or said he was, traveling on Commission business, and he had bright little eyes that went everywhere and rested nowhere except everywhere.

He took to dropping by the shop. Buying cough drops he didn't seem to need. Asking after trade, after the war—had I served? (I lied. Badly. It runs in our household.) Wasn't Mr. Coldharbour up late most nights? Thought I'd seen candlelight past midnight.

"Copies," I said. "Invoices. Thrilling stuff."

"Ah," said Mr. Pell, in a voice that filed my answer away in a drawer somewhere.

I told Kestrel that evening, standing in the dispensary grinding valerian root harder than the root deserved. "There's something wrong with him. He asks questions like Elodie does, except she asks because she wants to find things out and he asks because he wants to catch you out."

Kestrel didn't laugh it off, which frightened me more than anything. He stood very still, looking at the mortar in my hands.

"We move the copying," he said. "Nights only, door locked, and we finish fast. How many names remain?"

"Hundred and sixty-three. Maybe less, if I keep my letters small."

"Then we work every night. And Tam—" he caught my sleeve, gentle but firm, the field-nurse grip that meant *this matters* — "if anyone comes, the ledger goes in the flour bin and you're doing invoices. Say it back."

"The ledger goes in the flour bin and I'm doing invoices."

"Good lad."

We worked like men digging a tunnel. Every night, shuttered and locked, candle trimmed low, Kestrel dictating and me scratching away, page after page filling with my crooked, earnest, backwards-*b*-ed handwriting. Ninety names. Seventy. Fifty. Some nights my wrist seized up and Kestrel made me stop and rubbed it with liniment, grumbling that he was an apothecary and ought to know better than to let a boy ruin his hand, and the grumbling was love, and both of us knew it, and neither of us said it.

Thirty-one names left. That was the night it nearly ended.

* * *

It was past eleven. The wind was up, rattling the shutters, and I remember thinking the wind was a blessing because it covered the scratch of my pen. Kestrel was mid-dictation—*Sergeant Willem Corr, Fourth Dragoons, left at the ford, October the ninth, owed three months' pay and a letter to a sweetheart named Ada*—when we heard it.

Boots on the gravel outside. Then a knock at the front door. Then, worse, much worse: the rattle of the latch, and a voice through the wood, cheerful as Sunday.

"Mr. Coldharbour! Mr. Coldharbour, are you awake? It's Pell—terribly sorry—smell of smoke, you see, thought I saw smoke coming from your premises!"

Kestrel moved like water. The ledger was off the table and into his shirt before I'd even stood up, flat against his chest, and he was blowing out the candle and shoving the papers into the flour bin and hissing "*invoices*" all in the space of five heartbeats. I grabbed the nearest invoice book and a fresh candle and lit it just as the knocking came again, harder.

"One moment!" Kestrel called, unhurried as a Sunday sermon. He opened the door on his chain, blinking, wrapped in his coat like a man dragged from bed. "Mr. Pell. At this hour?"

"Smoke," said Pell, craning his neck. Bright little eyes going everywhere. Resting on the flour bin. Resting on me, sitting there with my invoice book and my guilty, guilty face. "Definitely smelled smoke. Fire risk, in these old buildings. Terrible thing, fire."

"Just my stove," Kestrel said smoothly. "I keep it in overnight for my rheumatism. Tam here is copying out some invoices for me—the boy's hand is quicker than mine these days."

Pell smiled. His eyes touched the flour bin again, the way a tongue touches a loose tooth.

"Invoices at midnight," he said pleasantly. "Diligent lad."

"Wars make strange hours of everyone," said Kestrel, "as I'm sure the Commission well knows."

Something flickered across Pell's face at that—the word *Commission* landing like a stone in still water. And I understood, sudden and cold, that this man wasn't a clerk traveling on business. This man was *for* somebody. For Ashgrave, maybe, or for whoever Ashgrave answered to. He hadn't smelled smoke. He'd been watching for candlelight, and he'd found it, and now he stood on the step memorizing everything.

"Well," said Pell at last, stepping back into the dark. "No harm done, then. Goodnight, Mr. Coldharbour. Goodnight, lad. Mind your candle."

"Always do," I said, and my voice only shook a little, which I counted a triumph.

Kestrel closed the door, dropped the chain, and stood with his forehead against the wood for a long moment. When he turned around, his face was gray in the candlelight.

"He knows," I said. "Or he guesses."

"He suspects. Which is worse, because suspicion can be reported and knowledge has to be proven." He took the ledger from his shirt and held it a moment, both hands, the way you hold something wounded. Then he set it on the table and opened it to the last written page.

"Thirty-one names," he said. "We finish tonight. All of them. Sleep is a luxury for people who aren't racing a spy."

* * *

We finished as the sky went gray. I copied the last name—Private Edmund Roke, Eleventh Foot, twenty years old, owed a violin to a cousin in Marleigh—with my hand cramping and my eyes full of grit, and when I wrote the final word Kestrel took the pages, all hundred-something of them, and squared their edges against the table, slow and careful, the way he'd bandage a wound.

Then he did something I didn't expect. He held out the fair copy to me.

"It should live apart from me," he said. "If they come for me, they come for the ledger under my floor. They won't think to search a deserter boy's chest under a widow's bed. And besides—" and here he hesitated, which he hardly ever did, "besides, it seems right. I began it. But it isn't mine. None of it was ever mine. It belongs to the names, and the names belong to whoever will carry them."

I took the pages. They were warm from sitting near the candle, or maybe from sitting near him, and heavier than paper has any right to be.

"I'll keep it safe," I said. "I'll keep it like—"

Like what? I didn't have a word. Kestrel, who always had the word, waited while I fumbled for it.

"Like it was mine to pass on," I finished. "When it's time."

He nodded once. And then, because the candle had burned to nothing and the sun was coming up over the yard and neither of us had slept, he put his hand on the back of my neck, brief and rough and gentle, the way fathers do who aren't practiced at it, and said, "Go to bed, Tam."

But sleep didn't come easy that morning, and it wasn't only the copying. Because when I got home, folding the fair copy into its oilcloth and sliding it under the loose board in my own floor—for I'd decided we were a house of hidden ledgers now, apparently, it was catching—I found Mrs. Halloway waiting up, white-faced, a note crushed in her fist.

"It's Bram," she said. "They've arrested him. Drunk and disorderly, they say." Her voice cracked clean down the middle. "Tam—he was shouting. Outside the Commission offices. Shouting about Vellmore. About *what he signed.*"

I stood there in the gray dawn light with thirty-one fresh names drying in oilcloth beneath my feet, and understood that whatever we thought we were racing, the race had just changed shape entirely.
