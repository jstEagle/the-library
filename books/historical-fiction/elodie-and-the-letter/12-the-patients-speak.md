## 12. The Patients Speak

* * *

Mrs. Halloway had buried three sons and one husband, and she had learned from all four funerals that grief makes people useless in company. They bring casseroles. They say "if there's anything I can do." They mean well and they vanish, because sorrow frightens them the way deep water frightens cats.

She had decided, somewhere between the second son and the third, that she would never be that kind of person again.

So on the morning after the notice of Kestrel's suspension was nailed to the apothecary's door—nailed, she noted with disgust, at an angle no carpenter would forgive—she put on her good black dress, the one she'd worn to Parliament's office when she'd first applied for her pension, and she marched down the lane with a written statement in her reticule and three jars of her preserved plums under each arm.

Tam was already there.

He was standing in front of the door with the crooked notice, arms crossed, glaring at it as though he could make it burst into flame through sheer adolescent fury. He was sixteen now, or seventeen—nobody had ever established it to anyone's satisfaction—and he'd grown enough that his coat sleeves were scandalously short.

"They can't do this," he said when he saw her. "They just *can't*."

"Of course they can," said Mrs. Halloway. "The question is whether they'll be allowed to enjoy it. Are you going to stand here scowling, or are you coming with me?"

"Where?"

"To the Commission's clerk office. I intend to file a statement." She held up the paper. "Four pages. My handwriting is legible, which is more than can be said for most official documents, so they've no grounds to complain."

Tam stared at her. "A statement saying what?"

"Saying that Kestrel Coldharbour pulled a splinter of shrapnel out of my son Daniel's shoulder with a fishhook and a spoonful of gin, and that Daniel lived eleven more months because of it, and that any man who calls such care falsification is welcome to say so to my face and see how his own face fares." She adjusted her bonnet. "It also says my pension papers were my own doing, which they were, and that Kestrel only helped me write them properly, which he did."

"You shouldn't—" Tam's voice cracked. "Mrs. Halloway, if you admit the forgery, they could take your pension. That's your bread."

"My sons bought me that pension with their lives, young man. The government merely holds it for me. And I will not sit in a warm kitchen while the man who kept Daniel alive gets called a liar by men who never once smelled a field hospital." She looked at him over the top of her spectacles. "Are you coming or not?"

Tam came.

By the time they reached the clerk's office in Millford, two miles down the coach road, they were not alone. Mrs. Halloway heard them before she saw them—the sound of boots and coughing and low village conversation—and when she turned, there were eleven people behind her.

Eleven. And growing.

There was Widow Prewitt, whose husband had come home from Vellmore with his lungs full of someone else's smoke, and whom Kestrel had kept breathing through two winters the doctors had written off in advance. There were the Fenwick brothers, conscripts both, who'd been fourteen and fifteen at the war's end and whom Kestrel had treated for frostbite without ever once asking why two boys that age had been carrying rifles. There was old Hobbs, who hadn't spoken more than six words in a row since the Campaign, walking steadily with his hand on young Fenwick's shoulder, holding a folded paper in his free hand that Mrs. Halloway suspected had taken him a week and a half to write.

Nobody had organized this. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing she would tell people years later when the whole affair became village legend: nobody had organized it. The suspension notice had gone up on Tuesday, and by Thursday morning the truth had walked into Millford on its own legs.

"We're here about Coldharbour," she told the clerk, a thin young man with ink-stained cuffs who looked up at the crowd the way a man looks at rising water.

"How many statements am I filing today, then?" he asked faintly.

"All of ours," said Mrs. Halloway. "And we'll wait while you copy the receipts, because I want proof in my own hand that you took them."

* * *

Bram Tull heard about it from the barmaid at the Green Man, who set down his pint harder than usual and said, "Half your village marched past my window this morning, Sergeant, and not one of them stopped for a drink. What have you done to them?"

"Nothing," Bram said. "That's rather the point."

He finished the pint without tasting it, which was becoming his habit even more than usual, and went out into the grey afternoon with his collar up.

He had been drinking to forget for six years, and it had worked about as well as bailing a boat with a fork. Every glass drowned a detail and left the important ones floating. He knew precisely which order he had signed at Vellmore. He knew the weight of the pen. He had spent six years trying to make the pen heavier than the names, and it would not be done.

Now the village—his quiet, gossipy, unremarkable village—was filing statements for Kestrel. For the ledger. For the truth Bram had spent six years paying in gin to avoid.

He found Kestrel at the apothecary, working by lamplight despite the hour, sorting dried comfrey with the careful hands of a man pretending the work required his whole attention. The license notice hung crooked on the door behind him; inside, everything was straight, ordered, familiar. Except Kestrel's face, which looked like a man's face does when he has been given something he doesn't believe he deserves.

"They came," Kestrel said, before Bram got the door fully open. "Bram. They keep *coming*. Widow Prewitt brought a statement written on the back of a flour sack. Hobbs wrote one. Hobbs hasn't written his own name in six years—he points at things—but he sat down and wrote a statement. Elodie says the clerk has filed thirty-one so far."

"Thirty-one," Bram repeated.

"There'll be more. There's talk of the Harrowfield nurses coming over. The ones I trained during the typhus winter." Kestrel set down the comfrey. His hands weren't quite steady. "I treated these people, Bram. Coughs. Cuts. A bad tooth or two. I didn't do anything *great*. Half of it was luck and the rest was goose fat. And they're marching nine miles in the rain to swear I'm honest."

Bram lowered himself onto the stool by the door. It creaked under him, as it always did, a small reliable complaint in an unreliable world.

"I know why it cuts you," he said. "You spent six years writing down men nobody saw. You thought you were one of them."

Kestrel didn't answer, which was an answer.

"But you weren't," Bram went on. "You were never invisible, Kess. We just hadn't found the words yet. Turns out the words are thirty-one statements and some plums." He paused. "Halloway gave you plums?"

"She left six jars. I don't know where I'll put them."

"You'll eat them, man. Slowly. It's what she'd want." Bram rubbed his jaw, feeling the stubble, feeling the years. "Listen. There's something I have to tell you. Something I should have—"

The door banged open and Tam came in with rain in his hair and triumph on his face, and whatever Bram had been about to say retreated back down into the cellar of himself, where it had lived so long it had furnished rooms.

"The Harrowfield nurses are coming!" Tam announced. "All four! And Mr. Quill the farrier says he'll testify about the time you sewed his thumb back on, except he wants it noted that he did most of the work by not fainting."

"He always did have a high opinion of himself," said Kestrel, and he laughed—a wet, surprised laugh—and Bram watched him laugh and thought: tomorrow. I'll tell him tomorrow.

He had been telling himself *tomorrow* for six years. But tonight, at least, he believed it.

* * *

Elodie Ferris had spent her professional life counting other people's households, and she had developed, over eight years of census work, a private taxonomy of villages. There were villages that were collections of strangers. There were villages that were collections of grievances. And very rarely—she could count them on one hand—there were villages that were, in fact, a single organism wearing many coats.

This one had woken up.

She stood at the window of Kestrel's back room—her temporary quarters, since the bail terms placed her in his custody, a phrase she privately treasured—watching a cart unload in the lane below. Not goods. People. An elderly woman being handed down carefully, a folded paper clutched to her chest like a hymnal. Two men in Harrowfield hospital aprons, travel-muddied. A boy, then another boy, then a whole knot of boys she recognized as the mill apprentices, who had been children during the war and had no business knowing anything about it, and yet here they were, carrying their own statements like lanterns.

"Another twelve," Kestrel said behind her. He was at the table, cataloguing statements into piles with the tenderness he usually reserved for medicines. "Elodie, the clerk sent word that he's had to requisition a second ledger book. A second book. For vouching statements. He says he's been a clerk nineteen years and has never filed anything but complaints and tax forms."

"And how are you sleeping?"

"Badly. But differently." He looked up at her, and there was something naked in his face, something he would have hidden a month ago. "I used to think the ledger was the only true record of me—that when they read it out, whoever read it, they'd find my name nowhere in it, and that was fitting. Record-keepers don't get recorded." He turned a page. "And now Widow Prewitt has written that I sang to her husband while he died, and she remembers the song, and she's included the words, and I—I'd forgotten I did that. I'd forgotten I had anything in me but counting."

Elodie came away from the window. Outside, the elderly woman had been helped to the door, and one of the mill boys was carrying her statement up the step for her, solemn as an acolyte.

"Do you know what I think?" Elodie said. "I think you thought love was a private thing. Something that happened quietly or not at all. And it's never been private. You were simply never in the room when they talked about you."

"In the room when who talked about me?"

"Everyone. For six years." She smiled. "Half these statements begin 'my mother always said' or 'the missus tells us.' You've been discussed over more suppers than any man in three counties, Kestrel Coldharbour. You just never bought a ticket."

He laughed, and then, quickly, pressed the heel of his hand against one eye, and Elodie had the grace to examine the ceiling until he'd recovered.

There was a knock. Then another—not a visitor's knock but a rapid, urgent drumming—and Tam burst in without waiting, soaked through, breathless.

"From Millford," he gasped, holding out a wet envelope. "Runner came up the coach road. Elodie—it's addressed to you. Official seal and everything."

Elodie took it. The seal was the Commission's. Her name in a clerk's careful hand. She broke it and read, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath the warmth.

*Kestrel,* she said, when she could. "Ashgrave has filed a motion. Tomorrow's session—he means to have the vouching statements ruled inadmissible. All of them. His counsel argues they constitute 'coordinated obstruction of an inquiry.'" Her voice stayed level; she'd had practice. "And there's a second schedule attached. Witness list. For the afternoon."

"Who?" Kestrel asked.

She looked at the page. One name. She read it aloud because she could not make herself say it any other way.

"Sergeant Bram Tull. Summoned by the Commission itself." She lowered the paper. "Kestrel—they're not calling him to vouch for you. The schedule lists him under *matters arising from the Vellmore rear guard.*"

The room went very still. Rain ticked at the window. Down in the lane, oblivious, the mill boys were helping the old woman's party toward the inn, their statements still dry under their coats.

Bram was not in the house. Bram, Tam said, had gone out an hour ago, toward the Green Man, and had not taken his usual stool, and had not ordered his usual pint, and had instead asked the barmaid for paper and ink.

"He said," Tam reported, frowning with the effort of exact recall, "that he'd be home late. That he was going to write something long overdue. And that—" the boy's frown deepened— "he said to tell you not to read any names tomorrow until he's had his turn."
