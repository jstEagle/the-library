## 13. Bram's Confession

Bram Tull had a system for drinking, honed over six years like a bayonet. You never drank before noon — that was for amateurs and men with no secrets. You never drank alone in public, because a man drinking alone in public was a man announcing something to the room. And you never, ever drank on the anniversary of Vellmore until after dark, because the daylight hours were for holding the door shut against what came at night.

He broke all three rules on the Thursday after Ashgrave's visit.

It was barely ten in the morning when he let himself into Kestrel's apothecary with the key he'd never been given but had used for years, and found his friend grinding valerian root with the particular care of a man pretending not to notice that another man was drunk at an hour decent folk reserved for bread.

"Morning," Bram said.

"It's morning," Kestrel agreed, not looking up. "Technically."

"I'll thank you not to be precise with me before noon. Precision is your department." Bram lowered himself onto the stool by the counter, and the stool creaked its familiar complaint, and for one merciful moment everything was ordinary: the smell of dried yarrow and camphor, the little brass scales, Kestrel's sleeves rolled up, the sun coming through the window in a dusty column. Ordinary. He could have stayed there inside the ordinary all day, if ordinary had been big enough to hold what he'd carried up the lane.

But it wasn't, and Kestrel knew it, because Kestrel had spent six years reading men the way other people read weather.

"You've been drinking since dawn," Kestrel said quietly.

"Since half five," Bram corrected. "Dawn would've been respectable." He looked at his hands. They were steady, which surprised him; he'd expected them to shake. "I need to tell you something, Kes. And I need you to put down the pestle first, because if you drop it you'll blame me, and I can't carry two things today."

Kestrel set the pestle down. Wiped his hands on his apron. Waited.

That was the thing about Kestrel Coldharbour that Bram had understood even in the war, back when Kestrel had been a nurse with blood to his elbows and a voice soft as snowfall: he waited. He gave you room. Most men filled silence like a burst pipe fills a cellar. Kestrel just left the door open and let you come through it yourself.

Bram came through it.

* * *

"Vellmore," Bram said. "The third night. When the line broke and Colonel Ashgrave ordered the rear guard to hold the ford so the main force could withdraw."

"I know the order," Kestrel said. His voice had gone very still.

"You know the order as it was given. I know it as it was written." Bram turned his glass on the counter, slowly, watching the last inch of spirits turn in circles. "Because I wrote it. Not the withdrawal order — that came from above, from Ashgrave himself, and God knows I don't pretend it wasn't his. But the second order. The one after. The one that doesn't appear in any dispatch anyone's ever shown me." His throat closed, and he took a breath around it, the way you'd breathe around a fist. "The wounded who couldn't walk. Two hundred of them, give or take, laid out in the mill yard and the church and the ditch along the ford road, waiting for stretchers that were never coming. Somebody had to sign the paper saying we were leaving them. Saying it was, and I quote myself, six years older and no wiser, 'a regrettable necessity dictated by operational circumstance.'"

The word *regrettable* sat between them like something dead on a table.

"Somebody had to sign it," Kestrel repeated.

"Captain Harrow was dead. Lieutenant Moss was dead. The adjutant's clerk had run. Which meant the surviving officer of the rear guard was—" Bram spread his hands, presented himself like a poor hand of cards. "Me. A sergeant field-commissioned four days earlier because everyone senior to me was under the mud of the ford road. Twenty-six years old, three days a captain, and a colonel telling me the army does not retreat, it redeploys, and would I please do my duty and make it lawful."

"And you signed."

"And I signed." He heard how it sounded, six years of hearing how it sounded every night when the lamp went out. "You want to know the worst part? It's not the signing. Any coward can sign a paper in the dark. The worst part is that I told myself it was mercy. That leaving them quick was kinder than leaving them slow, that the enemy would take prisoners, that — Christ, Kes, I told myself *stories*. Same as Ashgrave. Mine were smaller, that's all. Smaller stories, smaller lies. But I built them brick by brick same as he did, and then I came home and got decorated for the withdrawal and drank every building I ever built back down to the ground."

Kestrel hadn't moved. But Bram saw his knuckles whiten on the edge of the counter, saw the pulse working in his jaw, and understood — distantly, as if from across a river — that his friend was furious. Not the hot kind. The cold kind, the kind that keeps.

"The ledger," Kestrel said at last. "The names I keep. The ones from the mill yard and the church and the ditch. Those are—"

"Your list starts where my signature ends." Bram managed something that wanted to be a laugh and came out as gravel. "Forty-one years old and I finally understand why you never asked me about Vellmore. Six years, Kes. You knew. Didn't you? Maybe not the whole shape of it, but you knew I was rear guard, you knew I came out walking when others didn't, and you never once asked me a question about it. You just kept writing names."

"I kept writing names," Kestrel said. "That was always the work. Asking questions was yours to do or not do. It was never mine to force."

"Well." Bram looked at the ceiling beams, because looking at Kestrel was impossible just now. "It's done. The questions. Late, but done."

* * *

They sat in it for a while. Bram let him. You couldn't hurry a man through a thing like this any more than you could hurry a fever.

What Bram had not expected — what he genuinely, honestly had not prepared for, though perhaps he should have, because he'd known the man six years — was that Kestrel's first words after the silence would be these:

"Were they alive when you left?"

"Yes." No flinching from it. He owed the straight answer and he paid it. "Most of them. The surgeons' tent was still standing. Some were calling out. We could hear them behind us for the better part of a mile." He watched Kestrel absorb that, watched it land the way a blow lands, and did not look away, because looking away was the original sin and he would not compound it. "I've heard them every night since. You get so you can do things while you hear them. Drink. Sleep a bit. Laugh at Tam's jokes. It doesn't stop, you just — learn to carry it loud."

"That's why you drink," Kestrel said. "Not to forget."

"Don't be daft. Of course to forget."

"No." And now Kestrel looked at him, and his eyes weren't cold anymore, they were wet, which was somehow worse and better at once. "I've watched you drink for six years, Bram Tull. If you wanted to forget, you'd have managed it by now, or died trying. You drink to stay close to them. To keep the hearing of it. Because the moment you truly forget, they're gone twice." He stopped. Swallowed. "That's what the ledger is, too. I never thought of it that way until just now, saying it. But it's true of both of us, isn't it. You carried the guilt. I carried the names. Neither of us ever put our burden down because putting it down felt like abandoning them again."

Bram found, to his horror and relief in equal measure, that his eyes were burning. "You're not supposed to make a drunk cry before noon either. There are rules."

"I'm a apothecary's assistant. I break rules professionally."

And then, because six years of friendship does not dissolve over one confession — because grief shared is not grief halved, exactly, but it is grief *accompanied*, which is most of what any of us ever get — Kestrel came around the counter and put his hand on Bram's shoulder, and Bram let his head drop forward onto his friend's arm like a boy, and for a minute or two neither of them said anything at all, and the shop smelled of herbs and sunlight, and somewhere outside a cart went by, and the world continued, indifferently, which was its way.

* * *

When Bram had washed his face at the pump out back and drunk Kestrel's terrible chicory coffee — "it's coffee the way I'm a colonel," he told Kestrel, and Kestrel said, "you're not a colonel anymore, so it's actually quite good coffee," which was the closest thing to forgiveness either of them could manage out loud — he laid it all out.

Elodie's bargain. The contact inside the Commission. The conditions: corroboration, dates, units, witnesses, legal exposure. The clock ticking toward winter and the sanitized history and the century's turn, after which the last witnesses would scatter like leaves.

"They need corroborating testimony," Bram said. "Someone with rank. Someone whose name carries weight in that hall. Someone who can stand up and say, I was there, I held the ford, I signed the abandonment order, and here is what it covered, and here is who it erased."

"You," Kestrel said.

"Me." He laughed, and it came out steadier than anything had come out of him in years. "Do you see the joke of it? Six years I've begged God for a chance to un-sign that paper, and now the only way to do it is to sign my name to something else. Testify against myself. Stand in front of Ashgrave's counsel — and they'll have counsel, Kes, they'll have barristers with voices like razors — and confess to a war crime in open session. I'll lose whatever pension I've got. Likely face charges. Tam will hear the whole story of what I did, and Mrs. Halloway, and half the village that thinks Bram Tull is a harmless old soak who's fond of dogs." He turned the empty cup in his hands. "And I'll do it anyway. Gladly. But I can't do it alone, and I won't do it halfway, so here is my term, and it's the only one I've got."

"Say it."

"You read the ledger aloud. All of it. Every name, at the hearing, into the record, where no clerk can lose it and no historian can edit it." Bram leaned forward, and his voice dropped low and fierce, the voice he'd used at the ford, repurposed at last for something worth using it for. "Because understand me, Kes — my confession without your names is just one more story. A story with a villain, even, and villains make fine copy. But your names — four hundred and eleven of them, ages and units and next of kin — those aren't a story. Those are *people*. Read them aloud and they stop being omitted and start being counted, and once they're counted, no one can ever un-count them. That's what I'm buying with my neck. Not my own absolution. Theirs."

Kestrel was quiet for a long moment. Then he stood, crossed to the corner where the floorboard lived — Bram had guessed for years that something was under that board, and now knowing didn't feel like victory, it felt like grief arriving on schedule — and pried it up, and lifted out the ledger, and set it on the counter between them.

It looked so small. That was the thing nobody warned you. Six years of the dead, and it fit in two hands.

"If I read them," Kestrel said, "Ashgrave will come for you harder than he came for me. What he did to my license was a warning shot. What he'll do to a confessing officer—" 

"Bram Tull has been coming apart for six years, Kes. Let him come apart in public. It's more efficient." He reached out and touched the ledger's cover, gently, the way you'd touch a door you'd been afraid to knock on. "Besides. You think I'm doing this for courage?" He shook his head. "I'm doing it because Mrs. Halloway stood up in that hall last week and let them call her hysterical, and she's got three sons on somebody's list, and I signed the order that put two hundred men in the ground unnamed, and I have run out of every excuse I ever brewed. Courage's got nothing to do with it. This is just arithmetic, finally, done properly."

Kestrel smiled — a small, cracked, real smile. "Sergeants always were better at arithmetic than officers."

"We had to be. We were the ones counting rations when the officers were counting honors." Bram stood up. His legs felt strange — light, as if he'd set down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing. "There's one more thing. Fair warning, because you've earned it. When I testify, they'll ask me about the deserters at the ford. The ones who ran when the line broke. And among them, according to the rolls, is one Thomas Wick, private, aged nineteen, missing presumed deserted."

The color went out of Kestrel's face. "Tam."

"Tam. Whose name I can leave out, probably, if I'm careful and a coward in a new direction. Or whose name I can account for — sheltered, sick, shell-shocked, nursed back to health by a village apothecary's assistant — and thereby hang a target on this whole household." Bram met his eyes. "I don't know the right answer. I've been turning it over since dawn, since before dawn, and I don't know. So I'm asking you, and then I'm asking Elodie, and then I'm asking Tam himself, because it's his name and it should be his choice. But I wanted you to hear it from me first, in this kitchen, over your terrible coffee, rather than from a barrister in front of a hundred strangers."

For a moment Kestrel just looked at him. Then he said, softly, "Six years you've carried this alone. And your idea of a gift is to warn me before you spend yourself. You absolute mule."

"It's been remarked upon."

"*Thank* you, Bram." Kestrel's voice broke on it, just slightly, and he pressed his palm flat against the ledger as if taking its temperature. "Thank you."

* * *

They found Tam that afternoon down at Halloway's fence, mending posts, because Mrs. Halloway believed idle hands attracted sorrow and had assigned the boy work on the theory that labor was cheaper than laudanum. He straightened when he saw them coming together — Bram and Kestrel side by side, sober and grave — and Bram watched the fear arrive in the boy's face before either of them had said a word, because boys like Tam had learned to read the approach of bad news the way farmers read clouds.

"Sir," Tam said. He still called Bram sir sometimes, out of habit from a war that had chewed them both. "Is something wrong?"

"No, lad," Bram said, and found that it was almost true, which was the strangest discovery of the whole long day. "Something's finally right. But it's going to cost, and you've a stake in it, so pull up that bucket and sit down, because we've a story to tell you and a question to ask, and it begins with a paper I signed six years ago at a place called Vellmore."

Behind them, in the apothecary on the lane, the ledger lay on the counter in the afternoon light, waiting — four hundred and eleven names and, soon, one confession, all bound for the same public ear.

And in three days, the Commission would sit again.
