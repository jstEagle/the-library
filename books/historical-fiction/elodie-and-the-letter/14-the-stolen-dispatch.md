## 14. The Stolen Dispatch

There is a particular silence that lives in government archives, and Elodie Ferris had come to know it the way a shepherd knows the weather—not by studying it, but by standing in it until it told her everything.

The Commission on War Records kept its documents in the basement of the old customs house in Marlowe Green, three floors below the hearing room where men in good coats decided what had and hadn't happened at Vellmore. The basement was called the Record Annex on the official signage, though everyone who worked in the building called it the Cellar, with the affectionate contempt reserved for places where inconvenient things were stored. Elodie had been inside it eleven times since the hearings began. Ten of those times, she had left without taking so much as a pencil.

She intended tonight to be different, and she was frightened in a way she found almost interesting, the way one is interested in a fever—observing it, cataloguing it, wondering idly whether it would kill her.

*You are a census taker,* she reminded herself, climbing the steps from the lane with her document case under her arm and her heart behaving like a trapped bird. *You have spent years asking strangers intrusive questions and writing down their answers. Tonight you are simply asking the Commission a question it does not wish to answer.*

The night porter, Mr. Dunmore, was a widower of about sixty who dozed between his rounds and thought no one noticed. Elodie had noticed. She had also noticed, over weeks of cheerful greetings, that he missed his late wife, that his daughter lived in Corvale and wrote infrequently, and that he considered himself an excellent judge of character—which meant he liked being told his judgments were sound.

"Working late again, Miss Ferris," he said, straightening as she came through the side door, stamping cold off her boots. "They work you census folk to bone and gristle."

"They do," she agreed. "Mr. Pellow wants the household schedules collated before Thursday, and Mr. Pellow has never once in his life collated anything himself."

Dunmore laughed—a dry, satisfied laugh, the laugh of a man whose low opinion of Mr. Pellow had just been confirmed by an outsider. "Well, mind the third stair on the way down. It's got opinions."

"I always mind it. We're old adversaries, that stair."

She signed the visitor's book with her real name, because the first rule of stealing a document, as far as Elodie could determine, was that nothing drew attention faster than an empty space where a name ought to be. Then she went down into the Cellar carrying a lantern and a folder of genuinely dull census schedules, which served as both her alibi and, if she was lucky, her camouflage.

* * *

The Vellmore dispatches were filed under Northern Campaigns, 1888–1893, in the fourth bay, in boxes labeled by date. Elodie knew this because she had spent ten previous visits learning the filing system the way other people learned hymns—repetitively, devotedly, with her lips moving.

Here was the difficulty, and she turned it over in her mind for perhaps the hundredth time as she knelt on the cold flagstones: Kestrel's ledger proved that four hundred and eleven names were missing from the official record. Bram's confession proved how those men came to be abandoned. What nobody could prove—what Ashgrave's counsel had said three times now, smiling each time, *clerical error, your honor, the fog of paperwork*—was that the omissions were deliberate.

But Elodie had been a census taker for nine years. She read forms the way Kestrel read wounds. And when she had sat in the public gallery with Kestrel's copied pages open on her knee, comparing them against the dispatch summaries entered into evidence, she had seen something that had kept her awake half the night: the summary of the third-day dispatch listed casualties as *two hundred killed, wounded numerous*. But the original dispatch—the one referenced in the margin of the summary, cited as *source document, retained annex*—had never been produced. A clerical error loses a page. An edit keeps the page and quietly stops citing it.

The original was here. She had watched a clerk carry it down three weeks ago, wrapped in oilcloth, and shelve it in Bay Four, Box 118, marked *Annex Materials — Not for General Circulation.*

Not for general circulation. Well. She was not general. She was extremely specific.

Box 118 was heavier than it looked, or perhaps her hands had simply begun to tremble. Inside, beneath a layer of tissue paper gone grey with age, lay the dispatch itself—four pages, water-stained at one corner, written in a hand she recognized because she had seen it on every sanitized summary: Colonel E. Ashgrave, commanding.

Elodie spread the pages on the floor, laid her own notebook beside them, and began, line by line, to copy.

It took her over an hour. She did not trust herself to take the original—not yet; there was a version of tonight where taking it became necessary, but she wanted very much not to meet that version—and copying gave her something better than possession anyway. Because here, in the original, in Ashgrave's own upright, unhurried hand, were the names. Two hundred and six of the Vellmore dead, listed by regiment, by rank, by the cold arithmetic of a man closing accounts. And here, struck through with a single ruled line each, were the names of thirty-one wounded men marked *left of field, condition grave.*

Left of field. Condition grave. Written in ink that had dried while those men were still breathing.

And then, on the final page, in different ink—fresher, added later, the handwriting slightly hurried—the words that made Elodie close her eyes for a moment:

*Amend casualty figures per attached revision. Omit roster of abandoned personnel from record. Such entries serve no purpose and occasion distress to families.*

Omit roster of abandoned personnel from record. Not lost. Not misfiled. *Omit.* An instruction. A decision. Signed with initials at the bottom right corner: E.A.

"Oh," Elodie said softly, to the lantern, to the cellar, to four hundred and eleven people who could not hear her. "Oh, you *fool*, you actually wrote it down."

Gentlemen of Ashgrave's kind made a religion of paper trails only when the trails led somewhere pleasant. He had ordered the omission in writing because he had never imagined anyone would be in a position to see the order. History belonged to victors, after all. Archives belonged to victors. No one had counted on a census taker.

She finished copying the crucial page, checked it twice against the original, and was folding her notebook closed when she heard the door at the top of the stairs open.

* * *

Later, Elodie would reconstruct those next minutes with the terrible clarity of hindsight and find, each time, the same small moment where everything turned: the moment she chose to kneel back down beside the box instead of standing up.

Footsteps on the stairs. The third stair's opinion, audibly rendered. Lantern light swinging along the shelves.

"Miss Ferris?" It was not Dunmore. It was a younger voice, crisp and disapproving, and she knew it at once: Mr. Calloway, the Commission's assistant secretary, a man whose entire personality appeared to have been issued to him along with his desk. "The porter says you signed in at half seven. It is now past ten."

"I'm collating," Elodie said, still kneeling, still holding her notebook, and heard even as she said it how wrong it sounded. Collating took place upstairs. Collating did not involve kneeling in the dark beside an annex box with the lid off.

Calloway came around the bay, and his lantern found her, and his face performed the small, exquisite pleasure of a petty man encountering genuine trouble. "What is that box?"

"It's—" She stood. There was no lie that survived this geography. She was kneeling in a restricted bay at ten o'clock at night with an open restricted box and a notebook full of fresh copying. "Mr. Calloway, I am going to say something, and I'd be grateful if you'd hear all of it before you decide what you already believe."

"I don't think that will be necessary."

"You should hear it anyway. It concerns four hundred and eleven men whose names were deliberately struck from the official record, and the order doing the striking is in this box, in Colonel Ashgrave's hand, initialed and dated." She held his eyes. "I have copied it. The copy exists now, in more than one place. Taking me into custody changes nothing except how you feel about yourself afterward."

Something crossed Calloway's face—not conscience, exactly; she doubted it reached that deep—but calculation, and fear, and the particular misery of a man realizing a problem has arrived that cannot be filed. For one heartbeat she thought he might listen.

Then he said, "Close the box, Miss Ferris," and rang the bell pull by the stairs, and the moment folded shut like a ledger.

* * *

They questioned her for two hours in a room upstairs that smelled of lamp oil and cold tea. She refused to say where her copies were. She refused to say who had directed her to the dispatch. She refused, with a politeness she suspected would be described later as insolence, to express remorse.

At half past midnight, a tall man with grey side-whiskers and the Commission's chairman's authority arrived, looked at her for a long moment, and said, "Miss Ferris. You understand you are dismissed from His Majesty's census service effective immediately?"

"Yes, sir."

"And that this matter will be referred for prosecution?"

"So I've been given to understand."

He studied her the way she had seen magistrates study witnesses—with the faint disappointment of a man hoping to find a madwoman or a fool and finding instead someone entirely lucid. "Do you have anything to say for yourself?"

Elodie considered. Her hands ached from copying. Her career of nine years was lying behind her in pieces, and ahead of her, she could see with perfect clarity the shape of the rest of it: the warrant, the trial, the fine or worse, the word *thief* following her through every county that ever employed her. She thought of her brother Thomas, nineteen years old, left of field, condition grave, omitted. She thought of Kestrel reading names aloud by candlelight, his voice going rougher near the end, and Tam sleeping in the chair with the fair-copy pages across his knees like a cat.

"Yes, sir," she said. "I have this to say: the document in your basement proves that Colonel Ashgrave ordered the erasure of four hundred and eleven men, in writing, with his initials at the bottom. Your Commission was convened to correct clerical errors. That document proves there were no clerical errors. Only decisions. And I would rather stand in whatever court you choose and be called a thief than sit at home comfortable knowing the proof was locked in a cellar because it embarrassed a hero."

The room was quiet. The chairman's whiskers did not move.

"Take her statement," he said finally, to Calloway, wearily, as though she had added a burden to a cart already overloaded. "And fetch the warrant papers."

* * *

They released her just before two in the morning—released, technically, into her own recognizance, which meant they handed her hat back and let her walk out into a night so cold it felt personal. The warrant would be served at her lodgings in the morning. She had until dawn that was hers.

She walked to Coldharbour's shop without deciding to. Her feet knew the way the way old prayers are known.

There was a light in the back window. There was always a light in the back window; Kestrel slept like a soldier still, in shallow watches, and Tam slept like a boy, which is to say loudly and anywhere. Elodie knocked softly, twice, the pattern they used, and heard the bolt go back, and then the door opened and there was Kestrel in his coat over his nightshirt, hair flattened on one side, looking at her face and understanding it before she said a word.

"You have it," he said. "Or you had it."

"Copied. Not taken." She came in, and Tam emerged from the back room blinking, wrapping a blanket around his shoulders like a cloak, and the three of them ended up around the stove, and Elodie laid her notebook on the table and read the fatal page aloud—*omit roster of abandoned personnel from record*—and watched Kestrel close his eyes as if the words weighed something physical, as if he could feel each initialled stroke of the pen on the back of his neck.

"In his own hand," Kestrel said at last. "Initialed."

"Dated the ninth of October. Three days after Vellmore. Before any of the summaries were drawn up." She hesitated, then finished it: "It wasn't error, Kestrel. It was policy. It was never going to come out by accident. Someone had to go get it."

Tam had pulled the notebook toward him and was reading the struck-through names, his lips moving, and when he looked up his young face was fierce and wet-eyed at once. "They'll hang you? For copying it?"

"They'll try very hard to inconvenience me," Elodie said, and managed to make it almost light, and Tam almost smiled, and Kestrel did not smile at all.

"You knew," he said quietly. "When you offered the bargain. You knew what it might cost, and you didn't tell me the whole shape of it."

"The whole shape of it wasn't known to me either," she said. "I thought I might lose my post. I didn't plan the warrant. Though I suppose—" she looked at her hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle, "—I suppose I planned everything up to the edge of it and then walked off the edge without much deliberation. That's rather my method."

Kestrel was silent for a long time. The stove ticked. Outside, the frost was forming its own careful records on the glass.

"There's a thing I haven't said," he said finally, "because saying it felt like presumption. But I find I can't watch you carry this alone and keep quiet, so: whatever comes of tomorrow—whatever they take from you—you should know that you did not steal a document, Elodie Ferris. You stole back four hundred and eleven people. And I have kept a ledger for six years, and I am telling you as the district's leading authority on accounting"—and here his mouth bent, barely, into the ghost of humor that was all he ever allowed it—"that the debt runs entirely the other way."

Elodie found, to her horror and amusement both, that her eyes were stinging. "That," she said, "is the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me, and it was about bookkeeping."

"It's the only register in which I'm fluent."

Tam snorted. The stove ticked. And then came the knock at the door—heavy, official, three raps, far too early for anything innocent—and Kestrel rose with his candle, and Elodie sat very still, listening to the bolt draw back, to the murmur of voices, to the words *warrant* and *this morning* and *required to accompany us*, arriving hours ahead of schedule because of course they were; mercy had never once in her experience kept office hours.

She stood, and buttoned her coat, and took one last look at the warm kitchen, at Tam gripping the table edge, at Kestrel already reaching for his boots.

"Well," she said lightly, to neither of them and both of them. "I suppose I'd best not keep the Commission waiting. They do hate to be kept waiting by the help."

The constable at the door cleared his throat. Somewhere down the dark lane, the first grey light was coming up over Marlowe Green like a page being turned.
