## 15. Night Before the Commission

The snow came at dusk, soft and businesslike, as if it had been hired for the occasion and intended to do a thorough job of it.

Kestrel stood at the apothecary's window with a cup of tea going cold in his hand and watched it settle on the lane, on the fence posts, on the roof of Mrs. Halloway's cottage across the green, where a light already burned in the kitchen window because she had been up since four and saw no reason to change her habits for history. Tomorrow the Commission's carriage would come—or rather, tomorrow Kestrel would walk six miles through whatever weather was offered to the county hall at Marlow St. Anne's, because he had decided that if he was to be dismantled by barristers, he would at least arrive on his own feet.

Behind him, the shop smelled of liquorice root and camphor and lamp oil, and beneath those familiar smells, another smell entirely: fresh ink. Four hundred and eleven names, copied twice over in Tam's careful, slightly crooked hand. The original ledger lay under the floorboard where it had lived for six years, but tonight it sat on the table between the tinctures and the mortar, open to its first page, wearing a ribbon Elodie had tied around it that morning with the grave ceremony of a woman dressing a child for church.

"You'll wear a groove in the floor," Elodie said from the doorway. She had been bailed into his custody for three days now, which meant legally she could not travel beyond the parish boundary without his written permission—a state of affairs they had both found so absurd that Tam had drawn up formal papers authorising her to go to the privy, and Kestrel had signed them.

"I'm not pacing," Kestrel said. "I'm standing."

"You're standing in eight different places."

He looked down. He had, in fact, worn a faint crescent into the frost of the window sill. He set the tea down. "Where is everyone?"

"Bram's walking over from the Hart and Halter. He said—" She hesitated, and he watched her decide, deliberately, to say the true thing instead of the kind one. "He said he wanted to arrive sober enough to remember it, so he started this morning."

"And Tam?"

"Copying the last page again. He says his G looks like a goose and he won't have a goose in the permanent record of His Majesty's Commission." Elodie came to stand beside him at the window. In the glass their reflections overlapped—her dark head, his grey one, two people who had met over a census form and a lie and somehow arrived here. "Kestrel. Are you afraid?"

It was the sort of question only she asked him. Bram never asked; Bram simply poured. Mrs. Halloway answered it before you could ask it of yourself. But Elodie asked, and waited, because she understood—better than anyone he had ever known—that being seen was not the same thing as being caught.

"Yes," he said. "Not of the counsel. Not even of Ashgrave, though God knows I should be." He turned the ledger ribbon absently under one finger. "Tomorrow they will hear the names as evidence. Evidence gets cross-examined. Evidence can be thrown out. And I keep thinking—these men were thrown out once already. By their own army. I can't bear for it to happen twice."

"So don't let tonight be evidence."

He looked at her.

"Read them to us," she said. "Before the lawyers get hold of them. Read every name aloud, here, now, so that the first time we hear them, they're just—people. Private William somebody, who had a laugh like a cart on gravel. Not exhibit A."

"The copy has no such notes. You know that."

"I know what you have," Elodie said. "You've told me things about those men over these weeks that you've never written down. Which means you carry them. So carry them out loud, once, while we can still listen as friends instead of a commission."

* * *

They gathered by lamplight in the back room, the room where Kestrel did his real work—the mending of soldiers' nightmares and deserters' fevers and widows' hearts, though none of it appeared on any invoice. Bram Tull sat in the good chair with his great hands flat on his knees, shaved, combed, wearing the shirt he presumably kept for funerals and court appearances, and looking like a man who had argued with a bottle all day and lost narrowly. Mrs. Halloway occupied the settle with her knitting abandoned in her lap—abandoned, which from her was the surest sign of solemnity; she had been known to knit through thunderstorms and once through the announcement of a royal death. Tam perched on the stool by the fire with the fair copy stacked on the table before him, guarding it like a shepherd who had personally raised every sheep.

And Elodie sat near the door, bail papers in her lap folded into a neat square, as if even now she felt she ought to be taking a note of the household's occupants.

Kestrel stood at the table. He had rehearsed nothing. Six years of practice had taught him that some things should not be rehearsed.

"Tam copied every name faithfully," he began, "goose and all. Tomorrow, God willing, they go into the record. But Elodie is right about one thing, and I confess I hadn't thought of it until she said it. If the first time you hear a man's name is in a courtroom, then the first thing he is to you is an accusation. And none of these men were accusations. They were—" His voice caught on something small and hard. He cleared his throat. "They were my patients. Most of them. For however long they had."

Nobody spoke. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, the snow went on about its business.

So Kestrel opened the original ledger, and began.

"Corporal Aldous Renwick. Seventh Fusiliers. He played the fiddle badly and loved it completely, and the men let him because his bad playing made worse silence unbearable. He carried three men off the field at Harrow Ford before Vellmore, and at Vellmore he stayed with the wounded when the order came to withdraw, because Corporals are not required to understand orders, he told me, only to obey them, and he preferred understanding to obedience." He paused. "He gave me his watch for his mother in Duncastle. I sent it. She wrote back that it didn't run anymore, and neither did she much, and thank you."

"He'd have liked you," Bram said quietly. It was not sentiment. From Bram it was a military assessment, delivered like intelligence.

Kestrel nodded and went on.

"Private Samuel Ockham. He was nineteen and lied about it so fluently that I half believed him myself. He was frightened of horses, which in a cavalry column is a considerable handicap, and so the other men took turns walking between him and the mounts as though escorting a dignitary through hostile country. He called me 'sister' for the entire campaign despite my explaining otherwise weekly. He said nurses and sisters were the same thing if you squinted."

Mrs. Halloway laughed—one short, startled sound—and pressed her hand over her mouth. "That's exactly what our Thomas would've said. Exactly that."

"Then Thomas is in good company," Kestrel said, and read on.

He read for two hours.

Some names he could give only bare—men who had died too fast or too far from him for anything but their unit and their wound—but most he could give whole, because a nurse's ledger of omissions is also, unavoidably, a nurse's memory, and memory keeps what paper does not. He gave them their jokes and their superstitions, their debts and their sweethearts, the songs they murdered and the letters they couldn't finish. Private Ellery Vance, who wrote home every week describing meals he had actually eaten, so his mother wouldn't worry, and described them magnificently. Sergeant-Major Dunmore, who had survived four campaigns and died of a decision made at a map table forty miles away, and whose last words were an apology to nobody in particular for the state of his boots.

Bram listened with his eyes closed through most of it. Twice his hands moved on his knees, shaping something—words, perhaps, or an order, or the shape of the rear guard road at Vellmore, which he alone in this room had walked out of. When Kestrel reached the names of the men left at the mill—the thirty-one whom the withdrawal had abandoned, whose omission from the dispatches was not clerical error but architecture—Bram opened his eyes and mouthed each name along with Kestrel, silently, precisely, like a man reciting a catechism he had written himself in blood. He had confessed it to them all a fortnight ago. He would testify to it tomorrow. Tonight he simply kept the names company, as if to say: *you were not forgotten by everyone.*

When Kestrel reached the Halloway sons, Mrs. Halloway stood up.

She did not say anything. She simply stood, the way one stands when the anthem plays, and Kestrel understood, and read them slowly.

"Private Josiah Halloway. He was steady the way a wall is steady—you lean on it and forget it's doing anything. He used to mend the younger boys' kit without being asked, and he never once mentioned it, and I only knew because the stitching wasn't mine." Mrs. Halloway's chin rose. "Private Edmund Halloway. He was quick and sharp-tongued and generous underneath it, like a chestnut—prickly outside, and worth the trouble of getting at. He bet me a shilling he'd see Paris after the war. I never paid out and I never will; as far as I'm concerned he won." Her hand went to her mouth. "Private Wilfred Halloway. He was the youngest and the quietest and the bravest, which is a combination I saw too often and never got used to. He held my hand during the worst of the shelling at the ridge and apologised afterwards for squeezing it."

"He squeezed yours too?" Mrs. Halloway whispered.

"He squeezed every hand he held," Kestrel said. "It was how he said things."

The old woman sat down slowly, both hands folded now, and she was smiling, which no one in that room would have believed possible an hour before, least of all her.

* * *

Near midnight, the candle burned low and Tam fell asleep sitting upright, his head tipping against the mantel and jerking awake, three times, until Elodie eased the fair copy out from under his elbow and Mrs. Halloway produced a blanket from somewhere, as grandmothers can, from the very air.

"There's one more name," Kestrel said softly, closing the ledger. "Not in it. Never was."

Bram looked up sharply.

"A boy named Tam Weller, of no regiment whatsoever," Kestrel said, "who deserted an army that had starved him and lied about his age besides, and who crawled into my herb garden half-dead two winters ago and stole nothing but a chicken, which frankly we could spare. There are men in that book who broke every rule there was and stayed at their posts. And there is one boy in my kitchen who broke the one rule that mattered and is alive, and I have never once been able to decide which of those facts is the scandal." He looked round at them. "If tomorrow goes badly—if they take the ledger apart, if they call me a forger and a fraud—I want it said plainly, in front of witnesses, tonight, in this room: every name in that book is true. Every date. Every wound. I have checked them against my own eyes, and my own eyes are the only instrument I trust more than a thermometer. Let them think what they like about the man who kept it. The book itself is honest. That is all I have to offer, and it is everything."

Silence. Then Bram hauled himself out of the good chair, crossed the room, and did something he had never done in six years of acquaintance: he put his arms around Kestrel and hugged him, briefly, ferociously, the way soldiers hug—at a distance measured in inches and a pressure measured in grief.

"For the record," Bram said gruffly into his shoulder, "you smell of iodine."

"Noted for the transcript."

Mrs. Halloway kissed his cheek. Tam, woken by the commutation of his own name, mumbled "m'not a scandal" and slept on. And Elodie stood by the door with her hand on the latch, looking at Kestrel with an expression he was too tired and too full to interpret, except that it made the room feel warmer than the fire had.

"They'll hear them properly tomorrow," she said. "Because tonight they were heard properly first. That matters, Kestrel. Words know how they were introduced."

"It's late," he managed. "You should sleep. Big day. You're legally confined to this parish, after all."

"Under escort," she agreed gravely. "Terribly dangerous prisoner." She paused at the stairs. "Kestrel. Whose name comes last in the ledger?"

He almost said *the last man to die.* But he knew the true answer, and she was asking for the true answer.

"Yours isn't in it," he said. "But if it were, it would be last. Keepers go at the end. Someone has to close the book."

She smiled at that—slowly, as though trying it on—and went up.

* * *

He was alone with the ledger for perhaps ten minutes. Long enough to wrap it in oilcloth. Long enough to bank the fire. Long enough to look at the first page one final time and say, soundlessly, to the four hundred and eleven: *tomorrow, I promise.*

He was blowing out the last lamp when the knock came.

Three raps. Measured. Courteous. Unhurried—the knock of a man who knows perfectly well that everyone in the house is awake, and knows further that no one will mistake him for anyone else.

Kestrel stood in the dark with the ledger under his arm and felt the temperature of the whole night change. Across the room, Bram was already on his feet, and Bram's face, in the ember-light, had gone to Vellmore and come back.

"That'll be the Colonel's man," Bram said quietly. "Or the Colonel."

At the window, through the falling snow, a carriage stood in the lane with its lamps lit, patient as a verdict.
