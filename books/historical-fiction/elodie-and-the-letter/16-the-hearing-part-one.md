## 16. The Hearing, Part One

They gave the witnesses a room behind the hearing hall that smelled of cold tea and beeswax, as though someone had decided that if the furniture could not be comfortable, it might at least be shiny.

Kestrel sat on a bench meant for three and occupied it as though he were alone in the world, which was his habit and his armor. Elodie sat close enough that their shoulders touched when either of them breathed deeply, which was often. Tam paced. Mrs. Halloway did not pace; she sat with her hands folded like a woman waiting for a sermon she had already read twice, and Bram—Bram was nowhere, because Bram had insisted on arriving separately so that no one could say the nurse and the sergeant had conspired on the walk from the inn.

"Conspired," Kestrel said to the shiny table. "As though we hadn't been conspiring for months. As though Elodie hasn't got a list of everyone who's ever lent us a candle."

"Consolidated testimony," Elodie corrected gently. "That's what they'll call it if we do it right."

"And what they'll call collusion if we do it wrong," said Mrs. Halloway, "so drink your tea, all of you, and stop rehearsing your faces at each other."

Kestrel looked down at his hands. Six years of apothecary work had softened them back toward a civilian's hands, mostly—the burn scar from the Vellmore tents still ridged across the left palm, the needle-scars permanent as punctuation—but this morning they were shaking, and he resented them for it. He had dressed wounds under artillery fire. He had held a boy of nineteen while the boy explained, politely and at length, that his mother would want to know he hadn't been afraid, and then stopped explaining. Surely a room full of men in good coats asking questions could not be worse than that.

It could, actually, he thought. Because those boys had needed him. These men only wanted to unmake him.

A clerk put his head round the door. "Coldharbour. They're ready."

Tam gripped his hand hard enough to hurt, which helped. Mrs. Halloway said, "Tell them about Daniel first," which helped more, because it reminded him what the morning was actually for. Elodie said nothing at all. She simply looked at him the way she had looked at him over her census forms two years ago, when she had already known everything and was only waiting for him to catch up—and then she pressed the ledger into his hands, wrapped in its oilcloth, and he understood that whatever happened in that hall, it would happen to a man holding four hundred and eleven names, and no man holding four hundred and eleven names could be entirely alone.

* * *

The hall was larger than he expected and colder, built of gray stone by people who believed that justice should feel like February. The Commission sat on a dais behind a long bench: five commissioners, four of them soldiers, one of them a bishop who looked as though he had been assured there would be lunch. At a side table, with papers squared and pens aligned, sat Ashgrave's counsel—a Mr. Pell, whose voice Kestrel already knew from the preliminary sessions, a voice like a file drawn slowly across soft iron.

And at the counsel's elbow, upright, silver-haired, decorated, courteous even in stillness: Colonel Edmund Ashgrave.

Kestrel had seen him once before, across his own shop counter, being kind. It had been the kindest frightening thing he'd ever witnessed until now, because now Ashgrave turned his head, found Kestrel in the body of the hall, and inclined himself perhaps half an inch—the acknowledgment one gentleman gives another before the cards are dealt. Then he folded his hands and watched, and did not look away for an hour.

The chairman, Sir Aldous Wren, read the charge into the record in a voice sanded flat by years of reading charges: that Kestrel Coldharbour, formerly nursing orderly, Army Medical Corps, attached Third Division, had produced a document purporting to be a roll of the dead of the Vellmore Campaign containing names absent from the official record, and that the said document was alleged to be a forgery, calculated to bring discredit upon His Majesty's forces.

"Alleged," Sir Aldous repeated, and looked over his spectacles at Pell. "Mr. Pell, you may examine the witness."

* * *

Pell began, as such men always begin, pleasantly.

"Mr. Coldharbour—you were a nursing orderly. Not a commissioned officer. Not a muster clerk. Not, in fact, entrusted with any records whatsoever beyond your own ward book. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"Your duties consisted of changing dressings, administering laudanum, and carrying stretchers."

"Among other things. Yes."

"Among other things." Pell smiled at the hall, sharing the phrase. "You left the service without honors. You have since worked as an assistant to an apothecary in a village of two hundred souls. You hold no degree. You have published nothing. And yet you propose to this Commission that you—you personally—are in possession of information regarding the dead of Vellmore that escaped the attention of divisional staff, regimental adjutants, and the War Office itself. How do you account for that?"

Kestrel heard his own counsel—Elodie had found them a barrister named Mr. Quennell, kind-eyed and expensive, paid for by Mrs. Halloway's third son's pension money—begin to rise, and Kestrel answered before Quennell could arrange anything cleverer than the truth.

"I was there. They weren't."

A stir, quickly smoothed. Pell let it settle, unhurried. He was a man who liked his bread to rise slowly.

"You were there," Pell agreed. "At Vellmore. Tell me, Mr. Coldharbour—where exactly were you stationed during the action of the fourteenth of September?"

Kestrel hesitated. It was only a breath of hesitation, but Pell caught it the way a pike catches minnows.

"The rear dressing station," Kestrel said. "Behind the mill."

"Behind the mill." Pell consulted a paper. "Because the official siting report places the third field hospital a mile east, at the crossroads. The mill road was under intermittent shellfire throughout the afternoon of the fourteenth. No medical unit was recorded there."

"We moved," Kestrel said. "We were ordered back and we didn't all go. There were wounded who couldn't be carried that far in the dark, and some of us stayed with them."

"Some of us stayed." Pell wrote something down with great care, as though taking dictation from the truth itself. "Ordered back. So you disobeyed a direct order."

"I obeyed the order I thought I'd been given."

"Ah," said Pell softly, and the hall laughed, briefly, uneasily. "We come at last to it. The order you thought you'd been given. Mr. Coldharbour, is it not true that your memory of the entire campaign has been shown, by your own admission, to be unreliable? That in your deposition of October last you stated you served with the Second Battalion, Harrowgate Regiment, when in fact your discharge papers name the Fourth?"

Kestrel felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

"My deposition was taken at night," he said. "By candlelight. After eleven hours in the wards. Battalions blur. Boys blur. I have never once been confused about whether a man died or lived, sir. Only about which colonel owned him."

"That will do," said Sir Aldous, but mildly, and Pell bowed as though reprimanded and continued exactly as before.

Piece by piece, then. Kestrel understood what he was watching—he had watched fevers dismantle strong men the same way, organ by organ, politely. Pell produced the ward book Kestrel had kept and demonstrated that its arithmetic disagreed with itself in three places. Pell produced a fellow orderly, a nervous man named Petch, who testified that Coldharbour "wrote things all the time, sir, private-like, nobody knew what," and who could not remember his own regimental number. Pell produced the census returns and observed, to general appreciation, that a man who could not correctly state his own household to a census taker—"and here I quote the return, which gives one lodger, whereas I understand the household presently numbers four"—could scarcely expect to be believed about the disposition of an army.

"He counted us wrong on purpose," Tam called from the gallery, and was hushed.

"And finally," Pell said, turning a page with ceremony, "the matter of the names themselves. Two hundred and six of the names in your document, Mr. Coldharbour, appear in no muster roll of any regiment engaged at Vellmore. Not as killed. Not as missing. Not anywhere. Whom did these men belong to, Mr. Coldharbour? Which army did they serve in? Yours appears to have been a very creative war."

Kestrel stood in the witness box and felt the thing Pell had been building all morning finish itself around him: a small clean room with no windows, made entirely out of his own discredited life. No degree. No rank. Bad arithmetic. A wrong census. A blurred memory. Every true thing he knew, sealed behind a wall of every false or imperfect thing about him.

Quennell was on his feet, objecting to something procedural. Sir Aldous was consulting the bishop. In the gallery, Mrs. Halloway's spine was straight as a lance and Tam's fists were white, and somewhere behind him Elodie was watching him with an expression he could feel through the back of his coat.

Ashgrave caught his eye again. Half an inch of a nod. *There, now,* the nod said. *You've given a good account. Go home.*

And Kestrel Coldharbour, who had spent six years being careful, who had hidden a book beneath a floorboard and a self beneath an apron, who had lied badly to a census taker because lying was the one skill war had never taught him—understood, with a clarity that arrived the way pain arrives after frostbite, that defense was the wrong weapon entirely.

He could not win an argument. He had never been able to. But he had carried water for four hundred and eleven men, and water does not argue. Water is simply carried, and poured, and drunk.

"Mr. Chairman," he said. Quennell stopped mid-objection. "May I be heard on my own behalf? Not examined. Heard."

Sir Aldous considered him a long moment. "Briefly."

"No, sir," said Kestrel. "Not briefly. That's rather the point."

He unwrapped the oilcloth. He opened the ledger on the ledge of the box. The binding creaked—it always creaked; Tam had mended it twice with thread from Mrs. Halloway's sewing basket—and the sound went through the silent hall like a shot goes through a valley.

"These are the names of the men who are not in anyone's report," Kestrel said. "I cannot prove where most of them were born. I cannot prove their regiments, because their regiments were edited out of existence on the fourteenth of September and again in the dispatch printed the following spring. Mr. Pell says they belong to no army. He is nearly right. They belonged to no one—that was the finding, though no one wrote it down. They belonged to no one, and so no one has spoken for them, and so I will. I am going to read all four hundred and eleven names into this Commission's record. It will take some hours. I have checked the arithmetic myself, and it disagrees with itself in three places, and I will correct those places aloud as I go, because that is what honest books do."

Pell was on his feet. "Mr. Chairman, this is irregular—"

"So is abandoning four hundred men behind a burning mill," Kestrel said, without heat, and began to read.

"Able Seaman—no. Private Ableman, Josiah. Second Harrowgate, drafted to the Third Division in August. Age nineteen. Left-handed. Died of his wounds on the fifteenth, between two and three in the morning, with his hand in mine."

His voice was not remarkable. It was a village voice, an apothecary's voice, used to being gentle across a counter. But it carried, because he made it carry, the way he had made it carry over shellfire: not loud, but placed.

"Private Ackerman, Peter. Age twenty-three. Died of his wounds on the fifteenth, between two and three in the morning, with his hand in mine."

"Private Addington, Silas. Seventeen. Same night. Same hour. I did not know his Christian name until I copied it from his kit tag. I know it now."

The hall had stopped moving. The bishop had forgotten about lunch. Somewhere in the gallery a woman had begun, very quietly, to weep, and no one asked her to stop, because half the hall wanted to join her and decorum alone held the rest.

Kestrel read on.

He read through the hour before noon and past it. His throat dried and a clerk, unprompted, brought him water, and he drank and continued. Pell sat down—not defeated, Kestrel noted; a man like Pell did not know defeat, only delay—but sat down nonetheless, because there is no cross-examination for a man reciting a litany. You can discredit a witness. You cannot discredit a list. The list simply is, the way rain simply is, falling on everyone alike.

He read the names of the men of the rear guard, abandoned on the orders that Bram Tull had signed. He read the names of the men who died at the crossroads hospital while the dispatch writers two miles back composed their clean paragraphs. He read the name of a boy listed only as *Thomas, called Rabbit*, because that was the only name anyone had ever used for him, and Kestrel said, "I include him as he was known among us. If the Commission prefers anonymity, it may strike him. I would ask it not to."

He read until his voice wore thin and even, and then thinner. Three hours. Four. The light through the tall windows shifted from white to gold, and still he read, and the hall endured it with him, because endurance was the whole of the argument and everyone in the room had finally grasped it: this was what the omissions had cost. Not ink. Hours. Years. A man standing in a cold hall reading names into a dying afternoon because no one else would.

Mrs. Halloway's sons came at last, in the middle of the fourth hour: Daniel, James, and Wilfred Halloway, each in turn, each received by the record in the chairman's flat, careful script. When Kestrel reached them he paused, and found Mrs. Halloway in the gallery, and she nodded once, and he read her boys as clearly as he had ever said anything in his life.

Near five o'clock, hoarse almost past speech, he read the four hundred and eleventh name—Private Yorke, Matthew, age thirty-one, survived by a wife who had waited nine years for a word and gotten none—and closed the ledger.

The silence afterward had weight. It pressed on the benches, the dais, the bishop.

Sir Aldous Wren removed his spectacles, polished them unnecessarily, and replaced them. "The record," he said quietly, "will show that the witness read continuously from fourteen hundred hours." He looked down the bench at his colleagues, and then—at the end of it, where the man had sat unmoving all day—at Colonel Ashgrave, who met the chairman's eye without blinking and slowly, deliberately, pinned a decoration to his lapel that he had not been wearing that morning, as though armor could be put on over an open coat.

"Colonel Ashgrave," Sir Aldous said, "you will wish to respond, no doubt. In due course."

"In due course," Ashgrave agreed, pleasant as a drawn blind.

But it was not Ashgrave who rose next.

From the back of the hall, from the seats kept for late-comers and strangers, Sergeant Bram Tull got to his feet—a big man moving carefully, like a man who has set down a weight he has carried so long he has forgotten his arms—and said, in a voice that cracked only once:

"Chairman. Before the Colonel answers—I've something to confess."

Kestrel's hand found the ledger's worn cover, and held on.
