## 17. The Hearing, Part Two

*Bram*

Bram Tull had rehearsed this moment for thirty years, though he had always rehearsed it as a way of never having to live it.

He stood in the witness box of the Commission hall with his hat crushed in his fists like something he was trying to keep from escaping. The room was smaller than the newspapers had promised and colder, all stone and dark wood, with the commissioners seated on a dais behind a rail that looked borrowed from a church—perhaps deliberately, Bram thought, so the men testifying would feel they were confessing rather than speaking.

Kestrel sat in the front row beside Elodie Ferris, whose bail papers were folded in her coat pocket like a talisman. Mrs. Halloway was on Kestrel's other side, spine straight as a pew candle. Tam hovered at the aisle end of the row, too nervous to sit down, which meant he'd been standing since seven that morning and would stand until they carried him out.

Bram found Kestrel's eyes. Kestrel did not nod. He simply looked at him the way he used to look at men on the tables at Vellmore—the look that said *I see what's broken, and I'm not going anywhere.*

That helped. It helped more than the oath he'd just sworn.

"Master Tull." The counsel for the War Office, a dry stick of a man named Pellweather who had spent the morning dismantling Kestrel's character one splinter at a time, consulted his papers. "You served at the Vellmore Campaign. You hold the rank of sergeant, retired. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"And you are here to speak to the conduct of the rear guard action on the fourteenth of October."

Here it was. The door he had been standing outside of for three decades, hand on the latch.

"I am," Bram said, "but not the way you'd like me to."

Pellweather's pen stopped. One of the commissioners leaned forward.

"The gentleman you've been asking about," Bram went on, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect, "Colonel Ashgrave, did not order the abandonment of the wounded at the Vellmore ford. I know that because I'm the one who signed the order. He drafted it. He handed it to me because an order needs an officer's signature or a sergeant's, and there wasn't another officer alive within two miles. He looked at me and said, *Tull, someone has to be able to write this down later,* and then he mounted his horse and rode east and let me carry it. That's the sort of courage colonels have. The kind that delegates."

The hall had gone very quiet. Somewhere near the back a woman made a small sound and was hushed.

"There were sixty-one men at that ford," Bram said. "Wounded. Most of them couldn't walk. The enemy column was four hours out—I know because I counted the guns. Four hours. We could have moved forty of them if we'd used the gun limbers, but the limbers were needed for the officers' baggage, and I signed the paper that said so. Sixty-one names. Your clerk has thirty-eight of them listed as *died of wounds sustained prior to withdrawal.* That's a very tidy phrase. It means we left them on the ground and the weather finished what we started."

"You understand," Pellweather said slowly, "that you are confessing to a capital offense before a Royal Commission."

"I've been confessing to it every night for thirty years," Bram said. "I just hadn't been doing it into a transcript. Seems a waste of a good transcript otherwise."

Someone in the gallery laughed—one startled bark—and then the laugh caught, spread, and became something else, something that sounded almost like relief, and the chairman rapped his gavel without much conviction.

Bram turned, deliberately, and faced the front row.

"Kestrel Coldharbour kept a ledger," he said. "Four hundred and eleven names of men your official reports misplaced. I didn't know about it until last month. When he showed it to me, I looked for the ford first. All sixty-one were there. Every man. With dates, units, wounds noted in a nurse's neat little hand. He wrote down the men I abandoned so that somebody someday might count them, and he never once—not once in six years—came to me and asked me to explain myself. He knew I'd have to do that on my own." Bram's voice cracked finally, and he let it. "Well. Here I am, explaining myself. Put it in the record. All of it. My name too."

The chairman's gavel came down properly this time, though nobody could have said why.

* * *

*Mrs. Halloway*

They called her after the midday recess, and she was glad of the recess because it gave her time to re-pin her hat, and re-pinning one's hat is a form of prayer when done properly.

She had buried three sons and attended the funerals of none of them, which is the particular cruelty of a war death: the ground is always somewhere else. Edmund at Vellmore. Josiah at the river crossing, name misspelled on the dispatch as *Holloway*, an error she had written to the War Office about eleven times and received four different explanations, none of them compatible. And Peter—Peter had no grave at all, no date, no dispatch line, nothing but the memory of a boy waving from a train window with his cap off, his hair ridiculous in the wind.

So when the reading of names had begun yesterday—Kestrel standing in that box hour upon hour, voice going to gravel, refusing water, refusing rest, reading four hundred and eleven names while counsel sputtered and the hall fell silent by degrees like a tide going out—Mrs. Halloway had sat with her hands folded and listened for her boys.

Edmund was the ninety-seventh name. She had known it instantly, the way a mother knows a cough through a wall.

Now they put her in the box, and Pellweather, softened perhaps by Bram's confession or merely pacing himself, asked gently enough: "Madam, you wish to speak to specific entries?"

"Three," she said. "I wish to speak to three entries."

She took the printed copy Elodie had prepared for her—it seemed wrong, somehow, to handle the original, like touching a relic with unwashed hands—and read aloud.

"Ninety-seven. Coldharbour, E., private, second battalion, wounded at the ford, recorded in Colonel Ashgrave's dispatch as *returned to duty.*" She looked up. "My Edmund never returned anywhere. I have his last letter in my pocket, dated two days before. I have brought it. You may read it. His handwriting slants left and he apologizes for it every paragraph, which was like him; he apologized for existing, that boy, sweetest of my three."

She turned the page.

"One hundred and twelve. Halloway, J.—written *Holloway* in the official record, a mistake of the clerk, not of Mr. Coldharbour, who spells like an angel. Died at the river crossing. The dispatch lists him under a different mother entirely—a Mrs. Holloway of Marsh Bend, who does not exist, gentlemen, I have checked, I walked that village end to end two summers ago asking after her, and I promise you the only thing I found was a very confused duck."

Somebody on the commission bench coughed in a way that suspiciously resembled a suppressed laugh, and Mrs. Halloway fixed them with a look that had quelled three sons and half a county's worth of their friends.

"And entry two hundred and six," she said, and here her voice did waver, and she let it, because some things should. "Halloway, P. No unit given. No date of death. Only a note, in Mr. Coldharbour's hand: *witnessed by a conscript of the third company; the boy was seventeen and carrying a message he did not understand.*"

She looked directly at Ashgrave, who sat at the counsel table with the stillness of a portrait.

"That is my Peter. Seventeen. Carrying a message he did not understand. You sent him, Colonel, because a grown man would have understood the message and refused. I have waited nine years for someone to write that sentence where the world could hear it, and I am not going to apologize for how it sounds, because I assure you it sounds exactly like the truth."

She folded her pages. Her hands shook, and she let them do that too.

"Mr. Coldharbour never met my sons," she said to the commissioners. "He has simply carried them around in a ledger for six years, along with four hundred and eight strangers' sons, because somebody had to. I call that family. I hope this Commission knows family when it sees it."

Then she stepped down, walked past Kestrel, and squeezed his shoulder once—the exact squeeze she'd used on platform goodbyes, the ones that meant *come home or don't come home broken*—and sat down.

* * *

*Tam*

Tam had practiced his testimony forty times against the apothecary's back door, which was the most patient audience he'd ever had. In the actual hall, with the actual gas lamps and the actual commissioners, his mind emptied itself entirely, and the first question nearly finished him.

"State your name for the record."

"T-Tam. Tam Oakden."

"Your regiment, Mr. Oakden?"

And there it was—the trap he'd walked himself into a hundred times at night, staring at the ceiling. Say the regiment and be branded. Say nothing and fail Kestrel, who had fed him soup through the worst winter of his life and never once asked what he'd run from.

But Kestrel had told him, the night before, over cold mutton: *You don't have to say why you left. Only what you saw. What you saw belongs to the dead now, not to you.*

"I deserted from the third company," Tam said, and his voice held, mostly. "In the second year. I'm saying it plain because hiding it helps nobody, and because what I ran from is part of what you're here to hear about."

Nobody moved to arrest him. A commissioner actually nodded, in the manner of a man ticking a box marked *candor.*

"You were present at the retreat actions of the autumn campaign?"

"Yes, sir. Before I—" He stopped, steadied. "I saw the supply wagons turned back twice with wounded men already loaded in them. Turned back to make room. I saw the tally clerks burning papers at Harrow Field—whole sacks—and when a lieutenant asked why, he was told the figures were being *reconciled.* I saw a captain strike a hospital orderly for asking after a patient's name, because the patient was to go in the report as *unidentified* and knowing his name complicated things."

"Complicated things," the chairman repeated. "In what sense?"

"In the sense that dead men with names have mothers," Tam said, "and mothers write letters."

The silence afterward was the loudest thing Tam had ever heard. He gripped the rail of the box and kept going, faster now, words tumbling like water finding its level—the things he had locked in his chest for years because there was nowhere safe to set them down.

"I knew one of them," he said. "A man named Wick. Corporal Wick, from entry—" he fumbled for the folded scrap in his pocket, the one Kestrel had copied out for him in fair hand, "—entry three hundred and forty. He used to share his tobacco with me and tell me his girl could outrun any horse in her father's stable. He died at Harrow Field on a stretcher by the burned papers. Mr. Coldharbour's ledger says *died of fever improperly treated; supplies redirected.* That's true. But it leaves out the tobacco, and the horse story, and how he called everyone *sunshine* including the colonel, especially the colonel." Tam's eyes were stinging and he didn't care. "The ledger keeps the names. But it can't keep everything. That's why we're all here. To be the rest of it."

He stepped down on legs made of rope and discovered the entire front row grinning at him through tears—Kestrel, Elodie, Mrs. Halloway, and old Sergeant Merrick from two rows back who'd once called him a coward to his face and now mouthed *well said, sunshine.*

Tam sat down and shook for ten straight minutes and had never in his life felt prouder.

* * *

*Ashgrave*

Colonel Sir Edmund Ashgrave, K.C.B., had endured the day the way a cliff endures weather: correctly.

He listened to Tull's confession with a slight tilt of the head, as though appraising a mediocre horse. He listened to the Halloway woman with his hands folded. At young Oakden's testimony his jaw tightened once—at *especially the colonel*, of all things—but he recovered. He had spent thirty years recovering. It was his principal military skill.

When the chairman finally invited him to respond, he rose, buttoned his coat, and addressed the bench with the unhurried courtesy of a man being handed keys rather than accusations.

"Mr. Chairman. I have listened today to a deserter, a drunkard, a grieving woman, and a nurse of no rank who keeps private books like a moneylender. I do not intend to answer sentiment with sentiment. I will say only this."

He drew himself up, and the decorations on his breast caught the lamplight—campaign medal, star, cross, each earned in mud and smoke, whatever else might be said of him.

"I served this Crown for thirty-four years. I was decorated by two sovereigns. I made decisions at Vellmore that haunt no one's sleep but mine, and I made them because the alternative was losing the army, and the war, and very likely the century. History is written by those who win it, Mr. Chairman—that is not cynicism, it is observation—and the men in that ledger were the price of winning. I do not apologize for arithmetic."

He turned, at last, toward the front row, and his eyes found Kestrel Coldharbour with the flat patience of a hawk regarding a hedge.

"But I will note one thing for the record. Every accusation laid at my table today rests entirely upon a document of no provenance, kept in secret for six years by a man with a grudge and a forged pension in the next seat. Strip away the theater, gentlemen, and what remains is an unverified book. And an unverified book—" he smiled, thinly, courteously, "—is only gossip with good handwriting."

He sat. Pellweather gathered his papers with visible satisfaction. And in the front row, Elodie Ferris opened her leather case, withdrew a folder bearing the Commission's own archive seal—broken, recently, and unmistakably—and passed it up to the clerk.

"Mr. Chairman," she said pleasantly, "then I suppose it's fortunate we also have the original dispatch. With the edits still showing."

Ashgrave did not move. But Bram, watching him across the cold hall, saw the cliff do a thing cliffs are not supposed to do.

It flinched.
