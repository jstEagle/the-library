## 19. Sentences and Settlements

The Commission sat for the last time that winter on a Tuesday so cold the ink froze in the wells, and the clerk had to warm his pen over a candle before he could write down what became of us.

I remember thinking it was fitting. Justice, like medicine, is mostly a matter of keeping things from congealing long enough to be recorded.

We had waited eleven days since the hearing closed—eleven days in which the village conducted itself as though nothing had happened, which is the village way, and in which nothing at all had been decided about anything, which is the Commission's way. Eleven days of Mrs. Halloway bringing me stews I did not need and Tam finding reasons to sweep the shop floor that did not require sweeping. Elodie was with me still, her bail binding her to my custody, an arrangement that amused her enormously.

"They could have put me anywhere," she said on the ninth day, watching me grind valerian root. "Anywhere in the county. And they chose the house of the man they spent three weeks trying to destroy."

"Perhaps they hoped proximity would finish the job."

"Perhaps," she said, "they simply have no imagination. It's the great flaw of bureaucracies. They assume everyone wants what the forms offer."

On the tenth day, Bram's sentence came down.

* * *

They gave him two years, reduced to eighteen months for his confession, to be served at the military prison at Harrowgate—which was, everyone agreed, either mercy or irony, depending on how you felt about the fact that Bram Tull would spend his confinement within sight of the parade ground where he had once drilled recruits who now lay under the grass at Vellmore.

Bram received the news standing, as he received everything, with his jaw set and his hands loose at his sides. But I had bandaged those hands. I knew where the old break was in the left wrist, and I saw him press that wrist against his coat seam—the thing he did when he was holding himself together by main strength.

"Eighteen months," he said afterward, in the corridor outside the courtroom, in that voice he used when the drink wanted to come up and he wouldn't let it. "For signing away forty men. Ashgrave gets a knighthood rumor and I get Harrowgate."

"You also get," said Mrs. Halloway, drawing herself up to her full unremarkable height, which has always been considerable in effect if not in measurement, "the distinction of being the only man in this whole rotten business who confessed to his own face in a mirror before anyone made him."

Bram looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something I had never seen him do in all the years I'd known him. He bowed. Not a soldier's nod—a real bow, deep and deliberate, the kind you give to someone whose opinion of you matters more than your own.

"Mrs. Halloway," he said, "you are the most terrifying woman in three counties, and I include the Commission."

"She's mine," Tam whispered to me out of the side of his mouth. "I found her first."

The humor held us up, that day, the way a stretcher holds a man who cannot walk. We laughed too easily and too loudly in the corridor, and the bailiffs pretended not to hear, and I understood for the first time that laughter is not the opposite of grief but its packhorse—it carries what we cannot yet hold in our arms.

Before they took him back, Bram gripped my shoulder. His breath was clean. Sixty-one days sober by his own count, and he had counted every one aloud at breakfast like a man reporting for duty.

"Read them the names again sometime," he said quietly. "Not for the record. Just somewhere. Out loud, in the open air. A man likes to think they'll keep being said."

"I will."

"And Kestrel—" He stopped. The old Bram would have swallowed it and gone for the bottle. This Bram made himself finish. "Thank you for not hating me. You had cause. I'd have hated me."

"I did hate you," I told him. "Briefly. It didn't take. You're too stubborn a man to hate properly; the feeling keeps sliding off."

He laughed then, one short bark that startled a passing clerk, and they led him away down the corridor, upright as a flagpole, and I watched until the door closed, and then Mrs. Halloway put her hand through my arm and steered me toward the light like a woman steering a cart with a wobbly wheel.

* * *

My own accounting came three days later, delivered not in a courtroom but by post—a document of four pages in the gray ink of official forgiveness.

The charges of falsifying medical records were dropped. Fully, without reservation, with an apology so carefully worded it could have been dictated by a lawyer defending against future lawsuits, which of course it had been. The inquiry into Colonel Ashgrave's conduct was to continue in closed session. The ledger, all four hundred and eleven names, had been entered into the permanent record verbatim, exactly as the chairman had ordered, and copies were being prepared.

And then the fourth page. The one that began: *With respect to the reinstatement of your apothecary license...*

It will not surprise anyone who has read this far to learn that the license was not restored. It was, in fact, "suspended pending further review," a phrase I have come to understand means *forever, but politely*. The reasoning given was delicate: that while no falsification had occurred, the publicity surrounding the case had "compromised public confidence in the practice," and that confidence, having been compromised by other people's lies about me, was somehow my responsibility to rebuild, presumably from the grave.

Elodie read the letter over my shoulder. She was quiet for a moment. Then she set down her tea.

"They've hanged you with a ribbon," she said.

"With a very courteous ribbon. There's probably a clause about the courtesy somewhere."

"Kestrel." She turned me around to face her, and her eyes had that flinty brightness I had learned meant someone, somewhere, was about to regret something. "You spent six years keeping their dead for them. You read four hundred and eleven names into the transcript of the realm. And they're telling you that you may no longer mix a poultice."

"I may still advise," I said. "There's a clause."

"There's always a clause." She sat down across from me and pressed her fingers to the bridge of her nose, and I saw the exhaustion in her properly for the first time—her trial looming, her position gone, her name in the papers next to words like *improper removal of documents*, and still she was angry on my behalf before she was ever frightened for herself. I have thought about that often since. It is, I believe, the whole of what love is made of, or near enough.

"It's not the worst outcome," I said, because someone had to say it. "The names are in the record. That was the point. I never much cared what happened to the rest of me."

"That," said Elodie, "is precisely the kind of sentence that worries me about you."

But here is the thing I did not expect, the thing nobody at the Commission expected either: the village did not care about the fourth page.

Old Marta Grell came in the next morning with her grandson's fever, same as she had for years, and when I explained—apologetically—that I could fill the bottle but technically could not write the label, she took the bottle, wrote the label herself in a hand like a cart accident, and said, "There. Now we're both criminals. How does it feel?"

"It feels," I said, "like being in excellent company."

By week's end there were seven such labels in various hands around the shop, and Tam had appointed himself Registrar of Illicit Prescriptions, keeping a list in his careful copied-out script, and when I pointed out the irony of making a ledger of crimes he said, "You taught me that everything true should be written down. This is all true. Doctor Hensley charges double and once gave Mr. Prewitt syrup of ipecac for a cough, and everybody knows it."

Dr. Hensley himself came by on the Saturday. He stood in the doorway a long moment, working up to whatever he'd come to say, a big uncomfortable man with hay fever he refused to admit to.

"They tell me you can't practice," he said.

"Suspended pending review."

"Mm." He blew his nose thunderously. "I'm sixty-three, Coldharbour, and my knees have opinions. I find I'm in want of an assistant with full responsibilities and no paperwork. The position pays badly and comes with a horse that bites. Interested?"

I confess I had to look out the window for a moment at the winter light on the frost-bitten garden before I could answer.

"The horse," I managed at last, "sounds like the difficult part."

"The horse," said Dr. Hensley, "is the easy part. It's only honest about biting, which is more than I can say for the Commission." He stuck out his hand, and we shook on it, and that was that. The law had taken my license. The village simply declined to notice, which is a form of justice the law has never learned to administer.

* * *

Elodie's trial was set for the second week of March, at the assizes in Corvenhall, twenty miles east, and everything about it was smaller than the Commission had been—smaller courtroom, smaller stakes on paper, smaller attention from the newspapers, which had found new scandals to feed on. But smallness, I have learned, is not the same as harmlessness. A narrow river drowns as surely as a wide one.

The charge was theft of official documents. The dispatch itself—the original Vellmore order with Ashgrave's deletions visible in the margins, the edits that proved the omissions were chosen rather than clerical—had been entered into evidence at the hearing and thereby preserved forever. Her sacrifice had done precisely what she'd intended. But the law does not credit intentions accomplished; it credits permissions granted, and she had none.

"They could transport me," she said one evening, matter-of-factly, sorting her census forms by firelight the way other people sort photographs. "Probably won't. Fine, likely. Maybe a few months. The embarrassment of it all—the Commission would rather I vanished quietly than became a martyr with a subscription fund."

"You talk about it like weather."

"Because it is weather, Kestrel. It happens to me; I don't direct it." She looked up. "What I direct is this," and she touched the fair copy of the ledger, the one in Tam's careful hand, which she kept in her satchel now the way soldiers keep letters from home. "Four hundred and eleven names in the permanent record. My brother among them. Whatever they do to me in March, that's already true, and it will still be true when everyone in this room is dead. Do you know what that's like? To carry something that can't be taken back? I carried nothing but absence for six years. Now I carry proof. They can fine me all they like."

I wanted to say something equal to that, and I had nothing, so instead I said the true thing, which is usually what's left when eloquence fails.

"I'll be there," I said. "Every day of it. Front row."

"I know." And then, quieter, with the smallest smile: "You realize they'll call you as a character witness. You, a man whose credibility was dismantled in open court by a barrister with a voice like a closing door."

"I'll behave."

"No, you won't," said Elodie fondly. "But you'll mean well, and juries forgive meaning well. It's the one mercy of ordinary people: they always prefer the guilty who are sincere to the innocent who are polished."

* * *

The night before she and I were to travel to Corvenhall for the preliminary hearings, we gathered in the apothecary's cellar one last time—not for strategy now; there was no strategy left, only the ordinary business of people who have survived something trying to learn how to live beside each other in the aftermath.

Mrs. Halloway brought a seed cake she claimed was mediocre and which was, as always, transcendent. Tam brought the illicit prescription ledger to show off his penmanship. And we talked, as we had not managed to talk in all the months of crisis, about small things—about whether spring would come early, about whether the Halloway cow was feigning lameness for sympathy, about Tam's plan to apprentice himself to the surveyor's office in the autumn, because, as he said, "somebody ought to map things right the first time, and I'm told I have a feel for boundaries."

"A feel for boundaries," repeated Mrs. Halloway. "Child, you crossed half the borders in the county before you were nineteen."

"Differently," said Tam with dignity. "Now I'd do it legally. Mostly."

It was late when Elodie stood to go up, and she paused at the foot of the stairs, her hand on the wall, and looked around the cellar—at the table scarred by six years of quiet suppers, at the shelf where the original ledger had rested through everything, empty now, its contents living in the public record where nothing could quietly bury them again.

"When this is over," she said, "whatever 'over' turns out to mean—I've been thinking about what comes after trials. After sentences. Everyone talks about the verdict and nobody talks about Wednesday after."

"We'll talk about Wednesday after," I said.

"Yes." She smiled, and there was something in it I couldn't yet read—something shy and settled at once, a door opening onto a lit room. "I think we will. There's something I want to ask you, actually. When it's over. Not tonight. Tonight there's cake."

She went up the stairs, and Tam immediately began speculating in a whisper about what she meant to ask, and Mrs. Halloway told him to mind his own boundary, and the cellar filled with the ordinary music of people who love each other clumsily and well.

But I stood a moment longer by the empty shelf, doing the accounting the chapter of my life demanded. Two years, less time served, for Bram, who had earned every hour of it and paid in a currency harder than money. No license for me, and a horse that bites, and patients who labeled their own medicine. A trial for Elodie, and whatever Wednesday after might hold. Four hundred and eleven names safe in the record of the realm, and not one of them brought back by any of it.

Partial. Imperfect. Real. I had expected justice, if it ever came, to feel like a door closing on a finished thing. Instead it felt like a ledger balanced only to the extent that ledgers ever are—some columns settled, some carried forward, some entries that could only wait for an auditor with more authority than any court in the land.

I banked the fire and climbed the stairs, and outside the window the last of the winter stars stood over the village, over the graves and the gardens and the one lit window where Elodie was still awake, and I thought: March first. Corvenhall. Twenty miles.

Whatever she means to ask me, I thought, I already know my answer.
