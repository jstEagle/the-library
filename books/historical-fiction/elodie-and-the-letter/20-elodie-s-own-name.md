## 20. Elodie's Own Name

They gave me a hearing the way some towns give a funeral—promptly, respectfully, and hoping nobody makes a scene.

The summons had come on a Tuesday, delivered by a constable so young his cap sat on him like a pot lid. *Elodie Ferris, census clerk, to answer charges of unauthorized removal of documents from His Majesty's Commission archive.* I signed for it with the same hand that had signed for the dispatch, and I remember thinking how strange it was that a person could commit a crime in one signature and be formally accused of it in another, and both times the pen felt exactly the same in my fingers.

I had three weeks to wait. Three weeks of living in Kestrel's spare room under the terms of my bail—which is to say, in his custody, a phrase that made Bram laugh until he had to sit down.

"Custody," he wheezed, wiping his eyes. "Of Kestrel Coldharbour. The man can't keep custody of a packet of biscuits overnight."

"That was Tam," Kestrel said, without looking up from the ledger.

"It was me," Tam said cheerfully, from the doorway. "And it was one biscuit. Two."

I loved those evenings, though I never said so. After everything—the warrant, the dismissal, the way the Commission clerk had taken my badge off my coat as if removing a stain—I had expected to feel adrift. Instead I found myself folded into a household that ran on kettle-boiling and quiet argument. Mrs. Halloway claimed the good chair by right of seniority and grief. Tam read the newspaper aloud each evening, skipping the parts about the Commission until Mrs. Halloway told him firmly that she'd been publicly called hysterical by a barrister and could certainly survive a newspaper column. And Kestrel sat at the table with his ledger before him like a man keeping watch over a sleeping child, and every night he looked at me across it as if checking, without asking, whether I was all right.

I was not all right. But I was better than all right, which is a different thing entirely, and harder to say out loud.

* * *

The morning of the hearing, Mrs. Halloway came into my room with an iron and no discussion.

"I've pressed your collar," she said. "It was a disgrace. If they're going to ruin you, they'll do it while you look respectable."

"Thank you, Mrs. Halloway."

"Don't thank me. It made me angry, that collar." She set the iron down and looked at me with her small, fierce eyes—the eyes of a woman who had buried three sons and had run out of patience for anything less than honesty. "You're frightened."

"Yes."

"Good," she said. "Kestrel was frightened too, and look what happened. Half the county knows those names now." She smoothed my shoulder where she'd pressed it. "Frightened people do the counting. Brave ones just get counted."

She left before I could work out whether that was comfort or prophecy.

Kestrel walked me to the courthouse himself. He wore his good coat, the one with the mended cuff, and he offered his arm at the gate as if this were a market day and we were two people with nothing heavier between us than opinions about the weather.

"You don't have to testify," I said. "You've done enough. You stood up there and read four hundred and eleven names until the hall went silent—you've paid whatever they asked of you and more."

"Yes," he said. "But you didn't ask them to take the dispatch either, and you did it anyway."

"That was different."

"How?"

I opened my mouth and found nothing there. He watched me with that patient, unhurried attention of his—the attention that had undone me in my own parlor six months ago, when I'd asked one question too many about Vellmore and watched a lie die on a kind man's face.

"They'll call witnesses," he said at last, sparing me. "People who want to speak for you. I only intend to be first in the queue."

"There won't be a queue. There'll be a formal submission process."

"Then I'll stand near the front of the formal submission process," said Kestrel Coldharbour, apothecary's assistant, keeper of the dead, "in a queue-like manner."

* * *

The hearing room was smaller than the commission hall, and colder, and the commissioners sat behind their long bench looking like men who had hoped this would all go away by Christmas. There were three of them: a grey solicitor, a clergyman who kept touching his collar, and Chairman Wexford himself—who had, as the whole district now knew, carried a rifle at Vellmore, and who had ordered the ledger entered verbatim into the permanent record with a face that gave away nothing and cost him, I suspected, a great deal.

Wexford saw Kestrel come in and something moved behind his eyes. Recognition, or arithmetic. Perhaps both.

"The charge," the grey solicitor began, in the tone of a man reading someone else's shopping list, "is unauthorized removal of a document from the Commission's custody, namely the original field dispatch dated the seventeenth of October, being document forty-one in the Vellmore series. Miss Ferris, do you admit the removal?"

"I do," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Entirely. I took it. I knew it was wrong to take it, and I did it anyway, because the document proved that the omissions in the official record were deliberate edits rather than clerical error, and because it was going to be destroyed in the ordinary course of archiving within the month. I have the destruction schedule. It's exhibit C."

"We have exhibit C," the solicitor said, with a flicker of distaste, as though the paperwork itself had inconvenienced him personally.

"Then you know I'm telling the truth about the destruction."

"Miss Ferris," the clergyman said gently, "you must understand the position you put us in. A Commission exists to restore confidence in the record. Its own clerk removing documents—" he spread his hands— "well. It is difficult to explain from a pulpit, let alone a bench."

"With respect," I said, "it was easier to explain than leaving the dispatch in the fire."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. The solicitor wrote something down. Wexford did not.

They might have ended it there. I think they wanted to—a reprimand, a fine, everyone home by luncheon. But then the doors opened at the back of the room, and the sound came in ahead of the people: boots, and coats, and the particular shuffling hush of a crowd that has agreed beforehand to be polite about it.

Bram Tull came down the aisle first, clean-shaven and buttoned into a jacket I suspected Mrs. Halloway had assaulted him with the iron over as well. Behind him came Tam, holding a folder against his chest with both hands like a boy carrying eggs. Then Mrs. Halloway. Then, filling the back benches and standing along the walls because there were not enough benches, a congregation of faces I knew from the past months—nurses from the Vellmore tents, conscripts whose wounds Kestrel had dressed, widows whose names I had traced through three counties of parish registers, patients whose prescriptions had been quietly filled on credit during the suspension of his license. They had not been summoned. Nobody had organized them, or if somebody had, it had been done the way things got done in that village: over kettles, without minutes.

The solicitor looked at the crowd the way a man looks at weather coming in off the sea.

"Are these—all of these persons—witnesses?" he asked.

"No," said Bram Tull pleasantly. "Most of us are just here to listen. But we've all got statements, if you're short."

* * *

They called Kestrel first. Of course they did; he had been first in his queue-like manner since half seven.

He took the stand and put his hands flat on the rail, and I watched him find the stillness I had first seen in him in my parlor—that quality of a man who has decided exactly how much of himself he is willing to lose today and is therefore unshakeable.

"I've known Elodie Ferris since she knocked on my door with a census form," he said. "She asked questions I lied about, because I was afraid. She noticed the lies. That's her whole character in a sentence, gentlemen—she notices things other people have decided to stop seeing. She noticed four hundred and eleven names missing from the record of Vellmore. She noticed them before I was brave enough to say them aloud. Everything I did at that commission, I did after she showed me her own list of the omitted dead—her brother among them—and asked me nothing except whether I knew them too." He paused. "She stole a document. I won't pretend otherwise, and neither will she. But I was a battlefield nurse for three years, and I'll tell you what I know about triage: sometimes the rule gets broken so the thing that matters survives. She broke a rule. The record survived. Weigh it as you like, but weigh both sides of it."

The solicitor asked whether Kestrel understood that he was endorsing lawlessness.

"I understand," Kestrel said, "that the law was used at Vellmore to make dead men disappear, and that it took a crime to bring them back. I'd endorse her again tomorrow, and I'd hand her the key to the archive myself."

"That," said the solicitor, "is precisely the attitude that requires this Commission to exist."

"Then it exists for a good reason," said Kestrel, "and I hope it remembers which side of the ledger it was on."

Mrs. Halloway went next. She did not wait to be sworn before fixing all three commissioners with her gaze, which I believe unsettled them more than any evidence could have.

"I lost three sons at Vellmore," she said. "For six years I was told the records showed only one, and that I should consider myself fortunate the army acknowledged even him. This young woman"—she pointed at me, and I sat very straight—"is the reason all three names will be read in the village square on New Year's Eve. She worked for it. Night after night, letters to parish clerks, chapel rolls, pension offices. She did for my boys what the army would not do for a hundred pounds. If you punish her for that, punish me for sitting here saying so, because I'll say it every day you give me."

Tam's testimony was shorter and, somehow, worse for the Commission's composure. He held his folder upside down for the first thirty seconds, realized it, turned it over, and read in a voice that shook only a little.

"She taught me to write proper," he said. "I couldn't, before. She sat with me three evenings a week and never once made me feel a fool about it. She says a person who can write can witness, and a person who can witness can't be erased." He looked up. "That's all. Except—she never laughed at my handwriting. Even when it deserved it. Which it did."

Someone in the back benches laughed, and it loosened the room just enough that when Bram took the stand, he did so into something almost like warmth.

Bram kept it simple. He spoke of the rear guard, of the order he had signed, of the confession he had already entered into the permanent record at cost to himself. Then he said: "I've spent six years drinking so I didn't have to remember what that paper cost. This woman spent six years remembering for people who couldn't. I know which of us the country needs more. I'm offering mine as evidence of what forgetting does to a man, if the bench finds that useful."

Chairman Wexford, who had not spoken in an hour, said quietly: "It is useful, Sergeant. Noted."

* * *

They deliberated for forty minutes, which the solicitor assured us was efficient, and returned with their finding read aloud by Wexford in a voice pitched for a much larger room.

The theft was proven and admitted; the mitigating circumstances were, in the Commission's words, *"substantial, documented, and embarrassing to this body."* I heard the word *embarrassing* land and watched the solicitor close his eyes briefly, as if praying for strength. They were wary—Wexford did not quite say it, but I heard it underneath: wary of another session like the last one, wary of the hall going silent again, wary of four hundred and eleven names and the sort of public feeling that packed courthouse benches on a cold morning for a census clerk.

The penalty: a formal reprimand, entered in the record. Dismissal from service, already effected, affirmed. Costs of one pound. No warrant served, no further proceedings.

"And one further note," Wexford added, before the bench rose. He looked directly at me. "The amended roll of the Vellmore dead, compiled from testimony and corroborating documents including the dispatch in question, will be read publicly on the thirty-first of December. The Commission wishes to state—for the permanent record—that the reading proceeds by its own authority and design." A pause, dry as chalk. "Not, it need hardly be said, because anyone forced the matter."

"No, sir," I said. "Certainly not."

"Miss Ferris." He gathered his papers. "Your brother's name is on that roll. I carried a rifle near where he fell. I am sorry it took six years and a burglary."

Then the bench rose, and the room emptied slowly, in the manner of congregations, everyone wanting to shake a hand and be seen shaking it.

* * *

Outside, the light had gone the color of weak tea, and frost was beginning on the courthouse steps. The family waited by the gate—Bram stamping his feet, Tam breath-fogging his spectacles, Mrs. Halloway inspecting the sky as though it owed her money. Kestrel stood a little apart, hands in his pockets, watching me come down the steps.

"A reprimand," Bram said, falling in beside me. "One pound and a reprimand. I got two years for mine. There's a lesson in that about the relative dangers of theft versus honesty, but I refuse to learn it."

"Hush," said Mrs. Halloway. "Let the girl walk."

They let me walk. Kestrel fell into step beside me without a word, and we went along the frozen lane while the others trailed behind at a courteous distance, pretending loudly to discuss turnips.

"I keep thinking about what Wexford said," I told him. "*By its own authority and design.* He wants history to believe it was always their idea."

"History will manage," Kestrel said. "We have the ledger."

"So do they, now. Verbatim. In the permanent record." I smiled at the absurdity of it, the thing I had been turning over for days. "Do you realize what we did? We hid the truth inside the government's own filing system. It's the safest place in England. No one in the history of the Empire has ever accidentally read a government file."

Kestrel laughed—not his usual quiet exhale but an actual laugh, startled out of him, and I thought, not for the first time, that I would cross counties to hear that sound again. I supposed I already had.

We reached the lane's end, where the paths divided toward his house and my old lodgings, which I had given up, and his spare room, which I had not. He stopped. Frost silvered the hedge between us and the field, and the evening bell was starting somewhere below the hill.

"I've been meaning to ask," he said, and then stopped again, which was unlike him; Kestrel, having decided how much of himself a day would cost, generally spent his words carefully but completely.

"Ask, then."

"The ledger," he said. "Four hundred and twelve entries now, with the amendment. It's finished, as a record. The commission has it. The printed appendix will carry it. But—" He looked down at his hands, then up, and there was something in his face I had only ever seen there once before, the night before the hearing, when he read the names aloud alone in the kitchen and thought no one was listening. I had listened. I had stood in the dark of the stairwell and listened to every name, and by the end I had been crying too quietly for either of us to mention it afterward. "A record isn't the same as a keeping. Names want tending. Anniversaries, visits, corrections, the odd stubborn argument with a parish clerk who insists Private Hollis died in March when every man who was there knows it was February. That's keeper's work. It goes on past the record, past the hearing, past—" he gestured vaguely at the darkening sky, the dying year, the whole approaching century— "past us, if it's done properly."

"Yes," I said, not trusting myself to more.

"So I wondered," said Kestrel Coldharbour, in the voice of a man stepping off a wall he'd been sitting on for months, "whether the ledger has room for one more keeper."

The bell finished striking. Somewhere behind us, Tam said, far too audibly, "Ow," which suggested Mrs. Halloway had applied an elbow.

"One more keeper," I repeated. My throat was tight and my eyes were doing something inconvenient, and I found I was smiling anyway. "Is that an official position? Is there a form? Because I'm very good with forms."

"There's no form," he said. "There's just the ledger, and the names, and—" he hesitated, and then, with the courage of a man who had faced down a barrister that winter and found this harder, "—and whoever keeps it with you. Ideally. For a long while. That part isn't in the job description yet, but I'd be willing to draft something."

"In writing?"

"In writing. Witnessed, even. I know people."

"Well then." I put my hand through his arm, the way I had at the courthouse gate that morning, except that this time neither of us was pretending it was about the weather. "In that case, Keeper Coldharbour, you should know the century ends in nineteen days, and the village means to read the roll in the square, and I intend to be standing beside you when every name goes out over the frost—starting with my brother's."

"He'll be heard," Kestrel said. "They all will."

Above the hedgerow, the first star of the new century-to-come came out over Vellmore field, and we turned toward home together, the whole ridiculous, loyal procession of us falling in behind, discussing turnips.
