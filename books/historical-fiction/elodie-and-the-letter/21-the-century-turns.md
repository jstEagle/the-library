## 21. The Century Turns

The snow came down all morning on the last day of the nineteenth century, soft and unhurried, as though it had somewhere particular to be and no intention of rushing.

Kestrel watched it from the apothecary's window while he wrapped cough lozenges for Mrs. Petch, who did not have a cough but liked having an excuse to visit twice a week. His license was still suspended. The inquiry had been dropped, the charges dissolved like sugar in warm water, but nobody at the College of Apothecaries had seen fit to hand back the little brass plaque that said *Licensed*. He suspected it sat in a drawer somewhere in the city, being useful to no one, which struck him as a fair description of most official objects.

"You'll want a scarf," he told Mrs. Petch, handing over the paper twist. "The green one. The blue one makes you look like you're being punished."

"The blue one *is* punishment," she agreed solemnly. "My late husband gave it to me." She counted out her coins with great ceremony. "Are you coming tonight, then? To the hall?"

"I'm coming."

"They say there'll be two hundred people. They say the vicar's put up extra benches. They say—" she lowered her voice as though the walls might be loyal to someone else "—that they're going to read every single name. Out loud. All of them."

"That's generally what reading involves, Mrs. Petch."

"Don't you take that tone with me, Kestrel Coldharbour. I've known you since you were a boy with mud on your boots and I've earned the right to gossip about important things." She tucked the lozenges into her reticule and patted his hand where it rested on the counter. Her fingers were cold and certain. "You did this. Whatever they say tonight, however it goes—you did this."

He wanted to correct her. He wanted to tell her about Elodie, who had walked into his shop with a census form and a list of her own; about Tam, whose handwriting had gone from spider-stumbled to something almost beautiful in four months of candlelit copying; about Bram, sitting now in a cell in the county gaol with a reduced sentence for his confession and, by all accounts, the best sleep he'd had in twenty years; about four hundred and eleven names that had never belonged to Kestrel at all, only passed through his hands the way water passes through a mill.

He didn't say any of it. Mrs. Petch already knew. That was the strange arithmetic of the last year—he had learned that people know far more than they say, and love far more than they know.

"Go home and put on your scarf," he said. "The green one."

* * *

By four o'clock the light was going blue and the village hall windows were glowing gold, and the whole of Coldharbour Vale seemed to be walking toward them through the snow. Kestrel stood at the edge of the lane and watched them come: farm families and shopkeepers, the schoolchildren herded by Miss Pettibone like excitable ducklings, old soldiers from three parishes over wearing their campaign medals polished to a shine that suggested they'd been done that afternoon, possibly with vinegar and a sock.

Tam found him first, as Tam always did—a lanky shadow resolving out of the dusk, breath steaming, collar up.

"Everyone's come," Tam said. "Even people who don't come to things. Even the Hendersons, and they haven't left their farm since the flood."

"Someone read them a name they knew."

"Forty-some someones." Tam fell into step beside him. He'd grown into his shoulders that autumn, and he wore his good coat, and in his breast pocket—the pocket nearest his heart, because Tam had a sense of theater that Kestrel pretended to disapprove of—was the copy of the ledger. The fair copy. The inheritance. "I checked it twice on the way here. Nothing smudged. I checked it three times."

"You checked it three times."

"A man can't be too careful with other people's lives written in his own hand." Tam said it lightly, but his ears went red, and Kestrel understood that the boy had been carrying that sentence around for weeks waiting for a chance to say it aloud. It deserved saying. Four hundred and eleven men lived now in Tam's careful script, and Tam knew it, and it had made him walk differently—slower, straighter, like a man carrying a full cup across an uneven floor.

They went in together.

* * *

The hall had been transformed in the way village halls are transformed, which is to say imperfectly and with enormous effort. Garlands of ivy and dried yarrow along the beams—Elodie's work, she'd been gathering them for days. Extra benches borrowed from the church. A table at the front draped in white cloth, and on it, open, the original ledger itself, which the Commission had returned after entering it verbatim into the permanent record, with an official stamp on the inside cover that Kestrel found faintly absurd and could not stop looking at. Above everything hung the old regimental banner that had moldered in the vestry for six years, dusted off and rehung, its colors gone soft as memory.

The chairman of the Commission had come down from the city for the occasion—a broad, gray man with a veteran's stillness, whom Kestrel had last seen ordering Ashgrave's contempt entered into the record alongside everything else. Ashgrave himself was not present. He had retired to his estate in the south and issued a statement through counsel describing the proceedings as "an exercise in sentiment unworthy of the service." Kestrel thought about that statement sometimes, usually while doing dishes, and always arrived at the same conclusion: it was true, and that was precisely the point. Sentiment. Grief given its public shape. What else had anyone wanted from the century—all its blood and banners—but someone, finally, standing up and saying *these were our beloved, count them properly.*

The vicar opened with a prayer that managed to be brief, which in Coldharbour constituted a minor miracle, and then Elodie rose from the front row.

She wore her good gray dress and her hair pinned back, and she held the amended roll—not the ledger, but the official document now, the Commission's corrected roll of the Vellmore dead, printed in the city with all four hundred and eleven names in columns—and Kestrel's chest did the thing it always did when he looked at her lately, a kind of quiet rearranging, as if something long dislocated were settling home.

"There will be some repetition," she told the hall, "because brothers followed brothers, and fathers sons, and friends friends, and we will not tidy grief into variety. If your man is called, you may stand when you are able. You may sit when you need to. Nobody is watching how long you stand except everyone who loves you, and they will hold your coat."

A ripple of wet laughter went through the room. Kestrel saw Mrs. Halloway in the second row, upright as a mast, her three sons' photographs pinned over her heart in a cluster like a decoration of her own order. She caught his eye and nodded once, the way one soldier acknowledges another across a field.

Then Elodie began to read.

* * *

It took two hours.

She read until her voice thinned, and then Miss Pettibone read, and then the chairman himself read a column in a voice like gravel poured slowly, and then Tam stood with the fair copy and read thirty names in his clear young voice, and Kestrel realized, somewhere around the third hour's beginning, that he was the only one who hadn't yet spoken, and that Elodie had arranged it that way deliberately—saving him for last not as honor but as mercy, so that he would not have to perform while she watched, only witness.

The hall learned the rhythm of it. A name. A pause long enough for someone to rise or not. Another name. Men stood holding their hats. Women stood holding each other. The Hendersons stood together for four names and stayed standing for eleven more, because once you were up it seemed wrong to sit while others' boys were still unclaimed, and so the room filled by degrees until nearly everyone stood, swaying slightly, a congregation of the living keeping vigil over the counted dead.

When the name *Ferris, Josiah, private, 2nd battalion, aged nineteen* came, Elodie did not falter. Kestrel had wondered whether she would. She set the roll down on the table, laid her palm flat beside it as though steadying herself against weather, and said, clearly, "My brother. He wrote me a letter the week before Vellmore complaining about the boots. I kept the letter. I did not keep my promise to write back promptly, and I have regretted it every day since, and his name appears tonight on a government document because a nurse in a dressing station refused to forget him, and I will thank that man now in front of God and the whole parish."

She did not turn around. She did not have to. The hall turned for her, two hundred faces swinging toward Kestrel where he stood at the back, and Mrs. Petch began to cry loudly and unashamedly, and somebody started clapping, and Kestrel stood in the noise with his hands at his sides and thought: *six years of hiding, and this is what comes of it. This unbearable, ordinary warmth.*

He made his way forward through the parting crowd. When he reached the table, Elodie looked up at him, eyes bright and jaw set—the exact expression of the woman who had sat in his shop with her census forms and dismantled his lies one polite question at a time.

"You'll do the last page," she said. It was not quite a request.

"It's not a page of names."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

* * *

He had written the entry three nights earlier, alone in the shop with the lamp turned low, and he had rehearsed saying it perhaps forty times, and he now understood, opening the ledger to the final leaf, that none of the rehearsals mattered. Some words refuse to be practiced into safety.

The hall hushed. Snow ticked against the high windows. Somewhere a baby stirred and was soothed.

"For six years I kept these names in secret," Kestrel said, "because I believed the record had failed them and that a private book was better than no book at all. I was half right. The record had failed them. But a private book was never the remedy—it was only the wound, copied out nightly in my own hand." He paused. His voice held. "What remedied them was standing in this room tonight. It was a census taker who asked one question too many and wouldn't take silence for an answer. It was a sergeant who confessed to the worst thing he ever did so that the truth of it could serve the living. It was a widow who stood up before she was ready and taught us all how it's done. It was a boy who couldn't write, learning to write so that other mothers' sons wouldn't vanish. The lesson I take into the next century is this: nothing is remembered alone."

He looked down at the final entry. Tam had copied it too, in the fair hand, but this one—the original—was Kestrel's alone, the ink a little shaky even now under the lamplight.

"Last entry," he said, and read it aloud. "Thirty-first of December, eighteen hundred and ninety-nine. Four hundred and eleven names, entered, verified, amended, and read aloud in the parish of Coldharbour Vale. No soldier's name remains uncounted." He closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child. "*They were counted.*"

For a moment nobody moved. Then Mrs. Halloway stood, and the hall stood with her, and the sound that rose was not cheering—Kestrel would remember this all his life—it was lower and stranger and better, a great collective exhale six years held, two hundred people breathing out at once the breath they had been saving since the telegrams came.

The vicar, wisely, let it run its course before ringing for quiet and announcing that there were mince pies in the anteroom and that the century would not officially end until everyone had had at least one.

* * *

Later—midnight close, the snow stopped, the lane silvered under a fat moon—Kestrel and Elodie walked the long way home past the frozen millpond, their breath rising in twin plumes. From the houses came the sounds of the turning year: a fiddle tuning, glasses clinking, the Petches' dog barking at nothing in celebration of nothing.

"Nineteen hundred tomorrow," Elodie said.

"So they tell me. I've ordered no new calendar. I intend to go on writing '99 out of spite."

"You'll write '00 and you'll like it." She slipped her arm through his, an arrangement they had settled into sometime that autumn without discussion, the way villages settle boundaries. After a while she said, quieter, "Do you remember what you asked me? In the spring. Whether the ledger had room for one more keeper."

"I remember you asking *me* that. I believe you stole my question."

"I steal everything worth taking. It's how I got the dispatch." She squeezed his arm. "There's a letter coming, Kestrel. I've known for a week. From the archive office in the city. A clerk named Wrenn has found correspondence from the Vellmore campaign—field letters, unsent returns, things that were never entered anywhere. Hundreds of them. Names inside that aren't on our roll."

Kestrel stopped walking. The millpond lay pale and patient beside them, holding the moon like a coin.

"More names," he said.

"Dozens, maybe. Possibly more than dozens." Elodie looked up at him, and in the moonlight her face held the expression he had first seen across his own counter—that terrible, wonderful attentiveness, the look of a person who would ask exactly as many questions as the truth required. "The century turns tonight. And tomorrow morning there will be a letter on your doorstep with a new ledger's worth of work in it, and I wanted you to hear it from me first, tonight, under the old century, so you could begin the new one already knowing."

Kestrel stood very still. Six years he had spent closing books, and here was the world opening another one, and he found—to his complete lack of surprise—that his heart had already begun clearing shelf space.

"Well," he said at last, resuming their walk, his arm firm around hers, his breath and hers climbing together into the dark above the sleeping village. "Then we had better get a good night's sleep. And buy more ink."
