## 1. The Unfinished Coastline

The candle guttered at half past two, and Orsen Blackwood let it.

There was a discipline to the dying of light. Master Vane had taught it early: *a map drawn in bad light is a lie told slowly.* So when the flame bent low and began to swim in its pool of tallow, Orsen set down the burin, flexed the ache from their fingers, and sat for a moment in the blue dusk of the shop, listening to Edinburgh settle around them like an old dog turning twice before sleep.

Snow had come off the Firth that afternoon and lay against the windowpanes in soft grey ridges. Beyond the glass, the High Street had gone quiet except for the far-off complaint of a watchman's bell and the hiss of a coal thrown from some other window, some other life. The shop smelled as it always did after dark: linseed oil and hot metal, the dry sweetness of old paper, and beneath it all the faint iron tang of the engraving plates themselves—copper, which held its memory longer than any man did.

Orsen relit the candle with a spill from the banked stove, cupping the flame the way one cups a bird, and returned to the coastline.

It was the coast of Ross, the stretch between Tarbat Ness and the Dornoch Firth, and it was proving obstinate. Vane had left only fragments: a rough survey sketch with soundings marked in his cramped leftward-slanting hand, three sheets of bearings taken—God knew when—from a boat he must have hired out of Cromarty, and a note pinned to the topmost sheet that read simply, *The headland lies more east than Dampier says. Trust the water, not the books.*

Orsen trusted the water. That was the whole of the master's creed, and Orsen had absorbed it the way apprentices absorb everything—not by study but by breathing the same air for eleven years.

*Maps are for other people's histories.*

Vane used to say it while sharpening tools, or while drinking his morning small beer, or apropos of nothing at all, standing in the doorway watching the world go by on the street below with the mild, unreadable expression of a man who has decided to be furniture. *Nobody opens an atlas to find themselves, boy. They open it to find where they are relative to everyone else. A map is a mirror turned outward. We put down what is, and other folk do the remembering.*

Orsen had built a life inside that philosophy the way a wren builds inside a wall: snugly, invisibly, with great care and no ambition whatsoever. Thirty-one years old now, and still called *boy* by the few who knew the shop existed. Still working behind the ground-floor shutters while flashier houses on the other side of the North Bridge took commissions for estate plans and town improvements. Still signing nothing.

That was the arrangement, and Orsen loved the arrangement. Loved it the way the timid love their hiding places—which is to say completely, and with a small unexamined shame kept in the same pocket as a knife.

* * *

The atlas had been Vane's obsession for six years before his death. He had never named its purpose aloud; he had simply begun, in the spring of 1740, to draw Scotland.

Not the Scotland of the trade—a Scotland was already sold in ten shops in this city alone, rolled and ribboned and hung in gentlemen's hallways like a hunting trophy. No. Vane's atlas was to be something else. It went parish by parish. It recorded not merely the shape of the land but its texture: where the drovers' roads crossed the fords, which glens held mills and which held only ruin, how many hearths smoked in each settlement—*hearth-tax counts*, he'd say, tapping his nose, *the government collects them anyway; we merely collect them better.* He noted chapels, wells, standing stones, the sites of old battles and older marriages. He interviewed ministers, innkeepers, midwives, smugglers. He filled notebooks in a ciphered shorthand of his own devising that even Orsen, who mixed his inks and ground his acids and knew every tool in this room better than most men know their own hands, had never been invited to learn.

The atlas was to be *The General Description of North Britain*, and it was going to make them both respectable, Vane said, if either of them cared for that sort of thing.

Then, in February of this year, on a Tuesday, Aldous Vane had gone out to collect a debt from a client in the Canongate and had come back instead face-down in the Nor' Loch shallows with the back of his skull staved in.

*Robbery,* said the guard, shrugging. The city was full of robbery. A man of sixty-three carrying silver after dark might as well have carried a lantern painted with the word *please*. The case was closed before it was opened. There was no case. There was a body, a pauper's ledger line, and a shop that woke on Wednesday morning to find itself orphaned.

Orsen had not wept. This was remarked upon, quietly, disapprovingly, by the neighbors—the wig-maker across the close who had known Vane twenty years, the chandler who had supplied him candles since before Orsen could write. Orsen paid the debts, buried the master in Greyfriars under a stone modest enough to be invisible, closed the shutters for exactly nine days, and reopened on the tenth with the sign repainted: *A. VANE & CO., ENGRAVERS AND MAPMAKERS.*

*& Co.* One letter and an ampersand. It was the most public thing Orsen had ever done, and it had required sitting down afterward with shaking hands and a cup of tea.

Grief, Orsen had decided, was best performed as diligence. Grief made people look at you; diligence let you keep your head down over the plate. So Orsen rose at five, worked the plates until the light failed, ate standing at the counter, slept four hours in the loft above the workshop where the master's coat still hung on its peg because Orsen could not bring himself to move it and could not bear to see it moved, and told anyone who asked—and no one asked—that the atlas would be finished by Michaelmas.

By Michaelmas. As though finishing were the hard part. As though the hard part were not walking past the peg with the coat every morning, or reaching for the burnisher and finding it warm, sometimes, in a way that meant someone else had been in the shop—or that Orsen's own hands remembered the master's grip so thoroughly they could raise heat out of cold steel.

Michaelmas came and went. The atlas remained unfinished. Orsen remained diligent. The diligence was very nearly convincing.

* * *

Tonight the work was the Ross coast, and the Ross coast was fighting back.

Orsen warmed the plate over the stove until the wax took the point cleanly, then laid out Vane's survey sheets side by side, weighting the curling corners with the brass objects that had always served as weights: a cannonball from Culloden field—no, not Culloden, Vane had never said where the cannonball came from, only smiled that flat smile of his when asked—a broken horse-brass, and a stone from a beach Orsen had never seen, worn smooth as a knuckle.

The trouble was the headland. Vane's sketch showed it jutting further east than any printed chart had it, and the note insisted the books were wrong. But the soundings didn't quite agree with the sketch either—there was a discrepancy of a quarter mile at the second bearing, and Vane, who could triangulate a church spire from a moving rowboat without wetting himself, did not make quarter-mile errors. Which meant either the copyist had erred (there had been no copyist) or the master had taken two surveys at different times and the land itself had shifted, or—

Or the numbers meant something else entirely.

Orsen sat back, and the chair creaked in the silent shop, and for a long moment there was nothing in the world but the candle-flame standing up straight and the smell of hot wax and the quarter mile of missing sea.

Because here was the thing Orsen had been circling for nine months the way the tongue circles a loose tooth:

The numbers in Vane's later notebooks had stopped behaving like numbers.

Oh, the early work was clean enough—bearings and soundings, honest arithmetic, the plain grammar of survey. But somewhere in the third winter of the atlas, the margins had begun to sprout a different kind of notation. Little hooks and tails appended to the figures. Soundings written not as fathoms but as sequences. Bearings given to impossible degrees of precision—*N by E by N, 4 minutes 11 seconds*—when a compass of that era could not resolve to seconds at all. At first Orsen had assumed the old man's eyesight was failing, that he was dressing up dull data to amuse himself. Then Orsen had assumed drink. Then Orsen had assumed nothing at all, because assuming things about Aldous Vane's private codes felt like reading letters addressed to someone else, and there was already enough guilt in the house.

Now there was no one to ask. Now the marginalia sat in its locked box in the back room, forty-one notebooks in the master's crabbed hand, and Orsen went through them page by page each night after the real work was done, copying out the strange figures into a ledger of their own, telling no one, knowing nothing, being nobody.

This was the comfort of staying small. Small things were overlooked. The guard had looked at Orsen once, briefly, in February—a formality, the apprentice question—and had seen precisely what everyone saw: a slight, ink-stained person of indeterminate age in an apron too big, with the anxious politeness of someone whose entire strategy for survival consists of not being noticed. The guard had not even troubled to suspect such a person of anything, including grief.

Good, Orsen had thought. Good. Be furniture. Furniture survives.

And yet.

And yet tonight, tracing the disputed headland onto the plate, transferring Vane's stubborn eastward jut into wax, Orsen had paused at the curve of it—the way the land hooked round toward the Dornoch Firth like a finger crooked in summons—and had felt, unmistakably, the sensation of looking at handwriting.

Not map-work. Handwriting. The curve had a rhythm to it, an intention, a *slant*. The bay below it curved again, echoing, and the river mouth beyond that made a third mark, and together the three shapes fell into a pattern too regular for geology and too expressive for accident, and Orsen sat in the pooling candlelight with the burin idle in their hand and thought, with a cold sliding feeling under the breastbone: *He hid something in the shape of the land.*

Then thought immediately, the way one slams a door: *No. You are tired. You are grieving. You are reading meanings into geography because the alternative is reading them into a coffin.*

The clock in the corner—the master's clock, which Orsen wound every Sunday and had not allowed to stop once since February—chimed three. Orsen blew on the wax, tested the line with a fingertip, and found it good. Tomorrow the acid bath; tomorrow the first proof pulled and held up to the window light, that moment of truth every engraver lives for and dreads, when all the day's careful labor reveals itself as either art or wreckage.

Orsen cleaned the tools. Wiped the plate with bran to lift the last of the grease. Raked the stove. Hung the apron on its nail beside the master's—two aprons now on two nails, and every evening the same small ceremony of not-looking at the second one.

Up in the loft, undressing in the cold, Orsen permitted the day's one indulgence, which was to speak aloud to the empty room, very quietly, the way other people pray.

"The headland lies more east," Orsen told the dark. "Trust the water."

From the street below came a sound—faint, easily missed, almost certainly nothing. A footstep? The snow muffled everything these days; the whole city walked on wool. A cart, perhaps. A cat. The watchman, making his rounds, seeing the shuttered shop and passing on, as everyone passed, as Orsen intended to pass through the entire remainder of a life: unseen, unmolested, unremembered.

Orsen got into bed beside the cold window and pulled the blankets to the chin and closed their eyes and did not think about the shape of the headland, and did not think about the forty-one notebooks in the locked box, and did not think about the fact that tomorrow the watchman's rounds would bring him past the shop at half past ten, and that the knock, when it finally came—three nights hence, after curfew, with the snow falling thick enough to bury footprints in minutes—would be nothing like a watchman's knock.

But that is getting ahead. On this night, at least, Orsen Blackwood slept the sleep of the successfully invisible, and dreamed, faintly, of water.
