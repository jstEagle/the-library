## 2. A Knock After Curfew

The knock came at half past the curfew bell, which was the first wrong thing about it.

Orsen Blackwood knew every sound that shop made, had catalogued them across eleven years the way other men catalogued coins or grievances: the sigh of the doorframe settling when frost took the river road; the particular knock of wind-driven pebbles against the north window, like a fidgeting clerk tapping his pen; the groan of Master Vane's press in its sleep below the floorboards. The shop spoke to Orsen nightly in this language, and Orsen answered with silence, and between the two of them they kept a kind of peace.

But knocks—knocks belonged to daylight. Knockers wanted maps, or wanted payment, or wanted to complain that the coast of Kintyre had been drawn a quarter-mile too far east, as though the sea itself were Vane's fault. Nobody knocked at half past ten. At half past ten, honest people were behind shutters with candles pinched, because Corporal Hewes patrolled Candlemaker's Row with a lantern and a temper, and the penalty for opening one's door after the bell was a fine on the first offense and a watching on the second.

So when the knocking came—three raps, then two, then three again, growing weaker like a heart losing its argument—Orsen lay in the loft above the workshop and did not move.

It will go away, Orsen thought. Everything goes away, if you are quiet long enough. That was the whole of the creed, the entire philosophy Orsen had inherited from no one and perfected alone: be small, be still, be useful only to paper. History happened to other people—to masters with reputations, to soldiers with orders, to villages with the misfortune of standing where armies wanted passage. It did not happen to apprentices who kept their heads down and their inks mixed and their opinions folded away like unsent letters.

The knocking stopped.

And then, faintly, something else—a weight sliding down wood, a soft double thud at the threshold, like a sack of meal dropped by an exhausted carrier. Except sacks of meal did not whimper.

Orsen sat up in the dark. The candle stub beside the bed had gone out hours ago; the room was a blackness full of shapes Orsen knew by absence rather than presence—the blank rectangle of the window, the coat hook's silent question. Down through the floorboards came the smell of the workshop always rising, linseed oil and lampblack and the ghost of Master Vane's tobacco, though the master himself had been eleven days in the kirk yard and his pipe stood cold on the workbench below.

Eleven days. Long enough that grief had hardened into routine, which was how Orsen preferred it. Grief in its liquid state drowned a man; grief dried into habit could be carried.

The whimper came again, thinner now.

*Do not*, Orsen told himself, already swinging bare feet to the cold boards, hands finding the stair rail by memory. *Whatever it is, it is none of yours. Curfew exists so that what happens after dark can happen without witnesses, and you have spent your whole life being no witness at all.* It was not cowardice, Orsen had long ago decided; it was discretion. The distinction mattered at night, when a person needed it.

The bolt was stiff—it always stuck in damp weather—and the effort of drawing it made enough noise to wake the street, if the street had been anything but dead. Orsen opened the door the width of a hand, prepared to say *the shop is closed, come back Thursday,* prepared even to lie about a husband, a large dog, a fever in the house.

What lay on the doorstep was a child.

A girl, perhaps ten years old, though it was hard to tell under the grime and the way she had folded herself onto the stone, knees to chest, arms over her head, like something trying to become its own shelter. Her hair was dark with rain—or with something wetter than rain; the smell coming off her was rain and smoke together, and beneath that another smell Orsen knew from the tanneries but had never expected on a child. Her clothes had once been good wool, village-made, mended at the elbows with thread of a different fleece. One shoe was missing. The sole of that foot was torn and grey with walking.

She had knocked, Orsen realized. She had climbed through a garrisoned town under curfew, past whatever wall or wharf or culvert had let her in, and she had knocked, and then she had fallen down on the far side of the door like a letter too heavy for its box.

In her fist, clenched so hard the knuckles had gone bloodless, was a folded paper.

Orsen crouched, keeping the door between them like a shield held backward. "You've the wrong house," Orsen said, low, glancing up the Row. Nothing moved but rain in the lantern-glow at the corner, where Corporal Hewes would pass within the quarter hour. "There's nothing here for you."

The girl's eyes opened. They were the palest grey Orsen had ever seen, almost colorless, and they did not look like a child's eyes at all—they looked like eyes that had been open when they should not have been, that had seen a thing and could not now close.

She pushed the paper toward Orsen's boot. Her lips moved. No sound came out at first; then, cracked and small, as if the words had been carried a long way in the teeth:

"He said you'd open the door. He said—" A cough tore the rest away.

"Who said? Who sent you?"

She only pressed the paper harder against the stone.

Every sensible thing in Orsen rose up in a wall: *take the paper, shut the door, bolt it, do not read it until she is gone, put it in the stove unread, whatever it is it belongs to the dead and the dead do not need help.* Orsen had watched the whole town perform this arithmetic since the news out of Glenmara, six weeks past—had performed it personally. Soldiers had done something in the glen; the proclamation said rebels, said justice, said the matter was concluded; the minister preached submission on Sunday with a strange flatness; and everyone, everyone, had looked at their bread and their boots and their own dear skins and agreed with their silence that the matter was concluded.

Orsen had looked at the atlas instead. Plate forty-one, the western Highlands, Glenmara marked as a hamlet of some thirty souls beside a burn, awaiting its final engraving—awaiting, forever now, since Master Vane died before he could draw it.

*He said you'd open the door.*

Orsen took the paper.

It was cheap stock, water-spotted, and the writing on it was hurried but not illiterate—a scrawl in charcoal or soft lead, the letters leaning like fence posts after a storm: **forget the face.** Nothing more. No name. The face in question slept ten feet away on the doorstep, breathing in shallow hitches, one cheek laid against wet stone.

"I can't keep you," Orsen heard himself say, and was appalled at how quickly the words came, how ready they were, like tools kept oiled for exactly this job. "Whatever's happened—there's soldiers here, there's a commission coming, there is nothing I can—" 

The girl had stopped listening. She had turned her face to the sky and the rain, and her body had gone loose in a way that frightened Orsen more than any scream, the looseness of a rope going slack, and Orsen understood with cold suddenness that the choice was not *keep the child or turn the child away* but *bring the child in or find her on the step tomorrow, and explain her to Hewes, and explain her to everyone after Hewes.*

The wall of sensible things fell all at once, and Orsen stepped over the threshold—out into the open street, under the naked sky, for the first time after curfew in eleven years—and gathered her up.

She weighed less than the satchel she would not release.

* * *

Inside, with the door bolted and the shutters drawn and one candle lit behind the leather screen that shielded the workbench, Orsen set her on the settle by the dying embers of the workshop stove. She did not wake. That was its own terror—children should stir when handled by strangers; hers was the stillness of a body that had decided consciousness was a luxury.

Orsen worked with the competence of long solitude: shoes off (one), stockings peeled from skin that had worn them for days, feet washed in water warmed on the ember-edge, blistered sole wrapped in clean linen. The girl ate three spoonfuls of broth from the night's pot before sinking sideways into the settle's shadow, and even asleep her right hand stayed hooked through the strap of the satchel, fingers white.

Orsen sat opposite and looked at that hand for a long time.

The satchel was good leather, soldier-quality, the kind taken off a body. Its buckle was scored, its flap darkened in patches that Orsen refused at first to identify and then identified, because refusing had never yet changed what a thing was.

*Don't open it,* said the wall of sensible things, rebuilding itself brick by brick. *Put it by the door. When she wakes, send her on. Whoever "he" was, he's dead now, and dead men's errands are not binding.*

But Orsen had opened Master Vane's desk drawer eleven days ago to look for a tracing stylus and found instead the drawer's contents removed entirely, the papers gone, the lock scratched around its keyhole—not forced, but *turned*, by someone patient. And Orsen had said nothing to anyone, had filed the observation away in the same drawer inside himself where inconvenient things went, and had continued inking coastlines.

The satchel might hold the answer to that. The satchel might hold nothing. There was one way to stop wondering.

Orsen eased the strap free of the sleeping fingers—the girl frowned in her sleep, muttered something in Gaelic, softened—and lifted the satchel to the light of the candle.

Inside: a folded cloth. Unfolding it released the smell of old smoke and iron. And a journal.

Not Vane's fine folio stock—this was a commoner book, quarter-bound, swollen with damp, and stained down one side in browning arcs that Orsen's hands, unbidden, recognized as Orsen's own fingertips flinched away. Blood, and plenty of it, soaked and dried and soaked again.

Orsen opened it with the care owed to something explosive.

The first pages were survey notes—bearings and distances, place-names in Gaelic and English both, sketches of a burn's course, a bridge, a millstone. Familiar work. Familiar *hand*. Orsen's breath stopped in the throat, because the hand was Master Vane's—the slight forward rake of his capitals, the way his sevens crossed like a man signing against his own conviction. This was Vane's field notebook. These were Vane's surveys. Which meant Vane had been *in* Glenmara—had walked the glen after the soldiers came, or before, or during—and had written none of it into the atlas.

And interleaved between the survey pages, in a different hand entirely—looser, blunter, a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss three pages down—was testimony.

Names. Dates. The fourteenth of March. A sentence begun *They put the crofters in the kiln barn and* —and the ink dragged away where the writer's hand had failed, and resumed lower on the page, smaller: *I counted what I counted. Let God count the rest.* Then ranks. Then names again, officers' names, surnames Orsen knew from proclamations pinned to the kirk door, praised in the gazette for services rendered in the pacification of the western glens.

Captain Rennick. Lieutenant Fairfax. Ensign Mowbray, whose signature at the bottom of the page was steady as a ledger line.

The candle guttered. Orsen did not notice.

Page after page. What the soldiers had been ordered to do. What they had done beyond the order. What a witness had seen from the high pasture with a child's hand pulling at her skirt. Written testimony, sworn in the only court left to the defeated—the court of whoever survived to write it down. And at the back, folded twice, a list in Vane's hand: names of the village dead, tallied household by household, with ages. *Catriona MacIver, 34. Her son Donal, 9.* Nine. The girl on the settle was ten.

Orsen closed the journal and found that the workshop had gone strange—too quiet, the walls too near, the atlas itself seeming to lean from its shelf like a listener. Forty-one plates. Eleven years of Orsen's life poured into coastlines for other people's histories, and somewhere in those eleven years, Master Vane had been doing *this*—gathering the unwritten record, walking into the wound the proclamation had ordered closed—and had brought it home, and had died for it with his desk drawer emptied and his death ruled a seizure of the heart by a physician who had examined him for less time than it takes to boil an egg.

Rain worked at the shutters. On the settle, the girl slept with her ruined foot drawn up, and in sleep her face finally looked young.

*Forget the face.* But whose face? Hers—the witness to be erased? Vane's—the inconvenient corpse? Or Orsen's own, already entered on some list in some office, waiting only for confirmation?

Corporal Hewes's boots would come up the Row within the hour, lantern swinging, humming his tuneless hymn. A child was sleeping in the workshop of a murdered mapmaker, carrying a journal that named captains, and dawn was five hours off, and every hour between was a decision made over and over.

Orsen looked at the atlas on its shelf—at plate forty-one, Glenmara, blank where the engraving should be, waiting all this time as if it had known.

Then the girl stirred, and her eyes opened—those colourless, unchildlike eyes—and fixed on Orsen across the candlelight, and she said, in a voice scraped nearly to nothing:

"You knew him. The map man." A pause; her throat worked. "He drew our burn. He said—he said if ever anything happened, take it to where the atlas lives."

She looked at the shelf. She looked at the empty space on plate forty-one that Orsen had been avoiding for eleven days.

"They're coming to finish the book without him," she whispered. "That's what the soldier said. They're sending someone."
