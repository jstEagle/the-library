## 4. The Historian's Visit

The knock came at mid-morning on the fourth day after the child arrived, and Orsen knew before opening it that the world had found them.

Not because of any sound in particular. The knock itself was unremarkable—two raps, measured, patient. What troubled Orsen was the hour. Tradesmen came at dawn or dusk; creditors came at dusk and hammered; neighbors came whenever they pleased and did not knock at all. But a knock at mid-morning, soft and deliberate as a signature on a document, belonged to a certain kind of caller. The kind who wrote letters first. The kind who expected doors to open.

Orsen set down the burin beside the half-finished plate of the Sound of Sleat and stood very still in the workshop's amber gloom. Above, through the floorboards, came the small creak of the loft—the child shifting, waking, going silent. Good. Whatever was about to happen below, it would happen without her.

*Forget the face.* The note still lay in Orsen's coat pocket, carried now everywhere like a stone against the ribs. Someone had wanted this face forgotten badly enough to leave a child at a stranger's door with four words of instruction. And someone else, perhaps, wanted to be sure the forgetting had taken.

Orsen opened the door.

The man on the step was perhaps forty-five, dressed in good grey wool that had been cut in Edinburgh and worn carefully since—a coat neither fine enough to offend nor plain enough to be forgotten. He had a scholar's stoop already beginning at the shoulders and a face built for benevolence: soft around the eyes, quick to smile, arranged entirely for the comfort of others. In his hands he held his hat, and under one arm a leather satchel stamped with the crown's cipher.

"Master Blackwood." His voice was warm, low, unhurried. "Or should I say *Mister*? Forgive me—I've read your master's correspondence and I confess I feel I know you already. Ellery Shaw. His Majesty's Commission for Historical Records." He produced a folded paper with the flourish of a man offering not credentials but a gift. "I wonder if I might impose upon you."

Orsen took the paper and read it slowly, though the words blurred and reassembled themselves twice before settling. *Authorized to survey, collect, and prepare for publication such materials pertaining to the late rebellion as shall serve the public record.*

"The public record," Orsen said.

"The public record," Shaw agreed pleasantly, as though they had just exchanged the time of day. "May I come in? It's a cold morning for doorways."

It was, in fact, a mild morning. Mist sat in the street like wool carded and left unspun, and somewhere a cart rattled past, its driver whistling something jaunty and treasonous. But Orsen stepped aside anyway, because refusing would be a statement, and statements were things Orsen Blackwood had spent forty years learning never to make.

* * *

The shop received Shaw the way it received all strangers: with the smell of ink and size and old paper, with shelves rising into shadow, with the great flat table where Vane had worked for thirty years and where Orsen now worked alone. Shaw entered slowly, turning, letting his gaze travel the room with an appreciation so genuine it could not have been feigned—and yet there was nothing comforting in it. It was the appreciation of a man walking through an orchard and counting the trees.

"What a place," he murmured. "You can smell the work in it. Do you know how rare that is, Master Blackwood? Most workshops smell of their trades. This smells of its *purpose*." He touched nothing, which somehow was worse than touching everything. His eyes did all the handling. "And here—" he stopped at the table, at the plate under its dust cloth, "—the atlas itself."

"You'll have seen the correspondence, then," Orsen said. "You know Master Vane is dead."

"I know." Shaw's voice softened by precisely the right degree. "A terrible loss. A robbery gone wrong, they say. These streets grow no safer." He shook his head, and for a moment the benevolence seemed to reach all the way down. Then: "May I?"

He gestured at the cloth, and Orsen lifted it.

Beneath lay the unfinished Glenmara plate—or rather, the space where it would be, for Orsen had not yet begun to engrave it. What Shaw saw was copper, burnished clean, with only the faintest preliminary lines: a coastline sketched in drypoint, tentative as breath on glass. The village itself was absent. The hills were absent. Everything was absence waiting to become geography.

Shaw leaned over it for a long time. Long enough that Orsen counted his own heartbeats and lost track at thirty.

"A tragedy, Glenmara," Shaw said at last, quietly. "You'll know the account, of course. The village harbored rebels; the garrison moved to disarm them; in the fighting—well. In the fighting, villages burn. It is the oldest sentence in history, and the truest, and the most useless." He straightened. "His Majesty's commissioners wish the atlas completed. It was Aldous Vane's great work, and the crown honors great works. But it must be completed *accurately*, you understand. Accuracy is our whole profession. A map that flatters is worth less than no map at all."

"Of course," Orsen said.

"And yet accuracy requires sources." Shaw turned from the plate and regarded Orsen with those soft, attentive eyes. "Vane kept papers. Correspondence. Field notes, perhaps. Survey journals. When a craftsman dies mid-work, his materials are everything—they are his voice, speaking from beyond the grave, and we must hear him clearly if we are to finish his sentences properly. You have his materials?"

"They're being catalogued," Orsen said. It was true. They were being catalogued. Orsen had spent two nights doing exactly that, by candlelight, with the journal from the child's satchel hidden inside a folio of obsolete sea charts where no cataloguer would look.

"Splendid. Thorough. Just what I'd expect from his apprentice." Shaw smiled. "I'll need to see them, naturally. All of them. Not today—" he raised a hand, gracious, "—I know grief has its own schedule. Next week, perhaps. I'll call again."

* * *

He stayed two hours.

That was the thing Orsen understood only afterward, standing in the empty shop with the door bolted and the afternoon light dying gold in the windows: Shaw had stayed two hours, and every minute of it had been a search conducted in plain sight. He had asked to see Vane's tools and admired each one. He had asked about the apprenticeship—how long, what duties, whether Orsen assisted with fieldwork. He had asked, idly, pouring the tea Orsen had made because not making it would have been a statement, whether the master ever took commissions from *private* parties. Whether strangers ever came to the shop. Whether people from the Highlands ever brought him... materials.

"Testimonies, I mean," Shaw had said, stirring his tea with a small silver spoon that appeared from nowhere and vanished back into his coat. "Accounts. Old soldiers, displaced persons, that sort. Vane was thorough to a fault. A man that thorough collects more than measurements."

"He collected measurements," Orsen had said. "Coastlines. Elevations. The fall of rivers."

"Of course." Shaw had smiled over the rim of his cup. "Of course. Coastlines."

And once—only once—he had paused at the window that looked out onto the narrow wynd behind the shop, the one beneath the loft, and stood there a moment too long, hands clasped behind his back, watching the mist. "Lovely light back there," he'd said. "Good for working. Does the loft get much use these days?"

"No," Orsen had said, and heard the word land flat and dead as a coin on stone, and prayed it sounded like grief rather than fear.

Above them, throughout those two hours, the loft had made no sound at all. Sorcha—though Orsen did not yet know her name, would not learn it for another day—had been quieter than the roof beams. Quieter than the mice. Either she slept, or she understood, with the terrible instinct of children who have survived things no child should survive, that silence was the price of the morning.

* * *

When Shaw finally rose to go, he did it in stages, reluctantly, a man leaving a feast while still hungry. At the door he paused and turned, hat in hand, and delivered the sentence Orsen would carry to bed that night and wake with at dawn.

"You know, when we write the history of these years—and we will write it, Master Blackwood, that is the commission's whole purpose—there will be two kinds of people in it." His voice remained gentle, almost confiding. "There will be those who helped us remember correctly. And there will be those who"—he considered, choosing the word with a connoisseur's care—"*misplaced* things. Papers, mostly. Testimonies. Little evidences that cluttered the record until some diligent historian swept them away." He settled the hat upon his head. "The second kind always imagine they're being brave. They're usually just being late. Good day to you."

The door closed. The footsteps went down the wynd, unhurried, fading into the mist.

Orsen stood in the middle of the workshop for a long time, listening to the building settle, to the fire tick in the grate, to the enormous silence overhead. Then, moving quickly now, taking the stairs two at a time for the first time in years—

The loft was dim, smelling of straw and cold. Sorcha sat in the corner where the eaves dipped lowest, knees drawn up, arms locked around them, eyes enormous in the small pale face. On the floor beside her lay the slate Orsen had given her, and on the slate, drawn in white lines with a precision that made Orsen's chest tighten even now, was a village. Houses along a single street. A kirk with its little tower. A bridge over a burn. Hills behind, rendered in careful strokes, each one placed exactly where memory insisted it belonged.

Glenmara. Drawn from inside a ten-year-old's head, more accurately than any surveyor could have managed, while downstairs a man from the Commission for Historical Records drank tea and spoke of accuracy.

Sorcha watched Orsen cross the loft. Her lips moved, shaping a question, but no sound came out. She had spoken only a handful of words since arriving—*water*, *no*, and once, in a nightmare, a name that might have been *Da*.

"It's all right," Orsen said softly, and knelt, and looked at the slate instead of the child, because looking at the child made the fear rise like floodwater. "He's gone. He won't—"

But the reassurance died in the air, because it wasn't true, and both of them knew it. He *would* come back. He had said next week, and men like Ellery Shaw kept their appointments the way rivers kept their courses.

Orsen studied the drawing. The kirk tower. The bridge. Eleven houses—she had drawn eleven, and Orsen had counted, and the official account spoke of scattered dwellings, a hamlet, hardly worth the name of village. Eleven houses meant families. Eleven houses meant a tax roll, a parish register, graves with names on them. Eleven houses meant that someone, someday, might ask why the atlas plate showed empty moorland where eleven houses used to stand.

"We need to talk about your master's papers," Shaw had said.

They would come for the papers. Of course they would. And in those papers—if anyone looked closely, if anyone knew what to look for—was a journal in two handwritings, interleaved like braided hair, and a bloodstain on its cover shaped like a bird in flight.

Orsen reached out and, gently, turned the slate over, blanking the village, and saw the child flinch at even that small erasure.

"No," Orsen said quickly, catching the small hands. "No—not forgetting. Never forgetting. We just—we keep it where only we can find it. That's all keeping anything safe is. Hiding it well enough that the wrong people never ask."

Sorcha searched Orsen's face with those vast dark eyes, weighing the words the way children weigh promises, and whatever she found there—or failed to find—made her nod once, a small sharp motion, and take up the slate again and begin redrawing the village from the first line, as though to prove it could not be erased.

Downstairs, the workshop waited. On the table under its cloth, the copper plate gleamed, holding its tentative coastline, its beautiful emptiness. Next week Shaw would return and demand the papers, and Orsen would have to decide what a diligent executor of Aldous Vane's estate ought to show him—and what ought to be, in Mr. Shaw's elegant phrase, misplaced.

Orsen went down the ladder slowly, one hand pressed flat against the wall, and stood before the unfinished plate for a long moment in the failing light.

Then, deliberately, Orsen picked up the drypoint needle and added three new lines to the coast of Glenmara—small, insignificant, the kind of corrections any careful draftsman would make—and understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like calm, that the race had already begun, and that the finish line was a piece of copper not yet six inches wide.
