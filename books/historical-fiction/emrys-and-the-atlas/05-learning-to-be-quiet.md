## 5. Learning to Be Quiet

The loft smelled of cedar shavings and old paper, and Sorcha learned its geography the way she had once learned the hills above Glenmara—by touch, by dark, by the small sounds of a place deciding whether it meant her harm.

She lay on a pallet of folded sailcloth between two trunks of copperplate stock, and through the gap in the floorboards she could see the whole shop below, laid out like one of the maps she was not supposed to look at. The great table with its lamps. The flat wooden presses of drawers, each drawer full of paper that whispered. The window with its diamond panes, where morning came in grey and slow and made everything look like it was underwater.

And the person. Orsen Blackwood. Who moved like someone trying not to be noticed even when no one else was there.

Sorcha watched from the first hour of the first night, because watching was how you stayed alive. You watched the road. You watched the door. You watched hands, always hands—that was what Da had said, back when words still worked properly, back before the burning. *Watch their hands, a nighean. Faces lie. Hands don't.*

So she watched Orsen's hands.

They were long and ink-stained to the second knuckle, and they did the same things every day, in the same order, which after Glenmara felt like a kind of miracle. Hands that opened shutters at dawn. Hands that ground ink on a stone slab, the pestle going round and round with a sound like a slow heart. Hands that held a burin—a steel spike with a wooden handle worn smooth as sea glass—and drove it across a plate of copper in thin silver lines, *scritch, scritch, scritch*, for hours, until the light through the window changed and the lines became a coastline, or a river's branching, or the careful little marks that meant trees.

Nothing about those hands was quick. Nothing about them reached for her.

That last thing took the longest to believe.

* * *

For three days she did not come down. She ate what was left for her on the bottom stair—bread cut thick, cheese with a rind, sometimes an apple with a bruise cut away—only when the footsteps upstairs had gone and the shop stood empty, and she ate standing, one hand on the door latch, ready. She drank water from the rain barrel in the yard at dusk, crouched low behind the woodpile like a bird drinking at the edge of a loch, eyes moving the whole time.

At night the shop groaned and ticked as the timber cooled, and every groan was boots, and every tick was a musket being readied, and she pressed herself into the corner between trunks with her satchel clutched to her chest—the satchel with the journal inside, wrapped now in oilcloth, hidden under the loose floorboard Orsen had shown her without a word, only a look and a tap of one finger. She slept in pieces. Twenty minutes, maybe thirty, then awake, listening, then sleep again like falling into cold water.

On the fourth morning she woke to the smell of woodsmoke and porridge, and something in her body had decided before her mind agreed, because her feet were already on the ladder.

She stopped halfway down.

Orsen was at the hearth, stirring a pot, and did not turn around. "There's enough for two," Orsen said—not looking, voice low and even, the voice of someone who has learned that sudden movements frighten wild creatures. "Or for one. As suits."

Sorcha stood on the ladder and watched the hands. They kept stirring. Slow. Round. Round.

She came down the last rungs. She took the bowl that was set out on the edge of the worktable—set out, she noticed, not handed, so that taking it would be her own doing—and she carried it to the far corner behind the map chest, where the wall met the wall, where nothing could come at her from behind, and she ate there, fast, guarding the bowl with her arm curled around it the way she'd guarded it in the heather, in the ruined kirk, in the ditch along the military road.

Orsen never once looked over. The burin went *scritch, scritch* against the copper, patient as rain.

But when Sorcha had finished and crept back toward the stairs, she saw that a second apple had been placed on the bottom step. Already. Waiting for tomorrow.

* * *

The drawings started without her meaning them to.

It was the paper, she thought afterward—or she thought nothing at all, then, only that her fingers were restless and there were sheets of clean rag paper in a scrap bin by the press, misprints and spoiled proofs, free for the taking. She took four sheets and the stub of charcoal from the cold hearth and carried them up to the loft, and there, in the slatted afternoon light, she began to draw because her hands needed to do something other than shake.

She drew the way she remembered, not the way things looked, and remembering came out strange and true at once.

A gable end, blackened, with a doorway standing open onto darkness because the roof was gone but the lintel stone refused to fall. She knew that lintel. It had a horseshoe nailed above it, rusted to a curl of orange, and she drew the horseshoe too, though it made her chest hurt, so she drew it small.

A well, with a bucket rope snapped and hanging. Around the well, drawn very carefully, in a ring, a circle of small crosses. She did not know why she drew the crosses. Her hand did it. Later she would understand that her hand was counting, the way her hand had counted walking past, and that counting was the only prayer left to anyone.

A dog. A collie, lying flat, ears back, at the edge of the sheet, looking at something outside the frame. She could not make herself draw what the dog was looking at. She tried twice, and both times the charcoal broke or skidded, and the third time she turned the sheet over instead and drew the dog again from the other side, still looking away, still waiting, and this was somehow worse and also bearable, and she sat with it a long while until the light went.

She hid the drawings under her pallet. Then, that same night, she took them out again, because hiding them felt like letting them burn twice.

* * *

It was Orsen who found them—or rather, Orsen who did not find them, which was its own language.

Sorcha had left one sheet out by accident, weighted under a shingle against the draft, the drawing of the gable end with the horseshoe. She came up the ladder and Orsen was standing at the loft's edge, holding it, and everything in Sorcha went to iron. *Forget the face,* the note had said, and here was her face's whole country spread out in charcoal, and the stranger was holding it, and strangers had burned—

"They're good," Orsen said quietly.

Sorcha's breath came shallow and fast.

"Better than good." Orsen set the sheet down exactly where it had been, square under the shingle, touching nothing else. "Your master's eye is steady. Vane would have—" A pause. Something moved behind Orsen's face, some grief surfacing and being pushed under again. "Vane would have liked these."

Then Orsen climbed down and did not speak of it again.

But the next morning, on the bottom stair beside the apple, there was a small tin of charcoal sticks, and a soft white eraser like a piece of cloud, and a sheet of paper heavier than any Sorcha had ever held, smooth as a frozen puddle. No note. No words at all. Just the tools, laid out straight, waiting.

Sorcha looked down at them for a long time. Below, the burin went *scritch, scritch, scritch.*

She took the tin upstairs. And that evening, for the first time, she drew while Orsen was still in the shop below—drew badly and boldly, the well again, the crosses again, the collie again—and the sound of the engraving underneath her was like the sound of another person breathing in a house where you'd been sure you were alone, and it was the least frightening sound she had ever heard.

* * *

The flinching went slowly, the way frost goes off a field.

It went first from sounds. A cart rumbling past in the street no longer dropped her whole body; a dog barking in the lane raised her head but not her hackles. Then it went from movements: Orsen reaching across the table for a ruler made her shoulders climb less high each week, until one grey Tuesday she realized the hand had passed within a foot of her face and she had not so much as blinked.

Sounds of voices were hardest. When men spoke loudly outside—drunken soldiers mostly, singing the songs they sang, their boots striking cobbles in threes—she went into the corner between the trunks and put her palms over her ears and stayed until the street was empty, and Orsen never hurried her, never called her down, just let the porridge sit warm on the hearthstone until she came.

Once—she never learned how Orsen knew—the singing started and Orsen simply crossed to the shutter and closed it, mid-afternoon, for no reason a stranger would guess, and the room went dim and close and safe, and the songs were only sounds, only air.

Small mercies, done without ceremony. That was Orsen's whole manner, Sorcha was learning. A fortress built entirely of small courtesies, defended by silence, garrisoned by one nervous person who talked to maps more easily than to children.

* * *

The eighth morning, or perhaps the ninth—days had begun to lose their edges, which frightened her a little, because at Glenmara the days had all had edges, sharp ones—Orsen set two bowls on the worktable instead of one.

Not on the stair. Not on the hearthstone. On the worktable itself, in the middle of the room, in the lamplight, with two spoons.

Orsen sat down at the table. Began to eat. Did not look up.

Sorcha stood at the foot of the ladder a long time. The porridge steamed. The clock on the shelf ticked its dry tick. Outside, a milk cart rattled by, and somewhere a woman was calling a name—*Flora! Flora!*—the way her mother had once called hers across the fields at gloaming, and the sound went through her like a splinter finding an old wound.

Her legs carried her forward before she had decided anything. That was how the important things happened now; her body had begun making treaties her mind hadn't signed.

She sat. She picked up the spoon. The bowl was warm in her hands and the warmth traveled up her arms and into the place behind her sternum where the cold had lived since the burning, and she ate slowly, for the first time in longer than she could reckon, tasting the food instead of merely getting it inside her. There was honey in it. Actual honey, a thread of gold swirled through the grey, and honey meant trouble and expense and someone going out of their way, and she understood that too, and her eyes stung, and she kept eating, and Orsen kept reading a folio of proofs and pretending very hard that nothing whatsoever was happening.

Halfway through, Orsen said, without looking up: "There's a bay north of here the charts have wrong for forty years. Drawn too deep. I've half a mind to correct it, but my hand isn't sure."

A pause. The clock ticked.

"I'm told," Orsen said, turning a page with great care, "that yours is."

Sorcha looked at the maps stacked at the table's end. Coastlines and firths and hills rendered in fine brown lines, the world laid open like a fish, every secret of it written plainly for whoever could read the script.

Slowly, she slid from her chair, fetched her charcoal tin, and pulled the topmost proof toward her.

* * *

She drew the bay as she remembered seeing it once from the shoulder of Ben a' Chrochaidh, before—from a height, the whole curve of it, the water pale where the sand showed through. She drew it quickly and roughly, a child's line, but the shape was true, and when she pushed it across the table Orsen studied it for a long, long moment.

Then Orsen took up a pencil and measured the curve against the printed chart, and Sorcha watched the stillness spread through those careful hands, a stillness like the moment before lightning.

"It is wrong," Orsen murmured. "Forty years wrong." The pencil tapped the chart. "And you've seen it rightly once, from the hill." A glance at her then, the first direct look in nine days, and it held no pity and no hunger and no reaching—only a kind of wondering respect, as if Sorcha were a rare survey instrument recovered from a wreck. "You carry places in your head, Sorcha. Whole places. Do you know what a gift that is? What a *weapon*?"

The word landed strangely. Weapon. She looked at her charcoal-blackened fingers and thought of the well with its ring of crosses, and the collie looking away, and the lintel with the horseshoe that had refused to fall, and she understood that she did know. That the things in her head were the last copies in the world of certain things, and that copies could be killed.

Downstairs, in the street, boots struck past the shop in threes. Singing.

Orsen rose without hurry and closed the shutter against the afternoon light, the way that was becoming habit, the small mercy repeated. But this time Orsen paused at the window first, parting the curtain a finger's width, looking out—and Sorcha, watching, saw the whole body go rigid, saw the curtain fall back into place too smoothly, saw Orsen return to the table wearing an expression arranged with terrible care into calm.

"There's a gentleman," Orsen said lightly, sitting, taking up the burin, "who wishes to help finish the atlas. He'll call tomorrow, I expect. While he's here, you'll stay in the loft, and you won't make a sound—not a floorboard, not a breath. Can you do that?"

Sorcha nodded.

"And Sorcha." Orsen's eyes went to the drawings pinned now along the loft rail—the well, the gable, the collie—then to the satchel beneath the loose board. Whatever lived behind that calm face was moving fast, like weather. "If anyone ever asks, this shop holds nothing but maps. Maps are only pictures of places that are far away and already gone." A faint, grim smile. "Isn't that what everyone believes?"

Somewhere down the street, the boots and the singing faded toward the harbor, and Orsen bent again to the copper plate, and the burin began its patient *scritch*—but slower now, Sorcha thought. Slower, and deeper, as if the line being cut was carrying more than coastline. As if the master's atlas had become, overnight, something that people might kill for.

She gathered her charcoal tin close and climbed toward the loft, and from the gap in the floorboards she watched Orsen work late into the dark, and she wondered what the gentleman would want, and why Orsen's hands had begun to tremble, just slightly, whenever the bell above the shop door gave its small silver cough.
