## 6. The Master's Cipher

Three days after the child came through my door, I returned to the atlas as a man returns to a house where something has died—not to mourn it, exactly, but because the rooms still need sweeping and grief does not excuse disorder.

I had worked on Master Vane's plates for eleven months before Sorcha arrived, and in all that time I had believed I understood what I was making. An atlas of the occupied Highlands: forty-one plates, coastlines and lochs, glens and passes, roads drawn as the army's surveyors had measured them and corrected where Vane's own eye—sharper than any the Crown employed—found their instruments wanting. It was to be the definitive work. The kind of book that sits behind glass in a minister's library for two hundred years, consulted by men who will never walk the ground it describes.

That was the work I understood. Maps are for other people's histories—that was the creed I had learned at this bench, first as a boy grinding pigments and later as an apprentice with ink-stained cuffs, and I had never once thought to question it. The mapmaker draws; other men act. History happens elsewhere, in Parliament and on battlefields, in the mouths of ministers and the dispatches of generals, and we who make maps merely record the shapes left behind. I had made my peace with that smallness. There was comfort in it, the way there is comfort in any wall built low enough that no one thinks to scale it.

Then Glenmara burned, and a child carried its ashes to my door, and the walls did not seem so low anymore.

Sorcha slept upstairs. She had slept most of the three days, waking only to eat what I set before her and to look at me with those dark, unblinking eyes that measured everything and trusted nothing. Twice I had found her standing at the loft rail watching me work below, silent as a wren, and twice she had withdrawn the moment our eyes met. She drew, though. On scraps of paper, on the margins of spoiled proofs, in charcoal on the underside of a shelf when she thought I wasn't looking—the same village over and over, rendered with a precision that unsettled me more than speech would have. A kirk. A mill. A row of cottages with their doors facing the burn. I had compared her drawings against the unfinished Glenmara plate and found them correct down to the bend of the road.

But I could not sit with that knowledge all day. Grief disguised itself in me as diligence, always had, and so on the third morning I lit the lamps early, banked the fire, and went back to the atlas.

* * *

The problem that had stopped me was the marginalia.

Vane annotated everything. His working copies bristled with notes in his small, slanted hand—corrections of bearing, remarks on tidal range, complaints about the weather, the price of oats in Inverness, a running feud with a surveyor named Cathcart whom he considered a drunkard and a flatterer. Reading his margins was like hearing him talk: dry, exact, occasionally savage, endlessly particular. When he died, I had inherited not only his plates but the habit of his voice in those margins, and I confess I sometimes read them aloud to the empty shop just to hear a voice older and steadier than my own.

But the marginalia in the Glenmara materials was wrong.

I had noticed it before Sorcha came, and dismissed it. Fatigue, I told myself. Grief. The master was ill in his last months; his hand had faltered. But now, with lamplight steady and the shop quiet except for the tick of the cooling stove, I laid out the Glenmara correspondence beside the plate and forced myself to actually look.

The notes were legible. That was the first strange thing. A dying man's hand falters unevenly—here firm, there ragged—but these letters were uniformly precise, each one placed with the care of a man setting type. And they were nonsense. Not meaningless nonsense, which would have meant only that Vane's mind had wandered, but structured nonsense. Words that were almost words. *The coast at Duncraig runs very fair this season.* Fair? What did fairness have to do with a headland? *Cathcart counts his sheep twice and still miscounts.* Sheep, in a note pinned to a plate of inland forest?

And the spacing. Ordinary writing breathes unevenly—long sentences, short ones, the natural arrhythmia of thought. These notes marched. Every line nearly the same length. Every phrase divided into groups of letters with a regularity that no living hand produces by accident.

I had seen this once before, years ago, in a Jacobite pamphlet that passed through the shop wrapped in plain paper, its real contents hidden under a merchant's invoice written in the same maddening, even rhythm. Cipher. I had held the pamphlet, felt my heart knock once against my ribs, and put it in the stove unopened, because reading it would have been a crime and burning it was merely prudent.

That was the man I had been: a man who burned things rather than know them.

I am not entirely that man anymore. A child asleep in my loft had seen to that.

* * *

I worked until the lamps burned low and the grey of morning showed at the shutters, and I will set down here, as plainly as I can, what I found—though plainness betrays it, the way a transcript betrays music.

The key was in the bearings.

Every map carries numbers along its edges: the angles at which coastlines run, the compass courses between landmarks. Surveyors' shorthand, meaningless to anyone but another draftsman. Vane's were slightly wrong. Not wrong enough to shift the drawing—the plates themselves were accurate to a hair, better than accurate, they were beautiful—but the *recorded* bearings, the figures a reader would use to reconstruct distances, deviated from the true measurements by small, deliberate increments. Three degrees here. Seven minutes there. Deviations no mapmaker would tolerate and no soldier would notice, because the picture looked right.

The deviations spelled.

It took me an hour to see it and another two to believe it. Each deviation corresponded to a letter of the alphabet—zero for A, or some scheme near it, I never fully reduced it to a single rule because Vane had varied the system from plate to plate, the old fox, layering keys upon keys—and strung together along the margin of a single coastline, the numbers resolved into words. I took the eastern shore of the Glenmara plate, copied out its recorded bearings in order, applied the simplest substitution, and read:

*MACGREGOR, CROFTER, SAW REDCOATS FIRE ON THE KIRK DOOR AT DAWN.*

I sat back. My hands were cold. Outside, Edinburgh was waking—carts on the cobblestones, a fishwife crying her wares two streets over, the ordinary machinery of a city going about its ordinary morning—and inside my shop, on a sheet of paper I had traced with my own hands, a dead man was giving testimony.

* * *

Understand what I had thought I was doing all these months. I had thought I was finishing a book.

Vane would send me a plate roughed in—the coastline surveyed, the interior sketched—and I would fair-draw it: clean the lines, cut the engraver's strokes, lay in the hachures for relief, letter the names. Craftsmanship. Obedience, really, dressed up as craftsmanship. He chose what the maps said; I made them say it beautifully. I knew his opinions of the occupation—he made no secret of them in the shop, in that dry voice that could flay a general without raising itself above conversation—but opinions were air. Ink was ink. I drew coastlines. What happened in the glens happened elsewhere.

Except it hadn't. It had happened here, in these very lines, under my own hand.

Because the marginalia was only the shallow channel. Once I knew to look, the deeper water opened beneath it. The words in the margins—the "fair" coasts and miscounted sheep—were a second cipher keyed to the same deviations, a decoy for any casual searcher, gibberish that would read as an old man's wandering mind. But the plates themselves. God, the plates themselves.

A coastline's curve spelling a name. I found it on the Duncraidh plate: the way the shore bent around the bay was not the way the shore bent in Vane's own field sketches. He had altered it—a graceful, plausible alteration, the kind a master makes to improve a composition—and the alteration inscribed, in the language of curves that cartographers read without knowing they read it, the name *MARJORY FERGUSON*, and beneath the name, in the density of stippling along the waterline, a number. Forty-three. I counted the dots three times. Forty-three.

Forty-three was not a depth sounding. Forty-three was how many bodies Marjory Ferguson had seen pulled from the water.

The forests were worse. On the inland plates, Vane drew woodland with individual tree-symbols—standard practice, and no reader ever counts them, because why would anyone count trees? I counted. The wood east of Glenmara held two hundred and six trees where his field sketch showed one hundred and ninety. Sixteen extra trees, clustered in a pattern too deliberate for ornament, each one a soul. The dead of the village, planted in ink like a burial ground no spade would ever find. And interleaved among the tree-symbols, distinguishable only by a difference in the roots—a fork here, a straight stroke there—were marks that resolved, when I applied the bearing-key, into fragments of testimony: *they came at dusk. They asked first for the minister. They burned the records before the houses.*

They burned the records first.

I set down my magnifier and pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. The shop smelled of oil of cloves and hot metal and the tallow of the lamps, the smell I had loved all my life, the smell of safety, and none of it smelled safe anymore.

* * *

Here is what I understood, sitting in the grey light with the plates spread around me like the pieces of a disassembled instrument.

Aldous Vane had known he was going to die.

He had known it the way a surveyor knows a bridge is rotten—not by prophecy but by measurement. He had watched the army burn villages and the historians rewrite them, watched witnesses vanish and records go first into the fire, and he had understood, with his terrible dry precision, that anything written plainly could be unwritten. Testimony could be denied. Journals could be seized. Names could be struck from lists. But a map—an official atlas, commissioned, licensed, destined for the libraries of the men doing the erasing—no one burns a map they believe flatters them. No one looks twice at a coastline. You cannot suppress what you are proud to display.

So he had built the truth into the thing they would most want to own. Every witness who came to him—and someone had brought them to him, someone had smuggled their words across the country in letters and whispered conversations, and I thought suddenly, sharply, of Mrs. Abernathy the widow on Candlemaker Row with her sharp eyes and her parcels that weighed too little for their size—every testimony had been translated into geography. The victors would publish his atlas believing they had captured a monument. They would be publishing a grave-marker, a witness-stand, a list of the dead, hidden in plain sight under forty-one plates of impeccable craftsmanship, waiting for whoever held the key.

And the key had died with him—or so he must have believed. Except that he had spent the last year training me. Sending me plates to fair-draw. Correcting my strokes with patient red ink. Teaching me, stroke by stroke, the exact curve of every coastline, every stipple, every tree—so that when the moment came, his unwitting apprentice could complete the cipher without knowing there was a cipher at all. If I had drawn Glenmara freely, in my own hand, I might have smoothed away a name. Simplified a wood. Tidied the stippling, as any good craftsman tidies.

He needed my precision precisely because I did not know what the precision was for.

I do not know how long I sat with that. The awe came first—a great slow wave of it, admiration so fierce it was close to love, for the audacity of the plan, for the patience of the man, for the sheer *craftsmanship* of a truth engineered to survive its own suppression. He had turned the victors' vanity into the vehicle of their exposure. He had made their atlas a confession they would sign with their own seal.

Then the terror arrived, as terror does, quietly, wearing practical clothes.

Because if the atlas was evidence, then I was evidence. My hands, my ink, my unfinished plate lying on the bench with half its cipher already in place. Anyone who understood what Vane had done—and someone had thought Vane dangerous enough to kill, however carefully the coroner's report dressed it as a fall on the stair—anyone that determined would be looking for the continuation of his work. And the continuation of his work was me. Was us. Was a ten-year-old child asleep in my loft whose satchel held the plain-text version of everything the plates concealed, the key that proved the cipher was no accident of artistry but deliberate testimony.

I looked at the Glenmara plate—at the false, official Glenmara I was expected to draw, loyalist victory, regrettable but necessary excess, a village that would appear in the published atlas as a smudge of ruins and a note on pacification—and I understood that I stood at a fork with no neutral path through it. I could erase the beginnings of the cipher from the plates I had touched, draw the lie cleanly, and survive. The book would be published. The dead would stay dead in the only sense that still mattered: unmourned, uncounted, unnamed, redrawn.

Or I could finish what my master had started, knowing now what I finished, and become the sort of man who gets pushed down stairs.

From the loft overhead came a sound: the soft creak of a floorboard, the rustle of a small body turning in sleep. I listened to it the way sailors listen for landfall.

* * *

I did not decide anything that morning. Decisions, in my experience, are stories men tell afterward about things they had already become. What I did was work. I cleared the bench, locked Sorcha's satchel in the strongbox under the floor, and began—carefully, methodically, with the part of my mind that had always loved this craft independent of what the craft carried—to learn the whole of Vane's system. Plate by plate. Key by key. I catalogued the deviations, transcribed the testimonies, matched names to stipple-counts and tree-symbols to the fragments of statement scattered through the woods. It took me past noon and into the afternoon, and the deeper I went the more astonished I grew at the scale of it. Not Glenmara alone. There were others. A clearance in Kintail where the trees numbered the evicted. A ferry crossing on the Clyde coast whose bearing-deviations carried the words of a woman who had watched her sons transported. Eleven plates, at my last count, carried testimony. Eleven quiet landscapes, each one a courtroom in disguise.

Toward evening I heard the child come down the ladder. I heard her pause—she always paused at the foot, listening, taking the measure of the room before she entered it—and then cross to the stool by the fire, where she sat with her knees drawn up, watching me work.

I should have gone on guarding the secret. A year ago I would have; three days ago I might have. Instead I pulled the Glenmara plate toward the lamp, turned it so the light raked across the coastline, and said, "Come here, Sorcha. I want to show you something your master hid."

She came slowly, the way she did everything, and stood at my elbow. I pointed to the curve of the shore.

"There's a name in this line," I said. "Can you see it?"

She leaned close. Her hair smelled of woodsmoke still, no matter how she washed. For a long moment she studied the coast, her lips moving slightly as if sounding the shape, and I watched something move across her face—recognition, and under the recognition, grief, and under the grief, the first ember of something harder.

"That's Mrs. Ferguson," she said. Her voice was rusty from disuse, thin as leaf-ice. "She pulled people out of the water. She talked to the gentleman with the spectacles." Her finger rose and touched the stippled band along the waterline, gently, the way one touches a sleeping person. "Is this them?"

"Forty-three," I said.

She counted. I let her; it seemed important that she count. And when she reached the end and her finger rested on the last dot, she looked up at me and asked the question that undid the last of my comfort and set the hook of the next year deep into my life:

"If it's hidden," she said, "how will the soldiers ever know they lost?"

Before I could answer—for I had no answer; the question was larger than the shop, larger than the atlas, larger perhaps than both of us—a fist knocked twice at the street door, unhurried and confident, the knock of a man who has never in his life been made to wait outside.

Through the shutter-gap I saw, in the lamplight of the street, a gentleman's coat and beneath a gentleman's hat, a face I had hoped not to see again for a long while.

Mr. Ellery Shaw had come back to help me finish the atlas.
