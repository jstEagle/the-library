## 7. Debts and Denunciations

The knock came at an hour when knocks meant nothing good, but it was only Mrs. Colquhoun from the chandlery next door, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, her breath fogging in the doorway.

"I'll not keep you, Mr. Blackwood." She said this in the manner of people who intend to keep you considerably. Her eyes went past my shoulder into the shop, quick as a magpie's, cataloguing what she saw: the counter swept, the lamp turned low, the door to the workroom standing closed. "There were men asking after this place. Yesterday, and again this morning."

"Men," I said. A word with no shape in it at all.

"Soldiery-adjacent, I'd call them. Not in uniform, which is worse, if you take my meaning. One of them had a Lowland voice, soft as butter, asking did I know your hours, whether the old master kept visitors, whether there was a household above the shop." Her mouth compressed. "I told him mapmakers keep no company but their maps."

I thanked her, and I confess the thanks came out thinner than courtesy required, because my mind was doing arithmetic it did not want to do. Mrs. Colquhoun had never once, in six years of sharing a wall, taken any particular interest in me beyond the price of tallow. Kindness arrives most often wearing its own invoice.

"There's those would say," she went on, drawing the shawl tighter, "that a shop like this one—quiet, respectable, paying its rates—is best kept quiet and respectable. Whatever the master left unfinished, it's ink on vellum, isn't it? It's not worth a body's windows."

Windows, I understood, being the first thing that breaks.

"You've been a good neighbor," I said. "I'll mind what you've said."

She looked at me a moment longer, and something moved behind her eyes—not malice; I want to be fair to her, even now. Fear. The same fear that had walked her through my door at dusk instead of speaking over the fence in daylight. "Mind it well," she said, and was gone down the step into the blue evening, and I stood listening to her clogs until the sound dissolved into the noise of the tide turning in the harbor.

I barred the door. Then I stood with my hand on the bar longer than the act required, because my hand wanted to be sure of something my head already knew: that the shop was no longer a place where things happened to other people. It had become a place on other people's lists.

* * *

For six years I had built my life on a simple architecture: be small, be exact, be unremarkable. Master Vane had taught me the trade and, without ever naming it, the creed that went with it—that maps are drawn for other people's histories, that the cartographer's gift is to record the world while remaining outside it, a lens and not a light. A man who knows where everything is can arrange to be nowhere at all. I had believed this the way one believes in tides: completely, and without examining why the water moves.

Now I climbed the stairs to the loft where Sorcha slept, and the arithmetic resumed itself against my will.

She was curled beneath the eaves with her back to the dormer, and even sleeping she managed to look like someone prepared to run—one knee drawn up, one hand loose near the satchel she still would not let me store anywhere but within reach of her fingers. The bruise along her jaw had gone the yellow-green of a fading coastline. She had eaten half the oatcake I'd left her, which was progress measured in fractions so small I had learned to celebrate them.

Strangers asking about the shop. A household above it—Mrs. Colquhoun's soldier-adjacent man had known there was a household, or suspected one, or simply asked until the answer assembled itself out of neighbors' glances. A chandlery shares a chimney stack. Sound climbs flues the way smoke does. If Sorcha cried out in the night—and she had, twice, muffled by whatever instinct teaches children to suffer quietly—I could not say who might have heard.

I sat down on the stair and put my face in my hands and allowed myself, for the length of perhaps ten breaths, to consider the exit. It was a real consideration. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the whole point of what follows, and a man who pretends he never weighed flight has already begun falsifying his record. There are ports north. There are ships that ask no questions for coin. There is a version of Orsen Blackwood that carries a satchel of plates to Leith and takes passage under a dull name and draws coastlines in Amsterdam until he dies old and unremarkable and safe. That version existed for ten breaths. He was comfortable. He was plausible. He was everything I had trained myself to be.

Then Sorcha turned in her sleep and made a small sound—not a cry, nothing so dramatic; a sound like a hinge deciding whether to open—and the ten breaths ended, and the other Orsen dissolved the way ink dissolves when the paper goes under water.

* * *

In the morning I did what frightened people have always done: I sought information, and I paid for it.

Tam Muir was a printer's devil at Struthers & Kinnaird, fifteen years old, all elbows and ink-stains, with the amoral omniscience that boys in his trade acquire the way other boys acquire measles. Every sheet that passed through the town's presses passed first through Tam's hands, and every rumor in the town passed through his ears, which were large and always conveniently positioned. He also owed me. Two years past I had drawn him a map of the drains beneath the High Street—the sort of map a boy prizes more than gold, since it made him a landlord of secret territory—and the debt had never been called, which made it grow rather than shrink.

I found him lugging a ream of wet paper up the Cowgate and fell into step beside him, which took some doing, since Tam moved like water finding its level.

"Mr. Blackwood." He didn't stop walking. "You'll be wanting news, then, seeing as you're standing in the rain beside me instead of in your nice dry shop."

"What makes you say that?"

"Nobody walks the Cowgate for pleasure." He shifted the ream to his hip. "Two shillings says you're wanting news and it's the kind you don't want printed."

"One shilling, and you tell me what's been moving through the town about the commission."

"The commission," Tam said, and for once his voice lost some of its street-polish. We turned down a close where the eaves nearly touched and the rain arrived filtered and gray. "That's the thing, isn't it. Everyone's talking about the commission like it's weather coming. Official statements, they're calling for. Sworn testimony, taken proper, written into the record. Sounds very grand." He stopped, set the ream down on a step, and wiped his nose with the back of a wrist striped with ink. "Only here's what I hear from the men who carry the warrants and the men who sweep the wynds, which is better news than anything Struthers prints. They're saying folk are gone before the commissioners ever sit down. A crofter off the Dumbarton road—gone. An old woman who kept a change-house near the head of the glen—gone. A drover who talked too much at Stirling market about what he saw from the ridge—found in the Water of Teith, face down, and the verdict was drink, though everyone knew he drank little."

The rain ticked against the close's roof somewhere above us. I heard my own pulse in my ears, slow and enormous.

"How many?" I asked.

"Enough that it's not chance anymore." Tam picked the ream up again, hugging it like a child. "It's method, Mr. Blackwood. Somebody's tidying ahead of the commission the way you'd tidy a room before visitors. Only the room is people." He glanced up at me, and his boyishness fell away for a moment entirely, leaving something older and warier underneath. "And here's the other thing, since you're paying. Your master's name came up. Not loud. But a man in Struthers' back office—a man who doesn't do the printing, mind, only watches it—was asking which press had done Aldous Vane's proof-sheets over the years. Asking quiet. Writing answers in a wee book." He shifted his weight. "You want my advice, which you didn't pay for?"

"Go on."

"Don't be the last name on anybody's list. Lists get read top to bottom."

* * *

I gave him his shilling and walked home the long way, through rain that had settled into a fine persistent mist, the kind that doesn't fall so much as accumulate, gathering on wool and stone and eyelashes until the whole world seems to weep without conviction.

Witnesses disappearing. Not silenced by tribunals, not arrested on charges—that would leave records, and records are the enemy of the men Mrs. Colquhoun's butter-voiced visitor served. Disappearing. Erased the way one erases a surveyor's error: lightly, thoroughly, before the fair copy is made. And the commission would arrive with its seals and its candles and its sworn depositions, and it would find the pages blank, and it would write *nothing to be found*, and that nothing would be bound and shelved and cited for a hundred years as history.

I stopped on the bridge over the burn and watched the water worry at the pilings, and I understood something with the clean cold clarity of a freshly-cut plate.

My silence was no longer neutrality. That had been true for some weeks, perhaps, but I had not let myself see it, in the way one does not look directly at a stain on a beloved map. Every day I kept the shop quiet, kept the shutters latched, kept Sorcha invisible and the journal hidden and my answers to questions carefully hollow—every such day, the tidying went on elsewhere, and benefited from my tidiness. The men with the list needed the town quiet while they worked. A town full of people minding their own windows was exactly the weather they wanted. I had thought of myself as outside history, a lens and not a light. But a lens that refuses to focus is still part of the picture. Silence, it turns out, is not the absence of a position. It is a position, purchased at the price of everyone who could not afford to stay silent—the crofter on the Dumbarton road, the old woman at the change-house, the drover face-down in the Teith who drank little.

Master Vane had not stayed silent. That was the whole of it, really, stripped of cipher and margin and mystery: a man who believed maps should serve other people's histories had found a history that could not serve itself, and had set down his instruments and gone to Glenmara to listen, and had died for the transcription. And he had left me the atlas not as a legacy but as an assignment. I had been reading it wrong for weeks—reading it as grief-work, as duty to a dead master, as the melancholy pleasure of finishing another man's coastlines. It was none of those. It was a relay race, and I was holding the baton, and men were already disappearing the runners ahead of me.

* * *

The shop, when I reached it, was exactly as I had left it, which is to say it looked innocent, and innocence, I was beginning to learn, has a smell that the right noses find at a great distance.

Sorcha was awake. She sat at the worktable—not hiding in the loft, not flattened against the far wall, but seated at the table with a stub of charcoal and one of my spoiled proofs, drawing. This was new. She had begun, in these last days, to draw again, and her drawings were not children's drawings. They were surveys. She drew Glenmara from memory the way Master Vane drew coastlines: the mill with its wheel, the blacksmith's shed with the turf stacked against the windward wall, the rowan at the crossroads, the number of chimneys, the direction each door faced. As though memory were a country and she its cartographer, and the only way to keep a country alive was to fix its coordinates before the fair copy could be made without it.

She looked up when I came in, dripping, and did not flinch. Another new thing. She studied my face, and I had the uncomfortable sense of being read the way she read everything—completely, without mercy.

"You've been crying," she said. Not accusing. Observing. Ten years old, and she had already learned that tears are data.

"The rain," I said.

She considered this lie with the gravity of a magistrate, and let it pass, and returned to her charcoal. I hung my coat to dry and lit the lamp and stood a moment watching her small hand move across the ruined proof-sheet, laying down the black line of the millrace, and I thought of Tam's words: *the room is people.* And of Mrs. Colquhoun's: *not worth a body's windows.*

"Sorcha," I said.

She looked up.

"Soon there will be men come here. Important men, who will sit in the big room downstairs and ask people questions and write the answers in a book. A commission, they call it." I chose the words the way one chooses type, one at a time, checking each for cracks. "They are supposed to find out the truth about Glenmara. That is what they will say they are doing."

Her hand had gone still over the paper. In the lamplight her eyes were very dark and very steady, and I saw that she already knew the shape of what I was telling her, the way children always know the shapes of adult catastrophes before we deign to describe them.

"But there are other men," I went on, "who do not want the truth found. And they are making sure that the people who could tell it—" I paused. There are words one cannot say to a child, and then there are words one must, because the child has already survived them. "—are being taken away. So that when the commission asks, no one is left to answer."

She looked down at her drawing. The mill, the wheel, the doors facing the wind. Her thumb moved once across the corner of the sheet, smudging the black line of the millrace into a gray blur, like a place where the survey ran out.

"My grandmother said the soldiers burned the papers first," she said quietly. "Before anything else. Before the kirk even. Papers first." She looked up at me. "So nobody could say we were real."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the wynd a cart passed, and the shop windows trembled in their frames, and I felt the truth of it go through me like a blade going through wet vellum—cleanly, terribly, all the way to the board beneath.

They were not killing witnesses. They were unmaking evidence. The commission was not an inquiry they feared but an instrument they intended to use: it would certify the emptiness, notarize the absence, stamp the official seal upon a record already scoured. Against that, what use was a hidden journal, a frightened apprentice, a child's charcoal drawings?

Every use, actually. Precisely that use and no other. Because absence can only be proven empty, and the atlas was not empty. The atlas was the thing they could not burn, because they did not know it existed as what it was. Vane had built the counter-record into the very object the crown expected to inherit—an atlas of the occupied Highlands, commissioned, licensed, respectable, destined for the official shelves—and inside its coastlines, its forests, its hachured hills, the truth lay encoded, patient as seed. All it lacked was completion. All it lacked was me.

"Sorcha," I said, "I need you to listen to me very carefully."

She waited, charcoal poised, a small figure at a large table with the whole of a murdered village spread beneath her hand.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we begin work in earnest. You and I. You are going to help me finish a map."

And somewhere down the dark wynd, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots stopping outside the chandlery next door, and a soft Lowland voice asking, politely, whether Mr. Blackwood kept a household above his shop.
