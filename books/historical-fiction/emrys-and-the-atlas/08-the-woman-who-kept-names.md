## 8. The Woman Who Kept Names

I had been in the shop eleven days before I learned that Orsen Blackwood went out on Thursdays.

I learned it the way I had learned everything since Glenmara: by keeping still and watching. Stillness was a skill the soldiers had taught me without meaning to. They taught it to me on the night they came to the village, when I lay under the fallen loom with my cheek against the cold earth floor and my hand over my own mouth, and I learned that a body can be quiet if the mind will only let it. The mind does not want to let it. The mind wants to scream and run and find your mother. But you press the scream down like a hand pressing cloth into water, and you stay, and you live, and after that stillness is yours forever, a coin you carry that no one can take from you.

So on Thursday morning I watched from the loft.

The loft was mine now, or as near to mine as anything was. It sat above the workshop where the smell of ink lived — a bitter, sharp smell that had stopped making me sick by the fourth day and become, by the seventh, almost comforting, the way smoke becomes comforting to someone whose village burned because at least it means someone is warm. Below me Orsen moved through the morning rituals I had come to know like a psalm: the kettle, the lamp trimmed, the weights shifted on the great map that never seemed to grow any finished. Orsen talked to the maps. Not loudly. Little murmurs, half-words, *hold there, hold* — as though the paper were an animal that might startle. I decided early that this was not madness but manners. The maps had been Master Vane's, and Orsen was keeping them company the way you sit with the dead.

Orsen dressed carefully for going out. That was how I knew. A coat brushed, though the coat needed no brushing. Boots wiped at the heel with a rag, twice, three times. A pause at the little square of mirror — Orsen did not like mirrors, I had noticed; this one hung where it must have hung for years, angled so you could avoid your own face if you stood slightly to the left, which Orsen always did — and then a breath taken in and held and let go, the way you breathe before knocking on a door you are afraid of.

I should have stayed. I knew I should have stayed. Orsen had said, in the gentle voice that was worse than shouting, *You're safe here, Sorcha. Safest if you stay hidden.* Safe meant small. Safe meant the loft and the cold meat saved back from supper and the drawings I made on the backs of ruined proofs, my fingers black to the second knuckle. Safe meant being nobody, which was what the note had asked of me — *forget the face* — and which I had agreed to because agreeing was easier than thinking about what it meant, which was that my face was wanted somewhere by people who had already made my village forget its own name.

But Orsen put on the good coat and took the walking stick that was more ornament than need, and said to the empty room, in a voice pitched for no one, "Back before dark," and something in me would not stay folded up in the loft like linen.

I went down the back stair, the one that groaned on the third step, and I skipped the third step, and I slipped out through the yard where the drying line hung with its one grey apron turning slowly like a thing asleep, and I followed Orsen Blackwood into Edinburgh.

* * *

The town was loud in a way I had forgotten towns could be. Glenmara had been loud too, once — gulls and carts and Màiri Dòmhnallach's dog and the smith's hammer counting out the day — but that loudness was dead and this one was alive, and the difference between them was like the difference between a voice and an echo. I kept to walls. I kept my shawl up even though the day was mild, because the shawl was not for warmth but for being unseen, and being unseen was the whole of my trade now. Fish on barrels, silver-eyed. A woman selling heather. Red coats, two of them, at the corner by the coffee-house, laughing at something, their bayonets catching light, and I pressed myself into the shadow of a doorway until my heart slowed and the doorway smelled of old rain and I thought of nothing, nothing, nothing, which is the trick of it — you think of a wall inside your head, and you stand behind the wall.

Orsen walked ahead, unaware or pretending to be, and I marked the route the way I mark everything now: left at the sign of the gilded fish, down a close so narrow the buildings leaned toward each other overhead like gossips, cobbles slick with the leavings of horses, a cat on a sill with its ears flat against the world. Orsen's shoulders told me things. They were up around the ears at the start of the walk and came down, gradually, as we went, as though the town were pulling them lower inch by inch, and by the time we reached the door — a green door, paint gone chalky, brass handle worn to brightness by other hands — Orsen looked almost like a person and not like a held breath.

A brass knocker shaped like a thistle. Orsen lifted it, hesitated, knocked.

And I did the thing I will always be glad of and always be ashamed of, both at once: I crossed the lane and stood in the recessed entry of the house opposite, behind a stack of emptied crates that smelled of salt herring, and I listened.

The door opened. A woman stood there. She was old in the way that trees are old — bent but standing, weathered but alive — with white hair pinned up carelessly and an apron dusted with flour and eyes the colour of strong tea. She looked at Orsen for a long moment without speaking, and then she said, quietly, "You came, then," in the voice of someone who has been expecting bad news for months and is relieved and dismayed in equal measure that it has arrived.

"Mrs. Abernathy." Orsen's voice was low. Even across the lane I heard the care in it, the way you speak near a sleeping animal. "May I—"

"Get in before the whole close sees you standing there like a proclamation." She stepped back. The door began to close.

And then it stopped.

It stopped because I had moved. I don't fully remember deciding to move. My feet made the decision the way they had made decisions under the loom — without consulting me — and suddenly I was crossing the lane and the door was opening again and Mrs. Abernathy was looking down at me, and I was looking up at her, and her face was doing something I had never seen a face do.

She knew me.

Not my face — she couldn't have known my face, I had never seen her, she had never seen me — but she knew something about me, some quality of me, the way I stood or didn't stand or held my chin or hid my hands, and whatever it was went through her like a blade goes through bread, clean and fast, and her mouth opened and no sound came out and her hand rose to her breastbone and pressed there as though holding a door shut against wind.

"What is your name, child?" Her voice had gone strange. Thin. Like paper held to flame just short of burning.

I said nothing. Saying nothing was safe. Saying nothing was the rule. But Orsen had turned, and Orsen's face — I looked at Orsen's face instead of hers, because hers was too much — and Orsen's face said, helplessly, *it's all right*, or perhaps it said *God forgive me*, and either way Mrs. Abernathy had knelt down on her own threshold, flour and all, knees cracking, so that her eyes were level with mine, and she said:

"Are you — you'd be one of the *Murrays*, wouldn't you. Of Glenmara."

My name came out of me before I could stop it. It came out small and cracked and in the old tongue first, the way my mother had said it every morning of my life to wake me, and then in the Lowland way, the way the soldiers had demanded it, and hearing both of them together in my own mouth, on this street, in this year, broke something open in my chest that I had soldered shut.

"Sorcha," I said. "Sorcha Murray."

Mrs. Abernathy wept.

Not politely. Not the way ladies weep in church, one dabbed corner of a handkerchief. She wept the way the sky rains in the west of Scotland, suddenly and totally, her whole face undone, and she pulled me to her — and I let her, God help me, I let her, the first human arms I had allowed since the loom — and over her shoulder I saw Orsen standing on the threshold with one hand still raised toward the door, frozen, looking at the two of us kneeling and clinging on the step like figures on a monument, and Orsen's face was wet too, though Orsen made no sound about it whatsoever.

"I kept the letters," Mrs. Abernathy was saying into my hair, over and over, like a prayer that had lost its sense but not its force. "Eleven letters, and I passed them north, and I kept the names, I keep the names, I have kept every name—" She pulled back and held me at arm's length and studied my face with those tea-coloured eyes streaming. "You'll come in. Both of you. Off the street, in, now, before God and the whole Town Guard."

* * *

Her house was small and smelled of bread and lavender and, underneath both, faintly, of paper — the same smell as the shop, and I understood later that she was a kind of shop herself, a shop that sold nothing and bought everything and dealt only in one commodity, which was remembering.

There was a fire. There was tea, poured thick and dark into cups with flowers worn off them. And there, when she had shut the shutters and drawn a cloth aside in the back room, there were papers. She showed us because Orsen had shown her something first — I did not see what; the two of them spoke in a shorthand of glances and half-finished sentences, two people discovering they had been working for the same master without knowing it — and then she drew the cloth back and beneath it, in a sea chest lined with oilcloth, lay bundles. Letters, tied with string. A ledger. Loose pages covered in a cramped, upright hand.

"Your master wrote to me for six years," she said to Orsen. "Surveys, corrections, gossip, all innocent enough on the face of it. But there were eleven that weren't innocent, and those I carried north myself, wrapped in worsted, in the bottom of a butter firkin, and I'd do it again tomorrow with the whole Hanoverian army watching." She touched the ledger with one finger, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping child. "He trusted me because I'd lost people too. Culloden. My man and my brother both, and the government men came after and burned what records there were of our tenants, as if burning the page burns the man." Her jaw set. "They think if they take the names away they take the people away. That's their whole art, Mr. Blackwood. Everything else is just soldiers being cruel. This"—her finger tapped the ledger—"this is the cruelty that lasts. So I keep names. I've kept them since '46. Every tenant, every child, every croft. Written fair, copied twice, and the copies go where no red coat will ever look."

"How many?" Orsen asked. Orsen's voice had changed. I had heard it soft with me, careful with the maps, dry with the neighbours. This was none of those. This was Orson Blackwood standing at the edge of something.

"In the ledger? Nine hundred and forty." She said the number the way other people say scripture. Then she turned to me, and her voice gentled right down to almost nothing. "And now I'll add the ones from Glenmara. If you'll help me, Sorcha. You don't have to say a word about the rest of it. Only names. Your mother's name. Your father's. The smith. Màiri Dòmhnallach, if she was living still, God rest her and her dog both. Whoever you can call to mind. They'll be written fair, and they'll be copied twice, and long after every officer in that regiment is dust and damned, their names will sit beside yours in a book some bairn will read and think, *these people were real.*"

I looked at the ledger. I looked at the firelight on it, the ruled lines faintly gold, waiting like ploughed ground.

"They burned the parish records first," I said. It was the longest speech I had made in eleven days, and it came out hoarse, rusty, a gate that hadn't swung. "Before the houses. The kirk session books, the registers. An officer stood and watched them burn and he was smiling, and I thought — I thought, he knows. He knows the paper is the village."

Orsen made a sound, very small, like a stitch pulling tight.

"He knows," Mrs. Abernathy said. "So we'll make him a fool of his own cleverness. Ink for ink. You give me the names, child, and I'll write them so deep no fire reaches." 

And so, in the back room of a widow's house in Edinburgh, with the shutters closed and the tea going cold, I gave her the names. My mother's — Catriona, called Katie. My father's — Ewan, who mended nets and sang badly. The smith. Màiri. Old Donald who claimed he remembered the '15. The minister. The minister's three daughters, Flora and Marsaili and the little one, Annag, who was seven. I gave them one by one and Mrs. Abernathy wrote them fair in her upright hand, and each time her pen scratched I felt something leave me that was not grief exactly — grief stayed — but was the loneliness of grief, the sense of carrying the village alone in my one small skull where a fire could wipe it out as easy as wiping out a register.

They were going down on paper now. They were out of me and into the world.

When she finished the page she blew on the ink, and set down the pen, and looked at Orsen, and something passed between them — I saw it, though I was ten and did not have the words for it yet. It was the look of two people realizing they have just become conspirators together, and not shrinking from it.

"The atlas," Mrs. Abernathy said softly. "That's why you came. Aldous left you more than coastlines, didn't he."

Orsen was quiet a moment. Outside, far off, a drum beat — the guard changing, unhurried, certain of its town.

"There may be," Orsen said carefully, "more in the atlas than coastlines. I'm not yet sure what. But a crown historian visited the shop last week. Courteous as a knife. He lingered over one plate in particular." Orsen's hand found the table edge and gripped it. "If Aldous hid testimony in those plates — if that's what got him killed — then whoever finishes the atlas decides what the future gets to know. And they mean it to be finished *their* way."

Mrs. Abernathy sat back. She looked at me. She looked at the fresh page of names. And then she smiled — a fierce, unlikely thing, like frost flowering on a window.

"Then we finish it ours," she said. "Three of us, and the dead for witnesses. You'd better tell me everything, Mr. Blackwood. Start with this historian. Start with his name."

"Shaw," Orsen said. "Mr. Ellery Shaw."

And Mrs. Abernathy, keeper of nine hundred and forty names, went very still, and reached for her pen, and said, "Shaw. Then he goes in the ledger too — under a different column."
