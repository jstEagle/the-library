## 9. Shaw's Offer

The frost came early that year, silvering the windowpanes of the shop until the world outside looked etched—streets and rooftops rendered in the pale ink of winter, as though some greater cartographer had begun sketching the town and then lost interest. Orsen watched the frost climb the glass each morning before touching the atlas, a small ritual of delay, a moment of stillness before the day's careful labor of lying.

For the false plate was nearly done.

They had worked on it the way one works on any penance: slowly, with loathing disguised as precision. The official Glenmara—the one Mr. Ellery Shaw's masters required—showed a prosperous hamlet gathered loyal around the king's standard, its crofts intact, its kirk square and steepled, a company of redcoats drawn up in tidy formation at its edge, banners lifted, order triumphant. Orsen had drawn the smoke rising from a single cottage chimney, domestic and harmless. Had drawn children, because the commission wanted children, a whole generation of them standing in the lane as if the village had never stopped existing. Every line of it was a lie measured to the half-inch, and every morning Orsen sat down to refine the lie further, and beneath the drafting table, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden inside the hollowed base of Vane's old map press, the true plate waited like a heart beating under floorboards.

Sorcha had taken to sitting in the loft above the workroom during the drawing hours, legs dangling through the trapdoor, watching Orsen's hand move across the copper. She did not comment. She simply watched, the way she watched everything now—with the stillness of a creature that has learned that stillness is the price of survival—and when the false village grew too detailed, too populated, too *alive*, she would withdraw without a word and pull the trapdoor shut, and Orsen would hear the soft thump of her retreating into the dark where the drawings she made of the real Glenmara were pinned to the rafters like a congregation of ghosts.

It was on one of these mornings, the frost thick as spun sugar on the panes, that the knock came.

Three raps. Measured. Courteous. Unhurried in the way that only men who have never feared a knock can be unhurried.

Orsen knew before opening the door. Some knocks are weather; you feel them coming in the bones. The shop cat—Vane's cat, a grey torn named Ptolemy who tolerated all human beings on the principle that they existed chiefly to open doors—lifted his head from the hearth and hissed, low and continuous, which he did for no one else. Not for the tax collector. Not for the neighbors. Only, it seemed, for this.

Orsen wiped their hands on their apron, an absurd gesture, as though ink could be mistaken for guilt, and opened the door.

"Mr. Blackwood." Ellery Shaw stood in the cold with his hat in his hands and snow melting on the shoulders of a good wool coat, and he smiled the smile of a man arriving at a friend's house rather than a suspect's. Behind him, at the corner of the wynd, a sedan chair waited with its bearers stamping their feet—but no soldiers this time, no clerk with a ledger. He had come alone. That was worse. "I hope you'll forgive the hour. I find that craftsmen keep early hours, and I wished to catch you at your work."

"Mr. Shaw." Orsen stood in the doorway without moving aside. The cold poured past them both into the shop. "You've traveled far to find me at it."

"Not so far." Shaw glanced past them, into the dim interior—the shelves of rolled surveys, the great flat table, the press, the smell of size and vinegar and lamp oil that clung to everything like a second skin. "Edinburgh is not so far, when the matter is important enough. May I come in? The wind has teeth this morning."

There was no graceful way to refuse a man of the Crown standing on your step with courtesy folded around him like a coat. Orsen stepped back. Shaw entered the way water enters a hull: quietly, finding every low place.

He walked the shop slowly, hands clasped behind him, reading the room the way he read documents—thoroughly, and without any visible appetite. He paused at the shelf where Vane's instruments hung in their places, dividers and scale rules and the long brass sweep of a beam compass, each polished by thirty years of a dead man's hands. He paused at the wall where proofs were pinned. He did not pause at the map press. Orsen noted that. A man who does not look at the thing he came to find has already found it.

"You've made progress," Shaw said at last, nodding toward the worktable, where the false Glenmara lay under weights, its loyalist village gleaming faintly in the lamplight. "May I?"

Orsen lifted the weights. What else could they do?

Shaw bent over the plate with genuine attention, head tilted, and for a long moment said nothing. His finger hovered—not touching, never touching; he understood the sanctity of other men's work—tracing the air above the lane, the kirk, the drawn children.

"Remarkable," he said softly. "You have caught something of Master Vane's hand. I confess, when I first saw his plates, I thought: here is a man who believes a map can love what it describes. There is affection in his lines. And now there is affection in yours." He straightened. "That is rare, Mr. Blackwood. That is very rare indeed."

"It is craft," Orsen said. "Nothing more."

"Oh, I disagree most profoundly." Shaw turned, and the lamplight caught his face and made it briefly unreadable—a face like a coin passed too many times, worn smooth, features rubbed to suggestion. "Craft is what makes it possible. But conviction—that is what makes it good. You believe in what you're drawing." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Which brings me to my purpose."

* * *

He accepted tea because refusing would have been its own statement, and he held the cup without drinking, warming his fingers, while he laid out the future in the reasonable voice of a man discussing the weather over a fence.

"The Commission will publish the atlas," he said. "Under royal warrant. It will be the definitive geography of the Highlands—the record against which all others are measured, for a hundred years, two hundred. Schoolchildren will learn their country from it. Officers will march by it. Judges—" a small smile— "will settle disputes by it. Master Vane understood this. It is why he was given the commission at all."

"He was given nothing," Orsen said. "He bought the commission. With twenty years of work."

"And the Crown honors what it purchases." Shaw set the untouched tea on the corner of the worktable, precisely, deliberately, as though claiming a small territory. "Mr. Blackwood. I will speak plainly, because I believe you are a plain man pretending to be a quiet one, and plainness deserves plainness in return. When the atlas is published, it must carry a name. Vane's name, certainly—he earned the posthumous credit, and posthumous credit costs us nothing. But a dead man cannot answer questions. A dead man cannot defend his methods before Parliament. Someone living must stand behind this work. Someone living must say: *I drew this. This is true.*"

Orsen said nothing. In the loft overhead, they heard—or imagined they heard—the smallest shift of weight, a board settling. They prayed it was the house itself, breathing.

"The Crown offers patronage," Shaw continued. "A stipend of forty pounds a year—fifty, once the atlas is delivered. The title of Royal Geographer, or Geographer to the Commission, whichever phrasing pleases you. Protection from creditors, from litigious neighbors, from"—the smile again, brief and cold as light off a blade—"curious persons of all kinds. Your name on the cover of the most important atlas ever printed in this kingdom. Every apprentice who follows you will study your lines. Every library will hold your name. Is that not what you want, Mr. Blackwood? You have spent—" he glanced around the shop, taking in the shabby gentility of it, the mended windowpane, the single candle burned down to a stub— "you have spent your life making other people visible. Here is the Crown offering to make *you* visible. Permanently."

And there it was. The temptation, laid out like a banquet.

Orsen felt it move through them, warm and shameful, the way brandy moves through a cold body. All those years of the creed—*maps are for other people's histories; stay small, stay unseen, let the famous pass through your shop and leave you their footnotes*—and underneath the creed, all along, the wanting. To be named. To be *on* the map instead of merely making maps. Shaw had found it in ten minutes, the way a pickpocket finds the purse. Forty pounds a year. A title. Their name embossed in gold on calfskin, in libraries Orsen would never see, in centuries Orsen would never live.

"I am flattered," Orsen said, and heard how thin the words came out.

"Do not be flattered. Be accurate." Shaw picked up the tea again, drank at last, a small precise sip. "Accurate. That is the whole of my request, and I make it formally, so that there can be no misunderstanding later. The atlas must be completed accurately. The Commission has established what occurred at Glenmara, and the atlas must reflect the Commission's findings. A loyal settlement. A lawful action. An unfortunate but necessary episode, concluded with restraint." He set the cup down. "Render it accurately, Mr. Blackwood, and everything I have described is yours. Refuse—" he spread his hands, regretful, reasonable— "well. Then we must ask ourselves whether an unfinished atlas ought to be finished at all, or whether the Commission should entrust it to another hand entirely. There are men in Edinburgh who would leap at the work. Men with less... delicacy than you."

The threat arrived dressed as logistics. Orsen almost admired it.

"And if I finish it as you ask," Orsen said carefully, "what becomes of the master's own plates? His drafts. His papers."

Shaw's eyes came up, and for the first time all the pleasantness drained out of them, leaving something patient and bottomless, a well you could drop a stone into and never hear it land.

"Confiscated material is the property of the Crown," he said. "As you know better than anyone, having witnessed the search yourself. Why?" A tilt of the head, mild as milk. "Is there something among his papers you particularly miss?"

* * *

He rose to go, and it was then—at the door, hat in hand, snow whirling past the frame into the warm air of the shop—that he mentioned the child.

He did not mention the child. That was the artistry of it.

"I saw the strangest thing on my way up the wynd," he said, conversational, gazing out at the street as though admiring the weather. "Small footprints in the new snow. Coming toward the shop, not away from it. Made sometime in the night, I'd judge, or very early—they were already filling in." He turned, and smiled, and the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which was the worst part; the eyes meant it. "Of course, this being a shop, footprints mean little. Deliveries. Customers. Cats, even." He settled the hat onto his head. "But I did think, as I stood there: what a curious thing, that a shop which keeps such irregular hours should show so few visitors. Almost as though someone wished not to be seen coming or going."

"The wynd is narrow," Orsen said. "Snow falls in it from the eaves. Footprints fill in fast."

"Don't they just." Shaw pulled on his gloves, finger by finger, unhurried. "I mention it only because I care about accuracy, Mr. Blackwood. It is my profession, as mapmaking is yours. And an inaccurate account—one that omits a person, a detail, a small footprint in the snow—tends to unravel. Not today. Perhaps not this month. But records have a way of being checked against reality, and reality is stubborn stuff." He stepped over the threshold into the cold, then paused, half-turned. "Master Vane learned that lesson late. Do learn it early."

Then he was gone, down the wynd, his footsteps crisp and unhurried on the frozen cobbles, and the sedan chair bore him away toward the town and the Commission rooms where the official truth was being assembled sheet by sheet, and Orsen stood in the doorway until the cold drove them back, and shut the door, and leaned against it.

The shop was silent. Too silent. Then, above, the trapdoor creaked, and Sorcha came down the ladder slowly, one rung at a time, her eyes enormous, her hands white-knuckled on the rails.

"He knows," she whispered. "About me. He knows."

"He suspects," Orsen said. "There is a difference, and right now that difference is the only ground we stand on." They crossed to the hearth and fed a log to the fire, needing the motion, needing anything to do with their hands that wasn't trembling. "Listen to me. From today, you don't use the stairs after dark. You don't go near the windows. If anyone knocks when I'm out, you don't breathe—you sit in the map chest, and you wait, however long it takes."

"In the chest," Sorcha repeated. Her voice was flat. She had been hidden in enough things—carts, ditches, haylofts, the space between a dead woman's skirts and a stone wall—to know exactly what being hidden cost.

"In the chest," Orsen said. "I'm sorry."

Sorcha looked at them for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she nodded—not the nod of a frightened child obeying, but the nod of a soldier accepting orders she has already anticipated. Whatever Glenmara had left in her, it was not helplessness.

"And the plate," she said. "The true one. He'll come back for it. When he comes back, he won't bring tea."

"No," Orsen agreed. "He won't."

They returned to the worktable, where the false village lay gleaming under the lamp—loyal, lawful, concluded with restraint—and Orsen looked down at it and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the choice Shaw had offered was no choice at all. Patronage or ruin. Visibility or erasure. Take their name and sign the lie, or refuse and watch the atlas pass into hands that would bury Glenmara so deep even grief couldn't find it.

Unless.

Orsen's gaze moved—slowly, casually, a glance lasting no longer than a breath—to the map press in the corner, and to the hollow at its base where the true plate slept in its wrapping of oilcloth. Unless there were three roads instead of two. Unless a man could accept the Crown's commission and betray it, plate by plate, line by line, hiding a murdered village inside the very document meant to erase it.

Shaw thought he was buying a craftsman. Let him buy one. Let him pay his fifty pounds a year and hang his name in gold and sleep soundly in Edinburgh, believing that paper obeys whoever pays for it.

Paper, Orsen thought, picking up the burin, obeys the hand.

Outside, the snow began again, filling in the footprints in the wynd, filling in the world, soft and white and thorough—and beneath it, unseen, every road and river and buried village lay waiting, exactly where the maps said they would be.
