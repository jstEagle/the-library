## 10. The Plate That Lies

The false plate began with a lie about light.

That was the first thing Orsen understood, sitting before the copper at dawn with Shaw's instructions folded in the drawer beneath their elbow like a knife kept for cutting bread. Every map was an argument about light—where it fell, what it revealed, what it permitted itself to illuminate. Vane had taught them that in the third month of apprenticeship, tapping the margin of an old Dutch chart: *Look how the engraver shades the northern coast, boy. He has never been there. He imagines a sun that flatters the King.* And young Orsen had laughed, and Vane had not.

Now Orsen imagined a sun that flattered the Crown.

They began as they always began—with the coastline. Glenmara's true coast ran ragged as torn linen, indented with creeks where smuggling boats had slipped for generations, where the villagers had moored their little fleet in the lee of a headland the surveyors called simply *the Hook*. On the false plate, Orsen smoothed all of it. A gentle curve, untroubled, a coast with nothing to hide and nowhere to hide anything. They drew the village itself larger than it had been, neater, arranged around a kirk square that did not exist, with tidy fields running to the shore in obedient strips. A loyal village. A prosperous one. A village that had, on the fourteenth of March, welcomed His Majesty's troops with bread and salt, according to the official account—or rather, a village whose nonexistence as a site of resistance could be inscribed so deeply into copper that no future hand would think to question it.

Orsen's burin moved steadily. Their hands did not shake. That surprised them more than anything else—that grief and fury could be held so far below the skin that the fingers stayed calm, the way a loch stays glass over thirty fathoms of drowning dark. They worked the way Vane had taught them to work: slowly enough that no line needed correcting, cleanly enough that the plate might pass any examination.

*Any examination.*

Shaw would examine it. Shaw, who lingered. Shaw, who touched things. Shaw, whose soft voice made courtesy feel like a hand closing around your wrist.

So Orsen drew the lie beautifully—and beneath it, they built the truth.

* * *

The idea had come to them three nights earlier, in the hour when the candle burned low and the shop smelled of resin and cold iron, and it had come not as inspiration but as memory—the memory of Vane's own hands, years ago, holding a proof up to the window of the back room.

*Do you see?* he'd said. *Two engravings on one plate. The printer's trick. Strike the first image shallow—barely a whisper in the metal—then lay the true design deep above it. In ordinary light, the eye takes only the strong lines. But hold it to a lamp, tilt it, let the light rake across at an angle—* and the ghost image had risen out of the paper like something surfacing through water, faint but unmistakable, a second map sleeping inside the first.

He had shown it to Orsen as a curiosity. A demonstration of craft. *Every engraving is a palimpsest if you make it one,* he'd said. *Copper remembers everything you do to it. It never forgets a line; it can only be persuaded to keep quiet.*

Vane had known. Orsen was certain of it now, as certain as they were of the grain of the master's handwriting. The old man had gathered testimony from Glenmara—had carried it home in his satchel, had hidden it in ciphered marginalia—and then he had waited, patient as frost, for the atlas commission that never came, or came instead as a murderer's knock. He had left his apprentice a weapon disguised as an heirloom.

And now Orsen would forge it.

The work took four days. Four days of working twice upon every inch of copper, which meant thinking twice upon every inch of Glenmara. The upper design—the lie—went in deep, confident, the way official truths are always engraved: bold strokes, generous shading, a cartouche in the lower corner reading *GLENMARA, SURVEYED ANNO 1745*, with flourishes Orsen cut with real skill and real contempt. Below it, in lines so shallow they barely broke the surface, went the truth.

The true Glenmara was smaller. Orsen knew its dimensions now better than they knew their own heartbeat, because Sorcha had drawn it—not once but eleven times, always the same, her charcoal moving with the terrible fluency of memory that cannot be lied out of a body. Eleven drawings, identical in every particular: the cluster of nineteen houses along the burn, the mill with its broken wheel, the byre where she had hidden, the path up through the bracken to the shepherd's cot. Nineteen houses. Orsen counted them onto the copper in whisper-lines, each roof a hair's breadth deep in the metal, and thought: *each of these was someone's winter.*

The forest went in differently too. On the false plate, the wood south of the village was rendered as open, healthy stand—scattered trees, parklike, the kind of ground troops move through easily. On the true layer, beneath, Orsen crowded it dense, the tree-symbols pressed close as teeth, because density in Vane's cipher was counting, and Sorcha had told them—only once, in a voice like a dropped stone—how many had run into those trees and how many had come out. Orsen did not write the number anywhere. The number lived in the spacing of the symbols, exactly as Vane's cipher demanded, legible only to whoever had learned the code from the master's marginalia. Legible, perhaps, only to Orsen. For now.

By the fourth night the plate held two villages. One was visible to anyone who looked. The other waited in the metal like breath held underwater.

* * *

There was a discipline to deception that Orsen had never suspected, and learning it felt like being apprenticed all over again—to a darker master.

A forger, they discovered, could not afford hatred. Hatred made the hand heavy; heaviness made lines that a trained eye would read as strain. So Orsen practiced a kind of deliberate emptiness while working the false plate, breathing slow, letting the burin sing its small continuous note against the copper, refusing to think of Captain Hesse's name even though Hesse's name was the reason the kirk on the false plate stood intact where in truth it had been put to the torch with people barred inside. Refusing to think of it. Cutting the lie straight.

Then, when the daylight work was done and the shutters closed and the candle hooded behind the tin screen, they turned the plate and cut the truth—and there the discipline inverted. For the truth required delicacy too. Too deep, and it would shadow through under ordinary light; some clerk with sharp eyes might catch a ghost-line crossing the wrong way through a field. Too shallow, and no lamp would raise it. Orsen tested constantly, printing proofs on scrap and holding them to the flame, tilting, tilting, until the second village rose and fell with the angle of light like a face appearing and submerging in dark water.

Proof by proof, night by night, the thing became beautiful—which frightened Orsen worse than anything Shaw had said. Because beauty was the whole trick. The lie had to be beautiful enough that no one would look beneath it. And the truth had to be beautiful enough that when it finally surfaced—in a commission chamber, under lamplight, before men paid to disbelieve it—it would strike the eye with the authority of art itself. Not a scribbled accusation. Not sedition scrawled on a wall. A masterpiece of cartography, bearing the unmistakable hand-discipline of Aldous Vane, containing within its own copper a second hand-discipline that no honest craftsman could have produced by accident.

Let them call it forgery, Orsen thought, wiping the plate clean at midnight, arms aching, fingertips silvered with copper dust. Let them call it treason. A map that told the truth twice was still a map. The land did not stop existing because the King preferred another version of it. The dead of Glenmara did not unknot themselves from the earth because a historian in London ruled them inconvenient.

They were forging, yes. But the original was the massacre report. The original was the empty space on the official map where nineteen houses had been. Everything Orsen did now was merely restoration—repairing the record the way one repairs a damaged plate, hammering out the lie from the reverse side.

Cold work. Slow work. They had perhaps two weeks—perhaps less—before Shaw returned to inspect the progress, before the commission arrived in town to collect its sanitized testimonies, before witnesses who remembered too much finished disappearing into the administrative silence that had already swallowed Mrs. Abernathy's letters and, Orsen feared, would soon swallow Mrs. Abernathy herself.

Two weeks to finish a lie and hide a resurrection inside it.

Orsen blew out the candle, and in the dark the copper seemed to hold a faint warmth, as if the two villages were still arguing.

* * *

Sorcha watched from the loft stair, as she watched everything now.

Orsen knew she was there without looking—knew it by the quality of the quiet, the way a fisherman knows a shadow under the boat. The child had stopped hiding weeks ago in any meaningful sense; she had simply relocated her hiding to places from which she could observe, which Orsen suspected was not so different from how Orsen had lived the whole of their life. Two creatures of the margins, keeping watch on each other across a workshop full of dangerous paper.

On the fifth evening, Orsen printed the first complete proof.

It had to be done properly—a full impression, careful ink, dampened paper pressed in the old press Vane had bought secondhand from a bankrupt Edinburgh printer and rebuilt with his own hands. Orsen worked with the door barred and Sorcha standing beside them for the first time, close enough that Orsen could feel the child's breath, quick and shallow, against their sleeve.

The press groaned. The roller whispered. And then Orsen lifted the blanket and peeled back the paper, and there it was in fresh black ink: the false Glenmara, serene and loyal and immaculate, its invented kirk square gleaming with fictitious prosperity, its smoothed coastline promising nothing had ever happened here worth remembering.

"Look," Orsen said quietly. "This is the one Mr. Shaw will see. This is the map the commissioners will put in their book."

Sorcha stared at it. Her face did something complicated—her jaw tightened, her eyes went narrow, and for a moment Orsen feared she might spit on it, and could not entirely have blamed her.

"Now watch." Orsen carried the damp proof to the candle, tilted it, let the flame-light rake low across the paper. "Watch the wood."

The light moved. And the second village rose.

It came up slowly, the way things surface in dreams—first the dense tree-line, then the true crooked course of the burn, then the nineteen roofs in their nineteen places, faint as breath on glass but there, unmistakably there, a whole village living inside the lie of itself. The mill wheel. The byre. The path through the bracken.

Sorcha made a sound Orsen had never heard from her—not a word, not quite a cry, something between the two, the sound of a lock turning after a long time rusted shut. Her hand came up and hovered over the paper without touching it, the way she hovered over everything, as if the world were still hot enough to burn.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's—" Her finger found the byre. Held there. "I was there."

"I know."

"You put it *inside*." She looked up at Orsen, and her eyes were wet and furious and lit with something Orsen could only call ferocity, small and ten years old and absolutely undimmed. "Inside the lying one. Where they won't look."

"Where they'll never think to look." Orsen set the proof down flat on the bench, weighting the corners, and the two villages lay together in the lamplight—one shouting, one silent. "Your master Vane taught me that copper never forgets. I'm only making sure the paper doesn't either." They hesitated, then said the rest of it, because the child deserved the whole architecture of the plan, however fragile. "When the commission comes, I'll show them the false plate. And then I'll ask for a candle. And I'll tilt it. And everyone in that room will see what you saw just now."

"And then?"

Orsen had asked themselves the same question every night that week, and had arrived each time at the same honest answer. "And then we find out whether ink is stronger than soldiers. I don't know, Sorcha. I truly don't. But a record is a stubborn thing once it exists. It gets copied. It gets argued over. It waits. Even if they burn this shop tomorrow, the plates will have spoken somewhere, to someone. Memory isn't a place. It's a practice. Your mistress—" Orsen stopped. Corrected. "Mrs. Abernathy used to say that. *Memory is a practice.* Like penmanship. You keep doing it until the hand learns it."

Sorcha looked back down at the proof, at the small ghost-village rising and sinking with the tremble of the candleflame. When she spoke again her voice had gone very quiet, very level, older than ten.

"They burned the papers first," she said. "Before the houses. Did I tell you that? The captain made them bring out the kirk records and the rent rolls and the school books, and he burned them in the square, and he said—" her throat worked— "he said, *now there's no proof you were ever here at all.*"

Orsen's hands went still on the bench.

"No," they said softly. "You hadn't told me that."

And Sorcha drew a breath—the long, gathering breath of someone standing at the edge of deep water—and began, at last, to tell it all.

* * *

Outside, snow had begun again, fine and dry, ticking against the shutters like a slow clock counting down the days until Shaw's return. Down the street, a lantern swung past the window and did not stop—but Orsen, listening with half an ear while the child spoke, noted that it paused at the corner, a long moment, before moving on.

Someone was learning the rhythm of the shop. Someone was patient.

Orsen banked the fire, and let Sorcha talk, and began to count the hours.
