## 11. Sorcha Speaks

The loft above the map shop had its own weather. Rain found the north slates first and ran down in silver threads that Sorcha could watch from her nest of blankets, counting them the way she had once counted sheep on the shoulder of Glenmara's hill—though she did not let herself think of Glenmara, not often, not on purpose. Thinking of it was like putting her hand into a fire to check whether the fire was still there. It always was.

She had been in the shop for weeks now. Long enough that she knew Orsen's days the way she knew the psalm the minister used to mumble, all rhythm and no meaning until suddenly one line would catch and shine. Orsen rose before the light and built the fire small, because wood was dear. Orsen ground ink at the same window each morning, where the gray came up over the rooftops like a tide. Orsen talked to the dead master sometimes—*your coastlines are crooked, Aldous, and I will not fix them*—in a voice that was half scolding and half something softer Sorcha had no name for yet.

Sorcha watched all of it from the loft ladder's top step, chin on her knees, and said almost nothing. Words were heavy things. She had carried silence so long it had grown a shell around her, like a whelk, and everything true sat inside it, dark and salt.

But tonight the rain was loud, and the lamp below burned late, and Orsen was doing the thing that undid her: drawing Glenmara.

She could see it upside down from the loft—the plate tilted on the easel, the village rendered as cartography, all careful lines and little italic labels. *Kirk.* *Mill lade.* *Well.* Each label went through her like a needle finding the exact old wound. Because Orsen drew the village as it stood, and Sorcha alone knew what stood there now, which was nothing, which was less than nothing, which was ash raked flat and a new road laid across it as if the road had always been there and no village ever had.

She came down the ladder before she decided to. Her feet made the decision; she only followed them.

Orsen looked up, surprised but not startled—they had passed beyond startling, she and Orsen, somewhere around the night she'd stopped sleeping under the map chest and started sleeping in the loft like a person instead of a cargo. "You should be abed," Orsen said gently. "It's late even for mapmakers."

"You've drawn the well wrong." Sorcha's voice came out rusty, a hinge unopened since autumn. "It wasn't beside the kirk path. It was behind the MacAllister byre, under the rowan. The kirk path well dried up years ago. My mother drew water there every morning and complained about the walk every morning, and—" Her throat closed. She pressed on anyway, because the words were moving now and might not move again for months. "You've drawn a lie without meaning to."

Orsen set down the pen. Very carefully, the way Orsen did everything that mattered, as though care were a kind of prayer. "Then tell me how it was."

And that—that simple asking, that patient lamplight face—was what broke the shell.

* * *

"It wasn't only the killing."

Sorcha sat on the stool by the fire now, wrapped in Orsen's good coat, a cup of milk warm between her palms that she had no intention of drinking. She had learned in this house that you could be handed warmth and simply hold it, and no one would press you. That was one of the things that had made words possible tonight.

"Everyone, when they ask—and nobody asks me, but I hear them talk, the printer's boy, the neighbors—they think the killing is the whole of it. Soldiers come, shots, fires, done. A story with a middle and an end. But that's not—" She searched for it. "That's not what happened. Not mostly."

Orsen waited. Waiting was Orsen's great gift, Sorcha had come to understand. The master had been the talker, from what his letters showed; Orsen was the listener, the one who kept things.

"They came at dawn on a Tuesday. Market day, so folk were already stirring. I was sent to the Hendersons' shieling up the glen with eggs—I'm not meant to know any of what I know, do you see? I heard it more than saw it. And then I didn't hear it anymore because my ears had gone wrong, the way they do after a close thunderclap." She stared into the fire. The fire stared back, indifferent, and she hated it a little and needed it entirely. "When I came down—it was evening by then, I hid, I hid the way you hide when your body knows before your mind does—the village was still standing. That's the part nobody believes. It was still standing. Burning, some of it, but most of it standing. The kirk. The mill. Our house with its door open."

The milk trembled in the cup. She set it on the hearthstone.

"The officer in charge—he was called Captain Loxley, red coat, young face, older eyes, he had a voice like he was reading out a shopping list—he gave orders. And the orders weren't about people, because there were hardly any people left to order anything about. The orders were about paper."

Orsen had gone very still.

"The session records first." Sorcha's voice steadied strangely, the way a rope goes taut just before it bears weight. "Every parish keeps them—baptisms, marriages, deaths, who paid what, who sinned when, who was forgiven. Mr. Beath the minister kept ours in a kist in the manse, forty years of them, maybe more. Grandmother's marriage was in there. My brothers' baptisms. Everything that proved we had names. They took the kist out into the kirkyard and burned it on the graves, and Captain Loxley stood with his hands behind his back while it burned, and he said—" Sorcha made her voice into his voice, flat and tidy, a voice she had been unable to stop carrying: "'A village that cannot show it exists need never have existed.'"

The fire popped. Neither of them moved.

"Then the estate papers at the factor's house. Then the schoolmaster's registers. Then the tenants' own papers—leases, tacks, rent receipts, letters. Men going door to door with sacks, very polite, taking every scrap of writing in Glenmara and piling it in the mill and setting it alight in the kiln, so it would burn hot and complete. They spent longer on the burning than they had on the shooting. Hours. It was organized, like harvest work. Like they'd practiced."

"Why?" Orsen asked softly, though Sorcha could see in Orsen's face that the answer was already assembling itself there.

"Because dead men can't testify, but paper can." She said it the way she'd finally understood it, weeks of turning it over in the dark until it fit her hand. "You can kill a village and call it a battle gone wrong, call it rebels shot in the fray, and if anyone asks—where are the births? Where are the graves, where are the leases proving these families farmed that land for two hundred years?—there's nothing. No record, no village. They weren't destroying evidence of a crime, Master Orsen." Her grandmother's word for courtesy slipped out, absurd and precious. "They were destroying evidence of us. The crime isn't proven unless we were real."

Silence held the room. The rain worked the slates. Somewhere below the street a cart passed, wheels hissing through water, going somewhere warm.

Sorcha found she was not done. The words kept coming now like water finding a channel long dammed, and she let them, because stopping hurt worse.

"I saved what I could. That journal—the satchel—I took it from the manse steps where Mr. Beath had left it, or dropped it, I don't know, there was blood—" Her breath hitched; she rode past it. "But I also took other things. Small things. Papers from houses that hadn't been reached yet. A lease with my grandfather's name. A christening gown's lace, wrapped round a lock of hair in my mother's hand saying whose it was, written small, like a label on a specimen." Her mouth did something that wanted to be a smile. "I've still got them. Sewn in my spare shift hem. In case you ever wondered why I won't let you wash that shift."

"Sorcha." Orsen's voice had gone rough. "I never once wondered."

"I thought you'd have found them. You find everything in this house."

"Not everything. Not what people keep stitched next to their skin." Orsen leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a long moment studied her with those cartographer's eyes that measured distances and coastlines and, apparently, children. "Do you know what you're telling me?"

"That they burned it first. Before anything else. Before the fires finished, before the bodies were buried even—the burying party was still digging when the mill kiln went up. Records first. Always records first. If you want the truth of Glenmara, don't look for the massacre." She swallowed. "Look for the bonfire of everything that came after."

* * *

Afterward—or during, Sorcha couldn't have said where one thing ended and the next began, because grief and speech had blurred together like colors in wet ink—Orsen rose and went to the great plan chest against the north wall, the one with the brass handles worn bright, and unlocked it with the key from the chain at Orsen's neck.

Sorcha had seen the plates before. She had watched Orsen engrave them, hold them to candlelight, mutter at them. But she had never thought about them the way Orsen was thinking about them now, lifting the Glenmara proof and laying it flat on the table with both hands, like a thing being born or buried.

"Say it again," Orsen said. "The captain's words. Exactly."

"'A village that cannot show it exists need never have existed.'"

"And they burned the session records first. On the graves."

"In the kirkyard, yes."

Orsen turned away and stood at the window a long time, watching nothing, the rain making slow rivers of the lamplight on the glass. When Orsen spoke again, it was half to Sorcha and half to the dark street below, or perhaps to the absent dead master, whose presence in this room Sorcha had long since stopped finding strange.

"Aldous knew. He must have known—that's what his cipher is *for*. Look here—" Orsen's finger came down on the proof, on the fine engraved marks Sorcha had taken for decoration. "I thought these were conventional symbols. Rock hatching, scrub, marsh tussock. But count them with me. The density of the forest east of the village—one stroke per household in the tax rolls Vane copied before they were seized. The curve of the shore spelling out names in the old way he taught me, initial by initial. The kirk drawn with eleven windowsill marks—eleven, Sorcha, and I could never think why eleven, and now I think of eleven graves I haven't counted." Orsen's voice cracked on the last of it, quietly, and was gathered back. "He wasn't drawing a map of a village. He was drawing a *register*. Baptisms and marriages and deaths, hidden in geography, where no soldier would think to burn it. Every witness testimony folded into a coastline. He made the land itself remember what the paper was made to forget."

Sorcha slid off the stool and came to stand beside Orsen, and together they bent over the plate, her small head and Orsen's bowed one, the lamplight pooling gold on copper and ink.

"There," she said, and touched the rowan tree Orsen had drawn near the wrong-well place. "If you put the well behind the byre, under the rowan—then the tree line reads right. Grandfather's name. I told you it spells names."

Orsen looked at her. Something moved across Orsen's face that Sorcha would spend years learning to name: not pity, never pity, thank God, but recognition. The look of one keeper meeting another.

"You understand what this means," Orsen said slowly. "What you told me changes everything—not just what we know, but what we can prove. Shaw and his masters aren't merely hiding a massacre. They've built a whole machinery for unmaking villages—burn the records, then write histories over the empty ground, and there is nothing left to contradict them. Nothing official. Nothing legal." Orsen tapped the plate. "Except this. Except what Vane hid in it, and what you carried out in a satchel and a shift hem. We aren't holding evidence of a crime, Sorcha. We're holding the proof that Glenmara existed at all. This atlas isn't testimony." Orsen's voice dropped to nearly nothing. "It's resurrection."

Sorcha felt the truth of it settle into her chest, heavy and warm at once, like a coal wrapped in cloth. All those weeks she had thought she was carrying evidence—a lawyer's bundle, a case to be argued by important people in important rooms. But it was bigger than courtrooms. Somewhere in Edinburgh, men were burning paper so that a village could be unwritten, and here in a drafty garret in a map shop, a grieving apprentice and a ten-year-old girl were writing it back, stroke by stroke, name by name, in the one language the victors never thought to censor: the shape of the land itself.

"They'll come for it," she said. She didn't know how she knew, only that she did, the way her body had known to hide on that Tuesday long before her mind caught up. "They came for the paper in Glenmara. They'll come for yours."

"Then we'd best make sure it isn't here when they do," Orsen said—but lightly, too lightly, the way people speak when they are filing a thought away to be frightened of later. "Now. Tell me about the well again, from the beginning, and this time I shall draw it properly. And then the byre, and the rowan, and every house I've got wrong. Tonight, Sorcha, you and I are going to redraw a village."

So they worked, deep into the rain-dark hours: the child remembering and the engraver recording, the needle whispering through copper, names surfacing out of hatchings like drowned things rising gently to be counted. Sorcha spoke until her voice wore thin and then spoke past that, giving up her dead one at a time—Callum MacAllister, the Hendersons, the minister Beath who had died reaching for his kist—each name going down into the metal where no fire could follow.

It was near three in the morning when they finally blew out the lamp, and Sorcha, climbing back to her loft, paused on the ladder and looked down at the shop below: the plates stacked safe in the locked chest, the proofs laid out to dry, the whole sleeping archive of a murdered village.

She was almost asleep when she heard Orsen still awake below, moving quietly, and peeked through the slats to see Orsen at the window with the curtain drawn back a finger's width, looking down the long empty street toward the harbor, motionless, listening.

Watching for the sound of boots, Sorcha thought, and slept anyway, because she had learned in this house that some vigils were love.

She did not know that three streets away, in a room at the King's Arms, a courteous man with older-than-young eyes sat beneath his own lamp with a stolen letter spread flat before him, tracing Vane's marginalia with one pale fingertip—and that he had just, at last, understood the forest hatching.

He smiled, and reached for his hat.
