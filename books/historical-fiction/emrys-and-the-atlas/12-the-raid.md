## 12. The Raid

The morning came in grey and ordinary, the way terrible mornings do.

I remember it best by its smallness: the smell of ink left open overnight, the drip of the eaves from rain that had ended before dawn, Sorcha's chalk dust on my cuff when I bent to light the lamp. She had been drawing at the workbench since first light—she drew everything now, as though paper were a country she could retreat into—and I had stopped asking what she drew. Some of it was Glenmara. Some of it was the shop. All of it was truer than anything I could render with forty years of training, and that fact sat in me like a swallowed stone.

I had slept badly. The printer's bargain was three days old and already souring in my mind—not because I regretted it, but because every arrangement of that kind is a rope bridge over a gorge, and you only find out how rotten it is by walking it. I had paid Callum Frazer half of Vane's silver for the promise of twelve hidden impressions of the true Glenmara plate, struck by night, unrecorded, deniable. The other half waited in the tin behind Vane's old copy of Blaeu. And all I possessed to guarantee the arrangement was Frazer's shrug and his yellow smile and the words *every man has his price, Blackwood; mine is simply lower than most*.

So I was tired, and slow, and perhaps that is why I did not hear the boots until they were already on the cobbles outside—or why, hearing them, I mistook them for carters. Six men make a particular sound on stone, a rhythm like a slow drum, and there was another sound beneath it, metallic and patient, that I identified far too late as scabbards knocking against thighs.

"Orsen." Sorcha's voice. Very quiet. She had gone still over her drawing, head tilted, the way she listened to things I could not hear—a survivor's ear, tuned in a burning village to footsteps that meant one thing. "That's soldiers."

The world narrowed. I have read in books that fear makes men bold or cowardly, but no book ever described what it actually does, which is to make everything very clear and very fast while your hands remain absurdly slow, fumbling at latches you have opened ten thousand times. I looked at the door. I looked at the stairs to the loft. I looked at the map chest.

The chest was Vane's great iron-banded oak, older than either of us, big enough to hold a rolled folio plate or a folded surveyor's tent—and, as some merciful instinct informed me, big enough to hold a thin child who had learned in the worst year of her life how to be small and silent and airless. We had spoken of this once, obliquely, in the dark weeks after Mrs. Abernathy had warned us strangers were asking about the shop. *If anyone comes,* I had said, not looking at her, pretending to study a coastline, *the loft has its hiding place, but if there isn't time—the chest.* And she had nodded, once, the way soldiers nod, and said nothing, because we both understood that a household which rehearses its own catastrophe is a household that expects it.

There wasn't time for the loft. There was barely time for the chest.

"In," I said, and she went—no tears, no argument, only a flicker of those enormous eyes and then she folded herself down among the rolled charts like water finding its level. I laid the false bottom of survey linens over her, then two great rolls of vellum, then closed the lid and threw the draped printing apron across it and scattered Vane's burnishing tools on top, so the chest became merely furniture, merely clutter, merely the debris of a dead man's trade.

The knock came even as I straightened. Three blows, official and flat, the kind of knock that does not wait to be answered twice.

I made myself breathe once, fully, before I opened the door. It is a trick I learned from watching Vane face difficult customers: the body lies better when the lungs are supplied.

* * *

They were not redcoats. That surprised me, and the surprise frightened me more, because it meant someone had chosen the instrument carefully. A civil sergeant in a drab coat, a parchment-faced clerk carrying the writ, and four men whose coats were plain but whose boots were army boots, whose eyes swept the shop the way a sweep of scales passes over fish—pricing, sorting, dismissing. Tax enforcement. Revenue men. The pretext, when the sergeant read it out in a voice like a man reciting someone else's shopping list, was arrears owed on the premises since Michaelmas: eleven pounds, seven shillings, claimed against the estate of Aldous Vane, deceased, and against his executor.

"That debt was discharged in March," I said. "I have the receipt."

"I'm sure ye do." The sergeant's gaze did not change. "Ye'll show us the premises regardless. Where goods are kept. Where papers are kept."

Papers. The word dropped through me like cold water through a floorboard gap. Not goods. Papers.

I performed the small theatre of indignation—I let my voice rise, I demanded names, I invoked Vane's guild standing and the name of our solicitor, and all the while my heart hammered out the arithmetic of the room. The ciphered plates lay where they had lain for a fortnight now, in plain sight, because plain sight was their armour: the false Glenmara plate pinned to the drying rack where any visitor might see it, its loyalist village rendered neat and innocent, its little church flying the wrong flag of gratitude. Beneath it, in the shallow drawer of the workbench, the true plate—the palimpsest, the resurrection—wrapped in a chart of the Hebrides as though it were nothing but geography. And scattered across every surface, deliberately, the honest clutter of a working mapmaker: coastlines, meridians, soundings, nothing.

The plates were safe precisely because I had spent weeks making the shop look like exactly what it appeared to be. Whether luck or craft preserved them, I could not then have told you. I only knew that my hands shook as I pretended to be angry rather than afraid, and that the shaking served the performance admirably.

"Mind the chest," I said, as one of the soldiers drifted toward it. "Iron bands, and the lid drops true. It's taken fingers before."

He gave it a kick, testing, and the sound it made—hollow, dense, indifferent—was the most terrible sound I have ever heard, because beneath it, under three inches of oak and a layer of linen and a roll of vellum, a child was holding her breath, and I could do nothing, nothing, but stand there and keep talking about receipts.

* * *

They worked the shop the way ants work a carcass: methodically, without malice, without interest. The clerk followed at the sergeant's shoulder making entries in a ledger, and I understood quickly that the ledger was the point—that this was never about eleven pounds seven shillings, that somewhere a careful mind had decided that the surest way to gut a house is to come at it sideways, with paperwork, so the occupant cannot cry murder because no murder is being done.

They went through Vane's desk. They went through the drawers of the plan chest, handling the finished plates with fingers I wanted to bite. They went up the ladder to the loft, and I stood below listening to boots cross the boards overhead, each footfall driving a nail of dread into me, knowing that above them, beyond a low door I had shown them freely, was the pallet where Sorcha slept, the blanket with its worn corner she rubbed between finger and thumb, the little hoard of chalk sticks wrapped in rag. If they found a child's things in a childless shop—

But Sorcha existed nowhere in that room except inside the chest, and whatever the loft held, it held nothing with a name attached. Children leave less evidence than ghosts. They had taught her that, whoever *they* were; the world had taught her that long before she came to my door.

"Letters here," one of them called down, and my stomach turned over.

Vane's correspondence. Two years of it, bundled and boxed on the shelf behind the counter—letters from surveyors and suppliers, from guild masters, from Mrs. Abernathy's careful hand, from correspondents whose names I had never troubled myself to learn because a mapmaker's mail is mostly measurements. But Vane had been gathering testimony in those last months. I knew that now. Somewhere in those bundles, interleaved with invoices, were the voices of people who had seen Glenmara, written in a hand I would know anywhere, and the soldiers gathered the boxes like harvesters cutting a field, all of it, indiscriminately, the innocuous and the damning together.

"That correspondence is part of the estate," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "It will be needed for the commission's inquiry. Master Vane's materials are evidence, Sergeant. You'd be removing evidence."

The sergeant looked at me for the first time—really looked, a long flat look with something almost like sympathy in it, or perhaps only professional boredom. "Evidence for whom?" he said.

And there it was, the whole age in six syllables. Evidence belongs to the victor. History is written by the side holding the boxes.

I watched them carry Vane's letters out into the grey morning, and something in my chest went with them—not despair, exactly, but a cold recalibration. Let them take it. Shaw would sit in some warm room decoding my master's marginalia letter by letter, and he would find what he was looking for, because he was a patient man and the letters were patient too. Unless Vane had been cleverer than even I now understood. Unless the letters, like the atlas, said one thing on their surface and another thing in their bones.

You may think me a fool for finding comfort in that possibility. But I had begun, by then, to understand what kind of man I had apprenticed myself to, and I no longer believed Aldous Vane had ever written a single uncomplicated sentence in his life.

* * *

The search lasted two hours. When it was done, the sergeant presented the clerk's receipt for removal—*assorted correspondence, two boxes; sundry papers, one satchel*—and required my mark, and I signed, because refusing the mark of a mouse does not stop the trap from closing.

Then they were gone, boots fading along the cobbles, and the shop was silent in the peculiar ringing way of rooms after violence has passed through them without quite landing. Things were moved—everything was moved—but nothing broken except, I noticed much later, my composure, permanently.

I crossed to the chest. My hands were not steady. I lifted the apron, the tools, the vellum, the linens.

"Sorcha."

For a moment, nothing. Then the linens moved, and she rose out of the chest slowly, unfolding, blinking against the lamplight, and I saw that her face was wet but her jaw was set, and that she had done the whole thing perfectly—not a sound, not a stir, not a breath that mattered. Ten years old. She had endured two hours in an iron-banded box in the dark, listening to soldiers' boots, and she had endured it the way stones endure weather.

"There were six," she said. Her voice was rough with disuse. "One stayed by the door the whole time. He counted people passing on the street. I could see through the keyhole of the lid hinges—it's loose at the corner." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, businesslike, a soldier after action. "The one who kicked the chest kicked it twice. The second time was to see if it echoed. I pressed my back against the charts so there'd be nothing to rattle."

I sat down on the stool because my legs declined to hold me. "You pressed your back against—"

"You told me once the chest should sound full," she said, as if it were obvious, as if any child reasoned thus. "Full things don't echo."

I had said no such thing. I had said *mind the lid*. But she had heard the instruction underneath the instruction, the way she heard everything, and built her survival out of it, and I understood in that moment that I was not protecting this child nearly so much as I was sheltering beside her.

I put my arms around her, and after a moment—one heartbeat, two—she allowed it, stiffening briefly and then leaning in with the whole weight of her, which is the only currency of trust a child has and the most expensive thing anyone had ever paid me.

"They took the letters," I said into her hair. "All of them. Vane's letters."

She pulled back and studied my face with that unnerving directness. "Is that bad?"

"It means Mr. Shaw will soon know everything the master wrote."

"Did the master write what he meant?"

I thought of the ciphered coastlines, the counted trees, the villages buried in forest density and river curvature. I thought of a man who had spent his final months converting human testimony into geography because geography could pass a soldier's inspection.

"No," I said, and felt the truth of it settle and steady me. "I don't believe Aldous Vane ever wrote what he meant in his life."

* * *

We did not sleep well, that night, either of us. Sorcha refused the loft outright—she dragged her blanket down and made her nest behind the map chest itself, wedged between it and the wall, so that the great oak that had protected her stood guard over her in turn, and I had no heart to argue. I sat up late with the true plate unwrapped before me, checking it by candlelight, holding the copper up to the flame and watching the hidden village rise ghost-like through the false one: the kirk, the clachan's nine hearths, the names spelled in the curve of the shore. Untouched. Unfound. Lucky.

And yet the word *luck* kept curdling as I turned it over. Luck implies randomness, and nothing about the day had felt random. The writ had named the exact arrears figure to the shilling—an odd precision for a debt already discharged. The soldiers had gone to the correspondence shelf first, before the coin box, before the stock of finished maps worth real money. Someone had told them what to look for, and what to overlook. Which meant Shaw—or whoever moved behind Shaw—had known about the letters before the raid, and had wanted them, and had used the tax pretext to take them cleanly, deniable, with a receipt signed in my own hand.

The net was not closing blindly, then. It was being drawn by hand.

Near midnight there came a tapping at the back shutter—the old signal, three soft knocks, that Mrs. Abernathy and Vane had used in the smuggling days and that she had taught me with a twinkle, as one passes on a family recipe. My heart leapt and sank together. She should not be out past curfew; she was seventy if she was a day, and the streets belonged to patrols now.

I opened the shutter on darkness and a voice instead of a face—Tam, the widow's grandnephew, a boy of fifteen breathing hard, cap in his fists.

"Mr. Blackwood—they've taken her." His voice cracked on it. "This evening. Soldiers. They're saying sedition—saying there were letters of hers in with the mapmaker's papers, letters that passed through her hands—" He stopped, gulped air. "She told me if ever it happened, I was to come tell you she kept every name she was given safe, and hidden where they'll never look, and that you'd know what she meant by that."

I stood in the doorway with the cold coming in off the close, and behind me the candle burned beside the plate that held a murdered village, and I thought: they have the letters now, and they have the woman who carried them, and tomorrow they will come for the child and the apprentice both, unless—

Unless the atlas spoke first.

"Tell her—" I began, and found I had no sentence adequate to the moment, so I gave the boy the only true thing I had. "Tell her the atlas remembers. She'll understand."

Tam vanished into the dark. Behind me, from her nest by the chest, Sorcha's voice came small and awake: "They won't hurt her, will they?"

I looked at the true plate gleaming on the table, at the false one drying on its rack, at the whole fragile architecture of ink and copper and borrowed time we had built against an empire's eraser—and I knew the honest answer, and I also knew we had run out of nights for caution.

"Not if the commission hears the truth first," I said. "Sorcha, fetch the lamp. We're going to finish what your village started."
