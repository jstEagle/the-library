## 13. Mrs. Abernathy's Arrest

The news came on a Tuesday, in the voice of a boy who smelled of ink and fear.

Sorcha heard him before she saw him—the rapid, uneven clatter of boots on the cobbles outside the shop, the kind of running that stops itself abruptly. She was in the loft, flat on her belly with a charcoal stub in her fist, drawing the view through the gap between the shutters: a milk cart, two gulls, the crooked sign of the cooper's yard across the lane. Ordinary things. She had learned to collect ordinary things the way other children collected buttons, hoarding them against the days when nothing was ordinary.

Below her, the shop bell rang its small brass note, and Orsen's footsteps crossed from the drafting table to the door.

She should not have looked down. She knew that even as she pressed her eye to the gap. But looking was what she did now; it was what Glenmara had made of her. She had watched soldiers come down the glen road with her cheek pressed to cold earth, and she had watched them leave again the same way, and somewhere between the watching and the leaving her whole village had stopped existing—burned first on paper, then in fact, so that no record remained to say it had ever been anything but empty ground. So she watched everything now. Milk carts. Gulls. Boys who ran to doors and then stood outside them, afraid to knock.

He was a printer's devil, thirteen or fourteen at most, his apron spattered white, his cap wrung in both hands like the neck of a bird. Even from the loft Sorcha could see he could not decide whether to speak loudly or quietly, and so his words came out in both registers at once, a strangled thing meant for Orsen alone.

"—taken up this morning. The widow Abernathy. Sedition." A pause, and then, softer, as if the word itself were contraband: "They took her letters, sir. All of them. They had a list."

Sorcha's charcoal snapped in half.

The sound was small, but Orsen's head came up at once—they always heard her, always, as if grief had tuned their ears to her particular frequency—and then they were moving, getting their coat, pressing something silver into the boy's palm, saying words too low for her to catch. The door closed. The bell settled into silence.

She lay very still on the loft boards and did not cry. Crying made noise, and noise was the thing she had unlearned first, before speech, before sleep, before almost everything. In Glenmara you learned quiet the way you learned prayers. *Quiet* had kept her alive under the whin bushes while the granary burned. *Quiet* had walked her forty miles down the glen road with a dead woman's satchel strapped against her chest like a second heart.

But Mrs. Abernathy—

Mrs. Abernathy, who had held Sorcha's face in her soft, flour-dusted hands and said her grandmother's maiden name out loud, said it like ringing a bell, said *MacCrimmon, why, I carried letters for your grandmother's people for eleven years and never once knew the face that went with the name*—and then wept, freely and without shame, tears running into the creases of her smile until Sorcha did not know if the woman was laughing or grieving and understood, dimly, that she was doing both.

Mrs. Abernathy, whose kitchen smelled of barley and whose table always had a third place laid, because a widow who smuggles letters learns that no one should eat alone.

Mrs. Abernathy, who was taken this morning, because of the child hiding in a mapmaker's loft.

*Because of me.*

Sorcha lay with the broken charcoal halves in her fist until her palm was black, and she thought about the list. *They had a list.* Lists had names on them. Names led to faces. Faces led to lofts above map shops where a girl drew milk carts and gulls and tried very hard not to exist. If the soldiers had a list, and the list had led them to Mrs. Abernathy's kitchen, how long before the same thread, pulled gently, led them here?

She should go. That was the thought that arrived, clean and terrible, while she lay counting her own heartbeats. She should take the satchel—or leave the satchel, leave everything, just go, out the window and over the cooper's yard roofs the way she'd come, back to invisibility, back to being nobody from nowhere, a face forgotten on purpose. *Forget the face*, the note had said. She had never managed to forget it herself, but she had hoped, these weeks in the loft, that others might.

Then she would be safe. And Mrs. Abernathy would rot in a cell for knowing her name.

Orsen came back before noon. Sorcha heard them on the stairs and arranged herself at the loft's edge, legs dangling, the picture of a child who had been doing nothing at all. It was a performance they both permitted.

"They've taken her to the tollbooth," Orsen said, setting down a paper of buns they clearly would not eat. Their voice was carefully level, the way it got when they were working a difficult coastline—steady strokes, no tremor, all the feeling pressed underneath. "No formal charge yet. Sedition is what they tell the neighbors. What it means is that they found Vane's letters among her things."

"Because of me," Sorcha said.

The words came out flat and strange, her voice unused to full sentences after so many months of fragments. Orsen went still, one hand still on the paper bag.

"No," they said.

"You know it's true." She stood up. Her knees were shaking but her voice kept going, as if it belonged to someone braver sitting inside her chest. "They followed the letters. Letters go from her to here. Here is where I am. If I hadn't come—if I'd died on the road like everyone else—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "Then she'd be in her kitchen right now. Making barley bread. Being nobody."

"Sorcha."

"She kept names!" The words tore out of her now, ragged, unstoppable. "Eleven years she carried them, and she told me—I didn't even ask, she just *told* me, because she trusted you, because she thought this shop was safe, and now they have her, and it's my name that led them—" She ran out of breath. The loft seemed suddenly very high and the floor very far away.

Orsen climbed the ladder. Slowly, the way they did everything, a person built for patience rather than speed. They sat down across from her on the boards, knees up, and for a long moment said nothing at all. Below them the shop ticked and settled around its maps like a sleeping animal.

"Do you know what your master taught me?" Orsen said at last. "Vane. Before he was my master, when I was just the boy who swept the floor. He said a map that only shows what's safe to show isn't a map. It's a lie with good manners."

"I don't see what that—"

"He also said," Orsen went on, "that guilt is a luxury for people with choices. You didn't choose any of this, Sorcha. You didn't choose Glenmara. You didn't choose to carry that journal forty miles on a broken foot. You certainly didn't choose to have a grandmother worth remembering."

"She chose to write to Mrs. Abernathy. That's the same thing."

"It isn't." Orsen's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "You want to make yourself the center of it because that's easier than the truth. The truth is that men with lists decided, months ago, who they were going to take. Vane first. Then anyone who'd touched his letters. If you had never been born, they'd have found another reason. Wickedness is very efficient. It doesn't need our help."

Sorcha hugged her knees. "Then we should run anyway. You and me and the plates. Over the border, or to the coast, or anywhere. If we're gone, they stop looking here. They stop finding people."

"No."

The word was quiet and absolute, a line drawn on vellum.

"They'll stop finding us," Orsen agreed. "And everyone else they were going to find will be found instead. The old man at the ferry who looked at your face and didn't sell you. The printer's devil who runs messages. Every soul who ever passed a letter for Vane or fed a stranger or failed to report a knock after curfew. Flight doesn't save them, Sorcha. It just chooses who pays for our safety. And I have spent my whole life letting other people pay. I'm finished with it."

"You don't understand," Sorcha whispered. "You've never been the reason soldiers come to a village."

Something moved behind Orsen's eyes—something old and hurt—but they didn't flinch away from it, and they didn't look away from her either.

"No," they said. "But I've been the coward who read about it afterward. In the papers, in the official accounts, in the margins of other people's histories. I believed for thirty years that if I stayed small enough, quiet enough, history would pass me by like weather. Do you know what I know now? History doesn't pass anyone by. It only passes by the people it has already decided to erase. And I would rather stand in front of it and be seen than hide and let it happen twice."

They sat there in the failing light, the two of them, the cartographer and the child who was herself a kind of map—a map of a place that had been burned off the earth, carrying its coastlines in her memory, its villages in her drawings, its dead in her silence.

"If they come for you next," Sorcha said finally, "I'll testify. I'll tell them everything. I'm not afraid anymore."

"That," said Orsen, rising stiffly to their feet, "is precisely what frightens me."

* * *

That night Sorcha did not sleep in the loft.

She told herself it was to keep watch, and perhaps it was, but mostly she could not bear the distance from the sound of another living heartbeat. So she dragged her blanket down the ladder and laid herself on the settle beside the drafting table, where Orsen worked late by lamplight, engraving something she was not allowed to look at too closely.

She watched their hands instead. They were beautiful hands when they worked, sure and unhurried, and she had learned that Orsen's whole heart lived in those hands—that when their mouth said *no charge yet* or *we must be patient*, their fingers told the truth, pressing too hard into the burin, pausing too long between strokes.

Tonight the fingers were steady. Steadier than hers.

"Orsen," she said into the dark. "When Mrs. Abernathy comes back—"

*If*, said the part of her that counted heartbeats under whin bushes.

"When," Orsen repeated, not looking up, the way you correct a spelling error on a map. "When she comes back, I'll build her a proper garden box. South-facing. She talked about herbs the last time she was here. Sage and southernwood, for keeping moths out of stored wool."

"She'll want rosemary too. For remembering."

"For remembering," Orsen agreed gravely, and made another stroke of the graver, and the lamplight swung their shadow enormous across the ceiling like a guardian angel made of paper.

Sorcha pulled the blanket to her chin. She thought about rosemary. She thought about a south-facing window. She thought about the tollbooth, three streets away, where an old woman sat in a cell that surely faced north, with no herbs at all, and she felt the guilt rise in her again like floodwater—only now it had somewhere to go, channeled into something with edges, a plan, a shape. Not drowning. Irrigation. Orsen would have appreciated the distinction.

"We won't forget her," she said. It wasn't a question, exactly. More a contract, offered to the room.

Orsen set down the burin at last and looked at her across the lamplight, and Sorcha saw, for the first time since the news had come, something in their face that was not steadiness performed for her benefit. It was fear, plain and unhidden, and beneath the fear something harder and finer, like grain in close-sawn oak.

"No," they said. "We won't. Which is why tomorrow I'm going to visit someone about making copies of the plates—many copies, in many hands, so that taking any one of us becomes pointless. Memory that lives in a single house can be burned. Memory that lives everywhere cannot."

"A conspiracy," Sorcha said, and found, to her astonishment, that she was smiling.

"A conspiracy of memory," Orsen agreed, and snuffed the lamp, and in the dark Sorcha reached out until her fingers found their sleeve and stayed there until sleep took her.

She dreamed of Glenmara, but for the first time the dream changed. The soldiers still came down the glen road, and the granary still burned—but this time, when the smoke cleared, the village stood up out of the ashes letter by letter, drawn in Vane's precise hand, named aloud in Mrs. Abernathy's warm voice, every roofline and every grave restored to the map of the world, and the officers who had ordered the fires turned to look at her, across the impossible distance between a burning past and a lit window in Edinburgh, and began, at last, to be afraid.

She woke before dawn to the smell of smoke that wasn't in the dream—chimney smoke, ordinary, a baker lighting his ovens three streets off—and to the sound of Orsen already dressed, already at the door, coat buttoned, the hidden plates wrapped in oilcloth under their arm.

"Where are you going?" she asked, though she knew.

"To see a printer," Orsen said. "Lock the door behind me. And Sorcha—" They paused on the threshold, silhouetted against the grey morning. "Whatever you hear today, whatever knocks come to that door—do not open it for anyone but me."

The door closed. Through the gap in the shutters Sorcha watched them go, slipping into the waking street like a figure entering the margin of a map, and settled herself on the settle with her charcoal and her broken pieces and her fear, and began, with slow deliberate strokes, to draw the tollbooth from memory—its walls, its single north-facing window, its iron stair—as if a picture could reach inside and keep a prisoner warm.

She was still drawing when the knocking started.
