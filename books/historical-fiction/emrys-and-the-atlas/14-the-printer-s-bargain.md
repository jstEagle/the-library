## 14. The Printer's Bargain

The address had come to me the way most useful things came in Edinburgh that winter—by whisper, half-swallowed, passed across a counter with a look that said *forget you heard it*. The printer's devil I'd bribed for news of disappearing witnesses had taken my coin, then taken his time, and finally leaned close enough that I could smell the tar on his jacket.

"You'll be wanting Callum Rennie," he'd said. "Candlemaker Row. The shop with no sign. Knock twice, wait, knock once. And Master Blackwood—" he'd caught himself, corrected the name with a small bow that was somehow more insulting than getting it wrong would have been, "—*sir*. Don't tell him who sent you. He doesn't like owing."

I did not go that night. Nor the next. I told myself it was caution, and caution has always been the coat I wear best, but the truth was uglier: I was afraid. I had spent forty-one years perfecting the art of being overlooked, and every step down Candlemaker Row felt like stepping off the edge of my own life into country no map of mine had charted.

On the third evening, after Sorcha had fallen asleep in the loft with her charcoal stub still curled in her fist and a half-drawn kirk tower ghosting on the paper beside her, I took Vane's silver from its hiding place beneath the floorboards and wrapped it in flannel inside my coat.

There were thirty-two guineas there. It had been Aldous Vane's habit to keep coin rather than trust banks—he distrusted institutions the way some men distrust water—and he had left instructions, in that dry marginal hand of his, that the money was for "the completion of the work." He could not have known how literally I would take him.

Mrs. Abernathy was three days gone by then, sitting in the tolbooth on a charge of sedition for letters she had carried and never read, and the shop felt hollow without her humming. Sorcha had wept herself silent over it, blaming her own face, her own name, the simple fact of having survived. I had refused to run, and I meant it—but refusing to run is a different animal from knowing what to do while standing still. The plates were safe at the printer's-to-be, or they would be, if the man could be bought. Everything else—the letters confiscated, Mrs. Abernathy taken, Shaw patient as winter—was moving against us.

So I went to find a criminal.

* * *

Candlemaker Row descends toward the Grassmarket the way old sins descend on a family—gradually, then all at once. The smell of tallow gave way to smoke and horse and the river-smell of the Nor' Loch, and the houses leaned together overhead until the sky was a grey ribbon. No sign hung outside number nine, only a door the colour of old blood, scarred where generations of hands had knocked.

Twice. A pause. Once.

A slot scraped at head height, and an eye regarded me—not hostile, merely accounting, the way a fishmonger accounts a fish.

"Engraving," I said. "Delicate work."

The eye considered this. The door opened on a chain, then fully, and I stepped into a print shop that existed in defiance of everything I understood about order. Paper hung from lines strung across the ceiling like laundry in a poorhouse, drying in ranks. Type trays covered one whole wall in honeycomb. Presses—two of them, one enormous and black with age—occupied the middle distance, and amid it all sat a small man at a slanted desk, mending a broken forme with tweezers held like a surgeon's instruments.

Callum Rennie was perhaps fifty, bald as an egg, with ink worked so deep into his knuckles that his hands looked dipped to the wrist in night. He did not rise. He looked at me the way Shaw looked at maps—with professional appraisal, estimating what I was worth and what I would cost him.

"You're the mapmaker's man," he said. "Vane's apprentice. The one finishing the atlas."

News travels strangely in this city. Down here, apparently, it travels fastest of all.

"I'm completing Master Vane's work, yes."

"Aye." He set down his tweezers. "And the Crown's man has been asking after it too. Shaw. Comes up from London smelling of lavender and threat." He gestured at a stool with his chin. "Sit, if you're sitting. I don't talk to standing men; they leave too quick."

I sat. Somewhere above us a woman began to sing—a Highland air, worn soft with use—and Rennie cocked his head at it as though listening to a press running smoothly.

"Now. You want something struck that can't be struck openly. That's the only kind of customer finds me on this street after dark. Tell me the shape of it."

I had rehearsed the speech on the walk down, and now I heard myself deliver it and found it sounded thin—too much the tradesman, too little the conspirator. A plate existed, copper, engraved in my own hand. It carried two images, one visible and one hidden, revealed by light held behind it. The visible image showed Glenmara as the commission wished it shown—burned, loyal, grateful. The hidden image showed the village as it had been, and counted its dead in the density of its trees, and spelled the names of the officers who had done it in the curve of its shore.

"I want copies struck of the hidden plate. Quietly. In numbers."

"How many is numbers?"

"Fifty to start."

He laughed once, without humour. "Fifty copies of treason, struck off a palimpsest, in a city crawling with redcoats." He picked up his tweezers again, set a piece of type, put it down again. "You've come to the right shop and the wrong conclusion if you think I do this for love of the truth."

"I've come with silver."

"Silver's the smallest part of it." He rose at last, joints cracking like green wood, and crossed to the great press. He laid his palm flat on the platen as if greeting an old horse. "Do you know what happens to a printer caught striking sedition? They don't hang us—they're too squeamish for the obvious justice of it. They break the press. On the scaffold steps, with hammers, in front of a crowd, and then they transport you to the plantations to die of heat and melancholy among strangers." He turned. "My press is forty years old. It has outlived my wife, both my sons, and every law passed since the Union. You're asking me to risk it, and everything under this roof, for a man I never met and a village I couldn't find on a map."

"Then don't do it for them," I said, and heard my own voice come out steadier than any speech I had made in forty-one years. "Do it because the commission will strike their version in ten thousand copies, gold-tooled and official, and in twenty years no living soul will remember which one lies. Do it because when they burned the records of Glenmara before they burned the village, they weren't destroying evidence—they were destroying the *fact that it existed*, and there are men in London paid precisely to make sure no one ever draws it again. I have the plate that draws it. I need hands to pull it. Yours are the only ones in this city that won't ask permission first."

Silence. The woman upstairs sang on, the tune circling back on itself.

Rennie studied me with those appraising eyes, and something in them shifted—not warmth, nothing so generous, but recognition. One craftsman recognizing another's desperation, and pricing it.

"The plate itself stays here?" he asked. "Or do you hold it?"

"It stays where it is. I bring it for each run and take it away after."

His eyebrows went up a fraction. "Careful. Good. Careful keeps men alive." He rubbed his ink-blackened thumb against his fingers, the universal gesture of his trade and his class. "Thirty guineas for the first run. Paper, ink, my silence, and the hours between midnight and four when the watch changes. After that, five a run, and I keep no ledger, no proof, no memory of your face. If they take me, I never saw you. If they take you, you never saw me. Total deniability, both directions, sealed in the manner of my profession."

"And how is that?"

He permitted himself the ghost of a smile. "We deny things professionally. We're very good."

I unwrapped the flannel. The guineas caught the lamplight, and I watched a man decide, in real time, what his fear weighed against his price. Thirty-two guineas lay in my lap; thirty went across the desk. It cleaned out Vane's hoard to the last two coins, and I thought, absurdly, of how the master had always said money was just ink stamped on metal, and here was ink buying ink, metal buying memory.

Rennie swept the coins into a drawer without counting them, which was either trust or contempt, and likely both.

"One condition of mine," he said. "Not money. When this is done—if it's done, if it works—I want the story. Not for the papers. For me. I've printed a thousand lives and never been inside one. I want to know, afterward, how a mapmaker's apprentice came to forge a truth inside a lie and sell it to a nation. You'll sit in that chair and tell me, all of it, and I'll decide then whether it was worth my press."

"That's all?"

"That's everything." He stood and pulled a sheet from the drying line, holding it to the lamp—an engraving of a bridge, fine as frost. "Your work is good, they tell me. Better than good. The devil says the hidden image is cut so fine it needs damp paper and heavy pressure or the light reveals nothing but ghosts." He handed me the sheet. "Damp paper. Heavy pressure. I'll not have ghosts coming off my press half-made."

I turned the bridge print in my hands and understood I was holding his résumé, his argument, his answer given sideways. Any man who cuts frost this fine can cut testimony finer.

"Thursday," I said. "Midnight."

"Thursday," he agreed, and then, as I reached the door: "Blackwood."

It was the first time he had used my name. I turned.

"The Crown's man—Shaw. He'll come to me eventually, or his creatures will. Every printer in this town gets visited sooner or later; it's how they prune us." His voice was level, unhurried, a man reading aloud from a form he knew by heart. "When they do, I'll show them honest work and honest books and an honest indifference. But understand what you're buying. My deniability is real, but it isn't armour. If the net closes on this shop, your plate is safe because it won't be here, but *I* may not be. Print this in your atlas: there are men on Candlemaker Row who will burn for other people's truths, and the victors' histories will never record our names."

"I'll record them," I said, before I could stop myself. "In the atlas. Both names on the cover, and yours in the colophon, and every hand that touched this."

Something moved behind his eyes—surprise, then a long calculation ending in something I hesitated to call hope.

"Then Thursday," he said again, softly, and the door closed behind me with the sound of a book shutting.

* * *

The walk home climbed out of the Grassmarket and the city changed around me by degrees—the leaning houses straightening, the smells turning from tallow to coal-smoke to the clean cold off the fields, the sky opening from a ribbon to a vault. I kept my hand on the two remaining guineas in my coat and thought about Aldous Vane, who had died face-down among his proofs with his hoard still under the floorboards, saving for a completion he must have suspected he'd never see.

He had known. That was the thing I kept arriving at, night after night, from a new direction each time. In the cipher, in the margins, in the silver saved against exactly this hour—he had known the work would need money spent in dark places, because light places would all be bought. The commission, the historians, Shaw with his lavender and his patience: these operated by day, under warrant, in rooms with windows. Truth, in the year 1746, had to move the way contraband moves—by night, by cash, by the goodwill of men with everything to lose.

I should have found it despairing. Instead, climbing the hill with the lamps of the New Town glittering below me like a spilled constellation, I found it clarifying. The law served the victors; very well, then the lawless would serve the dead. There was a symmetry to it that Vane would have appreciated, the cartographer's instinct for balance—every false weight answered by a true one, every official copy shadowed by fifty hidden ones, drifting out of Candlemaker Row into the pockets of pedlars and sailors and disaffected clerks, fanning across Scotland like seed on wind.

Let Shaw hunt the archives. Let him buy every commission and court in the kingdom. Somewhere in a cellar without a sign, a small bald man with ink to his wrists was going to pull sheets at midnight, and the record would bend.

* * *

Sorcha was awake when I let myself in—sitting up in the loft, knees drawn to her chest, listening the way children listen who have learned that boots on stairs can mean anything.

"You were gone," she said.

"I had business." I lit the lamp and saw her face properly: the bruised shadows under her eyes, three days of Mrs. Abernathy's absence written there. "A printer."

Her eyes fixed on me with that unnerving directness she sometimes had, the surveyor's eye she didn't know she possessed. "For the true map? The one under the other one?"

"Yes."

"Will it work?"

I considered lying to her, the way one comforts children, and decided against it—she had earned the truth the hard way, and lies had killed everyone she'd ever loved. "Maybe. It depends on a man I met tonight, and on whether we stay careful, and on luck, which has been thin rations lately."

She thought about this, chewing her lip. Then she slid down from the loft, fetched her charcoal and the paper she'd been working, and held it out. I took it to the lamp.

She had drawn a printing press. Crude, but unmistakable—the screw, the platen, a figure bent over the type, and around the base of the machine, dozens of small sheets flying outward like birds startled from a field. She had drawn them leaving through a doorway, over a hill, into a country she'd sketched in at the top edge: lochs, glens, and one small cluster of roofs, drawn with that terrible precision of hers.

Glenmara, receiving its own name back.

"Where did you learn what a press looks like?" I asked, though I suspected I knew.

"Papa took me to Edinburgh once. To the pamphlet shop by the bridge." Her voice stayed flat, factual, the way it always did when she spoke of before. "They let me pull a sheet. It came out backwards and smudged and Papa laughed and said that was how all true things start." She took the drawing back, folded it with great care, and tucked it beneath her pillow as though filing evidence. "That's what you're doing. Starting true things backwards and smudged."

From the mouths of babes, as my mother used to say, though no babe of hers had ever put it so exactly. I banked the fire, checked the bolts, and stood a moment in the dark shop surrounded by Vane's plates and proofs, by the atlas that now contained a lie and a truth in the same copper, waiting for Thursday.

I slept better than I had any right to. That alone should have warned me.

Thursday came, and with it the midnight run—damp paper, heavy pressure, the first twelve sheets pulled by lamplight while Rennie worked in silence and I held the plate to the candle between each impression, checking that the hidden village rose faithful from beneath the false one. We finished at four, the sheets hidden in a false-bottomed paper crate, and Rennie shook my hand once, ink to ink, like two men sealing something older than contract.

I was halfway up Candlemaker Row, lighter by nothing but the plate's weight in my coat and heavier by the first fifty copies of a resurrection, when I heard the bells.

Not the hour. Not the quarter. All of them at once, tumbling over each other across the town—the alarm that Edinburgh had not rung since the Jacobite army stood outside its walls. Below me in the Grassmarket, torches bloomed. And running toward me up the Row, barefoot on the frozen cobbles, came Rennie's apprentice boy, white-faced, shouting my name into the dark:

"The tolbooth—it's Mrs. Abernathy—she's taken worse, they say she's dying—" he gasped, catching my sleeve, "—and the soldiers are saying it's a fever, sir, they're saying the whole tolbooth is locked down—but Mr. Rennie says—"

The boy stopped, gulped air, and delivered the rest in a whisper.

"Mr. Rennie says you should know—Shaw was seen at the tolbooth gate an hour ago. Asking for her. By name. *Both* names—hers, and yours."
