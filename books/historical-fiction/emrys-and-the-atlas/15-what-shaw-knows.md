## 15. What Shaw Knows

The rain came in off the firth that morning like something with an errand, driving sideways down the High Street and turning every close and wynd into a gutter running brown. I had told myself I would not follow him. I had said it aloud, in fact, to the empty shop, in the flat voice I use for accounts that will not balance: *You will not follow Mr. Ellery Shaw through the streets of this town like a common spy.* And then, because I am Orsen Blackwood, who has spent forty years mistaking cowardice for prudence, I put on my worst coat—the one that made me look like a clerk gone slightly to seed—and I followed him anyway.

He left his lodgings at the Crooked Stair at half past eight, carrying nothing but a walking stick and a leather satchel that rode against his hip the way a soldier carries ammunition. That was the first wrongness. A historian, a man whose whole trade was paper, who had twice sat in my shop fingering Vane's proofs with gloved delicacy—such a man did not need to carry his documents about in the rain. Unless the documents could not be trusted to any lodging unless his own. Unless they were worth hanging for.

I kept a hundred paces behind him and let the crowd do its work. Market day saved me; the street was thick with fishwives and drovers and a Highland regiment's baggage train cursing its mules, and Mr. Shaw moved through all of it the way water moves through a net—unremarked, unhurried, entirely at his ease. He is not what I expected a crown historian to be. He is young, or young enough, with soft hands and a voice like warmed butter, and he has the particular stillness of men who have learned that watching costs nothing and buys everything. Twice on the High Street he stopped to look into shop windows. Both times there was nothing in the windows worth looking at. Both times I understood he was reading the street behind him in the glass.

I turned my face to a haberdasher's awning and studied ribbons I would never buy until he moved on.

He went first to the tolbooth. Not the grand door where the magistrates sit, but the narrow postern on the north side where clerks come and go with their bundles. He was inside perhaps three-quarters of an hour. When he emerged, the satchel was fuller than it had been, and heavier—I could see the way his shoulder set itself to the weight—and he stood on the step a moment under the dripping arch, and he smiled.

I have thought about that smile more than I have thought about anything since. It was not the smile of a man collecting evidence. It was the smile of a man putting something away.

* * *

From the tolbooth he went down toward the harbour, and here I nearly lost him, because he did something no honest man does on a wet forenoon: he doubled back through Fisher's Close, came out again by the kirk steps, and walked the same ground twice. It was not confusion. It was craft. A man checking whether the street remembers him. I pressed myself into the doorway of the cooper's yard and felt my heart going like a drum in my ears, and I confess that in that moment I wanted, with a purity I had not felt since childhood, simply to go home. To lock the door. To sit at Vane's desk and trace coastlines and be no one, which had been my religion for forty years and had kept me alive and untroubled and useless.

But I thought of Sorcha upstairs in the loft, drawing Glenmara's kirk from memory for the ninth time, getting the lych-gate right every time, getting it right the way only grief gets things right. And I stayed in the doorway until my legs stopped shaking, and then I went on.

He took a room.

That is to say, he entered the Crown and Thistle on Shoregate, took private parlour number two, as I learned from the chambermaid whom I bribed with sixpence and a look of such helpless respectability that she believed instantly I must be somebody's lawyer, and he ordered no meal. The fire was lit for him. The shutters were closed. And he sat, according to the girl, who peeked through the jamb on her way past because private parlours breed gossip the way standing water breeds midges—he sat for two hours over papers, spreading them out on the table before him, comparing one against another, and now and again setting down certain sheets *on top of a map*.

A map.

I paid her another sixpence to describe the map. She described a large one, folded in eight, with writing all round the edges—"wee marks," she said, "like a hen had walked through ink and then through the paper." Marginalia. Symbols along the borders.

I stood in the yard of the Crown and Thistle in the rain, pretending to admire the signboard, and felt the ground rearrange itself beneath me. Because I knew those wee marks. I had spent three weeks learning them, night after night, holding Vane's marginalia to candlelight, tracing the little conventions—a curved stroke for a name, a clustered dot for a household, a hatch pattern whose density counted the dead—until the atlas stopped being an atlas and became, in my hands, a testimony written in geography. The master's cipher. His last and greatest plate: the truth of Glenmara hidden in the very language of maps, so plain that no one would ever think to read it, and so careful that once you had the key, you could not unread it.

And now someone else had the key. Or was cutting it.

* * *

He emerged at two o'clock looking rested, if you can call it rest, a man who has spent two hours doing violence to paper. He walked out along Shoregate to the powder house and stood looking at the water for a while, and I hung back among the nets and barrels feeling conspicuous as a crow on a communion table, and it was there—God help me—it was there that I made my second mistake of the day, or my first honest act, depending on how you care to keep accounts.

I got too close.

Not deliberately. The tide had come up over the causeway and I gave the puddle too wide a berth, and when I looked up he had turned and was facing me across twenty feet of wet stone, and he tipped his hat.

"Mr. Blackwood." Warm butter. "What a coincidence."

"Mr. Shaw." My bow was, I believe, adequate. My voice was steady, which surprised me; some ancestor of mine who never flinched at anything must live in the spine after all. "I didn't expect—"

"No one ever does." He gestured at the grey water with his stick, an easy, generous sweep, as though the firth belonged to both of us. "It is a fine view. In better weather. One forgets, in London, that Scotland has weather at all—one reads of it, certainly, in the old itineraries, but reading is a poor substitute." He paused, exactly long enough. "Though for a cartographer, I suppose, reading is everything."

"I wouldn't say everything."

"Wouldn't you?" He considered me with real interest, the way a man considers a clock he intends to take apart. "Master Vane would have said so, I think. A remarkable man. Remarkable correspondents, too. Did you know that? A quiet man, living quietly, and yet his letters went everywhere. To Mrs. Abernathy, naturally. But also to gentlemen in Edinburgh. To a minister in Perth who has since"—a small, apologetic motion—"removed himself. To persons in the Highlands whose names I am still—well. Still assembling." He smiled at me with great kindness. "You will forgive me. Old habits. A historian assembles."

"You've been reading his letters," I said. My own voice sounded distant to me, as though it belonged to someone standing further off, in better weather.

"The crown has been reading his letters. I merely have the honour of being the crown's eyes." He turned the stick over in his soft hands. "There were eleven packets, Mr. Blackwood, recovered from various drawers and confidants. Ten are dull as ditchwater. Survey notes. Prices of vellum. Complaints about a lodger's cat. But the eleventh packet"—he watched the water, not me—"the eleventh is a curiosity. Full of village names. Full of numbers. Full of little drawings of hills, each hill annotated in a hand that is not quite his own. Testimony, one might almost call it. If one believed in such things."

The rain filled the silence between us.

"And what," I said, "does the crown intend to do with a curiosity?"

"What it always does with curiosities, Mr. Blackwood. Curate them." Now he looked at me directly, and there was nothing of butter in it at all; underneath the warmth was something older and colder and very patient, a thing that had been waiting in him all along the way a blade waits inside a scabbard. "You imagine—I don't say you do imagine, but if one imagined—that the danger of Master Vane's little enterprise was exposure, one would be wrong. Exposure is easy. Exposure is a broadside fired into a crowd. What the crown wants is quieter. The crown wants the massacre to become impossible to prove. Do you see the distinction? A lie shouted down is a scandal. A lie *archived correctly* is history." He replaced his hat. "Your master understood this. It is why he encoded rather than published. He hoped to hide the truth inside the record. I am engaged upon the same labour, Mr. Blackwood, in the opposite direction. I am hiding the record inside the truth."

I did not trust myself to speak.

"You should take heart," he said, and his voice regained its lamplight warmth as easily as a man puts a coat back on. "The commission arrives within the fortnight. It will hear witnesses. It will weigh evidence. It will produce a document of great dignity and no meaning, and in fifty years scholars will cite it, and Glenmara will be a footnote, and footnotes, sir, are where the dead sleep soundest." He stepped past me, pausing close enough that I could smell the rosemary on him, absurdly, on this day of rain and tar. "Complete your atlas. Finish it beautifully. It is what you are for. The world needs handsome objects, and the crown is content to let the handsome objects be handsome—as long as nothing is *in* them."

Then he walked away along the causeway, stick tapping the wet stone in a neat triple rhythm, and I stood among the nets until the cold worked through my coat and into the marrow, thinking: *he knows about the cipher. He has the letters. He is decoding it himself, sheet by sheet, in a private parlour with the shutters closed, and he is not doing it to bring the dead justice. He is doing it so that he may find every name, every number, every witness, and erase them one degree ahead of me.*

* * *

I did not go straight home. A man followed by his own dread walks badly and is visible from a distance; I made myself take the long way, up through the kirkyard, reading other people's grief carved in spilling sandstone until my hands remembered how to hang at my sides. And somewhere between the lych-gate and the street—Glenmara's lych-gate, drawn nine times by a child's hand, and here was a lych-gate too, because every kirkyard in Scotland is every other one, which is precisely what Shaw is counting on—I understood something that changed the shape of the day entirely.

He had told me. All but explicitly, he had told me.

A patient man does not tell. A thorough man does not stand in the rain and explain his method to the suspect he is dismantling. Unless the telling *is* the method—unless the speech was a message meant not for me but for whoever watches me, confirming that I know and he knows I know, sealing my silence with the promise of its price. Or unless—unless he needed to see my face when he spoke of the eleventh packet. Unless my face is also a document, and he is assembling it.

Either way, one thing had become certain, and it settled in me not as fear, exactly, but as ballast. The race I had sensed since the day Sorcha fell through my door was no longer a race of hiding. It was a race of reading. He had ten dull packets and one dangerous one, and a cipher he was breaking by lamplight, and a fortnight. I had an atlas, a child who drew the truth from memory, a widow who kept names, a printer of no reputation, and the same fortnight.

For forty years I had believed history happened elsewhere, to other people, and my whole art was to make myself the margin around it. Standing in the kirkyard with the rain easing off and the crows arguing in the yew, I understood that margins are where the annotations go. The margin is not empty. The margin is where the record keeps its secrets.

I went home. I climbed the stair, and Sorcha looked up from her drawing—Glenmara again, the well this time, drawn with the terrible accuracy of love—and I said, as steadily as I knew how:

"Sorcha. Fetch me every name your mother taught you. We have less time than I thought, and the atlas is not finished."

Because tomorrow I would go to the printer, and tonight I would work by candlelight with the shutters closed, and somewhere across the town, in parlour number two of the Crown and Thistle, Mr. Ellery Shaw would also be working by candlelight with his shutters closed—and we were racing now, he and I, sheet by sheet, name by name, toward the same fortnight and opposite ends of the truth.
