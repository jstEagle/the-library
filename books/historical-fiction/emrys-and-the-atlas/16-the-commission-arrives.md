## 16. The Commission Arrives

They came into Inverness the way weather comes—announced for days beforehand, and worse than promised once arrived.

I felt it first in the shop, though I could not have said how. Perhaps it was the way the light fell that morning, thin and gray as unprimed paper. Perhaps it was the drums. They had been audible since dawn, somewhere past the bridge, a slow arterial pulse beneath the ordinary sounds of carts and gulls and the river worrying at its stones. Sorcha stood at the loft window with her chin on her fists, watching the street below, and she did not draw anything. In three months of living above my shop she had never once gone long without a pencil in her hand. The absence of drawing frightened me more than the drums did.

"They're hanging banners," she said.

"I know."

"On the tollbooth. Red ones."

"I know." I did not look up from the plate I was burnishing, an honest stretch of coastline near Applecross, because if I looked up she would see my face and read it the way she read everything now, with that terrible fluency children acquire when they have survived something no child should be asked to survive. "There's bread in the crock. Eat something before Mrs. Abernathy comes."

"She'll be late today," Sorcha said. "The whole town will be late today. Everybody wants to stand where they can see the carriages."

She was right about that, as she was right about most things lately. Through the window I could see them gathering along High Street in their Sunday-best despite it being Thursday: fishwives with their arms folded, apprentices pretending to sweep doorsteps, old men who had stood through two Jacobite risings and a Hanoverian coronation and had opinions about all of them which they kept, these days, behind their teeth. Everyone was looking south, toward the road from Edinburgh, where the commissioners would come rolling in with their clerks and their soldiers and their carefully blank commissions under seal, empowered to inquire into the events at Glenmara and to establish, beyond all doubt and before any inconvenient witness could speak, precisely nothing.

* * *

I had learned, in the weeks since Shaw's second visit, to read the town the way I read a map—not for what it showed but for what its silences meant. And Inverness that autumn was a map of silences.

The printer's devil, Tam, who took my silver twice a week and brought news in exchange like a boy selling contraband weather, told me names. He told them quickly, quietly, staring over my shoulder at the empty street, and every week there were fewer of them. The crofter from Muir of Ord who had buried cousins at Glenmara—gone to Caithness, some said; gone nowhere, others said, meaning the ground. The woman from Dores who had been a girl that summer and remembered the smoke. Gone. A factor who had kept accounts for the district and whose ledgers might have shown requisitions of powder in quantities that fed no lawful campaign—gone to Edinburgh, officially, to assist His Majesty's historians. Unofficially, Tam shrugged in a manner that said everything his mouth was too canny to risk.

"Witnesses are not disappearing," I had told Mrs. Abernathy over her kitchen table one evening, while Sorcha slept in the chair by her fire with her head on my coat sleeve. "Witnesses are being *collected*. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole point. You do not make people vanish when you fear what they will say. You make them vanish when you already know what they would say, and you cannot afford to let them say it."

Mrs. Abernathy had stirred her tea long after there was anything left to stir. She was a small woman made mostly of iron filings and grief, and she had smuggled Aldous Vane's letters through three counties during the worst months, and she had paid for it with a husband and, eventually, I feared, with herself. "And you, Orsen Blackwood," she said. "What is your difference? You are still here."

"Because they don't know yet whether I am a witness or a clerk."

"And which are you?"

I looked at Sorcha sleeping, at the smudge of graphite still on her cheek from yesterday's drawings, at the satchel she still would not let out of arm's reach even in sleep. "Neither," I said. "I intend to be ink."

* * *

The commission opened on a Monday, in the courthouse hall that smelled of wet stone and tallow, and I went to hear the first day's proceedings because I believed—I still believe—that a man who means to testify should first learn the shape of the room that intends to swallow him.

It was a performance, and a well-staged one. Three commissioners sat beneath the royal arms on a raised dais: a lawyer with a face like a closed ledger, a soldier with a face like an open wound, and between them a churchman whose expression suggested he had been assured in advance that God was on the panel as well. Below them sat clerks with quills sharpened to needles, and behind the clerks, in the gallery, the public—packed tight, silent in a way crowds are only silent when silence has been instructed.

The testimony collected that day was a masterpiece of the art I had watched Mr. Ellery Shaw practice so courteously in my own shop. Witnesses were called, but they were the right witnesses: a garrison surgeon who spoke of wounds treated and men saved; a quartermaster who produced figures proving grain had been issued to the district, as though grain issued were proof grain received; a minister whose parish bordered Glenmara and who testified, voice trembling with what I chose to hope was shame rather than rehearsal, that he could confirm the village had been "the site of a regrettable action taken against persons in rebellion." No one asked him which persons. No one asked how many. No one asked why the records of the parish, the births and marriages and burials of Glenmara going back a hundred and forty years, had been burned first, before a single cottage burned, so that within a season there would exist no paper in all of Scotland to say the village had ever been inhabited at all.

You may burn a village and leave ruins. Ruins argue back. But burn the record, and the ruins become geology. They become scenery. In fifty years a shepherd will point at a green hollow and say his grandfather always wondered what had been there, and no one will answer him, because no one will know, because someone understood in 1746 that the surest way to kill a place is to kill its paperwork first.

Aldous Vane understood it. That was why they killed him.

I sat in the gallery with my hat on my knee and my heart going like a drum in my ears, and I wrote nothing down, because writing marks a man. But I memorized. Names, order, phrasing, the particular cadence of each question and the way questions were cut off when they drifted toward the wrong horizon. By the time the session adjourned I knew the shape of the room, and the shape was this: it was built to receive answers, and it had been furnished in advance with all the answers it would need.

* * *

That night I did not sleep. I sat at the workbench with a single candle and the atlas open before me—the true atlas, Vane's atlas, the thing we had spent three months hiding in plain sight among plates of innocent geography—and I looked at the Glenmara plate until my eyes burned.

To anyone holding it flat under ordinary light, it was a modest piece of work. A glen rendered in Vane's immaculate hand, contour lines flowing like breath, a watercourse, a track, a cluster of buildings marked with the conventional symbols any cartographer would use for a settlement of that size. A settlement of forty-one households. I knew the number now the way I knew my own hands, because Sorcha had drawn me the village from memory eleven times before her hand stopped shaking, and I had counted, and the number was forty-one, and the number was also the density of the forest symbols Vane had placed along the eastern slope, each tree standing for a soul, because Aldous Vane had encoded the dead of Glenmara into the very foliage of the map, and the living into the curve of the stream, and the names of the officers into the bearing of the track that ran up the glen—a name spelled in angles, legible only to someone who knew to look for letters in a landscape.

Shaw knew to look. That was what terrified me. He had held this plate in his gloved hands in my shop and smiled at it and said it wanted finishing, and I had spent every night since wondering how much he had already decoded, and how long I had before he decided that decoding was no longer necessary and burning was more efficient.

But there was another thing on that plate, the thing I had made myself, the palimpsest beneath the lie, visible only when flame was put behind it. Truth written to be revealed by candlelight. It seemed to me, that night, a kind of prayer in copper and wax: hold us to the light, it said. Someone, someday. Hold us to the light.

Sorcha found me there near dawn. She came down the ladder without a sound, the way she still moved everywhere, a habit I prayed would someday leave her, and she stood beside me and looked at the plate.

"Is it tomorrow?" she asked.

"It's tomorrow," I said. "Today, rather. I go at nine."

"You're scared," she said. Not cruelly. Children who have seen soldiers do not romanticize fear; they inventory it.

"Yes."

"Good," said Sorcha, aged ten, who had carried a bloodstained journal eleven miles through occupied country with a note around her neck telling the world to forget her face. "If you weren't scared, I'd think you'd done something clever instead of something brave, and clever gets you out of it, and I don't want you out of it. I want it finished."

Then she went back up the ladder and left me alone with the candle and the truth, and I confess I laughed, once, quietly, at whatever God arranges such appointments, and then I got out clean paper and began to write my petition.

* * *

I had spent a lifetime believing history happened elsewhere. I want that recorded properly, since I am recording things now: elsewhere was my religion. My father had been a man to whom things happened—press gangs, debts, a bad winter—and he had taught me by example that the wise survive by being small, by keeping to the margins the way a cartographer keeps to the edge of the sheet, decorating the border with wind roses and sea monsters and never, ever venturing into the interior where armies march. Margins were safe. Margins were where I had lived my whole life, apprentice to a master whose genius I loved and whose risks I had privately pitied. When they found Vane dead in his own doorway with his skull broken and his papers disturbed, I had mourned him and, God forgive me, I had also thought: *he went into the interior.*

Now I sat down to write myself into it.

The petition itself was short; the law admires brevity in those it intends to devour. I, Orsen Blackwood, of Inverness, engraver, being the duly appointed literary executor of the late Master Aldous Vane, cartographer royal, do respectfully petition the honorable commission to admit into evidence the atlas of the Highlands compiled by the deceased, insofar as it bears upon the district of Glenmara and the events of the eleventh day of June last; and I further offer myself for examination touching the compilation thereof. Under my signature, because courage apparently required ornament, I added the words *and touching the sources from which it was compiled.*

Sources. Sorcha. The word sat on the page like a lit fuse.

Mrs. Abernathy read it over breakfast and pushed it back across the table with one finger, as if it might go off. "You are handing them her name."

"I am handing them a phrase. 'Sources.' They will assume sources mean documents. Documents mean Vane's papers, and Vane's papers—" I tapped the atlas, the ciphered plates hidden inside a binding of the most boring geography in Britain— "are exactly where I want their attention and exactly where it must not rest."

"And if they ask?"

"They will ask. And I will answer as a clerk answers: precisely, tediously, and about nothing." I folded the petition. "Shaw taught me that, though he'd hate to hear it. Courtesy, patience, and a great deal of nothing. It is remarkable how much protection there is in being underestimated."

Mrs. Abernathy looked at me for a long moment. She had lost a husband to this same war of paper and memory, and I think she measured every living man against that ghost and found most of them wanting. "Orsen," she said finally, "you know they can refuse you. You know they can admit you and destroy you. You know that once your name is in their record, it does not come out again. Records are how they kill people now. They've learned."

"That is precisely," I said, "why I am going. If the record is a weapon, then it cuts both ways, or it ought to, and I mean to find out whether it still does. They have written their version all autumn, ma'am, in burned houses and vanished witnesses. Very well. I am a mapmaker's apprentice. I know something about versions." I stood, and my knees were not steady, and I let them be unsteady, because steadiness is a performance and I had decided, walking out my own door that morning, to be done with performances except the ones strictly necessary. "Vane drew Glenmara into an atlas so that it could not be unwritten. Someone has to carry it into the room where they are doing the unwriting. It seems the margins are empty this morning, so I suppose the interior will have to do."

* * *

The courthouse hall at half past eight smelled of wet stone, tallow, and fear, and the fear was mostly mine.

I presented the petition at the clerk's table, and I watched it travel: clerk to under-clerk, under-clerk to the soldier commissioner's aide, aide to the dais, a little folded paper ascending the hierarchy like a supplicant climbing stairs. The lawyer-commissioner read it without expression. The soldier read it and looked at me—looked at me the way a man looks at a fence post he suspects of being a sniper. The churchman read it last, longest, and then leaned to whisper to the other two, and I stood below them in the sudden enormous quiet of the hall and understood that everyone in that room, witnesses and clerks and gallery alike, was watching the paper decide my fate, and that I had never in my life been so thoroughly on the record or so completely unable to control what was being recorded.

"The petition of Master Blackwood is received," the lawyer said at last, in a voice sanded smooth by decades of saying things that could not afterward be denied. "The commission will consider the admission of the atlas as evidence. Master Blackwood will stand for examination at the opening of tomorrow's session." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down beside a plate. "The commission notes that the late Mr. Vane's papers were subject to certain seizures under warrant, and that portions of his correspondence remain in official custody. Master Blackwood will prepare to speak to the completeness of the atlas as it now exists."

Which was, translated out of courtesy, this: we have Vane's letters. We have been reading them. We know there is a code, and we know your atlas is not what it pretends to be, and tomorrow you will stand in this hall and tell us, under oath and on the record, exactly what it is—and we will decide whether that becomes the truth of Glenmara or merely the confession of Orsen Blackwood.

Somewhere behind me in the gallery a bench creaked. I did not turn to look. I did not need to; I knew the particular quality of that silence, the patient, courteous weight of it, the way a room changes temperature when Mr. Ellery Shaw chooses to occupy it. He was there. Of course he was there. He had been there all along, ahead of me at every stage, decoding at his leisure, and tomorrow I would step onto the record he had been preparing for me like a man stepping onto ice over deep water.

I bowed to the dais, because one bows, and I walked out of the hall on legs that belonged to somebody older, and it was not until I reached the steps and the cold air and the gray light over the rooftops that I realized my hands were shaking—and that beneath the shaking, steady as a watermark, was something I had not felt since childhood and had nearly forgotten the name of.

It was not courage. Courage implies a choice comfortably made. It was simpler and fiercer than that.

Tomorrow they would ask me what the atlas contained. Tomorrow, for the first time in my life, I would be standing in the interior of history, in the room where memory was being redrawn—and I would have the plate in my coat, and the candle question in my pocket, and forty-one reasons written in forest symbols waiting to be held up to the light.

I only hoped, descending the courthouse steps past the red banners and the watching crowd, that I would be given the chance to strike the match before Sorcha, God help us both, decided to strike it for me.
