## 17. Testimony by Lamplight

The town of Inverlochy had never seen so many red coats in one square. They hung from the windows of the tolbooth like bunting, and their drums kept a slow heartbeat through the morning while I crossed the cobbles with the atlas wrapped in oilcloth against my chest. It was heavier than it had ever been. Paper does not gain weight, but guilt does, and purpose too, and I carried all three.

I had not slept. The candle in my room had burned to a stub while I rehearsed words to a panel of men who had already decided what the words must be. Mrs. Abernathy—what remained of her presence in my life, which was her empty chair and her unclaimed letters—had once told me that courage was only fear that had found its footing. I could not find the footing. I walked anyway.

The hearing chamber smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular staleness of rooms where important things are decided badly. A long table for the commissioners sat on a dais beneath the royal arms, five gentlemen in London wool who had travelled north to listen—or to be seen listening. Below them, benches for witnesses and clerks; behind those, a public gallery separated by an iron rail, sparsely filled with townsfolk who came to watch history being tidied. Soldiers lined the walls. Their stillness was the stillness of furniture, which is to say, of things that could stop being furniture very quickly.

And there, at a small side table with his own chair and his own clerk and a carafe of water no one else would dare touch, sat Mr. Ellery Shaw.

He wore dove grey. He looked rested, unhurried, faintly pleased, as though we were both guests at the same wedding and he already knew how the marriage would end. When he saw me he inclined his head a fraction—the courtesy of a man acknowledging a colleague across a professional inconvenience. His satchel lay open at his elbow. I knew what was in it. Vane's letters, taken in the raid, every page of marginalia spread out before him over these long weeks while he worked at the cipher with the patience of a man dissolving sugar in cold water. I had watched him come and go from the records office at hours when honest men slept. Whatever he had decoded, he had decoded quietly, and folded it away like linen.

I was shown to the witness table. The atlas went before me, and I unwrapped it as if unveiling something for consecration, because that is what it was.

* * *

The presiding commissioner was Lord Cathcart, a heavy-jawed man whose voice had the worn smoothness of a stone in a riverbed. He read my petition aloud with visible distaste: *Orsen Blackwood, apprentice and literary executor to the late Aldous Vane, cartographer, petitioning that the master's final atlas be entered into evidence.*

"An atlas," Cathcart said, laying the word down like a coin he suspected of clipping. "You understand, Mr. Blackwood, that this commission is concerned with matters of record. Testimony. Sworn accounts."

"An atlas is testimony, my lord," I said. "It is simply testimony drawn instead of spoken. And drawings do not forget."

A clerk smirked. Shaw did not. He watched me the way a man watches weather he has already predicted.

Cathcart sighed through his nose. "The atlas may be examined. Proceed—but keep to what is relevant. We have heard eleven days of evidence regarding the events at Glenmara, and I will remind you the official account is settled. The village sheltered rebels. A engagement followed. Regrettable losses."

"Regrettable losses," I repeated, and heard how the words sounded in my own mouth, and decided I would not soften anything after that.

I opened the atlas.

* * *

There is a moment when you speak publicly for the first time in your life after a lifetime of silence, and you discover that your voice belongs to someone slightly taller than you are. That happened to me. I stood—I had planned to sit, but standing came upon me unbidden—and I began at the beginning, because beginnings were what I knew best.

"Aldous Vane spent forty years mapping Scotland," I said. "Coastlines, roads, watersheds, the seats of clans and the passes they guarded. He mapped for kings and he mapped for merchants, and he taught me one thing above all others: a map is never neutral. Whoever draws it decides what exists. Leave a village off the map, and within a generation no one can swear it was ever there."

I turned to the first plates. The Sound of Sleat with its firths inked in three weights. The Great Glen rendered so faithfully you could nearly smell the peat. I spoke of survey chains and triangulation, of soundings and bearings, and I watched the commissioners' attention drift the way attention drifts when a man believes he knows where a thing is going.

Then I came to the Glenmara plate.

"The official account," I said, "records Glenmara as a hamlet of perhaps a dozen structures, recently abandoned, occupied by armed rebels. The engagement—" I would not say massacre, not yet; first the ground "—is described as occurring among ruins."

"That is the account," Cathcart said flatly.

"Then let us look at it." I lifted the plate free of its binding and laid it on the table before them. The false plate—the one I had engraved under Shaw's polite pressure, the loyalist victory rendered in impeccable professional hand. A scatter of small squares for buildings, marked *ruinous*. A burn. A road. Annotations in a neat italic: *formerly inhabited; rebel strength estimated forty; engagement of short duration.*

"Fine work," said one of the commissioners, a Colonel somebody, leaning forward. "Very fine line work."

"It should be," I said. "I drew it myself."

Shaw's mouth curved by a fraction. He thought he was watching me perform the surrender he had arranged. Perhaps I was. For a moment I let the silence sit, let them admire the lie in all its craftsmanship, and I confess there was a bitter pleasure in knowing exactly how good the lie was, because I had built it to be broken.

"There is one difficulty with this plate," I said. "Everything on it is true except the whole."

Cathcart frowned. "Explain that."

"Glenmara was not a dozen structures. It was sixty-one hearths, a mill, a kirkyard two centuries old, a school. It was not abandoned. On the morning of the twelfth of March it held four hundred and six people, of whom perhaps thirty had ever carried arms, and most of those fowling pieces for grouse." I heard my voice steady itself, which surprised me; inside I was the boy at Vane's door again, terrified, holding a note that said *forget the face*. "But you need not take my word. You need not take anyone's word. The plate will testify for itself—if you will allow it light."

"Light?" the Colonel said.

"Candlelight, sir. Held to a flame."

They looked at one another. Shaw shifted—not much, the smallest settling of weight, a man adjusting his seat at a play he had hoped would close early—and then, smoothly, pleasantly: "My lords, if I may. Mr. Blackwood is evidently devoted to his late master's memory, and grief makes for ingenious theatre. But this commission has sat eleven days on sworn testimony. I am reluctant to see its dignity surrendered to a parlor trick with a candle."

"Then indulge the trick, Mr. Shaw," Cathcart said wearily. "If only to have done with it."

Shaw bowed. "Of course. I only wonder whether Mr. Blackwood might tell us first what we are to see, so that we may judge whether we are seeing it or merely being told we see it."

It was beautifully done. He handed the panel a rope to cling to before there was anything to cling to: doubt, pre-installed. I made myself meet his eyes. They were grey and patient and entirely without malice, which was somehow worse than malice. He did not hate me. Hatred would have meant I mattered.

"What you will see," I said, "is the true village, engraved beneath the false one. Every hearth. The mill. The kirkyard. And along the northern field boundaries—" my voice caught once, and I mastered it, thinking of Sorcha's small pencil moving across paper, drawing her dead home from memory with terrible precision "—a count. Sixty-one dwellings. Four hundred and six souls. Engraved in symbols so fine that at ordinary light they vanish into shading. Master Vane designed the method. He called it speaking under the breath of the map."

"And why," Shaw asked gently, "would a cartographer hide truth inside a lie? Would it not have been simpler to draw honestly?"

"Because honesty gets burned, Mr. Shaw," I said. "As your soldiers burned the parish records at Glenmara before they burned the houses. As witnesses are burned out of the record even now, while this commission sits. A hidden truth survives its burning. An open one does not."

The chamber had gone very quiet. Even the clerks' pens had stopped.

Cathcart exhaled. "Bring candles."

* * *

They brought three, set in iron stands on the commissioners' table, and snuffed the lamps along the walls. In the half-dark the royal arms above the dais became a shadow of a shadow. A soldier lit the wicks; the flames took with small crackling sounds, and the light wavered up the table across the faces of five men who had, until that moment, believed themselves the authors of the day.

I lifted the plate with both hands. My fingers were steady. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here: my hands did not shake. Whatever else failed that night, the hands held.

I passed the plate slowly through the candlelight, tilting it, letting the flame's glow shine *through* the paper—for that is the whole secret of a palimpsest: the deep engraving bites harder, holds more ink in its recesses, and when light comes from behind, the shallow lines wash to translucence while the deep lines stand in shadow. From the front, the false village. From behind, lit by fire, the true one rising through it like a drowned town surfacing in clear water.

Sixty-one hearths. The mill wheel drawn with such care you could count the buckets. The kirkyard with its stones. And the field boundaries resolving, as the angle changed, from innocent hatching into rows of tally-marks, each stroke a soul, grouped as families, because Vane had learned the families from his witnesses and refused to let them die anonymous.

I heard the chamber breathe.

"There," I said quietly, and turned the plate so the deepest engraving faced the panel. "The mill. The school. Look at the burn—see how it bends east? Your official account places it west. A mapmaker does not confuse east and west. A forger might. A survivor does not."

I moved the plate toward Cathcart. He took off his spectacles, put them back on, leaned down until his face was a hand's breadth from the paper. The candlelight moved in his eyes. Around the table, the other commissioners bent likewise, and for a long moment nobody spoke, and the only sounds were the flames and the creak of leather and somewhere in the gallery a child coughing.

"The kirkyard," murmured the commissioner at Cathcart's left, an old lawyer named Fettes. "Those are grave-stones. Drawn individually." He looked up at me. "How many?"

"Two hundred and thirteen interments recorded before March," I said. "Master Vane marked them all. Because a kirkyard proves a parish, my lord, and a parish proves a people, and a people—" I looked at each of them in turn "—cannot lawfully be described as *formerly inhabiting ruins*."

Fettes sat back slowly. Cathcart continued staring at the plate as if it might change under scrutiny, and I understood in that moment that it already had changed: it had stopped being evidence and become a window, and no man looks through a window and then claims there is no view.

"Mr. Blackwood," Cathcart said at last, heavily, "you swore this plate bears the official account. Yet now you show us another beneath it. Which is Vane's work, and which is yours?"

The question I had dreaded and prepared for. "The false village is mine, my lord. Drawn at the suggestion—" I did not look at Shaw; I made myself not look at Shaw "—of parties who wished the atlas completed agreeably to the settled account. The true village beneath is Master Vane's, cut into the copper before his death, and I ask the commission to consider the order of events: the true plate was engraved *before* the murder of its maker. The lie was added after. I leave the commission to draw its conclusions about which layer required killing a man to produce."

That landed. I felt it land, in the shift of bodies, in Fettes's sharp glance toward Shaw, in the way the soldier nearest the wall moved his weight from one foot to the other.

"My lords." Shaw rose. Of course he rose; he had been waiting for his cue as surely as an actor. He did not hurry. He buttoned nothing, gestured at nothing, simply stood in his dove grey in the candlelight and became, instantly, the most reasonable man in the room. "The commission will forgive me, but Mr. Blackwood has just confessed—to use his own word—to falsifying a document submitted to His Majesty's inquiry. He tells us himself he engraved a false account. We have only his assertion, offered in the same breath, that the *other* layer is genuine. A man who admits to one forgery has provided, regrettably, the strongest argument against believing in any other."

"He provides the copper plates," I said. "Let your own engravers examine them. Deep cutting cannot be simulated over shallow; the sequence is physical fact, not assertion. And the cipher—" I reached for the atlas, for Vane's marginalia pages, the key I had spent weeks learning "—the method is documented in the master's own hand. Coastline curves spelling names. Forest density counting the dead. It can be verified independently by any scholar your lordships appoint. Unless," I added, before I could stop myself, "verification is precisely what certain parties fear."

"Mr. Blackwood," Shaw said, with a small pained smile, "impugns my motives now. I have served this commission without fee, purely from respect for the historical record. It grieves me that devotion to a dead friend should lead an otherwise respectable tradesman into conspiracy-mongering." He turned to the panel, spreading his hands. "Consider what is actually asked of you: that eleven days of sworn testimony be overturned by shadows on a piece of copper, produced by a self-confessed forger, interpreted according to a secret 'cipher' that only he can read. If we accept this, my lords, every record in Britain becomes hostage to whoever claims a hidden writing beneath it. Is that the precedent this commission wishes to set?"

It was masterful. I watched the panel waver—watched Cathcart's jaw set and unset, watched the Colonel nod along with Shaw's closing cadence, watched reason assemble itself around the wrong conclusion like ice forming over water. This was how it was always done, I realized. Not with violence, not usually. With smoothness. With procedure. With men in grey pointing out, correctly, that the truth is inconveniently strange.

"The commission will adjourn to consider," Cathcart began—

"If I may, my lord." Fettes. The old lawyer's voice was dry as August grass. "Before we adjourn. Mr. Blackwood—the cipher. You say it counts the dead. Demonstrate. Here. Now."

* * *

So it came down to that, in the end: not rhetoric, not loyalty, not the justice of the thing, but whether an old lawyer's curiosity outweighed a courtier's smoothness. I opened the atlas to the plate of the Ardnamurchan coast—a coastline Vane had surveyed years before the war, its bays and headlands drawn in his unmistakable hand—and I showed them what I had wept over alone at midnight: how the curve of one bay spelled, in the shorthand of survey notation, a name; how the spacing of depth-soundings in another encoded letters; how the name it spelled belonged to a woman of Glenmara whom three separate witnesses had sworn they saw driven into the water.

I showed them the forest plate, and the density of the tree-symbols, and how counting them by the master's own rule yielded a number, and the number was not forty, and was not twelve, and was not *regrettable losses*.

The candles guttered. Nobody called for fresh ones.

Fettes copied figures onto the margin of his notes with a pencil gone soft from use. Cathcart sat with his great hands flat on the table, looking at nothing. And Shaw—Shaw stood very still, and I watched something happen in his face that I had never seen there: not alarm, not defeat, but recalculation. A man discovering that the ledger he had been secretly balancing for months had a column he had failed to enter. He had Vane's letters. He had the cipher partially unfolded. He had known, I think, that the plates held something—but knowing a thing abstractly and seeing four hundred and six tally-strokes rise out of candlelight in front of five commissioners are different injuries, and the second bleeds.

"My lord," Shaw said at last, and his voice was still perfectly level, which told me everything about how dangerous the next hour would be, "I renew my objection, and I would ask the commission also to consider the sources of the late Mr. Vane's so-called testimonies. Unsworn villagers. Rebels. Persons of no credit and less neutrality. But—" a bow, immaculate "—I defer to their lordships' judgment on the admissibility of shadows."

"We do not adjourn in darkness," Cathcart said grimly. "Restore the lamps. Mr. Blackwood, the atlas remains with the commission tonight. Your plate—" he glanced at it, lying in the pool of new lamplight, the false village on its face and the true one sleeping beneath, patient as seed "—will be examined by persons of this panel's choosing at first light."

I bowed. I gathered my composure the way one gathers dropped papers, one sheet at a time. It was not victory; I knew that. Panels waver, and wavering can settle either way, and Shaw had lost nothing yet but tempo. But for the first time since a child had collapsed against my door with a note that said *forget the face*, the forgetting had stalled. The face was on the record. Sixty-one hearths. Four hundred and six souls. Spoken under the breath of a map, and heard.

I turned to go, and that is when I saw her.

At the rail of the public gallery, small between two grown men, a figure in a borrowed coat too large for her, dark hair escaping from beneath a cap, watching me with eyes I would have known in the dark of the world's end. Sorcha. She was not supposed to be there. She was not supposed to be anywhere near this room, this town, these soldiers lining the walls—and she was not hiding, not shrinking, not flinching. She was standing on her toes to see over the rail, and her gaze went past me entirely, past the commissioners, to the officer of the guards below the dais—a captain, resplendent, taking snuff—and I watched my child's face change into something older than ten years old, something carved and terrible, and her lips form a name.

She stepped toward the rail.

"Sorcha—" I said, too loudly, in front of everyone.
