## 18. The Witness in the Gallery

I had promised Orsen I would stay home.

I had promised it with my hand pressed flat against the door of the shop, feeling the cold of the morning come through the wood, and Orsen had knelt to look at me straight on—the way no one else ever bothered to do, as if I were a person and not a piece of furniture to be moved out of sight—and said, *Sorcha. Whatever you hear from the town square, whatever bells, whatever shouting. You stay here. You lock this door behind me and you do not open it for anyone but me.*

I had nodded. I was good at nodding. I had spent a whole winter learning how to make my face into a closed shutter, how to fold myself into the loft above the workroom like a shirt put away in a chest, how to be nothing at all. Being quiet was the first thing Glenmara taught me and the last thing anyone ever wanted undone.

But promises are made by the person you are in the morning, and morning does not last.

* * *

The shop without Orsen in it was a different country. For months I had learned its geography the way I had once learned the hills around the village—by the sounds it made when it thought no one was listening. The tick of the clock over Vane's old desk, slower than any honest heart. The settle-and-creak of the floorboards where the frost got under them. The small iron breath of the stove, which Orsen fed so carefully each dawn that I came to understand it was not a stove being tended but a grave being kept.

That morning I did my tasks the way I had been taught. I swept. I sorted the scraps of paper by their weight, because Orsen said the light papers went to proofs and the heavy ones went to records, and *never confuse them, Sorcha, never let a record be printed on proof-stock*. I watered the ink cake until it bloomed dark. I sat at the little table with my own sheet of paper and drew the well at Glenmara, because drawing it was the only way I knew to keep it from dissolving—not the massacre, never that, but the ordinary things: the rope wound round the beam, the moss on the north stones, the three shallow steps down to the water. Mrs. Abernathy was gone now, taken in her nightdress with her hair half-plaited, and every day she stayed in that cell was a day the names she kept grew quieter in her absence. Someone had to speak them aloud somewhere, even if only in graphite on rag paper.

I drew the well. Then I drew the kirk. Then I drew the rowan tree at the crossroads, split by musket shot, still stubbornly alive on one side.

And then I heard the bells.

They were not church bells. They were the commission's bells, rung to summon the town to the hearing at the tolbooth, and they rang the way weather rings before it breaks—a bright, wrong note in them, like silver dropped on stone. Through the window I could see people moving up the hill toward the square, a thin procession of coats and shawls, all of them drawn the way iron filings are drawn to a lodestone. They were going to watch Orsen stand before the commissioners and try to make a book testify.

I looked at the door. I heard Orsen's voice in my head again, steady and low: *You do not open it for anyone but me.*

But here is what Orsen did not know—what Orsen, who believed so completely in the power of paper, could not know, because Orsen had never been a child who watched soldiers burn a parish register before they burned the houses. Paper can be confiscated. Paper can burn; our own shop had nearly proven it once already. Paper can be declared forgery by men whose signatures outweigh yours. But there is one record in the world that cannot be seized, cannot be forged, and cannot be burned, and I was sitting inside it. I was the only surviving witness of Glenmara who could walk on her own two feet into that room and say *I saw*.

Mrs. Abernathy had kept names. Vane had kept maps. Orsen was keeping the atlas. Everyone I loved had made themselves into vessels for other people's memories, and every one of those vessels could be broken by the crown's long hands.

Mine couldn't. My memory lived in marrow and blood.

I am not going to pretend it was courage. Not entirely. It was courage, yes—or something with courage's face on it—but underneath there was also the simpler thing, the thing that had been growing in me since the night Mrs. Abernathy's door came off its hinges: fury. It had started as a coal in my belly the first time I hid under a cart while soldiers laughed about their pay, and Orsen's kindness had banked it and Mrs. Abernathy's tears had banked it, and now, hearing those bells, it caught. I set down my pencil. I put on the coat Orsen had bought me in autumn, too big in the shoulders, and I pulled the hood up, and I took the spare key from the jar where we kept the quills, and I opened the door that I had sworn not to open.

The morning air tasted of coal smoke and frost. I locked the shop behind me, because even in disobedience I meant to keep everything safe, and I followed the crowd up the hill.

* * *

The tolbooth was fuller than I had ever seen it. The great hall smelled of wet wool, tallow, and the particular sourness of a hundred strangers breathing the same cold air, and the noise of it was like standing inside a river. Farmers and fishwives stood packed along the walls; clerks perched on benches with their ink-stained fingers hovering; high up under the rafters, in the gallery that ran around three sides of the hall, children and servants leaned over the rail. Below, on the raised platform beneath the royal arms, sat the commission: five men in wigs and fur-trimmed robes, arranged in a crescent like a hand closing.

And among the guards at the foot of the platform—among the red coats and the bayonets and the bored, planted feet—

I stopped breathing.

He was older. Heavier through the jaw. His hair was powdered now instead of tied back plain, and there was a new scar through one eyebrow that he had not carried at Glenmara. But I would have known him if forty years had passed. I would have known him blind, by his voice alone, that dry unhurried voice giving orders in the square while the smoke rose. Captain—no, Major now, by the sash—Aldren Wexford. The man who had read the order aloud. The man who had said, when a woman came running with her arms out toward the burning kirk, *let her go, it makes no difference now.*

Orsen was already there. I saw the familiar figure at the counsel's table below the platform—straight-backed, hands folded, the way Orsen always sat when terrified, which fooled everyone but me. Beside Orsen lay the atlas, wrapped in oilcloth like something wounded. A clerk was unwrapping it now with the exaggerated care of a man handling either scripture or a snake.

I should have stayed at the rail in the gallery. I know that. I had meant only to see—to confirm with my own eyes that the hearing was real, that Orsen was safe, that the machine of the law was turning the way Orsen had promised me it sometimes, rarely, could turn. I meant to slip away before the doors closed.

But then Mr. Ellery Shaw rose from his place beside the commission, smooth as poured cream, and began to speak, and his voice did something to the whole hall—it thinned the air. He spoke of Master Aldous Vane with warm regret, as though mourning an old colleague he had personally buried. He spoke of the atlas's *admirable craftsmanship*. He spoke of the *regrettable tendency of certain parties* to impute political meaning to works of geography. And then, gently, terribly, he turned to the Glenmara plate.

"The commission will observe," Shaw said, holding the false plate toward the light of the tall windows, "that the settlement depicted here is rendered with notable vagarity. No kirk marked. No boundary stones. One might almost think the village had never properly existed at all." A soft, satisfied ripple moved along the platform. "Which is, of course, consistent with the official survey."

The lie went into the room and sat down among them like an honored guest. Five commissioners nodded. Clerks wrote it down. Somewhere behind me a woman whispered to her neighbor, *see, there was never aught there,* and I felt the future close around that sentence like a hand around a moth.

They were doing it again. Right now, in front of God and the king's arms and two hundred witnesses, they were burning the records first.

I did not decide to move. That is the truth of it, and I hold to it, because it is the only excuse I have. My body simply understood, before my mind could argue, that this was the moment or there would never be another. I went down the gallery stairs two at a time. I went along the wall where the guards' shadows pooled. And when the herald called for objections to the entry of evidence and the hall fell into that held-breath hush, I stepped out from the wall into the open floor, into the lane between the crowd and the platform, and I pulled back my hood.

"I saw it," I said.

My voice was smaller than the voice in my head. It cracked on the second word. But the hall was built to carry sound, and the silence was so complete that even a child's cracked voice reached every corner of it, and I watched two hundred faces turn to me at once like a field of flowers turning to the sun.

"There was a kirk at Glenmara," I said. "St. Maelrubha's, grey stone, with a bell. There were forty-one hearths. There was a well with three steps and moss on the north side. I can draw it. I *did* draw it." My hands were shaking; I clenched them to stop them and my nails left four moons in each palm. "It existed. We existed."

A murmur went through the hall like wind through barley. At the counsel table, Orsen had gone white—I saw it clearly, saw Orsen's mouth form my name with no sound coming out—and one of the commissioners, a lean man with a face like a folded letter, leaned forward and said, "Who is this child? By what right does she address the commission?"

"She's a witness," someone shouted from the gallery. "Let her speak!" And then, from further back, another voice: "She's one of them! That's a Glenmara brat!"

Major Wexford moved.

He did not run. He walked, unhurried, the way he had walked through the square that day, and the sight of that walk undid ten years of healing in a single heartbeat. I was nine years old again, standing in the road with ash falling on me like black snow. But I was not nine years old. I lifted my chin, and I pointed at him, and I said the thing I had carried in my chest since the night Orsen found me dying on the doorstep:

"That man was there. Captain Wexford. He gave the order at the kirk. He said—" and here my voice broke entirely, and I let it, and I said it anyway through the breaking— "*it makes no difference now.* Well it makes a difference NOW, sir. It makes all the difference in the world. Forty-one hearths. Sixty-three souls the week before. Nine who came out of the hills after and had nowhere to go home to. I have their names. Some of them I have only the faces. But I am the record now, and you will not burn me."

For one moment—one long, ringing, impossible moment—the whole hall hung still, and I believed we had won. The lean commissioner was staring at Wexford. A clerk had stopped writing, pen dripping. Even Shaw had turned, and on his smooth face was something I had never seen there before: calculation interrupted.

Then Wexford smiled at me. Slowly. As if I were a curiosity at a fair.

"Commissioners," Shaw said smoothly, recovering, "this is plainly a coached child—a plant, produced by the very party seeking to poison these proceedings with sedition. The petition before us is contaminated by association. I move that the petition be suspended, the document sequestered, and the child remanded for questioning regarding her identity and her handlers."

"Handlers," Orsen repeated, loud, standing, and Orsen's voice filled the hall with a fury I had never heard from that careful mouth. "She is a child, sir, not a weapon. And the atlas speaks for itself—"

"The atlas speaks for whoever engraved it," Shaw said, "which is precisely what remains to be determined. All in favor of suspension?"

Hands rose along the crescent, one after another, like gates closing. The gavel came down. Two guards started toward Orsen's end of the table; four started toward me.

I did not run. I want that remembered too. I stood my ground in the middle of the floor and kept talking—names, names, *Marion Gilchrist, the elder Tam Breac, the ferryman's twin girls*—pouring them out as fast as I could while the hall roared, because every name spoken aloud in that room was a name the fire could never have. Hands closed on my arms, hard, and I heard Orsen shouting, heard the crowd surging against the rail, heard a woman's voice screaming *shame, shame on ye*, and then the doors swallowed the noise and I was in the cold stone passage with the guards' boots striking echoes off the walls, and Major Wexford walking ahead of me, unhurried, not looking back.

But at the turn of the stairs, just before the daylight cut him off, he did look back. Just once. And he looked at me the way a man looks at a loose thread on a fine coat—something to be dealt with quietly, later, where no one important is watching.

They took me down, down, past the cells where debtors muttered, to a small room with a table and one high window. They sat me in a chair much too large for me. A clerk laid out fresh paper and an inkwell, and Mr. Ellery Shaw came in, closed the door softly, and smiled his cream-smooth smile.

"Now then," he said pleasantly, drawing out the chair opposite mine. "Let us begin at the beginning. Your name, your family—and perhaps you'll tell me exactly what young Mrs.—forgive me, *Master* Blackwood has told you to say."

Outside the high window, far above, I could see a slice of grey sky and, briefly, the flight of pigeons over the tolbooth roof. Somewhere up in that sky, in that town, Orsen was fighting for the atlas, and Mrs. Abernathy was in a cell, and the plates were hidden where only three people in Scotland knew to look.

Three, I thought. Now they need me back to make it three.

I folded my hands on the table, the way Orsen did when frightened, and looked at Shaw, and said nothing at all.
