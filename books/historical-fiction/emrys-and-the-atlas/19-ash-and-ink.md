## 19. Ash and Ink

The summons came the way winter comes to the coast—not announced but simply arrived, a coldness in the room that had not been there an hour before. A boy in commission livery stood in the doorway of the shop, too young for his uniform, holding out a card as though it might bite him.

*Mr. Ellery Shaw requests the pleasure of Mr. Orsen Blackwood's company. Eight o'clock. The King's Arms, private parlour.*

No threat in it. No threat at all, and that was the threat.

I turned the card over twice in my fingers while the boy waited, shifting his weight like a gull on one leg. Behind me the shop smelled of size and lampblack and the cold tallow of the morning's candles, and upstairs I could hear the small industrious sounds of Sorcha copying her letters, the scratch of her pencil pausing and starting again. I had learned to hear her the way sailors hear weather—by absence and change—and she was steady, so I made my face steady too.

"Tell Mr. Shaw I'll come," I said.

I did not tell Sorcha where I was going. I told her I had errands, which was true in the way that a noose is a kind of necklace.

* * *

The King's Arms had been a loyalist house since before the rising, and it wore its loyalty the way old men wear medals—slightly askew, polished anyway. The private parlour was at the back, beyond the ordinary din, behind a door with a brass handle worn to dull gold by forty years of palms. Shaw had taken it entire: fire lit, table set for two, a decanter of claret standing like a red-jowled magistrate between the glasses.

He rose when I entered, and shook my hand, and asked after my health with such unfeigned warmth that I understood how completely dangerous he was. Men who hate you show you their teeth. Shaw showed me his chair.

"Mr. Blackwood. You'll take wine? It's a Burgundy—a friend in Glasgow sends it. After the year we've had, I find I've developed a weakness for things from far away."

"I'll take whatever you're pouring," I said, "so long as we're honest with each other about what this is."

Shaw smiled. He poured. He was dressed in dove grey tonight, immaculate, not a hair out of order, and the firelight moved on his face like water over a stone. I thought—as I had thought once before in this man's presence—that he had the most patient eyes I had ever seen. Eyes built for archives. Eyes made for waiting out centuries if the record demanded it.

"Honesty," he said, settling back. "Very well. You have something of mine."

"I don't recall anything in this town belonging to you, Mr. Shaw."

"The truth, Mr. Blackwood. The true account of Glenmara. You are keeping it from me, and I have come to make you a gentleman's offer for it." He turned his glass by its stem, watching the wine climb the sides. "You will forgive me for being direct. I've watched you work these many weeks and I confess I admire you. Vane chose well when he chose you—better than either of you ever knew. You have his patience. His hand, even. There are plates in that atlas I would swear are his, but they were finished after his death, weren't they? By yours."

"You came all this way to review my engraving?"

"I came all this way," Shaw said softly, "to save a child."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass. I kept my hands still. Somewhere below us the ordinary world went on—a chair scraped, someone laughed, a pot banged down on a stove—and I envied them all, every loud oblivious soul.

"There is a child," Shaw continued, in the tone of a man discussing drainage improvements, "who has been seen. Once or twice. At your window. Small for ten years old, I'd say, though children of that district run small; famine does that. Dark hair. Draws constantly—I'm told the glazier's boy saw sheets of drawings in your loft when he came to mend a pane." He looked up, and his eyes were kind, and that was the most terrible thing in the room. "Orsen. May I call you Orsen? The commission sits for another fortnight, and then it goes away and writes its report, and the report will say what reports of this nature always say. And then there will be no more questions, and no more reason for anyone to look anywhere—including here, including at her. But between now and then—" he spread one white hand— "there are men in this town who are not patient the way I am patient. Soldiers mostly. Sons of officers whose names appear in certain documents. They drink at the other end of this building. If they were ever to learn, definitively, where the last living witness sleeps—"

"Then say what you've come to say."

He said it. He said it plainly, without cruelty and without mercy, the way a surgeon sets a bone.

"Give me the plates. The true plate of Glenmara, and every ciphered leaf besides, and your silence afterward—your word, given before witnesses, that you never speak of it again. In exchange: the girl is taken somewhere safe. Not a prison—a household. Good people, paid well, sworn discreetly. She will be educated. She will grow up. She will forget, because children do forget, Orsen, it is God's great mercy that they do, and in twenty years she will be a woman with a life and perhaps children of her own, alive, whole, *unremarked upon*—which in our time, in this country, is the only safety there is." He leaned forward. "Whereas if you keep her, keep the plates, keep your beautiful stubborn silence—you will lose her anyway, within the week, to men who will not ask politely. And you will lose everything else besides. Your liberty certainly. Possibly your neck. And the plates will burn regardless, along with everything else you love, and the record will close over Glenmara like water, and no one in fifty years will know there was ever a village there at all." He sat back. "I am offering you the only version of this story in which everyone lives."

For a long moment I looked at him and felt the full seductive weight of it. That is the thing no one tells you about temptation: it never comes wearing horns. It comes wearing sense. It comes speaking arithmetic—one child weighed against a truth; a life weighed against a legend; the certain warmth of a living girl against the cold abstraction of justice. Every reasonable bone in my body voted for his offer.

And underneath the reasonableness, quiet as a palimpsest under varnish, another voice said: *he is lying about the household.*

Because I had seen what happened to Mrs. Abernathy, who had done nothing worse than carry letters. Because I knew what happened to witnesses in this country now, and it was not education and good households. Because Sorcha had told me, in her flat small voice, what the soldiers burned first at Glenmara, and why: not the houses—the houses were nothing—but the records, the parish book, the names, everything that could prove the village had existed. Shaw's offer was the same fire wearing better clothes. He was not offering to save Sorcha. He was offering to erase her—to move her into some dark corner of the Empire's paperwork until she was nobody, from nowhere, related to no one dead—and call the erasing mercy.

"You misunderstand what I have," I said.

"Do I?"

"You think I hold the truth hostage, and you're offering to ransom a child for it. But you have it backwards." My voice was steadier than I had any right to expect. "The child doesn't buy the truth's freedom. The truth is what keeps the child free. The moment those plates leave my hands, she is worth nothing to you alive. She's worth everything silenced. You know it. I know it. So let us not insult each other further with arithmetic."

Shaw studied me for a long moment, and something in his face shifted—not anger; I almost wish it had been anger—something more like disappointment, the weariness of a man who has offered a drowning man a rope and watched him decline it on principle.

"Vane thought the same way," he said at last. "Did you know that? Right up until the end. I sat where you're sitting, in effect, and made him offers very like this one, and he looked at me exactly as you're looking at me now, and three days later he was dead, and none of his principles saved him." He stood, smoothed his coat, and rang briefly for the servant. "I want you to understand, Orsen, that I bear you no ill will. That is not flattery and it is not a lie. Men like you and Vane and the widow Abernathy—you believe history is a moral affair, that it can be *kept*, like a promise. I don't have the luxury of belief. I have a job. The job is that this country stops bleeding, and it stops bleeding when everyone agrees on one story, whichever story, and stops killing each other over the differences. You are not a villain in mine, merely an obstruction." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "You have until midnight to reconsider. Not that I expect you to. Men of principle rarely do, and it is always, always the people around them who pay."

The door closed behind him with the softest click, and I sat alone with two untouched glasses of Burgundy and understood, with perfect clarity, what midnight meant.

* * *

I did not go straight home. I walked the long way, through lanes that stank of kelp at low tide, past the dark hulk of the tolbooth where Mrs. Abernathy lay behind bars for the crime of remembering, and I made myself look at it, made myself feel the full weight of the choice, because I refused to make it lightly. Shaw was right about one thing: staying was pride, maybe madness. Flight was abandonment—of Abernathy, of the printer's bargain, of every name in the cipher that had no one left to speak it. And there was a third thing, which I turned over all the way home like a coin worn smooth: that everything Shaw wanted burned could burn whether I stayed or ran. The shop was known now. The shop was marked. If I fled with Sorcha tonight, the fire would come anyway, and it would find only ash, and they would hunt us, and the plates in the printer's cellar would be the sole surviving evidence of anything—with no testimony, no witness, no atlas, no names attached. Truth needs a keeper, not just a hiding place.

So I went home, and I woke Sorcha gently, and I lied to her beautifully, and I hated myself for how good I had gotten at it.

"We're going to visit the printer," I said. "Tonight. Bring your coat and your drawings—all of them."

She didn't ask why. That was the part that broke me a little: a ten-year-old who no longer asked why when woken in the night, who simply dressed, who counted her drawings twice, who paused at the loft ladder and looked back at the little room that had become hers and stood very still for a moment, saying goodbye to it in advance, the way children and animals know things before adults do.

We went out through the yard, over the wall where the mortar was crumbled, down the vennel stinking of rotting cabbage stalks, and MacRuaridh the printer let us in with a shuttered lantern and no questions, because I had paid him in silver and fear and he respected both. We spent three hours by candle stub in his cellar. I carried every plate—the false Glenmara and the true one beneath it, and the ciphered leaves, and Vane's proofs, and Sorcha's drawings wrapped in oilcloth—down the stair and into the dry-stone vault behind the press room, and I laid them flat between felts, and MacRuaridh locked the iron door and put the key on a cord around his neck and swore, for the second and final time, that if anyone asked he had never seen me nor the child nor any plates at all, and that the vault held nothing but spoiled stock awaiting pulping.

It was nearly four in the morning when Sorcha and I slipped back over our own wall. She was asleep on her feet by then; I carried her up the loft ladder and put her to bed among her spare pencils, and I sat a while in the dark workshop listening to her breathe and looking at everything I owned—the bench, the burins in their roll, Vane's chair with its broken cane seat, the smell of thirty years of craft soaked into the very plaster—and I thought, calmly, the way one thinks before a storm: *if this is all it is, it has been enough.*

Then I banked the fire, and I went to bed, and I slept two hours like a stone.

* * *

I woke to the smell.

Not smoke—not yet—but that wrongness in the air, hot pitch and scorched lime, the smell of a building thinking about dying. For one heartbeat I lay in my cot in perfect confusion, my dreaming mind insisting it was morning and someone had lit the forge too early. Then the second heartbeat arrived carrying understanding, and I was up and at the window.

Fire stood in my workshop.

That is the only way I can describe it—not flames in the workshop but Fire itself, upright, present, occupying the room where I had worked half my life, orange and total, licking along the bench where the ink stones sat, closing its bright fist around Vane's chair. Below me, glass went. Above me, the loft—her loft—was still dark and silent, and I went up the ladder faster than I had ever moved in my life.

"Sorcha—" She was already awake, sitting up, eyes enormous in the light leaking through the floorboards. No scream. God help us, no scream; she had heard buildings die before. I got her down the back stair on my shoulder with the wet blanket from the rain barrel over both our heads, and out into the yard, and over the wall, and into the vennel, where we stopped and turned and watched.

The whole street was waking now, buckets and bells, the fire engine dragging up from the quay with its men shouting, but everyone understood, in the wordless way crowds understand such things, that this fire was not for fighting. It had been set thoroughly, in three places, and it ate the shop the way hunger eats. I stood in the lane with Sorcha's hand crushed in mine and I searched myself for grief and found something stranger instead: a terrible, scoured lightness. The bench was gone, and the burins, and Vane's proofs, and every letter and ledger and unfinished coastline—everything that could be taken, twisted, forged, subpoenaed, or buried was going up in light right before my eyes, and there was nothing left for them to find. Shaw had burned my whole life, and in doing so he had burned his own evidence of it. Everything burnable was gone.

Everything that mattered was in a cellar under a locked iron door, wearing a printer's key on a cord.

Sorcha leaned against my arm. Her face was lit orange, her eyes wide open and dry, watching the roof timbers go. When the sign above the door—*VANE, ENGRAVER*—blackened, curled, and fell in a shower of sparks, she spoke, very quietly, the first words either of us had said in an hour.

"They can't burn it now."

"No," I said. "They can't."

By dawn the shop was a black ribcage against the grey sky, and the street smelled of wet char, and neighbours stood in knots talking low, and a constable took my statement with visible embarrassment, writing *cause unknown* in his book while a sergeant of the watch watched him write it. I answered every question like a man in shock, which required no acting whatsoever.

It was mid-morning, Sorcha asleep at last on Mrs. Cameron's settle two doors down, when MacRuaridh's youngest apprentice found me in the crowd. He was pale, breathless, and he pressed a scrap of paper into my hand and vanished before I could thank him.

In MacRuaridh's cramped hand, five words:

*Plates safe. Come at once—news.*
