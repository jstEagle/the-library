## 20. The Atlas Goes Forth

The official atlas arrived on a Tuesday, in the manner of all official things: with ceremony, with a receipt requiring signature, and with the smell of government ink—that thin, vinegar sharpness that had hung about every document that ever ruined a man.

Orsen signed for it without letting the courier see their hands shake. The crate was heavy, bound in oiled canvas, stamped twice with the crown and once with the seal of the Commission of Inquiry into the Late Disturbances in the North. They carried it inside past the scorch-shadow on the stair where the doorframe had been replaced, new pine pale against old oak, and set it on Vane's workbench between the burnisher and the empty silver drawer.

The shop was quieter now. After the fire, after the printer, after the long weeks of waiting on Mrs. Abernathy's fate, Orsen had rebuilt only what was needed to work. One lamp. One bench. The great press sold to pay debts, its absence leaving a rectangle of unfaded floorboards like a grave rubbing. Sorcha slept upstairs still, though she no longer pretended to sleep at the sound of boots in the lane; she had learned instead to count them, and reported the totals each morning with a soldier's flatness that broke Orsen's heart in a way they refused to examine.

They opened the crate with Vane's own chisel, prying the lid as if opening something already dead.

*An Atlas of the Highlands and Western Isles: Compiled from the Surveys of Aldous Vane, Deceased, Completed under Direction of the Commissioners.* Their name did not appear anywhere—not on the title page, not in the preface, not even among the acknowledgments where the Commission thanked "the late Mr. Vane's unnamed assistant for the custody of materials." Unnamed. It had a certain accuracy to it, at least. They had spent forty-four years being unnamed; the Commission had merely made it official.

And there it was—Plate Forty-Seven. Glenmara.

Orsen had drawn it themselves, or rather had drawn the lie themselves, over the winter weeks when Shaw's men were searching houses and witnesses were vanishing from crofts along the coast like snuffed candles. They knew every deception in it intimately, the way a parent knows a child's faults. The village sat too near the shore road, tidy and loyal, its crofts arranged around a kirk that in truth stood half a mile east. The burn wandered where the survey said it must wander rather than where it went. And the ground west of the village—the ground where forty-one people had died and been buried without rites—was rendered as pleasant open pasture, untroubled, unmarked, carrying no notation at all except a small ornamental clump of trees that had never grown there.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Orsen had done their best work making the lie, because anything less than their best would have betrayed them. A clumsy forgery invites suspicion; a perfect one invites praise. Shaw had praised it, in his letter accompanying the proofs—a courteous, chilling note thanking them for rendering the district "with appropriate sensitivity to the historical record."

Orsen read the plate for a long time under the lamp. Then they took it to the window, tilted it toward the grey afternoon light, and looked through it.

Beneath the pasture, faint as breath on glass, the true Glenmara lay engraved into the copper's ghost—the kirk where it actually stood, the burn where it actually ran, and across the western field, in the forest-density symbols Vane had devised, the counted dead. Forty-one marks. Each one a name spelled out in coastline curves if you knew how to read them, which by now perhaps two hundred people in Edinburgh and London were learning to do.

The copies were out there. They had been out since before the fire.

* * *

The printer—Orsen never learned to think of him by any name but the printer, though Sorcha called him Mr. Quill because he had ink-stained fingers and a nose like a parrot's beak—had come for the plates three hours before the soldiers came for the shop. Orsen had handed them over wrapped in oilcloth inside a bale of rag paper, and had watched the cart disappear down the wet lane, and had gone back inside to wait for the burning with the strange calm of a person who has already swallowed the worst of the medicine.

Now, standing at the window with the false atlas open on the sill, Orsen found themselves doing arithmetic they could not stop. How many copies? The printer had promised thirty, struck quietly on his second press during nights when his legitimate work was supposedly drying. Thirty plates pulled, then the copper filed down and re-engraved with something innocent—a canal scheme, a survey of some English river—and struck again for cover. Thirty copies sounded absurdly few against an official printing of five hundred. But thirty copies did not need to win. They needed only to exist, and to fall into hands that would not let them die.

News came slowly and then, all at once, quickly.

First through the printer's devil, Tam, who appeared at the back door every ten days or so with coal dust on his knuckles and gossip folded into his delivery receipts. Tam was fifteen and morally weightless, which made him the most reliable conduit in the town, because he had no principles to compromise and therefore nothing to confess. He reported that Mr. Quill had shipped six copies north in March, disguised as ledger paper for an advocate's clerk in Edinburgh. He reported that two more had gone south to London sewn into a consignment of hymnals, which Orsen found either blasphemous or fitting. He reported, with relish, that a bookseller in the Grassmarket had been seen wrapping something in brown paper for a gentleman very much interested in "the true state of Glenmara parish," and that the gentleman had paid in guineas and left without drinking the tea offered him.

Then through Mrs. Abernathy, once she was home—thin, coughing, her hands still swollen from the irons, but home, installed in the rebuilt loft room with a fire that Sorcha fed so attentively it nearly smothered itself. Mrs. Abernathy had lost none of her networks in prison; if anything, confinement had consolidated them. She received letters now openly, being famous enough in the cause that hiding her correspondence would have been more suspicious than displaying it. She read aloud at the table some evenings, and this was how Orsen learned what was happening beyond the rain:

That in April, a copy of the palimpsest plate had been laid before a meeting of the antiquarian society in Edinburgh, held to candlelight, and passed hand to hand down a table of twenty gentlemen while the secretary recorded in the minutes—which were later leaked, naturally—that "the undersigned have examined a certain engraving and find its double impression consistent with no innocent explanation."

That in May, a pamphlet had appeared in London titled *A Short Account of the Parish of Glenmara, with a Plate*, consisting almost entirely of the plate itself and four lines of text: *This map is printed by authority. Hold it to the light, and read what authority has buried.*

That in June, someone—identity unknown, and Orsen suspected the printer knew exactly who and would die before saying—had purchased the official atlas from all three London booksellers authorized to sell it, and had returned each copy the following day with the pages of Plate Forty-Seven cut out and replaced with slips of paper reading only: *See the true plate. It exists. Ask your bookseller.*

"They are not even arguing," Mrs. Abernathy said one evening, lowering the letter, her voice still hoarse from whatever the cell had done to her throat. "That is what astonishes me. I expected petitions, rebuttals, learned essays on the unreliability of secondary impressions. Instead they simply show people the thing. It is its own argument." She looked at Orsen over the rim of her cup. "You built a question that answers itself, Mr. Blackwood."

"I built a forgery," Orsen said. "Two of them."

"You built a mirror," she said. "The Commission is discovering it does not enjoy looking into one."

* * *

Shaw was reassigned in July.

There was no announcement, no disgrace, no drama—nothing so satisfying as downfall. Tam brought the news with the week's receipts: Mr. Ellery Shaw, formerly Historiographer attached to the Northern Commission, had accepted an appointment to compile a digest of colonial surveys in the Caribbean, effective immediately, with the thanks of the Board for his "diligent service." His office in town was emptied on a Thursday. His furniture went by cart on Friday. By Saturday the rooms above the wool merchant's had been let to a family of seven who cooked herring every evening, so that the stairwell where Orsen had once listened to Shaw's measured footsteps now smelled permanently of fish smoke.

Quietly reassigned. Buried with courtesy, the same method he had used on Glenmara. Orsen understood it perfectly and understood, too, that it was not victory. Men like Shaw were not punished for failing to bury a truth; they were moved aside for having let it surface, which was a different thing entirely, and temporary. Somewhere in the Admiralty or the Colonial Office, Shaw was already reading surveys of sugar islands, patient and thorough, waiting for the weather to change. He would not write to Orsen again. He did not need to. His silence had always been his most fluent communication.

But the commission reopens—this was the sentence Orsen read four times, in Mrs. Abernathy's careful copied hand, in a letter from the Edinburgh advocate whose clerk had received the first six copies. *The commissioners find themselves unable to reconcile the printed atlas with the plate now in general circulation, and have petitioned the Board to reopen the inquiry into events at Glenmara, with power to summon witnesses and examine records previously withheld.* Reopened. Not concluded, not corrected—reopened, which meant everything was to be argued again, which meant Sorcha's testimony might yet be asked for, which meant everything Orson had won was provisional.

Vindication, Orsen discovered, felt almost nothing like they had imagined it in the dark months. They had imagined it as a door bursting open, light flooding in, names spoken aloud in a hall. What it actually resembled was rain wearing away a stone: imperceptible hour to hour, undeniable year to year. No messenger came to apologize. No officer was led through the town in irons. The Gazette printed nothing. The minister preached, the Sunday after the reopening was announced, on the duty of charity toward former adversaries, and Orsen sat in the pew with Sorcha beside them and understood that most of the congregation considered the whole matter an unfortunate disagreement among mapmakers.

Perhaps that was right. Perhaps history, which Orsen had spent a lifetime believing happened elsewhere, happened precisely like this—halfway, grudgingly, in the tone of people correcting an account because the ledger has been shown to a third party, not because their hearts have changed. The record bent toward truth the way a young tree bends toward light: slowly, against its habit, and only because something in the dark had insisted on growing.

* * *

On a bright cold morning in September, Sorcha spread her drawings on the workbench and asked to see the false plate and the true one side by side.

She was eleven now, taller, her hair grown back thick where the wound at her temple had cost it. She had been drawing Glenmara steadily all summer—not the massacre, never that, but the village: the kirk, the smithy, the burn, the shape of her grandmother's hands on a loom rendered in pencil with a precision that made Mrs. Abernathy go quiet whenever she passed the papers. Orsen had begun teaching her surveying properly, the way Vane had taught no one, and Sorcha took to it with an appetite that frightened and delighted them both.

Orsen laid the official atlas open to Plate Forty-Seven and, beside it, one of the thirty hidden copies, and held a candle behind the pair.

"Show me what's wrong," Orsen said. It had become their game, and their lesson.

Sorcha leaned close, her breath fogging the paper. Her finger moved without hesitation. "The kirk is wrong. It should be there—" she tapped east "—and the burn bends too early, it's flattering the road. And the trees aren't real, they're just decoration." Her finger stopped over the western field. She did not tap it. She rested her fingertips there, lightly, the way one touches a grave marker.

"And underneath?" Orsen asked.

"Underneath it's all correct," Sorcha said. "Underneath, it's home."

Orsen looked at the child—at the witness, at the keeper of the face they had been instructed to forget—and felt the full strangeness of what the winter had made. An apprentice who had believed maps were for other people's histories had forged a lie to hide a truth and a truth to destroy a lie, and had published both under another man's name, and had somehow, in the wreckage of a burned shop, acquired a daughter, a widow, a parrot-nosed conspirator, and a place in the record of the nation. None of it felt like triumph. All of it felt like weather changing.

"The commission reopens next month," Orsen said. "They will want to hear from me again. Formally, this time, on the record, with the atlas entered as evidence." They hesitated. "And they may want to hear from you."

Sorcha lifted her chin in a gesture Orsen had last seen on a woman in a drawing, hands steady at a loom.

"I know what I saw," she said. "I kept the names. That was my job. Grandmother said so."

Outside, the lane ran loud with ordinary life—the fish cart, the gulls, children shouting at hoops. On the bench lay a fresh sheet of paper, and beside it a letter, unopened, bearing the seal of the reopened Commission of Inquiry, requesting the attendance of one O. Blackwood, mapmaker, at the hearing in October, *to answer such questions as may be put concerning the compilation of the Atlas, and concerning other persons resident in your household.*

Orsen picked up the letter and broke the seal, and read the last line twice.

They had named Sorcha. In writing. On the record.

The record was bending at last—but toward them, too, and there would be no more hiding in margins, neither for the dead nor for the living.
