## 21. Mrs. Abernathy Comes Home

The letter came on a morning that smelled of wet lime and new-sawn pine, because the shop below was being rebuilt and the whole street had taken on the scent of making-things-over. Sorcha carried it up the outside stair herself, taking the steps carefully — her leg still remembered Glenmara in cold weather — and found Orsen at the long window with a slate in hand, tallying beams.

"From Edinburgh," she said, holding it out. "The seal's broken already."

"I know whose seal it is." Orsen set down the slate. Their hands, Sorcha noticed, were not quite steady. In the two years since the hearing they had learned to be steady about everything else — about soldiers, about magistrates, about fire — but a plain letter from a plain correspondent could still undo them. "You read it first, if you like."

Sorcha did not need to be told twice. She had learned her letters properly now, at the school by the kirk where Miss Geddes rapped knuckles for slovenly capitals, and she read aloud, standing in the wash of grey light, because some things wanted to be heard in a voice.

*Mrs. Abernathy presents her compliments and begs to inform you that the charges against her have been withdrawn entire. She is released this day. She has nowhere laid out for her but the road, and asks — if the offer still stands — whether there is room above a mapmaker's shop for a widow and her cat.*

Sorcha read it twice. The second time her voice went sideways on *the offer still stands*, and Orsen took the paper from her gently, as if it might bruise.

"Room," Orsen said, in the tone of someone doing sums that would not balance. "There is one bed, a loft, a hearth that smokes when the wind comes from the east, and a cat we do not yet possess."

"She'll bring the cat," Sorcha said. She knew it the way she knew weather coming off the firth. "She said once she'd sooner give up her own name than the cat."

Orsen looked at her then — that long, level look they had, the one that had frightened her the first week and now felt like being counted, like being included in an inventory of precious things. "Then we shall want a litter tray," they said, "and another chair. Go and tell Tam to cut the beams shorter. We're putting a wall through the loft after all."

* * *

Mrs. Abernathy came on the afternoon coach, three weeks later, in a gown gone shabby at the seams and a bonnet that had survived prison better than most women would have. Sorcha saw the coach from the window and was down the stairs before Orsen could call after her, out into the street with its puddles and its cart-ruts, running the way she had not let herself run since she was small enough to run without consequence.

Mrs. Abernathy saw her coming and opened her arms, and Sorcha hit her at full speed, and for a while neither of them managed any words at all. Mrs. Abernathy smelled of coal smoke and coach leather and underneath it something that was still, stubbornly, herself — lavender water and ink, the smell of letters smuggled in bread loaves, the smell of names kept safe.

"There now," she said at last, into Sorcha's hair. "There now. You've grown half a foot and learned to write your name, I'll wager, and you've been crying, and none of it needs explaining on a public street. Where is that apprentice of mine?"

"They're not anyone's apprentice anymore," Sorcha said, pulling back. "They put their name on the door. *Blackwood.* Painted it myself, the B is crooked."

"The B is crooked?"

"Orsen says a crooked B keeps away pride."

Mrs. Abernathy laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through and became something else, and Sorcha held onto her again until it passed. Behind them the coach rattled off toward the inn, and Orsen came down the outside stair slowly, with dignity, wiping their hands on their apron as though they had just happened to be interrupted at some important task.

"Mrs. Abernathy," they said. "You are welcome. The house is unfinished, the hearth smokes, and I cannot promise what we will eat tonight beyond eggs."

"You cannot promise eggs either," Mrs. Abernathy said, "I know your marketing habits, Orsen Blackwood. You buy ink before bread." She kissed them on both cheeks, formally, as though sealing a document. "Now. My cat is called Provost, he is in a basket under my arm, and he bites. Let us go up before he decides the whole enterprise is a mistake."

* * *

The household assembled itself the way a tide assembles a shoreline: not all at once, but by constant, patient arrival of small things.

Mrs. Abernathy claimed the new-walled corner of the loft for her own and hung it with dried herbs she had somehow acquired within a day of arriving — Sorcha never learned whether she grew them in her pockets. Provost the cat surveyed the premises from atop the map chest, the same chest in which Sorcha had once spent the worst hour of her life listening to soldiers' boots, and pronounced it adequate, and slept there every night thereafter, so that Sorcha learned to open the chest quietly, without flinching, one small victory at a time.

Mornings had a shape now. Orsen rose first and built the fire and made the porridge too thin, exactly as always. Mrs. Abernathy rose second and thickened the porridge, exactly as always, complaining that mapmakers had no notion what a body needed of oats. And Sorcha rose last, in the little room that used to be Vane's proofs-room, where the walls were pinned now with her drawings — the firth in fog, the kirk steeple, the rowan tree at the head of the glen drawn over and over until her hand stopped shaking partway through and she could finish the branches.

School began after Michaelmas. Miss Geddes was strict and dry as a biscuit and took an instant liking to Sorcha precisely because Sorcha drew in the margins of every page, which Miss Geddes declared to be "the habit of a mind that will not sit still, which is the only sort worth teaching." Sorcha learned arithmetic, geography — she corrected the schoolhouse atlas twice, politely, and was caned once and praised once for it, in that order — and history, which she hated.

History at school was a list of victories. She raised her hand about it exactly once.

"Please, miss. Who writes down the other ones?"

Miss Geddes had paused, chalk in the air. "The other ones?"

"The battles that get lost. The villages that aren't in the list. Somebody knows their names or nobody could ever find them again."

A long silence, the kind that in a smaller person would have been anger and in Miss Geddes was something more like assessment. "Somebody keeps them," she said finally. "Usually somebody inconvenient. Sit down, girl."

That night Sorcha asked Orsen if they were inconvenient. Orsen considered the question seriously, the way they considered everything, pen stopped mid-stroke on a coastline.

"We are extremely inconvenient," they said. "It is the most useful thing we have ever been."

* * *

Winter came down from the hills and settled over the town like a lid. The rebuilt shop reopened in November — *Vane & Blackwood, Maps and Surveys*, the sign hung by Tam and painted by Sorcha, who had insisted both names deserved equal letters and gotten her way, because by now everyone in the household had learned that Sorcha got her way about letters.

On the first snow-heavy evening, the three of them sat around the hearth that no longer smoked — Tam had fixed the flue in October, and Mrs. Abernathy still credited him for it weekly — and Orsen brought out the proofs.

Not the true plate. That lived elsewhere now, in the printer's keeping, in places Sorcha knew of but was never told plainly, because Orsen believed — and Mrs. Abernathy agreed — that some knowledge was a weight children should not carry even when the children had already carried worse. What Orsen brought out was the working copy, the practice sheets, the false Glenmara plate and its palimpsest twin pressed together like cupped hands.

"I have been thinking," Orsen said, "about the final form. The dedication page. It should not simply say the atlas is dedicated to the memory of Glenmara. It should say *who*. Every name we hold. Every name Mrs. Abernathy kept, and every name Sorcha's drawings have given us back, and every name in the journal."

The fire ticked. Provost stretched one paw toward the hearth and retracted it, offended by its heat.

"Ninety-one," Mrs. Abernathy said quietly. She did not consult notes; she never consulted notes; the names lived somewhere behind her ribs. "Ninety-one, counting the unborn. I have said before I would count the unborn, and I say it still. A village does not stop at its churchyard wall."

"Then ninety-one lines," Orsen said. "Set small, at the foot of the page, like a coastline's fine print. The part people skip." They looked across at Sorcha. "Except that you taught me nobody skips a coastline's fine print if they know what it cost."

Sorcha looked at the plates a long time. The false village lay on top — tidy, loyal, emptied of its dead, the version Shaw had wanted, the version that had gone to press in the official atlas with the crown's arms at its foot. Beneath it, when light came through, the true one: the crofts, the rowan, the burn, the kirk with its bell that the soldiers had melted down and that existed now only here, in engraved line, because a child had remembered it.

"Can I draw the last plate?" she asked. It came out smaller than she meant. "For the book. The survey plate. Miss Geddes says my perspective is—" she stopped, embarrassed.

"Your perspective," Orsen said, "is the only one in this house that was actually there. Yes. Draw it. We will engrave it together, and your hand will be in the copper forever."

Mrs. Abernathy reached over and squeezed Sorcha's knee under the table, once, hard, and said nothing, which from her was a great deal.

* * *

Spring found Sorcha drawing the glen from the loft window, though what she drew was not the view — roofs and chimneys and the grey ribbon of the road — but the memory laid over it, the other glen that lived behind her eyes. She had gotten good at holding both at once. Miss Geddes would have called it concentration. Orsen called it surveying.

Her pencil stopped at the rowan tree. She was adding the spring leaves when Mrs. Abernathy came up the stair with the post.

"One for you," she said, handing it over. "One for Orsen, from the London bookseller, which means money or trouble. And one—" she paused, and Sorcha watched her face do something complicated, "—one from a Mr. Ellery Shaw."

The name landed in the room like a stone in still water. Sorcha's stomach went cold and old, back to curfew nights and burning records, back to the smell of that particular politeness.

"He writes to *congratulate* us?" she said.

"He writes to say," Mrs. Abernathy read slowly, "that having been relieved of his official duties, he finds himself at leisure to pursue certain private historical researches, and would be interested to learn whether the forthcoming edition of the atlas intends to include the Glenmara material 'in its original form.' He signs himself your obedient servant." She lowered the letter. Her mouth was a thin line. "He knows the book is coming, Sorcha. He wants to know what's in it."

Downstairs, Orsen's chair scraped. Footsteps on the stair, and Orsen appeared in the doorway with the bookseller's letter still in hand, and read their faces the way they read coastlines — quickly, completely.

"Shaw," they said.

"You've had word too?" Mrs. Abernathy held up the letter.

"No. Worse." Orsen came into the room and stood by the window, and Sorcha saw their jaw set in the way it did before testimony, before raids, before every moment when the easy thing and the right thing parted company. "The London bookseller writes that our atlas has attracted attention. Advance orders from three universities, the Advocates' Library, and — he thought it a great honour — a request from a private gentleman for the *first copy off the press*, at any price we care to name. Signed E. Shaw."

Sorcha looked down at her half-drawn rowan tree, its roots reaching into the paper's edge, and understood, with a certainty that felt older than her fourteen years, what the hook in the bait was. He did not want to admire the book. He wanted the first copy in his hands, ahead of the world, to learn what it proved — and to decide, perhaps, how much of it would ever be found.

"Well," Mrs. Abernathy said briskly, folding his letter in quarters, the way she folded everything disagreeable. "Then we had better make certain the first copy off the press is perfect."

"It will be," Orsen said. Then, softer, looking at Sorcha's drawing rather than at either of them: "It will be more than he expects."

Outside, the firth was turning silver with evening, and inside, the rowan tree waited for its roots, and somewhere south an enemy was reading their address. Sorcha picked up her pencil and drew the roots all the way to the edge of the paper, and past it, as far as it would go.
