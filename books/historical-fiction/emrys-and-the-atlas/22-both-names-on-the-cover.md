## 22. Both Names on the Cover

The proofs arrived from Edinburgh in a crate that smelled of ink and cold sea, and I carried it into the shop the way one carries an infant—awkwardly, against the chest, unwilling to let anyone else touch it.

Eleven years since the fire. Eleven years since I stood in the ash of Master Vane's shop and understood that everything burnable had already been saved. The shop was rebuilt on the same footprint, the same crooked floorboards copied from memory because I could not bear to lay them straight. The same window, north-facing, where the light falls grey and even and honest—the light mapmakers are owed.

I cut the crate open with Vane's own knife, the one I keep in the drawer beneath his chair, though the chair is mine now and has been for years. The topmost proof was the title page, and there they were, both names, set in type by hands that never knew either of us:

*The General Atlas of the Northern Highlands: Being a True and Particular Survey.*
*Aldous Vane, Master of the Craft.*
*Orsen Blackwood, His Apprentice and Executor.*

I had imagined this moment for eleven years, and in all my imaginings my hands were steady. They were not steady. They shook like a young man's, like an apprentice's, like the hands I'd had at twenty-two when I believed maps were for other people's histories and mine was not worth the ink to draw it.

*Orsen Blackwood.* In print. Beside his.

I sat down in the master's chair—I still called it that; I expect I always would—and read the names until the letters stopped swimming. Outside, Edinburgh's bells rang the quarter hour. Somewhere below, Mrs. Abernathy was arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the freshness of herring, her voice carrying up through the floorboards as it had carried through every morning for nine years now, filling rooms that had once known only silence and my careful, cowardly quiet.

There was a knock at the stair door. Two knocks, then a pause, then one—a rhythm we had invented during the raid years and never quite abandoned, though no soldiers had come near us since the commission reopened and Shaw was reassigned to some colonial survey where his thoroughness could do less harm.

"You've been up there since dawn," Sorcha said, coming in without waiting. She was twenty-one now, tall and brown-haired and unafraid in a way that still astonished me daily. She carried two cups of tea and set one on the corner of the drafting table without asking which corner, because she knew which corner—it was the corner nearest the window, always, the corner where Vane used to rest his cup and leave rings on the wood that I had long ago stopped trying to erase. Some marks deserve to remain.

"The proofs came," I said.

She looked at me. She did not look at the crate, not yet. She looked at *me*, the way she had learned to do in the loft above the old shop, when she watched my routines for weeks before she trusted them, before she trusted me. "And?"

"And they're real," I said, and my voice cracked on the word like ice on a loch. "They're real, Sorcha. Both names."

She put down her cup and pulled the title page from the crate, and she read it standing in the north light, and I watched her face do what my face had done—something breaking and something mending in the same instant. Then she laughed, short and startled, and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist the way she had done when she was ten years old and drawing Glenmara from memory with a precision that frightened us both.

"Both names," she said. "He'd have hated it."

"He'd have pretended to hate it," I corrected. "He'd have stood exactly where you're standing and turned very red and said something dry about apprentices presuming."

"Presumptuous apprentice," she agreed, grinning. "That's what he called you, isn't it? Mrs. Abernathy told me. She said he complained constantly and kept every letter you ever drafted for him."

Every letter. Every scrap of my hand he had preserved, interleaved into the journal that started all of this—he had been building my case for existence years before I knew I needed one. He had believed in both names before I did. That was the kind of man he was, and the atlas was only the largest of the many testaments he left behind.

Sorcha knelt at the crate then, properly, and began lifting out the plates one by one—the coastlines I had traced in grief, the forests whose density counted our dead, the curve of a shore that spelled a name when you knew how to read it. And last, wrapped separately in oiled cloth because it deserved its own protection, the Glenmara plate. The true one, engraved beneath the false, visible only when held to flame. We had printed both versions for years now, the lie for the official record and the truth for anyone patient enough to bring a candle close. But this edition—this final, complete edition—carried only the truth. There was no longer any need for palimpsest. The commission had reopened. The officers had been named. The record, so long redrawing itself in their favour, had finally bent.

Not broken. Bends. Records are like rivers; they bend slowly, and only under constant pressure, and someone must stand in the current the whole time. That was Vane's lesson. That was the whole of the atlas, really, if you held it to the light.

"Glenmara first?" she asked.

"Glenmara first," I said. "It's always Glenmara first."

She unwrapped the plate and laid it on the table between the teacups, and we looked at it together as we had looked at it a hundred times, and as always I saw it twice: once as cartography, coastline and contour and the careful symbols of a dead master's cipher, and once as testimony—the village that soldiers burned twice, once in flesh and once in paper, and that we refused to let die the second death.

* * *

We spent three days collating.

It is tedious work, collation, and I have done it for thirty years, and I will tell you what no one tells apprentices: tedium is where love lives in this trade. Anyone can feel the romance of a finished plate. Only someone who loves the work can feel it in the folding, the gathering, the checking of signatures against a pattern sheet, page after page after page, alone or nearly alone, hour after hour. Mrs. Abernathy came up on the second day to help, though her eyes are not what they were, and mostly she sorted by touch and talked—about Vane, about the smuggling years, about the widow she had been before she became the woman who kept names. She liked that name. She had earned it the hard way, and she wore it the way other women wore brooches.

On the third day she paused over a gathering, ran her thumb along its edge, and said, "You'll put the dedication in?"

"It's already in," I said. "Page after the title. You approved the list yourself."

"Aye, but approving a list and reading it printed are different animals." She set the gathering down and looked at me over her spectacles. "Have you read it printed yet, Orsen?"

I hadn't. I had avoided the dedication page through three days of collation, turning past it with a collator's efficiency that fooled neither of us.

"No," I admitted.

"Then do it now," she said, "with me here, because some pages you should not read alone." She had said almost the same thing to Sorcha once, years ago, about eating at a table instead of hiding behind the loft door. Mrs. Abernathy's whole wisdom was contained in variations of that sentence.

So I took the dedication proof and I read it aloud, in the north light, while she sat with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at kirk.

*To the memory of the souls of Glenmara,* I read, and my voice held, and then it did not hold, and Mrs. Abernathy's hand covered mine on the table and I continued.

*Iain MacCormick, shepherd.*
*Mairi MacCormick, his wife.*
*Donald MacCormick, aged fourteen.*
*Elsbeth Ross, midwife.*
*Callum Ross, ferryman.*

Forty-one names. It had taken four years to gather them all—through Abernathy's correspondents, through the printer's underworld network, through parish records that survived in odd corners the burning had missed, and through one source above all others: the memory of a child who drew her village again and again, house by house, field by field, family by family, until the drawing was not a wound but a monument. Forty-one names, and beneath them a line I had fought with for a year:

*Who lived, and were said not to have lived. Who died, and were recorded as never having been. This atlas restores them to the ground they walked on.*

Below that, smaller:

*Prepared for press by Aldous Vane, who gathered these testimonies at cost of his life, and completed by Orsen Blackwood, who learned too late that history does not happen elsewhere.*

Mrs. Abernathy listened with her eyes closed. When I finished she opened them and said, "Aldous would want a comma changed somewhere, just to be difficult."

"He would," I said. "Let him. He's earned his commas."

She laughed, wet-eyed, and patted my hand, and went back to her sorting, and I sat a moment longer with the forty-one names spread before me. Somewhere in the city, in a print shop that had once taken Vane's silver and my desperation, the formes were being locked for the full run—five hundred copies, more than any atlas needed, because this one was never meant only for gentlemen's libraries. Copies would go to parishes. To schools, if any schoolmaster dared. To Edinburgh and London, where the palimpsest copies had already done their quiet corrosive work years before. Five hundred copies, each one carrying forty-one names and two on the cover, travelling out into the world like seeds on a wind, and no power in Britain could call them back. That was the whole secret of print, the thing the historians and commissioners never quite grasped until it was too late: you cannot burn what has already scattered.

* * *

On the fourth day, Sorcha asked for the table.

I knew what she meant. There was only ever one request that required the whole drafting table cleared, every tool lifted, every cup removed—including the cup, even that cup, even the ring it had worn into the wood—and I cleared it without argument, because some things you do not argue about in this shop.

Her final survey plate. Her contribution to the atlas, promised for six years, begun and abandoned a dozen times. Not Glenmara—that was drawn, that was finished, that belonged to memory and to the dead. This was something else. This was the plate she had wanted to make for herself, and she had made me wait until she was ready, and I had waited gladly, because I remembered being twenty-two and believing I had nothing of my own to add to another man's work.

She worked for two days. I did not watch. That was the discipline of it—she worked at the cleared table and I worked at mine across the room, pretending to correct proofs I had already corrected four times, listening to the scratch of her stylus, the whisper of the burnisher, the small sounds of a craft becoming hers. Once she swore softly at a line that wouldn't sit right, in Gaelic, a word Mrs. Abernathy had taught her and probably regretted teaching. Once she stopped entirely for the better part of an hour, and I heard nothing but the clock, and I did not turn around, because I understood she was somewhere else—in a loft above a different shop, perhaps, or a village that existed now only in copper and ink and the mind of one woman who had been ten years old and carried the whole of it out through the smoke.

On the evening of the second day she said, "Come and look."

I came and looked.

It was a map of the road. That was all, at first glance—the road from Glenmara south, the road she had walked at ten years old with a satchel and a note that said *forget the face*, the road that ended at our door. She had drawn it with the whole of Vane's technique and something extra besides, something that was purely hers: mile-stones marked not with distances but with moments. Here, a cairn: *first night, slept in the heather*. Here, a ford: *a woman gave me bread and asked no questions*. Here, a crossroads: *almost turned back*. And at the southern end, where the road reached the town and became streets, one small building rendered in perfect detail—the shop, the window, the door.

Beneath the door, in her precise, unhurried lettering, a single word of label such as maps give to churches and market towns and seats of nobility:

*Haven.*

I stood over it a long time. The clock ticked. Downstairs, faintly, Mrs. Abernathy was singing to herself while she cooked, some old song about a boat coming home.

"Sorcha," I said at last, and found I had to start again. "Sorcha, do you know what you've done?"

"Mapped the road," she said. "Vane's method. Witness geography, he called it—everything a place means to the person walking it, not just what it looks like from above." She shrugged, but her ears had gone pink. "He wrote about it in the margins. Said no atlas was complete that only showed what the land looks like. It ought to show what the land costs."

"He never drew one himself."

"No," she said. "He left it for me, I think. Or I took it. Either way." She looked at the plate, at the little door at the end of the long road. "It goes last, Orsen. After the dedication. After Glenmara. Let the book end with arrival."

* * *

The atlas went forth in the spring, as atlases should, when the roads dry and the printers' carts can move.

I stood in the doorway of the rebuilt shop and watched the crates go onto the carrier's wagon, five hundred copies bound in grey cloth with both names stamped in gilt on the spine, and I felt—what did I feel? Not triumph. I had learned enough by then to distrust triumph; it belongs to victors, and we had not won anything so much as refused to lose. What I felt was closer to what a shepherd feels sending the flock down off the high pasture in autumn: relief, and worry, and the knowledge that from here on they must weather what weather comes without him.

Sorcha stood beside me, and Mrs. Abernathy beside her, three generations of a family that shared no blood but had shared a conspiracy of memory for eleven years, and none of us spoke until the wagon turned the corner and was gone.

"Well," said Mrs. Abernathy. "Someone should put the kettle on."

Inside, the shop looked strangely empty with the crates gone, and also strangely full—full of the absence that is really presence, the way a finished drawing leaves the white paper around it charged. Sorcha went straight to the wall where her childhood drawings hung pinned beside Vane's proofs, the first Glenmara she ever drew at age ten beside the final engraved plate, child's trembling hand and master's burin telling the identical story. She unpinned nothing. She only touched the oldest drawing once, lightly, two fingers, the way you'd touch a gravestone or a cheek.

"They'll try to dismiss it," I said. "Shaw's successors. They'll call it sentiment. A memorial, not a survey."

"Let them," she said. "Sentiment lasts longer than surveys. Nobody quotes a boundary line at a funeral." She turned and grinned at me, and she had her mother's grin—I knew that now, having found, through Mrs. Abernathy's correspondents, a cousin twice removed who remembered Mairi MacCormick's smile. "Besides, Orsen. They said the village never existed. Now there's a book with its name on forty-one pages and both ours on the cover. Which of those facts do you think outlives whom?"

I had no answer. I rarely did, with her. I crossed the shop instead and took down the atlas's one remaining copy—the printer's proof, the first assembled book, which I had kept back—and I opened it to the title page, and then to the dedication, and then, at the very end, to her road.

*Haven.*

Outside, the bells rang the hour, and the north light came through the window grey and even and honest, and fell across the open page, and across the table where a dead man's cup had left its ring, and across the two of us standing in a shop that had been burned once and would not be burned in any way that mattered, ever again.

History happens elsewhere, I had believed all my life. It does not. It happened here. It happens wherever someone refuses to forget the face.

And somewhere on a road between here and Edinburgh, in a crate on a carrier's wagon, the atlas was already travelling—outward, onward, unstoppable as water finding the sea—carrying both names home.
