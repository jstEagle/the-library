## 1. The Weight of Rivers

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width when Ulyana finally admitted the cold had won.

Not the work — never the work — but the cold had crept into the ink pot itself, thickening the black to a sluggish tar that dragged at the nib and left the river lines looking ill, half-drowned. Ulyana set down the pen, flexed stiff fingers above the flame until feeling returned as pins and needles, and allowed a single moment of honesty: *I cannot do this alone anymore.*

Then the moment passed, because it had to, and Ulyana cupped the ink pot in both palms like something rescued from drowning.

The workshop smelled of Emrys still. That was the cruelty no one warned you about — that the dead do not leave all at once but in installments, exhaled from their belongings over months. His pipe tobacco lingered in the grain of the drafting table. His lye soap clung to the drying rack where his brushes stood at attention, bristles up, exactly as he'd left them on the morning he didn't come home. And beneath it all, the mineral smell of the papers themselves, hundreds of them, the smell of rivers waiting to be named.

Ulyana warmed the ink and thought about weight.

It was an obsession now, three weeks old, born the night the soldiers came for the printing houses on Vodna Street and Ulyana understood that everything in this room would one day have to be carried out of it by hand. So Ulyana weighed things. The copper plates were impossible — forty pounds each, beautiful and fatal, good only for melting into bullets or bribes. The finished atlas, bound, was four pounds eleven ounces. The loose plates wrapped in oilcloth, six pounds even. A satchel could hold the plates and two days' bread and nothing else. A coat with deep pockets could hold the key sections folded to the size of a prayer book: the eastern crossings, the fords at Stary Brod, the pontoon anchors, the places where the river ran shallow enough to walk through if you knew which stones to trust.

Emrys had known. Emrys had walked every one of those stones with a surveyor's chain and a notebook hidden in his boot, an old man wading into freezing water on the pretext of sketching herons, while the occupation authorities believed him a harmless antiquarian obsessed with bridges nobody used anymore.

*They are not harmless,* he'd told Ulyana once, tapping the eastern bridge on a proof sheet. *Nothing is harmless. A bridge is a promise that two places belong to each other. Whoever holds the promises holds the country.*

Ulyana had laughed then. Ulyana did not laugh now.

The candle guttered; the wind had found some new gap in the shutters, or an old one had widened. Winter was coming down off the hills early this year, and the city below the workshop window lay under its first real frost, rooftops silvered, chimneys standing in ranks like a regiment at rest. Somewhere down by the quay a barge horn sounded, long and low, and Ulyana's hand paused over the plate mid-stroke.

Discipline. Emrys had drilled it in like a carpenter driving nails. *A line drawn in fear is a crooked line.* He'd said it the first week of the apprenticeship, when Ulyana was fourteen and all elbows and hunger, and had repeated it so many times over nine years that it had stopped being advice and become anatomy. So Ulyana breathed out, steadied the wrist against the little sandbag he'd filled from Emrys's own supply, and drew the Vistula's southern bend in one unbroken stroke, clean as a blade.

Beautiful. It was terrible how beautiful it was. The plate showed the river as it had been before the war — every ferry landing, every millrace, every footbridge marked in Emrys's small precise hand with load ratings and water depths measured across three seasons. An occupying army would pay a fortune for it. An occupied people would bleed for it. Either way, possession meant death; the only question was whose.

Ulyana turned to the final plate. The eastern bridge.

It lay under the lamp like a wound that hadn't decided whether to close. Emrys had begun it in September — the last survey, the last notes, his handwriting suddenly cramped and hurried compared to the serene capitals of the earlier plates. *Span 3, stone abutment, sound. Span 4, pier cracked 1911, monitored. Fords: none viable within 4 km. Rail spur intact.* And then, in the margin, in pencil, almost erased, almost apologetic, a single word Ulyana had not yet been able to interpret:

*Ask.*

Ask whom? Ask what? There was no one to ask. There was a hole in the world shaped like a stooped old man with ink-stained cuffs, and Ulyana kept catching herself turning to speak to it, the way you keep reaching for a limb that's gone.

Three weeks since they'd pulled him from the river. Three weeks since the constable had come to the door with his hat in his hands and his eyes carefully elsewhere, saying *accident, slipped on the ice already forming, these old men and their morning walks,* while behind him two soldiers sat idling in a truck, engines running, watching the windows. Ulyana had nodded and thanked him and closed the door and stood with her forehead against the wood until her breathing steadied, and then she had gone upstairs and taken inventory of everything Emrys had ever hidden, because grief, in this city, was a luxury you performed in private and quickly.

The soldiers had watched the workshop for four days afterward. Ulyana had seen them from the attic slit, seen the truck parked across the lane with its tarpaulin down, and so she had done what Emrys taught: nothing. She'd opened the shutters wide, hung washing, bought bread loudly at the corner shop, let the whole street see the apprentice sweeping the step of the dead man's shop. Invisibility was a craft too. She was getting better at it than she'd ever wanted to be.

On the fifth day the truck went away, and that night she lit the candle low behind the blackened glass and began finishing the atlas.

Now the candle was dying again, and with it the working night. Ulyana blew it out rather than let it drown itself, and the dark came in like water filling a hull. For a while she simply sat, listening to the building settle, to the frost ticking against the shutters, to her own heart doing its stubborn, unremarkable work.

Four pounds eleven ounces. She'd weighed the bound atlas again tonight, after the last proof dried. She kept thinking about the number the way other people prayed.

*If we must flee.* The *we* was itself a kind of fiction, a courtesy she paid to a house that held only her. But the maps demanded a *we*. Every route she planned — south through the fish market, past the rail yard, over the pontoon bridge if it came to that — she calculated for two bodies, because Emrys had made her promise, years ago, in one of his rare serious moods: *If anything happens to me, the atlas goes to the Allies, and you go with it. Not the atlas without you. They'll need a living witness, child. Paper can be forged. You can't.*

She hadn't understood him then. She was beginning to.

Ulyana rose, knees cracking, and did the closing ritual Emrys had insisted upon even in peacetime: plates into the false bottom of the map chest, proofs into the stove — she permitted herself one small fire, the flawed impressions only, watching the eastern bridge curl and go orange and become smoke — tools oiled and racked, the drafting table wiped down until it showed nothing but honest wood. Last of all she took the bound atlas from its hiding place inside the lining of Emrys's old winter coat and weighed it in her hands one more time, an absurd superstition she no longer bothered to be embarrassed by.

Heavy. It should have felt heavier. Four pounds eleven ounces, and somewhere in those pages was the word *ask*, and somewhere beyond the window, past the frost and the sleeping roofs and the frozen river, was whatever the word was pointing at.

She was hanging the coat back on its peg when she heard it.

Footsteps on the lane. Slow ones — the careful, deliberate pace of someone carrying something heavy, or someone who didn't want to be heard and had given up pretending. They stopped. Directly below the window. Directly below the door.

Ulyana stood motionless in the dark workshop with her master's coat in her hands and her heart going hard against her ribs, and listened to the silence that follows footsteps, the listening silence of someone deciding.

Then came the sound of something being set down gently on the doorstep — softly, the way you put down something alive — followed by the quick retreat of shoes on frosted cobbles, faster now, running, fading toward the quay and gone.

* * *

It took Ulyana a full minute to make herself move. Discipline, she told herself. A line drawn in fear is a crooked line. But her hands were not steady as she lifted the lantern, trimmed the wick to a bead of light, and descended the stairs to the street door.

Through the frosted pane, the shape on the step resolved slowly: small. Bundle-shaped. A bundle with a hood.

Ulyana opened the door on the cold and the child both.

A girl, perhaps seven years old, asleep or pretending to be, curled around herself on the worn stone like a comma. Her coat was good quality once, now too big for her, sleeves rolled and re-rolled. Her lips were blue at the edges. Beside her, weighted down with a stone against the wind, lay a note in a woman's hand — hurried, elegant, written by someone who had learned to write beautifully and was now using the skill for speed:

*Her name is Mira. She has not spoken since September. Feed her tonight and forget my face. Forget hers, if you can. If anyone asks, you found nothing.*

Ulyana read it twice. The frost crept up her ankles. Down the empty lane the river waited under its new ice, black and patient, holding everything it had ever been given.

The girl opened her eyes then — dark, enormous, older than seven — and looked up at Ulyana with an expression that was not fear exactly, and not trust either, but the flat, exhausted watchfulness of someone who had already seen the thing that everyone else was afraid of.

And there, where the collar of her oversized coat gaped open, Ulyana saw the corner of something sewn into the lining. Something small and rectangular and photograph-shaped, stitched in with clumsy, desperate thread.

Ulyana looked down the lane, at the frost, at the running footprints already filling with snow.

Then she picked the child up — lighter than the atlas, God help her, lighter than four pounds eleven ounces — and carried her inside, and closed the door on the winter.
