## 2. A Child at the Door

The girl did not cry.

That was the first thing Ulyana noticed, once the door was barred and the candle moved close. The child sat on the workbench where she had been set down — among the ink stones and the brass weights, as though she were another instrument to be catalogued — and she simply watched. Dark eyes, too old for a face of perhaps six years. Her hands lay folded in her lap like two small sleeping birds.

"You're frozen through," Ulyana said, mostly to herself, because she had spent eleven months talking to no one but a dead man's tools and had not yet unlearned the habit of speech. She unhooked the coat's remaining buttons with fingers that shook from cold or from something else. "Can you tell me your name?"

Nothing. Not a blink of refusal, not the stubborn silence of a child who has been told to hush. Just that watching, steady as water finding its level.

"Mira," Ulyana tried, because the woman in the lane had said it — *her name is Mira* — before her footsteps fled. "Are you Mira?"

The girl's gaze flickered, once, toward the sound of the name, the way a compass needle trembles near iron. But she gave nothing else.

The stove had gone cold hours ago; Ulyana fed it kindling now, then coal, and dragged the child's bench closer to the growing warmth. Steam began to rise from the wet wool of the coat as she worked it off those narrow shoulders, and the girl let her, arms lifting obediently when needed, like a doll — no, not like a doll, Ulyana corrected herself. Like a soldier submitting to inspection. Children did not hold still this way. Children squirmed and complained and demanded. This stillness had been taught.

The lining was worse than she'd thought. Sewn into the left breast, over the heart, a rectangle of oilcloth had been stitched flat against the fabric with thread so fine it was nearly invisible — surgeon's thread, Ulyana thought, or the thread of someone who had done desperate needlework by candlelight and could not afford a mistake. Her own hands knew that quality of desperation. She had sewn Emrys's final plates into the false bottom of a flour chest not three weeks ago, and her stitches had looked exactly like these.

"Forgive me," she murmured, and picked the seam.

The oilcloth packet came free with a whisper of tearing thread. Inside: a photograph, small, creased once across its middle as if it had been folded and hidden and unfolded many times, and beneath it, folded into eighths, a scrap of paper bearing a single line in a woman's hurried hand:

*Forget this face. If you cannot forget it, hide it. If you cannot hide it, make them remember.*

Ulyana turned the photograph toward the candlelight.

Four soldiers stood at the eastern railing of the bridge. She knew the bridge the way she knew the lines of her own palm — Emrys had mapped it last, in October, walking its length twice with his surveyor's chain while the occupiers' sentries watched him from the far tower, and he had come home that evening gray-faced and silent and had drunk his tea without touching the bread. Three weeks later his heart had stopped in this very room, though Ulyana had sometimes wondered, in the worst hours of the night, whether a heart simply stops on its own.

In the photograph the soldiers were laughing. That was what struck first — the laughter, the loose shoulders, one man with his cap pushed back and a bottle raised. Ordinary men on an ordinary afternoon. But they were standing at the railing beside a gap in it, a section maybe six meters wide where the parapet had been torn away down to the girders, and behind them, at the edge of the frame, half-cut by the border of the print, stood the shapes of people. Civilians. A crowd of them, dozens, packed against the railing on either side of the gap. And the soldiers were laughing at them.

There was writing on the back. A date — the twelfth of November, four days after Emrys walked the bridge — and beneath the date, in the same hand as the note in her palm: *They did not fall. They were put.*

Ulyana set the photograph face-down on the bench very carefully, the way one sets down a thing that might go off.

The official record — the record she had read pinned to the customs house door like everyone else — said the eastern span had collapsed under strain of ice. An act of the winter, regrettable. Forty-one souls lost, may God receive them. She had copied that notice into her memory the way she copied everything, idly, completely, and had thought nothing more of it except grief, general and shapeless, for forty-one strangers.

Forty-one strangers who had been standing on a bridge at dusk in November. On a span whose parapet was already torn open. Who had been *put* there.

Her eyes went to the survey cabinet against the far wall — Emrys's last plates, the eastern sheets, locked behind the panel he had shown her only once, the week before he died, when his voice had already begun its long retreat. *"If anything happens to me,"* he had said, *"finish the rivers. Never mind the bridges."* She had thought it sentiment then, an old man's fear of leaving work undone. Now the words rearranged themselves into something colder. *Never mind the bridges.* As if the bridges were the dangerous part. As if he had known what his measurements would show anyone clever enough to read them.

She looked at the girl. The girl looked back.

"What did you see?" Ulyana asked softly, kneeling so their faces were level. It was foolish — the child was mute, the woman in the lane had said so plainly, *she doesn't speak, they took her voice along with everything else* — but some questions press at a person until they must be asked even knowing there will be no answer. "What happened on the bridge, Mira?"

The dark eyes held hers. And then the girl did the strangest thing, the thing that Ulyana would return to for many nights afterward: she lifted one small hand and drew it slowly across her own throat, and then out, outward, both hands pushing at the empty air — pushing something away, something heavy, something falling — and her face never changed at all while she did it.

Then her hands returned to her lap, folded, patient, and she was a mute child on a workbench again, and the stove ticked, and the wind worried at the shutters, and outside the snow kept falling on the footprints of a woman who had run into the winter without looking back.

* * *

Ulyana did not sleep.

She told herself it was the atlas — the final plate, the southern marshlands, only the lettering left to do, and the lettering was Emrys's, she had promised to reproduce his hand stroke for stroke so the atlas would be wholly his when it finally left this house. She lit three candles and uncapped the ink and worked until her wrist ached, and the letters went down clean and true, and all the while the photograph lay face-down on the corner of the drafting table like a card dealt against her.

She turned it over seven times. Seven times she looked at the laughing men, at the gap in the parapet, at the crowded shapes at the frame's edge, and each time her eye measured — helplessly, professionally, the habit deeper than thought — the width of the gap against the known span. Six meters. She had the plate upstairs that said so, in Emrys's exacting hand: *eastern parapet breached, approx. 6m, cause undetermined, rail stanchions cut clean.* Cut clean. She had read that line a hundred times and filed it under decay, vandalism, the ordinary rot of war-touched things.

*They did not fall. They were put.*

At some hour past midnight the girl woke — Ulyana heard the small rustle from the cot she'd made up beside the stove — and instead of crying, which was the natural office of children woken in strange rooms, she sat up and checked, first thing, with both hands, the front of whatever garment she wore. Checking for the photograph. Finding it gone, her whole body went rigid, and her eyes flew around the room until they found Ulyana at the drafting table, and found, in Ulyana's hand, the familiar rectangle.

Ulyana rose and brought it to her and pressed it back into those small palms. "It's yours," she whispered. "I only looked. I'm sorry I only looked."

Mira clutched it to her chest and lay back down, and was asleep again within minutes, breath slow and even, one fist still closed over the oilcloth. Whatever else had been taken from her, whatever had cut the words out of her throat, it had not touched her certainty about that one small picture. It was the only thing she owned. It was, Ulyana understood suddenly, the whole of her inheritance: proof, carried next to the heart, stitched in by a mother's hand with surgeon's patience.

A mother who had run. A mother who had said *forget this face* and meant it as a blessing, the only kind left to give.

Ulyana stood at the window with the candle shuttered and looked out at the sleeping lane. Snow had filled every footprint; the street belonged to no one. Across the way, the warden's house was dark, but she knew — everyone on the lane knew — that Warden Brozek kept a ledger by his bed, and that in it went names, hours, comings and goings, the small commerce of suspicion that kept a man in coal and cigarettes during an occupation. A child appearing at the mapmaker's door in the dead of winter would be worth half a page.

And if the ledger traveled upward — and ledgers always traveled upward, to the district office, to the security bureau with its green-shuttered windows on Constitution Square — then someone there might ask questions she could not answer safely. Where the child came from. Whose child. Why a woman had run from this doorway like a hunted thing. And whether the mapmaker's apprentice, working alone these eleven months in a house full of paper and secrets, had any connection to the events of the twelfth of November at the eastern bridge.

Until tonight, Ulyana had been invisible. That had been the whole architecture of her survival, laid brick by brick since Emrys died: no visitors, no market on busy days, no letters, no opinions, a woman-shaped absence in a city full of them. The occupiers did not waste attention on absences.

But you could not be an absence while holding evidence of a massacre. You could not carry a photograph of laughing soldiers and a child who mimed the falling of the dead and expect the night to pass over you like weather. The moment she had picked that seam open, the moment she had read *make them remember*, the invisible life had ended — quietly, without ceremony, the way Emrys's heart had stopped — and some other life had begun, and she did not yet know its shape.

She only knew, standing in the dark with the frost flowering on the glass, that she was afraid in a way grief had never made her. Grief was a weight; it slowed you, but it held you to the earth. This was different. This was the feeling of a contour line on a map where the ground drops away faster than any road can climb — the cartographer's warning sign, the little row of teeth that says: beyond here, fall.

Behind her, the child slept on. On the drafting table, the atlas lay nearly finished, four pounds eleven ounces of rivers and roads and bridges, every crossing of the Vorsa rendered in a dead man's hand — including, on the eastern sheet, a bridge whose parapet had been cut clean.

Ulyana looked at the atlas for a long time.

Then she went and fetched the survey cabinet key from its hiding place inside the hollow leg of Emrys's stool, and unlocked the panel, and drew out the eastern plates, and spread them under the candle beside the photograph, and began — line by careful line, measurement by measurement — to understand what her master had known, and why he had died telling her to leave the bridges alone.
