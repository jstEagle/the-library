## 4. Neighbors Who Count Windows

The figure across the street closed its notebook with a small, decisive snap, like a trap taking a mouse, and walked on toward the quay.

Ulyana did not move from behind the shutter for a long time. The candle at her back threw her shadow against the wall, and she watched it tremble there—taller than she was, thinner, a shape that could have been anyone or no one. That was the trick of survival in an occupied city: to become the shadow. To be counted among things rather than people.

But the man had counted the windows. One. Two. Three. And he had written it down, which meant that somewhere in some office above the fish market, on some shelf between ledgers of grain requisitions and lists of names, there was now a record of this house. Its windows. Its habits. Perhaps its hours of light—the workshop lamp burned late, and late-burning lamps were the kind of detail men like him collected the way children collected river stones: without knowing why, only that they were smooth and plentiful and fit neatly in the hand.

She let the shutter fall and stood in the dark, listening to the building breathe around her. The presses were cold. Emrys's chair still held the dent of his body; she had not been able to bring herself to sit in it, and now she never would, because a chair that held two ghosts was worse than one.

*They know the house,* she thought. Not yet the work. Not yet the atlas wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards, three boards up from the hearth where the wood was already warped from being lifted so often. But houses, once written down, had a way of being visited.

She climbed the stairs to the room above the workshop and lay down fully dressed, boots on, coat over her like a blanket. Mira slept in the narrow bed beside the wall, curled small as a comma, her breath making a soft rhythm in the dark. The girl had not spoken since arriving. She ate what was given, slept when sleep took her, followed Ulyana from room to room with the silent diligence of a cat—and Ulyana, who had spent four years perfecting the art of not being perceived, found herself perceived constantly now, by eyes that never blinked away.

The photograph lay sewn into Mira's coat lining, and the coat lay folded at the foot of the bed, and Ulyana lay awake looking at it as though it might catch fire from her attention alone. Soldiers at the eastern bridge. Soldiers smiling. And below them, in the water, whatever it was that had made Emrys map that crossing last of all, in a hand that shook.

Sleep came eventually, thin and shallow as ice on a spring puddle.

* * *

In the morning she learned that other people had been watching the windows too.

She went out early, before the curfew lifted entirely but after the streets belonged to women with baskets, to buy bread and, if she could manage it, candles. The chandler on Krolowa Street had gone; his shop was now an office with a flag over the door. But the baker still baked, under supervision, and the queue outside his shop was the closest thing the quarter had to a parliament.

Ulyana joined it and made herself unremarkable: plain coat, plain face, eyes down, a woman with a household to feed and nothing else in her head. She was halfway to the door when a voice beside her said, quietly, "You shouldn't stand so near the corner."

Halina Sowek lived two doors down, in the ground-floor flat with the geraniums in the window—real ones, stubbornly alive, which in itself was a small act of defiance. She was perhaps fifty-five, built broad and practical like a barge, with grey hair pinned up and hands that had done every kind of work hands could do. Her husband had been a lock-keeper on the river before the war. He was gone now. Nobody said where.

"I don't understand," Ulyana said.

"You will." Halina didn't look at her. She looked at the bread queue, the street, the sky, with the comprehensive sweep of a woman who had survived forty years of other people's governments by seeing everything and remarking on none of it. "There's a man who comes along the quay most evenings now. Grey coat. He stops across from your workshop and writes in a little book." A pause. "He stopped across from mine last month. I moved my geraniums inside for a week."

Ulyana's heart performed a slow, heavy revolution in her chest. "What does he write?"

"Who knows? Windows, probably. They count windows." Halina accepted a loaf from the baker with a nod and counted coins into his hand. "It means they've noticed something. Not you specifically—yet. A pattern. Light at strange hours. A child appearing that wasn't there before. A door opening at times doors shouldn't open." She tucked the loaf under her arm and finally turned her head, just slightly, so the words could travel sideways between their faces without either mouth seeming to move. "You've been careful, girl. I'll grant you that. Four years careful. But careful isn't invisible anymore. The net is coming in closer all over the quarter. Ask anyone standing in this line and half of them know someone who was taken this month."

"The bakery—" Ulyana began.

"Is watched too, yes. Everything is watched. That is the weather we live in." Halina began to walk, unhurried, and Ulyana fell into step because her legs decided before she did. "You knew Emrys Voss," Halina said. It wasn't a question.

Every muscle in Ulyana's back tightened. "Everyone knew Master Voss."

"Mm." Halina's smile was brief and dry as paper. "I knew him longer than everyone, then. He mended the map in my hallway once, the old river chart my husband's grandfather bought. Wouldn't take payment. Said a house should know its own water." They turned onto Krolowa Street, past the flagged doorway of the chandlery, past the sentry who looked through them as though women with loaves were a species he'd ceased finding interesting. Only when they reached the geranium window did Halina stop, setting her basket down on the step as if her boots required adjusting. "I'm going to say something to you, and you're going to want to look at the ground while I say it, so do that."

Ulyana looked at the ground. The cobbles were dark with yesterday's rain.

"They marked your house," Halina said. "And there are people on this street—not the warden, he's just a tired little functionary with a notebook, he counts what he's told to count—but people who would trade what they've seen about you for a ration card, or a favor, or simply because fear makes them cruel. I know who they are. You don't. That's the whole difference between us right now, and it's the difference that will keep you alive another week if you let it."

"And what does it cost," Ulyana said quietly, "to let it?"

Halina straightened. For a moment her face was open, and underneath the barge-like sturdiness Ulyana saw something exhausted and grieving and very human—a woman who had buried a husband and kept watering flowers. "Nothing now," she said. "That's what nobody believes. It costs nothing *now*. It costs later, maybe. Maybe it costs everything later. Trust always has a bill attached; it just doesn't come itemized." She picked up her basket. "I'm not asking you to tell me anything. I'm telling you: grey coat, evenings, quay side. Keep your lamp low after nine. Don't let the child stand at the window—children at windows are the first thing they note, because children at windows mean a household that's changed." She unlocked her door, then paused with her hand on the latch. "And when you decide you need help carrying whatever it is you're carrying—you will decide, everyone does eventually—knock twice."

The door closed softly behind her. Through the lace curtain, Ulyana saw her move into the kitchen, put the kettle on, live her ordinary and perilous life.

* * *

Back in the workshop, Ulyana sat for a long time without lighting the lamp, though the afternoon was dimming toward early dusk and the atlas plates waited on the bench with their patient ink-lines.

Trust always has a bill attached.

Emrys had understood that. She remembered—or constructed, memory by now being mostly construction—a fragment: his pen pausing over a river bend, ink gathering at the nib like a drop about to fall. *"A map is a promise, Ula. Every line you draw says: this is here, this is true, walk here and you will not drown. So you had better be certain. A wrong promise kills faster than a lie."* He had trusted almost no one, professionally. Personally—he had trusted her, an orphan apprentice taken off the relief queues at fourteen, taught letters and latitudes, given a bed and a trade and, though neither of them ever named it, a family of exactly two.

And the thing he had trusted her with was going to kill her unless she became, very quickly, someone else: not an apprentice keeping a dead man's workshop alive, but a courier. A smuggler. A witness who moved.

She thought about Halina's offered hand. Twice-knocking, geraniums, forty years of surviving by seeing everything. It would be so easy—God, how easy—to knock on that door. To sit in a warm kitchen with real flowers and say the truth out loud for the first time: *there is a photograph, there is an atlas, the eastern bridge was no accident, and I am the only person left who can carry both out of this city.* To be, for one hour, not alone.

And Halina's son—she had mentioned no son, but Ulyana had seen the framed photograph years ago, a grinning boy in a cadet's uniform, and the frame had come down sometime in the second year of the occupation. People whose sons came home did not remove photographs. People whose sons went somewhere did.

If the occupiers squeezed Halina—and they would squeeze everyone eventually—what would she trade then?

But then, Ulyana thought, pressing her palms flat against the cool oak of the bench, what would *she* trade? If they took Mira—if a soldier held that silent, watchful child by the collar and asked questions—was there a line Ulyana would not cross, a secret she would not spend? She wanted to say no. She was honest enough with herself to know she couldn't.

That was the arithmetic of terror: everyone had a price, and the price was always the same—it was whoever you loved.

Above her, floorboards creaked. Mira was awake. Ulyana heard the small feet descend the stairs, and then the girl appeared in the doorway of the workshop, coat already buttoned against the cold, hair smoothed flat with spit and patience the way Ulyana had shown her, because a tidy child drew fewer eyes than a wild one. Mira looked at the unlit lamp, the waiting plates, Ulyana's face—and then crossed the room without a word and stood close, not touching, just near, the way she did. Present. Witnessing.

Ulyana looked down at her and felt the whole terrible geometry of her situation arrange itself into clarity: the atlas needed finishing, the photograph needed delivering, the house was marked, the street was watched, and the only person in the world she might trust was a neighbor who had already survived more than Ulyana could imagine—and the only person she *must* protect was a child who could not even say her own name aloud.

Or could she?

Mira's lips moved suddenly, silently, working around something. Then, in a voice rusty and small, like a hinge remembering its purpose:

"Twice?"

Ulyana froze. "What did you say?"

The girl pointed a small finger toward the wall—toward the street, toward the geraniums two doors down—and repeated, patiently, distinctly, the way one repeats something overheard through thin walls many nights running: "Knock. Twice."

Outside, on the quay, evening was coming on. And somewhere along the water, she knew, a man in a grey coat was walking toward them with his little book open, counting.
