## 5. Three Checkpoints

The workshop had never been meant for war councils. It had been built for quiet things—the scratch of a nib, the sigh of paper unrolled, the patient arithmetic of rivers. Now Ulyana stood over their own work with candlelight pooling on the boards, and saw it for what it had become: not an atlas but a battlefield laid bare.

They spread the maps in sequence, south to north as a traveler would meet them, and weighted each corner with whatever lay to hand—a brass divot-tool, a cold teacup, Mira's wooden horse. The girl sat at the far bench with her knees drawn up, watching. She had taken to watching everything Ulyana did now, with the fixed, unblinking attention of a creature that had learned the world could end without warning.

"Right," Ulyana said softly, to themselves more than to her. "Three ways out of this city. Three doors, and all three have eyes."

The first was the fish market.

They traced it with one finger: the cobbled square where the salt-herring barrels came up from the wharves at dawn, where the occupiers had set their checkpoint between the gutting tables and the old customs house. Every cart leaving for the southern road passed through that funnel of fish-smell and rifle-butts. But so did commerce—barrels, crates, the daily freight of a city still pretending to be fed—and commerce moved slowly enough to be searched and bored through without anyone caring much. That was its weakness. Boredom. Soldiers who searched herring barrels forty times a morning stopped believing anything worth hiding would come smelling of brine.

*Plates under the salted herring,* Ulyana thought, and almost laughed at the absurdity of it: Emrys's life's work, the true map of every crossing on the river, sealed in oilcloth beneath forty weight of fish. The atlas would arrive at the border reeking like a wharf at low tide. He would have hated that. He would also, Ulyana admitted, have been the first to suggest it.

But there was a second door, and it was faster. The rail yard east of the goods station, where the troop trains came through and the passenger service—if you could call two cars shunted behind cattle trucks a passenger service—ran south twice a week toward the mountains. A ticket was nearly impossible to get; papers were checked twice, once at the gate and once on the platform itself. Two checks meant two chances to fail. But it also meant speed. If the fish market went wrong, the rail yard was the fallback, and the fallback had to be planned as carefully as the plan.

Ulyana drew the yard from memory onto scrap paper, because memory was safer than ink. The gate on the western side. The platform. The long shed where luggage was stacked and sometimes, if the queue was long enough, never opened at all. The gap in the fence behind the coal store that Emrys had marked years ago with a small, dry notation—*loose board, boys use it*—in a survey that had supposedly been about drainage.

He had mapped everything, Ulyana thought. Even the ways around his own maps.

And then there was the river itself. The pontoon bridge.

Their finger stopped over it and stayed there, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The military crossing—planked barges lashed gunwale to gunwale, steel cable sagging between the stone moorings the occupiers had built on both banks. It was the fastest way across the water, and the only one they permitted civilians to use, and therefore it was the most dangerous place in the entire city. Guards at both ends. Names checked against lists. A chokepoint designed by people who understood exactly what a bridge is: not a convenience but a promise, and promises must be controlled.

Emrys had surveyed it last. His final field notes, the ones Ulyana had not yet dared read straight through, described its load limits, its anchor chains, the depth of the channel beneath it—all recorded in his neat, unhurried hand, as though he were taking dictation from something that would outlive everyone currently standing on it.

"Three checkpoints," Ulyana said aloud, and heard how thin their voice sounded in the cold room. "Fish market, rail yard, pontoon bridge. We pass through all three or none of us matter."

Mira's wooden horse had stopped moving. She was looking at the maps—not at Ulyana, at the maps—and her small forefinger rose from her knee and pointed, unerringly, at the eastern span.

Not the pontoon. Not the city bridges. The *eastern* bridge, drawn in Emrys's hand months before it died, rendered in the fine double lines he used for stone. The bridge in the photograph sewn into the lining of her coat. The bridge that had made Ulyana tear apart a dead man's papers until they understood what he had died protecting.

"You know it," Ulyana said quietly.

Mira did not answer. She never answered. But she kept pointing, and her pointing finger did not tremble, and Ulyana felt something shift in the room like water finding a crack.

* * *

That night Ulyana did not sleep. They banked the stove low, hung a blanket over the window, and worked by the light of a single stub of candle, copying the route onto three separate slips of rice paper thin enough to swallow. Fish market. Rail yard. Pontoon bridge. Each slip folded small as a fingernail and sewn into a different seam of a different garment, so that no single search could take them all. This was Emrys's teaching too, though he had applied it to archives rather than escape: *never let the truth live in one place, little starling. Truth in one place is called a target. Truth in many places is called a civilization.*

She had called them that once, early on—little starling—and the name still surfaced at odd hours, uninvited, like a coin turned up by a plow.

Somewhere past the smallest hour, a sound broke the rhythm of the nib.

"...the water was black."

Ulyana's hand froze mid-stroke. Ink gathered at the nib and dropped a single dark bead onto the blotter, spreading like a wound.

Mira was sitting up in her nest of blankets by the stove, eyes open, looking at nothing—or at something that was not in the room. Her voice was small and flat, the voice of someone reporting weather from very far away.

"The water was black," she said again. "There were lights on the other side. Green ones. And the men walked onto the bridge even though the soldiers told them to go back, because it was cold and they wanted to cross, and then—" Her hands made a shape in the air, two flat palms pressing down. "Then the middle wasn't there anymore."

Ulyana did not move. Did not breathe. They had spent weeks treating the girl's silence as a locked door, something to be waited outside of patiently, and now the door had simply swung open in the dark and they were terrified of making a sound that might close it again.

"And after," Mira said, "the soldiers picked things up out of the water. With hooks." Her brow furrowed, working at the memory the way one works at a knot. "One of them dropped a lantern. I had the picture already. Mama put it in my coat. She said run to the map house. She said the map man knows what happened. She said—"

Her voice snagged. The flatness cracked, just once, and underneath it was a child's voice, six years old and drowning on dry land.

"She said forget her face. But I didn't."

"No," Ulyana said, and their own voice came out rough as sandpaper. "No, you didn't."

Mira looked at them then, directly, for the first time since the night she'd arrived on the doorstep. Whatever lived in her expression was too old for her face, too heavy for her years, and it settled over Ulyana like a coat of wet iron.

"They said the ice broke," she whispered. "Everyone will say the ice broke. But it was loud like thunder, and there wasn't any ice."

*I know,* Ulyana did not say, because saying it aloud would make it true in a way that could not be survived in this small cold room. Instead they crossed to her, knelt, and tucked the blanket back over her shoulders with hands that were not quite steady.

"Sleep now," they said. "Tomorrow we go south."

Mira lay down obediently, but her eyes stayed open, fixed on the ceiling, and her lips moved once more before sleep took her—something Ulyana leaned close to catch, and wished they hadn't:

"*Count them. I counted them going under.*"

* * *

Afterward, Ulyana did not return to the route. Not immediately. They sat at the workbench with the candle guttering and let the horror finish arriving, wave after wave, the way grief had arrived months ago when Emrys's body came back to them in a plain box with no explanation and no apology.

He had known. Of course he had known—he had been given the girl as a courier, or she had been sent to him by someone who knew what he held, either way he had understood what happened at the eastern bridge before the official report was written. And he had kept mapping anyway. Kept inking plates by candlelight in this same room, knowing that the atlas in his drawers was not scholarship but testimony, that every measured span and every noted ford was a line in an indictment. That was why they killed him, if they had killed him—that word again, which Ulyana still could not make sit still—and that was why finishing the atlas was not grief-work any longer. It was witness-work. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

Ulyana pulled a fresh sheet toward them and began, in small precise letters, to draw the route south as it truly was: not geography but danger. At the fish market they wrote *slow, bored, searchable* and sketched the barrel rows. At the rail yard, *two checks, one gap behind the coal store.* At the pontoon bridge, they wrote nothing at all for a long moment, and then, finally: *he who holds the bridge holds the truth.*

A mapmaker plans routes. A smuggler plans *people*—where the guards stand, what they fear, what they want, what small hinge of boredom or mercy or habit might swing a gate open. Ulyana looked down at the sheet and realized, with a strange calm, that they had already begun thinking the second way. Emrys had taught them to measure the world. The occupation was teaching them, night by night, to measure its guards.

So be it. If the atlas was testimony, then Ulyana would deliver it the way testimony travels in occupied country: hidden, split into pieces, carried by someone who has already decided what they are willing to lose.

They blew out the candle. In the dark, Mira breathed slow and even by the stove, and Ulyana stood at the window with the blanket pulled aside one inch, looking south toward the invisible line where the fish market would open at dawn.

It was Halina's knock, an hour later—far too early, urgent, three raps and then two—that told Ulyana the net had already begun to close.

"We have to talk," the old woman whispered through the door, her breath ragged, her knuckles still pressed to the wood. "Someone counted your windows, child. And they've written the number down."
