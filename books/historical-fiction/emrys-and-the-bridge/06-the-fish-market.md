## 6. The Fish Market

The fish market smelled of salt and ice and the particular rot that comes when a city is too hungry to be fastidious about its dead things. Ulyana had walked through it a hundred times before the war, back when the stalls sold eels from the river and pike from the northern lakes, back when Emrys would stop at the same barrel every Saturday morning and haggle over smoked herring with a woman named Pani Rosa who cheated him cheerfully and always gave him the better portion anyway.

Now the barrels were stacked in military rows under tarpaulins, and Pani Rosa was gone—no one knew where—and the smell was the same but everything it meant had been taken out of it, like a map with all its names scraped off.

Ulyana kept their eyes forward and their breathing even.

Mira walked beside them, her small hand swallowed inside Ulyana's larger one. They had rehearsed this. In the workshop, by candlelight, they had rehearsed it eleven times: *You are my sister's daughter. She died of fever in March. You cannot hear, you have never been able to hear, and you do not speak because there is nothing wrong with your mouth except that it has never had anything worth saying.* Mira had nodded gravely at each repetition, her dark eyes solemn as wells, and once—only once—she had reached up and touched Ulyana's cheek, which was not part of the rehearsal, and which Ulyana still could not think about without something tightening behind the breastbone.

The plates rode beneath them now, or would soon. That was the morning's first labor, performed in the gray hour before the market opened, when the fish porters came down from the hills with their loads on handcarts and no one looked at anyone's face. Ulyana had bought two barrels of salted herring from a man who asked no questions because Ulyana paid too much for them, and had spent forty minutes kneeling in the mud behind the stall with a pry bar, lifting lids, laying the atlas plates flat against the false bottom Emrys had built into his own traveling chest years ago—a chest Ulyana now carried on their back, emptied of everything else. The plates went in wrapped in oilcloth, face to face, ink side protected by felt. Then the herring went back on top, layer upon brined silver layer, until the barrels weighed what honest barrels should weigh and stank the way honest barrels should stink.

Salt preserves. Salt also corrodes. Ulyana had calculated that even if the brine wicked through, the ink was fixed with gum arabic and iron gall; three days of damp would stain the paper's edge but not touch the lines themselves. Three days. The route south required three days, and between here and the southern road lay three checkpoints, and the first of them stood at the mouth of the fish market like a hook waiting in dark water.

*They will look at faces,* Ulyana told themself, guiding Mira along the cobbles. *So give them nothing to read.*

It was good advice. It failed immediately.

* * *

The checkpoint was not the casual thing it had been a month ago. Two sandbag emplacements flanked the market gate now, and a machine gun sat atop one like a black beetle, its barrel angled down the length of the fish stalls as though even the herring might attempt escape. Four soldiers. A table. A clerk with a ledger. And beyond the table, checking papers with the bored efficiency of a man who has done it four hundred times today, a soldier whose profile stopped Ulyana's heart mid-beat.

Tomasz Veren.

No—that wasn't possible. Tomasz Veren had sat two rows behind Ulyana in Master Corvin's drafting class, twelve years old and already famous for drawing hands, of all the impossible things, hands so alive that the other boys used to accuse him of tracing. Tomasz Veren had laughed with his whole body. Tomasz Veren had once smuggled Ulyana a plum during an examination, wordlessly, the way one conspirator passes contraband to another, and had never mentioned it afterward, which was the truest kindness Ulyana had ever received from a boy.

The soldier at the table wore the occupier's uniform and the occupier's cap pulled low, and he did not laugh at all.

*Mira.* Ulyana squeezed the child's hand. *Remember.*

Mira did not squeeze back. But she tilted her head slightly, let her gaze go vague and unfocused, and became—instantly, completely—a deaf child in a loud place: unstartled by the shouted orders around her, uninterested in the bayonets, her attention drifting to a gull wheeling overhead as though the entire machinery of occupation were merely weather.

God help me, Ulyana thought, she is better at this than I am.

The line moved. A farmer argued about a confiscated pig. A woman wept quietly and was waved through anyway, which meant the tears were either genuine or insufficiently interesting. Ulyana counted breaths. In for four. Out for six. The way Emrys had taught them to steady a hand before ruling a fine line: *the body is an instrument, girl, tune it or it will play itself.*

Then it was their turn.

"Papers."

The voice. It was the voice. Deeper than it had been at fourteen, flattened of its music, but the vowels were the vowels of the river district, of the school on Kessel Lane, of home before home became a word that required guarding.

Ulyana passed the travel documents across the table—the ones Halina had helped arrange, the ones naming Ulyana Yarrow as a cartographer's assistant traveling south to survey flood damage for the municipal works office, a lie built on the sturdy foundation of a truth, since the papers were real enough and the stamp was real enough and only the purpose was fiction.

The soldier took them. Began to read.

And then he looked up.

Ulyana watched it happen. Watched recognition move across Tomasz Veren's face like weather crossing open water—the flicker, the narrowing, the almost imperceptible stiffening of the shoulders—and understood in the space of half a heartbeat that everything now depended on what kind of person had grown inside the uniform of the boy who had passed a plum down two rows of desks without a word.

He said nothing. He looked at the papers again. He looked at Mira. He looked at Ulyana's hands—which trembled, damn them, despite all the tuning—and something in his jaw shifted.

"Name?" he said, and his voice was pure procedure, pitched to carry to the sergeant at the sandbags.

"Yarrow. Ulyana Yarrow." Their own voice sounded foreign to them, thin as tracing paper.

"Occupation?"

"Cartographic assistant. Municipal works."

"Mm." He turned a page. Somewhere behind Ulyana the line grumbled; somewhere above them the gull screamed; somewhere beneath their feet, forty meters away in the shadow of the third stall row, two barrels of salted herring held the death warrant of an empire's favorite lie. "Traveling with?"

"Niece. My late sister's daughter." The rehearsed sentence, delivered almost steadily. "She's deaf. She can't hear you. I'm sorry—she isn't being rude."

Something moved behind Tomasz Veren's eyes at the phrase *she isn't being rude*, some memory perhaps of a teacher saying exactly that about another silent child in another life, and Ulyana saw him catch himself, saw the muscle in his cheek jump as he bit down on whatever had risen in him.

"I need to verify these," he said.

The words dropped like stones into Ulyana's stomach.

"Verification" was the new word. Everyone in the occupied quarter knew what it meant now. Verification meant your papers went into a courier pouch and traveled to the district command, and you waited—hours, sometimes days—at the holding pen behind the rail offices while some clerk decided whether a cartographer's assistant from the river district merited the southern road. Verification meant the leash. Verification meant they knew your name, your route, your business, and that every step you took thereafter was taken at the end of a rope they could pull.

"The survey is time-sensitive," Ulyana said. Carefully. Not pleading—pleading was blood in water. "Spring floods. If we miss the window—"

"Then you'll survey dry land." He stamped something. Wrote something. His handwriting, Ulyana noticed absurdly, was still beautiful. "Papers stay with me. You collect them at the second checkpoint, rail yard, tomorrow, if verification clears." He slid the documents into a leather pouch without looking up. "You're free to proceed to the market. Southbound departure is your own affair."

*If verification clears.* The leash, attached and locked.

"Thank you," Ulyana said.

For a moment—one moment, no longer—his eyes lifted and met theirs fully, and there it was again, the flicker, and beneath the flicker something older and more dangerous: the faintest movement of his lips that might, in a different century, in a different country, have been the ghost of a plum passed silently down two rows of desks. *I know you. I am not going to say so. Do not thank me. Thanking me makes us both guilty.*

"Move along," he said loudly, for the benefit of the sergeant, and waved the next traveler forward.

* * *

They bought the barrels openly, which was the beauty of it—or would have been the beauty of it if Ulyana's hands had stopped shaking long enough to appreciate elegance. A cartographer's assistant purchasing salted herring for a three-day journey: nothing more natural. The porter loaded both barrels onto the handcart Ulyana had hired, and Ulyana signed the porter's chit with a name that was real on paper and ash everywhere else, and then they walked, slowly, a niece and her uncle—or aunt, the papers were mercifully ambiguous—pulling a cart of fish toward the south lane, past the machine gun, past the sandbags, past the table where Tomasz Veren checked the papers of a grain merchant and did not look up, or looked up and permitted himself not to.

Not until they had put three streets between themselves and the market did Ulyana allow the breath they'd been holding to leave them, and it left badly, ragged, bending them double over the cart handles while Mira watched with those well-deep eyes.

"He knew me," Ulyana whispered. "Mira, he—he sat behind me in school. He drew hands." The words spilled out shapeless, useless. "He took the papers. Which means they'll be waiting for us. At the rail yard, they'll know our names before we say them."

Mira considered this. Then she tugged the hem of Ulyana's coat, pointed at the barrels, and made a small motion with her fingers—sewing stitches, precise, deliberate, the gesture she had used once before in the workshop when Ulyana couldn't find the key plate of the eastern bridge and had torn the satchel apart looking for it.

*I know where it is,* the gesture said. *I've known all along.*

Ulyana stared at her. "You—" Their voice cracked. "When did you—"

But Mira only shook her head and pointed south, down the lane, toward the rail yard and the second checkpoint and the leash already coiled around their names, and Ulyana understood that the child was telling them, in her wordless way, that questions could wait and walking could not.

They walked. Behind them, faint over the rooftops, a whistle blew—not a train whistle, shorter, sharper, the sound of someone summoning someone—and Ulyana did not turn around, because turning around was a thing that people with nothing to hide did, and besides, the barrels were heavy, and the day was short, and the net had just closed one knot tighter.
