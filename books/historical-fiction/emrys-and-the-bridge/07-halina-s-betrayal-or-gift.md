## 7. Halina's Betrayal or Gift

The knock came at dusk, three soft raps in the rhythm Halina used when she brought bread—*shave the loaf, shave it again*—and Ulyana's hands went still over the last of the herring barrels, sawdust clinging to her wrists like frost.

Mira looked up from the corner where she sat mending a tear in her dress with clumsy, crooked stitches. Since the fish market, since the soldier named Anton who had taken Ulyana's papers and smiled with half his face, the girl had begun to speak in fragments—small offerings, dropped like coins into Ulyana's palm. *The water was loud. Mama held my hand too tight. The bridge made a sound like God clearing His throat.* Each fragment was a stone added to the weight Ulyana carried, and each one made the atlas in its satchel feel less like paper and more like a live coal.

"Stay here," Ulyana whispered. "Be small."

Mira nodded and folded herself smaller, the way children learn to do in occupied cities, knees drawn up, chin tucked, eyes wide and watchful as a sparrow's.

Ulyana opened the door on the chain.

Halina stood in the stairwell shadow with her shawl pulled low and something wrapped in waxed cloth clutched against her chest. She was sixty if she was a day, with knuckles swollen from decades of laundry work and a face that had once been pretty in the way of old photographs—soft-jawed, dark-eyed. Now the softness had been scrubbed out of it, leaving only structure and worry.

"Let me in, child," she said quietly. "Please. Not long. Not even five minutes."

Ulyana hesitated. Every instinct that had kept them alive through two winters of occupation said *no one inside, no one ever inside.* But Halina had brought milk for Mira when the girl arrived feverish. Halina had stood in the courtyard and loudly complained about Ulyana's chimney smoke to the warden's face, giving the workshop the dull reputation of a household too poor and too quarrelsome to be interesting. Halina had earned, thread by thread, a claim on Ulyana's trust.

She unhooked the chain.

Halina moved past her like a woman entering a church she no longer believed in—head bowed, steps quick, not looking at the candle or the ink-stained table or the barrels reeking of brine. She stopped in the center of the room and pressed her back against the wall as if she needed it to hold her up.

"I did something," she said. "And you have to hear all of it before you hate me. You have to let me finish."

The cold walked up Ulyana's spine one vertebra at a time. Behind her, Mira went very still.

"Say it," Ulyana said.

"The warden." Halina's voice was flat, rehearsed; she had clearly said this to herself a hundred times in front of a hundred mirrors, and it still came out wrong. "He came to me Tuesday. He'd already counted your windows twice—he told me so, he *bragged*, the little cockroach—and he'd noticed the candle burning past curfew. He noticed the deliveries of salt. Nobody buys that much salt, Ulyana. Nobody buys salt like a fortress is being provisioned." She swallowed. "He asked what I knew about the mapmaker's apprentice. And I told him where the workshop was."

The room did not change. The candle flame leaned and rightened. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobbles. Ulyana heard all of it from very far away, through the ringing in her ears, while the words arranged themselves into their meaning: *he knows. They know. It has already begun.*

"You told me," Ulyana said, and her own voice sounded like someone else's, "that informants had marked this building. You stood in my kitchen three days ago and warned me. You drank my tea."

"I know what I told you." Halina's composure cracked then, all at once, like river ice in March, and underneath it was not an old woman but a mother, raw and ruined. "God forgive me, I know. I warned you because I couldn't *not* warn you, and I told him because I couldn't not tell him either, and both of those things are true and I don't know how to live in between them." She fumbled at the waxed cloth with shaking fingers. "My son. My Jozef. They took him in the autumn sweeps—you remember, the whole street heard the trucks. He's in the transit camp past the rail yard. He is nineteen years old and he weighs nothing, Ulyana; his aunt saw him through the wire in December and he weighs *nothing*. The warden knew it. Everyone knows it. They hold him like—" Her hand closed. "They hold him like a coin in a fist, and they spend him whenever they want something."

"So you spent me instead."

"Yes." No flinching. Halina lifted her chin, and Ulyana understood with a lurch that this was the bravest thing the woman had done in her entire careful, frightened life: standing in the room of someone she had sold and refusing to look away. "Yes. I traded the workshop's location for his transfer off the transit lists. He'll be moved to a labor company by Friday. He'll eat. He'll—" Her voice broke and she mastered it. "He'll have a chance. That was the price. That was the whole price, one address, and God help me it was cheap and it was dearer than everything I own."

Ulyana's fury came up like floodwater, hot and blinding. It was so large she could not speak inside it. *This room,* she thought. *This table where Emrys bent over his plates until his back seized. This door where they left a child like a parcel. I measured every escape route in what could be carried, and she gave it away for a bowl of soup and a name off a list—*

But even as the thought formed, it turned traitor in her mouth. Because Emrys had died protecting his atlas, and Ulyana would burn the city down for Mira without counting the cost to anyone else, and what was Halina's arithmetic but the same sum done in different numbers? *One child saved. One son fed. One address given.* The price of survival, Ulyana was learning, was never paid by one person alone. It was passed hand to hand down the street like a debt everyone owed and no one had contracted.

"He comes Thursday," Halina said into the silence. "The warden. With men. Tomorrow night, maybe, if he wants credit for it. That's why I'm here tonight and not tomorrow." She thrust the waxed cloth bundle forward, arms straight, the way one offers bread—or surrender. "Take it. Take it now and go."

Ulyana unwrapped it.

Inside lay a travel pass, folded twice, soft at the creases from handling. A woman's pass—*Halina Wozniak, laundress, district four, authorized to travel south to her sister's household for reasons of family illness.* The stamp was real. The ink was the tired gray-blue of genuine bureaucracy, which no forger ever quite matched. Beneath the pass, wrapped in the same cloth, were two rings and a thin gold chain, and a handful of coins.

"It's good for one," Halina said. "It was always meant for me—I was going to run in spring, when my sister's letter came, but there's no spring coming for people like me anymore. One pass, one person. I can't make it be two." Her eyes went past Ulyana's shoulder to the corner, to the small folded shape of the girl, and softened into something ancient. "But you'll find a way. You're Emrys's apprentice. He used to say you could find a road anywhere, even where the map says there isn't one."

"He said that?"

"He said it to anyone who'd listen." A ghost of a smile crossed Halina's wrecked face. "He was proud of you, child. Loud about it, when the shutters were closed. You should have heard him."

Emrys had been dead eight months, and still, somehow, he could reach out of his grave and put his hand flat against Ulyana's chest. She looked down at the pass in her palm—at another woman's name, another woman's face description, close enough to pass at a distance and fail at a scrutiny—and felt the fury drain out of her the way heat leaves a stone at evening, slowly, leaving only weight.

"I should hate you," Ulyana said.

"You should." Halina wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, as if drying dishes. "Hate me after Friday. Hate me when your Jozef-equivalent is across the water. If you get across the water, you can hate me for the rest of your life and I'll consider it fair rent." She stepped toward the door, then stopped, one hand on the frame. "There's one more thing. The warden asked me about a girl. Whether the mapmaker's household had taken in a child. Someone talked at the fish market—not the soldier, someone who saw you buying herring with a mute girl at your skirts. He didn't believe me when I said I didn't know. But he'll believe his own eyes Thursday."

*Mira.* The word landed like a fist. Ulyana turned and found the girl already standing, already watching, having heard everything a child should never hear—the sale of the roof over her head, the arithmetic of her own danger. Mira crossed the room and took Ulyana's hand without a word and gripped it, and that small fierce pressure decided more than any speech could have.

"Thursday morning," Ulyana said. "We go south tomorrow. Through the market again—it worked once. Then the rail yard. Then the pontoon."

"The rail yard is being doubled. New men, new dogs, since Monday." Halina said it simply, a laundress reporting weather. "And your papers are gone. Whatever that soldier took at the market, he has it, and that means they have a thread and they will pull it."

"Then we cut the thread before they pull." Ulyana folded the pass into the lining of her coat, where Mira's photograph had lived, where secrets went to travel. "Why are you doing this? Truly. After informing. Why stand here?"

Halina looked at her for a long moment, and the last of her composure went out of her face like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

"Because my grandmother hid priests in a root cellar during the other war, and I told myself I would never be the kind of woman who sells her neighbors, and I *am* that woman now, and the only thing left to me—the only scrap—is that I sell you late instead of early. Do you understand? It changes nothing. It saves nothing. Except that I couldn't sleep again, ever, if I let them come for a child and an atlas and did nothing but count my son's ration tickets." She pulled her shawl tight. "Jozef is alive because I did a terrible thing. Maybe you'll be alive because I did one decent one. Neither balances the other. Nothing balances. That's the whole lesson of this war, Ulyana, and I'd spare it to you if I could."

At the door, on the threshold, in the draft that smelled of coal smoke and the river, Halina paused one final time.

"There's a fishwife named Dorota who runs the second stall from the wall. Salted herring, terrible temper. Tell her Halina says the ice broke early this year." A beat. "She moves things south. Barrels mostly. She owes me for forty years of washing her husband's shirts, and she doesn't ask questions because asking questions is the one thing she's never learned."

Then she was gone, her footsteps fading down the stairs, quick and light, lighter than they had been coming up—as though setting down a weight, however terribly, always makes a body younger.

Ulyana closed the door and slid the bolt and stood with her forehead against the wood.

Behind her, Mira spoke—her new voice, still rusty, still small. "Are we going to hate her?"

"No," Ulyana said, surprising herself with how quickly the answer came, how completely it had already been decided somewhere beneath thought. "We're going to owe her. Which is worse, and better." She turned and began moving through the room with sudden purpose—barrels, satchel, candle, knife, the plates wrapped in oilcloth. "Pack nothing you love. Only what we need."

Mira gathered her three possessions without argument, and then, hesitating, held up the dress she'd been mending—the one with the crooked stitches along the hem, the one whose seams had already proven they could carry more than thread. "This one," she said. "I love this one."

"Then wear it," Ulyana said. "We leave before first light. And pray the fishwife's ice truly broke."

Outside, far off toward the eastern districts, a whistle shrilled—once, twice, the warden's patrol making its rounds, counting windows. Counting them even now.
