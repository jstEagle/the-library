## 8. The Rail Yard

The rail yard at dusk was a country of iron and smoke, and every country had its borders drawn in barbed wire.

Ulyana saw it the way Emrys had taught her to see all places: not as chaos but as geometry. The tracks ran in long ruled lines southward, converging like meridians toward the distant signal tower. Between them lay the platforms, and between the platforms the soldiers moved in slow patrols, their boots striking a rhythm she could almost chart. There — the gate with the lamp. There — the shed where luggage was opened and turned out onto tables. There — the queue, shuffling, breathing, waiting to be measured.

She had studied her own maps for two nights by candlelight, and she knew this place before she ever entered it. That was the mapmaker's curse: to arrive everywhere already knowing how it could kill you.

"Stay close," she murmured to Mira. "Hold my sleeve. Not my hand — my sleeve. Hands get pulled apart."

Mira's small fingers closed on the wool anyway, knuckles white, and Ulyana did not correct her.

The satchel hung against Ulyana's hip beneath her coat, heavy as a sleeping animal. Inside it lay the atlas — eleven copper plates wrapped in oilcloth, six months of ink and candle smoke, Emrys's last survey of every bridge and ford and hidden crossing from the northern marshes to the sea. The key plate, the one that showed the eastern bridge and the demolition charges Emrys had marked in his small, furious hand, rode at the very bottom, between two false covers of blank chart paper.

*If you are ever caught with it,* Emrys had told her once, in the workshop's cold blue dawn, *you will not be questioned. You will simply disappear, and the plate will be melted, and the truth will weigh exactly what it weighed before: nothing.* He had smiled then, the rare smile that made him look young and doomed at once. *So don't be caught.*

The queue at the second checkpoint moved in jerks, like a cart with a broken wheel. Ahead of them a woman argued over a sack of flour; behind them a man prayed under his breath, an old habit worn smooth as river stone. The soldiers at the table were methodical, bored, dangerous in the way boredom makes men dangerous — they searched because there was nothing else to do until the train came.

And the train had come. It stood down the line, a long grey serpent exhaling steam into the last light, its cars marked with chalk numbers Ulyana did not want to read. A transit train. She had seen them from the hills, mapped their sidings without letting herself think about what they carried.

"Mira." She crouched, adjusting the girl's collar, buying a moment at eye level. "When we pass the table, you are deaf. You are mute. You look at the floor. Can you do that?"

Mira looked at her — that flat, ancient look the child had, older than her years, older than the war — and nodded once.

They moved forward. Ten meters. Five. Ulyana felt her heartbeat in her teeth.

Then the whistle blew.

It came from nowhere and everywhere — a soldier up the platform waving his arm, shouting something about a random search, a spot inspection, *everyone step back, form two lines, women and children to the left.* The queue dissolved around them like sugar in rain. Hands — gloved, impersonal — took Ulyana's shoulders and turned her. Another hand took Mira's other sleeve.

"No," Ulyana said. "She's with me. She's my—"

Papers. They wanted papers. A young soldier held out his palm while his eyes slid past her face toward the next person, the next problem. Behind him she saw Mira being guided left, toward the children's line, toward a sergeant with a clipboard who was counting heads the way a butcher counts carcasses, and Mira was looking back at her, and for the first time since the doorstep, since the note that said *forget this face*, the child's composure cracked.

Her mouth opened. No sound came out — she had sworn to be mute, and even terror kept her oath — but her eyes screamed, and Ulyana heard it anyway.

"*Family groups stay together only if documents correspond,*" the soldier recited, in the tone of a man reciting weather. "*Correspondence is verified at the table. Next.*"

Verification. The word landed in Ulyana's stomach like a dropped anchor. Her papers were Halina's papers now, borrowed, altered, good enough for a woman traveling alone or alone with a niece — if no one looked too long, if no one checked the registry, if the gods of small lies were feeling generous tonight.

But there were no gods at the rail yard. Only the table, and the sergeant with the clipboard, and the train breathing steam.

* * *

They opened the satchel first.

Ulyana stood at the table with her hands folded, performing the emptiness she did not feel, while a corporal with red hands unbuckled the straps and lifted out the oilcloth bundles one by one. He unwrapped the first plate, frowned at the engraving of the fish market district, set it aside. The second. The third. Each unwrapping was a small eternity; each etched bridge line that passed under his indifferent gaze was a coin spinning in the air.

"The surveyor's plates," Ulyana said, when the silence had stretched too far. "My late employer's work. I'm delivering them to the printing house in—" She named the southern town, the name she had rehearsed until it sat in her mouth like her own teeth.

He grunted. Unwrapped another.

And down the platform, the children's line began to move.

Ulyana saw it over the corporal's shoulder: the sergeant ticking names, the file of children shuffling toward the train steps, a soldier at the bottom lifting each one up by the arms like sacks of grain. Mira was fourth from the front. Then third. The chalk numbers on the car swam in Ulyana's vision. Transit. East. She knew what the eastern trains meant now — she had read it in Emrys's marginalia, in the photograph sewn into a child's coat, in the careful hatching he'd used to mark a bridge where men had been murdered and called it an accident. She knew exactly what weight the trains carried, because she had spent half her life weighing things in grams and ounces, and this knowledge outweighed them all.

Second in line.

The corporal reached the false cover. His red fingers paused on the blank chart paper. "And this?"

"Nothing," Ulyana said. "Scratch paper."

He turned it over. Beneath his thumb, through the paper, she could see — or imagined she could see — the faint ghost of the engraved grid, the key plate hidden between its leaves like a heart inside a chest. One more layer and he would find it. One more layer and everything ended here: the atlas, the evidence, Mira's testimony, the truth of the eastern bridge, all of it confiscated and melted and forgotten, and Ulyana Yarrow would vanish into a registry ledger with a chalk number beside her name.

First in line.

A soldier at the train steps held out his hands for the first child, a boy of perhaps seven, who went up screaming for his mother three places back in the other line, and the mother's scream answered his, and no one moved to let them touch.

"Scratch paper," the corporal repeated slowly, and began to peel back the false leaf.

Ulyana stopped thinking.

That was the only way to describe what happened — thought simply left, the way water leaves a cracked bowl, and what remained was older than thought. Her elbow found the table's edge. Her shoulder found the corporal's wrist. The satchel's remaining contents — three plates wrapped in oilcloth, months of Emrys's patient burins and acids, irreplaceable, unrepeatable, the master's whole last testament — went over the side of the table in a clatter of tin and wood, skidding across the frozen gravel toward the drainage ditch and the dark.

"Oh — forgive me—" She was stumbling after them, clumsy, weeping on command, the scatterbrained widow's apprentice, dropping everything, so sorry, so sorry. The corporal shouted. A lantern swung. And in the beautiful chaos of it — soldiers turning, boots crunching, someone laughing cruelly at the clumsy woman chasing her precious tin across the yard — Ulyana wrenched two plates from the ditch, abandoned the rest where they lay glittering like fish scales, and ran.

Not away. Across.

The children's line was boarding. The soldier at the steps had his hands out for Mira. Ulyana crossed the space between the lines at a dead sprint, coat flying, lungs full of coal smoke, and reached the steps as the soldier's fingers closed on Mira's arm.

"She's mine." The words came out ragged, enormous, louder than anything Ulyana had said in six years of careful living. "My sister's daughter. My papers — check my papers, I have the guardianship letter, it's here—"

There was no guardianship letter. There would never be a guardianship letter. But she shoved Halina's travel pass into the soldier's face as though it were scripture, and she grabbed Mira with both hands and pulled, and the child came to her like iron to a lodestone, arms locking around her neck, small body shaking, face buried in her shoulder.

The soldier looked at the pass. At her face. At the sergeant, who was watching from ten meters off with the flat expression of a man deciding whether this was worth the paperwork.

Behind them, down the platform, another corporal was fishing plates out of a ditch and swearing.

"Chalk number?" the soldier asked the sergeant, nodding at Mira.

The sergeant consulted his clipboard. His pencil hovered. Somewhere a locomotive shrieked, covering whatever Ulyana's face was doing.

"Not registered yet," the sergeant said at last, bored. "Take her if she's documented. Next!"

* * *

They walked to the far end of the platform because walking was the only thing that would not draw the eye, one deliberate step after another, past the lamp, past the gate guard, past the mail wagon whose shadow swallowed them at last. Only there, in the coal-dark between the wagons, did Ulyana allow herself to stop and set Mira down and press her forehead to the child's hair and shake.

"I dropped them," she whispered. The words tasted like ash and copper. "I dropped the plates, Mira. Six of them. Maybe more. Everything he—" Her voice failed. Emrys's life work, scattered in a drainage ditch at a transit station, to be gathered by soldiers' red hands and melted down for scrap. She had done the arithmetic a hundred times in the workshop — what could be carried, what must be left — but she had never once put the atlas itself on the side of what could be lost. And she had thrown it away in four seconds, for one child.

She would do it again in four seconds more. That was the terrible discovery. Grief could wait; arithmetic could wait. The small hands gripping her coat could not.

Mira pulled back. In the thin lamplight leaking between the wagons, her face was fierce and wet and absolutely certain of something. She tugged at Ulyana's sleeve — *not my hand, my sleeve* — and then, with the gravity of a child performing a ritual, she reached into the bodice of her dress and drew out a small hard shape wrapped in the scrap of yellow silk Halina had given her at parting.

She pressed it into Ulyana's palms.

Ulyana unwrapped it with fingers that would not behave. Oilcloth. Engraved lines under her thumb. The grid. The bridge. The small, furious marks of the demolition charges, and beside them, in Emrys's hand, the letters she had deciphered once by candlelight and never forgotten.

The key plate. It had never been in the satchel. It had never been in the workshop. For however many days — since the fish market, since the doorstep, Ulyana would never know — the most important object in the occupied territories had been riding south against the heartbeat of a seven-year-old girl, stitched there by hands no one had thought to search, because everyone, even Ulyana, still believed the child carried nothing but silence.

Mira spoke. Not much. Just enough, in that rusty, half-forgotten voice, her eyes fixed on the dark rails running south.

"You didn't drop the important one."

Down the line, a whistle blew — long, urgent, wrong — and lanterns began to move along the platform toward the gate they had just passed through.
