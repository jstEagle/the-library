## 9. The Pontoon Bridge

The river announced itself long before they saw it—a gray breathing under the fog, wide and patient, as if it had been waiting all their lives. Ulyana felt it in the soles of her feet before she heard it, the way Emrys had taught her to feel a change in terrain through a floor: the ground remembers water, even when the water is hidden.

Mira's hand was small and sweat-damp inside her own. They had walked out of the rail yard with the girl's dress rustling against Ulyana's hip, the key plate sewn into its hem pressing against them both like a second heartbeat, and they had not spoken of the transit train or the satchel left open on the platform, plates scattered like shed scales. There would be time for grief later, or there would be no time at all. The map said *south*. The map said *bridge*. And so they walked south toward the bridge while behind them, faint as a memory, the sirens began.

Ulyana counted streets by habit—Emrys's discipline had outlived him, lodged in her hands and eyes—and found the fog doing half her work for her, softening corners, swallowing uniforms. Twice they passed soldiers walking the other way, and twice the soldiers looked through them as through weather. It was Mira who stopped first, planting her heels at the mouth of the embankment road, and Ulyana followed the child's gaze up over the rooftops to where the pontoon bridge lay across the water like something fallen mid-flight: a spine of gray planking on gray drums, low against the current, guarded at both ends by sandbags and men.

The third checkpoint. The last one.

She had rehearsed this in her head through every hour of the night walk, rehearsed lies the way other people rehearse prayers—*we are visiting an aunt, we have passes, we have lost our papers in the crowd at the station.* She had believed none of the lies even as she built them. Lies were scaffolding; they held you up only until someone leaned on them hard enough. What she carried could not survive a lie. It could only survive the truth, offered whole, like a blade laid flat on a table with the handle turned outward.

And standing at the near end of that bridge, apart from the sandbagged post, hands clasped behind his back in the posture of a man who has been waiting a long time and does not enjoy it—was Aurel.

Her heart did not lurch. It dropped, quietly and completely, the way a stone drops into deep water, leaving no ripple on the surface at all.

He had aged ten years in the four since the schoolroom, though he had been older than her then, already half a man, already good at drawing maps in the margins of his Latin exercises because he loved the shapes of places the way she did. That was how they had been friends once—two children bent over the same atlas, trading the names of rivers like marbles. Now the same face stood between her and the far shore, wearing the uniform she had spent four years learning not to see.

"He's waiting for us," Mira said. Her voice was thin, but steady—the voice of a child who had learned that fear was a room you could still walk around in.

"Yes," Ulyana said.

"You knew he might be."

"I hoped," Ulyana said, and found that it was true, "and I dreaded it, and both were right."

They walked forward together. The fog thinned as they came down onto the embankment, and the river rose around them, huge and dark and indifferent, carrying on its surface the reflections of searchlights that hadn't switched off yet, pale ghosts wheeling in the current.

Aurel watched them come. His rifle was slung, not leveled—that was something—but two other soldiers stood by the sandbags, and beyond them the planks of the bridge ran out into the mist, each one a small wooden promise that someone else would have to keep.

"Papers," he said. His voice was careful. Flat. A voice holding itself shut.

Ulyana opened her coat slowly and drew out the folded travel pass—Halina's pass, Halina's sacrifice, a dead woman's name in living ink—and put it in his hand. He looked at it longer than a pass deserved. Then he looked past it, at Mira, and something moved in his face like a fish moving under ice.

"Verification," he said. "There's an order. A woman and a child, southbound tonight. I'm to hold them." He said it as if reciting someone else's handwriting. Perhaps he was. "You understand I have no choice."

"Everyone has choices now," Ulyana said. "That's what the last three days taught me. That's what the eastern bridge taught me."

His jaw tightened. Around them the fog breathed; the river breathed; the two soldiers by the sandbags stamped their feet against the cold and looked elsewhere, bored, mercifully bored.

"You don't know what you're saying," Aurel said quietly.

"No," Ulyana agreed. "I don't know anything anymore. But I can show you."

She took out the photograph.

It had traveled in her breast pocket since the night of the doorstep, warm against her heart through checkpoints and rail yards and the long terror of the transit platform, and when she unfolded it now it was soft at the creases, going furred at the edges like something alive that had been handled too much. Soldiers at the eastern bridge. She turned it so the light from the post lamp fell across it, and she watched Aurel's face instead of the picture, because she knew the picture by heart—knew the ranks of men along the railing, knew the crates stacked at the span's center with their stenciled markings, knew the officer checking his watch as if the morning were an appointment.

"What is this," Aurel said, and it wasn't a question, it was a man asking for more time before he understood.

"It's your side's photograph," Ulyana said. "Found sewn into a child's coat lining by a woman who told me to forget her face. Look at the date stamp. Look at the time. The bridge collapsed at noon, they told the city—structural failure, tragic accident, forty-one souls. Now look at the sky in this photograph, Aurel. Look at the shadows. That's morning. Hours before. Your engineers, your crates, your officer with his watch. They loaded that bridge to fall, and they filled it with people first, and then they called it an accident, and my master died keeping the proof of it alive in ink because paper is the only place truth survives a regime."

The words came out of her level and quiet, and she was distantly surprised by her own voice, which sounded nothing like grief anymore. Grief had burned down to something else. Timber becomes charcoal; charcoal, under pressure, becomes almost stone.

Aurel held the photograph the way a man holds a document he has been subpoenaed to read. His thumb moved across the corner. He did not look at the soldiers by the sandbags. He did not look at the bridge.

"The eastern bridge," he said. "My cousin's company was rotated off that crossing two days before. Two days." His voice had gone somewhere young. Somewhere back in a classroom with maps in the margins. "He wrote home that he'd been lucky."

"He was," Ulyana said. "Forty-one others weren't. And the ones who ordered it are writing reports right now saying the river did it. The river, Aurel. As if the river has hands."

Somewhere behind them, back through the sleeping streets they had crossed, the sirens changed pitch—closer now, or more of them, a sound like the city clearing its throat. One of the soldiers by the sandbags straightened and cocked his head, listening. Mira's fingers tightened around Ulyana's hand until the bones pressed together, and Ulyana pressed back, and between them passed everything that needed no language: *I am here. We are here. Whatever happens, it happens to both.*

Aurel turned the photograph over. On the back, in Emrys's small, precise, dead hand, was the notation Ulyana had deciphered by candlelight among his hidden papers—the load calculations, the demolition charges disguised as maintenance cargo, the word *deliberate* underlined three times in ink gone brown as old blood. Aurel read it. She watched him read it twice, his lips moving slightly on the second pass, and she watched the moment arrive in his face—the moment when the uniform and the man underneath it pulled in opposite directions and the seam showed.

She did not beg. She had promised herself that on the embankment road, walking through the fog with a child's damp hand in hers: no pleas, no tears, no invocation of the schoolroom or the old friendship. Pleas made him a judge, and judges defer. Truth made him a witness, and witnesses must choose.

"They'll ask why I let you cross," Aurel said at last, very quietly.

"Yes," Ulyana said.

"They'll ask where I was when the order came down." He folded the photograph along its worn crease and held it out to her, and his hand was shaking—not much, just enough to make the lamplight tremble in the creases. "Keep it. Keep it away from me. If I'm searched—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again in a voice scraped down to bare wood. "If they stop me afterward and find nothing, I'm a fool who waved two travelers across. If they find this, I'm an accomplice. So take it, and go, and when you tell whatever court or committee or God ends up hearing about the eastern bridge—" his eyes came up and fixed on hers, and they were wet, and he did not wipe them, "—tell them one of the men they sent to guard the crossings read the report and let the evidence go. Tell them conscience isn't only for the winning side. Promise me that."

"I promise," Ulyana said, and meant it the way she meant the lines she inked: completely, permanently, in a hand that did not shake.

Aurel turned to the soldiers at the sandbags, and his voice snapped back into parade-ground shape like a banner catching wind. "Woman and child, cleared southbound. Papers verified. Get back to your posts." They moved without interest, grateful for any order that required no thought, and the gap between the sandbags opened like a door.

Then he stepped aside, and the bridge was there—gray planks running out into the fog, the river muttering beneath the pontoons, the far bank invisible but certain, the way the future is certain only when you refuse to look at it directly.

Ulyana took one step, then another, Mira's hand in hers, the key plate riding against the child's ribs and the photograph riding against her own heart, and at the third plank she heard him speak once more, softly, without turning around, pitched only for her.

"The atlas," Aurel said. "Your master's atlas. When it's finished—when it's safe—put my name in the margin somewhere. Small. Where no one will see it. I'd like to exist somewhere as what I was before all this."

"A mapmaker's margin," Ulyana said, "is the most honest place in the world. I'll write you there."

Behind them the sirens crested, and boots began—many boots, on cobbles, coming fast through the fog toward the checkpoint, toward the man who was already turning to face them with his papers squared and his story ready and his cousin's luck burning cold in his chest.

Ulyana took Mira's hand and ran out onto the river, plank after plank after plank, the far shore hidden but coming, and the child laughing—a small, cracked, astonishing sound, the first laugh Ulyana had ever heard from her—half terror and half something that had no name yet but would, someday, have a country.

* * *

The fog on the far bank swallowed them whole, and the sirens kept rising behind, and neither of them looked back.
