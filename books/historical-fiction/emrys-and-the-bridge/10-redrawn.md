## 10. Redrawn

The officers of the Allied commission had set up their headquarters in a requisitioned schoolhouse on the far side of the river, and Ulyana noticed, before anything else, the chalkboard. Someone had scrubbed it hastily; ghosts of a geography lesson still clung to the slate—the curl of a coastline, a dotted line where a border had been taught to children as though it were a law of nature. Now soldiers moved desks aside and spread documents across them, and the room smelled of wet wool, coal smoke, and ink.

Ink. After everything, it was ink that nearly undid Ulyana. The smell of it rose from the officers' table and went straight through the ribs like a needle finding the seam of an old wound. Emrys had smelled of ink always—fingers stained brown-black to the second knuckle, the scent ground so deep into his skin that even in his coffin, when she had bent to straighten his collar one last time, that was what came back to her. Not his face. His smell.

"Name?" asked the officer behind the desk. He was young for his rank, with a clerk's hands and a soldier's eyes, and he had already reached for a blank form as if names were simply things one wrote down and filed away.

Ulyana stood in the doorway of his office with Mira's hand small and cold inside their own, and for a long moment said nothing at all. Behind them, through the tall schoolhouse windows, the river ran gray under a sky the color of unprimed paper. Somewhere on its far bank was the city they had crawled out of—the checkpoints, the fish market stinking of brine, Halina's shuttered window, the workshop with its cold stove and its candle stubs burned down to pools of wax. Somewhere back there was a name written in ledgers, in warden lists, in the verification file of a soldier named Anton who had let two figures cross a pontoon bridge while sirens screamed behind him like something wounded.

"Your name," the officer repeated, not unkindly, but with the flat patience of a man who had processed a hundred fugitives this month and would process a hundred more.

"I have something better than a name," Ulyana said.

The satchel came off the shoulder slowly. It was lighter than it had been three weeks ago—they all were lighter, the way travelers become, pared down mile by mile to what cannot be abandoned—but it still carried weight, the particular dense weight of paper that has been protected above all other things. Ulyana unwrapped the oilcloth with fingers that would not quite stop shaking, laid the plates out one by one on the officer's desk, and watched his face change.

He had been reaching for a form. He stopped reaching.

The atlas of Emrys covered the desk the way a spilled tide covers sand: every bridge in the river country, every ford, every ferry crossing marked and dated in a hand so fine it looked embroidered. And there, at the eastern edge, the last plate he ever worked on—the bridge over the gorge at Velmory, drawn twice. Once as it had stood. Once as it stood now, or had stood before the war moved past it: broken. And in the margin, in Emrys's smallest, most secret script, the notation Ulyana had spent four nights deciphering by candlelight while a mute child slept against the cold wall of a hay barn: *Load calculated for demolition. Timed charges. Not collapse. Never collapse.*

The photograph went down on top of it. Soldiers at the eastern bridge, grinning into a borrowed camera, crates at their feet with their stenciled markings just legible enough. A woman had died—Ulyana believed this now with a certainty that sat in the chest like swallowed stone—to carry this image out of the occupied zone. A woman had pressed it into her daughter's coat lining and told a stranger to forget her face, and the least Ulyana could do was remember everything else.

The officer read for a long time. The clock on the schoolroom wall ticked like something counting down, but to what, no one could say anymore. Mira leaned against Ulyana's hip, and Ulyana felt her weight settle—not asleep, not afraid, just present, the way she had learned to be present these past weeks, watching everything with those dark quiet eyes.

"This is evidence," the officer said at last. His voice had lost its clerkly flatness. "Do you understand what you're giving me?"

"No," Ulyana said. "I'm giving you my master's work. He gave eleven years to it. I only carried it the last hundred miles."

"You carried it past three checkpoints," the officer said. "We know about the fish market. We know about the rail yard." He looked up then, and there was something human in his face, tired and surprised. "There will be questions. Names, dates, testimony."

"There is testimony," Ulyana said, and touched the top of Mira's head. The girl did not flinch. She had begun speaking again on the road south, in fragments, in a voice rusty as a hinge—*the lights went blue, Mama said run, the water was loud*—and someday, when she was ready, she would say the whole of it to someone who wrote things down properly. But not today, and not because anyone demanded.

"The atlas stays," Ulyana said. "The photograph stays. Both belong to the record now. What happened at Velmory belongs to everyone who will come after and ask how that bridge fell."

"And you?" the officer asked. "You'll want recognition. Restitution. There are provisions—"

"I want paper," Ulyana said. "Blank. And a candle."

The officer stared. Then, being a man who had learned when not to ask, he provided both.

* * *

They walked out of the town before evening, past the last of the billets, to a rise above the river where a stand of birches had gone gold and let the wind take whatever it wanted. Ulyana had chosen the place the way Emrys would have chosen it—with an eye for sightlines, for cover, for beauty that served a purpose. From here they could see the water bend away south, silvering as the light dropped, carrying toward countries Ulyana knew only as shapes on other people's maps.

The old papers came out of the coat first. The identity card with its official stamp and its photograph taken in a year when Ulyana Yarrow had still believed invisibility was a kind of safety. The workshop registration, naming Emrys Blackwood as master and Ulyana as apprentice, as though a life could be entered into a ledger and thereby contained. The travel passes—Anton's verification paper, which had become a leash, and Halina's own pass, given with shaking hands and a son's name spoken like a prayer. Each document was a stitch in the garment of a self that had never fit.

Ulyana struck the candle, cupped the flame, and fed the papers to it one at a time.

It should have felt like dying. That was the strange part—it didn't. It felt like finishing a plate. Like the moment when the last line is inked and the pen lifts and the drawing is no longer yours but simply true. The identity card curled and blackened; the name on it went first, eaten from the middle outward, and Ulyana watched it go without grief and almost without fear. *Ulyana Yarrow.* Apprentice. Orphan of no particular importance. A woman who had kept her head down for six years of occupation and told herself that survival was the same thing as living.

The wind took the ash. It rose white against the darkening birches, scattered, and was gone south over the river—the same wind, Ulyana thought, that would have carried the smoke of Velmory once, and the smoke of the fish market, and the smoke of every fire this war had lit. Let it have the name too. Names were only labels, after all. Emrys had taught that, though Ulyana hadn't understood it then. Every border on every map was a line someone had decided to draw, and what was a name but a border drawn around a person?

Mira watched solemnly from her perch on a fallen log, knees hugged to her chest, chin resting on them. She was wearing the dress with the hidden seam, though the key plate had long since been surrendered with the rest of the atlas. She had sewn it there herself, Ulyana had discovered at the rail yard—six years old, mute with terror, and clever enough to understand that the most important thing should live closest to the body. Ulyana thought of that often now. It had rearranged something fundamental, learning that. The child had known before any of them: you do not entrust your treasure to the world. You make it part of yourself.

"What will we be called?" Mira asked. Her voice was still new, still an astonishment every time it came.

"Whatever we choose," Ulyana said, and sat down beside her on the log, close enough that their shoulders touched. "That's the whole discovery, little one. Nobody tells you. You just draw it."

Mira considered this with the gravity she brought to everything. "I want to keep Mira."

"Keep it, then. It suits you."

"But you can't be—" She frowned, searching. "The lady at the farmhouse called you something. Yesterday."

"The lady at the farmhouse called me many things," Ulyana said, "most of them wrong."

From the inner pocket of the coat came the last piece of paper, the only one worth keeping blank things company: a sheet of good stock, thick and cream-colored, the kind Emrys reserved for plates he expected to last centuries. Ulyana smoothed it across both knees. A pencil stub appeared from somewhere—there was always a pencil stub; there would be a pencil stub in the coffin, Ulyana thought, if anyone thought to check.

And then, unhurried, in a hand that had grown surer with every mile since the workshop, Ulyana began to draw.

Not a map of anywhere. That was the point. No coastlines, no borders, no rivers with their fords and bridges ranked by strategic value. Just a single road, beginning at the bottom edge of the page in a wide confident stroke. Along it, two small figures—one tall, one short, hand in hand—and where the road forked, it did not fork; it simply continued, curving gently toward the upper right corner and off the edge of the paper, into whatever space lay beyond maps. No legend. No scale. In the corner, where a cartographer would put a title and a name, Ulyana drew instead two circles overlapping, like rings, like orbits, like a Venn diagram of a single shared life.

"That's us," Mira said. Not a question.

"That's us."

"Where does the road go?"

"Nowhere yet. Everywhere later." Ulyana turned the pencil in their fingers and looked out at the river, at the far shore dissolving into dusk, at the whole unmarked territory of a future with no master's voice to guide it and no occupier's boot to flee. The grief was still there—Emrys was still dead, the woman at the door was still dead, Anton was somewhere behind that water facing whatever conscience cost a man in uniform—and it would be there always, Ulyana understood now, braided into everything the way sorrow braids into joy in any honest account of a life. But grief, too, was a kind of ink. It wrote things. It made marks that lasted.

Belonging, Ulyana thought, was not a place. It had never been the workshop, though she had loved it; never the city, though she had grieved it. It was this: a log in a birch grove, a child's shoulder warm against her own, a blank page and the means to fill it. Identity was not issued. It was drafted, revised, redrawn—as many times as the hand could hold the instrument.

Mira leaned her head against Ulyana's arm. Below them the river went on toward the sea, indifferent and patient, erasing banks, redrawing shores.

"We'll need a new name for you," Mira said drowsily. "For the story. When I tell it."

"A story? Already?"

"About the bridge," Mira said. "And the mapmaker. And the soldier who let us go." Her eyes were closing. "Every story needs a name for the one who carried it."

Ulyana looked down at the page—at the road, at the two small figures walking it, at the empty corner waiting where a title would go—and picked up the pencil one more time.

That night, for the first time in six years, Ulyana signed a work of their own.
