## 2. A House That Keeps Its Own Time

The regulator clock stood nine feet tall in the counting-house's central hall, and it was the first thing Master Verrin had ever taught Mirabel to love.

Not the pearls — though he had learned to love those too, in the way a man loves a thing that might kill him. Not the ledgers, with their patient columns marching like rows of winter wheat. The clock. It had come upriver from Kingsford on the spring barge eleven years ago, crated in straw, and Mirabel, who was six then and still allowed to stand near his father without an indenture paper between them, had watched eight men carry it through the doors of the Verrin Trading Company as if they were bearing a coffin into a cathedral.

*That*, Halvard Grell had told him, crouching to the boy's height, *is the most honest thing in this building.*

Mirabel was seventeen now, and he believed it still. He believed it the way he believed in the weight of water and the patience of ice — because he had tested it every day of his bound life and found it true.

* * *

The counting-house woke before the town did, and it woke by the clock.

At half past five, its great brass pendulum — visible through the glass door of the case, swinging slow and golden in the lamplight — counted the quarter hour, and the apprentices rose from their pallets in the loft above the storerooms. At six, it struck, and the clerks came down the stairs two at a time with their collars buttoned and their pens already behind their ears like carpenters' nails. At seven, the outer doors were unbarred, and the rivermen began to queue at the pay window with their chilblained hands cupped around tin lamps. Every minute of the company's day issued from that clock the way light issues from a lamp: not violently, but completely, so that you could not afterward imagine the room without it.

And every Sunday evening, and every Wednesday at dusk, Mirabel Amberdale wound it.

It was considered delicate work, and there were only three people in Coldharbour permitted to touch the mechanism: Master Verrin himself; old Prosser, the factor, whose fingers had grown too stiff these last two winters; and Mirabel, because Halvard had recommended him when he was twelve, telling the master — Mirabel had overheard this through the door, and never forgotten it — *the boy's hands don't shake, sir. Not even under water.* It was the only compliment anyone in authority had ever paid him, and he had built a small, secret house inside himself to keep it in.

He went up the winding stair behind the clock case at dusk on Wednesdays, carrying the brass key on a cord around his neck and a shuttered lantern, and for a quarter of an hour he existed in the narrow dark country between the walls of the counting-house, among the weights and wires, where the whole building's time was made.

There was a discipline to it. Two weights on iron cables, one driving the pendulum and one striking the hours, and each had to be raised exactly so — too far and the cable would jump its groove and the clock would run fast for a week, which meant a week of men docked wages for lateness they hadn't earned. Too little and the clock would starve before Saturday and the whole town would drift out of order like a boat off its mooring. You learned the exact pull. You learned to feel, through your palms, when the weight had reached the top of its travel, the way his father had known through the rope when the pearl bed was beneath him and it was time to let go of the air in his lungs and drop.

His father had taught him that. The clock taught him everything else.

Tonight was a Wednesday in late February, and the cold had teeth. Mirabel climbed the stair with his breath smoking in the lantern light and set his shoulder to the crank, and the great weights rose groaning through the darkness above him like things being pulled out of a well, and he did what he always did at the top of the wind: he stopped, and listened.

You could hear the whole house from back there. The tick of the escapement, enormous at close range, a heart beating inside the walls. Below it, fainter, the sounds of the counting-house settling into night — the last clerk locking the ink cabinet, the banked stove ticking as it cooled, the muffled argument of the river under the ice, talking to itself the way it did all winter, low and constant, a language nobody in town admitted to hearing anymore except the divers.

And tonight, something else. Hooves on the frozen yard. More than one horse, arriving fast.

Mirabel frowned. No barge had come up from Kingsford since the week before; the river road was closed, and the sledge track from the south ran only to the tannery. Nobody traveled after dark in February who didn't have to. He lowered the lantern's shutter to a thread and stayed where he was, because the stair behind the clock had no door at the bottom — only a slatted panel that opened onto the upper gallery of the central hall, and through those slats he could see the hall below the way you see a stage through a curtain.

The front doors were unbarred from within — someone had been expecting this, then — and the men who came in out of the dark brought the cold with them in a white breath that rolled across the floorboards like smoke.

There were four of them: three servants, wrapped and hooded, who took up positions near the door with the practiced stillness of men who had done this many times in many towns. And a fourth man, smaller than the others but somehow larger in the room, who removed his fur gloves finger by finger as he walked, unhurried, down the center of the hall as if he owned the boards beneath his boots.

Mirabel knew him from the engraving above the pay window, and from Halvard's careful lessons in company history. Director Ashcombe. Third name on the charter. A man Mirabel had seen perhaps twice in his life, both times at a distance, both times in daylight, both times surrounded by the ordinary deference owed to any director making his rounds.

But directors made their rounds in summer, when the accounts could be argued over openly and everyone could watch everyone else watching. Directors did not come to Coldharbour in February, by night, with servants posted at the door.

Master Verrin came out of his office with a lamp already lit, and Mirabel felt something turn over quietly in his chest, because he had never seen the master hurry, and the master was hurrying now. Old Verrin — sixty-eight that winter, gout-swollen, his voice long since worn down to gravel — crossed the hall at a pace that made him stumble on the last step of the dais, and Ashcombe did not move to steady him, and Verrin did not expect him to.

"Director." The word came out flattened, formal, wrong. "We weren't told—"

"You weren't," Ashcombe agreed. His voice was mild. It carried perfectly in the empty hall, up through the slats, into the dark where Mirabel stood not breathing. "I prefer it so."

* * *

Afterward — for years afterward — Mirabel would try to reconstruct exactly what he saw through that panel, and he would find that memory had kept it with terrible fidelity, every detail sharp as a diver's view of the surface from below: bright, unreachable, distorted only by the water between.

Ashcombe sat at the long table. Verrin remained standing, which was itself a reversal of the natural order, like seeing the moon beneath the horizon. A servant brought a leather folio from one of the saddlebags — not papers from the office archive, Mirabel noted, papers that had come from outside, from the south, past all the checkpoints that existed to make sure nothing moved on the river without the company knowing it — and laid it before the director, and Ashcombe opened it and turned it around and pushed it across the table with two fingers.

"Read the third page," Ashcombe said. "Then tell me how it comes to be in my possession instead of in your strongroom."

Verrin read. Mirabel could not see his face from above — only the bald crown of his head, bent toward the lamp, and his hands, which were flat on the table on either side of the folio, and which, after a long moment, pressed down as though steadying themselves against the movement of a deck.

"I can explain—" 

"No," said Ashcombe, still mild. "You can account. There is a difference, and I have come a very long way in poor weather because I require the second thing and am no longer interested in the first."

The clock ticked above it all. Mirabel became aware, distantly, that he was supposed to be somewhere — the weights were wound, the stair waited — but he could not have moved for a purse of gold. He stood in the dark with his hand against the cold frame and watched the company he had given his childhood to conduct business he was never meant to see.

Papers changed hands. He could not read them from his angle; he caught only shapes — a ledger page, folded twice, passed from Verrin's hand to the director's; a second sheet returned; Verrin signing something with the heavy company seal, pressing it down into the wax with both thumbs, the way he signed the indentures every quarter-day. Mirabel had watched him do it a dozen times, always with the same unhurried gravity, the seal coming down like a small verdict. Tonight the thumbs pressed hard and long, longer than the wax needed, as if the master wanted to be sure the thing was dead.

And once — only once — Verrin looked up from the table and out across the hall, at nothing, at the dark windows, at the frozen river he had spent forty years turning into money, and Mirabel, peering down through the slats, saw on the old man's face an expression he had no name for yet. It was not guilt. Guilt looks outward, toward the injured party. This looked inward, like a man doing sums he cannot make come out even, and knowing the fault is in the figures themselves and has been for years.

Then Ashcombe spoke again, and the expression was gone, and there was no more time to wonder about faces.

"The matter is concluded," Ashcombe said. He rose, drew on his gloves, and Mirabel understood that whatever had just happened — whatever names had been written and sealed and carried through the winter night — was, for the director, finished. Filed. He gathered the folio under his arm, and paused, and added, almost as an afterthought, in the tone of a man noting a line item:

"You are unwell, Verrin. Everyone can see it. Put your affairs in a condition that will bear inspection. The company dislikes disorder, and it dislikes surprises more. Do not oblige me to experience another one."

"Yes, Director."

"And the boy who winds the clock." Ashcombe said it without looking up, shrugging the folio deeper under his arm, and the floor seemed to tilt very slightly under Mirabel's feet. "Prosser mentioned him in the autumn report. The diver's son." A pause, in which the pendulum swung twice, three times, golden and indifferent behind its glass. "A reliable child is a valuable thing. See he stays one."

The doors were barred behind them. The horses stamped and took the men away south along the sledge track, and the sound of them faded into the great white silence of the river valley, and the counting-house was empty again, and warm again, and orderly again, and none of it was the same.

* * *

Mirabel came down the stair at last on legs that felt borrowed. In the hall, the lamp Verrin had abandoned still burned on the dais, and the master himself still sat at the long table, quite alone, quite still, staring at the place where the folio had been as though the wood might confess what it had held.

He heard Mirabel's boots and did not startle. Perhaps he had known the boy was there all along; perhaps he had chosen, in whatever currency directors and masters traded in, to permit it. He looked up, and his eyes were very tired, and for one moment — Mirabel would swear this later, would stake his freedom on it — the old man looked at him with something that was almost pity, or almost warning, or almost both.

"The clock's been wound?" Verrin asked. His voice was gravel and frost.

"Wound and true, Master. She'll hold till Saturday."

Verrin nodded slowly. He looked at the clock — nine feet of oak and brass and honest arithmetic, the most truthful thing in the building — and then he said, softly, to it rather than to the boy, words Mirabel did not understand yet but would carry south with him through snow and dogs and broken ice until they made sense:

"A house that keeps its own time." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "So does a coffin, boy. So does a coffin."

He rose, took up his lamp, and went to his rooms, and his door closed with the small final click of a man shutting a book.

Mirabel stood alone in the great hall. Above him the pendulum swung, patient and golden, keeping perfect time the way it had kept perfect time through every season of his bound life, and he found he could not look at it. For the first time in eleven years, the tick of the escapement sounded less like a heartbeat than like something being counted down — and he did not know to what.

Three nights later, Master Verrin sent for him privately, and the girl at the gate said the master had not risen all day, and the doctor's boy had come twice, and Mirabel climbed the stairs to the sickroom carrying the brass key on its cord around his neck, though there was no clock up there to wind, and nothing in the world he could think of that would ever be wound and true again.
