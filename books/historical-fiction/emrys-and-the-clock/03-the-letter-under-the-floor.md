## 3. The Letter Under the Floor

The summons came at the hour when the counting-house clock was wound but not yet believed.

Mirabel had learned to read the regulator's moods the way other men read weather. By day it ruled the room with its brass pendulum and its pale, unblinking face; every clerk in Verrin's employ bent their hours to that ticking as though it were scripture. But in the deep of night, when the fires were banked and the ledgers slept in their locked cabinets, the clock seemed almost mortal — a great heart beating in a dark chest, keeping time for no one.

It was then, on a night so cold the windows wore frost like old lace, that Tamsin the housekeeper touched his shoulder where he slept in the loft above the stables.

"He's asking for you," she said. "Only you."

She held a storm lantern low, so the light fell away from her face, and Mirabel understood from the set of her mouth that she had been told not to ask questions. Tamsin had served the house of Verrin for thirty years and had learned that in a trading company, obedience was cheaper than curiosity.

"Is he worse?" Mirabel asked, already pulling his boots on. His fingers were clumsy. The cold came up through the floorboards like something rising from under the ice of the river itself.

"He is leaving," Tamsin said simply. "He knows it, and he is putting his affairs in order. You are one of the affairs."

* * *

Master Aldous Verrin lay in the great bed on the third floor, in the room Mirabel had entered perhaps five times in eleven years of indenture. It smelled of camphor and candle tallow and, beneath both, of river water — though how that could be, this far above the waterline, Mirabel could not say. Perhaps the whole house was soaked through with the river. Perhaps everything in Coldharbour was.

The master was a ruin of the man who signed contracts with a flourish that made the clerks wince. He had been tall once — you could see the architecture of it still, in the long bones of his hands and the proud slope of his shoulders even against the pillows — but the wasting sickness had drawn him down like a tide going out over mudflats, leaving everything exposed and diminished. His skin had the grey-green sheen of pearl shell. His eyes, though. His eyes were still awake.

"Boy." His voice was a dry rope creaking over a pulley. "Lock it."

Mirabel turned the key in the chamber door. The click of it went through him strangely. In eleven years he had never been alone with Master Verrin behind a locked door. Indentured boys did not share locked rooms with masters. They shared them with lice and cold and the arithmetic they weren't supposed to understand.

"Come here where I can see you. Not the lamp. *You.*"

Mirabel came and stood at the bedside. This close, he could hear the wet rattle in the old man's breathing, a sound like a bucket drawn up from a very deep well.

"You dive for winter pearls," Verrin said. "Under the ice. Your father's trade before it was yours."

"Yes, Master."

"How long can you hold your breath?"

"Four minutes if the water is very cold. Longer if I don't struggle."

Something moved across the ruined face — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Your father could do six. I timed him myself, twenty years ago, standing on the bank like a fool with a turnip-watch while he sat in the shallows breathing slow as an ox. Six minutes." The eyes closed. "I have thought about those six minutes many times since. About what it costs a man to stay under that long. What he must make himself not care about."

"Master, should I fetch the physician—"

"The physician is paid to keep me comfortable. I am not comfortable, boy, I am *dying*, and there is a difference between the two that your physician will never understand because he has never had to answer for anything." A cough took him then, thin and terrible, folding him nearly double until Mirabel instinctively put a hand behind the sharp ridge of his spine. When it passed, Verrin lay back gasping and said, "Thank you," in a voice stripped of all command, and that thank-you frightened Mirabel more than anything else in the room.

"I don't have strength for a speech," Verrin said. "So I will give you facts instead, and you will hold them the way you hold your breath — without struggling, and for as long as it takes.

"Fact the first: the Verrin Trading Company runs contraband south every spring when the ice breaks. Salt-escaped goods. Untaxed brandy. Arms, in the bad years. We hide it in the pearl crates, in bales marked for legitimate sale, and we buy the silence of every checkpoint between here and Kingsford."

Mirabel did not move. The fire ticked. Somewhere below, faint as a pulse, the regulator kept its time.

"Fact the second: it was not always so. My father built this house on pearls and honest freight, and I ran it honestly for nineteen years. Then Director Ashcombe came to me with figures instead of threats, which is how Ashcombe does everything — he makes theft look like arithmetic. And I listened. God forgive me, I listened, because the figures were beautiful."

"Master—"

"Fact the third." His hand came out from under the coverlet and closed on Mirabel's wrist — shockingly strong, the grip of a man who had spent his life pulling ropes and signing names. "Your father found out."

The words went into Mirabel like cold water into the ear on a dive — that sudden, sickening pressure, the world tilting.

"Dannen Amberdale was the best diver on this stretch of river and too honest by half, which on this river is the same as being reckless. He noticed the pearl crates were heavier than they should be. Weighted wrong. He was a man who knew weight the way a priest knows prayer — he felt sin in an ounce. He came to me privately. He gave me the chance to stop it quietly, because he was decent, and decency always warns before it reports."

Verrin's grip tightened.

"And I went to Ashcombe instead."

The fire settled. Mirabel heard himself breathing and made himself stop, made himself breathe the way he breathed before a dive — slow, deep, deliberate — because otherwise he would have shaken apart.

"What happened to him," Mirabel said. It was not a question. Questions go up at the end. His voice went down, like something dropped into water.

"He refused to dive the contraband. That is written somewhere, boy, though I doubt you'll ever see the writing — he refused, formally, in front of witnesses, and Ashcombe could not allow a refusal with a witness attached. So the debt was manufactured. The ledger was cooked. Your father's indenture — which should never have existed, which my father's day would never have permitted — was called due in full, and when it could not be paid, the company took everything. The boat. The shed. The diving rights. And then, when he still would not bend, when he still walked into my office with his hat in his hands and his spine straight as a mast—" Verrin's breath hitched, failed, caught again, "—the river was arranged for him."

"Arranged."

"His lines were cut. His marker buoy untied. Men saw a boat go out that evening with two aboard and come back with one. The inquest said misadventure. The inquest was paid for. Your father drowned three hundred yards from shore, boy, in water he had worked since he was seven years old, because I was too much of a coward to stand between him and men who counted human ruin the way they counted tonnage."

The room swam. Mirabel found he was sitting — he had no memory of sitting — on the edge of the bed with the old man's hand still locked around his wrist, anchor and chain both.

Eleven years. Eleven years he had lived in the house of the man who had done this, worn the company's cloth, wound the company's clock, eaten the company's bread. Eleven years he had believed — had *needed* to believe — that his father died of the river's indifference, that grief could be carried like a stone without ever asking who had tied it around a drowning man's neck. And all the while the proof had slept upstairs, in camphor and linen, breathing with that wet rattle, calling him *boy*.

"Why," Mirabel heard himself say. "Why tell me now."

"Because I am dying, and dying men pay their debts or damn themselves past redemption." Verrin reached beneath his pillow with trembling hands. "And because you are the only one left who has reason to carry this and nothing to lose by carrying it. Everyone else the truth touches has either been bought or buried."

What he drew out was wrapped in oilcloth and tied with sailmaker's twine. Even through the wrapping Mirabel felt the stiffness of good paper, heavy stock, and the hard little ridge of a seal.

"Unwrap it. Look. Don't open the seal — the seal matters, understand? A magistrate wants wax unbroken. Break it early and half the force goes out of it."

Mirabel's fingers seemed to belong to someone else. The oilcloth fell open. Inside lay a letter thick as a psalter, folded and sealed in red wax stamped with the Verrin mark — the pearl in the open oyster shell — and addressed across its face in the master's own failing hand:

*To the Magistrate of the Peace, Kingsford Assizes. To be delivered by hand only.*

"It is my confession," Verrin said. "Full. Names, dates, cargo manifests copied true, the payments to the checkpoints, the arrangement of the inquest. Ashcombe. Pell. The two Harrow brothers. Myself. Every man who sits at the directors' table, and the sums that bought each of them. It names Dannen Amberdale as an honest man murdered by paper, and it restores his name if not his life."

"Restores—" Mirabel's voice cracked on it. "My father is dead. What is restored?"

"A name is not nothing." For a moment the old merchant's eyes flashed with something of the man who had once commanded forty clerks. "You of all people know what it is to carry a name like a debt. They made your father's name into the story of a cheat who drowned in his own folly. They made *you* into the payment on his sins. This letter says: no. It says it in ink and wax and witnessed figures. It says it in a court where it cannot be unsaid."

"Why me?" Mirabel whispered. "You have clerks. Riders. Grell—"

"Grell serves Ashcombe now, whatever else he serves." Something flickered in the dying face — regret? warning? "Ashcombe does not yet know this letter exists. But he knows I am dying, and dying men make him nervous. There are men watching this house tonight. There will be more after. If any soul in this company learns what I have given you, you will not reach the end of Wharf Street alive, and the letter will burn with you."

"Then why—"

"Because the spring ice breaks in six weeks, boy." The urgency gave him strength he hadn't had a minute before; he pulled himself half upright, and Mirabel had to steady him. "When the river opens, the spring freight moves south, and with it the last crates that could ever corroborate what I've written. Ashcombe means to scuttle them in the deep channel — sink the proof with the season. After the break-up, this letter is one madman's word against four directors' books. Before it — before it, it is a nail. You must reach Kingsford before the ice breaks. Do you hear me? Before. Not after. There is no after."

Mirabel looked down at the letter in his hands. It weighed almost nothing. That was the obscenity of it — that a thing which had cost his father his life and him his childhood should weigh less than a loaf of bread, less than a diving weight, less than the knife at his belt. He had spent eleven years learning that large things come in small packages: a pearl, a key, a stub of ledger paper. But this —

"Why keep it under the floor of your own house?" he asked, because some part of him was still a clerk's apprentice, still needed the order of things accounted.

"Because Ashcombe searches banks and strongrooms and solicitors' offices, and never once thinks to look under the feet of the man he is waiting to die." Verrin sank back against the pillows, spent. "There is money in the lining of my green coat — enough for coaches, enough for bribes. Take the coat. Take whatever else you need and take nothing you don't. Travel like a diver, boy. Carry only what you can swim with."

The fire had burned low. Through the frost-laced window, the sky was turning from black to the colour of wet slate. Somewhere below them, the great regulator struck four, and the sound came up through the floors of the house like a stone dropped down a well.

"There's more I should tell you," Verrin murmured. His eyes had gone distant, fixed on the canopy above. "About your mother. About the second page, the one under the floor — no. No, listen, it matters, the second copy, I hid it, I hid it where—" His breath snagged. His hand groped along the counterpane, finding nothing. "Where the light comes down at noon. Where the light... comes..."

"Master?" Mirabel leaned close. "Master Verrin. Where the light comes down at noon — where?"

But the rattling breath had stopped climbing. It went down instead, slowly, the way a body goes down through cold water when the struggle is over and only the depth remains — and then that too ceased, and the room was so quiet that Mirabel could hear the pendulum far below, beating its faithful, indifferent seconds into a house that no longer had a master.

* * *

He did not remember crossing the room. He found himself at the window with the letter pressed to his chest, looking down at Wharf Street, where a lantern burned beside the company warehouse and two men stood beneath it in the snowfall, talking, stamping, waiting. Watching the house. Just as Verrin had said.

Men watching for a letter they didn't know existed. Which meant they were watching for something — for the dying man to send word out, for papers to move, for anything at all. And when the master's death was discovered at dawn, there would be more of them, and the house would be picked clean to the joists, and every servant questioned, and a certain indentured pearl-diver's son who had been seen going up to the sickroom at midnight would be the first name on every list.

Mirabel looked at the letter in his hands. Then he unwrapped the oilcloth completely, tore a strip from it, and sewed it — clumsily, with waxed thread and fingers that would not entirely stop shaking — into the inner lining of his coat, flat against the small of his back, where a man carries warmth and cannot easily be searched. The green coat hung in the master's wardrobe with coins in its lining. He took that too.

Below, the two men under the lantern had finished talking. One of them turned and looked up at the house — up, it seemed to Mirabel, directly at his window — and the glow of the lantern caught the badge on his chest: the open oyster shell, the pearl within.

Six weeks until the ice broke. Perhaps fewer than six hours until the household woke and found its master cold.

Mirabel blew out the candle and stood in the dark, listening to his own heart learn a new rhythm — quicker, harder, the rhythm of a man measuring distance in a single held breath — and began to plan how a boy who belonged to the company would steal himself out of Coldharbour before the morning bell.
