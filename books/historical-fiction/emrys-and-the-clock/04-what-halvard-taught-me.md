## 4. What Halvard Grell Taught Me

The order came at the hour when the counting-house lamps burned lowest and every clerk's hand slowed by a fraction, as though ink itself grew heavier in the dark. Halvard Grell read the note twice — once for its words and once for what lay beneath them — then folded it along its crease and set it beneath the brass weight he used to hold his drafts flat.

*Find a missing document. Spare no effort. Report to me directly, and to no one else.* No signature beyond the initial **A**, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss it.

Grell sat very still. Around him the great room breathed with the small sounds of an institution at rest: the tick of the regulator clock above the entry door, the settling of ledgers in their shelves, a draught moving through the high windows like something patient. Forty-one clerks had worked here today; thirty-nine had gone home to their lodgings in Coldharbour, and one lay dying upstairs in the master's quarters, and one stood in the copying-room holding an order that did not say what document was missing because Director Ashcombe did not wish it said aloud.

That was the first thing Grell noticed about the order, and noticing it felt like finding a hairline crack in a cup you had drunk from every morning of your life. Honest orders named their business. *Retrieve the Kessington invoices. Confirm the freight tally for the spring run.* Ashcombe's note was silent as a held breath.

He looked up at the clock.

* * *

It had been Grell who taught the boy his letters. He reminded himself of this now, sitting in the lamplight, because memory in that house was not encouraged — the company kept records, not remembrances — and yet the remembering came anyway, unbidden, the way water finds the low places.

The boy had been eight years old when they brought him upriver from the diving camps, small and wiry and blue-lipped from cold, his father's son in every line of him. The indenture papers described him as *one male child, able-bodied, bound to service for term*, and the clerk who drew them up had written the description the way one describes cargo. But Master Verrin had taken one look at the boy shivering in the entry hall and said, "Teach him his figures, Grell. A house that keeps accounts should keep its promises."

Grell had never wanted a pupil. He had been twenty-six then, already ten years at his desk, already convinced — as thoroughly as a man is convinced of the sunrise — that the Verrin Trading Company was justice made orderly. That was how he had come to it as a young man himself, half-starved off the moors, and old Clerk Wenna had set him down before a ledger and said, *Every debt written truly, every payment entered honestly, and no man can starve while the books are kept clean.* It had seemed to Grell the most beautiful sentence ever composed. In Coldharbour there were no lords to flog a man on a whim, no tithe-men, no press-gangs. There was only the clock and the ledger, and both were kept true. He had believed — he could still feel the shape of the belief, the way an amputee feels a hand — that a company which measured everything fairly would therefore *owe* everything fairly, and that arithmetic was a kind of mercy.

So he had set the pearl-diver's son on a stool beside his own desk, in the hour after supper when the lamps were cheap to burn, and begun with letters.

The boy learned the way water freezes: slowly, invisibly, and then all at once solid. Grell remembered the exact afternoon it happened. He had written *AMBERDALE* across the top of a slate — the boy's own name, though the boy had never seen it written before — and Mirabel had traced it with a finger gone still and careful, and asked, "Is that my father too? His name is the same."

"It is," Grell had said. "You carry it."

"Where does it go when he isn't using it?"

And Grell, who had answers for everything in those days, had none, so he said only, "Keep your letters even, and the name will hold its weight." The boy had nodded gravely, as though this were doctrine, and copied the name eleven times, and the last copy was better than Grell's own hand.

After letters came figures. This was where the boy astonished him. Sums came to Mirabel Amberdale not as symbols but as *things* — weights and measures he could hold. Grell would set him problems of tonnage and fare, and the boy would work them out in a whisper, narrating, "Four hundredweight, that's like four of my father's dive-weights, that's a whole sledge-load on good ice" — translating everything into what could be carried. Grell had laughed the first time, then stopped laughing, because it was precisely correct. The boy understood weight. He understood that every number was a burden borne by somebody, somewhere, and he never treated one carelessly.

"A ledger is just a promise wearing clothes," Grell had told him once, feeling wise.

"Then whose promise is it when the numbers lie?"

He had rapped the boy's knuckles with the slate-edge for impertinence — lightly, more startled than angry — and the boy had not flinched, only watched him with those diver's eyes, calm under grey water, waiting for a real answer. Grell had never given it. Twenty years later, holding Ashcombe's unsigned order, he understood that he had spent two decades not giving it.

* * *

Master Verrin's illness announced itself the way such things do in river towns: a cough in autumn that did not leave by winter. By February the doctors spoke in the careful voices of men describing ice conditions, and the counting-house ran itself while its master faded floor by floor toward the rooms under the eaves.

Grell climbed those stairs on the evening after receiving the order, telling himself it was to inquire after his master's health, knowing it was a lie the moment his boot touched the first step. He needed to see Verrin's face and find in it some reassurance that the world below stairs still made sense — that whatever document was missing, it was a small thing, an invoice misfiled, a receipt gone astray.

What he found instead was a room already being emptied.

Not by looters — by tidiness. A junior man was sorting the master's correspondence into burn-piles and keep-piles with the serene efficiency of a man following instructions, and the smell of the burnt pile was still in the air, sharp and papery. Verrin lay propped against pillows, yellowed, his breathing doing the labor of several men. His eyes found Grell in the doorway and something moved through them — relief or regret, impossible to say.

"Grell." The voice was a wreck. "Send that one out. Close the door."

Grell dismissed the junior man with a nod and stood by the bed while the door clicked shut.

"You've had a note from Ashcombe," Verrin said. Not a question.

"I have."

"And you haven't acted on it yet." A smile flickered under the grey mustache. "I told them you wouldn't. I told them you'd sit at your desk first and look at the clock. You always look at the clock when something troubles you. Thirty years, Halvard, and I have watched you consult that clock like a priest consulting scripture."

Grell said nothing, because it was true.

"There is a letter gone from this house," Verrin whispered. "Or soon to be gone. And Ashcombe wants it back before anyone reads it." A cough took him then, long and wet, and Grell found himself holding the man's wrist without being asked, feeling the pulse flutter like a bird in a sack. When it passed, Verrin's grip tightened on him with shocking strength. "Listen to me, clerk. In thirty years I have kept honest books in a dishonest trade, and I told myself that was enough. That keeping the numbers true excused the hands that signed them. Do you know what I have learned, dying?"

"No, Master."

"That a ledger is only a mirror. It shows exactly what stands in front of it. Nothing more." His eyes, bright with fever, fixed on Grell. "If Ashcombe sends you hunting, then the thing lost must be worth the hunt. Ask yourself why a director of this company fears a piece of paper more than he fears the magistrate, the weather, or God."

"He says nothing in the order," Grell managed. "No document named."

"Of course he doesn't." Verrin sank back into the pillows, exhausted, but his gaze did not waver. "Silence in an order is itself information, Grell. You taught the Amberdale boy his figures. So do the sum." His eyes drifted toward the window, toward the frozen river invisible in the dark. "The ice will break early this year. I can feel it in the walls. Everything that waits for spring is running out of winter."

He released Grell's wrist and turned his face away, and within minutes he slept, or lapsed, and Grell let himself out past the burn-piles with their sweet ash smell, down the stairs, past the clock.

* * *

Back at his desk, Grell did what he had trained himself to do for thirty years: he made an inventory.

Item: an order without a named document. Item: a director's initial pressed deep enough to emboss. Item: a burn-pile in a dying man's room, authorized by no one Grell had seen. Item: Master Verrin's warning, delivered like a bequest. Item — and this item he wrote nowhere, admitted nowhere even to himself until the lamp guttered and he trimmed the wick with hands that were perfectly steady, which troubled him more than shaking would have — item: the Amberdale boy had been seen coming down from the master's rooms at an hour when no servant should have been there. Two nights ago. Before the order arrived. Before anything was missing, if anything was missing.

He laid the items side by side in his mind the way he had once laid slates before a pupil, and waited for the sum to declare itself, and the sum refused. Or rather, the sum declared itself twice, in two different hands, and both totals claimed to be correct.

In one column: the company. Thirty years of warmth in winter, wages in lean springs, order in a lawless world. The company had raised him from a starving moor-boy into a man whose handwriting was trusted. The company had given him the boy's education as a kindness, and the boy's indenture as a contract, and contracts were sacred — were they not? — because without them there was only the moor, only hunger, only the strong eating the weak. If the company required a document returned, then returning it was order, and order was mercy, and mercy was all he had ever meant by his life.

In the other column: a boy of eight asking *whose promise is it when the numbers lie*. A master dying in a tidied room, saying *do the sum*. An order so quiet it had to be quiet, silence wrapped around it like oilpaper around contraband. Honest instruments did not fear light. Whatever this document was, it was feared — and the fear, not the paper, was the true thing being tracked.

Grell blew out his lamp and sat in the dark, and the regulator clock ticked above the empty entry hall with the sound of a patient heart, and he understood, finally, with the mild surprise of a man discovering a wound he has carried for years, that he had loved two things in this world: the order of the company, and the boy who had learned his letters at this desk. And that Ashcombe's order required him to choose between them as though the choice were simple, as though loyalty were a single-entry account.

He thought of the ice road south. Kingsford lay six days' travel in warm weather, less if a man traveled light and desperate, and the spring ice was already talking in the pilings. He thought of the checkpoints — Marrow Bridge, Ferren Gate, the weir — and knew with a clerk's certainty that Ashcombe would already have sent word ahead, descriptions circulated, rewards posted. The boy would be walking into a net drawn tight by men Grell had eaten supper with, worked beside, trusted with his whole adult life.

And he thought — the thought arriving like the first crack across a frozen pond, thin and bright and terrifying — that he could report the boy missing tonight, quietly, and set the net closing early. Or he could lose a day. One day, badly kept books, a search begun at the wrong end of the river road. A day was nothing. A day was everything. He had spent thirty years believing that a false entry anywhere poisoned the whole ledger, and here he sat with a false entry in his hands, weighing it, finding it lighter than he expected, which frightened him more than any heavy thing ever had.

Above him, faintly, through floors and beams, he could hear the house settling around its dying master, and beneath everything, so far down it might have been imagination, the river muttering under its lid of ice.

Grell rose, took his coat from its peg, and went out into the blue dark of Coldharbour to begin his search — south, he decided. Toward Marrow Bridge. Slowly. Thoroughly. Asking careful questions at each hearth, missing nothing, and missing, perhaps, one small diver's son by exactly the length of one well-spent day.

Behind him the regulator clock ticked on, keeping the house's time, and Halvard Grell walked beneath it without looking up — the first time in thirty years he had left that hall without consulting it.
