## 5. Departure Before the Bell

The counting-house kept its dead as it kept its ledgers: neatly, and behind locked doors.

They took Master Verrin down the back stair at moonset, wrapped in sailcloth, four bearers moving with the careful rhythm of men who had done this before and would do it again before winter ended. Mirabel stood in the shadow of the winding-room doorway and watched them pass, and none of them looked at him, and he understood that this too was part of the arrangement. Grief in the Verrin house was a private transaction. The company's business — and a master's death was, above all, business — proceeded on schedule. The bell would ring at six. The clerks would climb to their stools. The great regulator would tick its iron heart above them all, indifferent as weather.

The letter lay against his ribs.

He had felt it there since the night Master Verrin pressed it into his hands with fingers like frozen rope, since the old man's voice had cracked apart mid-sentence and left Mirabel kneeling by the bed with a sealed envelope and no instructions beyond two words that had already begun to feel like a wound: *Kingsford. Quickly.* He had not slept. Sleep, he suspected, would not come again for a long time, and perhaps that was fitting. His father had drowned with his lungs full of river; Mirabel would carry his own water now in a different form, an ocean of it folded into paper and sewn beneath his coat.

The house settled into its small night sounds. A shutter ticking against its frame. The cellar door swelling in the frost. Somewhere below, the last of the watch-coals shifting in their grate. Mirabel waited until the bearers' footsteps faded into the yard, until the house achieved that particular depth of silence that meant even the clerks lodged in the attic were past waking, and then he moved.

He went first to the winding-room, because he could not help himself. The regulator clock stood taller than a man, its case of black river-walnut polished by twenty years of his own cloth, its pendulum swinging in the dark with the patience of something that would still be measuring time when everyone in Coldharbour was dust. He opened the case. He checked the weight. He gave the great wheel its quarter-turn, feeling the resistance come up through his palm like the pulse of some enormous sleeping animal, and he thought: *I have wound you every morning for eleven years, and I never once asked what you were for.*

Time, he understood now. Time was what it was for. Not hours — power. Every minute the bell governed, every worker's arrival it measured and fined and recorded, belonged to the company. Master Verrin had owned the minutes of every soul in Coldharbour, and now the old man was dead, and somewhere under the floor of a room Mirabel had cleaned since he was six years old lay the confession that could unmake all of it.

Or lie there forever, if Mirabel failed.

He closed the case softly, leaving the clock running. It seemed important somehow — a debt to be paid later, or a promise — that the clock should not stop tonight on his account.

His room was a closet off the drying loft, seven feet by nine, containing a straw pallet, a stool, and a window that looked out over the frozen river. He had lived in smaller places. He had lived, in the early years, in the hull of a pearl barge, in a space he could not have sat upright in, listening to water move beneath him and learning the river's moods through the planks. That memory came back to him now with sudden force: the sensation of being *carried* by something vast and indifferent, trusted to it without any say in the matter. That had been his whole life. Indentured at five when his father drowned, bound by papers he had never signed to a company that had ruined the man whose name he bore, useful, punctual, invisible.

Tonight he would find out what he was when none of those things were true.

He worked by the light of a single tallow stub, shielding the flame with his body the way he had learned to shield a lantern during night dives, so that no glow escaped the ice to mark a diver's position. From under the loose floorboard where he kept his few possessions — a knife with a horn handle, three ledger stubs he had rescued from the burn barrel because paper was paper, his father's diving weight, forty ounces of lead worn smooth as soap by another man's hand — he took out needle and thread and the waxed sailcloth scrap he used for patching dive-bags.

The letter he laid on the stool first, and looked at for a long moment.

It was a modest thing. One page, folded thrice, sealed with plain wax — Master Verrin had been too far gone for ceremony, and had pressed the seal with his thumb rather than a signet, so that the wax carried the whorls of a dying man's skin. Mirabel found he could not read it again. He had read it once, by candlelight, with his hands shaking so badly the words swum, and what he had read had rearranged the world: names, dates, sums. Director Ashcombe. Director Pell. Director Verrin himself, confessing his share with the flat precision of a man balancing accounts. And threaded through it all, one sentence that Mirabel had already memorized without wanting to, the way you memorize the shape of a keyhole in the dark:

*Amberdale refused. Amberdale was made an example of. The river did not take him; we did.*

His father had not drowned. His father had been sent down into bad ice with contraband sewn into his dive-bag, and had refused, and had gone down anyway, weighted — Mirabel's throat closed on the thought and he mastered it, the way he had learned to master the first shock of black water, by breathing slowly and doing the next necessary thing.

The next necessary thing was the needle.

He unpicked the lining of his coat along the left seam, the side that lay against his ribs, and folded the letter flat, and slid it home, and began to stitch. He sewed the way his father had spliced line — evenly, tightly, without ornament — and he made the stitches small enough that only a man looking for them would ever find them, and as he worked he spoke to the letter silently, the way divers speak to the river before a descent. *Hold fast. Stay flat. We are going south together, you and I.* The wax seal crackled faintly under the cloth each time he pressed the seam, a tiny sound like a beetle turning over, and each time his heart slammed and he stopped and listened to the house, and the house said nothing.

When it was done he tried the coat on and moved in it — arms up, arms across, a full turn, a bend at the waist, everything a search might ask of a body. The letter rode against his ribs like a second skin. Good. It would have to be good.

Then he took stock, because taking stock was the one discipline the company had given him that he meant to keep.

What can be carried. That had always been the arithmetic of a pearl diver's family — everything weighed, everything counted, nothing owned that could not be abandoned in a breath when the ice groaned its warning. His father had taught him that before the company ever did: *the river takes whatever you cannot run with, boy, so love nothing heavy.* He looked at his possessions spread on the pallet and heard the lesson in his own voice now.

The knife. Yes. Horn handle, honest blade, small enough to hide, sharp enough for cord and kindling both.

The ledger stubs. He hesitated over these. Three scraps of company paper, blank on one side. But paper was currency in another sense — you could write on it, sell it, burn it, eat it if desperation went that far. He took two, left one. No reason. The reason was that taking exactly what he needed and no more was a kind of prayer.

The diving weight. His hand closed around it and stayed there. Forty ounces of lead. Useless on a road — dead mass, the single heaviest thing he owned, the one thing his father's rule forbade. And yet. It had hung from his father's belt in cold black water for fifteen years; it had been pried from the silt by other men's hands and sold back to Mirabel in installments out of his own indenture-wage, and it was the only object on earth that had touched his father and survived. He could not leave it. He knew this the way he knew the depth of water by pressure in his ears — not with reasoning but with his body. He looped its cord through his belt at the small of his back, where it sat against his spine like a cold hand, steadying him.

Everything else — the pallet, the stool, the spare shirt, the tin cup, eleven years of punctual invisible service — he left.

Food was the problem. There was bread in the kitchen, but the kitchen door complained, and Grell —

Grell.

Mirabel sat down slowly on the pallet and made himself think about Halvard Grell, because thinking about Grell was like pressing on a bruise to learn its size, and he needed to know its size tonight more than he needed anything except speed.

Grell had taught him his letters. That was the truth underneath every other truth, and he could not depart from it no matter how it cut. He had been seven years old and feral with grief, biting the under-clerks, refusing the counting-house routines, and Grell — then a young clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a patient voice — had sat with him evening after evening drawing letters in spilled salt on the table. *A is the first stroke of a diver's arm,* Grell had said. *Numbers are just knots, Mirabel. Learn to tie them right and they'll never slip.* Nobody else in the company had ever spoken to him as though he were a person who could be taught rather than a tool that could be sharpened. For eleven years Mirabel had believed that Grell's orderliness was a form of justice — that the ledgers, the bells, the clocks, all of it, was the world trying to be fair in the only way the world knew how.

And now Ashcombe had set that same Grell on the trail of a missing document, and Mirabel had heard him say — had heard his only friend say, in that patient classroom voice — *I'll find it, sir.*

Maybe he would. Maybe Grell would hunt the letter the way he tallied figures, thoroughly and without mercy, and maybe at the end of the hunt he would look at Mirabel and see only a fugitive piece of company property making off with company secrets. Mirabel could not know. He could only know that the man who taught him his letters would also, very likely, be the man sent to drag him back, and that between Coldharbour and Kingsford lay three checkpoints, ninety miles of ice road, and Halvard Grell.

He put the thought away carefully, the way you coil a line that has gone slack — because a slack line is more dangerous than a taut one.

* * *

The last hour before dawn is the coldest, and the river knew it. Frost-smoke rose off the black leads in the current channel and drifted through the town like the breath of something enormous asleep beneath the bridge pilings. Mirabel let himself out of the drying loft by the window, dropping the eight feet into snow that received him without sound, and crossed the yard keeping to the shadow of the woodshed, and the whole time the letter beat against his ribs in time with his heart so that he could not tell which of them was trembling.

The kitchen door, as predicted, complained. He did not use the kitchen door. He went over the wash-house roof instead, along a spine of crusted snow, and dropped beyond it, and came out on the river road through the tanner's gate, which the boys had bent years ago and which no one had bothered to straighten because no one important used it.

Coldharbour at that hour was a town of chimneys. Smoke stood straight up from every one of them in the windless cold, a forest of grey columns against the star-scoured sky, and Mirabel walked through the streets beneath those columns knowing that in one hour the bell would ring and the smoke would bend as the working day began, and every soul in the town would step into their appointed minute, and he would not be among them. The strangeness of it made him dizzy. Eleven years of arriving before the bell, and now —

Now he was walking away from it, and his legs wanted to run and his training held them to a workman's pace, because a man who runs at four in the morning is a message, and a man who walks is nobody.

The ice road began at the town's southern stair, where the pearl barges tied up in season. In deep winter the river froze bank to bank, a mile of white marked out with felled saplings like a highway through open country, trodden and swept by sled-runners into a corridor of blue-grey glass that ran south toward Marrow Bridge and, beyond it, in fits and starts of coach road and moorland and river path, toward Kingsford. He had traveled it twice in his life, both times as company freight, riding in the covered sledges with the pearl casks, counted at every checkpoint like inventory.

He stepped down onto the ice and the ice accepted him with a sound like a struck jar — one low ringing note that ran away south ahead of him under the snow, and he stood listening to it fade and thought absurdly: *it knows I'm here.* Then, less absurdly: *so will everyone, if I don't move.*

He moved.

For the first mile he did not let himself think at all. Thinking was for later, when the body had settled into the rhythm and fear had burned down to its coals. He simply walked, measuring distance the way his father had measured depth — by feel, by the ache in his shoulders, by the changing quality of the light. And the light was changing. The east had gone from black to iron to a deep bruised purple, and the frost-smoke glowed faintly along its length, and somewhere behind him Coldharbour's chimneys began, one by one, to lean and waver as the first thermal stirred, and Mirabel Amberdale walked south on a road made of his own drowned history and felt — for the first time in eleven years, terrifyingly, gloriously — unmeasured.

No bell governed this hour. No clock. No clerk would note his arrival or fine his absence. The minutes he spent on the ice road belonged to no one but the man spending them, and the extravagance of that nearly undid him. He laughed once, a short bark of steam, startled to hear his own voice out here in the middle of nowhere with nobody to hear it. Freedom, it turned out, did not feel the way the stories said. It felt like standing on thin ice and finding that it held — every step a wager, every step a wonder.

By the time the sun cleared the eastern bluffs he was three miles south and the wind had risen, combing the surface snow into long grey streamers that hissed across the ice like dry silk. He ate nothing. There was nothing to eat — a failure of planning he noted grimly, filing it under lessons already learned — and his stomach had shrunk to a hard knot, but the knot was warm compared to his fingers and he flexed them inside his mitts and kept the town of his spine and walked.

At the five-mile stake, where the road passed between pressure ridges reared up like the keels of sunken ships, he stopped and looked back.

Coldharbour was a smudge of smoke-columns on the white, barely distinguishable from cloud-shadow. He could not see the counting-house roof, or the bell-tower, or the great dark rectangle of the regulator's case in the winding-room window. But he could see it anyway, in his mind, perfectly: the pendulum swinging, the works turning, the minutes marching out over the town as they always had, marking a vacancy in the roster where Mirabel Amberdale used to be. Somebody would notice by six. Somebody would check the loft by seven. By eight, if Ashcombe had already whispered in the right ears, the description would be written down — age, height, coat, scar, name — and copies would be riding south faster than any man on foot.

He turned his back on it.

*Quickly*, the old man had said, and quickly meant before the spring ice broke, meant before the river reopened and the company's ships could carry the evidence — no. There was no other evidence. There was only him, and the letter, and ninety miles. He was the evidence now. Everything the directors had buried was walking south on two legs at four miles an hour, and every hour he bought with his feet was an hour they had to spend buying witnesses and burning papers and pretending grief for a dead colleague. He understood suddenly why Master Verrin had chosen him: not for his loyalty, not for his discretion, but because he was the one man alive with nothing left to lose and everything to prove, and such men do not stop.

The wind picked at his hood. The diving weight swung gently at his back, tapping his spine with each stride, patient as a metronome. South, it said. South. South.

He was twelve miles out, with the sun high and the frost-smoke burning off in silver sheets, when he saw the figure on the road behind him.

It was very far away — a dark fleck at the limit of the long white perspective, near enough to the horizon-line of ice that he might have mistaken it for a tree, a post, a sled drawn by a slow horse. Except that it moved the way men move. Except that it did not stop when he stopped, and did not falter when he stumbled on a hidden ridge of ice, and gained — slowly, steadily, with the terrible patience of ink spreading through wet paper — as he watched.

Mirabel did not wait to identify it. Identification was a luxury for men with time to spare. He pulled his hood down, turned off the road at the next sapling-marker, and dropped onto the raw ice of the current channel, where the going was harder and his tracks would fill with wind-driven snow within the hour — and where, he knew, the route toward Marrow Bridge split into three paths, and only a man who had dived these waters since childhood knew which of them was solid to the banks and which would sing warning under a stranger's weight.

Behind him, dark against the snow, the figure reached the five-mile stake and paused. Paused, and looked around, the way a clerk looks up from a ledger when a column refuses to balance.

Mirabel ran.
