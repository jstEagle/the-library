## 6. First Checkpoint: The Toll at Marrow Bridge

The ice road ended at Marrow Bridge, and what began there was something else entirely — a narrowing, a funnel, a throat the whole southbound world had to pass through on its way to Kingsford.

Mirabel saw it from a half-mile off, coming down off the riverbank where the road bent inland around a stand of black spruce. The bridge itself was nothing remarkable: old timber on stone piers, thrown across the frozen Marrow where the water ran fast beneath and never froze solid. What was remarkable was everything gathered at either end of it. Lanterns on poles. A barricade of sledges turned on their sides. And men — company men, in Verrin grey with the pearl-and-cog stamped on their breasts — standing in a line across the width of the road, stopping every traveler, turning out every pack.

He stopped behind the spruce and watched, and his breath went in and out of him very slowly, the way he had been taught to breathe before a dive.

*You do not fight the water,* his father had said, so long ago that Mirabel sometimes wondered if he had invented the memory rather than remembered it. *You learn what it wants. Then you are not fighting anything at all.*

Beneath the ice, in the diving holes, that had been literal instruction. Here on the open road, with a letter sewn into his coat lining riding against his ribs like a second heartbeat, it had become the only instruction that mattered.

He counted. It steadied him, counting — Halvard Grell had taught him arithmetic on counting-house slate before Mirabel could properly hold a slate pencil, and numbers had never once lied to him or left him. Nine men at the southern end. Two walking the bridge itself, boots throwing up little ghosts of frost-dust. One at a plank table with a ledger open before him and a brazier glowing at his knee, and this one wore a clerk's armband over his company grey, which meant he wrote things down, which meant he mattered more than the rest of them put together.

Beyond the checkpoint, on the southern bank, Mirabel could see the bridge town: two rows of houses leaning together like tired drunks, a chapel with a stunted spire, smoke rising from perhaps thirty chimneys. And one smell reaching him even across the distance, even through the cold — baking bread. His stomach answered before his mind did, a low insistent cramp, and he realized with dull surprise that he had not eaten since leaving Coldharbour in the dark. He had carried almost nothing out of that house. Deliberately almost nothing. A knife. His father's diving weight on his belt. The letter. Hunger had simply not seemed like a thing worth planning for.

Now it made itself known, and he let it, because hunger was honest and fear was not, and he wanted only honest things inside him today.

* * *

The trouble with approaching Marrow Bridge was that there was no approach at all. The road came down to the river, the river was crossed by the bridge, and the bridge was held. To go around meant striking east into the high fells for days he did not have, through country where the snow would be drifted to a man's chest and no shelter existed between here and Kingsford. Or west to the falls, where the water never froze and no crossing existed at all.

Or through.

He worked his way forward along the tree line as the morning brightened, gray light thickening toward silver on the eastern hills, and joined the slow accumulation of travelers gathering short of the barricade. A tinker with a handcart that complained at every rut. Two farmers with a sled of turnips going soft with cold. A woman wrapped in so many shawls she seemed less a person than a weather event. They queued and blew on their fingers and asked each other what the searching was about, and nobody knew, and everybody had theories, and Mirabel kept his hood up and his face angled down and listened.

"—say it's smugglers," the tinker was saying. "Say they've found contraband moving down the ice road all winter."

"Contraband," the turnip farmer snorted. "Twenty years I've hauled goods past tolls and never once seen a search like this for contraband. Somebody's lost something. Something they want back bad."

Something they want back bad. Mirabel felt those words land on him like a hand on the shoulder, and had to remind himself that the farmer meant cargo, papers, money — anything but a nineteen-year-old indentured diver's son with frost in the seams of his boots. He was nobody. He was a boy from a drowned father's house. He was invisible by every law of the world except the one Ashcombe's reward was about to write.

Because that was when he saw the paper.

It was nailed to the sledge-barricade at eye height, weighted against the wind by four iron pins, and even at forty yards the printed words were large enough to read. He should have looked away. Some part of him understood that looking was dangerous, that attention drawn to attention was how men got caught, and yet he read it anyway, the way you cannot stop yourself reading your own name chalked on a door.

**NOTICE OF REWARD. By order of Director H. Ashcombe, Verrin Trading Company. Ten pounds sterling to any party providing information leading to the recovery of absconded property—**

Absconded property. His own name, then, three lines down, rendered in the flat language of ledgers: *Mirabel Amberdale, age nineteen, indentured, last seen Coldharbour. Carries documents belonging to the company.* And below the name, worse than the name, worse than the ten pounds — a description. *Slight build. Dark hair cut short. Scar on the left thumb. Walks with the deep-set stance of a diver. Likely dressed poorly and traveling alone.*

Likely dressed poorly and traveling alone. He almost laughed, there among strangers, and swallowed the laugh because laughter kills more fugitives than bloodhounds ever have. They had described him exactly, precisely, with the same loving accuracy Master Verrin brought to a column of figures. Scar on the left thumb — the scar from the oyster shell, from the winter he was twelve. Deep-set stance of a diver. Every detail true. He had spent his whole life being exactly this person, and now being exactly this person was worth ten pounds to every hungry soul between the rivers.

The queue shuffled forward. The searching had begun again at the table — he could see it clearly now: packs opened, coats turned out, seams run through with fingers. A peddler woman protesting mildly as a company man went through her bundles of ribbons with rough thoroughness. They were looking in luggage. In linings.

In linings.

His hand wanted to go to his chest, to press the place where the letter lay sewn flat between the outer cloth and the lining of his coat, and he forced the hand to stay at his side, curled loose, stupid, empty. Fear will tell you to touch the thing you're hiding. Fear is a bad counsel. Breathe the way you breathe under the ice.

And then, cutting through the queue's murmur like a blade through wet rope, a sound he knew better than his own heartbeat.

Dogs.

No — not dogs. Chains. Sled-chains, ringing, and then the dogs themselves rounding the corner of the tollhouse: four of them, heavy-shouldered, gray and white, moving with the deliberate economy of animals that work for a living. Not hunting dogs. Not trackers. These were crowd dogs, fear dogs, beasts kept to make people docile, and Mirabel's heart unclenched by half a beat before clenching again, because docile was not the same as safe. A dog did not need to smell a letter to change a man's plans. A dog needed only to notice that one traveler smelled of terror, and terror was the one scent Mirabel could not wash out of his own skin.

* * *

He did what his father taught him. He learned what the water wanted.

The water wanted a crowd. The searchers were pulling people out of the line one at a time, and a lone slight young man in a poor coat was the easiest kind of pull there was. But a family — a family moved through checkpoints like fish through a net too coarsely woven. Company men grew bored of families. Families were full of crying children and bundled grandmothers and endless tedious possessions, and boredom was a current Mirabel intended to swim with.

And he knew — he suddenly, gloriously knew — who was coming down the ice road behind him.

Every winter, when the shallows iced hard enough to shore, the pearl divers of the upper Marrow moved their families downriver to the deeper beds, following the oysters the way their grandparents had followed them, and every winter they passed this way. He had grown up half a season of each year among these people, in the tent-villages on the ice, learning the languages of rope and breath-hold that all divers speak regardless of blood. Old Wenna Corvane, whose hands were knotted like tree roots and who had once pulled six-year-old Mirabel out of a testing hole by his ankle. Her sons. Her sons' wives. The whole brown, weathered, salt-tough tribe of them, with their sledges of nets and diving irons and their children swarming everywhere like sparrows.

He turned and walked back up the road a quarter mile, and waited at a bend where the trees thinned, and within the hour he heard them coming — the groan of runners, the jingle of harness, the high argumentative music of many children, and beneath it all the deep familiar rhythm of divers talking about water.

Old Wenna saw him before he had fully stepped out. She never missed anything. She sat her lead sledge like a figurehead, wrapped in sealskin, and her eyes — pale as shallow water — went over him once, head to boot, and came back to his face.

"Amberdale's boy," she said. Not a question.

"Mother Corvane."

"You're alone." Again not a question. Behind her, the sledges slowed, the children quieting with the instinct children have for sensing adult weather.

"I'm going south," he said. "To Kingsford."

"Nobody goes to Kingsford in thaw-year for pleasure." She looked past him, down the road, toward the distant barricade and its lanterns, and he watched understanding arrive in her face the way ice forms — gradually, then all at once. "There's a search on."

"There is."

"They're searching for a boy." Her pale eyes came back to his. "Alone. Dressed poorly. With a scar on his left thumb."

The wind moved in the spruce. One of her sons muttered something to another, and a woman drew a child closer against her side.

"You knew," Mirabel said.

"I can read," said Wenna Corvane, with terrible mildness. "My son nailed a copy to the drying-shed door at the camp two nights back. Ten pounds." She spat over the side of the sledge, an act of pure punctuation. "Your father dove my lines for eleven winters. Eleven winters, and never once came up short-weight, never once cut a line to save himself, never once left a partner's hole uncovered. When the company men came asking after him, after — after it happened — I told them what he was. They wrote it down wrong on purpose." She flexed her root-knotted hands. "Get on the sledge, Amberdale's boy. You'll travel as a Corvane hireling today. Boy-of-all-work. You'll carry rope and keep your mouth shut and if anyone asks, your name is Corvane, because that's what happens when you work for Corvanes."

He climbed up beside her, and his eyes were burning, and he blamed the wind.

* * *

The queue received them without interest. That was the first mercy: to a checkpoint, the Corvane flotilla was weather, not news. Six sledges, twenty-some souls, diving irons lashed and rattling, children everywhere, and the company men wading into it with the resigned air of men doing arithmetic they already hated.

The clerk at the table looked up as Wenna drew rein.

"Name?"

"Corvane. Wenna Corvane, diving-master of the upper Marrow lines. Same as I've been called at this bridge every winter since before you were shaving, and I'll thank you to check your own prior entries instead of asking me to spell it."

The clerk checked. There were, indeed, prior entries; Mirabel watched him find them, watched his shoulders lower a quarter inch as the great machinery of routine took his weight off him. Routine. Bless routine, bless boredom, bless the infinite laziness of institutional men.

"And these?" A chin-jerk at the sledges.

"Family, hires, gear. Nets and irons for the deep beds. You want us to unload twenty sledges in the cold, boy, you go ahead and start with that one there, mind the barbed hooks." She nodded at the nearest company man, a callow youth already eyeing the diving irons with dread.

The youth did not want to unload twenty sledges. It was visible on him like a rash. He walked the line instead, lifting corners of oilcloth, prodding net-bundles, and when he came to Mirabel's sledge he looked at Mirabel for perhaps three seconds.

Mirabel gave him back those three seconds wearing the face divers wear for company men: blank, patient, faintly stuporous, a face that expected to be ordered about and had stopped taking it personally years ago. He was carrying a coil of rope, and he shifted it, and he said, in the flattened tone of a hireling, "Mind the splice, sir, she's wet yet."

The youth glanced at the rope, at the splice, at the boy holding it — a boy-of-all-work, dark-haired, slight, exactly as advertised, but buried in the middle of a family, marked as property of Corvane, uninteresting, accounted for. His gaze slid off. He moved to the next sledge.

Somewhere behind Mirabel's sternum, the letter pressed warm against his beating heart, and he breathed the way his father had taught him, long and slow, on the bottom of the world.

The clerk wrote something. Wenna paid the toll in coin counted out knuckle by knuckle from a purse that had clearly felt thinner other winters. And then, with a jerk and a groan of runners, the whole flotilla rolled forward onto Marrow Bridge, over the black running water, past the lanterns and the notice with ten pounds on it, and down the southern road, and no one shouted, and no bell rang.

Only when the bridge was half a mile behind them did Wenna speak again, quietly, without turning her head.

"There's bread and cheese in the box at your feet. Eat slow. Fasting men make mistakes."

He ate, and the bread was dense and good and tasted like kindness, and he did not trust it, because kindness was now a currency someone might pay ten pounds to betray.

* * *

They parted ways at the fork above the bridge town, where the divers' route bent east toward the deep beds and the south road ran on alone. Wenna pressed a second loaf into his hands and refused thanks with a scowl.

"One thing," she said, as he turned to go. "The notice. It says 'documents belonging to the company.' Papers, boy. They're searching linings at Ferren Gate, my son heard it from a carter — and they keep dogs there. Dogs trained on ink and paper."

*Dogs trained on ink and paper.* The words went into him like a splinter driven home with one clean blow. He stood at the fork with the loaf in his hands, watching the Corvane sledges shrink eastward, and understood that Marrow Bridge had not been the test. Marrow Bridge had merely shown him the shape of the trap so that he would know it again.

Behind him, the town smoked gently in the noon light. Ahead, the road ran white and empty toward the fells and Ferren Gate, and somewhere on that road he would have to solve a problem no amount of hiding among families could touch: how to carry a thing whose scent betrayed it.

He thought of the tannery at the edge of the bridge town, its reek drifting on the wind all morning, foul enough to make the children wrinkle their noses even from the road.

Fear sharpened, and became cunning, and Mirabel Amberdale turned back toward the stink.
