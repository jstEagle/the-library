## 7. The Girl Who Sold Warm Bread

The ovens woke before I did, the way they always did, breathing heat through the floorboards into my room until the cold gave up its argument and left. By the time I came down the ladder, my father had already shaped the first hundred loaves, his hands white to the wrist with flour, his back a question mark bent over the trough.

"You're late," he said, without looking up.

"The fire was late."

"The fire is never late. You are." But there was no edge in it. There never was, not since Tam. We had both learned that sharpness was a luxury for households that still had two children in them.

I tied on my apron and took up the scoring knife. Outside, Marrow Bridge lay under a skin of frost so fine it looked like sugar sifted over the rooftops. The river ran black beneath the bridge itself, only here — this far south, this close to the sea's pull — the ice had failed to hold all winter, and the water moved with a low continuous mutter, like an old man complaining in his sleep. The company men had built their toll-house at the bridge's northern end, a squat timber box with a stove chimney, and every traveler coming down from Coldharbour country passed beneath their eyes before they touched our cobbles.

We sold them bread. That was our part in the great machinery of the Verrin Trading Company: we fed the inspectors, the toll-keepers, the drivers who hauled pearl and tin and whatever else the company moved that never appeared in any ledger I'd seen. My father baked; I sold; the company took its tithe of everything, including, three years ago, my brother.

I did not think about Tam in the mornings. Mornings were for work. But some mornings the thinking came anyway, uninvited, the way damp comes through stone.

* * *

He came into the shop just after the church bell rang seven, and I knew him before he reached the counter.

Not from his face — though the face was familiar too, older and hungrier than the boy I remembered from the company school days when Father sent me downriver to stay with my aunt one winter. I knew him by his hands. He held them wrong for a customer, tucked against his sides like something he was ashamed of, and he looked at the bread the way poor men look at bread: not with appetite but with arithmetic. He was counting coins in his head, and I could see the count coming up short.

"Loaf," he said. His voice was roughened, river-country vowels, the Coldharbour burr underneath. "Whatever's cheapest."

"That's the barley." I pulled one from the rack. It was still warm enough that the crust crackled when I wrapped it. "A penny."

He set coins on the counter, and I saw then what I had already half-known, the way you know weather before it arrives. His fingers were diver's fingers — scarred at the knuckles, the nails bluish at the quick, the skin of his palms ridged where rope had worn grooves into them year after year. And on the coin he laid down last, half-hidden under the others, was a stamp I knew better than my own name. A pearl in an open shell. The Verrin company scrip that only indentured workers carried, redeemable only at company stores, worth less than the paper it was printed on everywhere else in the world.

Nobody paid with scrip unless they were running.

I should have kept my eyes down. That was the rule of the bridge town, unwritten but iron: see nothing, say nothing, sell your bread, keep your family fed. The company men drank at the Anchor across the lane and tipped generously to anyone who talked, and Mother's medicine cost four shillings a month, and Tam had taught us all exactly what happened to families who made themselves inconvenient.

But his hands were on my counter, and they were Mirabel Amberdale's hands.

I had known him slightly, that winter at my aunt's. He'd been eleven or twelve, solemn as a minister, forever carrying things — buckets, coils of line, his father's diving weight, which he lugged around like other boys carried dogs. My aunt had said once, laughing, *that child will break his back being useful*, and Mirabel had looked at her with those flat grey eyes and said, *it's not heavy if you carry it right.* Even then he'd had that quality, the terrible competence of children raised to be property. The divers' sons all had it. They were taught early that their bodies belonged to the company's ledgers, and some of them — most of them — went quiet inside to survive it.

His father had drowned the summer after. I remembered that too, because my aunt had wept, and because it was the first time I understood that the river didn't only take people by accident.

Now the son stood at my counter with company scrip in his palm and fear coming off him like steam off a fresh loaf, and behind him, through the window, two of Ashcombe's riders sat their horses at the toll-house with the loose-legged patience of men being paid to wait.

"A penny," he said again, when I hadn't moved.

I swept the coins into the drawer without counting them. "You'll want more than one loaf," I heard myself say. "Walking south. It's two days to Ferren Gate even if the road's clear, and the road's not clear."

Something flickered in his face — not gratitude, not yet. Calculation. He was deciding how much I knew, whether I was kindness or trap. I had seen that look before, on Tam's face, in the last month before the company men came for him. Debt does that. It teaches a person that every open hand might be a closing one.

"I can pay for one," he said carefully.

"And I can give away what I like. It's my father's shop, but it's my morning's baking." I reached past him and set three more loaves in a cloth — the dark rye, which keeps, and a seed loaf, and the end of yesterday's honey bread, which was my own favorite and which I told myself I wasn't giving him out of anything but practicality. "Sit by the oven a moment. Your coat's wet through and you'll take chill. Nobody buys bread off a shivering girl's counter faster than off a warm one, and I've a reputation."

It wasn't true. Nobody cared. But he sat, because his body wanted the heat more than his pride wanted to refuse it, and I turned back to the counter and began scoring the morning's second batch, and let him sit in the warmth of my family's fire while I decided what kind of coward I was going to be today.

* * *

Tam owed the company nine pounds, fourteen shillings, and sixpence.

I have done that sum ten thousand times. I will do it ten thousand more. Nine pounds, fourteen and six — that was what my brother's life was entered at in Director Ashcombe's books, the price of a boat he'd co-signed for a friend, plus interest compounded at rates no honest lender would dare print. When he couldn't pay, they didn't take the boat. They took him. Indenture for term of debt, the papers said, seven years' service aboard the company barge *Vigilant*, and my mother signed the consent because they explained to her, very patiently, that refusal would convert the debt to fraud, and fraud meant the assizes, and the assizes meant transportation.

He wrote twice the first year. Short letters, cheerful, full of descriptions of ports he'd seen. Then nothing. When Father petitioned for news, the clerk — a different clerk then, not Grell, though I've wondered since — looked up the entry, ran his finger down the column, and said, *transferred, account closed,* as if Tam were a shipment of tin.

Account closed. My brother was nineteen years old and somewhere on the water, and the company had closed his account.

So understand me: I knew what saying nothing was worth. I had practiced it for three years. I had watched company men search travelers at the toll-house while I sold them rolls, and I had smiled, and I had said nothing, and every silence had felt like a small stone added to the cairn of stones I was building over my brother's memory — useless, heavy, mine.

And now here was a boy the whole bridge town was whispering about. The reward notice had gone up on the toll-house door two days past, and I had read it while pretending to adjust the awning: *one Mirabel Amberdale, indentured diver's son, seventeen years, absconded with documents belonging to the Verrin Trading Company. Reward of five pounds for information leading to recovery.* Five pounds. More than half of what Tam had died owing. They had priced this boy too, and the price was generous, because documents are worth more than bodies in Ashcombe's arithmetic.

He was sitting at my oven with flour dust settling in the light around him, eating the honey bread in small controlled bites like a man rationing joy, and I thought: five pounds would buy Mother's medicine through summer. Five pounds would buy Father a new kneading trough and me a coat that didn't leak. All I had to do was finish selling him his loaves, walk forty feet across the lane, and speak two sentences to the men in the toll-house.

I scored a cross into the top of a loaf and pressed deeper than I needed to.

"Why south?" I asked him. I hadn't meant to ask. The question came out of me the way sap comes out of cut wood — because something inside had been severed.

He looked up. For a moment I thought he wouldn't answer, and I wouldn't have blamed him. Then he said, "Kingsford," and nothing else, and the word itself was answer enough. Everyone on this road was going somewhere, but nobody went to Kingsford except to hand something to the magistrate. That was the one direction the company feared.

"You know there's a notice," I said quietly. "With your name on it. On the toll-house door."

"I know."

"They're not searching hard yet. Not like they will be." I set down the knife. Through the window, the riders shifted; one dismounted and went inside to the stove, and the other walked his horse along the queue of carts waiting for inspection, leaning down to peer under canvas, into faces. "But listen to me. Ferren Gate — the next checkpoint, two days south — it's not like this one. Here they look at faces. There—" I stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, made me a conspirator, made me the sort of person whose family pays for her principles. I said it anyway. "There they have dogs. Trained on paper and ink. Company ink. Whatever you're carrying, it smells of the counting-house, and the dogs will find it no matter where you hide it on your body."

His face went very still. Not blank — I want to be clear about that, because people mistake his stillness for emptiness and it isn't, it's the opposite, it's everything packed down tight. His eyes moved, quick, doing sums. I watched him arrive at the same conclusion I had: that the letter, if letter it was, was sewn into his coat, and that a dog would find it in a heartbeat, and that he had perhaps two days to solve a problem that had no solution he could see.

"There's a tanner," I said. "Halfway to Ferren, the village of Dunmore. Ossler. He owes my father for bread going back years and has never once complained about the price." I tore a scrap of paper from the order book and wrote the name, pressing hard. "Tell him Sela sent you. Tell him he still owes me for the wedding loaves he never paid for, and tell him I'm calling the debt. He'll grumble. Pay him no mind. He'll do it."

"Do what?" Mirabel asked. And then, understanding dawning: "Mask the scent?"

"Tanneries stink of lime and dung and blood. A day in Ossler's yard, sleeping among the hides, and you'll smell like nothing on God's earth wants to find you. The dogs will hate you, which is the point." I folded the paper and pushed it across the counter toward him. "That's all I know. That's all I'm saying."

He took the paper. Our fingers didn't touch, but nearly, and I saw his hands properly then, up close — the rope-grooves, the old scar across the left thumb where a shell must have caught him, the way the fingertips were flattened and pale from cold water. Diver's hands. His father's hands. Hands that had been spent, coin by coin, into the company's accounts since he was old enough to hold a breath.

"Why?" he said.

It was a fair question and an awful one. Why does anyone risk anything for anyone? I could have lied. I could have said it was Christian charity, or that I liked his face, or that I gave every hungry traveler extra bread, which was false and would have been an insult to us both.

"My brother rode a company barge out of this town three years ago," I said, "and the company closed his account, and I don't know if he's alive. Somebody has to be on the side of the people the ledgers eat. Today it's me. That's all."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way men nod over a contract they intend to honor, and I understood that I had just been entered into something — not a ledger, nothing so clean — but a debt between the two of us that neither of us would ever speak of again.

He stood, gathered his loaves, and paused at the door.

"The bread was good," he said. It was such a small thing to say, such an ordinary courtesy, that it undid me more than thanks would have. Then the bell over the door chimed, and he was gone into the frost-bright street, shoulders hunched, walking not fast and not slow, a boy trying to be invisible in broad daylight.

* * *

I sold bread for the rest of the morning. I smiled at the toll-men when they came over for their rolls and hot cider, and one of them, a fat good-humored fellow named Brack, complained about the cold and tipped me a farthing, and I thanked him, and my hands did not shake, and I hated myself a little for how easily the smiling came.

At noon my father came up from the ovens, floury and squinting, and stood beside me at the counter surveying the thin traffic.

"You gave away four loaves this morning," he said.

"Three."

"I counted four. The honey loaf's gone, and I know you didn't sell it, because you hide that one from customers." He wasn't angry. He was watching me with the tired, patient attention of a man who has already lost one child to the world's cruelty and is measuring the distance between his remaining child and the same cliff.

"He was hungry," I said.

"They're all hungry."

"This one was Mirabel Amberdale."

My father went still. Flour drifted in the oven-light between us. Down the lane, the toll-house door opened and shut; voices rose and fell; the river muttered under the bridge, patient as ever, taking everything eventually.

"Sela," he said at last, very quietly, "there are men across that lane who would burn this shop down around us for five pounds."

"I know."

"And you sent him—"

"I sent him nowhere. I sold him bread." I met my father's eyes, and I saw there not fear exactly, but grief — the particular grief of parents who recognize their own stubbornness in their children and know it cannot be argued with, only survived. He exhaled through his nose, long and slow, the way he did when a loaf came out wrong and couldn't be saved.

"Your mother's medicine comes Thursday," he said.

"I know that too."

He nodded and went back down the ladder to the ovens, and that was the whole of it. That was my inheritance: a father who disagreed with everything I'd done and baked the afternoon batch anyway, because the world did not stop for our choices, and neither did the bread.

* * *

Evening came down blue and bitter over Marrow Bridge. I banked the ovens, swept the floor, and stood a while at the window watching the lamplighter make his rounds and the toll-house chimney pour its straight black line into the darkening sky.

They would find him or they wouldn't. The dogs at Ferren Gate would smell him or they wouldn't. Ossler would shelter him or turn him in, and I would never know which, and I would lie awake wondering, and the wondering would change nothing, and I would get up before dawn anyway and feed the fire and shape the loaves and sell them to the men who served the machine that ate my brother.

But tonight, for the first time in three years, the silence I kept felt less like a stone and more like a seed. Small. Buried. Possibly nothing.

Down the lane, a lantern bobbed — one of the toll-men, walking his rounds early, stopping at each doorway. When he reached ours, he knocked, and my heart went to ice before I even opened the door, and it was only Brack, stamping his feet, holding out a folded paper.

"New notice from the north," he said cheerfully. "Wanted man's been sighted on the moor road, they think. Ashcombe's sending his own clerk down tomorrow — Grell, some such name. Head of the search now, personal like. Means they're serious." He grinned. "Means more custom for your father, eh? Men on the road need feeding."

I took the paper. I thanked him. I closed the door and stood with my back against it in the warm dark of the empty shop, reading by the banked glow of the ovens the words *Halvard Grell, company clerk, empowered to search and seize*, and understanding, with a certainty that settled into my bones like cold, that the gentlest man in this story had just been handed a wolf's commission — and that whatever kindness I had planted that morning was about to be tested by weather far worse than dogs.
