## 8. Ashcombe's Arithmetic

The counting-room of Director Ashcombe kept its own hours, and they were not the hours of the clock downstairs.

He had never trusted the great regulator in the hall of the Coldharbour house — that brass-pendulum monument to Master Verrin's love of order — because a clock tells you what time it is, and Ashcombe had no interest in what time it was. He was interested in what time it cost. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of his career.

So he worked by lamplight at a desk imported from Kingsford at ruinous expense, its surface sanded so fine that paper slid across it like a fish, and he worked with three ledgers open before him and a fourth closed under his left hand, where he could feel its weight the way other men feel a pistol.

The first ledger was the company's. It was a fiction, beautifully kept, and he admired it the way a man admires a well-made lock: for what it keeps out.

The second ledger was also the company's, but true, and it lived in a strongroom beneath the floor of his own private chamber, behind a door that did not officially exist. It recorded the contraband runs under the pearl manifests, the divers who had been sent down into black water to lift crates that were not pearls, the sums paid to officials whose names would make a bishop weep. It recorded, on one page, in Ashcombe's own small dry hand, an entry from nine years ago: *Amberdale, T. — diver — refused — resolved.*

Four words. Nine years later, four words had begun to cost him money.

The third ledger was the newest, and it was the only one that mattered tonight. In it, since the old man's death and the discovery of the missing document, Ashcombe had begun to keep a different kind of account. Not goods. Not sums. Probabilities.

He dipped his pen and wrote, in his neat clerk's script — he had been a clerk once, before he was the thing clerks reported to — the evening's entries.

*Item: One letter, sealed, in the hand of the late Master Verrin, addressed to the magistrate at Kingsford. Contents presumed: full confession of the winter routes, names of directors, the Amberdale resolution. Value if delivered: incalculable. Probability of delivery if unopposed: certain. The old man wrote it because he was dying, and dying men develop scruples the way rotting fruit develops flies.*

He paused over that last line, considered it, and let it stand. It was unkind, but unkindness was only accuracy spoken plainly, and accuracy was the one devotion he had never broken faith with.

*Item: One boy. Mirabel Amberdale, indentured, nineteen years of age or thereabouts. Father drowned by company arrangement; son bound to the company that arranged it. Skills: can hold his breath longer than any man on the river, reads and writes above his station — Grell's doing — knows the ice road to Kingsford, having walked supply twice. Flaws: honest, which is to say predictable; young, which is to say slow; known, which is to say findable.*

Ashcombe sat back and looked at the candle. It burned very straight. Everything in this room burned straight; he allowed no draughts, no waste, no wandering of flame any more than of ledger-line.

The scandal itself he could price. That was the first thing people never understood about him, the thing that made magistrates uneasy and directors grateful: he was not a cruel man, and he was not a violent one, and he had ordered remarkably little blood in his life. Blood was expensive. Blood made widows, and widows made stories, and stories made juries. What Ashcombe dealt in was silence, and silence had a market rate like anything else.

A trial at Kingsford, if the letter arrived: depositions, subpoenas, the seizure of assets, the hanging — no, the companies had outlived hanging now; it would be dissolution, fines, the quiet transfer of everything to other hands wearing other faces. His own name would survive in the margin of someone else's history, spelled wrong. Cost to the concern: perhaps forty thousand pounds, all told, counting the bribes already sunk like ballast in the river mud. Cost to Ashcombe personally: everything, which is a figure bookkeeping does not have a column for, and so he gave it a column anyway, and entered it as *total*.

Murder, on the other hand —

He turned back a page and reviewed the figures honestly, because a dishonest accountant is worse than a thief; a thief steals from you, but a liar steals from arithmetic itself, and arithmetic was the only god Ashcombe had ever served without reservation.

One boy, dead on the ice road between here and Kingsford, in a season when men died on the ice road every week. A fall through pressure-cracked ice. Exposure on the moor — there had been a blizzard two nights past; the reports said whiteout from Marrow as far as Ferren Gate. A drowning at the weir, come the melt. Any of these would be entered in the parish books as what it appeared to be: God's weather, God's river, God's will. No inquest follows a frozen boy. Nobody audits the cold.

Cost of murder, then: nothing. Less than nothing. The river would do it gratis and sign its own name to the work.

That was the horror of it, if he had permitted himself horror, and he did not: the horror was how cheap it was. The scandal cost forty thousand pounds and the truth cost a boy's life and the difference between those two figures was the entire architecture of the world he moved in, and had always moved in, and every man of consequence he had ever dined with stood upon the same floor and heard the same numbers and slept soundly, and why should they not? He had never once done a wicked thing for pleasure. Every terrible entry in the second ledger had been made to protect wages, contracts, the livelihoods of four hundred souls in Coldharbour who owed their bread to the company's continuity. If the concern fell, four hundred families fell with it, and the boy's father would still be dead, and the letter would still be true, and truth has never fed anyone. Weigh it properly: weighed properly, the arithmetic was not even difficult.

He wrote: *Resolution: the document must not reach Kingsford before the spring sitting. Method: any. Expenditure authorized: as required. Note: prefer methods requiring no expenditure.*

"As required" was as far as he ever went toward saying *kill him*, even alone, even in a ledger no eyes but his would read. A man should be able to show his accounts to God, if God existed, and defend every line. He believed he could defend every line. He had rehearsed the defense for thirty years and found no flaw in it, which was itself, perhaps, the flaw, but there was no column for that either.

He blotted the page and rang the small bell at his elbow — once, softly; the servant class understood gradations of bell the way musicians understand notation — and waited.

What came instead of a servant was a knock he recognized, and did not want.

"Enter."

Halvard Grell filled the doorway the way he always did, which was to say reluctantly, as though doorways were a formality he had never quite made peace with. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, gray at the temples, with ink-stained fingers and the particular stoop of a man who had spent twenty years being careful with other people's property. He had been careful with Ashcombe's property for nineteen of those years, and Ashcombe trusted him precisely, in the way one trusts a bridge one has inspected annually: completely, and without affection.

"You rang, sir."

"I did." Ashcombe did not invite him to sit. Grell would not have accepted. "You have read the warrant I had drafted."

"I have." Grell's voice was level. Twenty years of level. "Search and seizure of a runaway indentured man and the recovery of stolen company documents. It's broadly worded."

"It is worded the way a net is worded. Do you object?"

Grell was quiet long enough that the candle leaned and recovered. "No, sir. I'm asking what the document is. You've told me to recover it. You haven't told me what I'm recovering."

"And you imagine that knowing would help you recover it?"

"No, sir. I imagine it would help me know what I'm working for."

There it was. Ashcombe regarded him with something that in a less disciplined face might have been called interest. Nineteen years, and the man still asked questions in the shape of loyalty — *tell me what we are protecting* — as though the company were a household and Ashcombe its anxious father, rather than what it was, which was a machine for moving value across distances, including the distance between what is and what is admitted.

"The document," Ashcombe said, "is the dying fantasy of an old man whose mind went soft in the same season as his body. It names men in connection with matters they were never connected to. If it reaches Kingsford, reputations are injured, contracts are questioned, and four hundred people in this town spend a hungry decade learning why gossip is heavier than cargo." He folded his hands. "You taught the boy his letters, I'm told."

Something crossed Grell's face — quick, deep-water, gone. "Someone had to, sir. He was diving at eleven. Reading seemed the more useful skill by then."

"Was it? Look what reading has bought him." Ashcombe rose and went to the window. Below, Coldharbour lay blue-white under the last of the moon, chimneys smoking, the river a pale scar of packed ice running south toward everything that mattered. "Here is what I want you to understand, Grell, and then you may go and catch your coach. I am not sending you because you are my best man. My best men are riders and they are already on the road — three of them, paid by the day, answerable to no one, and if the boy simply must be stopped they will stop him. I am sending you because of all the people in the world who might stand on the ice between Mirabel Amberdale and Kingsford, you are the only one he will not run from."

"Sir—"

"He will hide from my riders until the thaw takes the choice away from everyone. He will not hide from you. He watched you teach him sums with the patience of a saint and the handwriting of a drunk archbishop—" Ashcombe had seen samples; the memory was faintly comic, and he permitted himself half a smile, the kind that stopped at the teeth. "He trusts you. Trust is the one instrument in all this world that cannot be purchased, forged, or counterfeited, and I am sending you out with the only genuine article in the province. Bring back the paper. Bring back the boy if it costs nothing extra. Above all, bring back certainty — I want to see the seal broken in front of me, and I want to see it burned."

"And if he won't give it up?" Grell asked quietly. "If he's past Ferren by the time I—"

"Then you will have learned what I learned an hour ago, which is that the melt has come early. The ice road has perhaps ten days left in it, and after that there is no crossing at the weir and no road worth the name until the floods go down — weeks, Grell. Weeks past the spring sitting." Ashcombe turned from the window, and for a moment let his voice drop into the register he used for figures rather than for people. "Nature herself is on the company's side this season. All I require of you is that you not be the exception to her."

Grell stood very still. Ashcombe watched him the way he watched a column refuse to sum: patiently, attentively, with the conviction that the error was there, somewhere, waiting to be found.

"Yes, sir," Grell said at last.

"One more thing." Ashcombe came back to the desk, took up the fourth ledger — the closed one, the heavy one — and set it aside without opening it. "The reward posted at the checkpoints describes the boy. Fifty pounds. See that it is increased to two hundred in your name — your name, Grell, so that every hired eye between here and Kingsford understands the recovery is personal to you, and reports to you, and to no rider wearing my colors. I want this handled quietly, and quiet things need one master, not three."

"That's a large sum to put on a boy you say stole paper."

"It is a small sum to put on forty thousand pounds of exposure. Proportion, Grell. It is the beginning and end of every virtue I have ever practiced." He drew out a purse — company coin, drawn against an account that did not exist — and held it out. "Expenses. The coach leaves for Marrow Bridge at five. You'll overtake the riders by Ferren Gate."

Grell took the purse. Their fingers did not touch; Grell saw to that, with a delicacy Ashcombe noted and filed. Nineteen years, and the man still flinched from contact the way a ledger flinched from a smudge.

"You said 'if it costs nothing extra,'" Grell said, at the door. "About bringing the boy back. I'd like to hear the rest of that sentence, sir."

Ashcombe looked at him across the lamplit desk, at this good, careful, ink-fingered man who had spent two decades mistaking employment for innocence, and told him the truth, because the truth was free and therefore always in budget.

"There is no rest of that sentence. There is the letter, and there is everything else, and everything else is weather. Good night, Grell. Five o'clock comes early, even for men who don't sleep."

The door closed. The candle burned straight.

Ashcombe sat alone for a while with his three ledgers and his lamp, and did what he always did at the close of an entry: he read back over the page, checking each figure against the world it described. One letter. One boy. Ten days of ice. Three riders, one clerk, two hundred pounds of incentive, and a river rising under all of it like a creditor growing impatient.

It balanced. Of course it balanced. It had balanced nine years ago, on a night much like this, when a diver named Amberdale had stood in this same room — Ashcombe remembered the smell of river water on him, the stubborn set of a man holding his ground on solid ground for once — and refused, and been weighed, and found affordable. The entry had taken four words then. It would take fewer now. The boy, unlike the father, would never even reach the room.

He opened the third ledger and made the final notation of the night:

*Item: One boy, in transit. Status: expense pending. Classification: weather.*

Then he snuffed the candle himself — he trusted no servant with flame — and stood in the dark listening to the far-off river, groaning in its sleep, talking in its slow cracking voice about everything that was coming loose.

At five o'clock, Halvard Grell boarded the southbound coach with a purse of company coin, a wolf's commission folded in his breast pocket, and a face that gave up nothing — and Ashcombe, watching from the counting-house window as the lamps swung away down the ice road, felt the mild satisfaction of a man who has posted every figure and closed every book, unaware that somewhere ahead of his clerk, walking south through the white silence with a dead man's letter sewn against his heart, the one variable he had never learned to price was already awake, and cold, and free.
