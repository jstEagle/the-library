## 9. The White Silence

The moor began where the road ended, and Mirabel understood too late that this was not the same thing as a beginning.

He had left the coach route at a place called Harrow's Cross — a leaning stone, a dead fire, a signpost with its arm rotted off so that it pointed nowhere and everywhere at once. The coach would take two more days winding through the valley towns, stopping at gates where men with ledgers looked at faces. The moor was empty. The moor asked no questions. On the map Grell had once drawn for him, in another life, in a clerk's neat hand he now could not stop seeing, the moor was crossed by a single dotted line and annotated in that careful schoolroom script: *short but unkind. Do not attempt in bad weather.*

The weather had been kind for three days. Mirabel had begun to believe it would continue.

The sky over Coldharbour had always been a ceiling — low, grey, pressed down by the weight of the fells on either side of the river. Here there was no ceiling. The sky went up and up into a white that was not cloud and not light but some third thing, and the land went out and out beneath it, brown and rust and old gold, cut by black gashes of stream, dotted with sheep that stood like thoughts someone had forgotten to finish. He walked with the wind quartering behind him and made good miles, and by noon he could no longer see Harrow's Cross behind him or anything at all ahead, and this, he thought, was freedom: to be small in an enormous place, accountable to nothing.

By two o'clock the wind had come around against him.

It came the way cold water comes at depth — not all at once but as a pressure, a slow tightening against the chest, and then it found every seam in his coat and every gap at his wrists and began patiently, methodically, to take him apart. The white thing in the sky thickened. The temperature fell through layers, each one colder than the last and each one feeling, impossibly, like the last one. And then the snow came — not falling but flying, horizontal, hard as shot, and the moor vanished.

Mirabel had been under ice many times. He knew what it was to have the world reduced to a hand's reach of visibility, to work by memory and count and faith while the dark pressed in. But under the ice there was always the hole above him, the one bright circle, the rope around his waist held by someone who wanted him back. Here there was no circle. There was no rope. There was only white going white, and the wind speaking in a voice with no words in it, vast and indifferent as water.

He counted his steps because counting was what his father had taught him. *A man in the dark is a man with numbers,* Tam Amberdale used to say, oiling the diving line in their one room while the river muttered under its lid of ice. *Count your strokes down, count your breaths, count your pulls on the line. A man with numbers has somewhere to be.* So Mirabel counted. Three hundred steps. Six hundred. At nine hundred his right foot went through a hidden drain into freezing water to the knee, and he hauled himself out and kept counting, and somewhere past twelve hundred the numbers stopped meaning steps and became only a rope he was paying out behind himself into the dark, a way of measuring how far he was from being saved.

The bothy appeared the way things appear in dreams — not approached but simply arrived at, a low black shape resolving out of the roar. It was hardly more than a stone tent, dry-stone walls, a roof of turves sagging on old beams, built for shepherds caught exactly as he was caught. The door was a plank on leather hinges. He put his shoulder to it because his hands no longer worked well enough to push.

Inside, the silence hit him harder than the storm.

It was the silence of deep water. After an hour of wind screaming, the sudden deadness of the bothy seemed less like quiet than like pressure — the whole weight of the white world pressing down on the little stone shell, patient, wanting in. Mirabel got the door shut and braced it with the bundle of sticks that lay inside, and then he stood in the dark breathing, and listened to his own body the way he listened to the river: for cracks.

* * *

There was old peat stacked in the corner and a hearthstone black with the fires of men fifty years dead. His hands were beyond him now — they moved like tools belonging to someone else, clumsy, wooden, striking the flint against the knife-back while sparks died on the tinder and his fingers refused to close. He could not say, afterward, how long it took. He only knew that at some point a flame caught, and the bothy filled with a wobbling amber light, and he understood with a strange distant clarity that he had very nearly died six feet from salvation because he could not make his fingers obey.

He stripped his boot from his frozen leg — the pain of it came later, and when it came it came like a bell struck inside his skull — and he wrapped his foot in the spare shirt and held it toward the fire, and he wept without quite knowing it, tears freezing in the creases of his collar, until exhaustion took him sideways onto the stone floor with his coat still buttoned and the letter still sewn flat against his ribs.

That was where his father found him.

* * *

Tam Amberdale sat cross-legged on the far side of the fire, exactly as he had sat a thousand evenings, in the room above the harbor at Coldharbour, turning a pearl between finger and thumb and holding it up to the lamp so the light could get inside it. He was wet. He was always wet in Mirabel's memories of him — river-water beaded on his brows, his hair plastered flat, the skin of his hands white and ridged from long immersion — and here, in a shepherd's bothy on a burning moor, the water ran off him and did not dry, pooling under his crossed legs, though the stones stayed dark.

"You're holding your foot wrong," his father said. "Closer to the flame. Warmth is patience, boy. You were always the impatient one."

"I'm dying," Mirabel said. It seemed important to state it plainly, the way you stated things to company men. "I think I'm dying, Da."

"Everyone's dying." His father turned the pearl. Light swam in it, green and rose. "Some slower than others. Some slower than they'd like." He looked up, and his eyes were the eyes Mirabel remembered from the one time he had seen his father truly frightened — not underwater, never underwater; his father had been more at home below the ice than above it — but the day the Verrin men came to the door with papers. "You've got it with you? The letter?"

"I'm not talking to you," Mirabel said aloud, to the empty bothy, and the wind outside agreed with him, hurling itself against the walls. "You're dead. I know you're dead. I pulled your weight out of the river myself."

"You did," his father said, pleased, as if Mirabel had done sums correctly. "Nine pound of lead. Good lead. It's on your belt now, I see. Heavy, isn't it?"

"It keeps me steady."

"Heavy isn't steady." Tam Amberdale shook his head slowly, water flicking from his hair. "Heavy is heavy. Steady is something else. Steady is knowing why you carry it." He set the pearl down on the stones between them, and it did not roll; it sat there glowing faintly, which pearls do not do, and Mirabel looked at it and felt the truth of his own condition settle over him like snow. Fever. This was fever, the fire working on a frozen man, cooking his mind in its own heat. Knowing did not help. Knowing only let him watch it happen.

"Why are you here?" he asked. "Why now? I've called for you a hundred nights. I've dived your dive, I've carried your name through three towns of people who'd sell me for the reward on it, and you never came. One blizzard and here you sit."

His father considered this, the way he had considered everything, slowly and from underneath, like a man checking the bottom before committing his weight.

"Because tonight," he said, "you're going to ask yourself whether it was worth it. And I wanted to be here when you did. Because I can't answer it for you. No one answers it for anyone. That's the whole trouble with the world."

"I wasn't going to ask that."

"You were going to ask it in about an hour," his father said, "when the fire gets low and the cold comes back and you start doing arithmetic. You're your mother's son for arithmetic. You'll lie there and you'll count it out. The letter against your life. My name against your years. And you'll wonder if a dead man's good name is worth a living boy's warm blood, and you'll want me to tell you yes, so the sum comes out right." He leaned forward, and the light moved in his drowned face. "I won't tell you yes."

* * *

The fire got low. The cold came back. And Mirabel, shivering in waves now, curled on the stone with his thawed foot throbbing like a second heart, did exactly the arithmetic his father had promised.

Because here was the ledger as he could count it. Master Verrin, dying, had put a letter in his hands and died before explaining half of what it meant — died leaving him a burden shaped like a duty and no way to know if the shape was true. Halvard Grell — *Halvard*, who had taught him letters, who had shown him how the great regulator worked, who had bought him honey-bread with his own wages the winter his mother went — was hunting him across the frozen country with Ashcombe's orders in his pocket, and every mile Mirabel walked made that betrayal wider, or made it shallower; he did not know which; he could not bear to find out which. Two checkpoints behind him. One ahead, at the weir, where the company would be thickest. And Kingsford a shape on the map, a promise made of ink, worth precisely nothing until he stood in front of a magistrate and produced a document that might, for all he knew, name his father wrongly; might be a dying man's madness; might be anything at all, because Mirabel had not dared break the seal and read it, because reading it would make it his.

And against all of that, in the other column: a name. *Tam Amberdale, diver, honest.* Scratched into the memorial wall of a chapel he had never seen. Worth — what? The dead did not collect debts. The dead did not stand in the counting-house with their caps in their hands. Restore the name and the only man who heard it spoken clean would be a boy who already knew it was clean, standing in front of a wall, alone.

The wind took the bothy in both hands and shook it. Sparks climbed. In the red light the pearl on the stones had gone out, or been taken back, or had never been there.

"Worth it?" Mirabel said to the dark. His voice cracked on the second word, and he hated how young it sounded. "Is it worth it? Answer me."

The silence answered. It was the white silence, the deep-water silence, the silence that had closed over his father fourteen years ago without comment or apology, and it said: *no one answers it for anyone.*

But his father's voice came anyway, from wherever voices go when the fire is low — not beside him now but everywhere, in the wind, in the stones, in his own chest.

"I'll tell you what I told the directors," it said. "The day they asked me to dive for their boxes, their contraband, their pretty stolen things. They offered me money. Then they offered me my indenture cleared. Then they mentioned you, boy — mentioned what happens to indentured boys whose fathers are difficult. And I said no three times, and the third no was the loudest thing I ever said in my life, and it cost me everything, and I have been dead fourteen years, and I would not unsay it. Not for warmth. Not for you. Especially not for you." A pause, and the gentleness in it was worse than any anger. "You keep asking what the truth buys. It doesn't buy. That's the trick of it, Mirabel. It's not a coin. It's a weight. You don't spend it. You dive with it, and it takes you down, and you hold your breath, and you come back up because you choose to come back up. Every stroke. Every time."

"And if I don't come up?"

"Then you don't," his father said, "and the river keeps what it's owed, and the letter stays under your ribs until the spring melt takes the paper too. And some junior clerk in Kingsford writes *missing, presumed dead* in a margin, in a fine neat hand, and closes the book." Another pause. "I'd rather you didn't. I'd rather you came up. But it's your breath, boy. It was always going to be your breath."

* * *

Mirabel woke to grey light and a stopped wind and the enormous ringing quiet of a world buried fresh.

Snow had drifted against the door in the night; he dug his way out into a morning so bright it hurt, the moor transformed, every stone wearing a white cap, the sky rinsed blue and high. His foot was stiff but whole. His fingers worked. He ate the heel of Sela's bread, which she had baked sweet with caraway, and it tasted like the finest thing he had ever eaten, and he laughed once, briefly, at nothing, at being alive to taste it.

Then he saw the tracks.

They came out of the south, where he had come from — a single line of boot-prints pressed deep, hours old, glazed with refrozen melt. They did not pass the bothy. They came to it, circled it once, and stopped at the door, and then turned away again, north and west, toward the coach road.

Whoever had found this shelter in the night had looked in on a sleeping boy and a dying fire, and had chosen not to wake him.

Or — Mirabel crouched, heart suddenly loud in the white morning — whoever had made these prints had looked through a crack in the plank door at a face they knew, a boy they had taught his letters, and had not been able to make themselves open the door. And had turned north, toward Ferren Gate, to wait.

Mirabel tightened his father's weight on his belt, pulled his coat close over the letter against his ribs, and set out south across the burning snow, walking now not away from the man following him but toward whatever waited at the end of the prints — and at the end of the moor, three days on, the weir at low water, and Kingsford, and the magistrate's seal that would either raise his father's name or bury it with the spring ice.
