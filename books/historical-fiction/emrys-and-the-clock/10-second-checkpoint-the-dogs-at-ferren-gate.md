## 10. Second Checkpoint: The Dogs at Ferren Gate

The road to Ferren Gate ran along the spine of a hill, and from its crest Mirabel could see the checkpoint laid out below him like an anatomy lesson: the gate itself, two stone towers joined by a timber palisade across the road; the queue of carts and travelers backed up half a mile in the grey morning; and, moving through that queue with terrible patience, the dogs.

There were four of them. He counted twice to be sure, though once would have been enough to change everything. They were not the loose-ribbed village curs he had grown up with on the Coldharbour docks, dogs that would trade their loyalty for a fish head and forget your scent by supper. These moved at heel, silent, their handlers holding the leads short. They were heavy through the chest and narrow through the skull, and they did not bark at the queues or the carts or the crying children. They worked with their noses down, methodical as clerks, going from traveler to traveler the way Halvard had once gone down a column of figures — and like Halvard's columns, they left nothing unexamined.

*Trained to paper*, Sela had said. *And ink.*

Mirabel crouched in the gorse at the hilltop and let the wind explain the rest to him. It carried smells up the slope in layers: woodsmoke from the guardhouse, wet wool, horse dung, the tar someone was recaulking onto a cart bed below. Somewhere in all of it, sewn into the lining of his coat against his left ribs, sat a letter written in iron-gall ink on rag paper, sealed with wax that had been pressed by a dying man's thumb. To a dog's nose, he understood with a cold settling in his stomach, that letter was not hidden at all. It was shouting.

He thought about turning back. He gave the thought its full weight, the way his father had taught him to weigh a stone before tying it to the descent line — feel it honestly, then decide. Behind him lay four days of moorland, the bothy where he had nearly died, the blizzard that had spoken to him in his father's voice. Ahead lay the gate, and beyond the gate the river road, and beyond the river road Kingsford, where spring was already being measured out in melting ice. If he turned back now, he would not outrun the thaw. Nobody could outrun the thaw. The letter would rot in his coat lining while the company's ledgers burned in Kingsford, and his father's name would stay drowned forever under the weight of other men's arithmetic.

He got up out of the gorse and walked down toward the queue.

* * *

The line moved slowly enough that Mirabel learned it the way he had once learned the rhythm of a dive: by breath, by count, by surrender to the interval. A family of cheese factors ahead of him, their cart smelling of whey gone sharp. Behind him, two pedlars who bickered in low voices about which of them the dogs would dislike more. Overhead, the sky hung low and the colour of dishwater, and the wind came off the moor carrying the last wet ghost of the week's rains.

At the head of the queue, the procedure never varied. Papers were demanded. Faces were compared to a sheet that one guard held pinned against the wind — and Mirabel, craning when he could, saw with a lurch that the sheet bore sketches. Small ones, quick ones, charcoal on cheap stock. One of them was a young man with dark hair and a diver's shoulders, and beneath it someone had written *AMBERDALE* in letters that had been traced over twice, hard enough to groove the paper.

They know my name now, he thought. Not just my face. Ashcombe has given them the name like a curse to speak.

But it was the dogs that held his fear. They worked the line in pairs, one on each side of the traveler being searched, while a third handler watched. The animals pushed their muzzles into hampers, into boot cuffs, into the seams of coats. Twice they paused, and twice the guards went through whatever they'd found with a thoroughness that made people in the queue look away — a woman's bundle of letters from her sister read aloud in the mud, a merchant's sample books confiscated whole. Paper and ink. They were hunting paper and ink, and every soul in that queue was guilty until their smell proved otherwise.

Mirabel's mind, trained by years of measuring breaths underwater, began to work the problem the way it had been taught to work all problems: precisely, coldly, item by item.

He could not abandon the letter. That was settled; he did not permit himself to revisit it. He could not bury it and come back — Ferren Gate stood at a crossroads, guarded night and day, and anything left in the ground here was as good as delivered to Ashcombe. He could not pass the search by force or fraud; the guards checked faces against the sheet, and his face was on it.

Which left the dog.

A dog is not a machine, he told himself, watching one of them work a fat drover three places ahead. A dog is a nose with a devotion attached. It was trained to find paper and ink because those smells are rare on a body and easy to name. But a nose can be drowned. His father had drowned worse than noses. Every winter of Mirabel's childhood, before a dive into black water, old Amberdale had done the same thing: rubbed tallow and tanner's oil into his skin until he smelled like nothing human, like a thing rendered and cured, so that the eels and the lampreys would take him for driftwood and leave him alone. *You don't beat a nose,* his father used to say, cinching the weight belt. *You lie to it so completely it stops asking questions.*

Tanners. Mirabel looked down the line, past the gate towers, to where the queue bent around a cluster of sheds at the roadside — and there, exactly as if the world had decided to answer him before he finished asking, hung the hides. Racks of them, oak-bark brown, dripping slow in the damp, and beside them a pit, and beside the pit an old man working a hide across a beam with a two-handed knife, his forearms stained to the elbow in a brown that no washing would ever fully surrender.

Mirabel counted what he had. The tanner's fee at Marrow Bridge had taken most of his coin, but not all. There remained a little — a few shillings wrapped in cloth in his boot, meant for bread and ferry passage, and his father's diving weight hanging at his belt, which was not for sale at any price, and which he did not let himself consider selling even for a moment. Though the moment came. It always came. He put it back in the drawer where he kept such thoughts and shut it.

When the queue's shuffling brought him level with the sheds, during the small chaos of a cart argument flaring up near the front, he slipped sideways out of the line and walked to the pit.

* * *

The tanner looked up without stopping his work. His eyes were pale as pickle-brine and about as welcoming.

"Work's not offered," he said. "Roads are choked with folk wanting work."

"I'm not wanting work." Mirabel kept his voice low and flat, the voice of a man buying rope, nothing more. "I'm wanting smell."

That stopped the knife. The old man straightened a fraction, and Mirabel watched him do the arithmetic that men of the road learn to do faster than any clerk: this boy is running from something, and helping him may cost me, but coin spends regardless.

"Smell," the tanner repeated.

"Your pits. Your oils." Mirabel unwrapped the cloth from his boot and set the coins on the beam, keeping two fingers on them. "I need my coat to smell of nothing but your trade. Soaked in it. The lining worst of all — the lining most of all," he corrected himself, and hated the stumble, and watched the pale eyes note it.

For a long moment the tanner said nothing. Wind moved the hanging hides behind him with a soft, meaty slapping sound, like slow applause.

"Company's paying gold for a name," the old man said at last, conversationally. "Boy's name. Diver's son, off Coldharbour." He nodded at the sheet Mirabel knew was pinned up by the gate. "Reward's more than I see in a year of skins."

Mirabel's hand did not move from the coins. His heart, which had been hammering since the hilltop, seemed suddenly very loud in his own ears, loud as the river under ice. *So this is how it goes wrong,* he thought, with strange clarity. *Not with dogs. With a tired old man and a year's wages.*

"And yet you're talking to me," Mirabel said quietly, "instead of walking to the gate."

The tanner looked at him for a long time. Then he spat, brownly and with precision, into the pit.

"My son went south for wage-work on company roads six years back," he said. "Letters came regular for a while. Then debt came instead, and after debt, nothing came at all." He wiped his knife on his apron and held out a hand the size and colour of a cured ham. "Give us the coat."

What followed was fifteen minutes that Mirabel would remember for the rest of his life in fragments, each one separate and vivid as things seen through water. The coat over a rack, doused at the hem and sleeves from a jug of pit liquor — the tanner's word for it, delivered with grim pride — a fluid so rich with oak-bark and lime and rendered fat that Mirabel's eyes streamed at ten paces. The lining painted with a brush made of bound horsehair, the tanner working it into the seams with the same unhurried thoroughness he gave to hides, muttering, "Ink sits in seams, that's where a clever nose goes first, seams and folds, so we drown the folds too." The whole garment then rubbed with tallow from a crock by the fire, worked in with hands like paddles until the wool beneath was stiff and shining.

"There," the tanner said finally, holding it up. The coat had changed colour slightly, gone darker, heavier-looking, a coat that belonged to someone who worked with carcasses and never thought about it. "Put it on while it's warm. Body heat opens the smell. Walk among them stinking of honest filth, and their dogs will read you as a tanner's drudge, and nobody searches a tanner's drudge twice unless the drudge gives them reason."

Mirabel pulled it on. The warmth of it was obscene, almost unbearable, and the smell closed over his head like water.

"The letter," he began. "Inside—"

"Don't tell me what's inside." The tanner held up the huge hand. "I've a guess and I'll keep it. Guesses are free. Knowing gets a man hanged." He swept the coins off the beam into his apron pocket, then paused, and added, gruffly, as if embarrassed by the whole exchange: "The seam at the left ribs. You want it restitched tighter? Things soak through stitching if the stitching's loose."

Mirabel stared at him.

"You're not the first runner to stop at my shed," the old man said, and turned back to his beam. "Go on. Queue's moving."

* * *

Rejoining the line was the hardest ordinary thing Mirabel had ever done. He took his old place between the cheese factors and the pedlars, and the pedlars recoiled from him at once, wrinkling their noses, drawing their bundles in — and he nearly laughed aloud at the beauty of it, at being despised for exactly the right reason. He apologized humbly, explained that he was in service to a tannery down-road, carrying his master's work-coat home for repair, and the explanation fitted him so perfectly, so smoothly, that he felt the story settle onto him like the coat itself: true-seeming, supple, worn-in.

By mid-afternoon he stood within sight of the gate.

Up close it was worse than it had been from the hill. The palisade timber was new, pale and raw — raised within the month, he realized; Ashcombe had built this throat into the road deliberately, funneling every southbound traveler past the noses of his dogs. Guards in company grey stood in pairs. The charcoal sheet hung between two posts, and next to it a smaller notice bearing the reward figure, and Mirabel made himself look directly at his own sketched face, memorizing what they thought they knew of him: the hair, the breadth of shoulder, the eyes drawn too soft. A boy might pass who matched that drawing badly. A tanner's drudge, hooded, red-eyed, reeking, might not be compared to it at all.

Then the dogs reached him.

There were two this close to the front, working opposite sides of the queue. The nearer one was liver-coloured with a pale blaze on its chest, and it came along the line of waiting bodies with its muzzle sweeping low, and Mirabel felt his entire self contract around the seam at his left ribs. His heart was not hammering now; it had gone somewhere past hammering into a deep, resonant pounding, like the sound the river makes under ice just before the ice speaks. He fixed his eyes on the middle distance and breathed the way his father had taught him to breathe at the bottom of a dive: slow, shallow, economical, giving the body nothing extra to spend.

The dog reached the woman ahead of him, nosed her baskets, found nothing, moved on.

It came to Mirabel.

The muzzle touched his coat hem. He kept his eyes on the distance. He thought, absurdly and uncontrollably, of Halvard teaching him his letters at the counting-house table, the clerk's dry finger moving under the words: *a name is only a word you agree to answer to.* He thought of the tallow, the pit liquor, the drowned folds. He thought: *I am a tanner's drudge. I am a tanner's drudge. I am nothing worth a second question.*

The dog breathed him in. Once. Twice. Its head swung along the line of his ribs — left side, the seam, the place where paper and iron-gall ink sat wrapped in wool — and Mirabel's lungs went empty and stayed empty, and the whole world narrowed to a wet black nostril and the width of a stitch.

Then the smell arrived. Even the dog seemed to meet it — the great reeking wall of oak-bark and lime and rendered death that the coat had become — and the animal's head jerked back, sneezed, shook itself once, and pushed on past him to the pedlar behind, who made a strangled noise as the muzzle dove into his pack of pamphlets.

Pamphlets. Paper and ink, and plenty of them.

What happened next took less than a minute and lasted, Mirabel was certain, a season. The dog planted itself before the pedlar and did not move, and a guard shouted, and the man was hauled out of the line with his wares scattering, and every eye and every guard and both dogs converged on the shrieking pedlar and his seditious little library. The queue surged forward to fill the gap. Someone behind Mirabel shoved; someone else cursed; and Mirabel drifted with the crowd, hood down, eyes streaming from the reek of his own coat, one indistinguishable drudge among twenty.

At the table, a bored sergeant glanced at him, glanced at the charcoal sheet, glanced at him again — and Mirabel met his eyes with the flat, weary, faintly aggrieved stare of a man who has been standing in the cold all day and wants only to get home, and the sergeant waved him through with two fingers, already calling for the next traveler.

The gate's shadow passed over him. Then the road opened southward, empty and brown and running with meltwater, and the wind at his back no longer smelled of smoke or dogs but of the river, far off, getting ready to break.

Mirabel walked until the towers were out of sight behind a rise. Then, and only then, he allowed his legs to shake, and leaned against a fence post, and laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound that frightened a crow off the hedge — because he had done it. Through Marrow Bridge by cunning, through Ferren Gate by nerve, and the letter still rode warm against his ribs, and Kingsford lay five days south.

He was still laughing when he saw the rider.

Far back along the road he had come, at the crest of the last hill, a single horseman stood motionless against the grey sky — watching the gate, not the road — and even at that distance, even motionless, there was something in the set of the shoulders that Mirabel knew the way you know a voice through a wall. He had seen those shoulders bent over ledger desks and schoolroom copybooks for half his life.

Halvard Grell had reached Ferren Gate.
