## 13. The Ice Begins to Speak

The rain woke me before the light did.

It came against the coaching inn's small window like fingers tapping — soft at first, then insistent, then a steady drumming that had no patience in it at all. I lay in the narrow bed above the stable yard and listened, and something in my chest turned over the way a sleeper turns when a dream goes wrong.

Rain. In the high country, in what should have been the last hard weeks of winter.

I dressed in the dark and went down to the yard, and there it was: water running in the ruts of the coach road, water dripping from the eaves, water standing in every low place where three days ago there had been only frozen mud. The air smelled wrong — or right, depending on how you counted it. It smelled of thaw. Of earth opening its mouth after months of silence. It was the smell of spring, and spring was the one thing I could not afford.

Kingsford sat south of me still, two days by coach if the roads held, longer if they didn't. And the whole arithmetic of my journey — the whole desperate ledger I'd been keeping since Coldharbour — rested on a single entry written in ice: *the river stays frozen until the letter is delivered.* The magistrate would act while the evidence could still be destroyed; Ashcombe's men would destroy it the moment the river opened and the company's barges could run again. Every mile I'd walked, every checkpoint I'd slipped through, every coin I'd spent — all of it assumed I was racing a deadline measured in weeks.

Now the sky itself had changed the terms.

* * *

I stood in the yard until my shoulders were soaked through, watching the water run. A groom came out to check the horses and gave me a look of mild pity, the kind country people give town-bred fools who stand in the rain.

"Early thaw," he said, as if reporting the price of oats. "Earliest I've seen in twenty years. Old men are calling it an omen."

"Which way does it break first?" I asked. "The river?"

He squinted at me. "You mean upcountry? The upper reaches go first, always. Ice rots from the top down, same as everything." He said it without thinking, the way he might have said *the sun rises in the east*, and then went on about his horses, leaving me with the words sitting in my stomach like swallowed stones.

Ice rots from the top down. The upper reaches go first.

Coldharbour was upstream. The counting-house, the great regulator clock, the ledgers with their columns of names — all of it stood on the upper river, on ice that was, at this very moment, being eaten from beneath by warm water and from above by warm rain. And Grell — Grell, who had let me pass at Ferren and told his masters I was lost in the blizzard — Grell's lie had bought me days, but days were not what they had been. Every hour of warmth was a knife taken to the schedule on both sides of the race. Ashcombe did not need to catch me anymore. He only needed to wait for the water.

I went back inside and counted what remained of the money Sela's bread and my own careful hoarding had left me. Enough for a coach seat as far as the last relay. Not enough beyond it. Enough, perhaps, for four more days of walking and eating if I was ruthless about both.

I sat by the inn's cold hearth and made myself think it through the way Halvard had taught me to think, years ago, over sums scratched on slate: *show your work, boy; a number you can't defend is a number you don't own.*

Option one: stay on the coach road. Fast when it was dry, impassable when it wasn't — and it was not going to be dry. Meltwater was already finding the low ground; by tomorrow the culverts would be roaring, and a coach line that couldn't run would strand me in a town with a name, a face, and a description posted on its notice board. Worse: coaches kept records. Waybills, passenger lists, a clerk's neat hand writing *Mirabel Amberdale, bound Kingsford* in ink that would outlive me if the letter didn't.

Option two: the river path.

Every trading town on the Coldharbour-to-Kingsford run had grown up along the water, because before the coach roads there had been the ice road, and before the ice road there had been the towpath, and before the towpath there had been the river itself. The path followed the bank the whole way down — narrow, muddy, forgotten now that the coaches ran — but it passed no toll gates, asked no names, kept no books. Divers' families had walked it for generations, carrying their weights and their nets. My father had walked it. Somewhere along it, I remembered suddenly, there were places the divers had marked, little shrines and shelters, chapels even, where a man could get out of the rain.

And it ran beside the thing I feared most, which meant it also ran beside the truth of it. If I was going to lose the race, I wanted to watch the clock while it beat me.

I paid for nothing but the bread and cheese I carried out, and I left the inn before the coach came down from the north, so that no clerk would ever write my name in that yard at all.

* * *

The river path was everything the groom's weather had promised and less. What had been a firm track in frost was now a ribbon of sucking clay, and I learned within the first mile to walk on the grass margin, where the ground still held some memory of winter. Rain fell steadily off the low grey clouds, not violent, just ceaseless — the kind of rain that doesn't attack anything, only wears it, patiently, the way water wears stone and debt wears families.

The river itself was changing under my eyes.

In the morning it had still worn its winter face: white along the margins, dark and slow in the channel, silent under its lid of ice near the banks where the current slackened. By midday the lid was talking. I heard it before I understood it — a sound like a whale's moan carried through the ground and up through my boot soles, a long groaning note that rose and fell and rose again. I stopped and stood listening, and the hair rose on my arms though the air was warmer than any day since I'd left Coldharbour.

I knew that sound. Every diver's child knows it. It was the sound the lake ice made at the end of the season, the sound we were pulled out of the water for, the sound that meant *go home*. Ice under strain is not quiet. Ice dying is a chorus. And this river, a hundred miles below its frozen source, was beginning to sing.

By late afternoon the groans had become cracks — sharp reports like distant musketry, each one making me flinch though I told myself I was past flinching. Where the current ran close to the bank, the ice had gone grey and rotten, honeycombed, sagging into the black water in plates that tilted and slid with a hiss when the river nudged them. In one bend a whole shelf had calved away and floated downstream, turning slowly, a small white island carrying a fence post upright on its back like a flag of surrender.

I walked faster.

There was a strange companionship in it, I'll admit that. All winter the cold had been my enemy — the cold that had nearly killed me in the blizzard, the cold that had stiffened my hands at every checkpoint, the cold that had held the whole world shut like a fist. Now the warmth was my enemy instead, and the cold, retreating, felt almost like an ally abandoning a shared trench. *Hurry,* the river seemed to say, in its groaning voice. *Hurry, hurry. I am keeping your appointment for you whether you reach it or not.*

* * *

Toward dusk I came to a place where the path climbed a low bluff, and from the top I could see a long stretch of the valley laid out below me: the river looping silver-grey through bare fields, the coach road paralleling it on the far side, and on the road — lights. Moving lights. Two of them, riding fast, too fast for the season, throwing long reflections off the flooded ruts.

Riders.

I dropped flat behind a hawthorn before my mind had finished deciding to, heart going hard, rain soaking through my coat to the skin. I watched them come on along the far bank, and I tried to read them the way Halvard had taught me to read a ledger: not what it says, but what it *costs*. Two riders, pushing pace in weather that lamed horses, on a road that any sensible man would have abandoned till morning. That was not travel. That was a search pattern. Someone was sweeping the valley, and the valley was exactly as wide as the distance between the river and the road.

They passed below me without slowing, torchlight swinging across the water, and once — I would swear this later, and did swear it — one of them raised a hand and pointed, briefly, toward the river itself, toward the rotting ice, before the dark took them.

Ashcombe had stopped looking for a boy on a coach. He was looking for a boy on a river. Which meant someone had guessed what I had only decided that morning — or meant that Grell's lie had finally been tested and found thin, and the hunt had widened, and the clock on my life had started running faster than the clock on the ice.

Or both. With Ashcombe it was always both. He never placed a single bet when he could place two.

I stayed behind the hawthorn until the lights were gone and my knees ached from the wet ground, and then I did the thing that fear makes simple: I left the open path and cut down toward the water, into the tangle of willow and alder that fringed the bank, where a rider could not follow and a walker could barely pass. The branches dragged at me. The mud took my boots to the ankle at every step. Twice I went down and got up again with my palms stinging and the bread in my pack miraculously uncrushed, and I did not stop, because stopping was a luxury priced in hours and I had begun to suspect I was spending hours I didn't own.

* * *

Night came down brown and warm, smelling of rot and growth, and the river talked to me in the dark.

I made a rough camp in the hollow of a fallen willow, back against the trunk, coat buttoned to the throat, and I did not dare a fire. Across the water the far bank was invisible; the world had contracted to the width of my arms and the sound of the thaw. Groans, cracks, the long hiss of shifting plates, and underneath it all the deep mutter of the current rising, week by week, day by day, hour by hour, gathering strength for the moment it would throw the whole winter off its back.

I sat with my hand pressed flat against my coat lining, over the place where the letter slept sewn into the cloth, and I thought about water.

Paper fears water. Everyone knows that. Ink runs, paper pulps, words dissolve into grey ghosts and then into nothing. Master Verrin had died with his confession sealed in wax and wrapped in oilcloth, and I had carried it through snow and search and suspicion, and it had survived all the winter's weapons — the cold, the dogs, the men. But it had never yet met the river. And the river was coming for both of us now, patient as a creditor.

If I went into that water, the letter and I would sink together or swim together. There was no third arrangement. I found, examining myself honestly in the dark, that this did not frighten me as much as it should have. Perhaps the blizzard had burned that fear out of me. Or perhaps it was simpler than that: my father had given his life to the river rather than carry what the directors ordered him to carry. It seemed fitting that I should risk mine to the same river to carry what he'd died refusing to abandon. The symmetry was not comfortable, but it was true, and I had learned on this journey that true things hold you up better than comfortable ones.

Somewhere upstream — a hundred miles, maybe less now, with the thaw eating a mile of winter every day — the ice above Coldharbour was groaning the same song. And somewhere in that counting-house, the ledgers were waiting, and the great regulator clock was ticking in its case, marking minutes that belonged, for a few more days, to men who believed they owned time itself.

Let them believe it. I had been property all my life; I knew how property thinks. Property believes its owner will always arrive in time.

I slept in snatches, waking at every crack of the ice, and in the grey before dawn I broke camp and walked on, and the rain had thickened overnight into something with intent behind it. The river had risen visibly — I marked it against a drowned fence post, then against a willow root, then against a stone I'd noted the evening before — and the rotten shore ice had vanished entirely in the night, swallowed without ceremony. Spring was not approaching. It had arrived, quietly, in the night, while I slept, and it was moving down the valley toward me at the speed of warm water.

That was the morning I saw the chapel.

It stood on a rise above a bend in the river — small, stone, older than any company, with a roof of mossed slates and a door that hung slightly open, the way doors do in abandoned holy places, as if the building itself had exhaled and not bothered to breathe in again. Divers' country. I knew it the way you know your own name: the carved stones, the offerings niche, the path worn bare by generations of feet that had come to leave flowers for the drowned.

My father's people had built such places all along this river. And somewhere among them — I felt it with sudden certainty, the certainty of blood — somewhere in one of them was his name.

The rain drove at my back. Downstream, faint under the water-noise, I thought I heard the coach road again, or riders, or only wind — I could no longer tell fear from weather. The river groaned, long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

I went up the path to the chapel door, and pushed it open, and stepped out of the rain.
