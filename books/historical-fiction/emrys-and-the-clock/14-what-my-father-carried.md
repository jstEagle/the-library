## 14. What My Father Carried

The chapel was smaller than I expected, and older.

It had been built by divers — that much was plain from the door, which still bore the marks of their craft: a row of small notches cut into the lintel, one for each man the river had taken since the timber was raised. I counted them without meaning to. Forty-one notches in black oak gone silver with age, each one an inch wide, each one a life measured the way my father had taught me to measure everything — precisely, and with respect for what it cost.

Inside, the rain became a sound behind me instead of a sound all around me, and that small difference felt like mercy. The air smelled of cold stone and old tallow and, faintly, of river mud tracked in over generations of boots. There was no altar worth the name, no gilded anything. The Verrin Trading Company had never spent a shilling on God; there was no profit in Him. What the chapel held instead was the wall.

It ran the length of the eastern side, floor to eaves, and it was covered in names.

Not painted. Carved. Cut deep by men who worked with knives for a living, because a diver's hands know knife-work better than brushwork, and every name had been scored into the stone with the same patient strokes a man uses to mark his diving weight before the cast. Some of the letters were crude. Some were beautiful. A few were barely more than scratches, made by dying men or by widows whose own hands shook too badly to cut clean. The whole wall caught what little light came through the horn window, and it seemed to move as I moved, shadow shifting inside every groove like water under ice.

I stood just inside the door with rain running off my coat and pooling at my feet, and I did not go to the wall at once. I am not sure why. Perhaps because I knew, the way you know the weight of a thing before you lift it, that once I found his name I could not unfind it. The letter pressed against my ribs where I had carried it through blizzard and checkpoint and open moor, sewn into my coat lining by my own clumsy stitches, and I understood suddenly that the wall and the letter were the same weight. One I had been carrying since Coldharbour. The other I had been carrying since I was nine years old.

The chapel had been a long time waiting for both.

* * *

I hung my coat to dry on the iron hook by the door, out of some habit Halvard had drilled into me — *wet wool rots, Mirabel, and rot costs money* — and only then realized what I had done: bared the hiding place of the most dangerous paper in the province in an unlocked building on an open road. I laughed at myself, once, short and hard, the first laugh I had let out since Marrow Bridge. It sounded strange in the stone room. Then I took the coat down again and sat with it across my knees on the low bench near the wall, and began, name by name, to read.

I read the way I had been taught to read ledgers: top to bottom, left to right, nothing skipped. Halvard's lessons. My letters, my numbers, my whole ability to do this — to stand in a dead place reading dead men's names and understand them — came from the clerk who was even now riding my trail. I did not know yet whether that thought was a wound or a comfort. Perhaps it would always be both. That was the shape of things now; I was learning to hold two truths the way you hold two stones in one hand, letting them knock against each other until they wear smooth.

*Amberdale,* the wall said, in three places.

The first was too high, near the eaves, weathered almost smooth — some ancestor, probably, drowned before the company ever bought its first license on this stretch of river. The second was lower, mid-wall, with a date beside it I couldn't read. The third was at eye height, exactly eye height, and the carving around it was fresh enough that the grooves still held shadow crisply, and beneath the name, in smaller letters, someone had cut:

**JOREN AMBERDALE — DIVER — HE WENT DOWN FREE**

I put my hand on the letters. The stone was cold as the river in January. My fingers fit the grooves the way they fit the notches of my father's diving weight, which I wore even now on my belt, and I stood there in the empty chapel with my palm flat against his name and felt nothing at all for a long moment, and then felt everything at once, so that I had to sit down on the bench before my legs gave out.

He went down free.

That is not a phrase divers use lightly. Every man on that wall went down; that is what killed them, and the wall says so plainly, date after date after date. But *free* — *he went down free* — means something particular among diving folk, something my father taught me when I was small enough to still ask questions he answered honestly. It means the last dive was his own choice. It means no debt drove him, no company ledger, no overseer's stick at his back, no contraband run with a gun behind the current. It means whatever took him, it took him as a man and not as property.

And it was a lie. Or rather — it had been carved as a truth, and then the truth of it had been stolen, and I had spent eight years believing the theft. Because everyone in Coldharbour knew the story of Joren Amberdale, told in the company's voice: how he had run a night dive off the books, how he had been greedy, how the river had simply collected its usual fee from a man who reached past his station. An indentured man dies on an unauthorized dive, and the company shrugs, and writes off the debt, and takes his son as surety. Clean arithmetic. Ashcombe's arithmetic. I knew that now.

But the wall said otherwise. The wall — carved by his own people, who had been there, who had known — said he went down free.

* * *

I unwound the stitches.

My hands were steadier than I deserved. The rain hammered the roof, the river groaned downstream, and I picked at the waxed thread in my coat lining with my father's knife until the seam opened and the oilcloth packet slid into my lap, sodden at one corner but whole, the seal broken since Ferren Gate, since Grell had read it and been unmade by it. I smoothed the pages on my knee. The ink had feathered where the damp had reached it, but Master Verrin had written with a heavy hand, pressing hard enough that even the wet had not erased him.

I had read pieces of it before. In the inn outside Ferren, torn halves, Grell's white face above mine. Fragments since, by candle stub and grey dawn. But I had never read it whole, end to end, in a quiet place, with his name on a wall behind me and nowhere left to run from any part of it. So I read it now, aloud, though there was no one to hear — because my father had taught me that a ledger read silently can hide from you, but spoken line by line it must be reckoned.

The confession began with dates. Of course it did; Verrin had been a merchant before he was anything else. The spring of 1839, the year before I was born, when the company's legitimate pearl trade was failing and Director Ashcombe proposed a remedy: the river routes south of Coldharbour carried more than pearls if you knew how to pack a diving bell, and there were buyers in Kingsford who paid in gold that asked no questions. Contraband. Gun-running, mostly, and worse than guns besides — Verrin named the cargoes plainly, in his dry hand, as though shame were a currency he had already spent.

The divers were needed because only divers could work the mid-river transfer points under the ice shelf, where revenue boats could not follow. And the divers refused. All of them. To a man. My father foremost — Verrin wrote it himself, in words that were nearly admiring:

*"Joren Amberdale stood in my counting-house with river-water still on his boots and told me the river gives us pearls and our bread, and we will not make her carry poison for any wage. He spoke for all forty-two licensed men. I have bought many things in my life, magistrate, but I have never bought a diver's refusal."*

So Ashcombe had arranged a different purchase. A forged debt, entered in the company books against Amberdale's indenture — a debt large enough that his freedom papers, his son's freedom, everything he had dived fifteen years to earn, hung upon obedience. One run. One contraband transfer at the mid-river point, in darkness, off the books. His family freed if he complied. His family sold if he refused.

And Joren Amberdale — who could have saved himself with a single dishonest dive, who owed the lie nothing, whose own name had been stolen to bind him — chose the river's honesty over his family's freedom. He walked out onto the ice in February, alone, in front of witnesses, and he cut a hole with his own hands at the honest pearling grounds, and he went down in water no overseer ordered him into, and he did not come up. Free. The only choice left to him that the company could not forge, and he spent it like a man spending his last coin on the one thing worth having.

Verrin's final lines were not about smuggling at all. They were about arithmetic, the only language he had left:

*"Let the record show what the books were made to hide: that Joren Amberdale died solvent, obedient to no order but his own conscience, owing this company nothing. The debt we entered against his name was false. I entered it myself, at Ashcombe's instruction, and I have drawn interest on a lie for eight years. Magistrate, I am beyond your fines. But the boy — the boy they took as payment on paper that was never true — send him back to the river whole. It is the only entry I cannot balance."*

I finished reading, and the rain went on, indifferent and enormous, and the river below the bank kept turning over in its sleep, and I sat in a drowned men's chapel holding the truest account ever kept of my father, and I wept.

Not the way I had wept as a child, blind and gasping. This was quieter and worse — tears that came steadily, without shaking, while some cold clear part of me stayed open-eyed and noted everything: the exact wording of the forgery clause, the page number where Ashcombe's name appeared with the word *instructed*, the fact that the ink on the third page was smearing and I must angle it away from the drip coming through the roof. Grief doing clerical work. Halvard would have appreciated that. I wept and I catalogued, and somewhere in the middle of it the two stopped being separate, and I understood that this was what purpose felt like when it finally arrived: not fire, but precision.

* * *

Afterward I knelt at the wall and cut something with my father's knife.

I am not a carver. The letters came out crooked and too shallow, and I had to go over them twice, and any of the old men of the diving families could have done better blind. But I cut them anyway, below his name, in the space the wall keeps for what the living remember:

**HE OWED NOTHING. THE BOOKS LIED. THE RIVER KNEW.**

Then I packed the letter back into the oilcloth, and restitched the lining with knots Halvard had taught me when I was ten — the same knots, the same hands' inheritance, used now to carry his master's confession to the gallows-paper — and I put the coat on, and it was nearly dry, and I stood a moment longer in the doorway looking back at the forty-one notches and the wall of names and the crooked new lines that were mine.

Eight years I had believed my father weak. Believed he had gambled his life and lost me in the losing. Believed the story the company told because the company told everything, and everything the company told had the weight of law. Now I knew the truth, and the truth rearranged the world: my father had been the strongest man on that river, strong enough to die on his own terms rather than live on theirs, and the only weakness anywhere in the story belonged to the men with ledgers.

Ashcombe had sent riders after a boy. He should have sent them after a son.

The rain had thinned to a drifting mist when I stepped out. Downstream, maybe four miles where the road bent back toward the water, the weir at Low Water waited — the last crossing, the last company post between me and Kingsford, and beyond Kingsford the spring, and beyond the spring the ice going out. The melt had come early; the river was rising even now, brown and fast and loud, chewing at the banks. I had meant to reach the ferry by tomorrow's dusk. Looking at the water's speed, I no longer believed I had that long. The river would take the crossing when it took it, and it would not wait for magistrates or murderers or sons.

I tightened my belt, checked my father's weight hanging at my hip, touched the stitched letter once through the cloth — the way you touch a talisman, or a debt — and turned downstream.

Behind me, faint through the mist, I heard hooves on the north road.
