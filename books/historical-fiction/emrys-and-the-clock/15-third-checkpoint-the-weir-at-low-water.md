## 15. Third Checkpoint: The Weir at Low Water

The river told me before I saw it.

I heard it from half a mile off, a sound like a great beast turning in its sleep, and I stopped on the towpath with the rain running down my collar and let the noise fill my ears until it was all there was. All winter the river had been a held breath — white, silent, passable, a road you could walk with a ledger under your arm. Now the breath was going out of it. The ice downstream had begun to rot, and beneath the rot the water moved in the dark like something impatient, shoving its shoulders against the underside of winter, saying *move, move, move*.

Three weeks ago I had left Coldharbour on ice hard as church stone. Now I came down to the weir at Low Water through a grey drizzle that could not decide whether it was rain or sleet, and found the crossing drowned.

The weir itself was an old thing, older than the company, older than the town that had grown up around the company: a long low comb of black timber set across the river's throat, built to hold back the current and raise the water so barges could pass above. In summer, they said, you could stand on the weir-walk and spit into the foam churning below and old men would box your ears for tempting the river. In winter, the water below it froze in terraces, blue and glassy, and children slid down them shrieking. I had never seen it any other way. I had never seen it like this.

The meltwater had come down from the moors in the night. The pool above the weir — the mill-pool, the ferry-pool — had climbed its banks and swallowed the landing stage to the second plank. The river ran brown and muscular over the comb, folding itself again and again down the fall in ropes of white, and the spray came off it in a cold mist that hung in the air and beaded on everything: the fence posts, the ferry rope, the face of the man who sat hunched under the toll-shed with his hands wrapped around a clay pipe that had gone out long ago.

The ferry was still there — a flat-bottomed thing, broad as a barn door, riding at its rope midstream where the pull of the current held it trembling like a dog at the end of a chain. But the rope. That was the trouble. The ferry crossed on a great hempen cable anchored to an oak on either bank, and the near anchor had shifted in the flood, dragged its stone, and slumped so low that the rope now dipped into the racing water above the weir. Every swell bowed it. Every bow strained it. It sang — there was no other word for it — a low humming note that changed with the river's mood, and I did not need to be a ferryman to hear what note it was tending toward.

*Snap.*

I stood in the drizzle and understood, with the flat clarity that exhaustion brings, that this was the last crossing. Behind me lay two hundred miles of road, three checkpoints, a blizzard, an inn where a friend had unmade himself for me, and a river path I had walked day and night because the coaches were watched. Ahead of me, across that brown thundering pool, lay Kingsford — nine miles by the far bank, maybe ten, the magistrate's chambers, the letter sewn against my ribs growing soft with sweat and damp. Nine miles between my father's ruined name and whatever might yet be done about it. And between me and those nine miles: fifty yards of water moving like a landslide.

The ferryman looked up when my boots crunched the frozen mud at the edge of his ground. He was sixty if he was a day, built like the bollard he leaned against, with a beard gone yellow-grey and eyes the same color as the water, and he took me in the way such men take in everything — boots, coat, the absence of luggage, the way I kept my left hand pressed to my ribs without knowing I did it.

"Crossing's shut," he said, before I opened my mouth.

"I can pay."

"Can't cross anyway." He jerked his chin at the singing rope. "Hear that? She'll go by morning. Maybe tonight. Company engineer was sent for. Sent for yesterday." His mouth twisted around the word *company* the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "Nobody comes. Too busy, I expect, watching roads."

My heart did something complicated — sank and lifted at once. Watching roads. Of course they were watching roads. Ashcombe had riders out; Grell had told me so with his own mouth, standing in the inn yard at Ferren with the letter's words still wet in his eyes, telling me to run for the river path because the coach route was dead. If the company men were watching the roads, some of them would think of the weir. It was the last crossing. Any man fleeing to Kingsford must funnel through it like water through a sluice.

"How much," I said, "if it were open?"

He named a figure that made me laugh — a short, ugly laugh with no humor in it, startled out of me. I had eleven coppers and a knife, and one of those I would not sell. My freedom papers were not mine yet; they were a rumor, a promise, a page in a magistrate's future docket. Everything I had carried out of Coldharbour fit inside one coat, and none of it was money.

"That's what I thought," the ferryman said, not unkindly. He sucked at his dead pipe. "You're the one they're after, then."

I went very still. Rain dripped off my hood. Above us the rope hummed its one rising note.

"Don't look so stricken, boy. Reward paper came through two days back, and every man between here and Kingsford has read it twice over. Dark coat. Young. Pearl diver's build — shoulders like yours, if you'll forgive a man for noticing." He said it mildly, almost sleepily, and I could not tell — God help me, I could not tell — whether he meant to turn me in when the riders came or only wanted me to know he could.

"Then why haven't you—"

"Riders are an hour behind you. Maybe less." He stood, slow as a crane taking load, and looked at the water rather than at me. "I've ferried for the Verrin company thirty years. Watched them buy the tolls, buy the magistrates, buy the priests. Watched them take a man's boat off him when he wouldn't carry their crates and call it *contract law*." He spat a brown arc into the flood. "Thirty years I kept my head down and my mouth shut, because a man with daughters learns arithmetic. So don't ask me to be a hero. I'm too old and too tired and I've got girls getting married in the spring."

"Then don't be a hero," I said, and heard my own voice come out low and steady, which surprised me, because inside I was nothing but wind. "Just name what the crossing costs, and I'll owe it. Written debt. Witnessed. I'll be worth something when this is done — there are people in Kingsford who'll stand surety for—"

"You don't understand me." For the first time he turned and looked at me straight, and his eyes were not sleepy at all. "I'd row you across for double that price if the rope would bear it. Look at it. You put weight on that cable midstream and she parts, and the ferry goes sideways into the weir, and you and me both go into that—" he nodded at the brown ropes of water folding over the comb, "—and there's no coming out of that. Divers drown there every thaw, and divers know water. You'd have better luck diving under the fall. At least that way it's quick."

*You'd have better luck diving under the fall.*

I turned and looked at the weir properly for the first time. Not as an obstacle. As water.

I had been diving since I was seven years old. My father put the belt on me before he died — his belt, the lead diving weight I wore now at my hip under my coat, smooth as a riverstone from thirty years of his hands and four years of mine. I knew what water did to a body. I knew cold, and pressure, and dark, and the particular silence of being somewhere air cannot follow. I had held my breath in holes in the river ice while pearls the size of peppercorns paid a master who never once said my work was good. I had measured my whole life in what could be carried down into the cold and brought back up.

Fifty yards. That was all. A pearl bed is deeper than fifty yards is wide, and colder than any flood, and darker than this grey roaring noon.

"The rope will go," I said. "When?"

"Soon. Tonight, I said. Might be an hour. Might be less, with this rain."

"And when it goes, the ferry's done. No more crossing here at all until they rig a new anchor. Days."

He didn't answer, which was answer enough.

Behind me, faint under the river's roar, I heard it: hooves. More than one horse, coming fast along the towpath through the trees. The sound arrived in my chest before my ears sorted it out, the way a diver feels a storm through the hull of a boat.

The ferryman heard it too. Something moved behind his face — regret, maybe, or just resignation, the tired arithmetic of a man whose daughters needed feeding. "That'll be your hour gone," he said quietly. "Best get into the shed, boy. Or the woods. They'll search, but—"

"No."

I do not know where the word came from. It came up out of me like a breath held too long, four years deep, from the bottom of everything. The riders were close now — I could pick out individual hoofbeats, three horses, maybe four, iron on frozen mud — and I stood at the edge of the drowned landing with the rain on my face and the letter warm against my ribs and my father's weight dragging gently at my belt, and I thought: *this is the shape of it, then.* This is what the last four chapters of my life look like. A flooded pool. A rotten rope. A few minutes' lead and no place left to run, because I had run out of map. Ahead there was only water, and behind there was only them, and between them there was only me, and what I chose.

My father chose the water once, too. That was the thing the letter had told me in the chapel at Ferren Reach, the thing I had read with tears blurring the ink while his name stared down at me from the memorial wall. He refused to dive their contraband. They took his contract, his boat, his good name, his health, his life — piece by piece, politely, in triplicate — and he never once dove for them, and he drowned in honest water doing honest work, and they called it misadventure. He could not swim to safety with the weight on. Neither could most men.

But I was not most men. I was a pearl diver's son, and I had been holding my breath against the Verrin Trading Company since before I had a name for it.

"Ferryman," I said. "Give me your longest line. Twelve feet of good rope, anything. And keep hold of the near anchor point when I'm gone, because I'll come up on the weir-walk if the river lets me, and I'll need something to catch."

He stared at me. Then at the water. Then at me again, and I watched him understand — watched the arithmetic run behind his eyes, the sums of risk and reward and the strange third column that has no number, the one that made him take the coil off its peg and press it into my hands without another word about prices.

"They'll see you," he said. Hooves now, very close, crashing through brush along the towpath.

"Then they'll see a man cross a river," I said, "which is what ferries are for."

I stripped off my coat — the coat, the coat with the letter sewn into the lining — and rolled it tight and bound it to my father's diving weight with the ferryman's cord, knot after knot after knot, my fingers numb and certain. The letter would ride on my hip, weighted, protected, exactly where his hands had held thirty years of river. If I went down, it would not float away. If I went down deep, the weight would find the bottom and wait for me, the way it always had.

The first rider broke from the trees at the top of the bank — a wet bay horse, a man in company grey, and behind him another, and the ferryman shouted something, and the rope above us gave one great groaning note like a struck bell—

And I walked off the landing into the meltwater, into the cold that closed over my head like a door, into the brown roaring dark, holding my father's weight against my ribs and my breath in my chest, while behind me the world dissolved into the sound of the river, and the rope above the weir began, at last, to sing its breaking note.
