## 16. Grell's Choice

The rain had been falling for two days without conviction — not the hard rain of summer but a thin, persistent weeping out of a sky the color of wet slate — and it had turned the last miles of the river road into something between a path and a wound. Halvard Grell rode it at a pace he would once have called reckless and now called necessary, his horse laboring in the deep mud, his coat soaked through to the linen, his ledger-mind cataloguing despite itself: *fourteen miles from Ferren, ground rising eastward, water in the fields where no water should be.*

He had left the riders behind at the crossroads above Dunmarrow with an order that was almost true — *sweep the coach road, he'll want speed now* — and then he had turned his horse down toward the river alone, because there was a crossing at the old weir that did not appear on any company map, and because he had understood, somewhere in the sleepless arithmetic of the last three nights, that Mirabel Amberdale would know about it.

Boys who grow up on the water learn the water's secrets. Grell had taught him letters and figures; the river had taught him everything else.

The weir announced itself before he saw it: a long, low thunder under the rain, the sound of the river being made to work. Then the trees opened and there it was — the low stone lip of the dam running bank to bank, black water sliding over it in a smooth, terrible curtain, and below it the churned white fury of the plunge pool, and above it the flooded reach where the meltwater had come early and come angry, backing up into the willows, tearing at the banks with small brown hands.

The ferry was still there. That surprised him. He had half expected Ashcombe's men to have reached it first, or the ferryman to have abandoned it to the flood. But the ferryman was there — a broad, weathered man named Corren, whom Grell knew by reputation as a man who took coin from anyone and asked no names — and he stood at the landing with the rope in his hands, arguing with the river.

Grell dismounted, tied his horse to a willow, and walked down the sodden planking of the landing with his boots ringing false and hollow against the wet wood. The sound carried across the water. Corren turned, squinting through the rain.

"One at a time," Corren called. "And it'll cost you double in this water. Triple if you're carrying anything the river wants."

"I'm not crossing," Grell said. "Not yet."

Corren's squint deepened. Men like Corren could read a coat the way Grell read a ledger — cut, cloth, condition — and Grell watched him read the company grey of his, watch him arrive at the obvious conclusion and file it away behind a face that gave up nothing.

"Then what," Corren said slowly, "are you doing?"

"Holding your rope," said Grell.

* * *

He had rehearsed nothing. That was unlike him. His whole life had been rehearsal — twenty years of drafting entries twice before committing them, of composing reports in his head while walking, of never saying a word that could not be entered into evidence on his own behalf. Halvard Grell had lived the way he kept books: every line ruled straight, every figure carrying forward cleanly into the next column.

And then, eleven days ago, in a coaching inn outside Ferren, a boy he had taught to write his own name had unsealed a letter in front of him, and every line in every ledger Grell had ever kept had turned out to be written in someone else's hand.

He had read the confession twice. He would read it a hundred times more, in memory, in the dark, in the space between one breath and the next. Master Verrin's dying hand, cramped and blotted, naming Director Ashcombe, naming the contracts, naming the contraband that moved down the frozen river under pearl-boat manifests for nine years — and naming, in a paragraph Grell had needed to read three times because his eyes kept refusing it, Tobias Amberdale, diver, who refused the cargo, who was ruined for the refusal, whose debt was manufactured in the counting-house in a single afternoon by clerks who were only doing their work, who were only following the figures, who were only —

Who were only Halvard Grell.

He remembered the boy's face across the inn table, waiting for him to do what he had come to do. He remembered saying, *Go. Ride through the night. Don't stop until Ferren is behind you.* And he remembered walking back out into the dark afterward and standing in the inn yard until the cold stopped meaning anything, understanding that he had just unmade himself, entry by entry, and feeling — this was the part he could not account for, the figure that would not balance — feeling lighter.

Now he stood on a flooded landing at a weir that was groaning like something alive, holding a ferry rope, waiting for either a boy or a squad of riders, and he did not know which he hoped for.

Both, he admitted at last, in the privacy of his own skull, where no audit could follow. He hoped for both. He hoped they would arrive together, because then the choice would be taken out of his hands, and God forgive him, some cowardly remainder of himself wanted it taken out of his hands.

The river would not permit cowardice much longer. He could hear it. All morning the ice above the weir had been speaking — a deep, irregular knocking, like knuckles on a door, like fate asking politely to be let in. The flood had pushed the broken ice downstream until it jammed against the weir's lip, and now the whole reach above them was a white field under strain, plates shifting, ridging, shrieking in the small hours of its collapse, and when it went — and it would go today; Corren knew it too, Grell could see it in the way the man kept glancing upstream — it would take the landing, the ferry, the rope, and anything on them.

"You want to tell me what you're actually doing?" Corren said. He had a flat voice, a river voice, that wasted nothing.

"Waiting for a traveler," Grell said. "When he comes, put him across."

"And when the other travelers come? The ones with horses and company money?"

"Then I'll lie to them," said Grell, and heard the words leave his mouth and enter the world as fact, irreversible, posted to the permanent record.

Corren looked at him for a long moment. Rain ran off the man's beard.

"That's a strange thing for a man in that coat to say," Corren observed.

"It's a strange day for it," Grell agreed.

* * *

Mirabel came at midafternoon, out of the willows on the north path, and Grell's heart did something complicated and painful at the sight of him.

The boy was soaked to the waist, mud to the knees, thin as a winter branch. Eleven days on the road had carved him. But he came down to the landing the way his father must have come down to the water — sure-footed, head up, reading the surface — and when he saw Grell standing at the rope he stopped, ten paces off, and his hand went to the knife at his belt, and Grell felt the full weight of what he had been to this boy settle onto him like wet snow.

That hand on the knife. For him. Halvard Grell, who had held him as a child when the fever took his mother, who had taught him his nines on a slate, who had slipped him honey-cakes behind the counting-house — that man was now a thing to draw steel against.

"I couldn't be sure," Mirabel said. His voice was hoarse. "I saw you turn off at Dunmarrow. I've been parallel to you on the high path for six miles, trying to decide if you were leading me here or herding me."

"What did you decide?"

"That I didn't have another crossing in me," Mirabel said. "The bridge at Hallowford is washed out. The ford at Netherby is guarded. This is the last way across before Kingsford, and Kingsford is twelve miles on, and the ice—" He stopped. Listened. The knocking under the river had risen to something like speech. "You hear it too."

"Everyone hears it now." Grell looked past him, up the north path, empty, dripping. "There are riders behind you."

"Ashcombe's?"

"Six of them. I told them you'd taken the coach road." Grell heard himself say it plainly, without decoration, and understood that this was the confession, that everything after it was only consequence. "That bought you hours, not days. They'll work it out. When they do, they'll come down the south bank and they'll ride hard."

Mirabel stared at him. The rain fell between them. Somewhere above the weir, a plate of ice the size of a chapel roof groaned and settled, and both of them flinched.

"Why?" Mirabel said at last. It was not accusation. That was somehow worse. It was a real question, asked by a boy who had once trusted the answer to everything.

"Because I read the letter," Grell said, "and it was true. Because I spent twenty years believing the company was order, and order was justice, and it turns out I was the neat handwriting on top of something rotten. Because your father—" His voice failed him, briefly, on the first attempt in his life. He tried again. "Your father refused them, Mirabel. Refused to dive their contraband. And I signed the paper that invented his debt. My figures. My rule-straight lines. I built the trap and called it bookkeeping."

Mirabel said nothing. The river spoke for him, knocking, insisting.

"I can't give you back what I took," Grell went on. "No restitution covers it. But I can hold this rope. And when the riders come, I can lie again, better this time, because I've had practice since Ferren." He almost smiled. It hurt his face; the muscles had gone unfamiliar. "Get on the ferry."

"Not without knowing what it costs you." Mirabel's jaw was set. Stubborn. His father's jaw. "They'll know you lied. Twice."

"They'll know more than that, eventually. Everything ends in a ledger, boy. Mine is simply closing early." Grell turned to Corren. "Put him across. Now, before the ice goes."

Corren hesitated — one professional assessing another's ruin — then shrugged and began paying out the rope, hauling the ferry off the landing with the unhurried economy of a man who had decided the flood would kill them all equally and saw no reason to hurry toward it.

Mirabel stepped aboard. Then he turned, one hand on the gunwale, and looked back at Grell across four feet of black, accelerating water.

"Come with me," he said.

And there it was. The door, open. The second column, waiting for his entry. He could cross. Twelve miles to Kingsford, testimony to give, a magistrate to face, a new life of rule-straight lines drawn in honest ink. He could stand beside the boy he had wronged and spend the rest of his years paying down the debt in public, in daylight, in the clean currency of visible virtue.

"No," Grell said.

Mirabel's face flickered — hurt, then anger, then something older and sadder.

"Somebody has to meet those riders on this bank," Grell said, "and make the lie worth believing. If they find this landing empty, they'll conclude the ferry took you, and they'll ride for the Kingsford ford, and they might yet cut you off. If they find me here, with a story—" He shook his head. "Besides. What I owe, I owe here. To the company's record. To your father's name. You carry the letter, Mirabel. I'll carry the rest."

"The rest of what?"

"The weight," Grell said. "Someone should carry the weight."

Above the weir, the ice screamed — a long, tearing note, higher than anything it had voiced before — and every man on the landing went still. Downstream, the plunge pool boiled. The river had raised its voice from knocking to demand.

"Go," Grell said.

Mirabel looked at him a moment longer. Then he did something Grell had not anticipated and had no column for: he touched two fingers to his breastbone, the old divers' salute, the gesture Tobias Amberdale had used coming up out of green water with a shell in his fist. A diver's thanks. A free man's acknowledgment.

Then Corren hauled, and the current took the ferry, and the black water carried the boy sideways and downstream toward the far landing, and Halvard Grell stood on the near bank holding a rope that no longer mattered, watching the only good thing he had ever helped make row itself toward the truth.

* * *

The riders came forty minutes later.

He heard them first — hooves in the flooded willows, the wet slap of branches — and he arranged himself carefully on the landing, seated on a coil of rope, hat dripping, hands folded, a clerk's posture, an accountant's calm. He had used the time well. There was mud on his boots from the north path, deliberately acquired, and a plausible fatigue in his shoulders, and a story drafted and redrafted until every line carried forward cleanly.

Sergeant Locke reined in at the head of the track, water sheeting off his oilskin, and swept the landing with soldier's eyes. Grell noted the five men behind him, noted their horse-flesh exhaustion, noted that none of them had eaten since Dunmarrow. Fatigue was a material. He had worked with worse.

"Master Grell." Locke's surprise was genuine and useful. "They said you'd gone to ground at Ferren. Where is he?"

"Gone," Grell said. He let weariness into his voice, and defeat, and the small bitterness of a loyal man whose quarry has slipped him — none of it feigned, all of it true, merely aimed. "I had him at the inn outside Ferren. Read him his situation. He bolted in the night, took the high moor track west. I followed as far as the Netherby road. Lost him in the meltwater." He gestured at the drowned fields with a hand that did not quite tremble. "Half the country between here and the hills is under water, Sergeant. A man could walk into a drain and vanish."

Locke's eyes narrowed. "West? Not south?"

"He's a river boy," Grell said. "River people distrust bridges in flood. He'll go high and dry and come down to Kingsford from the moor side, thinking no one watches the north gate. Which is why you should be there, Sergeant, and not here talking to a tired old clerk."

It was beautiful, really. Every figure true; the fraud only in the total. He watched Locke weigh it — watched the sergeant's arithmetic run: *Grell is company, Grell has no reason to lie, Grell looks like a man who has lost.* Twenty years ago, Halvard Grell would have believed him too. That was the whole sickness of it, laid bare on a flooded landing: the company's certainty in itself, extended to everyone who served it.

"The director wants him alive," Locke said. "Or wanting."

"The director wants him silent," Grell corrected mildly. "The distinction may matter to someone, someday. In a court, perhaps."

Something passed across Locke's face — the faintest hesitation, the flicker of a man who had also done sums he preferred not to finish — and Grell pressed, gently, in the tone he had used for twenty years to close reluctant accounts: "North gate, Sergeant. Two men on the moor road, the rest at the gate. You can still have him by dark."

Locke wheeled his horse. The riders followed, mud flying, and the sound of them faded up the south bank and away, and Halvard Grell sat alone on the landing with the rain coming down and the lie completed and posted beyond recall.

His career. His pension. His name in the company's rolls, twenty years of rule-straight lines — spent. All of it traded for a boy on a ferry and a letter crossing a river, and the astonishing thing, the thing he would think about in whatever cell or exile waited for him, was how little the loss weighed. How the great heavy ledger of his life, closed abruptly, turned out to contain so little worth keeping.

Upstream, the ice gave its longest cry yet, and Grell rose to his feet and looked at the white reach above the weir and understood, with a clerk's infallible instinct for a figure about to fall due, that the river was finished waiting.

The far bank was empty. Mirabel was already moving along the water path toward Kingsford, a small dark figure against the grey rush of the flood, and Grell found he could not look away from him — could not, in fact, stop himself from stepping to the very edge of the drowning landing to watch, as the knocking under the world rose to a roar and the first crack split the ice field from shore to shore like the sky breaking open.

Twelve miles to Kingsford, he thought. And the whole river between them and the magistrates' door, and the spring let loose at last.

The ice dam burst with a sound like the end of an argument, and the river came down.
