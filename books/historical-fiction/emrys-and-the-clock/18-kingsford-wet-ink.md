## 18. Kingsford, Wet Ink

The river gave me up at the tail of the weir pool, spitting me onto a shingle bar like something it had chewed and found wanting. I lay there a long while with my cheek against the stones and the water pulling at my boots, too empty to move, too stubborn to stop my heart from going on without my permission. The sun was low and gold behind the smoke of Kingsford, close enough now that I could smell it — coal smoke and tanneries and horse dung and, beneath all of it, the green rot of spring coming off the water in sheets.

I had been swimming in my coat. That was the whole of my cleverness: when the ice dam burst above the weir and the river stood up on its hind legs, I had not tried to fight it. I had wrapped my arms around myself, around the coat, around the letter sewn into its lining, and let the flood do what floods do. It had thrown me against the ferry pilings, held me under until my lungs burned like lit paper, and then — bored of me, perhaps — set me down here among the stones like a dropped parcel.

I sat up. My hands shook so badly I had to press them flat against my knees to make them behave. Somewhere upstream, the checkpoint at the weir was a scatter of splinters and shouting, and Grell was —

No. Not yet. I could not think about Grell yet, or I would turn around, and turning around was death for everyone whose name was stitched against my ribs.

I got up. I walked.

* * *

Kingsford at dusk was a city made of noise. After nine days of moorland silence and river roar, the sheer volume of human sound struck me like weather — cart wheels on cobbles, a fishwife's cry, bells from three churches arguing over the hour, a hundred chimneys breathing smoke into a sky gone the color of pewter. I came up from the river stairs through a lane of sailmakers' lofts, dripping, leaving a trail of meltwater and river mud that honest folk stepped around without looking at me. A watchman glanced my way once, took in the sodden coat and the grey face above it, and decided I was someone else's problem. Drowned men's ghosts were common enough in Kingsford in thaw season.

The magistrate's chambers stood on Chancery Row, a narrow stone front between a chandler's and a law stationer's, marked by a brass plate gone green at the edges and a lamp burning in the ground-floor window. I knew the address the way I knew my own heartbeat; Master Verrin had made me write it out forty times when I was ten years old, his dry voice behind me saying, *A man who cannot spell the law's address has no business asking the law for anything.* I had thought then that he was teaching me penmanship. I understood now he had been teaching me where to run.

There was a clerk in the entry hall, a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular expression of someone who has spent the whole day telling people no. He looked up as I came in, dripping on the tessellated floor, and his expression did not change so much as harden.

"Chambers are closed. Petitions are heard at nine o'clock tomorrow, in writing, addressed to the registrar. If you're destitute, the parish poorhouse is—"

"I have testimony." My voice came out ruined, sanded down by river water and cold. I swallowed and tried again. "Testimony concerning the Verrin Trading Company. Concerning Director Ashcombe. Concerning smuggling, fraud, and the death of Tobias Amberdale, pearl diver, of Coldharbour."

The clerk's pen stopped moving. For a moment the hall was very quiet, and I could hear the clock somewhere above us ticking with the same patient authority as the great regulator in Coldharbour, as though all clocks in England answered to one another across the distance.

"The Verrin Company," the clerk said carefully, "is chartered, bonded, and in good standing. If you have a grievance, you may file it in writing. There is a fee."

"I have a letter," I said. "Sealed. Addressed to this office by name. Written by Josiah Verrin, master of the company, four days before he died." I put my hand flat against my chest, against the lining where the shape of it rode like a second heart. "It names directors. It names cargoes. It names dates. And every hour it sits unopened is an hour they have to burn it out of existence — starting with me."

He looked at me then properly, and I watched him do the arithmetic clerks do: the boy's age, the company's standing, the trouble either way, and which trouble could be laid at someone else's door. The answer, visibly, was mine.

"You'll wait there," he said, "and you'll drip on the mat, not the floor."

* * *

They kept me in the antechamber for the better part of two hours. I did not sit, because sitting meant my body would discover how cold it was, and cold discovered is cold surrendered. I stood by the fire instead, steaming gently, watching the water darken the hearthstones, and I went through the letter in my mind the way a miser counts coins — not reading it, for I knew it nearly by heart now, but confirming each page was still there, still legible, still real. The river had gotten at it. I could feel the difference under the cloth, a softness where there should have been crispness, and the fear of that softness was worse than anything the weir had done to me.

A senior magistrate came down at some point — I heard him before I saw him, a voice like a door closing — and spoke to the clerk in the passage.

"A pearl diver's son making accusations against a chartered company? On a wet night? Boy's half-drowned and fully mad. Tell him to come back when he's dry and represented."

"He says Verrin wrote it himself, sir. Sealed."

"Verrin is dead?"

"By all accounts, sir, some weeks."

A pause. Then, wearily, the way a man agrees to look at a bill he suspects is wrong: "Show me the seal. Only the seal. If it's broken, send him to the poorhouse and be done."

Footsteps receded. I stared into the fire and understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that everything now depended on wax and water. All the miles, all the checkpoints, the dogs at Ferren Gate, the blizzard, the weir, Grell's face across the black water — all of it funneled down to whether a circle of red wax had survived the river better than I had.

When they called me in, I nearly failed to move. My legs had opinions of their own by then. But I walked, because walking was the last thing left that I could choose.

* * *

The junior magistrate's chambers were small and over-warm, crowded with shelves of bound precedents and a single window fogged with steam. The senior magistrate had withdrawn to whatever room senior magistrates withdraw to, and what remained was a young man — younger than Grell, near enough my own age plus five years — with untidy fair hair, spectacles slightly askew, and the air of someone who had been given the cases nobody else wanted and had begun, quietly, to want them himself. His name, according to the brass stand on his desk, was E. Quillon, Deputy Assize Clerk and Commissioner for Summary Matters, which I suspected was a long title for a short salary.

He did not ask me to sit. He asked the clerk to bring towels, which I thought was a better question.

"Your name," he said, spreading a towel across the desk like an altar cloth.

"Mirabel Amberdale. Of Coldharbour-on-Frost. Indentured to the Verrin Trading Company since I was seven years old." I said it plainly, and something in saying it aloud in a room with law books in it made my throat tighten. Seven years old. I had never once said the number to anyone who might find it remarkable.

Quillon's pen paused. "Seven," he repeated, not writing it yet, just holding it up to the light.

"Company indentures begin early on the river towns. It's legal. There's a schedule of fees." I heard Master Verrin's voice in my own mouth, explaining the machinery, and hated how gentle I made it sound even now.

"And the document you're carrying. You understand that if it's what you claim, opening it destroys its standing as sealed evidence? And that if it isn't what you claim, you've walked a long way to hang yourself?"

"I understand the seal's already ruined," I said. "The river got to it at the weir. I crossed under the ice when the dam broke." I reached into my coat then, and my fingers shook so badly on the stitches that Quillon leaned across and, without a word, cut the lining open himself with a small knife from his desk drawer. The gesture was so matter-of-fact, so entirely without ceremony, that it undid me more than any kindness could have. I had carried this thing sewn against my body through blizzard and flood, and this stranger opened it the way a man opens a delivery of stationery.

The letter came out in three pieces of paper and a ruin of red wax. The seal — Verrin's signet, the anchor-and-key — had cracked clean across when the water hit it, but it had held its impression, and the wax had held its grip on the fold, and the fold had held its secret. The paper was swollen, wavy, the ink feathered at every stroke where damp had crept in, but legible. Legible. I watched Quillon's eyes move down the first page and saw his face change the way weather changes — nothing dramatic, just a pressure dropping, a light going strange.

"'To the Magistrate at Kingsford,'" he read aloud, softly, as if testing whether the words would hold weight. "'Written in my hand and of my free will, being in my last illness and wishing to die owing less than I owe.'" He looked up at me over the spectacles. "This is Verrin's hand? You'll swear to that?"

"I wound his clocks for eleven years," I said. "I know his hand better than my father's."

Something moved behind his eyes at that — pity, maybe, or recognition — and he bent again to the page. I stood there dripping more slowly now, watching him read the confession of a dying man: the contraband runs under the pearl fleets, the false ledgers, the bribed surveyors, and — third page, third paragraph — the name Tobias Amberdale, and beside it the word *innocent*, and beside that the account of a diver who refused to carry what he was told to carry in his float-bag, and what the river was told to do about it.

Quillon read that paragraph twice. When he finished the second time, he took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with great thoroughness, and put them back on, and I understood this was how he bought time to become the officer he needed to be instead of the young man he had been a minute ago.

"The ink's smeared here," he said, tapping a line. "And here. Water damage throughout. Defense counsel will call it forgery, or deterioration, or both."

"It's not the only evidence," I said. "It's the key to the rest. Ledgers in the Coldharbour counting-house. The winter manifests. Ashcombe's correspondence passes through the company post. And there are divers' families on the Frost who will testify about the cargo runs if anyone asks them under protection — which no one ever has, because no one ever asked." My voice was steadier now. Exhaustion had burned everything else away and left only the plain bones of the truth, and plain bones stand up well.

Quillon looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the church bells were ringing the hour — nine o'clock, or ten; I had lost count of hours somewhere on the moor and never recovered them. The fire ticked. The fogged window held the lamplight in a soft halo.

"Do you know," he said at last, "that Senior Magistrate Halloway declined to see you? That the registrar's standing instruction is that matters touching chartered companies go to assizes in the autumn term?" He began squaring the pages of the letter on his blotter, aligning their ruined edges with a care that bordered on tenderness. "Autumn. When the river's been open six months and every ledger between here and Coldharbour has learned to tell a new story."

"Then why are you still reading it?"

"Because," he said, "the seal is broken by floodwater rather than by hand, the hand is witnessed by a named deponent present in this room, and the matter concerns a death — which makes it coroner's business before it is company business, and coroner's business does not wait for the autumn term." He pulled a fresh sheet toward him and dipped his pen. "Also because I read the third page, Mr. Amberdale, and I find I am not able to unread it."

He wrote for a while. I heard the scratch of the nib and thought of Halvard Grell teaching me letters on the counting-house floor, the alphabet chalked on boards, *a round a, a straight t* — and pushed the thought down where it couldn't bleed. There would be time for Grell later, one way or the other. There would have to be.

"We'll take your deposition tonight," Quillon said, writing as he talked. "Full statement, sworn, witnessed by my clerk. Then the letter goes into evidence under seal — a new seal, mine — and a copy goes by rider to the coroner at first light, with a request he attend on the Verrin matter urgently. The original stays in this safe." He nodded at an iron box in the corner, squat and ugly and, I hoped, honest. "You should understand what happens next. By morning, Ashcombe will know the letter exists. Men like him don't wait for trials. They send riders, they send lawyers, and they send questions about whether the deponent is a credible witness — an indentured apprentice, a runaway, a boy half-drowned giving testimony against his own master's company." He glanced up. "Are you prepared for that?"

I thought about the shingle bar below the weir, and the black water, and Grell's arm pointing me downstream while the riders' torches swung along the far bank. I thought about my father's diving weight hanging on my belt, cold as ever, heavier than lead has any right to be.

"I've been preparing for it since I was seven years old," I said.

Quillon held my gaze a moment, then nodded once, as one professional acknowledges another, and I felt something in my chest loosen that had been clenched since the night Master Verrin pressed the letter into my hands and said *Kingsford* with his last breath's worth of air.

"Now," he said, ruling lines on the deposition form. "From the beginning. Your name, your father's name, and the day the company first asked him to dive for anything other than pearls."

* * *

They finished with me past midnight. My statement ran eleven pages, and my signature at the end of it — Mirabel Amberdale, written slow and careful, the way Halvard taught me — was the first thing I had ever signed as a free-acting man, and my hand shook doing it, and I did not care.

The clerk walked me out through the dark hall to a door that opened on Chancery Row, where the rain had started again, fine and warm, spring rain, the kind that eats ice. The lamps along the row burned in halos. Somewhere a cab horse stamped. The world was utterly ordinary, and I stood in it swaying, eleven pages lighter and no lighter at all, alive in a city where nobody knew my name except one tired young magistrate and God.

"Where will you go tonight?" the clerk asked, not unkindly.

I had no answer. No money, no friend, no bed — and a reward notice with my description on it traveling faster than any rider, because Ashcombe's arithmetic never slept. Tomorrow there would be depositions and riders and the long machinery of the law grinding slowly toward Coldharbour. Tonight there was only rain, and the letter locked in iron, and the fact that I had done the thing — the impossible, improbable, whole-of-my-life thing — and it had been enough, so far, barely, by the width of a cracked seal.

I turned up my collar and started down Chancery Row toward the river, because the river was the only direction I knew — and that was when I saw the figure standing under the chandler's awning across the street, hat brim low, coat beaded with rain, waiting with the terrible patience of a man who has been there for hours.

He stepped forward into the lamplight, and my heart stopped and started again, because it was not Ashcombe's man.

It was Sela.
