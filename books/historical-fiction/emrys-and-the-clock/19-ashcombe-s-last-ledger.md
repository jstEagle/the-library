## 19. Ashcombe's Last Ledger

The fire in the counting-house grate had been fed too well, and Director Ambrose Ashcombe noted this with a small internal correction, as he noted all excesses: *fuel, one night's extravagance, unavoidable.* The flames stood up straight and orange against the dark of his office, and into them, one by one, he was feeding the year 1847.

He did not burn everything. That was the mistake lesser men made — panic was a kind of arithmetic error, and Ashcombe prided himself on never making errors. He burned selectively, as a merchant culls stock. The correspondence with the Kingsford warehouses went first, then the duplicate manifests showing the pearl shipments that had never contained pearls, then three years of letters in a hand he recognized with distaste even now — Halvard Grell's neat clerk's script, so careful, so obedient, recording figures that proved everything and implicated everyone. Grell had written what he was told to write. Ashcombe had always found something almost touching in that quality, the way one finds a well-made hinge touching: it existed to turn when pushed.

The paper curled, blackened, and let go of its words. Ashcombe watched each page until it was fully ash, because half-burned documents were confessions with the signature left on. He wore gloves. He had worn gloves all evening, thin ones, so that no ink would mark him that had not already marked itself into legend.

Outside the windows, Coldharbour lay under the early thaw-rains, the whole town steaming faintly in the lamplight like a beast cooling after labor. The river had begun to speak. He could hear it even here, through glass and brick — a long subterranean groaning, the ice shifting its weight after four months of silence. All winter the river had been his ally, a white sealed road locking the north closed, locking evidence in place, locking men where he could count them. Now it was opening like a mouth.

*Spring comes at a price,* he thought, feeding in another page. *Everything comes at a price. The only question is who pays.*

He worked through the ledger of his own affairs with the same dry methodical care he had brought to forty years of other people's money. There was a comfort in it. When the world grew loud — and it had grown very loud these past days, with runners coming back empty-handed from Marrow Bridge, with Ferren Gate reporting searches that found nothing but a tanner paid suspiciously well in advance — the ledger remained sane. The ledger did not tremble. Figures did not lie awake at night.

Item: one letter, sealed, written by a dying man whose sentimentality had finally outweighed his sense. Unaccounted for. Presumed traveling south in the coat lining of a pearl diver's son.

Item: one clerk, Halvard Grell, dispatched nineteen days ago, who had reported his quarry *lost in the blizzard* and then, unaccountably, remained on the road another fortnight without sending further word. Ashcombe turned that fact over more slowly than any other, the way a man turns a coin he suspects is clipped. Grell had taught the boy his letters. Grell had sat across from him at the counting-house desk for eleven years. Grell had been heard, once, saying that the company was justice made orderly — and Ashcombe had liked hearing it, had perhaps believed it flattery when it was faith. Men of faith could be disappointed. Flatterers could only be bought.

*Lost in the blizzard.* Ashcombe said the words silently, tasting them, and found in them the flavor of a lie told by an honest man, which is the most durable kind.

He fed Grell's letters to the fire last of that bundle, and watched them go without ceremony.

* * *

The house clock — a small French carriage clock, the only timepiece in the room he trusted more than his own pulse — showed half past two when he opened the hidden drawer beneath the ledger shelf and took out the final account book. Not the company's books; those were already ash or would be by morning. This one was private, bound in gray calf, and it recorded a different kind of trade: sums paid to magistrates, stipends to informants, the price of a drowned diver's silence assessed against the profit of a season's contraband. His father had kept such a book before him. It was the family inheritance — not money, which spent, but method, which compounded.

He turned to a fresh page. The nib scratched in the quiet except for the fire and the far-off grinding of the river.

*Being a final reckoning,* he wrote. *In the matter of A. Ashcombe, director, versus the same.*

It amused him to structure it so. If he was to be entered into history, he would enter himself, in his own hand, with his columns ruled straight. No one else would do it properly. Magistrates wrote in prose; prose was imprecise.

*Assets remaining:* he listed them. Cash in notes, sewn value, portable — four hundred and twelve pounds, a figure he had reached by rounding down rather than up, because a fugitive who overestimated his means died of optimism. One address in Calais, purchased years ago against exactly this contingency, held under a name that had never once been spoken aloud in Coldharbour. One set of papers, genuine, in that name, describing a wool factor of modest means and blameless habits. He had built the man carefully over a decade — a dull man, deliberately dull, the sort of person officials' eyes slid off like rain off oiled cloth.

*Liabilities:* the list was shorter, and he permitted himself the smallest pause before writing it. One confession, in transit. One boy, Mirabel Amberdale, whose existence he had dismissed for seventeen years as a line item — indenture fee, boots, bread, wages withheld per contract — and who had now become, absurdly, the single greatest threat to forty years of sound management. One clerk, status unknown. One magistrate at Kingsford, junior, ambitious, whose reputation for incorruptibility Ashcombe had previously filed under *useful* and now re-filed under *hazardous*. And the spring itself, arriving early, dissolving the frozen season during which he might have moved openly.

He totaled the column. The assets outweighed the liabilities, if one discounted the incalculable — and Ashcombe had spent a lifetime discounting the incalculable, which was, he could see now with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, possibly the error that would undo him. The boy carried a letter worth nothing in any market Ashcombe controlled and everything in a market he had never priced: the market where dead men's names were weighed against living men's convenience. He had no exchange rate for that. He had never needed one.

*Conclusion: departure tonight,* he wrote. *Kingsford road is watched; take the drovers' track west, cross above the weir at low water, join the south coach at Tarn Mills. Traveling as J. Fellows, wool factor. Estimated clearance from all claims: complete.*

He blotted the page, closed the book, and put it in the fire — because the final entry of a final ledger has no reader, and he did not intend to leave the prosecution a tidy summing-up. The gray cover caught, warped, went black at the corners. He watched it with something that was not quite regret. Regret was a cost with no offsetting entry, and he had never permitted one on his books.

Then he took off his director's coat, folded it over the chair with a care that surprised him — thirty years of winters in that coat, every button still original — and put on the plain brown wool of Mr. J. Fellows, wool factor, a man who had never ruined anyone because he had never been important enough to try.

* * *

The back stairs of the counting-house were built for clerks and errand boys, and Ashcombe went down them sideways, one hand on the cold wall, feeling for the first time in decades the true steepness of stairs. The cellars smelled of wet stone and old tallow. At the coal door he paused, lifted the bar, and looked out into the alley behind Verrin's yard, where the snow had rotted into gray slush and the rain fell with the soft persistence of a season changing its mind.

No one in the alley. He crossed it quickly, boots quiet on the melting drifts, and came out onto the lane that led up past the salt sheds toward the drovers' track.

There was a man standing at the corner of the salt sheds.

Not moving. Not challenging. Simply standing, under the eave, collar up, hat dripping, with the settled air of furniture — of something that had been placed there hours ago and intended to remain. As Ashcombe slowed, another figure detached itself from the shadow of the second shed, unhurried, and stood a little way down the lane, hands in pockets, blocking nothing and therefore blocking everything.

Ashcombe stopped. His breath went out of him in a thin cloud, and he observed, with the detached interest he brought to all unwelcome figures, that his heart was maintaining its rhythm almost perfectly. Almost. There was a flutter, a small irregularity, like a smudged figure in an otherwise clean column.

*Drovers' track: compromised.* He revised the route mentally, standing there in the rain. *Then the river path south. Then—*

He turned back toward the main street, walking briskly, a wool factor late for a coach, and found the street already occupied. Two men by the chandler's, talking quietly, faces he half-knew — constables' men, or worse, the magistrate's new special force raised since the seizures began in Kingsford. A lantern hung from the bridge of the nose of one of their horses, tied at the trough, throwing a wobbling circle of light across the slush. Every road out of Coldharbour, he understood then, had been furnished with the same furniture: patient men under eaves, standing in the rain for hours, paid by the day to be immovable.

They had not come for him at his office. They could have — a dozen of them, at any hour, with a warrant and no trouble at all. Instead they had ringed the town in the night like men setting a fence, and now they were simply waiting to see which direction a certain gentleman would choose, so that the record could show flight. Flight was an entry that helped the prosecution. Ashcombe respected the thinking. It was, he reflected, exactly the kind of thoroughness he would have ordered himself.

He calculated, standing in the open street with the rain running off his brim. Four hundred and twelve pounds on his person. Papers in his breast pocket. The Calais road required the coast, ninety miles of watched territory, and the weir crossing, and the weir — the reports from the ferryman's district had said it plainly — was drowning in meltwater, the ford gone, the checkpoint chaos itself. Chaos was a door, but doors that opened for fleeing directors opened for pursuing magistrates equally.

Every alternative ended in the same column total. He ran the figures twice, because one does not accept a ruinous result on a first pass, and both times the balance came out the same.

A voice spoke behind him, level and official and almost gentle.

"Director Ashcombe."

He turned. A young man in a magistrate's coat stood there with the rain beading on his shoulders, holding out not irons but a folded paper — the warrant, served correctly, witnessed, dated. Behind him, unhurried, the others converged, closing the street the way a ledger closes: line by line, no drama, just the ruling of a final margin.

"Mr. Fellows," Ashcombe said, and offered the name with a small courteous bow, testing the last of his assets in the field.

"We know," the young man said. "We've known about Mr. Fellows since Tuesday."

Ah, thought Ashcombe. *Since Tuesday.* Somewhere there was a clerk — probably a junior one, probably badly paid — who had traced a false name through a shipping register, and that clerk had undone in an afternoon what forty years of discipline had built. He felt something move in his chest that was not fear exactly. It was closer to professional admiration curdling into grief, the sensation of watching a rival firm beat you on terms you invented.

They walked him back up the street through the rain, no handcuffs yet, no need — the town was fenced, and fences make irons redundant. Past the chandler's, past the salt sheds where the patient men touched their hats to the magistrate's officer with the deference of workmen acknowledging a foreman. Past the mouth of the alley by the coal door, where his whole evening's burning waited upstairs in the grate, going cold. At the steps of the company offices he paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back once at the river.

The ice was talking loudly now. Down the whole length of the valley it groaned and cracked and boomed, the great white road opening along seams no one could have mapped, and somewhere south of the weir — he was sure of it, sure the way one is sure of a sum checked twice — a boy with sodden coats and a ruined letter was moving toward Kingsford faster than justice could ride.

*Final entry,* Ashcombe thought, as they took his arm at the top of the steps and the young magistrate began, dryly, reading the charges aloud in the language of accounts — conspiracy, fraud, the ruin of one Tobias Amberdale, diver, deceased, *value of losses to be assessed* — *all assets seized; balance struck; the books of A. Ashcombe closed this day, and closed in deficit.*

The strangest thing, he reflected as the door shut behind him, was how light he felt with nothing left to carry.

And downstream, under the rain, the river answered no questions — it only went on breaking, all night, toward the sea.
