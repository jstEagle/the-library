## 21. Free Papers

The magistrate's clerk had ink on his fingers the way my father used to have river-mud on his — ground in past the first knuckle, worked into the whorls, part of the skin itself. He dipped his pen three times writing my name, and each time he wrote it I felt it settle a little deeper into me: *Mirabel Amberdale*. Not Amberdale's boy. Not Verrin's indenture. Not lot forty-one on a company ledger, valued at eleven pounds against debts accrued. Just a name, spelled out slowly by a stranger's hand, as if it were being born.

"Say it back to me," the clerk said. He was a small man with spectacles that sat crooked, and he had the patient voice of someone who had spent his life listening to people say things they could hardly believe.

"Mirabel Amberdale," I said. "Pearl diver's son. Freedman."

"Freedman," he repeated, and made one more mark.

The room above the magistrate's chambers smelled of tallow and wet wool and, faintly, of the river itself, which still clung to me like a second coat. My clothes had dried stiff where they'd dried at all; the letter's wax was a red ruin in my pocket, cracked into islands, and I would not let them take it from me even though its work was done. The junior magistrate — young Mr. Calloway, whose name I did not yet know when I half-drowned at his door — had examined it under a glass for the better part of an hour while I dripped on his floorboards and shook so badly a clerk brought me brandy I couldn't taste.

Now, two days later, the machinery of Kingsford turned around me with the slow grinding patience of the ice going out. Depositions taken. Seals compared. A courier gone south to London with copies. Directors named in dry, terrible columns: Ashcombe, Renner, the elder Verrin posthumous, two men whose names meant nothing to me and everything to men they had ruined. The company's ledgers, seized, lay open along a long table like patients on a surgeon's ward, and clerks moved among them probing for wounds.

And here was mine, being written: the free papers.

The document was smaller than I expected. I had imagined something grand — a scroll, a seal as big as my palm, trumpets somewhere out of sight. Instead it was a single sheet, closely written, with a ribbon through it the color of old wine. The clerk blew across the signatures, held it up to the window light as if checking cloth for flaws, and passed it over the desk to me.

"That's yours," he said. "First thing you've owned outright since you were seven, I'd wager."

I took it the way you take hold of a dive-weight at the edge of the hole — testing whether your hands will obey you. The paper trembled. Mine, not the paper's. Or perhaps both.

"There's the matter of restitution," the clerk went on, shuffling papers. "Seized assets are in receivership. Claims against the company run to four hundred names — divers' families mostly, and boatmen, and widows. There won't be much per head after the creditors, but you're owed wages in arrears going back eleven years, calculated at—"

He read out a number. It was precise to the farthing, which I respected. Halvard had taught me arithmetic at this very kind of desk, in a counting-house not so different from this one, and I heard his voice in the clerk's careful figures: *a debt is only honest when you count all of it.*

"The sum comes to thirty-one pounds, six shillings, and tuppence," the clerk finished. "Payable now or in installments. Most take installments."

"Now," I said.

* * *

Thirty-one pounds, six shillings, and tuppence in a canvas bag weighs about as much as a gull's egg. I stood on the steps of the magistrate's building with the free papers inside my coat — sewn now into the lining where the letter had lived, because some lessons you keep even after they've saved your life — and looked out at Kingsford in the first true thaw.

The town was loud. That was the thing that struck me hardest, after Coldharbour's muffled hush. All winter I had lived inside a world wrapped in wool and ice, where sound traveled only a few yards before the cold ate it, and here the noise went up and up into a bright gray sky: cartwheels in slush, gulls screaming off the warehouses, a handbell man crying fresh fish, two women laughing at something outside a milliner's. The river ran high and brown between the quays, carrying whole trees southward with their roots waving like drowned men's hair. Ice floes rode down on it, tilted plates of it, white scars on the dark water.

Somewhere downriver, that water had been the road I came north on. Somewhere beyond the first bend it had eaten Grell's ferry and the weir and very nearly me. I kept expecting to feel something about that — triumph, grief, relief — and instead what I felt most was the weight of the coin-bag in my hand and a strange, stubborn wish that my father could have seen how precisely it had all been counted.

"You'll want lodging," the clerk had said, following me to the door. "There's respectable houses near the cathedral, cheap now the company men are gone. And Mr. Calloway says — well, he says a young man who did what you did ought not vanish before the trials. His testimony's wanted, but beyond that—" the clerk had hesitated, embarrassed by warmth, "—he says there'll be places for steady men in the new administration. Men who know ledgers. Men who can be trusted with a clock."

A clock. I almost laughed. Winding the great regulator had been my duty for eleven years, every evening at six, and there had been nights — many nights, toward the end — when those sixty turns of the key were the only honest act in the whole counting-house, the only thing in it done purely because it should be done.

"I'll think on it," I told him, which was a lie, because I already knew.

* * *

The pawnshop was on Tanner's Row, in the shadow of the salt warehouse, and I had walked past it twice before I made myself go in.

I knew it would be there. Of course I knew — Sela had told me, that night at Marrow Bridge, her hands still floury as she broke the bread. *They took his weight when they took everything,* she'd said. *Company men don't pawn things themselves, they sell, but somebody bought it from the sale and somebody pawned it, and things pawninged in Coldharbour come home to roost in Kingsford eventually, like everything else.* She had drawn the shop's location on the back of my hand with a floured fingertip, and I had carried the map in my skin for four hundred miles.

The bell over the door rang thin and tinny. Inside, the air was thick with the smell of other people's lives: old velvet, lamp oil, brass polish, the ghost of a hundred desperate winters. Tools hung in ranks. Watches slept in trays. A stuffed pike glared from a shelf with glass eyes that had watched better days. And behind the counter, on a nail, exactly as if it had been waiting — hung my father's diving weight.

It is not a beautiful object. It never was. It's a lump of lead, cast in a mold hammered out of whatever iron was to hand, roughly teardrop-shaped, with an iron ring at the narrow end for the belt-cord. My father poured it himself the year I was born, in the yard behind our house, and when it cooled he cut my initial into it with a chisel, badly, because he was a diver and not a smith and letters came hard to him. *So you know which one's mine,* he said, meaning the weight, though I understood later he had meant something larger. It hung on his belt every diving day of his life. When the company men came to strip our house after the ruin, it was the one thing they left, because lead scrap wasn't worth the trouble of carrying. My mother sold it a month later, in the worst week, for bread. I was nine. I remembered standing in this same kind of shop — maybe this very one — watching a stranger's hand close around it, and understanding for the first time that the world could reach inside your house and take the shape of your father's body away.

The pawnbroker was a wide soft man with a voice like gravel settling. "Looking or buying?"

"Buying." I lifted the weight off its nail. The cold lead warmed instantly against my palm, as if it knew me. "What's the redemption price?"

He squinted at a ledger, licked his thumb, found the page. "Pawned last autumn. Fourteen months back. Principal ten shillings, interest at the legal rate, storage—" he named a figure: eighteen and six altogether, and I counted it out of the bag without letting my face do anything at all, because Halvard taught me that too, that money respects a still hand.

The broker bit one of the coins, grunted, and pushed a slip of paper across the counter. "Sign there. Pledge redeemed, title passes."

I signed. Mirabel Amberdale, again, in my own hand this time, and the letters came easier than they had for the clerk.

I threaded a cord through the iron ring and hung the weight on my belt, where it settled against my hip with the particular rightness of a thing returning to its socket. Eighteen and six for the weight. Free papers, cost nothing but a river. Which left me — I did the sum standing in the doorway, the bell quiet behind me — just under thirty pounds, in a town full of new opportunities, with every door that had ever been shut to my name suddenly standing open.

It was the richest I had ever been and the lightest I had ever felt, and I noticed, with a kind of wonder, that the two feelings were the same feeling.

* * *

I spent three days in Kingsford, because I owed the dead that much.

I gave my deposition properly, all of it, from the regulator clock to the letter under the floor to the dogs at Ferren Gate, and the junior magistrate Calloway wrote it all down in a hand as neat as Grell's had ever been. I told them about Ashcombe arriving in the night with snow on his shoulders. I told them about the reward posted at Marrow Bridge describing me so exactly it might have been written by someone who loved me — which, in its way, it had. I did not tell them what Grell had done at the weir, because he hadn't asked me to protect him and I didn't want the gift to be cheap; they found enough of it themselves in the riders' statements, the contradictory directions, the rope held steady against orders. But when they asked me, at the end, whether I had anything further, I said: "The clerk Halvard Grell read the confession before he let me pass. Whatever else he is, he isn't ignorant. Judge him knowing."

Calloway looked at me over his spectacles for a long moment. "That's not mercy, Mr. Amberdale. That's testimony."

"It's both," I said. "My father taught me you can't dive clean unless you carry the weight honestly."

On the third day I walked down to the quay where the receivership men were auctioning the company's movable goods — ledgers going cheap, furniture cheaper, the great brass ship-lamp from the counting-house entry knocked down to an innkeeper for less than my father's weight had cost to redeem. I watched a director's carriage, lacquered black, sold for twelve pounds to a fat grain merchant who immediately got it stuck in the spring mud, and stood there while half of Kingsford laughed, and found I could not laugh with them. Not because I pitied Ashcombe. Because I had seen what laughter costs when it's all you're paid in.

That evening Calloway found me at my lodgings — a narrow room above a chandler's, smelling of hemp and tallow — with an offer written fair on good paper. A clerkship. A salary of sixty pounds a year. Rooms provided. "The new administration needs men who understand how the old one worked," he said. "You understand it better than anyone living, I'd guess. You could help make certain nothing grows back wrong."

I read the offer twice. Sixty pounds a year. Warm rooms. A name that opened doors instead of closing them. Every orphaned boy in every river town dreams of exactly this paper, and I had been that boy, and some honest part of me wanted it with an ache I could have dived on.

But I thought of the regulator clock, and how Master Verrin had once said to me, in one of his last lucid hours: *A clock doesn't keep time, Mirabel. Time keeps the clock. You only wind it.* And I thought of how I had wound that clock for eleven years believing the winding mattered, believing the order it measured was justice, and how it had taken one sealed letter to show me the order was a case built around rot, and how the rot had been paid for, in the end, with my father's lungs full of river.

"I'm honored," I said. "Truly. But I need to learn what time keeps *me* before I wind anything for anyone."

Calloway tucked the paper away without disappointment, as if he'd expected the answer, as if men who carry letters through blizzards are not, in his experience, men who sit still at desks. "The offer stands," he said. "Kingsford will be here."

* * *

I left on the fourth morning, before the bell — because that habit, too, I kept.

The pack on my shoulder held almost nothing, deliberately: a knife, the ledger stub with my father's wages scratched in a dead man's hand, two days of bread from a baker on Tanner's Row whose daughter had sent word ahead that I should be fed. The free papers rode sewn into my coat lining, next to the ruined seal. On my belt, striking my thigh gently at each step like a slow heartbeat, hung the weight.

At the top of Quay Street I stopped and looked back once. Kingsford lay spread below me in the raw spring light, chimneys smoking, cranes swinging, the river running fast and free between the quays with the last of the ice riding out to sea. A town rebalancing its books. A town that had offered me a place in its new column and would go on fine without me, which was, I understood at last, the best thing a town could do for a man.

Thirty pounds lighter and infinitely richer, I turned south, toward Coldharbour, toward a counting-house with a clock that needed winding one last time — and found, leaning against the milestone at the crossroads, arms folded, watching the road north as if she'd been waiting since dawn, a girl with flour still dusting the shoulders of her traveling coat.

Sela looked me up and down, taking inventory: the pack, the papers hidden somewhere no dog would find them, the weight on my belt. Her eyes stopped there, and softened.

"So it came home," she said.

"It came home," I said.

She pushed off the milestone and fell into step beside me, facing south, and together we started down the river road, the thaw-water roaring in the fields on either side like the whole world counting itself out loud.
