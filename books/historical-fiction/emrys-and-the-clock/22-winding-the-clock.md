## 22. Winding the Clock

The road into Coldharbour wore a different face in the green month than it had in the white one, and Mirabel walked it slowly enough to learn every feature of that new face. Where the drifts had stood chest-high in winter there were now ditches running loud with snowmelt gone to seed, and the alder thickets that had been only black strokes on the horizon stood out in full leaf, shaking their small leaves at him like a crowd of children who could not decide whether to laugh or scold. The river itself was the greatest change. It had shed its ice entirely; it ran brown and muscular and full of itself, shouldering past the town with the swagger of something that knew it had broken a checkpoint, drowned a ferry, and remade the map in the space of a single night.

He came down the last hill above the town at late afternoon, when the light lay long and gold across the water, and stopped where the road bent to look.

Coldharbour looked smaller than he remembered. That was the first thing. In all the months away — through Marrow Bridge and Ferren Gate, the bothy, the weir, the long gray corridors of the magistrate's chambers — the town had grown enormous in his mind, a fortress, a prison, a weight pressing down on the whole geography of his life. Now it sat along its bank like any other river town: two hundred rooftops, three chimneys smoking, the masts of the pearling boats standing bare in their spring moorings, and above them all the counting-house, its slate roof catching the sun, its regulator clock's gilded face just visible in the cupola window.

The clock. He could not read the hands from this distance, but he found himself calculating anyway — an old habit, Halvard's gift to him, arithmetic as constant as breathing. If the clerks still wound it, it kept true time. If they did not, it had been drifting for weeks, losing a minute here, a minute there, the way everything in Coldharbour must be drifting now that the company that owned the town was being taken apart bolt by bolt in Kingsford.

He touched the diving weight at his belt. It was a small gesture, half unconscious, that he had made ten thousand times since Kingsford — the way another man might touch a crucifix, or the empty place where one used to hang. His father's weight. Bought back from Pell's pawnshop for eleven crowns and six shillings of restitution money, which meant that the Verrin Trading Company, in its death, had paid for the return of the one object it had ever truly failed to own.

*You carried me,* he thought to the river, to his father somewhere in the sound of it. *Now I carry you.*

And he went down into the town.

* * *

They knew him, of course. Word traveled upriver faster than any boat. But knowing him and knowing what to do with him were different matters, and Mirabel felt the difference in every doorway he passed. A woman gathering washing paused with a wet sheet dripping in her arms and looked at him the way people look at weather they have heard forecast but never seen. Two boys fishing off the landing stage stood up so fast their lines went slack. Old Maren, who had sold his father rope and resin for fifteen years, came out of her shopfront and stood in the road watching him pass, and did not speak, and did not need to; her eyes went to the weight on his belt, then back to his face, and she nodded once, slowly, the nod you give at a graveside.

No one said, *indenture's ended, hasn't it.* No one asked what he was doing back. The papers from Kingsford had come upriver a fortnight before him — depositions, seizures, the names read out in the market square by a crown officer with a cold in his nose — and the town had spent those weeks learning to think of itself as unowned, which is a harder adjustment for a place than for a person. They were all of them, that afternoon, standing in the wreckage of an order and wondering what time felt like when no one else set it.

The counting-house doors stood shut. Of course they did. The seal on the lock was new wax, crown yellow, and beneath it the old company stamp showed through like a bruise under skin. Mirabel stood before the doors a long moment, hand raised to knock, and heard — faintly, through oak and iron — a sound he would have known blindfolded, underwater, in his sleep.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

The regulator was still running.

He knocked. He waited. He knocked again, and this time there were footsteps, unhurried, and the door opened on a chain to the width of a hand, and one eye regarded him: a young man's eye, red-rimmed, exhausted, belonging to a clerk whose name Mirabel half remembered from the old days — Tice? Tuller? — one of Halvard's junior copyists, once.

When the eye recognized him it widened, and the chain came off fast.

"They said you'd died," the clerk blurted, and then, mortified, "that is — the blizzard — Mr. Grell reported—"

"Grell lied." Mirabel said it gently. "To buy me time. You'll be hearing more about that in Kingsford. Most of it will be unfair."

The clerk — Tuller, it was Tuller — swallowed and stepped back to let him in. "I don't know if I'm meant to admit anyone. The order says the premises are closed pending—" He gestured vaguely at the world. "But it doesn't say anything about the clock."

"No," Mirabel said. "It wouldn't."

* * *

Inside, the counting-house smelled the way it had always smelled — tallow and ink and cold slate and, underneath, the faint river-damp that got into the bones of every building in Coldharbour — but emptier. The long desks where forty clerks had once scratched in unison stood bare, their inkwells dry, their ledgers crated and stenciled EVIDENCE — KINGSFORD in a hand that took no pleasure in its work. The great iron safe hung open and gutted. Dust had begun its patient settlement on everything, fine and gray, the first snowfall of a new kind of winter.

Only the clock lived.

It dominated the end wall the way it always had: the regulator, seven feet of mahogany and brass and hand-blown glass, Master Verrin's father's pride, older than the company's charter, older than Mirabel. Its pendulum swung in its case with the slow golden patience he had watched since he was small enough to stand on a stool to wind it, and its tick filled the emptied hall the way a heartbeat fills a quiet room — so that the emptiness was not silence but a held breath.

"You've been winding it," Mirabel said.

Tuller flushed. "Someone had to. There's a clause — I found it in the seizure schedule — the clock is listed as a fixture, not an asset, so it isn't impounded, but nobody thought to stop it either, and it seemed..." He faltered, looking for the word, and found the wrong one first. "Disrespectful. To let it run down."

Mirabel looked at him. Young men did not wind clocks out of disrespect-avoidance. Young men wound clocks because the ticking was the only voice left in a building full of ghosts, and stopping it would have been a second, smaller death.

"How long has it been since you worked here alone?" Mirabel asked.

"Nine days. Everyone else gave notice when the pay stopped. Someone ought to lock up properly, sign things over, keep the rain off the records until the commissioners collect them." Tuller rubbed his eyes. "I keep meaning to leave. I keep winding the clock instead."

"I know how that goes," Mirabel said.

And then, because some duties do not ask permission, he crossed the floor, took the winding key from its hook inside the case — it hung exactly where it had hung for thirty years, on a nail worn bright by generations of hands — and opened the long door.

The smell of the mechanism rose to meet him: oil and warm brass and the ghost of Master Verrin's pipe smoke, sunk forever into the wood of the case. He fitted the key to the great wheel. For a moment he simply stood with his hand on it, feeling through the metal the steady resistance of the mainspring, the machine's whole stored patience coiled and waiting, and he understood — not for the first time, but fully now, the way you understand a thing only after it has nearly killed you — why the company had built its whole dominion around this one object. Time, governed. Minutes, owned. Every worker's day divided and sold by the tick. He had wound this clock as an indentured boy and felt the winding as service, as belonging, as the small reliable goodness in a hard world. He wound it now as a free man, and the sensation was the same in his wrist and utterly different in his chest.

He gave it eight turns, no more, no less — precision was dignity, his father had taught him with a pearl between finger and thumb, and Halvard had taught him again with figures on a slate — and closed the long door, and hung the key on its nail.

"There," he said quietly. "That's another week."

"It'll run down again," Tuller said.

"Yes."

"And then?"

Mirabel considered the question honestly, because it deserved honesty. The building was closing. The company was dead. Somewhere in Kingsford a commissioner was deciding whether the counting-house should become a customs post, a granary, a school. Whatever came next, it would not need a regulator governing anyone's minutes; the new order, whatever else it was, did not measure men's lives by the tick.

"Then someone winds it again," he said. "Or doesn't. That's the difference, Tuller. Before, nobody got to choose. Now somebody will." He put his hand flat against the case one last time, feeling the vibration like a living thing under the wood. "Leave it running. Whoever comes next should hear it and know the place was kept."

Tuller nodded, and something in his shoulders loosened, as if he had been given permission he had not known he was waiting for.

At the door, Mirabel paused. "There was a clerk here," he said. "Halvard Grell. His rooms are above the sail-loft, if he left anything—"

"He didn't leave anything," Tuller said. "That's the strange part. When the rest of us cleared our desks, his was already clean. Except—" the clerk hesitated, then reached under the nearest desk and brought out a single object, wrapped in oilcloth. "Except this. He left it addressed. It says *for M. Amberdale, when he comes.* I've been keeping it. I wasn't sure you'd... I wasn't sure."

Mirabel took the package. It had weight, but not much. Small things. He had learned, years ago on the river ice, to measure his life in what could be carried, and his hands judged this parcel instantly, expertly, the way they had once judged pearls: something flat, something bound, something that rattled very slightly against paper.

He unwrapped it there in the doorway, with the clock ticking behind him.

A slate. One of Halvard's teaching slates, the wooden-framed kind, scrubbed pale with use. And written on it in Halvard Grell's upright, meticulous, unmistakable clerk's hand — the hand that had taught Mirabel his letters across four winters — was a single line of arithmetic:

*One boy. One truth. One debt. Paid.*

Below it, smaller: *Wound the clock every Sunday. Thought of you. — H.G.*

Mirabel stood a long time with the slate in his hands while the regulator counted out the seconds behind him, and the river counted its own below the windows, and he did not try to say anything, because there was nothing the size of what he felt.

Grell was in Kingsford still, awaiting trial beside the directors he had served and deceived. Refusing counsel. Refusing excuses. Mirabel had testified for him already — would testify again, as many times as they asked — but testimony could not unwrite twenty years of silence, and Mirabel did not want it to. Some debts were paid in freedom papers. Some were paid in standing trial with your spine straight. He turned the slate over. The back was blank.

He wrapped it again, carefully, in its oilcloth, and put it in his pack beside Sela's bread.

* * *

He did not stay the night in Coldharbour. There was nothing to stay for and much to walk toward, though if you had pressed him on what, precisely, waited down the river road south, he would have found the question charming and unanswerable. Sela was in Kingsford — her father had taken a stall there, she wrote, in a round confident hand that made Mirabel smile every time; the bread in his pack was from a Kingsford oven but she called it *bridge bread, for luck, eat it before Ferren Gate or it doesn't count*, and he had learned better than to argue with Sela about ritual. He was going south to see her. After that: the coast, perhaps. The pearl grounds his father had worked, now under new lease, taking divers on honest terms at honest shares. Or not the pearls. Perhaps something that never once required going under the ice. The point, he had discovered, was not the destination. The point was that for the first time in his life the choosing was his, and he intended to spend a season doing nothing but exercising the muscle.

But first — one more errand.

The river path behind the counting-house led north along the bank for half a mile to the divers' chapel, low and stone and older than the trade itself, and Mirabel walked it in the last of the light. Inside, the memorial wall ran the length of the nave, every name of every diver lost to the river cut into the soft gray stone, and his father's name sat where it had always sat, third row, between Olem Barrow and the boy Anse who had been fourteen. Mirabel had found it in the spring, on his way to Kingsford, and had stood before it unable to pray. He stood before it now and found that he could.

Not a prayer, exactly. More an accounting — and he heard Halvard's wry voice in that, *everything's bookkeeping, boy, even grief* — rendered simply:

Father. Your name is cleared. The men who ruined you are ruined themselves, properly, by law instead of by river. Your weight is on my belt. Your letter did what you died refusing to do: it kept me from diving contraband, in the end, because it taught me what refusal costs and what it buys. I am going south. I don't know yet what I'll carry for a living. But I know I'll carry it myself.

He laid two fingers on the carved name, briefly, the way you touch the shoulder of a sleeping man you do not wish to wake, and went out.

The evening was turning violet over the water. At the landing stage the pearling boats knocked gently together, and somewhere upstream a loon called, that wild rising laugh, and Mirabel stood a moment listening to the two clocks — the brass one behind him in the darkening counting-house, faithful to the second, and the brown one in front of him, faithful to nothing but its own vast strength — and understood at last that he had spent his whole life believing these were enemies, order and flood, ledger and river, when in truth they had only ever been asking him the same question: *what will you carry, and who chose it?*

He settled his pack. He checked the bread without quite meaning to — habit, comfort, Sela's voice in his head saying *before Ferren Gate, it doesn't count otherwise*. He touched the weight at his belt, and the slate through the canvas, and the knife, and the folded freedom papers in his breast pocket with the crown seal that had cost the company everything and him almost as much.

Then Mirabel Amberdale, freeholder of his own name, heir to a diving weight, a slate, and a loaf, turned his face south on the river road, and walked into the green evening at exactly the pace he pleased.
