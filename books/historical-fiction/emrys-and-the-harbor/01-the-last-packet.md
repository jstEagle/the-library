## 1. The Last Packet

The colors came down at dawn, and Orsen Crane did not look at them.

She made herself study the gangplank instead—the warped pine boards, the rope railing stiff with river frost, the tally clerk's breath clouding as he counted bodies like sacks of grain. Eleven passengers for the *Meridian*'s spring run downriver. Twelve, when a latecomer came clattering over the quay with his heels ringing on the stone. Orsen stood at the rail of the queue with her collar up and her papers inside her coat, where her hand kept drifting to touch them and she kept pulling it away.

Behind her, above the customs house of Vessany's last loyal river town, the flag was already halfway down its halyard. She could see it without looking. She could feel it, the way one feels weather coming—a pressure in the old scar along her ribs, a taste like coins at the back of her tongue. The blue field with its silver weir, stitched by her mother's hands thirty years ago out of cloth meant for a funeral banner, sliding down the pole in the gray light while a victors' officer stood beneath it with his hands clasped behind him and his face arranged into something that was not quite a smile.

She looked at the gangplank. One board. Then another.

"You there. Papers."

Orsen produced them with the flat, unhurried motion she had practiced in stable lofts and station latrines for six years: the travel warrant first, then the work card, then the exemption chit, fanned like a gambler's modest hand. The clerk took them without ceremony. His fingers were red with cold. Somewhere behind him the flag reached the bottom of its pole and the officer said something to a soldier, and there was a small sound—cloth folding, or boots shifting—and Orsen fixed her eyes on a knot in the gangplank wood and did not flinch.

"Crane, is it. Orsen Crane. Deck class?"

"That's right."

"Purpose of travel."

"Carpentry work. Harbor district." Her voice sat where she had trained it to sit, low and unremarkable, a voice like a door left half shut. "There's rebuilding down there. They say there always will be now."

The clerk grunted at that, because it was true, and truth was the best mortar for any lie. He stamped her warrant against his knee and handed the papers back without meeting her eyes, and Orsen Crane walked up the gangplank into the belly of the packet boat *Meridian*, and the republic of Vessany ended behind her without her watching it go.

* * *

The dispatches rode against her left side, between the coat lining and the shirt beneath, sewn there by her own hand in a rented room whose window had looked out on a brick wall. Four packets, oilskin-wrapped, each no thicker than a prayer book. Sixty-one stitches held them. She had counted them as she sewed, because counting was what you did instead of screaming. Three hundred and forty pages of orders, ledgers, and correspondence, microcopy pressed onto film and folded into waxed paper—the true account of how the peace had been sold, and by whom, and for what. It weighed less than a loaf of bread. It had cost more lives than she could count without stopping.

A deckhand showed the deck-class passengers their places: benches along the covered midsection, straw pallets in the after hold for those who could afford nothing better, a stove at the center that would be lit only when the river fog came off in earnest. Orsen chose the bench farthest from the stove and nearest the rail, back to the bulkhead, so that no one could come at her left side without her seeing it. Old habits. Older than the war.

The *Meridian* cast off at the third bell. Her engine turned over with a sound like a giant clearing his throat, and the quay began to slide away, and Orsen took out the little knife she carried and the stick of birchwood she had cut at the last waystation, and she began to whittle.

This was the discipline of it. Hands busy, eyes down, mouth shut. A man who whittled was a man who wanted nothing from anyone. A man mending his own seams asked no questions and answered fewer. She had crossed three hundred miles of occupied country on the strength of busy hands—mending a harness here, patching a tarpaulin there, becoming useful and invisible in the same motion. Usefulness was the best disguise ever devised. It gave people no reason to look closer, and every reason to look away.

Around her the other passengers arranged themselves according to the ancient arithmetic of decks. A family of five from the mill towns spread their belongings in a territory marked by a grandmother's cane laid across the boards. Two clerks in good coats argued quietly over a newspaper four days old. A drover slept sitting up with his hat over his eyes. And at the rail near the bow, a woman in a gray traveling dress stood watching the receding town with her hands folded at her back, straight-backed and still as a figurehead.

The flag was gone now. In its place, at the top of the customs house, a plain white banner with a black bar—the victors' interim mark, the color of surrender paperwork. Orsen saw it reflected once in the brass fittings of the rail and then the town swung behind a bend of the bluff and was gone, all of it, the whole gray smudge of it, and the river opened ahead the color of gunmetal streaked with milk.

She whittled. The birch curling away under the blade in pale ribbons. She was making—she had decided some hours ago—a fish. It was going to be a poor fish. It didn't matter. The point was the blade's rhythm, the small resistance of the wood, the shavings collecting in the seam between her boot and the deck planking. The point was that her hands had work, so that the rest of her could stand very still inside and watch the dread pass through her like weather through a shuttered house.

Because the dread was there. She would not insult herself by pretending otherwise. It sat in her chest, patient and enormous, like ballast. Not fear of dying—she had made her peace with that arithmetic years ago, in another life, in a uniform nobody would ever find record of. What she feared was smaller and worse: that she would fail, that the *Meridian* would be boarded somewhere below the falls, that sixty-one stitches would be picked open by someone else's fingers, and the truth would go into a victor's furnace unread. That everything—Vessany, the peace terms, the ministers' signatures, her mother's name, her own name—would end not with a shot but with an absence. A blank page where the record should have been.

They were counting on the blank page. That was the whole design of the peace. Burn the ledger, rewrite the census, call the dead casualties of their own treachery. She had seen the lists of proscribed names. Hers wasn't on them—that was the bitter joke—but the names of everyone who might remember why were.

So: Harborton. Neutral ground, or near enough, swollen past its docks with refugees and profiteers and the victors' new administrators all jostling for space in streets built for a tenth of them. And in Harborton, the harbor archive—a fat, stubborn, centuries-old institution that had survived three wars by refusing to notice them—and in the archive, a deposition office that still, if Bess's information held, accepted testimony from anyone who walked in with a story and a name. Timestamped. Sealed. Public record.

Get the dispatches into that office before the ice broke and the crossings closed, and the truth would exist somewhere no army could burn fast enough. That was the mission. No one had ordered it; the men who might have were dead or worse. She had promoted herself. It seemed fitting. The republic's last self-appointed officer, carrying its last honest paperwork, disguised as a carpenter going where the rebuilding was.

The fish was taking shape. Sort of. She held it up, squinted at it, and shaved down what was threatening to become a second tail.

"You're wasting good wood," said a voice above her. "That's a pike. I can tell by the jaw. Pike are all jaw."

* * *

The speaker was the drover with the hat over his eyes, except he wasn't sleeping after all, or hadn't been for a while. He was perhaps fifty, weathered to the color of his own luggage, with a gap in his teeth and eyes gone soft at the edges. He lowered the hat now and looked at her fish with the frank disappointment of a man reviewing a subordinate's work.

"I was going for trout," Orsen said.

"Trout don't have jaws like that. Trout are shy." He leaned over, plucked a shaving off her knee, rolled it between finger and thumb. "Birch. You want willow for fish. Birch for birds." He settled back, put the hat over one eye this time instead of both, and appeared to consider the conversation closed.

Orsen returned to her carving. She felt the familiar tightening along her shoulders, the body remembering what the mind kept insisting was unnecessary: that attention was a debt and conversation was interest accruing. Every exchange was a door left open. Doors got walked through.

But the drover asked nothing further. That was the trick of men like him, she reminded herself—or rather, the grace of them. They talked because silence on a river boat for four days was a form of drowning, and having talked, they required nothing back. He dozed. She whittled. The *Meridian* ran the slow green channel between hills still holding their morning mist, and the fog came off the water in ropes, and around noon the deckhand lit the stove anyway because the cold had gotten into everyone's boots.

At the second bell of the afternoon, the family's grandmother produced bread and hard cheese and a jar of pickled things, and began dealing portions around her territory with the ruthless precision of a quartermaster. Orsen watched this the way she watched everything—sidelong, cataloguing—until the old woman's eyes met hers, sharp as a tack through cataract haze, and the old woman said:

"You. Carpenter. You've got hungry hands. Come here."

"I'm not—"

"It's bread, not a marriage contract."

And there was nothing to do but cross the deck and take the bread, because refusing would have been more remarkable than accepting, and being remarkable was death. So Orsen sat among strangers for the first time in longer than she could reckon and ate black bread with pickled cabbage, and the grandmother—Marta, she was called, though she pronounced it as though daring anyone to shorten it—asked her exactly two questions: whether she had people downriver (no), and whether she could fix a wagon axle (probably). Both answers satisfied her. She dealt Orsen a second helping of cheese as wages for the probably, and returned to her grandchildren.

It should have been nothing. It was bread. It happened on ten thousand decks on ten thousand boats every day of every year, and meant nothing to anyone.

Orsen sat with the crust in her hand and found she could not finish it.

Because it had been kind. That was all. Undeserved, uncalculated, extended by a woman who owed her nothing and expected nothing back, and Orsen had been living so long outside the economy of kindness that the simple weight of it in her palm nearly undid her there on a public bench between the stove and the rail. When had someone last handed her food? She ran the ledger backward: the waystation woman at Kessel Ford, eight days ago, and before that—before that the memory went gray, not because it was lost but because she had banked the fire of it deliberately, because remembering warmth was dangerous for a person in her trade. Warmth made you turn your head. Turning your head made you slow. Slow got you taken at a checkpoint with sixty-one stitches in your coat.

She ate the bread. It tasted like almost nothing and like everything, and she thanked Marta with a nod that cost her more than any forged signature ever had, and went back to her bench and her terrible fish.

The river widened below the bluffs. Toward evening the light went long and coppery, and the two clerks stopped arguing, and the children fell asleep in a heap like puppies, and the drover snored gently under one eye of his hat. Orsen carved until her thumb ached, and then she mended the second seam of her right cuff with thread she'd waxed herself, and then, when the dark came down fully and the passengers were shapes and breathing around the stove's red glow, she allowed herself, briefly, privately, to stop performing.

No one knew who she was.

The thought arrived the way it always did, uninvited, and hit the same old bruise. Not merely that no one knew her secret—that was the design, that was safety—but the larger, quieter fact underneath it: that there existed no living person to whom Orsena Crane was a real name. Her mother dead eleven years. Her brother at the bottom of the Weir Channel with the gunboats, unmarked. Her unit scattered, her handlers burned one by one down the chain like lamps going out in a long corridor, until the corridor was dark and she was the last lamp, walking. The woman on the bench whom Marta had fed was a carpenter named Orsen Crane, registered nowhere, rooted nowhere, a fiction with a knife and a mending kit. If the *Meridian* sank tonight—and packets sank on the spring river every year, ice-out was treacherous, everyone knew it—there would be no one in the world to grieve anything but the bread.

She let the feeling pass through, because she had learned to, the way you let a cramp pass—don't fight it, don't feed it, breathe. Above the deck canopy the stars came out hard and cold over the water. Somewhere behind them, upriver, the republic's archives were burning or being sorted for burning, and its ministers were signing whatever they were told to sign, and its last flag was folded in a victor's trunk.

Not all of it, she told the dark. Not the part that matters.

She wrapped the finished fish—it had ended up, unmistakably, a pike—in a rag, sewed it into the pocket of her kit bag where she kept her needle case, and lay down on the bench with her coat buttoned to the chin and her left side toward the bulkhead, and slept the shallow, listening sleep of the professional.

* * *

Twice in the night the boat stopped—at a blackout landing to take on coal, and once for no reason she could determine, just the engine dropping to a mutter and the hull knocking gently against pilings while voices murmured somewhere forward. Each time she surfaced from sleep with her hand already moving to the coat lining, checked the stitches through the wool with her fingertips—sixty-one, all present—and sank back.

Toward the coldest hour, they stopped again, and this time did not start again.

Orsen came awake all the way and stayed still. Lantern light moved beyond the canvas side-curtains. Boots on the deck. The family's grandmother stirred; the drover swore softly in his sleep. Out on the water, oars—not the engine's wake, oars—and the flat voice of the pilot calling numbers into the dark.

Then, from the forward deck, the deckhand's voice, resigned and carrying: "Census man coming aboard. Mid-river transfer out of Brayford. He'll be updating manifests, so have your papers to hand come morning."

Footsteps passed close—close enough that Orsen could hear the particular sound of them, unhurried, deliberate, the tread of a man who counted things for a living. There was a pause at the curtain nearest her bench. A shadow fell across the thin stripe of lantern light at the deck edge, paused there a moment, and moved on.

Orsen lay with her heart working steadily against her ribs and her fingertips resting on the eleventh stitch, and told herself it meant nothing. Manifests were updated on every run. Census men were vermin on every route; the victors had doubled them all, hunting draft-dodgers and deserters and unregistered persons, which categories contained, between them, most of the surviving population of Vessany, including her.

Her papers were perfect. That was the trouble with perfect papers, she thought, as the engine finally woke and the *Meridian* slid back into the current—perfect papers were their own kind of flag to the right pair of eyes.

She did not see the census taker in the dark. But she felt, absurdly, irrationally, the way one feels a change in weather through an old wound, that the shadow at the curtain had paused a half-second too long.

Morning, and papers to hand. She began rehearsing her biography against the ceiling of the canopy, syllable by syllable, like a rosary.

Orsen Crane. Carpentry work. Harbor district.
