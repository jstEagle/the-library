## 2. A Man of Few Answers

The Meridian carried thirty-one souls below her decks, and Orsen Crane had counted them twice before the first full day was out.

It was not suspicion that made him count. It was habit—the old habit, drilled into him by men whose names he could no longer say aloud without risking something. Know your exits. Know your numbers. Know which sleepers might wake hungry and which might wake curious. He had learned it in a courtyard in Vessany's capital under a sky the color of gunmetal, and he carried it now the way other men carried prayer: silently, compulsively, without expectation of answer.

Thirty-one. Eleven crew, twenty passengers, himself among them. A grain merchant with soft hands and a loud voice. Two brothers from the mill towns going north for work, their knuckles split and scabbed. A widow and her sister traveling with a caged thrush that sang at dawn and made Orsen's chest ache in ways he could not afford to examine. An elderly pair who spoke to no one, played cards with each other, and lost graciously. And a dozen others whose faces he catalogued as a quartermaster might catalogue stores—so much risk here, so much danger there, marked and shelved.

The packet was small, built for cargo and mail rather than comfort, and the passenger cabin smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and the river itself—that gray-green breath of thawing water and silt that got into everything. Bunks lined the walls in double tiers, canvas stretched over slats, close enough that a man could hear his neighbor swallow. There was no privacy aboard the Meridian. There was only the fiction of it: curtains that didn't quite close, corners where a body could turn away, the long pretense that forty people breathing in one room were somehow alone.

Orsen had lived inside fictions for eleven years. He knew how to make one hold.

* * *

The trick, he had found long ago, was to keep the hands busy.

Idle hands invited eyes. A man who sat staring at nothing became a question—*what is he thinking, why does he look so far away, what's wrong with him?* But a man mending a seam or shaping a stick of wood into something small and useless was a closed door. People respected busy hands. They assumed a working man wanted nothing from them but to be left at his work.

So Orsen mended. His coat, his spare shirt, a strap on his satchel that needed no mending—he stitched it anyway, carefully, badly enough that no one would think to ask where a man had learned such fine work. Because they wouldn't ask about the stitching itself. Men did not ask men about needlework; their eyes slid off it, embarrassed or uninterested, and that sliding was exactly what Orsen needed. The seam that would have drawn attention to a woman passed unremarked on a man. One more small mercy of the disguise, purchased at the price of never being seen.

In the afternoons, when the light through the deck hatch went gold and the river ran wide and slow, he whittled. He had taken up a length of soft pine from the woodbox near the stove and was making nothing—a boat, maybe, or just the shape of a boat emerging from the grain—and the knife moved in short, sure strokes while the water slid past the ports and the republic fell away behind him mile by mile.

He tried not to think about the colors coming down.

He failed, mostly. Every time the packet rounded a bend and he caught a glimpse of some riverside town—smoke rising straight from chimneys that had flown Vessany's blue and silver since before his grandfather was born—his mind supplied the image unbidden: the flag hauled down by men who had not earned the right, folded or burned or trampled, and another banner run up in its place, black and iron-gray, crisp and wrong. It was happening all along the river. It would keep happening long after the Meridian reached Harborton. Whole histories were being taken down like laundry, and he was carrying the only clean copy of one small corner of it, sewn against his ribs.

Sometimes, when the engine's rhythm softened and the other passengers dozed, he could feel the dispatches against his side like a second heartbeat. Thirty pages of oiled paper, folded small, wrapped in waxed linen, stitched into the lining of his coat with thread pulled from his own spare shirt. He had done the sewing himself in a privy in the last free city, by the light of a borrowed match, sweating though the night was cold. If the coat was taken from him, he was dead. If the coat was searched, others died with him. If he simply stopped walking, sat down somewhere, let the river take him—well. Then the truth would drown quietly, and no one would ever have to know what cowards the ministers of Vessany had been.

That last thought came to him most often in the dark hours, and it frightened him more than the victors' patrols did. Not the fear of dying for the dispatches. The fear of wanting to.

* * *

The grain merchant's name was Hobb, and he was incapable of silence.

Orsen marked him early as harmless—vain, garrulous, a man who talked to fill the room because an empty room reminded him of his own unimportance—but harmlessness in a close space has its own dangers, the way a leak is dangerous not for its size but for its persistence. Hobb dripped questions. He asked the mill brothers about wages and the widow about her late husband's trade and the old card players whether they preferred the mountains or the coast, and every answer he harvested he replanted immediately as new conversation, a farmer of other people's lives.

Orsen kept to his bunk corner, kept his hands moving, and waited for the drip to find him.

It took until the second evening.

"Crane." Hobb settled onto the bench across from him uninvited, a tin cup of something spirits-adjacent balanced in one soft hand. "That's the name, yes? Crane. Funny, you don't talk like a clerk."

"I wasn't aware clerks talked a particular way."

"Oh, they do. All ink and apology." Hobb grinned, pleased with himself. He was the kind of man who thought teasing was intimacy. "You've got the hands for it, mind. Ink-stained. But you sit like a soldier."

"A man can sit however he likes on his own coin."

"So it *is* your coin." Hobb leaned back, satisfied at having extracted this much. "Good. I like to know who I'm sharing a wall with. So—where from, then? Before?"

There it was. The first real question, delivered with a smile, floating on a cup of liquor, entirely innocent and entirely lethal.

Orsen let three seconds pass. He had learned that a pause read as reluctance, and reluctance read as honesty, whereas an instant answer read as rehearsed—and rehearsed was memorable, and memorable was fatal. So he frowned slightly, as if the past were a place he visited reluctantly, and said:

"Coldharbour. Upstream. Small place—you'd not know it."

"Coldharbour," Hobb repeated, tasting it. "Registry town? Had cousins through there once. Paper mills?"

"Tanneries."

"Huh." Hobb sipped. "And your trade before the war took the men off?"

"Clerk. Weighmaster's office, then the provost's ledger room." This much was true, or true-shaped; the details had been built years ago, stone by stone, until the false Orsen Crane stood in Orsen's mind almost solidly enough to be mistaken for a man. "When they conscripted the office staff I took the exemption exam. Failed it. Weak lungs." He coughed then, briefly, dryly—a flourish he disliked using but had learned to deploy like a card. "They didn't want me. I signed on as a tally clerk with a shipping concern instead. Numbers are safer than bullets."

"They are that." Hobb nodded along, comfortable now, the story settling over him like a blanket. "Weak lungs, eh. This river air'll either fix you or finish you. My grandfather swore by—" and he was off, into grandfathers and remedies and the price of grain downstream, and Orsen nodded in the right places and felt the familiar dull relief of a lie well received.

That was the thing no one warned you about lying well: it worked, and the working of it was the loneliest sensation in the world. Every successful falsehood built the false Crane higher and buried the true one deeper. Somewhere behind his breastbone, under the sewn dispatches, under the flat chest and the bound days and the lowered voice practiced into gravel, there was a woman named— 

He shut the thought like a door. He always did. But doors wear thin with use.

* * *

The kindness came on the third morning, and it nearly undid everything.

They had tied up overnight at a waystation because of ice floes coming down from the upper reaches, and the cabin had gone cold enough that breath showed indoors. Orsen woke stiff, jaw clenched from a night of dreams he would not permit himself to remember—dreams of a house with a blue door, of a woman's voice calling a name that was hers and no one else's—and he lay still, cataloguing sounds, counting souls out of pure reflex.

Twenty-nine in the cabin. Two missing. He located them by ear: the mill brothers on deck, smoking. Fine. He rose, rolled and strapped his bedding with efficient movements, splashed water from the basin, and was reaching for his coat when he realized the widow's boy—no, not the widow's; the sister's, the thrush woman's nephew, a child of perhaps seven who had boarded at the last landing and whom Orsen had somehow failed to count—

The realization hit like cold water. He had miscounted. For the first time in eleven years, asleep and unguarded, he had *miscounted.*

He must have made some sound, because the widow looked up from her mending and said, "Sit, love. You're gray as the ice out there." And before he could refuse, she had poured him a cup of tea from the communal pot—weak, stewed, terrible—and pressed it into his hands, and added, unasked, unpurchased, expecting nothing:

"There's honey in my bag. My sister brings it from her bees. You look like a man who's forgotten what sweet is."

Orsen held the cup and could not move.

It was nothing. It was less than nothing—a spoonful of honey from a stranger, tea already brewing for everyone, a term of endearment aimed at a tired traveler. Women did such things a hundred times a day without weighing them. But Orsen had not been a woman in the sight of the world for eleven years, and in eleven years no one had called him *love*, no one had given him anything that was not a transaction, because men were not given things; men paid, men hauled, men kept to themselves. The kindness landed on him like rain on cracked ground—soaking in faster than he could control, finding every fissure, and he sat there on the bench with steam rising against his face and understood that if anyone asked him anything at all in the next ten seconds, anything, even his name, he might weep.

"Honey," he managed. His voice came out rougher than he'd intended. "I—thank you. That's kind."

"Eat, first. Talk after." She fetched the jar herself, opened it, stirred it in without waiting for permission, and pushed the cup back toward him. Her hands were quick and brown, a working woman's hands, and there was flour ground into the creases still, or something like flour. "My mother always said grief travels lighter fed."

"Grief," Orsen repeated, carefully.

"You've got the look." She said it mildly, already half-returned to her mending, offering no intrusion in it—just acknowledgment, the way one acknowledges weather. "Half these people do. Half this whole river does, if you ask me. But you carry yours like it's got a name in it, if that makes any sense."

It made perfect sense and it terrified him.

He drank the tea. It was too hot and too sweet and he tasted salt in it that was not the pot's doing, and he kept his face turned toward the porthole and the gray ice-chunked water so that no one would see. The thrush sang in its cage two bunks down, bright and oblivious. The child—the seventh passenger he'd failed to count, the gap in his armor—chased a cork around the floorboards, laughing at nothing.

No one alive knows who I am, Orsen thought. The thought arrived whole, the way truths do, without being summoned. No one alive knows. Not the ministers, who knew only a courier's codename. Not the handler, dead now or turned. Not the victors hunting the dispatches, who hunted a shadow. Not this kind woman with her honey, who saw a gray-faced man and fed him. The last person who had known her—known the girl with the blue-door house and the name spoken softly in kitchens—had been her mother, and her mother had been in the ground four years, and there had been no letter, no marker, no mourning permitted, because mourners ask questions.

She had constructed a self so complete that even she could barely find it beneath. Orsen Crane, clerk, weak-lunged, from tannery Coldharbour. A man of few answers, because answers were graves.

The tea cooled in his hands. The widow hummed to her mending. And Orsen finished the cup to the dregs, every sweet drop, because refusing kindness seemed suddenly the most dangerous act of all—more dangerous than the patrol boats, more dangerous than the sewn pages against his ribs. Some part of him, starved eleven years, had decided that if this was the last warmth he ever accepted from another human being, he would at least accept all of it.

* * *

Near dusk the packet's engines changed their beat—a hesitation, then a slower churning—and voices stirred on deck. Orsen climbed the ladder into the wind and saw why.

Ahead, where the river narrowed between wooded bluffs, a small boat was putting out from a jetty: a government launch, riding low, carrying three figures in the iron-gray of the victors' administration. Even at that distance Orsen's eye picked out the ledger one of them held, and the folding desk, and the air of routine inspection that was somehow worse than menace because it meant systems, means, thoroughness.

"They'll want the manifest updated," the mate said beside him, chewing something. "New regulations. Every soul accounted for, papers checked mid-river now. Census business." He spat over the rail, unhappily. "Some clerk from the harbor district, by the look of it. Boarding at Tarn Reach."

A census taker.

Orsen stood at the rail with the wind pulling at his collar and watched the launch grow larger, and made himself breathe slowly, and did not touch the left side of his coat where thirty pages slept against his heart.

Below him, through the deck boards, he heard Hobb already asking someone, cheerfully, "—and where were you registered before the war, then?" and heard the mill brother laugh and answer, easy as breathing.

Ordinary questions. Ordinary lies. Orsen had answered them for eleven years without a tremor.

But as the launch came alongside and the gangway rattled down, and a slight, neat figure in a harbor-district coat stepped aboard with a ledger under one arm and an expression of quiet, methodical attention, Orsen felt—for the first time in eleven years—the specific cold of being exactly the kind of man who gets noticed.
