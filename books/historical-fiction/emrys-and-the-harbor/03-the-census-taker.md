## 3. The Census Taker

The Meridian came about mid-river to take on the census taker, and Orsen Crane watched from the rail as a small rowboat fought the current toward the packet's lowered gangway. The man in the stern sat very straight, an oilcloth satchel held flat on his knees like a sleeping animal he did not wish to wake. Even at fifty yards Orsen could see that he was not dressed for the river. His coat was harbor wool, gray and precise, buttoned to the throat, and his hat—the round-crowned kind worn by clerks and registrars—sat on his head with the particular squareness of a man who had never once thought about how his hat looked.

She turned back to her mending before he boarded. It was safer not to watch new passengers arrive. Watching invited being watched.

Emrys Vale felt the packet's deck roll beneath him like a living thing deciding whether to accept his weight, and he disliked it immediately and in silence. Twelve years in the harbor district of Harborton had made him a man of planks that did not move—pier decking, archive floors, the stone steps of the registrar's office worn into shallow bowls by a century of petitioners' feet. Water was for other men. His business was paper, and paper did not care whether the ground under it held still.

He introduced himself to the purser in the saloon, which smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and the ghost of a hundred dinners, and laid his credentials on the table with both hands, squaring them.

"Emrys Vale," he said. "Harborton district census office. Manifest verification, inbound passengers. There's a requisition order from the Commission on Records."

The purser, a heavy man with a face like a boiled ham, read the order twice without appearing to understand any of it, which Emrys had learned was the standard response of rivermen to anything bearing an official seal.

"Mid-river inspection," the purser said at last. "That's new."

"The war is over," Emrys said. "Everything is new." He did not say it unkindly, but the sentence landed on the room in a way he hadn't intended. The war was over—that was the whole trouble. Half the people on this packet were running from something that had ended and something that had begun, all at once, and Harborton's registers were about to receive all of it, name by name, whether the city wanted it or not.

He worked through the manifest cabin by cabin as the Meridian beat upstream against the current. The packet carried thirty-one souls: merchants going home with the peace in their pockets; a family of tanners from the burned districts with everything they owned in four bundles and a birdcage; two soldiers of the victorious Coalition traveling on passes so new the ink still looked wet. Emrys took each name, each origin, each stated purpose, entering them in his ledger in a hand so regular that his supervisor once remarked it looked less written than printed.

It was good work, or it had been. There was comfort in the ledger's columns, in the way a person could be reduced—kindly, he thought, usefully—to a line: name, place, purpose. Every line accounted for. No one lost in the margins.

He reached the name in the fourth column from the bottom and stopped.

*Orsen Crane. Occupation: clerk. Origin: Vessany, lower provinces. Purpose: employment, Harbor District.*

He read it again. Something in him—the part that had spent twelve years among registers, that could find any name in the harbor rolls the way a musician finds a note—leaned forward.

There was no such name.

Not *no such record*—that happened constantly, that was half the job, war swallowing paper the way fire swallowed houses—but no such name, in the deeper sense, the sense that lived below memory. Emrys Vale had handled the Vessany trade manifests for six years before the borders closed. He had copied names out of captains' lists until they ran through his head like a litany: Ferrow, Ansel, Duquesne, Marnett, Ivo, Pellam. He knew the shape of Vessany's naming, the clusters of families along the lower rivers, the way sons followed fathers into the shipping clerks' guilds. Crane was a harbor name, a northern name—and no Crane of the lower provinces had ever crossed his desk. Not once.

He closed the ledger gently, as though the thought might escape if he moved quickly, and went up on deck.

* * *

The passenger was at the rail amidships, mending a coat sleeve by the failing light. That alone was unusual. Men of Emrys's acquaintance did not mend; they wore things until the things gave up and then bought more things, or went threadbare with a certain stubborn pride. But this passenger sewed with a strange competence—a soldier's stitch, tight and quick, the kind of repair meant to survive hard use—and the coat itself was wrong in subtler ways. Cut for a bigger man. Shoulders slightly proud of the flesh inside them. Cuffs worn shiny at the outer edge where a wrist thinner than the sleeve's intention had rested on desks and rails for years.

Emrys had learned, in twelve years of census work, that bodies testified. Papers lied fluently, cheerfully, endlessly—he had seen forged exemptions of heartbreaking beauty—but bodies were poor liars. They gave themselves away in wrists and shoulders, in the way a person's weight settled, in hands. This man's hands were small and scarred across the knuckles, ink-stained at the right finger and calloused at the wrong places for a clerk, and they moved the needle like they had done it in the dark.

"Passenger Crane?" Emrys said.

The needle stopped. Not startled—stopped, deliberately, the way a clock is stopped, and in the pause before the face turned Emrys had the distinct impression of someone setting down one identity and picking up another.

"Yes." The voice was low, even, a voice kept carefully in its middle register. "Something wrong with my papers?"

"Nothing at all. Routine verification. The Commission requires updated manifests for all inbound traffic." He opened the ledger against the rail. "Name as registered: Orsen Crane. Is that correct?"

"It's what my mother called me." A thin smile, dry as old glue. It was the first thing resembling humor the man had offered anyone aboard, Emrys suspected, and it had been rationed like the last of the salt.

"And your prior registration? Where were you entered before the war?"

"Littell's Cross, on the Vessany side. Warehouse clerk for Corver & Sons." The answers came smoothly, unhurried, each one arriving exactly when expected, like cars of a train on a schedule. "Trade traffic down the river most years. Your predecessors will have seen the firm's manifests."

"They may have." Emrys wrote the answers down. He was aware, distantly, that he was performing the interview correctly, asking the correct questions in the correct order, and simultaneously that some other faculty in him—some quiet instrument behind his breastbone that had nothing to do with procedure—was taking different measurements altogether. "Duration of residence in Littell's Cross?"

"Six years."

"Family?"

"No family."

The word arrived without hesitation and yet somehow pre-built, ready-made, like furniture from a catalog. *No family.* Emrys wrote it down and found himself looking not at his ledger but at the man's face while he did it, which was against his own practice and knew it.

It was a forgettable face by design—medium coloring, medium build, the sort of face that dissolved the moment you looked away, that would defeat any identikit artist in the Coalition's service. But the eyes defeated the design. They were dark and quite still, and they held the specific patience of someone who has waited a long time in one place, watching a door.

Then the eyes met his, and the patience flickered—or rather, something behind it did, one quick movement, like a fish turning under ice. Recognition, Emrys thought. Not of him. Of what he was. The man had seen the ledger and known precisely what it meant, the way a deer knows a certain silhouette against a ridge, and in that shared instant Emrys understood that this stranger had spent the entire war being counted by somebody, somewhere, and had survived every counting.

"You'll forgive the questions," Emrys said, and heard how his voice had dropped half a tone without permission. "New regulations. Everyone's papers are being reborn, so to speak."

"I know what new governments do with lists." Crane said it lightly, but the lightness had edges. "I've been on enough of them."

"Haven't we all." Emrys squared the ledger. "That's all I need. You'll be entered clean at Harborton."

"Clean." Crane repeated the word as though tasting it for doneness, and found it slightly undercooked. "That would be a novelty."

Emrys moved aft to finish the third-class berths, and it took him four full cabins to admit what had happened back there at the rail: he had enjoyed it. Not the verification—verification was bread and water, twelve years of it—but the other thing, the instrument behind his breastbone taking its own soundings. He had gone twelve years without being surprised by a name. Now one name had walked up out of the river fog with no history attached to it whatsoever, wearing a coat cut for another man, sewing a soldier's stitch, looking at him with eyes that measured him right back, and Emrys Vale, forty-one years old, meticulous, unmarried, reliably unexcited, stood in a pitching corridor with a pen behind his ear feeling something adjacent to vertigo.

He finished the manifest by lamplight. When he came to the fourth column from the bottom he wrote *Crane, O.* in his fair copy with more pressure than the pen required, and the stroke went slightly too dark, and he looked at it the way a man looks at a crack in a wall he owns.

* * *

Supper was salted pork, hard bread, and coffee made from something that had never met a coffee bean. Emrys ate in the saloon with the tanner family, who talked endlessly and gratefully about Harborton—its neutrality, its fish market, an uncle who dealt in rope—and he answered in monosyllables and heard almost none of it. Across the compartment, Crane sat alone with his back to the bulkhead, eating in quick efficient bites, positioned—as Emrys now realized he had been positioned since boarding—with a wall behind him, the lamp between himself and the room, and sightlines to both doors.

Old habits, Emrys thought. Whatever else the man was, he was not merely a clerk from Littell's Cross.

After supper the packet ran into dusk and the dusk into fog, and the passengers thinned to their berths. Emrys stayed in the saloon with his ledger doing the night's fair copying, because the alternative was his berth and his thoughts, and his thoughts kept circling the same gap. He pulled from his satchel the one reference work he permitted himself on journeys—the *Consolidated Registry Index*, harbor edition, seven years out of date now and missing whole provinces of the war—but he consulted it anyway, running his thumb down the C volumes by lamplight while the river muttered under the hull.

Chabert. Chadron. Chalke. Charbonne. Cheverel. Clapper. Cormany. Cray.

No Crane. Not in the Vessany sections, not in the Coalition border districts, not anywhere he could find, and Emrys sat back and looked at the lamp flame and let the professional curiosity do what it always did, which was grow teeth. Because there were three kinds of missing names, and he knew them all. There were names lost to fire and shelling, the honest gaps. There were names suppressed—deserters, debtors, collaborators—which left scars in the paper trail, warrants and cancellations, marks in margins. And there was the third kind, rarest and strangest: names that had never existed at all, constructed whole, born in some back room with a forged baptism and a purchased stamp. The first kind broke his heart. The second kind he pursued. The third kind—

The third kind he had only ever read about, in training memos with titles like *Indicators of Fabricated Identity*, and every indicator on that old list was presently sitting at the rail of the Meridian, mending its cuff with a soldier's stitch.

Except that the list had never mentioned the eyes. Nobody trained you for the moment the fabricated name looked up and met you over the open ledger, and instead of fear you saw—what? Appraisal. Arithmetic. And underneath the arithmetic, so briefly he might have invented it, a tiredness so complete it looked geological, like something that had been carried so long it had become the landscape.

Emrys closed the index. He was aware that the correct course of action was simple: flag the entry, note the discrepancy, pass the sheet to the Commission's examiners at the harbor office, and wash his hands in the bureaucratic manner, thoroughly and with soap. That was the procedure. That was what the columns were *for*. A census taker who noticed things and declined to say so was worse than useless; he was a hole in the map.

And yet he found himself writing, in the margin of his working copy, in pencil, in his smallest hand, a single annotation that was not procedure at all:

*Papers genuine. Man is not.*

He looked at those four words for a long time. Then he blew out the lamp rather than erase them, which was a decision of a kind, and stood in the dark saloon listening to the river and the rigging and the far-off grinding of ice floes against the hull, and told himself that the notation meant nothing, that he simply preferred accurate statements even in pencil, that accuracy was the whole of his religion and he had never before been asked to choose between two accuracies.

* * *

Near midnight the fog thickened until the packet anchored midstream rather than risk the shallows, and the world contracted to lamplight and dripping cordage. Emrys came up for air and found the deck empty except for Crane, standing at the chain locker with his collar up, watching the fog eat the banks.

There was room enough to withdraw. Emrys stayed.

"Census taker," Crane said, without turning. It wasn't a greeting. It was identification, the way one identifies weather. "You work the harbor district?"

"Twelve years."

"Then you'll know it better than I'm going to." A pause, filled by water. "What's it like? Harborton. Now. With the peace."

Emrys considered the question honestly, which was his habit and his affliction. "Full," he said at last. "Half the world is arriving and the other half is filing reports about it. Refugees in the warehouses, victors' officials in the best rooms, and everyone waiting to see which kind of country the peace intends to be." He hesitated, then added, because the man had asked truly and deserved truth: "The harbor keeps better records than the armies do. Whatever actually happens in the next month, some version of it will end up in our archives. Paper is patient."

Crane turned then, and the lamplight from the companionway caught his face, and Emrys watched the words go into the man like coins into a deep well, listened for the splash, heard none.

"Paper is patient," Crane repeated softly. Something moved through the stillness of his expression—not a smile, but the place a smile would live if it ever got permission. "Yes. I'm counting on that."

"On what, may I ask?"

"Filing a complaint." The dry glue-smile again, briefer this time. "Against the century."

Emrys laughed—one short, surprised syllable of it, the first laugh he'd produced in company in longer than he cared to calculate—and saw the man register the laugh with something close to wonder, as though it were an artifact from a country long closed to travel. For a moment they simply stood there, the census taker and the non-existent clerk, two careful men in a fog, and the silence between them was not the silence of strangers.

Then Crane's gaze went past Emrys's shoulder, to the dark water, and sharpened by a degree.

"You didn't write down everything I told you," Crane said.

It was not a question, and Emrys, who lied badly and rarely, said nothing, which was answer sufficient.

"Good," said Orsen Crane quietly, to the fog more than to him. "Because I told you almost none of it."

He walked away along the deck, sure-footed in the dark, and left Emrys Vale at the chain locker with his heart behaving irregularly and his professional judgment in ruins, already composing—in defiance of every regulation he had served for twelve years—the questions he would ask tomorrow, beginning with the only one that mattered, the one he should have asked today and had somehow, mercifully, inexplicably saved:

*Who are you, really, Orsen Crane? And why does no registry in the world contain you?*
