## 4. One Question Too Many

The census taker's cabin aboard the *Meridian* was a converted storage locker no wider than a coffin stood on end, and Emrys Vale had made of it a small temple to the god of exactitude. Three ledgers lay open on his desk, weighted at the corners with brass ink pots. A lamp with a green glass shade threw its light downward in a cone, and within that cone everything was orderly: pens aligned like soldiers, blotting paper squared, a tin of spare nibs sorted by width. Beyond the cone—the whole restive world of the packet boat, forty-odd souls packed between cargo and river, breathing the same coal-smoked air.

He had been updating manifests since Kiln's Reach. It was dull work and he loved it the way some men love drink: it took him out of himself. Names, ages, points of origin, destinations. The river gave up its passengers one line at a time, and each line was a small door he could close behind him.

He had noticed Crane on the first day, in the way he noticed everything—peripherally, catalogued, filed. A tall passenger with careful hands, traveling alone, who kept to the leeward rail and mended his coat with stitches so fine they looked machine-made. Crane read little and watched much. He whittled without ever seeming to finish anything. He answered civilities with exactly as many words as courtesy required, never one more.

None of this was remarkable. Half the people on the river were fleeing something, and reticence was cheap currency in wartime. What was remarkable—and it had taken Emrys two days of turning pages before he understood that it was—was that Orsen Crane did not appear anywhere in his memory of registries.

Emrys had worked harbor-district census for nine years. He had copied, cross-checked, annotated, and archived the manifests of three separate administrations, through annexation and counter-annexation, through the border revisions that had redrawn half the map of Vessany with a ruler and red ink. Names moved through him like silt through a channel. He forgot faces; he almost never forgot entries. And there was something about the way certain names *sat* on a page—a texture to genuine registration, the smudged thumbprint of clerks, the crossed-out corrections, the marginal notes in four different hands—that forged or invented names never quite reproduced.

Crane, O., he had written on the manifest. Laborer. Origin: undeclared prior. Destination: Harborton.

*Origin: undeclared prior.* He had written it himself, in his own tidy hand, and then sat looking at it as though someone else had written it. Because he could not recall ever writing such a thing before. Passengers without papers were common enough; the war had stripped documents from people the way floodwater stripped fences. But those people were recorded differently—they were flagged, annotated, referred onward. They left wakes in the paper. Crane left nothing. It was as if the man had been decanted directly onto the packet, fully formed, out of nowhere at all.

Which, of course, happened sometimes. Which had a procedure. Which had a *form*, form 11-C, duplicate copy to the district office, and Emrys knew—he checked twice, that evening, going back through his own carbon book—that no such form existed for Orsen Crane.

So when the announcement came that the census office would conduct secondary verification at Brant's Landing, Emrys volunteered. The other clerks looked at him strangely; verification duty meant standing in the cold sampling wind off the water while farmers complained. But Emrys took his ledger and his pen and went out onto the main deck, where the passengers were formed into a shuffling line, and he called them forward one by one, and he closed doors behind each of them, name by name, until only one remained unclosed.

"Orsen Crane."

* * *

The man came forward with that unhurried economy Emrys had begun to think of as characteristic—no wasted motion, hands emerging from pockets, chin level. Up close he was younger than his bearing suggested, perhaps thirty, with wind-scoured cheeks and eyes the flat gray-green of river ice in February. His travel coat was good quality gone deliberately shabby, the elbows re-stitched, the cuffs turned. Everything about him said *I have chosen what you see.*

Emrys opened the ledger to the current page and set his pen against it and did not write.

"State your point of registration prior to the war," he said. It was the standard question. He asked it forty times a day. It came out of him with the smoothness of river water over stone.

Something passed across Crane's face—not fear, Emrys would swear later, not fear exactly. More like a shutter drawn halfway down a window. Light still came through, but slatted now, striped.

"Corvel," Crane said. "District registry at Corvel, before the annexation."

Corvel. Emrys turned back three pages in his mind without moving a muscle in his face. Corvel registry had been flooded in the second winter—the whole ground floor of the records hall, waist-deep in meltwater when the levee failed. Thousands of entries lost, transferred incompletely, reconstructed from memory and tax rolls. It was the single most convenient answer a person of uncertain origins could give on this stretch of the river. It was the answer that could not be checked.

And yet Crane had not hesitated. That was the thing. A liar caught without a prepared story fumbles, bluffs, reaches for the nearest plausible town. A liar *with* a prepared story delivers it too fast, too polished, the seams ironed flat. Crane had done neither. He had answered as a man answers his own name—wearily, factually, with the faint impatience of anyone asked to prove the sky is up. The answer fit him the way his coat fit him: worn smooth by use.

Emrys wrote: *Prior reg.: Corvel (records destroyed, ann. 2).* His pen moved evenly. Then, because the question was standard, he asked the follow-up that was also standard, the one printed in small type beneath the first:

"Family names registered at same address?"

"None living." A beat, precise as a metronome. "A mother. Died in the fever year. Registered death at Corvel, if any record survived the water."

"And your occupation prior to the war?"

"Clerk. Provisioning office." Crane's gaze came up from the ledger and met his. "Requisitions and stores. I can show you my hand if you doubt it—I wrote numbers all day for six years. My figures lean forward. Most clerks' lean back."

It was such an odd, specific, verifiable offer that Emrys nearly laughed. Instead he heard himself say, in a voice quieter than the official register of his questions, "Where were you when Vessany fell?"

The deck noise went on around them—the slap of water, the creak of the paddle housings, a child complaining somewhere aft—but between the two of them there was a pocket of stillness, like the eye of the wind under a bridge. Crane held his gaze. He did not look away, did not glance at the guards by the gangway, did not do any of the hundred small things a frightened man does. He simply looked at Emrys with those ice-colored eyes, and Emrys felt—as clearly as if a page had been lifted and held to the lamp—that the man was reading *him*: measuring what he was, what he wanted, what he might be dangerous with.

"The capital," Crane said finally. "I saw the colors come down on the ministry tower. I don't care to discuss it."

"No," Emrys agreed softly. "Few do."

He completed the entry. He initialed it. He gestured Crane back into the line with two fingers, the way he'd dismissed four hundred passengers before him, and Orsen Crane turned and walked aft with his careful stride, and the door closed behind him the way all the others had.

Except that it didn't.

* * *

That night Emrys could not sleep. The packet bucked and knocked against the current, and the lamp in the shared saloon guttered, and he sat with his personal notebook—the private one, the one with his observations that no administrator had ever demanded—open on his knee, doing what he always did when a record refused to settle: writing down the discrepancy until it stopped moving.

*Corvel,* he wrote. *Correct answer. Records destroyed, unverifiable. Mother dead, fever year—also unverifiable, also correct. Clerk, provisioning—verifiable in manner but not in fact. Every answer checks. Every answer is checkable-in-the-negative, i.e., cannot be confirmed, cannot be contradicted. This is either extraordinary luck or extraordinary preparation.*

Then, because honesty was a discipline like any other, he wrote the sentence he had been avoiding:

*Or the person has lived the answers so long that they are no longer answers at all.*

He thought about the way Crane had offered his handwriting as proof. Who does that? A forger offers samples with confidence; a genuine article offers them with indifference. Crane had offered them like a man offering the one true thing he had, the single coin he knew was not clipped. And the detail itself—figures leaning forward—was not a detail a person invents. It was a detail a person *has*. You cannot fake six years of numbers pressing on your wrist.

Everything about the man was like that. True details arranged in an untrue—or at least an unreadable—pattern. Emrys had spent nine years among refugees and deserters and widows with altered papers, and he had learned that most lies were lazy, most deceptions frayed at the edges within a day's questioning. Crane's edges were not frayed. Crane's edges were *overlocked*, finished with a precision that itself was the only flaw—because nothing born ordinary was finished so well.

He closed the notebook and pressed his palm flat against the cover, as if to keep the thoughts inside from escaping into the boat, into the wrong ears.

The trouble—the real trouble, and he admitted it to himself with professional discomfort—was not only the ledger. It was the moment on deck. It was the pocket of stillness. When their eyes had met over the open page, Emrys had felt something shift in his chest with the specific gravity of interest becoming attention becoming—something he declined, for the moment, to name. Nine years of faces, and this one stayed lit after the lamp was trimmed. There was a whole person behind those shutters, and the person was exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with travel, and Emrys recognized the exhaustion because he saw it every day in the archive: the tiredness of people carrying more than luggage.

He was a census taker. His entire moral architecture rested on the belief that recording a thing truly was a form of respecting it. And here was a man whose truth, whatever it was, could not survive being recorded truly. To enter him correctly would be to destroy him. To enter him incorrectly—to let the too-perfect answers stand unexamined—would be to fail the only duty Emrys had ever believed in.

He slept badly, and dreamed of flooded records halls, pages floating past his hands, every name legible except one.

* * *

Morning came down gray and thin over the water. Emrys took his tea on deck because the saloon was full of victors' officers newly posted to the river inspections, their gray-and-crimson uniforms loud as wounds against the drab boat, and he did not want to be near them or their clipboards or their new forms—*verification of movement, category B, persons of former republican association.* The war was over; the paperwork of the war was just beginning, and it would be vaster than the fighting.

He found Crane at the stern rail, whittling. Close up, Emrys saw it was a small wooden boat, half-formed, the hull already fair and clean. Not a toy. A model. Something made by hands that remembered ships.

"Mr. Crane." He kept his voice at conversational depth. Around them, other passengers pretended not to listen, which meant they all listened. "A correction to your entry. Standard practice. I misspoke yesterday—wrote *laborer*. You said clerk. I like my sheets to agree with themselves."

It was a pretext so transparent that he almost winced saying it. Crane regarded him for a long moment. Then, mildly: "Amend it, then. Clerk. Provisioning office, Corvel."

"I will." Emrys turned the page in his pocket ledger, made the correction against the rail, and found that neither of them moved away. The river ran brown and heavy beneath the stern, carrying ice shards south like a fleet of small white boats crewed by no one.

"You ask questions carefully," Crane said at last. It was not gratitude and it was not accusation. It was observation, delivered like a figure written in a neat forward-leaning hand.

"It's the job. Questions are tools. Careless men cut themselves."

"And careful men?" Crane's eyes came around, that slate gaze resting on Emrys with an attention that felt less like scrutiny than like recognition. "What do careful men cut?"

Emrys capped his pen. Somewhere forward, an officer laughed at something, the sound carrying flat across the water.

"Careful men," Emrys said, "usually know, by the second question, whether they're going to use the tool or put it away. I knew by the second question."

"And?"

"I haven't decided."

Something moved in Crane's face then—the shutter trembling, just at its edge, the light behind it shifting from slats to a single bright seam. He did not smile, but the whittling knife paused above the little wooden hull, and for one breath he looked young, and unarmored, and terribly tired.

"You noticed," Crane said quietly. It was not a question either.

"That your answers check out?" Emrys looked out at the water. "They check out perfectly. Nothing in this world checks out perfectly, Mr. Crane. Not weather, not ledgers, not people. Perfection of that order isn't truth. It's craft." He slid the ledger back into his coat. "I've no idea what you're carrying or what you're running from. But I know fine work when I hold it, and yours is the finest I've seen in nine years."

For a moment neither spoke. The packet's wake spread behind them, wide and pale and quickly erased, the river closing over its own memory the way rivers do.

"You should be careful," Crane said at last, and there was something underneath the words, something that was almost—almost—warmth. "Men who notice things are useful. Useful things get used."

"So I've heard." Emrys nodded once, formally, the census taker again, the clerk of doors. "I'll make my entry. *Passenger cooperative. Answers consistent.*" A pause. "Consistent, Mr. Crane. Not perfect. I've learned to leave myself room."

He walked forward along the deck, and he felt the gaze between his shoulder blades the whole way, steady as a hand.

Behind him, at the stern rail, the knife resumed its work on the small wooden boat, stroke by patient stroke, shaping something that would float.

That evening, the barometer began to fall. By full dark the wind had swung north and hardened, the rigging had gone singing and white, and the pilot rang down for reduced steam, shouting into the black that the waystation at Cold Ferry was the last shelter before the narrows froze.

By midnight, it was snowing sideways, and the *Meridian* was beating toward the ice.
