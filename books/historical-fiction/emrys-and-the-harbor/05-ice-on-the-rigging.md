## 5. Ice on the Rigging

The storm came down out of the north like something with intent.

Orsen felt it before she saw it—a pressure in her ears, a stillness on the river that made the deckhands stop and look upstream. The Meridian had been fighting the current for two days, and now the current seemed to be listening to something older than water. By late afternoon the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the first snow arrived not as flakes but as a fine, malicious dust that found every seam in her coat and melted against the back of her neck.

"Waystation's ahead," the pilot called down from the wheelhouse, his voice half-swallowed by wind. "We'll tie up or we'll freeze mid-river. Those are the choices."

The waystation was little more than a wooden fist thrust out from the eastern bank—a fueling depot for the packets in better years, now reduced to a long low shed, a coal dock, and a tower of stacked cordwood wearing a shroud of white. Ice had begun to fringe the shore in pale teeth. The Meridian bumped against the dock twice before the lines caught, and the whole vessel shuddered like an animal being haltered.

They disembarked into a wind that smelled of iron and coming dark.

Inside, the station keeper—a woman with forearms like ship's cable and a face that had stopped expecting good news decades ago—got the stove roaring and counted heads with the flat efficiency of someone tallying livestock. Eleven passengers, four crew. Not enough bunks for all, so they would share watches through the night, she said, and anyone who complained could take the loft above the coal chute, where the rats were fat and friendly.

Orsen claimed a corner near the wall, farthest from the door, back to the timber, where she could see everyone at once. It was an old habit. Old habits had kept her breathing through the siege, through the crossing, through eleven days on this packet pretending to be exactly what her papers said she was: Crane, a clerk of no particular importance, going to no particular place.

She took out her mending. A sock, darned past dignity. Her fingers moved in their practiced rhythm, needle flashing, eyes down—the universal posture of a man who wanted nothing from anyone.

It did not work.

"Crane, isn't it?"

Emrys Vale settled onto the bench across from her with the unhurried precision of a man who measured everything, including his own movements. He had a tin cup in one hand and the storm-light made his face hollow and serious. Up close, in this close yellow room, he looked younger than he had on the deck, and more dangerous, which she had not thought possible.

"It's my watch soon," she said. "I'll want sleep."

"Third watch is mine. Yours is fourth. We've hours yet." He nodded at the stove, the snow-blind window, the knot of crewmen arguing over cards. "No one's going anywhere. Not even you."

There it was again—that mild, terrible attentiveness of his. On the packet he had asked his questions like a man dealing cards, one at a time, watching what she discarded. She had answered them like a woman folding a hand she couldn't afford to play.

"I don't recall inviting conversation," she said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You never do."

He drank his tea. She darned her sock. Outside, the wind rose through registers she hadn't known wind possessed, until the whole structure groaned around them like a ship at sea rather than a shed on land. Ice began to build on the rigging of the packet outside—she could hear it through the walls, a soft crystalline ticking, thousands of small weights accumulating on ropes and spars—and somewhere past midnight, she knew, something would give under the load. That was how ice worked. That was how most things worked. Quietly, quietly, and then all at once.

"You were in Vessany," Emrys said. Not a question. "When it fell."

Her needle did not stop. She had trained it not to stop. "The whole world was in Vessany when it fell. It was on every map."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one that costs nothing."

He considered that, turning his cup. Snow ticked against the shutters. And then he said, quietly, without accusation: "My mother's family came from Vessany. Two generations back. Fishermen, up the coast near Sarrow. I used to ask her what it was like and she'd say—" he paused, and his voice changed slightly, taking on an echo, someone else's cadence— "she'd say it was a country that loved paper more than people. Charters, treaties, ledgers. Beautiful paper. Then she'd cry into the bread dough and not know why."

Something moved in Orsen's chest, slow and unwelcome, like ice shifting on a hull.

"The bread was probably bad," she said.

He laughed—a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody. And then they were both quiet, but it was a different quiet than before. Less fortified.

* * *

Her watch came, as watches do, at the worst hour—between three and dawn, when the body believes it is dying and the mind supplies evidence. The keeper woke her with a boot to the bench leg and a jerking thumb toward the door: wood needed moving from the stack to the stove-room, lamp trimmed, and above all the ice knocked off the packet's rigging before it dragged the moorings under. Crew would handle the rigging; passengers on watch handled the wood and the light.

Orsen dressed layer over layer and went out into a night that had been remade while she slept. The storm had spent its fury and left behind a vast ringing silence. Snow stood knee-deep and unmarked. The stars were brutal in their clarity. Every surface wore a crust of white, and the Meridian lay against the dock like a ghost ship, every line and spar furred with ice an inch thick, creaking under its silver burden.

Emrys was already there.

Of course he was. He had a maul in his gloved hands and was tapping along the forward stay, knocking the ice loose in great slabs that fell and shattered on the dock with sounds like broken glass and struck bells. His breath plumed. In the starlight he looked carved rather than born.

"You're early," he said.

"You're supposed to be asleep."

"I don't sleep well on land." Tap. Crack. A sheet of ice slid off the forestay and rang on the boards. "Never have. Even as a boy. My father said it was because I counted things in my dreams." He glanced at her. "Censuses, probably. It was always leading here."

She should have gone straight to the woodpile. Instead she picked up the hand-maul leaning against the bollard and started on the aft shrouds, working from the bottom up, matching his rhythm without deciding to. The physical labor was good. Labor asked nothing about registration districts.

For a long time there was only the sound of their work, and beneath it the river muttering under its new skin of ice, and beneath that the enormous silence of a world holding its breath.

"They told us it would take years," she said.

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. That was the danger of this hour, this cold, this rhythm—the body loosened its grip on the tongue.

Emrys set down his maul. He didn't turn fully toward her; some instinct in him understood that a direct gaze would slam the door. He looked instead at the ice, at the river, giving her the mercy of an oblique angle.

"The city," he said. "Vessany City."

"Nineteen days." Nineteen days, and she could still hear the bells—not celebration, at the end, but alarm, rung until the ringers fled and the bells went on alone. "Everyone said the walls would hold for a season at least. The ministers said it on the radio every evening. *The city stands. The republic stands.* They said it right up until the morning the artillery reached the bridges." Tap. Crack. The ice came away in plates like broken mirrors. "Then they said other things. Mostly they said goodbye, in various languages, from various airfields."

"You were there. In the ministries."

"A clerk," she said. "A small gray clerk in a small gray office. I carried papers from one desk to another. That's all anyone needs to know about me."

"And the papers?"

"Were papers." Her voice came out harder than she intended. She made herself soften it, made herself smile the thin smile of Crane the unremarkable man. "Ministers love memoranda. Half of them were about the heating allowances."

Half-truths. She had become fluent in them, a native speaker of the language between the words. Yes, she had been in the ministries. No, she had not merely carried papers. The dispatches sewn against her ribs—she felt them even now, a faint stiffness under her left arm like a second heartbeat—were proof of what the men with heating allowances had actually done: sold the peace terms in three separate negotiations, each sale promising a different ally the same territory, each signature buying its author passage out while the army drowned at the gates. She had watched them lie with the serene confidence of men who had never once been contradicted by consequences.

But she could not tell Emrys Vale any of that. So she gave him the siege instead, the parts that were safe because they belonged to everyone.

"The last week, there was no government anymore. Just committees. Just neighbors. People formed bucket chains when the water mains broke, and food queues where nobody pushed, and—" she stopped, surprised by the thing rising in her throat. She swallowed it. "It was the only time in my life the city was honest. When everything was lost, everyone stopped lying. It was almost worth it. Almost."

The word hung in the frozen air. *Almost.*

"What happened to your family?" Emrys asked softly.

"I have none," said Orsen Crane, and it was true, and it was the loneliest true thing she owned.

He knocked one more slab of ice free, and then he set the maul down entirely and came around the shrouds to stand near her—not too near. Close enough that in the killing cold she could feel the small animal warmth of another person, and hated herself for noticing it.

"When I boarded the packet," he said, "and started asking questions—it wasn't suspicion. At first. It's just that names are my trade. I collect them. And yours didn't fit anywhere. Like a key that turns halfway." He exhaled, watching the vapor climb. "I want you to know that whatever you're carrying, whatever you left behind—I'm not the river police. I count people. I've never once been told what to do with them after."

The terror arrived then, precisely as she'd known it would.

It came not as a flood but as warmth—which was worse, which was infinitely worse. Because she could withstand suspicion, interrogation, the checkpoint stare that stripped a man to his paperwork. What she could not withstand was kindness offered without conditions, in the dark, at four in the morning, by someone who said *I collect names* as though it were a confession of love.

If she let him in, even an inch, then there was one person alive who might guess at Orsena Crane. One person whose capture, whose coercion, whose casual cruelty or simple mistake could unravel eleven years of careful erasure. Every person who knows is a door. She had sealed every door. She had bricked herself into her own life like a relic in a wall.

And here was this census taker with ice melting in his eyebrows, offering to leave a door open.

"Don't," she said. Her voice cracked on the single syllable, and she steadied it with effort that cost her something permanent. "Whatever you think you're doing—don't be kind to me. I can't afford it. Neither can you."

Emrys held her eyes. In the starlight his expression was impossible to read, or perhaps perfectly readable: the look of a man who has found a flaw in a ledger and understands that the flaw is a life.

"Fourth watch ends at dawn," he said gently. "Better get the wood in. It's going to be a hard freeze tonight."

He picked up his maul and went back to the rigging, and the tapping resumed, and the ice fell and rang on the dock like the small bells of an unseen church.

* * *

Dawn came gray and enormous. The storm had passed overnight, dragging the clouds south, and the sun rose over the eastern hills into sky scrubbed clean and merciless blue. The keeper put out porridge and weak coffee, and the passengers emerged blinking into the brightness like animals after a rockslide.

At midmorning the pilot walked the dock, tested the ice along the hull, and pronounced the river navigable if they left within the hour, before the refreeze at dusk. There was a general stirring, luggage and lines and engine smoke.

Orsen stood apart at the rail of the dock, watching the ice floes grind past in the channel. Behind her, inside the station, Emrys was helping the keeper restack the wood they'd burned, sleeves rolled despite the cold, and she could hear him thanking her for the coffee in that precise, courteous voice, and she hated that she already knew his voice would be easy to find in a crowd, that she had catalogued it without permission.

Eleven days to Harborton remained. Eleven days of close quarters and shared tables and those attentive gray eyes across a ledger. She had survived sieges, crossings, checkpoints. She had survived the total erasure of everyone who had ever known her name.

She did not know whether she would survive being liked.

The Meridian blew its whistle, a long mournful note that shattered a shelf of ice from the pilot-house roof. Orsen pulled her collar up, checked the stiff secret weight under her left arm—still there, still sealed, still damning—and went to board, and behind her the waystation shrank into the white, taking with it the last hour of the night when, for a few minutes, standing in the falling dark beside a stranger, she had almost remembered what it was to be warm.

Two berths down the corridor, she heard Vale telling another passenger, cheerfully, that he kept a record of everything—that it was a professional habit, that nothing escaped him for long.

Orsen Crane closed her cabin door, sat down on the narrow bunk, and unlocked the satchel she had sworn not to open before Harborton.
