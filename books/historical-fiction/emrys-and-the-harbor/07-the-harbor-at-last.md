## 7. The Harbor at Last

The river announced Harborton before the city itself appeared. First came the smell—brine and tar and cold smoke, the mineral breath of a tidal harbor after winter—and then the sound, faint under the packet's churning paddles: bells. Three of them, maybe four, ringing from different quarters of the city with no agreement among them about the hour. Then, around the last wooded bend where the Vessany side of the water narrowed to a throat, the harbor opened all at once, gray and glittering and impossibly crowded, like a drawer of spilled coins.

Emrys Vale stood at the rail with his ledger strapped shut under his arm and did not move for a long time.

He had lived his whole life within earshot of water, but he had never seen it used this way. The basin was matted with shipping: coastal brigs, herring boats pressed three deep against their moorings, a hospital barge flying white flags gone yellow with soot, two troop transports of unmistakably Corvessan build riding high and empty, having delivered whatever they carried. Between the proper vessels lay everything that could float and some things that should not have—barges with their cabins bulging, fishing smacks with families camped under oilcloth, a ferry listing badly with its rails hung with washing. The refugees had come down every road that led to neutral water, and finding the crossings open, they had stopped here at the edge of it and made a country of the queue.

Above it all, on the customs tower, three sets of colors flew in the stiff east wind: the harbor's own white-and-blue neutrality banner in the middle, flanked by the standards of the victors, each exactly as large as the harbor's, neither quite as clean.

"First time?" asked the deckhand beside him, coiling rope without looking up.

"To the harbor, yes."

"You'll want to file your papers early." The man nodded at Emrys's ledger. "Whatever that is. They've requisitioned half the customs house. Men in good coats walking about asking whose ink is whose."

Emrys said nothing, but his hand tightened on the strap.

* * *

Disembarkation took two hours, which gave him two hours to watch Crane.

He told himself it was professional habit—the census taker's instinct to keep his subjects in view until their entries were closed—but habit had never before felt like this, like a line drawn from his chest to another person that pulled when they moved. Crane stood apart on the main deck, as he had stood apart the whole voyage, coat collar up, one hand resting on the satchel he never set down. He watched the harbor the way other men watched a fire—as though calculating what it would take from him.

The gangway went down at last onto a pontoon that bobbed sickeningly, and the passengers funneled toward the quay. Emrys let himself be carried in the current of bodies and bags. A family pushed past him with a birdcage wrapped in a blanket; an old woman swore steadily and creatively at her own trunk; somewhere a child was crying with the tireless rhythm of surf. On the quay, harbor officials in armbands checked papers with a speed that was not efficiency but triage—they were not looking for errors, Emrys realized. They were looking for shapes. For men of a certain age, certain build. For anything the war might still owe anyone.

Crane passed through in under a minute. His exemption papers must have been flawless, because the officer barely glanced at him—and yet Emrys noticed that Crane exhaled only when he'd put thirty paces between himself and the table, and that he walked first not toward the lodging houses, whose signs hung out along the front street, but toward the far end of the quay, where a stone building with barred windows bore the carved inscription of the harbor archive. He stopped there just long enough to read the posted hours. Then he turned away, and the crowd took him, and Emrys stood holding his ledger like a fool who has lost his place in his own book.

* * *

The census office occupied three rooms above a sailmaker's shop on Rope Street, and by afternoon it had become what Emrys privately called the Kingdom of Queues. Every disembarking passenger over the age of ten was meant to be registered within forty-eight hours; every vessel's crew list amended; every lodging house reported. The victors' liaison officers wanted copies of everything, and the harbor council, protective of its neutrality the way a poor man is protective of his one good coat, wanted copies of the copies first. Emrys joined the line of district takers waiting to file, a clerk ahead of him sneezing continuously in the coal-smoke air, and worked through his sheets one last time while he waited.

That was the trouble with his own handwriting. It never lied to him, and it never let him lie to himself.

There was Crane, Orsen, entered at mid-river boarding, occupation given as courier-clerk, former registration listed as the parish of Aldenmere. There was his own marginal note, small and neat: *No prior entry located in district rolls consulted.* And beneath that, added later in pencil, in the shorthand he used only for things he intended to verify: *Aldenmere records burned? Verify.*

He remembered the moment he'd written it. The wardroom lamplight, the storm still worrying the hull, Crane sitting across from him with that half-drunk cup of tea going cold, telling him about the fall of Vessany in a voice that was perfectly level and perfectly wrong—level the way ice is level, with something moving underneath. He remembered thinking: this man is reading me a report he memorized. He remembered also—noting it now with the same clinical unease—that he had not cared. That was the irregularity his training had no column for: that the discrepancy in the record interested him less than the man who'd caused it.

"Vale," said the clerk at the window. "District sheets. Forty-one souls? You're late."

"The storm kept us at Tarnholt two days."

"Everything keeps everyone everywhere two days." She took his sheets, riffled them, stamped the top sheet, and paused. Her eyes came up. "You flagged one. Crane, Orsen."

"He wasn't in any registry I consulted."

"They're saying half the republic's registries are ash by now. Half the republic is ash." She stamped again, harder, as if the stamp could settle the question. "The liaison office will pull anything flagged. Probably tomorrow. They're building something over there—a narrative, a case, I don't ask." She slid the sheets into a tray already thick with paper. "If your flagged man is a deserter, you've done your duty. If he isn't, you've done it anyway. Sleep well."

Emrys climbed down the stairs into the blue dusk of Rope Street and found that his hands were cold in a way the wind did not account for.

* * *

His lodgings were a rented room above a chandler's, narrow as a coffin standing up, with a view of nothing but a light-well and the sound of gulls quarreling over fish guts at dawn. He unpacked with the economy of a man who owned little: razors, spare collars, the brass instruments of his trade, a tin of pens. He ate bread and hard sausage at the chandler's counter downstairs while the chandler's wife talked at him about prices, about the victors' officers billeted on Harbor Row, about how you couldn't get lamp oil above a fair price anymore unless you knew someone, and did he know anyone, and wasn't it strange how many people were asking that question lately?

He agreed with everything. He thought about Crane.

Not the suspicion—he had examined the suspicion thoroughly on the last night of the voyage, turning it over the way he'd turn a coin of doubtful metal. The biography too smooth. The answers arriving a half-beat late, as if translated from another language. The hands: a clerk's calluses were one thing, but Crane held his knife like a soldier holds one, thumb along the spine, and he scanned rooms without seeming to, doorways first, exits always. Emrys had written census forms for six years. People lied on them constantly—about ages, about trades, about whether there was a husband or wasn't—one small lie per form, the ordinary tax of living. But everything about Crane was one consistent lie, load-bearing, structural. A whole architecture erected where a person used to be.

And none of that, none of it, explained why Emrys lay awake that night in the chandler's narrow bed listening to the harbor bells disagree, and thought not *what is he hiding* but *where did he go when the crowd took him*. Why he kept seeing the particular angle of Crane's shoulders as he'd read the archive's posted hours. Why the image would not resolve into mere curiosity no matter how he filed it.

He got up, lit the stub of candle, and took out his pocket notebook—the unofficial one, the one no liaison officer would ever see, where census takers kept the observations too human for forms. He wrote, in the shorthand of his trade:

*O.C. Disembarked ~11th hour. Direction: harbor archive (noted hours). Carries satchel constantly; weight suggests documents. No luggage beyond it. A man traveling light travels either short or forever.*

He looked at what he'd written and understood that he had just done a thing with consequences attached. If the liaison office pulled his flagged sheet tomorrow—if they began pulling threads, as clerks do, mechanically, competently—then every fact he had gathered about Orsen Crane would become ammunition available to whoever chose to fire it. His notes were not observations anymore. They were a weapon lying in a public street.

He blew out the candle and sat in the dark listening to the tide work at the pilings, and felt the specific vertigo of a careful man realizing that care, henceforth, would have to be a moral act rather than a habit.

* * *

Morning brought fog off the sea, thick enough that the harbor bells sounded closer than the street below. Emrys dressed, shaved by feel, and walked to the customs house to see whether the liaison office had pulled his sheets.

They had not. Instead he found the census office in a state of controlled panic, and the cause standing in the middle of it: three persons in excellent wool coats, two men and a woman, surrounded by crates of requisitioned ledgers, directing the harbor clerks with the serene entitlement of people who have never once been told no. A stencil on the largest crate read *HISTORICAL COMMISSION — EASTERN THEATRE*, and under it, smaller: *Section IV: Civil Administration & Population Records.*

"Historians," muttered the sneezing clerk beside Emrys, with the contempt clerks reserve for amateurs handling paper.

Not historians, Emrys thought, watching them. Historians worked slowly, in libraries, over dead men's letters. These worked fast, in customs houses, over living ones. The woman—the senior of the three, by the way even the harbor master bent toward her—moved down the tray of filed district sheets with a finger like a customs seal, tapping, sorting. When she reached Emrys's bundle she slowed.

"Vale. River district, Tarnholt way-station manifest." She read his flag note aloud, flatly, to her assistant: "'No prior entry located.' Aldenmere." Something flickered—recognition, appetite, Emrys couldn't name it. "Aldenmere again. Make a card for this one. Everything touching a Crane, Orsen, comes to Section Four directly. Not to the general review. Directly."

She looked up then, and found Emrys watching her, and smiled the way officials smile: pleasantly, and at your function rather than your face.

"Census taker Vale? Good work. Most men don't notice absence. Absence is very difficult to see." She tucked his sheet into a separate folder, one that closed with a string and a wax disk. "You'll likely hear from us. We're writing the true account of the war, you understand. Everything feeds it. Even small paper."

Even small paper, Emrys thought, all the way back down the stairs into the fog. Especially small paper. He had spent six years believing his ledgers were the sediment of other people's lives—dust that settled and was stored. He saw now that they were more like a net, and that nets do not choose what they catch, and that somewhere out in the fog-thick streets of Harborton, a man named Crane was walking toward the archive with everything he was sewn into his coat lining, and did not know that the net had already begun to close around his name.

Emrys stood at the bottom of the customs house steps and faced the truth he had been avoiding since the pontoon, since the wardroom, perhaps since the first time Crane had looked up from his whittling and met his eyes across the lamplight: that he was no longer capable of being neutral ground. Whatever he did next—report, conceal, warn, follow—would be a choice, and choices in wartime were called by other names.

The fog thinned for a moment, and through it, at the far end of Rope Street, he saw a familiar figure in a collar-up coat, satchel against its hip, pausing at the corner as if feeling watched. Their eyes met across forty yards of morning. Crane did not wave. But he did not look away either, and the look that crossed the distance was not fear and not greeting but a question, asked in the only language they shared:

*Which side of the ledger are you on?*

Then the fog closed, and the figure was gone, and Emrys turned toward the harbor archive with his heart going like a man's who has decided to run before his legs have agreed.
