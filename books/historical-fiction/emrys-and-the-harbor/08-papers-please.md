## 8. Papers, Please

Harborton announced itself by smell before sight: tar and brine and the particular sourness of too many people boiling too little fish in too little water. From the packet's deck Orsen watched the harbor resolve out of morning fog—masts first, a forest of them, then the gray shoulders of warehouses, then the city climbing its hills in terraces of slate roofs and smoke. She had seen ports before. She had never seen one drowning.

Refugees lined the quay three deep, their belongings bundled in blankets and bound with rope, their faces turned toward the customs sheds with the same expression she had learned to read on every road out of Vessany: hope worn down to something more like arithmetic. *Will they let me in. Will they feed me. Will it be today.* The Republic had taught her that look from the other side of a desk.

She adjusted her collar, checked the weight of the satchel against her ribs—the dispatches rode flat against her left side, sewn where a man might carry letters or a keepsake—and joined the line coming down the gangway. Around her, men of the Meridian stretched and joked about solid ground. She kept her hands busy with the buckle of her bag. Busy hands. Quiet feet. A face that asked for nothing.

The customs shed was a long timber building that had clearly been a fish-packing hall until last month. Salt still ghosted the walls. Inside, the queue divided: families to the left, single men to the right, and at the head of the right-hand line a table behind which sat two officers of whatever authority now governed Harborton's neutrality—dark green coats, armbands stamped with the interlocking circles of the Coalition, faces chosen for their ability to express nothing.

Orsen counted the men ahead of her. Seven. She used the time to rehearse.

*Orsen Crane. Clerk. Exempted from Vessany's levy on account of a lame hip, certified by the magistrate of Lower Maren, second district. Traveling to take a position with a shipping concern.* Every detail true except the ones that mattered, which was how a good forgery worked—not paper alone but a life built around it, load-bearing walls of habit and hesitation timed to seem like honesty.

The hip was real enough. That was the joke she never told anyone: the limp was genuine, an old break badly set, and the exemption certificate that described it was not. Somewhere in a burned ministry there was a file with her name in it, and somewhere in a smuggler's workshop there had been a stamp, and between those two facts she had constructed eight years of survival.

"Next."

She stepped up to the table. The nearer officer was young, with chapped hands and the weary diligence of someone who had been processing human beings since dawn. The older one sat slightly behind, ostensibly reviewing manifests, actually watching. He was the dangerous one. There was always a dangerous one.

"Purpose of entry."

"Employment." She let the word come out roughened, unpracticed, as a clerk's would. "Crane. Orsen Crane." She set her papers on the table: identity card, travel pass, exemption certificate. Three documents, fanned just wide enough.

The young officer picked up the identity card. His eyes moved from it to her face and back—the standard transit, performed ten thousand times a day across this whole coast, and still capable of ending her life if it lingered half a second wrong. She kept her gaze at his collar. Not servile. Just unremarkable.

"Clerk," he read.

"For Harrow & Sons, shipping agents. I'm told they've lost staff to the war."

"Haven't we all." It wasn't sympathy exactly. It was the reflexive muteness of the exhausted. He flipped the card over, checked the watermark against something under the table—she heard the small click of a comparison plate, and her pulse went loud in her own ears—and moved to the exemption certificate.

This was the moment. Always this moment. Paper could survive any amount of looking; it was the pause after the looking that killed. If he raised the certificate toward the light. If he rubbed the ink. If he asked her to state the date of certification, because the forger had been good but not perfect, and the magistrate's seal of Lower Maren had been retired eleven days after the document she carried was supposedly issued—

"You came through the river blockade," the officer said.

"The packet. The Meridian."

"Frozen in at Corvel Waystation, were you? We've had three boats report that crossing."

"Four days on the ice." She allowed herself a thin smile. "I can recommend the company and nothing else about it."

Behind the young officer, the older one looked up.

It was a small thing, humor—a clerk's complaint about bad lodgings, a man's ordinary grievance—but she felt it leave her like warmth leaving a hand, unrecoverable, and she watched it work. The young officer's mouth twitched. The tension in his shoulders, the professional suspicion that had been gathering through the examination, loosened by one degree. Men who lied under interrogation did not complain about the coffee at waystations. Men who lied kept themselves smooth and careful all the way down.

She had learned that from a woman in Vessany's foreign service who had also once been somebody else, briefly, on a train through occupied Corvess. *Give them a truth wrapped around your lie,* she'd said. *Nobody unwraps a package that rattles.*

"Exemption for the hip," the officer said, reading. "Old wound?"

"Cart fall. I was fourteen."

He glanced at her leg without asking her to move it. She shifted her weight so the limp showed—not exaggerated, just honest, the way it always was by late afternoon when the ache set into the old fracture like weather into a wall.

"All right, Crane. Harbor district, third ward—you'll register residence within two days. Quota inspection is random, but it happens. Don't make anyone come looking for you." He stamped the card, slid the papers back. "Next."

Orsen took the documents with steady fingers and walked out of the shed into the gray light of Harborton, and only when she was forty steps clear, wedged between a stacked wall of lobster pots, did she let herself breathe. Her heart would not slow. It climbed the ribs as though trying to get out ahead of her.

*Barely,* she thought. *That was barely.*

And the city was full of tables like that one. Full of eyes.

* * *

Lodging in a refugee-swollen port cost what the desperate could pay and not a copper less. Orsen spent an hour in the streets above the harbor learning the geography of scarcity: boarding houses with FULL chalked on their doors, pension signs whose prices changed depending on how you looked, a convent taking women only—which she passed without slowing, though something in her chest pulled toward it like a compass needle, the way it always did toward the doors she couldn't enter anymore.

She found a room at last in a house on Chandler's Row owned by a widow named Mrs. Pellik, who rented to single men of sober habits because single men of sober habits asked no questions and paid on Fridays. The room was four paces by five, contained a cot, a washstand, and a window overlooking a lightwell the width of a coffin, and cost nearly everything Orsen dared spend. Mrs. Pellik took her coin, wrote her name in a ledger—another ledger, another record of Orsen Crane blooming into official existence—and handed her a key on a loop of string.

"Curfew at nine. The gentleman on the third floor snores; you'll live. And if the patrols stop you after dark, have your papers out before they ask. They're quicker with men who are ready."

"Thank you," Orsen said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it—that gratitude for a coffin-sized room and curfew advice was what safety had been reduced to.

She unpacked nothing. There was nothing to unpack that mattered. What mattered was against her ribs, and had been since Vessany fell, warm now from her body heat as though the papers themselves were alive, some sleeping animal she carried.

She waited until full dark. Then she went out.

* * *

The address Emrys Vale had mentioned—casually, on the second night of the ice, when conversation had been the only fuel they had left—was a tavern called the Salt and Ledger, on a lane that ran down toward the fish market. Orsen remembered the moment of hearing it with a clarity that still embarrassed her: she had been mending a seam, hands busy, and Emrys, hunched over his census ledger by the stove, had said, *My brother-in-law drinks at the Salt and Ledger. Says half the harbor's business gets done there by men who aren't supposed to be doing it.* And she had filed the name away in the part of her mind where contraband lived, alongside tide tables and patrol rotations and the face of every officer who had ever looked at her twice.

Now she walked toward it through streets that felt like held breath. Harborton after curfew belonged to the patrols: pairs of green-coated soldiers at intersections, lanterns swinging, boots ringing off the cobbles in rhythms she memorized without meaning to. She moved the way she had learned to move in the last weeks of the republic—purposeful, unhurried, a man going about his dull lawful business—and she passed within ten feet of two separate checkpoints and felt each one like a hand laid flat against her spine.

The Salt and Ledger was marked by a sign whose painted ledger had faded into a blank book, which struck her as accidentally honest. Light leaked under its door. Noise leaked over it: laughter, argument, a fiddle played by someone who knew six notes and loved each one.

Inside, the heat and press of bodies hit her like water. She had forgotten how loud neutral places were—how people who were not being shelled drank like it was a discipline. She worked the room with her eyes from the doorway: dockers, clerks, a table of officers too well-dressed for their rank, and behind the bar a broad woman with gray-streaked hair and forearms like capstans, pulling beer and settling disputes with a look.

Not Bess. Bess was supposed to be the contact—the cutout, the last living link in the courier chain that ran from Vessany's drowned ministries to whoever, wherever, still wanted the truth. But cutouts moved. Addresses were shells. First you found the shell; the shell led to the tenant.

Orsen ordered a beer she didn't want, paid with exact change, and drank a third of it while appearing to drink all of it. She let an hour pass. She listened. The tavern's language was money and tonnage and which officials could be bribed and which had to be flattered, and beneath it, quieter, the other language: *did you hear about the family on Rope Lane, taken in the night; the historians are requisitioning records; better burn anything with a Vessany postmark.*

Her beer finished itself. She approached the bar when the crowd around it thinned.

"Good ale," she said, in the tone of a man opening negotiations with a fact. "I was told this house knew about cargo. Small cargo. Personal effects, needing to go south."

The woman's hands did not stop moving—glass, cloth, tap handle—but something in the quality of her attention changed, the way water changes when a stone goes into it.

"Who sent you?"

"A friend of a friend. Name of Marren. Out of the old ministry district."

The cloth stopped.

The woman looked at Orsen then, fully, for the first time, and Orsen understood with a cold sliding certainty that she had made a mistake—not in coming, but in the name, or the phrasing, or simply in the year. The look lasted two seconds. In those two seconds Orsen saw the woman decide something, and she could not tell which way the decision had gone.

"Back room," the woman said. "Through the curtain. Wait by the stove. Don't talk to anyone."

Orsen waited. The back room was smaller, warmer, lit by a lamp turned low, and empty except for crates and the stove's iron bulk radiating a heat that should have been comforting and was instead like the breath of something patient. She stood with her hands loose at her sides and counted her exits: curtain, coal chute, window barred but the bar loose—she could see it was loose, someone had worked it before.

After twenty minutes the curtain moved and the woman came in, and behind her came a man Orsen had never seen: slight, quick-eyed, with the soft hands of someone who handled paper for a living and the wary stance of someone who had recently started expecting it to be confiscated.

"You're Crane," the man said.

"I was given to understand—" 

"I know what you were given to understand." He said it fast, low, glancing at the curtain. "Whatever you were told about the network, forget it. It's gone. Rolled up in Kessel's Reach three weeks ago—your handler, my handler, the print shop on Tanner's Court, all of it. They took thirty-one people in one night. Some of them are talking." His eyes flicked to her chest, to the coat, and back up, and Orsen felt the look like a blade laid flat against fabric. "Which means anything that was moving through that chain is now evidence. Anything that hasn't surfaced is something they're hunting for. Do you understand me?"

The stove ticked. Somewhere beyond the curtain the fiddle ground through its six notes again.

"I understand," Orsen said.

"Then hear the rest. There's no courier. There's no dead drop. There's no safe passage south, and there won't be—the victors are sealing the crossings as soon as the ice breaks, and the ice is breaking. Whatever you're carrying, the network that was supposed to receive it doesn't exist anymore." He paused, and something almost human crossed his quick face—pity, or its exhausted cousin. "I'm sorry. You walked into the middle of a grave and you're standing in it."

Orsen heard the words and felt them land one by one, like stones dropped into her from a height. Eight years. Forged papers and limping past inspections and the slow amputation of everyone who had ever known her name—all of it built toward delivery, toward handing the truth to hands that would carry it onward, and the hands were gone. Taken in a night. Talking, some of them, in rooms with lamps in them.

"Is there anyone," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved, "left at all?"

The man was already moving back toward the curtain. He stopped with his hand on the fabric.

"There's the archive," he said. "Harbor archive, deposition office, east wing. Neutral charter—older than the war, older than the Coalition, and they guard their independence like a dragon guards gold. Anyone can swear testimony into the record. Sealed. Timestamped. Once it's in, even the historians can't touch it without making it famous." He gave a short laugh with no floor under it. "Famous, of course, is its own kind of danger. But it's the only door still open, friend. And I wouldn't stand in it long."

He slipped out. The tavern-keeper brought Orsen another beer she hadn't asked for and didn't charge her for, and would not meet her eye.

* * *

She walked back to Chandler's Row through the emptied streets, and the fear that had ridden her all day finally caught up and climbed on.

It was not the sharp fear of the checkpoint. This was slower and worse. This was the fear of a city with eyes everywhere and no network left to blur between them: Mrs. Pellik's ledger with her false name in it; the census registration due within two days, which meant a census taker, which meant questions, which meant a form traveling to some office where some clerk might flag the gap where Orsen Crane's prior registry should be. Thirty-one people taken in one night. Some of them talking. Every hour she stayed alive and undocumented, she became more valuable to the victors than anything she carried—because she was the dispatches' context, the courier, the living link, and links could be interrogated in ways paper couldn't.

At the corner of Rope Lane a patrol was questioning a boy of perhaps sixteen against a shutter. One officer held the boy's papers up to his lantern, tilting them, hunting for the flaw. The boy stood very straight with his hands at his sides, and Orsen recognized the posture from the inside: busy hands quiet, feet planted, face offering nothing. She slowed—one stride, no more—and the boy's eyes met hers for a fraction of a second, and in them she saw the same arithmetic she had seen on the quay that morning, worn down to its final sum.

She walked on. She did not look back. Looking back was how networks got rolled up; she knew that too, knew it in the marrow, and hated knowing it.

In her coffin-sized room she bolted the door, sat on the cot in the dark, and took stock the way she had learned to take stock: assets, liabilities, time.

Assets: the dispatches, intact. Her disguise, holding—barely, but barely had carried her eight years. One address: the archive, east wing, deposition office. A door still open, if she could reach it before the victors reached her.

Liabilities: everyone. The whole apparatus of her survival, reduced to her own two hands in a city being combed house by house. And a census taker named Emrys Vale, somewhere in the harbor district, holding a sheet of paper with ORSEN CRANE written on it in his meticulous hand, and behind those steady gray eyes the question he had been too decent to ask on the ice—or too clever, and decency had been her guess, but her guesses had been wrong before, and lately her life depended on guessing right.

Time: until the ice broke. Days. Perhaps fewer.

Orsen lay down without undressing, one hand resting over the flat sewn weight against her ribs, and listened to the strange sound the harbor made at night—the ice groaning out in the channel, a long cracking sigh like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Spring was coming. The thaw was coming. Everything frozen was going to move soon: the river, the crossings, the armies of clerks and historians and hunters already gathering on the far shore.

Tomorrow she would find the archive. Tomorrow she would learn its hours, its doors, its blind corners. Tomorrow.

But sleep, when it finally took her, brought not plans but a face: Emrys Vale across the waystation stove, steam rising off tin cups, saying in his dry precise voice, *You know, Mr. Crane, you're the most interesting entry I've had all year*—and the terrible warmth of that moment, and the terror of it, arriving again now, on the far side of the ice, where interesting entries ended up flagged in ledgers and ledgers ended up in the hands of historians.

She did not yet know that the historians had arrived that same evening, that their requisition orders were already moving through the harbor offices like ink through water, and that among the records they would demand—first, earliest, by name—were the census sheets of the harbor district, third ward.

Emrys Vale's sheets. Every line. Every name.
