## 9. The Historians Arrive

They came on a Tuesday, in the gray hour after the fish carts had gone through and before the harbor offices properly opened, when the fog still lay against the windows like something waiting to be let in.

Emrys Vale was at his desk with his third cup of chicory going cold beside him, reconciling the winter manifests against his field notes, when he heard the wheels. Not cart wheels—carriage wheels, iron-shod and deliberate, the kind of sound that did not belong on Coal Quay Lane and knew it. He looked up through the frosted glass of the census office window and saw two vehicles draw up before the harbor registry, lacquered black, each bearing on its door a crest he had only ever seen stamped on requisition forms that arrived pre-signed and were not to be questioned: the double anchor and laurel of the Coalition Provisional Authority.

The men who stepped down wore long coats of good wool and carried themselves with the particular confidence of people whose paperwork had never once been challenged. There were four of them. Three went into the registry across the lane. The fourth stood a moment in the street, breathing the harbor air as though tasting it, and then turned and came directly toward the census office.

Emrys set down his pen. He had learned, in eleven years of counting the harbor district's living and dead and departed, that the first thing to do with an unexpected visitor was to know what you wanted from the encounter before it began. He found, to his mild surprise, that he did not know what he wanted. That was new. That was worth noting.

The door opened without a knock.

"Master Vale?" The man was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, spectacles on a chain, with the soft hands of someone who had spent a war writing rather than fighting. "Aldous Renwick. Historical Commission." He produced a card as though producing evidence. "I'm told you keep the district manifests."

"I keep them," Emrys said. "Among other things."

"May I?"

Renwick did not wait for the yes. He crossed the small office the way a man crosses a room he already owns, his gaze moving over the shelved ledgers, the pigeonholes of returns, the map of the harbor districts pinned above the stove with its water stains blooming like continents. He stopped at the map. He touched the stain where the river district met the ice road, absently, the way one touches a scar.

"You've been here through the war," Renwick said. It was not quite a question.

"The office doesn't close for wars. People are born in wars. They die in them. Someone has to write it down."

"Yes." Renwick smiled, and there was something almost genuine in it. "That is precisely our problem."

* * *

The Historical Commission occupied three rooms in the customs house that had been seized, repainted, and refurnished within a fortnight of the republic's fall—faster, Emrys thought, than the victors had managed to feed their own troops. He was summoned there that same afternoon, and again the next day, and by the end of the week he had been given a desk of his own in a corner of the second room, beneath a window that looked out on the masts of ships flying flags he was still learning to read.

The work, as Renwick explained it over the first of many cups of genuinely good coffee—the first coffee Emrys had tasted in three years—was simple in conception and infinite in execution. The war was over. The republic was ended. What remained was to establish what had happened, and in what order, and why, so that no future generation would have to rely on rumor.

"History is not memory," Renwick said, tapping the table with one finger. "Memory is a drunk telling you about his childhood. History is the ledger. We are here to build the ledger."

Emrys drank his coffee and said nothing. He had spent his life among ledgers. He knew what they kept out as well as what they kept in.

The requisition orders came down like weather. Harbor shipping records. Customs rolls. The pilot guilds' logs. The ferry manifests for the last five years, every crossing catalogued, every name. And—the order that made his hand pause on the wax seal, though his face did not change—the complete census returns of the harbor district, including all field notes, all annotations, all supplementary observations, for the duration of the war and one year prior.

All annotations. All supplementary observations.

Emrys walked back to the census office through the fog with the order folded in his breast pocket, and the whole way he was thinking about a line of ink he had written in October, in a freezing waystation, in a hand shakier than usual because the stove had gone out and because the woman sitting across from him had just said something that had made him forget, briefly, how to be a census taker.

*Crane, O. — no prior registration located. Claims Vessany interior origin; documentation consistent but unusually complete. Recommend standard follow-up.*

He had written it because it was true, and because it was his job, and because some part of him had believed—even then, even with her watching him across the dying fire—that the truth belonged in the record whether or not it served anyone. Now the record was being taken away to serve someone else, and he could not stop turning the sentence over. *Documentation consistent but unusually complete.* A clerk reading that would see nothing. A historian looking for a courier—a historian who had been told, perhaps, that a courier had come down the river in the last weeks of the war carrying dispatches that certain parties very much wished had drowned with the republic—might read it another way entirely.

Or might read it as nothing at all. That was the thing about paper. It did not argue. It lay there and let you make of it what you would, and then it outlived you.

* * *

They took the ledgers in batches. Two clerks from the Commission, young men with ink-stained cuffs and the flat courtesy of the newly empowered, carried them out in canvas satchels and logged them against receipts that Emrys signed without reading, because reading them would have changed nothing. His office emptied shelf by shelf. The room grew quieter in a way that had nothing to do with sound. Eleven years of mornings sat in those books—the births he had entered in blue, the deaths in black, the migrations in pencil because people on the move were always provisional—and now they were stacked in a customs-house room under the eyes of men who had never smelled the harbor at low tide.

On the fourth day, Renwick himself came to collect the final batch: the current year's working ledger, the one still open on Emrys's desk, the one with October in it.

"You'll want this back," Emrys said.

"In time. Nothing is destroyed, Master Vale. I want to be clear about that." Renwick wrapped the ledger in oilcloth with his own hands, carefully, the way other men handled infants or explosives. "The Commission does not burn records. We are not the republic." A thin smile. "We build on them."

"What is it you're building toward? If I may ask."

Renwick paused, oilcloth crackling. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"Toward certainty," he said. "There are stories circulating, Master Vale. About the last days of the republic. About gold leaving Vessany in diplomatic pouches. About ministers who sold the armistice terms before they'd finished negotiating them. Some of these stories are enemy fabrications. Some may even be true. Our task is to determine which, definitively, and to close the question forever." He tucked the ledger under his arm. "Closed questions are the foundation of peace. You must have noticed, in your work, how much trouble open questions cause."

"I've noticed how much trouble answers cause," Emrys said. "When they arrive ahead of the questions."

Renwick regarded him for a moment with an expression that was difficult to read—appraisal, perhaps, or amusement, or the particular patience of a man deciding whether a thing was a threat or merely furniture. Then he laughed, short and dry.

"They told me the harbor clerks were dull," he said. "They were wrong about that, at least." At the door he stopped, half-turned. "One more thing. Purely administrative. In your October notes—you'll remember them—I saw a flag on a passenger named Crane. Orsen Crane. Packet *Meridian*, late season crossing. Your note recommends follow-up. Was the follow-up conducted?"

The stove ticked. Somewhere down the lane a barrel hoop broke loose and rolled, ringing, along the cobbles until it fell flat.

"It was," Emrys said. "Mr. Crane's papers checked out. Registration gap explained—interior parish records lost in the Vessany evacuations. I closed the entry."

"Ah." Renwick nodded slowly. "Lost records. Yes. We're finding a great deal of that." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "You'll be hearing from us, Master Vale. The Commission values thorough men. Thoroughness is so rare, and so useful."

The door closed behind him. Emrys stood in his half-emptied office and listened to the carriage pull away, and understood, with the slow cold clarity of water seeping into a hull, exactly what he had just done.

He had lied to the Commission. Smoothly, plausibly, in the language of his profession—and Crane's papers had not checked out, not really, and the gap had not been explained, and he had closed nothing. He had opened a door in himself he had not known was there, and Renwick had walked past it without looking, and now Emrys Vale, meticulous man, keeper of the harbor's small truths, was a falsifier of records. It had taken one sentence. One sentence spoken in a warm room to a smiling man.

And underneath the fear, quieter and more alarming, was the other thing: the realization that he was glad.

* * *

That night he could not sleep. He lit the lamp at his kitchen table and took out the one document the Commission had not taken, because they did not know it existed: his private notebook, the pocket-sized one he kept against the day the official ledgers burned or flooded or simply wore out, because a census taker who trusted paper alone was a fool. In it, in his small upright hand, were thirty years of marginalia. Corrections. Doubts. Things observed and not entered.

He turned to October.

*Crane, O.* The name sat on the page in his own ink, and around it clustered everything he had chosen not to put in the official ledger: the way Crane's hands had moved while mending that netting on the packet deck, quick and sure and oddly graceful; the way Crane had answered the question about the home parish a half-second too fast, the answer polished smooth as river stone; the way Crane had looked at him across the waystation stove—not with guilt, which he had seen a thousand times in a thousand faces, but with a kind of braced attention, like a deer that has decided the hound is not yet a threat and is measuring the distance to the treeline anyway.

And beneath that, in pencil, added later in the dark of his own rooms, the sentence he had never let himself finish:

*A person who answers perfectly is a person who has practiced.*

He sat with the notebook open for a long time. The lamp hissed. Fog pressed at the glass. Out on the water, unseen, the ice groaned and shifted in its winter sleep, and somewhere beyond it, upstream, lay the drowned republic and whatever truth had come down the river sewn into a coat lining.

He thought about what paper could do. He had never thought about it much before; paper had been his trade the way rope is a rigger's trade, useful, unexamined. But now he saw it plainly. A line in a ledger had sent men to the press-gangs. A missing line had erased families entire. And soon, in the Commission's hands, lines like his—*no prior registration located, recommend follow-up*—could become a thread pulled through the whole fabric of a life, until it unraveled, unless someone chose to knot it off.

His notes could expose her. That was the plain arithmetic of it. Renwick had already read the flag; the Commission's clerks would cross-reference the *Meridian*'s manifest, the waystation logs, the harbor lodging registers, and if any of them was as thorough as Emrys was, the thread would hold weight.

But paper cut both ways. Paper could protect. A corrected entry, a closed question, a record that said *investigated and resolved*—paper could stand between a person and the state like a wall, if the state believed its own paper. The Commission built on records, Renwick had said. Which meant the Commission could be built against, with records. With better ones.

Emrys closed the notebook. He did not yet know what he was going to do, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like swallowed river water—cold, heavy, impossible to spit out.

What he knew was this: somewhere in Harborton, in the crowded lanes below the citadel where the refugees sold what they had left, a person named Crane was walking through a city full of eyes, believing herself invisible. And Emrys Vale held, in a lamp-lit kitchen, the single most dangerous page in the harbor district—and the only one that might, if he was careful enough, if he was brave enough, if he was as good with paper as he had always believed himself to be—be made to save her instead.

He blew out the lamp. In the dark, the ice on the river spoke again, a long cracking sigh, like something enormous beginning to turn over in its sleep.

Three days later, a summons came from the customs house, unsigned except by the Commission's seal. Renwick requested his presence. The subject line, in a clerk's neat hand, read:

*Re: Passenger Crane, O. — further inquiry.*
