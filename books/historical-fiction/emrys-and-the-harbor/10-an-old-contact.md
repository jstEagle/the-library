## 10. An Old Contact

The summons had come for Emrys, not for her—she had learned that much by watching the customs house from the fish-market colonnade, three mornings running, the way she had once watched ministries in Vessany: not staring, never staring, only letting her eyes rest where they needed to while her hands did something innocent. She had shelled sunflower seeds into her pocket until her fingertips stank of oil and salt, and through all of it the ledger clerk's window had stayed shut, and Emrys Vale had gone in at half past eight and come out at noon with his collar crooked and his face the color of old tallow.

So they had found him. Which meant they had found the thread, and were pulling it gently, the way a careful hand draws a splinter.

Which meant it was time to move.

She left her lodging at the Widow Sarn's before dawn, paying what she owed and a little more, saying nothing about luggage because she wanted none of it remembered. The dispatches rode against her ribs as always, sewn flat into the coat lining in four packets no thicker than prayer cards, and beneath them, folded small as a confession, the scrap of paper she had carried since Vessany burned: *If I am lost, ask for Bess at the Salt and Anchor. Tell her the eel is in the net.* Her handler Corvin's handwriting, done in haste on the last night, when the ministers' carriages were already rolling east out of the capital and the sky over the Ministry of Rivers glowed the dull orange of a city burning its own paperwork.

Corvin was dead. She had known that since the crossing—known it the way one knows a limb is gone even through the phantom ache of it. But Corvin had built his chains to outlive him. A cutout was supposed to be exactly that: a blade of separation, so that no link in the chain could name the next, only the shape of the next. The Salt and Anchor. Bess. *The eel is in the net.* Somewhere at the end of that phrase there was still a functioning world—a courier, a route, an archive door that opened. Without it, she carried four packets of paper worth a dozen hanging offenses, and every hour she kept them made her less a courier and more a reliquary of evidence with legs.

*Evidence walking,* Corvin would have said, and he would have said it laughing, because Corvin had laughed at everything, right up until whatever it was that had stopped him.

Harborton woke around her as she walked north along the harbor road. Gulls quarreling over fish guts on the cobbles. A victors' patrol marching in the easy slouch of men who had won and knew it, their new flags—black field, white wheel—snapping above the customs pier where last month the republic's colors had hung. Refugee children playing knucklebones against a warehouse wall with stones instead of bones, because bones cost food. The whole city smelled of pitch and brine and woodsmoke and too many people, and under all of it, faint but constant, the river ice groaning like an old floor.

She had dressed down for the walk: patched coat, mended collar, the stoop she had taught herself years ago, shoulders curled inward as if apologizing for their breadth. In Vessany she had learned that a man who took up no room drew no eyes. It was a different grammar here, but the language was the same.

The Salt and Anchor stood where the harbor road bent away from the water, at the seam between the fishing quarter and the warehouse district—a low, tar-black building with a signboard so salt-scoured the anchor on it had become more suggestion than image. Smoke stood up from its chimney, thin and straight in the windless morning. A good omen or a bad one; smoke told you nothing except that someone was home.

Orsen paused across the street, in the shadow of a stack of herring barrels, and gave the tavern the full discipline of her old training. Windows: two front, one side, all shuttered except the side one, through which lamplight leaked yellow. Door: closed but not locked-looking; a cat sat on the step washing one paw, which meant the door opened often. Patrons: none visible, but it was barely seven, an honest hour for a tavern to be empty. Watchers: none that she could find, though she looked twice at a cart parked too neatly at the corner, its driver asleep—or arranged to look asleep—and then decided she was being hunted and everything would look like a trap if she let it.

*You cannot stand here until the barrels rot,* she told herself. *And you cannot go in blind.*

But there was no other way in. That was the cruelty of cutouts: they were doors that could only be tested by opening them. Corvin had never given her a password beyond the phrase itself, never given her Bess's description, never told her whether Bess was young or old, sharp or soft, loyal or merely paid. He had said once, in one of their candle-lit lessons in the back rooms of the River Office: *"A chain you can see entirely is a chain already broken, Crane. You trust your link. That is all anyone ever gets to trust."*

She crossed the street.

The tavern's interior was warmer than she expected and darker than she liked: a low ceiling black with decades of pipe smoke, a bar of planched oak worn pale where elbows had rested, a stove ticking in the corner like a slow clock. The smell was beer, brine, tobacco, and underneath it something else—candle wax, and paper. Fresh paper. Her nose caught it before her eyes found anything: the particular dry, faintly sweet scent of new stock, the kind used in record offices and counting houses.

Behind the bar stood a woman of perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, gray hair pinned in a coil so tight it looked load-bearing. She was polishing a glass with a cloth and watching the door as if she had been watching it since before Orsen knocked, which she had—the latch had clicked twice before Orsen touched it, a signal any professional gives without thinking, and the woman's hands had not paused in their work, but her eyes had moved.

"The eel," Orsen said quietly, "is in the net."

The cloth stopped.

For a long moment the woman looked at her—not at her coat or her boots or her forged papers, which was what everyone else in Harborton looked at, but at her face, at her eyes, at the way she stood. Reading her, the way one reads a seal on a letter. Then she set the glass down with a small precise click and turned toward the stairs at the back of the room.

"Not here," she said. Her voice was low, unpolished, a harbor voice. "Kitchen."

* * *

The kitchen behind the Salt and Anchor was clean in a way the front room pretended not to be: copper pots scoured to mirrors, bread rising under cloth, a knife rack with every blade accounted for. The woman lit no lamp. Gray morning came through a high window, enough to see by, and she positioned herself beneath it so that her own face stayed shadowed while Orsen's was lit—an old trick, and Orsen noted it, and hated that noting it gave her comfort.

"Bess," the woman said, without warmth and without offering a hand. "Who sent you?"

"Corvin. Of the River Office. He wrote—" Orsen began, and reached for the scrap of paper.

"I know what he wrote." Bess's eyes flicked to Orsen's hand and back. "Don't take it out. Nothing written comes out in this kitchen anymore. Sit."

There was a stool by the bread table. Orsen sat, keeping her coat loose, keeping her weight forward, the way one sits when one may need to stand quickly. Bess did not sit. She moved to the stove, lifted a kettle that had already been hot, and poured two cups of something dark and bitter-smelling, and the gesture was so nearly kind that Orsen felt her chest tighten around it before Bess spoke again and killed the kindness dead.

"How long have you been carrying?"

"Since the ninth of Frostmonth. Six weeks."

"And before that?"

"Three weeks in the capital. Waiting on the final seals."

Bess set a cup in front of her. "Six weeks," she repeated, as if weighing the number on a scale and finding it obscene. "Six weeks you've been walking around this city with—how many pages?"

"That isn't—"

"It is precisely my business." Bess pulled a stool out from under the table and sat opposite, forearms on her knees, close enough now that Orsen could see her eyes properly for the first time: brown, bloodshot, and very tired, tired in a way that sleep does not touch. "Girl. Listen to me, because I'm going to say this once and then neither of us will ever say it again. There is no network."

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard complained under a footstep—lodgers, real lodgers, ordinary ones—and settled.

"The archive couriers were rolled up before the ice set," Bess went on, level as a clerk reading totals. "Eleven days after the capital fell. They didn't torture anyone, if that matters to you—they didn't need to. They simply took the harbor registry cross-indexes, the toll ledgers, the ferry manifests. Every face that had crossed a certain way too many times, at certain hours, in certain seasons. Names. Addresses. My brother-in-law was one of them; he ran the southern leg for nine years and never knew my name, and they had him inside a week anyway, because paper remembers what people are promised it forgets." She drank, grimaced at her own coffee. "Four are in the citadel yard now. The rest scattered. Some south, some into the marsh towns. One drowned himself off the coal quay rather than be questioned, and I won't tell you his name because you'd only grieve him and grief is a luxury today."

Orsen heard all of it, and understood all of it, and yet some part of her mind kept returning to the smallest detail, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. "The candle wax," she said. "And the paper. Upstairs. What do you stock fresh paper for?"

Something moved in Bess's face—not quite respect. "Victors pay for copies," she said. "Of anything. Shipping schedules, cargo manifests, whose boat went where. I sell them paper trails. It keeps this house boring, and boring is the only flag of safety left on this coast." She leaned back. "It also means I know what they're looking for, and lately they're looking for a courier out of Vessany. Unlogged passenger on the *Meridian*. Papers too clean. They've offered forty crowns for information leading to recovery of 'documents of state significance.'" She pronounced the phrase with delicate contempt, quoting it perfectly; she had read the notice more than once. "Eighty if recovered intact. That notice went up eleven days ago."

Eleven days. The same arithmetic, everywhere. *Re: Passenger Crane, O. — further inquiry.* Emrys summoned to the customs house. The splinter being drawn gently.

"So there is no depot," Orsen said slowly, working it through aloud because her mind refused to do it silently. "No courier leg north. No sealed channel to the archive. No one to receive—"

"There's an archive," Bess said. "Buildings don't get arrested. There's even a deposition office with a public duty to receive sworn testimony—it's in the old charters, neutral law, and the victors haven't dared close it yet because closing it looks bad in front of the trading consuls. That door exists." She set her cup down. "What doesn't exist anymore is anyone to carry you to it safely. What doesn't exist is me knowing a soul in that building I can trust past sundown. What doesn't exist is the chain, girl. Corvin built a beautiful chain and the victors melted it down for lead. You're standing in the last link."

The words landed one after another, each with its own small weight, like coins counted out onto a table. Orsen found her hands had gone cold around the warm cup. She thought of six weeks of discipline—the mending, the whittling, the rehearsed biography, the hours spent being nobody so that the papers could survive—and felt the entire architecture of it tilt beneath her, the way ice tilts before it goes.

"You should burn them," Bess said, not unkindly.

"No."

"They're paper. However true, however damning—they're paper. You're not. Melt the wax, feed the stove, walk away, take a boat south, grow vegetables under some other name—"

"They prove the ministers sold the peace terms." Her own voice came out lower than she intended, roughened. "They prove the armistice was signed by men who had already divided the river tolls among themselves a week before. Fourteen signatures. Sealed orders. If they're burned, then in twenty years the official history says the republic died of its own weakness, honestly, tragically, and the men who sold it write the prefaces. I've seen what victors' histories do, Bess. I've been reading one being written since I stepped off the packet."

Bess regarded her for a long moment. The light from the high window had shifted; morning was advancing out there, patrols and notices and eighty-crown informants, and here in this kitchen two women sat over cooling coffee with the whole weight of it between them.

"You sound like him," Bess said at last. "Corvin. Same stubbornness. He sat in that stool"—she nodded at Orsen's—"eleven years ago, with a satchel of toll-fraud evidence nobody wanted, and said almost those exact words. *Paper is the only country that doesn't fall.*" She exhaled through her nose, something too brief and too bitter to be a laugh. "He was wrong, incidentally. Paper falls faster than cities. I've watched it happen."

"Then why keep this house?" Orsen asked. "Why sell the victors their paper trails and stay? Why not run south with your brother-in-law's share of the fear?"

For the first time, Bess smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of someone holding a knife handle-down and letting you see that she held it. "Because somebody has to know which of them can be bribed, which can be frightened, and which are merely stupid. And because when someone like you walks through my door carrying the end of a story, I want to at least be able to tell her how bad it is before the ending finds her." She stood, taking both cups, and turned to the basin, and spoke to the window rather than to Orsen. "Here's how bad it is. Your census-taker friend—the tidy one from the packet, don't look surprised, this is a harbor and harbors hear things—he was called to the customs house three days ago by a historian named Renwick. Aldous Renwick. Commission on Records and Reconciliation. Clever man; writes beautiful prose; believes sincerely in what he does, which makes him ten times worse than a cynic. Renwick is assembling the official account of the fall, and he has requisitioned every manifest, every ledger, every scrap of small paper this port has produced in two years. Including census sheets." She set the cups in the basin, one after another, deliberate little chimes. "Including, I would wager, a certain passenger listed as Crane, O., whose registration history appears nowhere in any book Renwick can find."

"He wouldn't give me up." The words left Orsen before judgment could catch them, and she heard how they sounded—heard the raw thing in them—and pressed her lips shut.

Bess turned around, drying her hands. She looked at Orsen for a long moment with something almost gentle moving behind the fatigue.

"No," she agreed. "I don't suppose he would. Not straight away. Men like that bend slowly, and it costs them, and they hate every degree of it. But he'll be followed now, whether he knows it or not, and anything he does for you he does under their eyes. You can't use him. Do you understand me? Whatever he is to you—and I can see that he is something—you cannot afford the luxury."

Outside, somewhere down toward the water, a patrol whistle blew twice and fell silent. The ice on the river groaned, long and low, the same sound Emrys had heard through his darkened window, as if the winter itself were keeping count of the days.

Orsen rose. Her knees were stiff from the stool, from six weeks of stiffening, from the cold that had gotten into her somewhere on the *Meridian* and never quite left. She buttoned her coat to the throat, over the four flat packets that were now, as of this morning, the entire surviving chain of a broken network—the last link, walking.

"If there's no one to carry them," she said, "then I carry them myself. To the deposition office. Under my own name."

Bess had been reaching for a cloth to dry the basin. Her hands stopped.

"Do you know what happens," she said quietly, "to women who put their names in victors' records?"

"I know what happens to women whose names appear nowhere at all when the historians come looking." Orsen pulled her cap down, squared the stoop back into her shoulders, and became, in the space of a breath, plain Crane again, nobody, a mended coat on a mended man. At the kitchen door she paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"Thank you for telling me how bad it is," she said. "That's more than the chain ever did."

Behind her, in the clean bright kitchen of the Salt and Anchor, Bess said nothing at all—which was, Orsen would understand later, the closest the old smuggler could come to a blessing.

Out on the harbor road the morning had turned hard and clear, the gulls loud, the victors' flags snapping above the customs pier. And halfway down the first street, leaning against a post at the fish-market edge as though he had been sent by no one and had simply failed to stop waiting, stood Emrys Vale—with two men in Commission gray standing thirty paces behind him, and his eyes fixed on her with an expression she had seen once before, over a ledger, on the frozen deck of a stranded packet:

the expression of a man who has finally decided which side of the page he is writing on.
