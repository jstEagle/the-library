## 11. Ledger Crossings

The requisition order arrived on a Tuesday, folded twice and smelling faintly of pipe smoke and authority.

Emrys Vale read it standing in the doorway of the harbor census office while the morning fog burned off the water outside the window, and he read it three times because the first two readings refused to settle into sense. It was a simple document, as such documents went. The Office of Historical Commission—*victors' historians*, the dockhands called them, with the flat contempt men reserve for those who write about wars they did not fight—required access to all population registries, manifest ledgers, and census records pertaining to the harbor districts of Harborton for the years of the war and one year prior. All records. All years. *In the interest of constructing an accurate and permanent account of the late conflict.*

Permanent, Emrys thought, was doing a great deal of work in that sentence.

He set the order on his desk, weighted its corners with the inkwell, the paper knife, a chipped cup of coffee going cold, and looked at it the way he had learned to look at anything that frightened him: as a problem of columns and entries, of names and numbers, of the small honest arithmetic by which he had made his living for eleven years.

The trouble was that somewhere in those columns was a name he had entered himself, in his own careful hand, and that name had begun to feel less like an entry and more like a debt.

*Orsen Crane.*

---

The historians' clerks arrived at midday: two young men in gray coats cut too well for clerks, and a woman with spectacles and a portable copying press who set up in the corner of his office as though she had been assigned to it at birth. They were polite. That was the worst of it. No one kicked down doors; no one put a hand on his shoulder. The new state did not need to shout. It needed only to ask, patiently and in triplicate, until everything a man knew lay open on the table like a gutted fish.

"We'll need certified copies," the woman said, ink-stamping a receipt without looking up. "Originals remain in your custody pending transfer. You'll be contacted regarding any discrepancies."

"Discrepancies," Emrys repeated.

"Gaps." She glanced up then, briefly, over the rims of her spectacles. Her eyes were not unkind; they were simply very thorough. "Every record has them. Men lost between registries. Names that appear nowhere before the war and vanish after. The commission's task is to close those gaps."

"And when they can't be closed?"

She smiled, thin as paper. "Then we note who failed to close them."

They worked through the afternoon, and Emrys worked beside them, because refusing to help would have been its own kind of answer, and because some stubborn professional instinct told him that if he was going to be audited, he would rather hold the pen himself. He pulled volumes from the shelves. He unlocked the cabinet of river manifests. He answered questions about ink formulas and filing conventions in the level voice of a man discussing weather, while inside him something paced and watched and waited.

By evening the clerks had gone, taking their receipts and their courtesy, and Emrys sat alone in the lamplight with the ledger he had not let them see opened past page forty.

---

The thing about a ledger, Emrys had told apprentices once—he had never had apprentices, but he imagined telling them—is that it does not merely record. It *argues*. Every entry is testimony. Every column is a cross-examination. A well-kept registry will tell you things about a town that the town itself does not know: which streets emptied in the famine year, which families doubled up behind single doors, which names changed spelling when the border moved. Paper remembers what flesh is too tired or too frightened to say aloud.

And paper, he thought now, turning to the section he had been avoiding since the packet came up the river with her aboard, paper also *accuses*.

He found the entry where he had known he would find it. Harbor district, winter quarter, arrivals by river conveyance. *Crane, Orsen. Occupation: clerk. Point of origin: Vessany interior, via the Meridian packet.* His own handwriting, steady as ever even then, even with her eyes on him across the cabin table—or perhaps steadier because of them, because he had pressed harder than he needed to, the way a man grips a railing.

Beside it, in the margin, his own small notation—the private shorthand census takers use when something does not reconcile: *no prior reg.*

No prior registration. In eleven years of keeping these books he had written that notation perhaps thirty times, and twenty-eight of those times it had meant a stillbirth unrecorded, a smuggler, a dead man's papers on a living face. He had followed up on every one of them, because thoroughness was not a virtue in his trade but a reflex, like blinking. He had followed up on Crane too—or told himself he intended to—and had not, and the gap between intention and act had widened quietly all winter, the way ice widens beneath snow, invisible until it gives.

Now the historians wanted gaps closed. Now failure to close them would be *noted*.

Emrys dipped his pen, and stopped.

Here was the arithmetic of the thing, laid out as plainly as any column: A man calling himself Orsen Crane appeared in no registry before this winter. The commission required explanation. Explanation, if honestly pursued, ended either in exposure—a woman traveling under false papers in a city filling with victors' agents, and Emrys Vale the man whose diligence delivered her—or in falsification, which was a crime against the state's records, and the state had shown, in its first months, exactly how patient it was with crimes against its records. There was no third column. He checked again, the way he always checked, running the entries down the page with his fingertip. There was no third column.

Except.

Except that there was a fourth possibility he kept declining to enter, and it was this: that the question itself was wrong. That a registry could be complete and still be false—that the truest account of a man might be the one the columns could not hold. He thought of her hands that night at the waystation, mending a seam in the lamplight with the absorbed precision of someone who had learned that idle hands invite questions. He thought of the half-truths she had told him about the fall of Vessany, each one accurate as far as it went, and how the distance between what she said and what she meant had felt less like deception than like armor. He thought of the moment their eyes had met over his ledger, and the way suspicion and something warmer had arrived together, inseparable, like weather off the water.

Duty, he wrote in his mind, was a word the new state used constantly now, stamped on proclamations and painted on customs sheds. Duty to the record. Duty to the truth. But whose truth? The commission wanted a *permanent account of the late conflict*, and Emrys had lived long enough among archives to know what permanence meant when victors held the pens: it meant that whatever was written first, in official ink, became the truth retroactively, and everything else became rumor, and then nothing.

If he flagged Crane—if he wrote *no prior reg. referred to commission* in the margin, the way procedure demanded—then his small honest notation would become an instrument. Not a record anymore. A warrant. Somewhere down the chain, a clerk would copy it, an agent would circle it, and a woman who had done nothing he could name except survive would find the net drawn tight around her, and his conscience would be permitted to observe that he had only followed procedure.

Complicity, Emrys thought, did not knock loudly. It knocked precisely the way this requisition had knocked: politely, in triplicate, smelling of pipe smoke, asking only for copies of what already existed.

---

He worked late. Outside, the harbor sounds thinned—winches falling silent one by one, gulls settling on the masts, the foghorn beginning its long patient argument with the dark. The river, he knew, was still frozen solid out to the middle channel; the ice would hold another month at least, maybe six weeks, and until it broke, the crossings stayed open and the victors' writ ran uninterrupted along every road into the city. When the thaw came, everything would change at once. Everyone said so. The whole harbor seemed to be holding its breath against the sound of cracking ice.

He turned pages. He copied what the clerks had marked, collated what they had flagged, and built—as his nature demanded—a private reconciliation of his own: a list of every person in his registers whose existence the commission's criteria rendered suspect. Refugees whose origin towns no longer existed. Men whose regimental records contradicted their stated ages. Widows remarried under slightly altered surnames. Forty-one names by midnight. Any one of them, handed over, would buy him the clerks' approval and the woman's thin smile.

At the bottom of the list, unnumbered, he wrote *Crane, Orsen*, and beneath it, after a long pause, the entry he could not stop composing:

*Point of origin unverifiable prior to winter arrival. Demeanor and particulars consistent with concealment of identity. Nature and motive of concealment unknown. Subject committed no offense observable to this office.*

He read the last clause back and heard how it sounded—how it would sound to a hearing officer, how it would sound to her. *Subject committed no offense observable to this office.* As though she were a discrepancy to be adjudicated rather than a person who had sat across from him in a frozen waystation and told him half-truths because the whole truth would have killed her. As though the ledger were the only room she existed in.

His lamp guttered. He trimmed the wick, and in the steadied light his eye fell on the requisition receipt, still lying where the clerk had left it, and he noticed for the first time the line item at the bottom he had skimmed past in the morning:

*Particular attention to be given persons of interest identified in transit manifests, incl. couriers, dispatch-bearers, and agents of the former Vessany diplomatic service.*

Couriers. Dispatch-bearers.

He sat back slowly, and the chair creaked in the empty office, and the sound seemed very loud. He thought of the coat she had worn aboard the *Meridian*, heavy for the season, handled carefully, mended at the lining. He thought of the way she had watched the republic's colors come down through the packet's stern glass and turned away with the expression of a woman carrying something heavier than luggage. He had catalogued all of it months ago, in the wordless way he catalogued everything, and filed it under a heading he had never dared to write out. Now the commission had written it for him.

They were not hunting discrepancies. Discrepancies were pretext. They were hunting *her*—or whoever else like her moved through his pages—and his ledger was the net they meant to cast, and he was being invited, courteously, in triplicate, to draw it closed with his own meticulous hand.

Emrys took the private reconciliation list—all forty-two entries—and held its corner over the lamp flame until it caught, then dropped it into the cold grate and watched the names curl and brighten and go gray. It was not courage. He understood that. It was housekeeping, and insufficient, because the originals remained in his custody pending transfer, and every fact on the burnt page existed elsewhere, in official ink, waiting only to be connected.

What remained was the choice itself, reduced now to its smallest possible form: a single marginal notation, on a single page, in a single ledger. *Referred to commission*—four words that would make him safe and make him a collaborator. Or silence—which would make him suspect, and might make him a shield.

He turned to the winter-quarter page. He wet his pen. His hand, so steady with eleven years of entries, hung above the margin for a long moment, the nib trembling a fraction, gathering a bead of ink like a held breath.

Then, downstairs, the street door of the census office clicked—softly, deliberately, the latch lifted by someone who knew it stuck—and footsteps started up the stairs, unhurried and familiar, and Emrys knew before she rounded the landing that it was her, and that she had seen the requisition order posted at the harbor master's office, and that whatever she had come to say or ask or confess, he would have to decide, very soon now, which side of his own ledger he was standing on.
