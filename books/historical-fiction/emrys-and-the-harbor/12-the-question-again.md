## 12. The Question Again

The tide had turned against the coal quay, and the smell of the harbor changed with it—brine giving way to something older, darker, the mineral breath of mudflats exposed at low water. Emrys Vale stood beneath the leaning eave of a shuttered chandlery, his ledger case in one hand, and watched the figure cross the frozen ruts of Rope Walk with a gait he had come to know better than his own signature.

Orsen Crane walked like a man who had learned to walk twice. That was the thing Emrys could not stop turning over, the splinter lodged under the skin of his thoughts since the packet: that every motion of this person was correct, and yet the correctness itself was wrong. Men did not think about how they walked. Men simply walked. But Crane moved through the world the way a student moves through an examination hall—watchful of his own hands, his own shoulders, the set of his jaw—as though at any moment an examiner might rise from the crowd and say, *again, from the beginning.*

Emrys had spent three days telling himself it was none of his business. He had filed his census sheets. He had answered the historians' clerks with the flat courtesy of a small man protecting a small office. He had walked past the Gull and Anchor twice without going in.

Three days was enough. The question had grown teeth.

He stepped out from the eave. "Crane."

The effect was immediate and instructive. Crane did not startle—that would have been a tell, and Crane did not permit himself tells—but something behind the eyes *reorganized*, swift as cards being reshuffled, before settling into mild inquiry. "Vale." A nod, measured to the ounce. "Still counting heads?"

"Always someone uncounted." Emrys fell into step beside him without invitation, because an invitation could be refused and proximity could not. "Walk with me. There's a stretch of the seawall past the salt sheds where the wind makes conversation difficult for anyone who might be listening."

Something flickered across Crane's face—appraisal, calculation, and beneath both, briefly, a weariness so profound it looked almost like relief. "You've been practicing that sentence."

"Since Tuesday."

They walked. The harbor lay gray and breathing around them, ice floes grinding in the channel with a sound like slow applause, the masts of refugee barges standing up out of the fog in their hundreds, bare as winter trees. Gulls argued over the fish-market leavings. Somewhere a victors' patrol drummed its unhurried rhythm along the customs house steps, and both men's ears bent toward it and away again in the same instant.

At the salt sheds the wind did indeed rise, tearing the words from their mouths if they spoke above a certain pitch, and Emrys stopped where a broken bollard gave them shelter and the sea beyond was loud enough to drown a confession.

"I'm not going to report you," he said.

Crane went very still. Not the stillness of innocence—the stillness of a hunted animal that has heard the hound stop. "I don't know what you mean."

"Yes, you do." Emrys kept his voice level, pitched under the wind. "I take censuses. I have taken them for eleven years, first for the harbor district of Vessany and now—" he gestured vaguely at the flags of occupation drooping on the customs house, "—for whoever is willing to pay for the same numbers. I know what a man looks like when his papers are true. And I know what you look like when yours are perfect. Perfection, Mr. Crane, is the loudest lie there is. Everything about you checks out. Nothing about you breathes."

For a long moment Crane said nothing. The wind filled the silence with the harbor's own noise—the groan of ice, the slap of black water, the far-off clang of the buoy bell—and Emrys understood that he was watching a decision being made inside another human being, a decision with doors opening and closing behind it, and that whatever came next would not come again.

"You asked me once," Crane said finally, "where I was registered before the war."

"In Kellin parish. You said. With dates."

"With dates. And you checked."

"I checked."

"And it held."

"It held perfectly." Emrys looked out at the water rather than at him; it seemed kinder. "That's what convinced me. No living memory holds perfectly. Only paper does. You'd built yourself out of documents, Mr. Crane, and documents don't remember rain."

* * *

They found the lee of the salt shed itself, a slatted wooden wall stained white with years of spray, and sat on an upturned dory with their backs to the wind. Crane pulled off one glove, then the other, slowly, as though undressing were a ceremony requiring witnesses, and Emrys saw that the hands were shaking—not with cold.

"My name," Crane said, "is Orsena."

The word landed between them on the wet planks like a coin dropped in a church. Emrys did not move. He had suspected—he had *known*, in the way a census taker knows, by arithmetic rather than certainty—but knowing a thing and hearing it are different countries, and the crossing between them is longer than any river.

"Orsena Crane," she said again, steadier now, as though the second saying had cost less than the first. "Born in Vessany, in the year the republic adopted its second constitution, which my father called the year of beautiful lies. My mother kept a stationer's shop. I kept the accounts. When the war came, they conscripted the men of the parish by lottery, and when the lottery missed me and took my brother instead, and my brother died at Marrow Ford, and my father followed him within the season—there was no one left to keep the shop but a woman, and a woman alone in a garrison town is not a person, Vale. She is a resource. Or a target. Or a rumor waiting to happen."

She turned her hands over, studying the palms as though reading a map of everywhere they had not been allowed to go.

"So I became my own cousin. I buried Orsena in the churchyard in all but fact—I put her death in the parish register myself, in my own hand, because I had kept the register and no one questioned my pen. And Orsen Crane came out of that grave and signed on as a dispatch clerk, because a clerk travels, and a clerk is ignored, and a clerk's coat has linings." Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "You sewed a straight seam once in your life?"

"Never."

"Then you can't imagine what four years of straight seams will teach a person about patience."

Emrys found his voice had gone somewhere and had to go looking for it. "Why are you telling me this? You don't know me. I'm a census taker. I am, by profession, the state's memory—and the state that remembers now is not your friend."

"That's exactly why." She looked at him then, fully, and it was the first time he had seen her whole face do anything but perform. What lived underneath the performance was tired, and fierce, and young in a way the disguise had never permitted. "Because you found the flaw in me three weeks ago on a freezing packet and said nothing. Because you burned nothing, reported nothing, sold nothing. Because everyone else who knows what I am wants to use it—Bess wants my cargo, Renwick wants my name, the victors want my body in a cell or a ditch—and you're the only soul in this harbor who seems to want only to *understand* it. I have been no one for four years, Vale. Absolutely no one. It kept me alive and it was killing me by inches. If I am to be caught—and I may be caught—I find I cannot bear the idea of being caught by strangers who never knew there was anyone here at all."

The wind gusted, flinging spray against the shed wall, and in the white noise of it she said, very quietly: "So now one person knows. One. And I find the sky doesn't fall."

Emrys looked down at his hands, the ink-stained fingers, the callus where his pen rested, and felt the full weight of what she had handed him settle onto his shoulders like wet wool. She had given him the power to end her. She had done it deliberately, with open eyes, because the alternative—dying unknown—was worse. He had never been trusted with anything heavier than a ledger, and here was a whole life, folded into his keeping like a page too important to file.

"Orsena," he said, trying the name aloud for the first time. It fit the air differently than *Crane* had—softer at the edges, older somehow, as though it had been waiting in storage all these years.

Her breath caught. Just barely. But he saw it, and she knew he saw it, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

"No one has said that name since I wrote it into the burial register," she said. "Four years. It sounds—" She stopped, started again. "It sounds like someone I might have become, if the war hadn't happened. Someone with a shop and bad handwriting and a cat."

"What happened to the shop?"

"Burned in the second winter. With most of the street." She said it flatly, the way people say things they have decided not to feel anymore, and then, unexpectedly, the flatness cracked: "I keep the inventory anyway. In my head. Reams of foolscap, forty dozen steel pens, three gross of sealing wax, red and black. I could stock that shop tomorrow from memory. Isn't that mad? The republic is dead, my family is dead, everything I was is ash—and what keeps me awake at night is *forty dozen steel pens.*"

"It isn't mad," Emrys said. "It's grief. Grief always goes where the door was left open."

She laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, as though laughter too were contraband—and pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. When she lowered it her eyes were bright and furious at themselves. "Don't be kind to me. I can't afford it. Kindness is how Bess got her hooks in half the couriers in Vessany, and kindness is how Renwick will get his hooks in you, if he hasn't already."

"He's tried."

"And?"

"And I told him I was a very dull man with a very complete ledger." Emrys hesitated. "Which is becoming less true by the hour."

The tide was coming back in; they could hear the change in the channel, the ice loosening its grip with small reports like distant musketry. Orsena rose from the dory, and the performance began to reassemble itself even as she stood—the squared shoulders, the level gaze, the man named Crane settling back over her like a coat taken down from its hook. But it went on more slowly now, and Emrys understood that it would never fit quite the same way again. A mask, once removed before a witness, does not sit flush upon the face thereafter.

"The dispatches are safe," she said. "That's all you need to know, and less than you want to. In a few days, one way or another, they'll be in the archive or they'll be at the bottom of the channel, and either way the truth will be somewhere the historians can't burn it."

"And you?"

"I'll be wherever the truth is." She pulled her gloves back on, finger by finger. "Thank you for the wind, Vale. It was a good choice of place."

She turned toward Rope Walk, and Emrys watched her go, and it was only after she had passed the first corner that he realized two things at once, arriving together like a blow and its echo.

The first was that he loved her—or had begun to, somewhere on the frozen packet, in the space between a question and an answer too perfect—and that this love had no honest shape, no lawful future, no name the census would recognize.

The second was that in the pocket of his coat, against his heart, lay the flagged page from his own ledger—the page bearing the name *Crane, O., registration unresolved*—the page Renwick's clerks had already asked to see once, and would ask to see again.

And the river ice, all down the gray length of the harbor, had begun, very softly, to crack.
