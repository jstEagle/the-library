## 13. Bargains in Smoke

The Salt Latch sat where the harbor district forgot itself—a crooked lane of ropewalks and tar-sheds that ended in mud and mooring posts, the kind of street a mapmaker would leave blank out of embarrassment. Orsen found it by the smell before she found it by the sign: brine, woodsmoke, and the sweet rot of spilled beer soaked so deep into the floorboards that no winter could freeze it out.

Inside, the light was the color of weak tea. A peat fire struggled in a hearth built for coal, and the smoke had nowhere to go but into the rafters, where it hung in layers like sediment—years of it, decades of it, every argument and confession ever muttered beneath this roof pressed into the black beams overhead. The tavern keeper moved through it all like a woman who owned the weather.

Bess was broader than Orsen remembered from the one glimpse she'd been given, years ago, across a customs yard in Vessany: a big-shouldered woman with forearms like capstans and gray hair cropped close as a convict's. She wore an apron gone stiff with old stains and a ring of keys at her hip that announced her authority to the whole room. When Orsen came through the door, shaking river water off her shoulders, Bess looked up from tapping a cask, and something passed between them—not recognition exactly, but the acknowledgment of two people who knew what kind of cargo the other carried.

"You'll be wanting the corner table," Bess said, not a question. "The one by the window that doesn't face the street."

"I was told to ask for the woman who waters the good beer."

"And I was told to expect a man who asks stupid questions." Bess's mouth did not quite smile. "Sit down before someone decides you're interesting."

Orsen sat. The corner table was tucked behind a stack of empty crates and a coat rack hung with oilskins, and its window gave onto nothing but the blank brick wall of the chandlery next door. From here she could see the whole room without being seen from the door—or nearly. In Harborton there was no *nearly* anymore. The city had grown eyes the way a corpse grows flies.

She ordered nothing at first. She had learned on the packet, and learned again in the lodging houses of the last three days, that a man alone who bought no drink was either poor or watchful, and both drew notice; but a man who nursed one cup for two hours drew less than either. So she ordered the small beer, which was cheap and mostly water anyway, and she let it sweat on the table while she waited for the room to thin.

It did not thin. Refugees came in with the dusk—families from the eastern provinces, their accents thickened by grief, counting coins on the tabletops like men counting bullets. Two victors' officers in slate-gray uniforms took a booth near the fire and drank in silence, and everyone pretended not to see them, which was itself a kind of performance, and everyone performed it well. Harborton's neutrality was a stage play now, Orsen thought, and every soul in the city had been conscripted into the cast.

Bess worked the room with a pitcher and a hard eye, and when she finally came around to the corner table it was with a bowl of fish stew Orsen hadn't ordered.

"House account," Bess said, setting it down. "Eat. You look like a rope left out in the rain."

"I can pay."

"I know you can pay. Eat anyway." She lowered herself onto the bench opposite, which creaked under her, and folded her arms. Up close her face was a topography of old weather—sun-cracked, wind-burned, the lines around her eyes carved deep enough to lose a coin in. "Now. You're Crane. You were sent by a man who used to sign his letters with a drawing of a lark, and you're carrying something that needs to go into the ground where no one will ever dig it up. Am I warm?"

Orsen's hand had gone still on the spoon. "You were told about me."

"I was told about a courier. I wasn't told your name until you said it just now, so we'll call that even." Bess glanced once at the officers by the fire, then back. "Here's what else I was told, before I tell you anything: the network is dead. You know that already or you wouldn't have come looking for me yourself instead of waiting at the drop. So don't waste my time pretending otherwise."

"The drop was burned," Orsen said quietly. "I waited two days. No one came. The chalk mark changed hands twice."

"The chalk mark changed hands because the men who used to change it are in a pit outside Kesselvane, or on a prison barge, or writing confessions with their fingers broken. The lark is done, Crane. Whatever he promised you, whatever chain of trust he built—he built it on sand, and the tide's come in." Bess said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. It was the tone of a woman reporting the price of herring. "So. You have papers. Real ones, I'm guessing, given what you crossed a war to carry. And you have nowhere to put them. And every day you hold them, you're not a courier anymore—you're evidence walking around on two legs."

*Evidence walking.* The phrase settled into Orsen's chest like a swallowed stone. She had been thinking it herself for days, in the rented room with its paper-thin walls, listening to the boots in the corridor, cataloguing every sound for the weight of it. But hearing it said aloud, flatly, by a stranger—it made the thing real in a way her own fear had refused to do.

"What are you offering?" she asked.

Bess leaned forward. Her voice dropped into the register of a woman who had conducted a hundred transactions at this table and buried most of them. "There's a coaster going north on Thursday's tide. Master's name is Ilven; he owes me more than he owes God. He runs salt and timber up the coast and sometimes other things when the money's right. He can take a passenger as far as the free towns past the straits—three weeks' sail, maybe four if the weather turns. Clean papers, clean berth, nobody asks questions because Ilven doesn't hire crew who ask questions." She paused. "Passage isn't cheap. Passage for a man carrying what you're carrying, with the victors' historians crawling over this harbor like ants on a dropped sweet—that's not cheap at all."

"How much?"

"Not coin." Bess's eyes went to Orsen's chest, briefly, the way a man's eyes go to a purse. "The dispatches."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the room a refugee child laughed at something, a bright unguarded sound that seemed to belong to another country.

"No," Orsen said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I heard enough. You want the dispatches."

"I want copies. Originals stay with you if that matters to your conscience—I'm told couriers have consciences, though I've met few. Ilven has buyers. There are men in the free towns, and men further than that, who'd pay a great deal to know what Vessany's ministers signed away and who signed it. Truth's a commodity same as salt, Crane. Better, actually. Salt spoils."

"They aren't mine to sell." Orsen kept her voice level, though her pulse had climbed into her throat. "They're testimony. They were entrusted to me so they could reach the archive, not the auction block."

"The archive." Bess almost laughed. "You mean the Harbor Archive? The one the historians requisitioned yesterday morning? Where every clerk now works under victors' seal, and everything deposited gets read, copied, and filed according to whose story it flatters?" She shook her head slowly. "Child, you'd be handing your truth straight into the victors' mouths and calling it sanctuary."

"They haven't sealed the deposition office."

"They haven't sealed it *yet*. The ice hasn't broken yet either. Everything in this city is a *yet*." Bess spread her big hands on the table. "Listen to me. I've been running things through this harbor since before you could shave, whichever face you were wearing when you learned. I know how records work. Paper in an archive is paper in a fire, sooner or later. Paper in the right buyer's hands is paper that lives forever—and pays for your life besides. You walk out of here with a berth on Ilven's boat and enough coin to disappear properly, and somewhere across the water the truth comes out anyway, in books, in pamphlets, in whatever shape sells. Isn't that what you wanted? The truth known?"

"It's not the same thing." Orsen heard her own voice harden, and did not soften it. "A pamphlet is a weapon. Testimony is a vow. What I carry was sworn—witnesses' names, dates, seals. Orders signed by men who sold the peace while their soldiers died holding the line they'd already traded away. If that reaches the world as merchandise, it becomes just another version. Another account that can be bought and argued with and buried under better-funded accounts. Put it in the record, sworn and sealed and timestamped, and it becomes fact. Fact is harder to kill."

Bess studied her for a long moment. The smoke above them seemed to lean in.

"You'd rather die correct than live rich," she said at last. "That's a luxury, Crane. Luxuries get people buried."

"So does poverty of principle. I've watched a whole republic die of it." Orsen pushed the stew aside untouched; her stomach had closed like a fist. "I crossed a frozen river with these papers sewn against my skin. I watched the man who trained me put a bullet in his own head rather than be taken with half of what I'm carrying. I have spent eleven years being no one so that this—" she touched her chest, lightly, over the lining, "—could be *something*, someday, in a place that keeps what it's given. I am not going to sell it to profiteers so I can run north and spend the rest of my life wondering whether the truth ever surfaced or drowned with a salt-coaster in a storm."

"You think the archive will keep it safe?"

"I think the archive will *keep it*. That's different, and it's enough. Safety was never on offer. Not for me, not for anyone from Vessany. All that was ever on offer was the record." She met Bess's eyes. "You asked if you were warm. You weren't. I'm not carrying something that needs to go into the ground. I'm carrying something that needs to come *up*—into the light, under seal, in my own name if it comes to that. Especially in my own name."

Something flickered across Bess's broad face—surprise, or respect, or the memory of having once been young enough to say such things herself. She sat back, and the bench groaned again.

"In your own name," she repeated. "Well. That's a fool's errand with a martyr's trimmings."

"Probably."

"And you understand that refusing my offer means refusing the only way out of this city that doesn't run through a checkpoint?"

"I understand it perfectly."

"Then you're staying." It wasn't a question either. Bess reached over and pulled the stew back toward Orsen, pushing the spoon into her hand with the imperiousness of a woman feeding a stubborn animal. "Then eat, damn you. Martyrs who faint in the deposition office make poor martyrs."

Orsen ate. The stew was good—too good, seasoned with the generosity of a woman who respected refusal even as she mourned the fee—and Orsen ate all of it, because her body was a soldier that obeyed orders regardless of her heart's negotiations.

When the bowls were cleared, Bess lingered, wiping the table with a rag though it didn't need it. Her voice, when it came, was lower still.

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—the deposition registrar on the second floor is a woman named Maren Ostrey. Assistant registrar. She was married to a Vessany naval man, before. She hates the victors with the quiet hate that lasts longer than loud hate. If you must do your fool's errand, do it through her, and do it fast. Renwick's people have requested the deposition ledgers for review by week's end." She stood, slinging the rag over her shoulder. "After that, there won't be a door to knock on."

"Renwick." The name landed in Orsen's memory and stayed there. She'd seen it twice in three days—once on a posted notice granting the historical commission authority over harbor records, once murmured by a frightened lodging-house keeper. *Historian Aldous Renwick.* A name that traveled ahead of itself like cold weather. "He's here, then. In person."

"He arrived Tuesday. Big man, soft voice, spectacles. Buys drinks for clerks and compliments their penmanship." Bess's mouth twisted. "Men like that frighten me worse than soldiers. Soldiers only want your body. Historians want your *story*, and they'll take it apart bolt by bolt to see what it's worth to them."

She turned to go, then stopped, and for a moment the tavern-keeper's armor slipped and Orsen saw the smuggler underneath, and beneath the smuggler something older and wearier—perhaps simply a woman who had buried too many couriers.

"The lark," Bess said. "He was a friend of mine, thirty years back. I'm sorry he's dead. I'm sorryer that he filled your head with the idea that paper is a fortress." She shook her head. "Thursday's tide sails with or without you. If you change your mind, leave word with the boy who sweeps the fish-market steps. After Thursday, don't bother."

She walked back into the smoke, and the room absorbed her.

Orsen sat a while longer in the corner, letting the small beer go flat, watching the fire guttle and the refugees count their coins and the officers rise and button their coats and leave into the dark. Through the blank window she could hear the harbor working at night—the slap of water, the groan of ice shifting in the channel, a fog-bell somewhere keeping its patient count.

*Evidence walking.* Yes. But evidence had one power that gold did not: it could be entered. Sworn. Sealed. Made permanent in the one place permanence still lived. Bess was wrong about archives—or rather, Bess was right about archives and wrong about everything else, because an imperfect sanctuary was still a sanctuary, and a timestamp was still a wall that even historians had to climb.

She thought of Emrys Vale then, unexpectedly—his careful hands on his ledger, the way he had looked at her across the packet's lamplit cabin as though she were a sum that wouldn't resolve. She wondered where he was tonight, and hated herself a little for wondering, and wondered anyway. If the historians had requisitioned the census rolls—if Renwick's soft voice had already reached the manifests of the *Meridian*—then her name was sitting in Emrys's neat columns like a lit fuse.

She finished the beer, left coin on the table despite Bess's house account, and pulled her collar up against the night.

Outside, the fog had come off the water in earnest, swallowing the lane whole. Orsen turned toward the archive district, mapping the route in her head—the ropewalk, the chandlery cut, the long exposed stretch along the customs wall—and told herself the fear in her chest was only the cold.

Behind her, in the warm dark of the Salt Latch, Bess stood at her taproom window and watched the small figure vanish into the fog, then reached beneath the bar for a slate and wrote three words on it, and did not erase them:

*Crane. Refused. Renwick.*

Because Bess had survived thirty years in this harbor by knowing one thing better than any courier ever had: when a man refuses a bargain, someone else will always be willing to buy what he wouldn't sell—and it was better, in the end, to choose your buyer than to wait for him to choose you.
