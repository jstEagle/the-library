## 14. The Historian's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy in a victor's uniform too large for him, who stood in the doorway of Emrys's counting room with a card held at arm's length as though it might bite.

*Commission for the Historical Record. Aldous Renwick, Esq., Historiographer. Requests the attendance of Mr. Emrys Vale, census taker, at the customs annex, third hour past noon.*

Emrys read it twice. The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, watermarked with the twin towers of the Coalition—the new seal that had begun appearing on every official document in Harborton since the thaw began, like frost forming on glass in reverse. He had filed census sheets under that seal now for eleven days. He had not expected it to look back at him.

He told himself, walking to the customs annex through streets running loud with meltwater, that there was nothing remarkable in being summoned. Census takers were called in all the time lately. The Commission had requisitioned six years of harbor manifests, four registries of residency, and every ledger with his signature in the corner. It stood to reason they would have questions. It stood to reason they would ask them of the man who kept the answers.

It did not stand to reason—no amount of reasoning could make it stand—that his hands were damp inside his gloves, or that three times on the walk he caught himself rehearsing an answer to a question no one had asked yet.

The customs annex had been a shipping house before the war, all dark wainscoting and brass fittings gone green at the joints. Now the ground floor rang with the noise of occupation: clerks from the Coalition provinces, their accents stacking on top of one another like cargo; soldiers checking manifests against seals; somewhere a printing press working steadily behind a partitioned wall, producing the future one sheet at a time. Emrys gave his name at the stair and was waved upward without ceremony, which frightened him more than scrutiny would have. They knew who he was. They had never doubted it.

Renwick received him in a room at the end of a corridor smelling of pipe smoke and paste. The historian was smaller than Emrys had imagined, and older—a compact man with white hair cut close as a hedgerow and eyes the pale gray of old ice. His coat was civilian but fine, cut in the Coalition fashion, and on the table before him lay a chaos of papers that Emrys, professionally, could not help but assess: misfiled, uncollated, annotated in at least four inks. A man who worked by abundance rather than order. A man who drowned in documents and called it scholarship.

"Vale." Renwick did not rise. He gestured with two fingers at the chair opposite. "Sit. Do you take tea? No—don't trouble yourself," he added, before Emrys could answer, ringing a small bell anyway. "You'll take it. Everyone takes tea when they're nervous, and I'm told I make people nervous. I've decided to find this useful."

"I'm not nervous, sir."

"No," Renwick agreed mildly, pouring from a pot a clerk had already brought, "but you will be, and I'd rather we were both comfortable when it happened." He pushed a cup across the table. Steam rose between them like something escaping. "You've served the harbor district nine years. Seven hundred and forty-one registration entries in your own hand, give or take. You have the neatest ledgers of any man in this port—I checked. Neatness is a virtue in your profession, Vale. In mine it is a catastrophe. Nothing true is ever neat."

Emrys held the cup and did not drink. "How may I be of service to the Commission?"

"Ah, service. Good. We'll get along." Renwick drew a folder from beneath the disorder and opened it with the tenderness of a man handling a bird he intended to pin. "I am writing the history of the fall of Vessany. The *true* history—which is to say, the history the Coalition can live with. There is a distinction, and you'd be surprised how few people grasp it. Truth is what happened. History is what can be attested. My commission concerns itself entirely with the second thing, and the second thing is made of paper." He tapped the folder. "This is a list of persons whose movements into Harborton between the last autumn and the present are flagged for inquiry. Do you know what a flag looks like in a census ledger?"

"A red thread at the binding, sir. Or a marginal cross."

"A marginal cross," Renwick repeated, pleased. "Yes. And do you know who makes those crosses?"

"The registrar's office, upon requisition—"

"You do, Vale." Renwick looked up. The gray eyes were not unkind. That was the worst of it. "Six weeks ago you flagged an entry yourself. Crane, O., arrived by river packet *Meridian*, papers examined, exemption noted. Your hand. Your ink. And beside the name, in the margin, where no registrar asked you to write anything at all—" he turned the folder around and slid it across the table "—a question mark."

The room went very quiet. Behind the partition the press clattered on, indifferent, printing its thousands.

Emrys looked at his own handwriting and felt the peculiar vertigo of seeing a private thought made evidentiary. He had written that mark late at night, alone with his lamp, in the first raw days after the packet landed—written it because he could not *not* write it, because the ledger was his conscience made leather-bound and he had promised himself long ago that it would never contain a lie. He had thought it a small thing. A question mark weighed nothing. He saw now that it weighed exactly as much as a man's life, and that weight does not diminish for being measured in punctuation.

"I flag anomalies," he said carefully. "That is my duty. If my notes are of use to the Commission—"

"They are of enormous use." Renwick leaned back, lacing his fingers over his waistcoat. "Here is what my research has established, Vale, and you may correct me where my sources err. Somewhere on the *Meridian*, or near her, traveled a courier carrying dispatches out of Vessany in her final days. Dispatches proving—and here I confess even I find this interesting—that certain ministers of that republic negotiated the sale of peace terms while their army was still dying for them. Scandalous material. Material that would embarrass certain parties now comfortably seated in the Coalition administration, men who would very much prefer the republic's death to look like fate rather than fraud." He smiled without warmth. "Material, in short, that my Commission exists to ensure never enters any record anywhere. Do you follow?"

"I follow that such papers would be contraband."

"Such papers would be *sedition*, depending on who holds them when." Renwick sipped his tea. "Now. A woman named Bess, who keeps a tavern called the Salt Latch, tells my agents a curious story. She says a courier came to her seeking the archive route and found it burned. She says this courier refused a smuggler's bargain and walked into the fog with a satchel full of truth. She declined to say the courier's name." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down between courses. "But she did say—my agents are thorough, Vale, it is why I keep them—that the courier boarded the *Meridian*, and that a census taker examined the courier's papers and wrote something in a margin he was never asked to write in."

The tea had gone cold in Emrys's cup. He set it down with great care, so that no sound betrayed the shaking of his hands.

"What is it you're asking of me, sir?"

"Protection," Renwick said simply. "And in exchange, everything. Every entry, every note, every impression however faint. Where the courier lodged. Who the courier spoke to. What you believe—and I think you believe a great deal, your margins suggest a man who believes things at night—about who the courier truly is. In return: your position confirmed under the new administration. Your ledgers absolved of irregularity—including, I should mention, the page concerning Crane, which contains certain alterations my auditors have not yet traced to your hand, though they will. And immunity from the charge of falsifying state records, which carries, in the current climate, a sentence of some years in a place I would not wish on either of us." He spread his hands, a gesture of almost paternal openness. "You are not the subject of my inquiry, Vale. You are standing in the doorway of it. Step one way and you're a witness. Step the other and you're evidence. I would rather you were a witness. I collect witnesses. They are the whole of my trade."

Outside, a cart went past, iron-shod wheels finding every seam in the cobbles. Meltwater dripped somewhere in the walls, patient as a clock. Emrys became aware that he was looking at the question mark in his own handwriting and had been for some time.

He understood, with the clarity that comes only at altitude or in extremity, precisely what he was. Not a bystander. Not a functionary. A hinge. Everything that happened next would swing on what he did in this room in the next five minutes: whether a woman who had carried truth across a frozen river in the lining of her coat would find the archive door open or barred; whether the record of her existence would be a testimony or an indictment; whether Emrys Vale would spend the rest of his life as the man who protected her or the man who sold her—and he saw, too, with terrible honesty, that Renwick was offering him the chance to be neither, to be simply the neat-handed clerk again, anonymous, absolved, safe. All he had to do was hand over everything he knew. All he had to do was give up the only secret that had ever made his small gray life matter.

And the vertigo of it was this: he could not remember anymore which way he wanted to fall.

"Take your time," Renwick said, and refilled his own cup, and looked out the window at the harbor as if the answer were moored out there among the ice.

Emrys thought about the waystation storm. The shared watch. Her voice low across the coals, telling him half of everything. He thought about the ledger page he had already burned, and the corrected entry in its place—*Crane, Orsena. Witness.*—entered quietly into the deposition rolls where no auditor had yet looked. He thought: *she trusted me without knowing she did. She walked into the archive on the strength of my silence.*

He thought, also, of prison. Of the annex. Of seven hundred and forty-one entries and the comfort of routine, and how a man can build an entire life out of minding his own business until his own business becomes the only thing standing between another human being and erasure.

"I can tell you what I tell every inquiry," Emrys said at last, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he had any right to expect. "What the record shows. Arrival date. Vessel. Papers presented and their condition. Beyond that, sir, a census taker counts people. He doesn't presume to know them."

Renwick turned from the window. For a long moment the gray eyes studied him, and Emrys felt them moving over him like fingers over a document, checking for forgery.

"That," Renwick said finally, "was a very careful answer."

"I'm a careful man."

"Mm." Renwick closed the folder and squared it on the table, and his voice lost nothing of its courtesy, which somehow made it worse. "Then let me be careless for both of us. My agents are sweeping the district tonight, Vale. The harbor quarter, the fish-market roofs, the archive steps. Something will be found, or someone, and when it is, I will not need your margins at all—you will simply be the man who knew and didn't say, and there is a cell with your name provisionally assigned to it, third floor, north side, good light. I would hate for you to occupy it out of loyalty to a person who, I promise you, would not hesitate to sacrifice you." He rose, extending his hand, and the interview was somehow over without having ended. "You have until the day after tomorrow. Think about which kind of history you'd rather appear in—the kind that survives, or the kind that gets corrected."

Emrys shook the offered hand. It was warm and dry and surprisingly strong.

At the door, Renwick spoke once more, softly, without looking up from his papers.

"One more thing, Vale. The corrected deposition entry. Crane, Orsena, entered as witness." The pen scratched on, unhurried. "My auditors haven't found it yet. But they found the ash of the page you burned in your stove, and ash, unlike paper, keeps no secrets—it only keeps shapes. Sleep well."

Emrys walked down the stairs past the clerks and the soldiers and the press printing its thousands, out into the street where the meltwater ran silver in the gutters toward a harbor still locked in ice. He had come to the annex as a man with a secret. He left as a man with a deadline.

Two days. And somewhere across the district, unaware that the net had already been drawn to its final knot, the woman in his margin was moving toward the archive door—and Aldous Renwick's agents were moving toward her.
