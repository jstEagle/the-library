## 16. Betrayal at the Tavern

The Salt Latch smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular sweetness of herring left too long in the sun—a smell that would have turned her stomach once and now felt almost like shelter. Orsen sat in the back corner with her shoulders against the wall, a habit she had carried out of Vessany along with her name, and watched Bess Karrow work the room.

Bess was older than Orsen remembered. The war had taken two years from her face and given them back as gray at the temples and a new carefulness in her hands, which never stopped moving—wiping the bar, sorting glasses, tucking loose hair beneath her kerchief—as though stillness itself were a confession. She had been her handler's cutout for eleven years, the only living link in the courier chain Orsen trusted, and eleven years ago she had laughed easily. Tonight she did not laugh at anything.

Orsen nursed a cup of small beer and waited for the tavern to thin.

It did not thin. That was the first wrongness. Harborton swelled with refugees and victors' clerks, true, but the Salt Latch's evening crowd had always emptied by the ninth bell when the fish carts rolled out toward the morning markets. Tonight the benches stayed full, and the fullness had a texture to it—men who drank slowly, who looked up whenever the door opened, who sat with their chairs angled toward one another in clusters of two and three like punctuation marks in a sentence being composed around her.

She counted six of them before her cup was half empty. Boots too new for working men. Coats brushed but not mended. And by the door, a seventh, younger, who kept touching his breast pocket the way soldiers touch the thing they are paid to carry.

*You walked into a lit room with the dispatches on your body,* she thought, and the self-contempt was so clean and cold it steadied her. *Eleven years of tradecraft and you walked into a lit room.*

Bess came down the bar toward her, filling glasses that did not need filling, and stopped across the scarred planks from her seat. Up close Orsen could see the damage properly now: a bruise gone yellow-green along Bess's jaw, hidden by the turn of her head, and the fine tremor in her left hand that no amount of wiping and sorting could disguise.

"Crane," Bess said, too loudly, using the name like a bell to warn the whole room where she stood. "You'll be wanting a room? We're full."

"I'll want ten minutes of your time, is all."

"We're full," Bess repeated, and her eyes did the terrible thing—they flicked, just once, toward the man touching his breast pocket—and then came back to Orsen carrying an apology so heavy it seemed to bow her spine. "Full through Sunday."

Understanding arrived the way ice water arrives: not all at once but in a rising line up the body, from the feet to the scalp.

They had her already. They had Bess, and Bess had given them enough that this whole theater—the crowded room, the warning, the yellowed bruise—was Bess's last honest act, performed in the only language left to her. *Run. I am sorry. Run.*

Orsen set two coppers on the bar with hands that did not shake, because she would not let them shake, not here, not in front of seven pairs of patient eyes. "Another night, then," she said. "I'll come back when you've a bed free."

"Don't come back," Bess said softly, and for a moment the tavern-keeper's mask slipped entirely and Orsen saw the woman beneath it, the one who used to laugh, the one who had held her handler's letters against her breast like a warm loaf on a cold day. "Whatever you're carrying, Crane—whatever they told you it was worth—it isn't worth what's coming up that river when the ice goes. Go home. There's no home left, but go anyway."

The seventh man pushed off the doorframe.

Orsen rose, nodded to Bess as if accepting a refusal about a room, and walked toward the kitchen door at the pace of a man going to relieve himself—not fast, nothing worth chasing, nothing worth remembering. Behind her she heard the scrape of a bench, and then Bess's voice pitched high and bright over the room: "Oi, that barrel wants changing and none of you lot are helping—" and the crash of something heavy dropped deliberately, and the blessed chaos of six drunk fishermen deciding whether to care.

Then she was through the door, into steam and stink, past a cook who swore at her, out into the alley behind the Salt Latch where the cold hit her lungs like a slap.

* * *

She ran.

Not toward the street—that was where they would watch—but away from it, along the alley's greasy cobbles toward the fish-market, where the stalls stood empty under their sagging canvas and the great gabled roofs of the packing houses climbed three and four stories against the gray sky. The market district was built vertical, Harborton fashion, every warehouse leaning its neighbor's shoulder, every roofline within a leap of the next, and eleven years ago another courier had taught her the paths across those rooftops in case the harbor ever closed behind her.

She had thought she would never need them. She said a silent, furious thank-you to a dead woman whose name she had never known.

The first wall was the worst. A drainpipe, a stack of empty herring barrels wired together, a desperate grab at a lead gutter, and then she was up, coat dragging at her like a drowning man's hand, the dispatches thudding against her ribs inside the lining where they had ridden since Vessany. She rolled onto the slates and lay flat, breathing through her mouth, listening.

Shouts below. The alley door of the Salt Latch banging open. Lantern light sloshing across the cobbles.

"—went through the kitchen—"

"—seal the market end, he'll double back for the quay—"

*He.* Good. Let them hunt a man named Crane. Every hour they spent hunting a fiction was an hour bought for the truth.

She moved along the roof ridge in a low crouch, boots finding the sound slate, the sound slate only, the way she'd been taught. Below her the fish-market opened out in its grid of stalls and poles, hung with lines and netting like the rigging of some grounded fleet, and beyond it the harbor lay black and glittering under a scud of cloud, the river ice groaning out in the channel with a sound like slow doors opening on hell. The thaw had begun. Two days, maybe three, and Renwick's people would seal the crossings and the archive would be the only door left in the world.

The gap to the next roof yawned eight feet across, over a drop into the dark between buildings where the market refuse rotted. She backed up three steps, ran, and jumped.

Her right foot found the far gutter. Her left found nothing, skated on frost-slick slate, and for one long second the whole world was her own weight swinging outward over emptiness, fingers hooked in cold iron, breath tearing white. Then she got a knee over the edge and hauled herself up onto the packing-house roof, shaking, laughing once—a short ugly bark of a sound—because terror and fury had nowhere else to go.

Fury came flooding in behind the fear and filled it like tide fills a breach. At Bess, for the bruise on her jaw and the apology in her eyes—at herself, for making Bess's betrayal necessary. At Renwick and his historians with their requisitioned ledgers, their patient nets, their belief that a republic could be murdered twice, once with armies and once with paper. At the ministers whose orders rode against her ribs, the men who had sold the peace terms and called it necessity and sent a woman named Orsena across a border to die for documents that would hang them if anyone ever read them aloud.

*I will read them aloud,* she promised the night. *If it costs everything, I will stand in a room with witnesses and read them until someone is forced to hear.*

She crossed the packing-house roof and dropped to the next, and there, crouched among the chimney pots with the harbor wind pulling at her collar, she allowed herself the thing she had forbidden herself since Vessany: she thought of Emrys.

His face would be in their ledgers. He had written her name—*Crane, Orsen, no prior registry*—in his careful hand, and Renwick's people were pulling census sheets like teeth. When they failed to find her tonight, and they would fail, they would go looking through paper instead, and every page Emrys had ever touched would become a door they could kick in. She had wanted, without admitting she wanted it, to come back from the archive and find him at the harbor district office, bent over his forms, alive in his ordinary meticulous way. Now that small future was bleeding out on the slates beneath her hands, and she had put it there the moment she walked into the Salt Latch.

*Get the dispatches deposited. Warn him if you can. In that order. Survive first, feel later.*

She rose to move again, and that was when she heard the boots above her.

Not behind—above. On the roofline she had just left, silhouetted against the cloud-scud, two figures moving with the unhurried competence of professionals. They had not sealed the streets. They had gone vertical. Of course they had; Renwick would have hired harbor men, riggers, men born on these rooftops.

Orsen ran.

She ran the ridgeline south with the wind screaming past her ears, took the low jump over the alley between the smokehouse and the salt store without slowing, slid down a slope of slate on her boot heels with sparks of frost spraying from her soles. Behind her a whistle blew—one note, two, answered from somewhere near the quay—and lanterns bloomed along the market lanes below like fever spots on skin. They were folding the net closed around the whole district, calm and thorough, and the only openings left were upward and inward and hers alone.

There was a way down she knew: the old net-drying loft at the market's north corner, its upper floor open to the roofline through a loading hatch, its ground floor giving onto the lane behind the ice houses. She reached the hatch, wrenched it up on shrieking hinges, and swung down into darkness that smelled of hemp and brine.

And that was when she understood what the night intended to take from her.

The satchel.

She had transferred it at the tavern—no. Before the tavern, at the lodging house, while pretending to shave: the dispatch originals sewn into her coat lining, safe against her heart, but the satchel—her working copy, her cipher draft of the testimony, three weeks of nights spent transcribing and paraphrasing and hiding the truth in plain script between innocent ledger lines—that satchel she had strapped beneath her coat for the meeting because Bess was supposed to take it tonight. That had been the plan. Bess takes the copy to the courier chain; Orsen deposits the originals at the archive as insurance. The plan of a woman who still believed her chain held.

The strap had come loose on the roof run. She knew it the way you know a wound before you look: she knew it from the lightness at her hip, from the absence of that familiar knock against her thigh. Somewhere between the Salt Latch and here, across two rooftops and one nearly fatal gap, the satchel had torn free.

She stood in the dark of the net loft with her hand pressed flat against her coat, feeling the hard rectangle of the originals beneath the cloth, and made herself think it plainly: her written confession, her cipher key, her notes naming the ministers and their terms—lying somewhere on a slate roof or in a gutter or, God, in the hands of whoever climbed fastest.

Panic came first, animal and complete, closing her throat. Then fury, white and clarifying, burning the panic off like frost off iron.

*Find it. You can find it. It didn't grow wings.*

But even as she turned toward the hatch, lantern light crawled across the loft floor from the lane outside, and voices sounded at the mouth of the alley—not two men now but four, spreading, calling to each other in low controlled tones. The net had a hole in it exactly her size, and it was closing.

She went up through the hatch instead of out through the door, back onto the roofline, and dropped flat behind the chimney pots as a lantern swept the loft below her feet. Her heart slammed so hard she felt it in her teeth. Rain was starting now, fine and needling, turning the slates to glass, and out on the roof she had crossed minutes ago, two more lights were moving in a careful grid, searching.

Searching the very slates where her satchel must lie.

She could go back for it and be taken. She could leave it and let Renwick read her soul by lamplight—except that the copy was cipher where it mattered, half-masked, keyed to a phrase only she carried, and even that comfort was thin, because ciphers fell to patience and patience was the one thing historians had in abundance.

Below her, one of the searchers called out, voice flattened by rain: "Here! Something's caught in the gutter—north slope!"

Orsen closed her eyes. For one heartbeat she was on the roof with the satchel, and the next she was over the far edge of the net loft, sliding down a mooring post into the ice-house yard, landing hard, running blind through freezing rain toward the harbor with the original dispatches against her heart and her testimony abandoned on the slates above, and the last thing she heard from the fish-market rooftops was a shout of triumph, one note, carried on the wind like a bell tolling for something she had not yet finished writing.

The crossings would close in days. The archive was her only door. And somewhere behind her, in a victor's wet and careful hands, her own words were about to begin the journey she could not allow them to finish.
