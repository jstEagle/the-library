## 17. The Ice Begins to Break

The river spoke first.

Emrys Vale heard it before dawn on the fourth of Thawmonth, a sound like knuckles cracking beneath the floor of the world—a long, splintering groan that rolled up from the harbor and through the pilings of his lodging house and into the frame of his bed as though the water were trying to wake him personally. He lay in the dark with his breath visible above the blankets and listened. All winter the river had been a held tongue, mute and white, crossed freely by anyone with sturdy boots and no fear of the far shore. Now the tongue was loosening. Now it would begin to say things.

By full light the ice had gone grey at its seams, and by noon the first lead of open water showed black near the channel markers, thin as a lie. The packet men stood along the quay in their ruined coats, spitting and squinting, arguing about tonnage and dates. Everyone in Harborton understood what the black water meant even if no one said it aloud: the victors' commissariat had posted its notice three weeks ago, pasted in tar-red letters to the customs house door. *Upon break-up of the river, all crossings to be closed pending inspection. No passage north, south, or seaward without victors' seal.* The ice had been the last indifferent thing left in the harbor district—ice asked no man for papers—and in days, perhaps hours, it would be gone.

Emrys dressed, ate his bread standing, and walked to the census office with his collar up against a wind that smelled of open water and old fish and something almost like green.

The office occupied the ground floor of the harbormaster's annex, a long room lit by windows fogged with river damp, lined with shelves that groaned under forty years of manifests, registers, and corrected sheets. He had worked in this room through the whole war and half the winter after it, entering names in his careful upright hand: births registered late, deaths registered later, lodgers come and gone, boatmen, rope-makers, refugees who slept three to a room in the Salt Quarter and gave whatever name suited them when asked. He knew better than most how thin a name was—how a man could wear one like a coat and shed it at a doorway and put on another by morning.

He also knew that somewhere in this building, or in a crate already bound for the historians' requisition depot across the quay, sat the page that would undo Orsen Crane.

* * *

He had flagged her himself. That was the wound of it—that was the part he could not stop turning over, the way one turns a splinter toward the light. In the first week of Frostmonth, auditing his own winter sheets against the victors' demand for *all persons of military age unaccounted for in prior registries*, he had drawn a clean line down the margin beside *Crane, Orsen, cordage-worker, no prior registration, arrived by river packet Meridian*, and he had initialed it, because his hand did what it did. Because order was the shape his conscience took, or had been, once.

A flag meant scrutiny. A flag meant the page would be copied, cross-referenced, carried upward into the growing sediment of the victors' files, where historians named Renwick sifted lives the way dockers sifted coal—looking always, always, for the courier. For the woman who had walked into Harborton carrying the true account of Vessany's fall stitched into her coat lining, and who had, by some mercy Emrys still did not fully understand, deposited it under seal before they could close their net.

They had not found her. Not yet. But paper outlives flight. Paper is patient.

That was the thought that had kept him awake since the equinox: *paper is patient.* A flag could wait all spring. A flag could wait ten years. And someday, when the histories were being polished and the inconvenient witnesses winnowed, some clerk with Emrys's own tidy habits would find that initialed line and pull the thread, and everything would unravel from it—the tavern, Bess, the fish-market rooftops, the deposition office, the name.

Unless the line itself ceased to exist.

* * *

The office was empty when he entered, save for young Pell at the far desk, yawning over the coal-duty tallies. The requisition crates stood stacked by the door, stenciled with the victors' double-cross mark, awaiting the afternoon collection. Emrys hung his coat, sat at his desk, and did nothing for a quarter hour but listen to the river crack and reform, crack and reform, like a man rehearsing a difficult sentence.

At half past eight Pell was sent for—harbormaster's clerk wanted the coal figures reconciled before the collection—and went, gratefully, into the wet morning. Emrys heard the outer door thump. He heard the wind take the chimney. He heard, very clearly, the sound of his own heart, which was behaving badly for so methodical a man.

He opened the current ledger to the Frostmonth sheets.

There it was: *Crane, Orsen.* The flag-line down the margin, his own initials, small and damning as a signature on a confession. Below it, in the corrections column, the notation he had added weeks ago under Renwick's eye—the summons record, the interview note, the whole careful apparatus of a man trying to be honest while honesty curdled into betrayal. He read it twice. It was accurate. That was the horror; every word of it was true, and together the truths made a noose.

In the stove, last night's coal had burned down to a red honeycomb of embers. He crouched before it.

He thought about his father, who had kept the parish registers in a river town not unlike this one, and who had once told him—Emrys was eleven, bored, impatient with ink—*the book is the only place a poor man is ever safe, boy. Kings can burn your house. They can't unwrite you.* His father had believed it. His father had never lived to see historians requisition registers by the crate, or learn what happened to clerks whose pages displeased the new state. Suspension. Investigation. The slow gray ruin of a career. Possibly worse; Renwick had been delicately vague about worse.

And yet.

He drew the page free of its binding with a razor, cleanly, the way he did everything. He held it a moment over the embers—one heartbeat, two, in which he saw with perfect clarity both futures, the obedient one and the other—and then let it go.

It caught slowly, the way thick paper does, curling at the corner like a leaf in autumn fire, then blooming orange along its whole length. The flag-line burned first, his initials going up in a bright whisper. Then *Crane, Orsen* darkened, blistered, and was ash. He watched until there was nothing but a lace of char floating on the coals, and he stirred it under with the poker so that not a letter remained legible, and he stood, and his hands were steady, which surprised him.

What surprised him more was what he felt, which was not fear—or not only fear—but something older and quieter, something that lived in the same chamber of him as his father's voice. Defiance, yes. But defiance was too grand a word for the sensation, which was closer to *shelter*. He had torn down a wall and found himself inside a sanctuary. Paper had been the instrument of her exposure; paper would now be the instrument of her protection. If the record could be made to hold her safely, then the record was worth any risk to him.

He returned to the desk and pulled a fresh correction sheet toward him.

His hand did not shake. That, too, astonished him. He wrote in the fair hand that three supervisors had praised and none had ever suspected:

*Correction of entry, Frostmonth register, folio 114, line 9. Original entry: "Crane, Orsen, cordage-worker, no prior registration." Upon review of packet Meridian's supplemental manifest (recovered from waystation records, Frostmonth), prior registration located: registered at source landing, Vessany civil rolls, under birth name "Orsena Crane," female, recorded occupation dispatch-clerk, civil service. Discrepancy arising from wartime irregularities in documentation; entry amended accordingly. Witness status: Orsena Crane has lodged sworn testimony with the Harbor Archive deposition office, sealed and timestamped, and is to be regarded as a protected witness of record, not an unregistered person of military age. Flag withdrawn as erroneous. — E. Vale, census taker.*

Every word defensible—or nearly. The waystation manifest existed; he had seen it, months ago, in the bundle Renwick's people had missed. The deposition was real; he had witnessed its sealing with his own eyes, had heard her voice fill the deposition hall while agents hammered at the doors. The rest—*protected witness of record*—was a construction, an archway built of plausible bricks spanning empty air. But archives made things true. That was the whole secret of his trade, and today, for the first time, he loved it: a corrected entry, properly witnessed and filed, became fact. Became shelter. Became a roof she could live under.

He signed it, dated it, blotted it, and carried it—not to the requisition crates by the door, but to the office's own reference shelf, the duplicate set, the humble working copies that no historian had bothered to demand because no historian believed small paper mattered. Let them have their crates. This page would live here, in the shabby ordinary ledger that Pell consulted weekly and the harbormaster's clerks trusted absolutely, and it would quietly insist, to anyone who read it, that Orsena Crane belonged to the record, and the record would keep her.

He shelved it between a coal-duty amendment and a dispute about wharfage fees, and stepped back, and looked at the row of bindings, all identical, all gray, all patient.

"There," he said aloud, to the empty office, to the river, to his father. "Now you're written."

* * *

The river finished its argument with winter that afternoon.

From the annex steps Emrys watched the channel ice go all at once—not melting but failing, sheet after sheet rearing up and folding under with reports like cannon, rafting into ridges along the far mudflats. Gulls came screaming down to feast on what the breakup uncovered. Boys ran the quay's length cheering as though a battle had been won. Perhaps it had; the ice had held the crossings open all winter, and now the age of easy escape was over. By nightfall the commissariat's launch would chuff up from the fort to anchor mid-channel, lanterns burning at bow and stern, and after that only the victors' seal would move upon the water.

He should have felt exposed. What he had done could be discovered—Pell might remember the Frostmonth folio, Renwick certainly remembered the flag, and a missing page in a requisitioned register was its own kind of evidence, an absence shaped exactly like guilt. He turned the risk over as he walked home along the blackening river, examining it from every side the way he examined a suspect manifest, and found, to his mild astonishment, that it no longer frightened him the way it had. Fear of exposure required a man to have something left to lose intact. He had spent the winter losing pieces of his certainties—duty, neutrality, the comfortable fiction that a clerk merely recorded the world and bore no burden for it. What remained felt lighter and harder, like bone. Like keel.

At the corner by the ropewalk, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the awnings and fell into step beside him. Small, quick, wrapped in a fishmonger's oilcloth two sizes too large. He did not startle; some part of him had been walking toward this meeting all day.

"You look like a man who's been to church," Orsen said quietly, eyes forward.

"I've been to the archive of myself," Emrys said. "It amounts to the same thing."

She glanced at him then, and he saw the exhaustion in her, deep as the channel—weeks of hiding, of attics and fish-cellars, of moving before dawn—but under the exhaustion something else, alert and appraising. She had heard, of course. News traveled through the Salt Quarter faster than through any ministry. Someone had told her the census taker had been seen feeding his stove at an unusual hour.

"The crossing closes tomorrow," she said. "Maybe tonight. After that, nobody leaves this harbor without their seal."

"I know."

"And your page?"

"Burned," he said. "And replaced. You're in the record now, Orsena—as a registered person, a civil servant of the old republic, and a protected witness of deposit. As good as I could make it with honest ink and one dishonest omission." He hesitated. "It will hold unless someone looks very hard at the absence where the flag used to be."

"Someone will."

"Yes. Renwick will."

They walked a dozen steps in silence. The river boomed and cracked behind them, shedding winter.

"Then we'd best not wait for him to look," Orsen said, and there was something in her voice he had not heard before—not dread, exactly, and not hope either, but the flat bright resolve of a woman reading a chart and choosing her line through dangerous water. "Because I've had word from the Salt Quarter. Bess talked before she vanished—talked enough. And there's a deposition clerk in the archive annex, a boy named Willet, who was seen taking notes for Renwick's people. Which means Renwick knows the testimony exists, knows where it sleeps, and knows precisely which registrar's stamp made it untouchable." She stopped at the mouth of her alley, half in shadow. "He can't unseal it. So he'll do the next thing. He'll prove the witness was a fraud from the beginning—that the name in your ledger is forged, the registration invented, the whole record poisoned—because a fraudulent record can be struck."

Emrys stood very still. The ice groaned out in the dark like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"He'll need my ledger for that," he said.

"He'll need *you*," said Orsen. "And Emrys—he's already sent for you. The message reached the annex an hour ago. You're summoned to the customs house at first light."

The lanterns of the commissariat launch appeared mid-channel just then, two small flames riding the broken water, marking where the crossings used to be. Emrys looked at them a long moment, then at the woman in the alley shadow who had given her true name to the record and her safety to the winds.

"Then I suppose," he said, "we should decide by morning what it is I know."
