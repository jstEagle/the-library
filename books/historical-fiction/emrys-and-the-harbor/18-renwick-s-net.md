## 18. Renwick's Net

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy too clean for the harbor district—a clerk's apprentice in a pressed coat who stood in the doorway of the census annex as though he feared the floor might stain his boots. He carried a card of heavy cream stock, and on it, in a hand so controlled it looked printed: *A. Renwick, Commission on Historical Record, requests the attendance of E. Vale, census taker, at nine o'clock.*

Emrys had known it would come. He had known since the night he fed that flagged ledger page into the stove and watched the ink curl and go to ash, since the morning he entered a corrected record naming Orsena Crane as witness to the fall of Vessany. He had told himself, in the small hours when honesty was unavoidable, that such an act could not stay hidden forever. Paper remembers. That was the whole of his profession, after all—that was why he had done it. Paper was the only sanctuary he could offer her, and paper keeps every debt written upon it.

Still, knowing a storm is coming is not the same as being ready for the wet.

He dressed carefully. Not too well—he did not want to look like a man dressing for judgment—but cleanly, because Renwick's people would read his collar and his cuffs the way Emrys himself read a manifest, for discrepancies. He ate nothing. His hands were steady until he picked up his pen to log out of the annex, and then they were not quite steady, and he set the pen down and breathed until they were again.

The Commission occupied the old customs house, a gray stone pile at the head of the quay with columns like the legs of some patient, feeding animal. Vessany had built it, back when Vessany still believed in permanence. Now its lintel bore the victors' seal in fresh black paint over the old republic's faded blue, and Emrys thought: *that is what we are doing to everything. Painting over. Calling it restoration.*

* * *

Renwick received him in a room that had been a harbor master's office once. The maps were still on the walls—charts of the river channel, soundings marked in a dead surveyor's careful hand—but between them hung new things: organizational diagrams of the fallen government, arrest lists, timelines pinned up like anatomical charts of a body laid open on a table. Aldous Renwick stood before the largest of these, hands clasped behind his back, and did not turn when Emrys entered.

"Census taker Vale," Renwick said. "Thank you for coming promptly."

"You left me little choice, sir."

"Choice is the subject of this meeting." Renwick turned then. He was younger than Emrys had expected—forty perhaps, with a scholar's softness settling over a soldier's frame, spectacles catching the gray window light. He had the voice of a man who lectured, measured and unhurried, each word set down like a stone in a wall. "Sit, please."

There was one chair, placed facing the desk, and behind the desk sat another man whom Emrys had not noticed at first because the man sat utterly still—a thickset individual with a face like a closed ledger, wearing the plain dark coat of the Provost's office rather than any uniform. He did not introduce himself. That omission said more than an introduction would have.

Emrys sat. Renwick remained standing, and lifted from the desk a single sheet of paper.

"Mr. Vale. Twenty-two years you have kept census for the harbor district. Your ledgers are, I am told, exemplary. Cross-referenced. Annotated. A model of civic memory." He smiled without warmth. "I have read your work. Genuinely—I am not flattering you. It is rare to find a provincial official who understands that a registry is not an inventory but a narrative. Every entry says something about the person who made it."

"I try to be accurate, sir."

"Yes." Renwick laid the sheet down and slid it across the desk. "And yet."

Emrys looked. It was a facsimile—a clerk's copy—of his own January manifest page, the crossing register from the packet *Meridian*. Beside his entries, in Renwick's hand, ran annotations in red: passenger Crane, O., no prior registration located; annotation dated the ninth; annotation dated the seventeenth, referencing the requisitioned archive search; and then, circled twice, a note in Emrys's own cramped script—the corrected record, entered the twelfth of February. *Crane, Orsena, of Vessany City; witness deponent; status: protected deposition, registrar's seal.*

Someone had copied his correction verbatim before he had even finished the ink drying, it seemed.

"You will notice," Renwick said quietly, "the sequence. On the ninth of February, my staff flags a gap. A passenger with no history, traveling under a name that appears nowhere in eleven years of registries. On the eleventh, I interview you—or rather, I attempt to, and you are diligent and unhelpful in equal measure. And on the twelfth, before my requisition of the archive search has even concluded, you amend your ledger to establish—retroactively, Mr. Vale—a documented identity for this person. Complete with a reference to a sealed deposition that, I assure you, took my staff entirely by surprise."

"The deposition exists," Emrys said. "It is timestamped by the assistant registrar. You have verified that yourself or you would not be quoting it."

"Oh, I have verified it." Renwick pulled off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, an oddly domestic gesture. "That is precisely what makes this interesting. The record is genuine. Sealed, lawful, untouchable—which means the woman, if she exists, is beyond my reach through ordinary channels. But records do not enter themselves, census taker. Someone carried her testimony in. Someone knew where to bring her, at what hour, past whose desks. And someone"—he set the spectacles back on, and his eyes behind the glass were pale and very patient—"amended a ledger two days ahead of necessity, which is the signature of foreknowledge. Foreknowledge is complicity. Complicity, under Article Fourteen, carries a penalty I will not insult you by pretending you don't know."

The man in the dark coat did not move, and his stillness was the loudest thing in the room.

Emrys found, to his surprise, that his fear had a strange texture to it—not the blind animal thing he might have imagined, but something finer, threaded through with an almost exhilarating clarity. He had spent three weeks rehearsing this conversation in his head at night, lying awake above the chandlery, listening to the ice groan in the channel like a great beast turning over. He had composed answers, discarded them, composed others. But now, sitting here, he understood that all his rehearsals had been wrong in the same way: he had prepared to defend himself, when what Renwick actually wanted was to recruit him.

"The woman gave testimony," Emrys said. "Lawfully, before a lawfully constituted registrar. Whatever she was before that moment, the seal covers it. You said so yourself. So I fail to see what Article Fourteen has to do with me, or with anything."

"Because the record is only half of it." Renwick came around the desk at last, perched himself on its edge, close enough that Emrys could smell the lamp oil on him—he had been working through the nights, this historian, hunting. "The sealed deposition protects her person. It does not recover what she carried. Dispatches, Mr. Vale. Diplomatic correspondence from the last days of the Vessanine ministry. Documents that certain parties in my own commission would give a good deal to acquire—and other parties would give a great deal more to destroy. There are men on both sides of the former war who wake at night thinking about a courier satchel that vanished into Harborton. Do you understand what I am telling you? She is not merely a fugitive. She is a live grenade rolling through the neutral quarter, and everyone in this city with an interest in how the war is remembered wants to get their hands on her first."

"And you want me to find her for you."

"I want you to help me find her," Renwick said, "before someone less scrupulous does. Because I am a historian, Mr. Vale, not a provost. My colleague here—" a thin gesture toward the motionless man "—represents the other outcome, the one where she is taken quietly and the documents are buried and in forty years the official account of Vessany's fall is the comfortable one, the one written by the winners, uncorrected forever. I do not want that. I have given my life to the idea that truth can survive its enemies. Her deposition proves the ministry sold the peace terms—it indicts the men my own commission is nominally celebrating. I want the rest of what she knows. I want the dispatches in an archive, not a furnace." He paused. "And I want you to bring her to me."

"Why me?"

"Because she trusts you." Renwick said it simply, without embarrassment, and it landed harder than any accusation could have. "You watched her across a packet cabin for six days. You spoke with her through a frozen night. Don't look startled—people tell us things without meaning to, in manifests and watch-logs and the observations of landladies. Bess Ferrow's tavern register notes you dined there twice in March. You burn a page from your own ledger for her, and then you write her into history under her own name. That is not clerical error, Mr. Vale. That is devotion. And devotion," he finished softly, "cannot be manufactured or faked. Which makes you the most valuable instrument in Harborton."

The room was very quiet. Out the window, down the gray length of the quay, Emrys could see the river—the ice gone rotten and dark at the edges, the channel pilots already rigging the warning lights. Three days, perhaps four, and the crossings would be sealed for the spring maneuvers. Everything was converging on those few days, and he felt it in his chest like weather.

"What you are offering," Emrys said slowly, "is clemency."

"I am offering more than clemency. I am offering purpose. Help me, and the falsification disappears—not ignored, *resolved*, reframed as an administrative error corrected under commission supervision. Your position restored fully. Your name clean. Refuse, and Article Fourteen proceeds, my colleague takes over the inquiry, and the next person who speaks to Miss Crane will not be a historian with a respect for documents." Renwick straightened, adjusted his cuffs. "You need not answer now. But you should know that I will have my answer either way. If not yours, then hers—extracted under far worse circumstances."

Emrys looked at the red annotations on the facsimile. At the silent provost. At the timeline charts of a murdered republic hanging between portraits of its drowned harbor.

He thought of Orsen—Orsena—in the deposition hall, her voice steadying as she swore her testimony aloud while the doors shook. He thought of what she had told him on the frozen waystation watch, half-truths offered like coins counted out one at a time, and of the moment she had finally given him the whole coin: *I was born Orsena. Survival required becoming someone else.* He thought of the price she had already paid—the anonymity surrendered, the name entered into a record that every ambitious man in this city was now reading—and of what she had said afterward, on the ice-choked quay, when neither of them would promise what they could not give: *They can take everything except what I put on the record. That is mine. I bought it with myself.*

She would not run, now. He understood that at last, with a clarity that felt almost like grief. She had spent her whole life running as someone else, and she had finally, at terrible cost, stopped. She would not become a shadow again—not even for him, not even for safety. Which meant the net would close, whatever he did. The only question was who held it when it closed.

Renwick was watching him with those patient pale eyes, and Emrys understood something else too: that Renwick was telling the truth. That he really was a scholar who wanted the documents archived and the record corrected, and that his colleague by the wall represented exactly the alternative he described. The choice was real. That was what made it monstrous.

"All right," Emrys said.

Renwick did not smile, but something in his shoulders eased. "All right?"

"I will help you find her." Emrys kept his voice level, clerk-flat, the voice he used for reading numbers aloud. "But on conditions. When you have her—when you have the dispatches—they go into the public archive, catalogued, accessible. No quiet furnaces. No sealed annexes. I want that in writing, witnessed."

"Done."

"And she is not handed to the provosts. She is brought in as a witness, under the same protection as her deposition. In writing."

"In writing," Renwick agreed, and there was the faintest note of satisfaction in it, the sound of a man closing a purchase. He drew a paper toward himself, wrote swiftly, signed it, and pushed it across for Emrys's mark.

Emrys signed. His hand did not shake. He had expected it to shake, and it did not, and that frightened him more than trembling would have—this ease, this terrible administrative calm, signing away his conscience in neat copperplate while the provost watched.

"You've made the right decision," Renwick said, walking him to the door. "Where will you begin?"

"The tavern registers," Emrys said. "Bess Ferrow's house. She kept rooms for river people all through the war—she'll know where a woman alone would hole up." Every word true, and every word aimed. "Give me two days. She trusts me, as you say. She'll come to me if I send the right signal. There's a chalk mark we used on the packet—watch rotation, a question about cargo. I'll set it tonight at the coal quay lamp post. If she's watching her own trail, and she will be, she'll see it within a day."

"Two days, then." Renwick shook his hand. His grip was dry and precise. "My colleague will accompany you at a distance. For your protection, you understand."

"Of course," said Emrys, and walked out of the customs house into the raw wind coming off the breaking river, and did not let his breath shake until he was three streets gone.

* * *

He went down to the water.

The coal quay stretched out black and slick into the graying channel, heaped with anthracite that dusted everything within fifty yards—the bollards, the gulls, the pilings—with a soot like mourning crepe. Downstream, the ice was talking to itself, long cracking reports rolling across the water like distant cannon, and between the floes the river showed through, black and quick and impatient. The pilot boats were out already, placing the spring markers. Two days. Perhaps less. After that, no one crossed until the victors permitted it, and everything the victors wished forgotten would be safely frozen in place for another season.

Emrys stood at the lamp post at the quay's end, the one with the rusted base where the iron had scaled away in long curls, and he took out his chalk, and he made the mark.

He had learned it from a deckhand on the *Meridian*, during the storm—half-joking instruction, watch-duty shorthand: *a cross with a tail means waiting; a cross with the tail broken means moved on.* Simple river code, older than the war, meaningless to provosts and historians both. He drew the cross with a broken tail on the lamp post's leeward side, low, where the eye of a woman scanning her own backtrail would catch it and the eye of a following agent would slide past.

Then he straightened, and looked out at the river, and allowed himself, for one moment, the full weight of what he had done in that upstairs room.

He had agreed to betray her. Those were the words, and he would not soften them, because softening words was how men like Renwick built their comfortable histories. He had sat in a dead republic's customs house and sold his help to the machinery hunting her. And none of it was true, and all of it was true, and the trick of the next two days was to keep both statements alive inside him without letting either show on his face—for Renwick would be reading him now the way he read manifests, hunting discrepancy, and the discrepancy he must hide was this: that everything he had promised, he intended to deliver, and none of it as Renwick imagined.

He would take them to her, yes. He would lead Renwick and his silent provost by the nose through every wrong room in Harborton—first the tavern, where Bess Ferrow owed Orsen a betrayal already paid and might owe a misdirection besides; then the boarding houses on Rope Walk where single women of a certain age took rooms under names that were almost their names; then, when the net was strung tight and confident, he would let it close on empty air while the tide ran and the pilot lights burned and the crossings stood open for one last day.

And Renwick—here was the pivot on which everything turned, the point of the knife Emrys had swallowed in that room—Renwick wanted the dispatches archived. Wanted the truth public. Was, in his scholar's soul, on her side of the war over memory. So the bargain Emrys had extracted in writing was not a shield for a betrayal. It was the destination. He would deliver the courier to the historian, in the end—but on her terms, at the archive door, with the registrar's seal already set and the whole city able to read what she had sworn. Let Renwick have his documents. Let him catalogue them, cite them, build his revisionist history on them. That was the only victory that mattered, and Orsen had already won it; all that remained was to keep her alive long enough to enjoy being known.

The wind came up the channel, carrying coal dust and the smell of open water, and far downstream the ice gave a long groaning report, and somewhere in the rotting sheet a seam split wide enough that Emrys could hear the water moving underneath, fast and free.

Two days. He touched the chalk mark once, feeling the grit of it, then turned his back on it and walked up the quay toward the harbor district, unhurried, a tired civil servant going home to supper—while thirty paces behind, patient as a shadow at noon, the provost followed, and ahead of him somewhere in the crooked streets a woman with a borrowed name waited beside a river that was about to break.
