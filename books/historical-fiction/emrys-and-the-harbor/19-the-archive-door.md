## 19. The Archive Door

The fog came off the harbor that morning like a held breath finally released, and Orsen Crane walked into it carrying the republic of Vessany against her chest.

Not in the coat lining anymore. She had cut the stitches out at Bess's tavern before dawn, working by the light of a banked stove with her fingers numb and her ears tuned to every creak of the floorboards overhead, where Bess's nephew slept—or pretended to sleep—and where any moment now the knock might come that meant Bess had given her up hours ago and the agents were already on the stairs. The dispatches rode instead in a satchel slung across her body, strap crossed over the flat place where her breasts had been bound away for eleven years, the leather warm against her ribs from her own heat. It was a small change. It was everything. A coat could be taken off her back in a checkpoint scramble; a satchel would have to be torn from her, and she had decided sometime in the black hour before morning that they would have to tear it.

The fish market was already awake, gulls screaming over the gutting tables, the cobblestones slicked silver with scale and meltwater. Spring was in the air the way a blade is in a folded cloth—you couldn't see it yet, but you could feel its edge waiting. The river ice groaned out beyond the breakwater, long moaning reports like distant cannon, and every soul in Harborton paused when it spoke, looked toward the water, and went back to work a little faster. Everyone understood what the cracking ice meant. Within days—hours, if the wind turned south—the victors' gunboats would come up the channel, the crossings would seal, and whatever paper was not yet stamped and shelved by then might as well never have existed.

Orsen bought a mackerel from the first stall she passed. It was not hunger that moved her, though she had eaten little in three days. It was craft. A man buying fish was a man going home to a wife, a pan, an ordinary morning; a man walking empty-handed through a market with his shoulders set was a man with somewhere specific to be, and somewhere specific was dangerous this week. She haggled over the price with a fishwife whose hands were scaled to the wrist, and let herself be cheated out of two coppers without protest, and walked on with the fish wrapped in newsprint—a victors' broadsheet, she saw, announcing the *Consolidation of Provincial Administration*, as if the republic she had served since she was seventeen had been a filing error.

The deposition office of the harbor archive sat four streets inland from the quay, behind the old chandlery district, in a gray stone building with a pediment worn smooth as driftwood. She had learned its face from Bess's directions and from a city map stolen from a hotel lobby, but maps were one thing and streets another. The streets of Harborton this morning were wrong. Not empty—worse than empty. *Populated* with purpose.

She saw the first sweep from the mouth of Cooper's Lane: six men in the victors' slate-gray greatcoats moving door to door with the unhurried method of men who knew the net was already closed and were merely gathering its edges. One carried a sheaf of printed likenesses. Even at forty paces Orsen knew what her own face looked like reproduced in cheap ink—the jaw too soft in the drawing, always, the artist unable to resist softening it once he'd been told the subject was a woman. They had her description because Bess had broken. Of course Bess had broken. Everyone broke; Orsen had simply been lucky for eleven years, and luck was not a wall, only weather.

She turned down the herring row instead, past the barrel yards, her pulse loud enough that she half-believed the agents could hear it across the district. There was a second approach to the archive—Bess had described it in the last honest hour they ever spent together: *the archive backs onto the salt-house yard; there's a deposition clerk's door on the north side where witnesses go in quietly, so as not to meet their accusers coming out.* A door for the frightened. It figured that the republic's last functioning institution had been designed around fear.

She was three streets from it when the whistles started.

They came from behind and to the left, two short notes and a long one, and the market changed around her the way water changes when a stone goes in—not dramatically, but totally. Conversations died mid-word. A porter set down his crate. An old woman drew her shutters with a sound like a struck match. Orsen did not run. Running was a confession written on the body for everyone to read. She walked, steadily, mackerel in hand, past a line of victors' soldiers double-timing down the cross street, their boots keeping time with her heart, and turned into the salt-house lane with the taste of copper at the back of her throat.

The lane was narrow, walled high with fish-reeking brick, and it emptied into the salt-house yard—and the yard was not empty. A wagon stood there, and men were loading it, and among the men stood one in civilian wool with the particular stillness of someone who did not carry things because his job was to notice them. Aldous Renwick. She had seen him once, through a window, at the requisition hearing; she had memorized him the way she memorized all threats, by the set of his shoulders and the angle of his attention. He was looking at the archive's back wall. At the clerk's door.

Orsen stepped backward into the shadow of a buttress and pressed herself against brick that was cold enough to burn through her coat, and made herself breathe, and understood with perfect clarity that the door she needed was thirty yards away and guarded by the attention of the one man in Harborton who most wanted it to stay shut until spring.

Somewhere beyond the rooftops, the river ice boomed again, longer this time, a sound like the world clearing its throat.

*Not yet,* she told it. *Wait for me.*

* * *

Emrys Vale had signed his name to a lie at seven o'clock that morning, and it had felt, in the act, exactly like signing his name to anything else: the same nib, the same ink, the same small scratch of conscience he had learned to write through the way a man learns to write through a blister.

He had burned Crane's flagged page at his lodging before dawn—fed it to the stove a corner at a time, watching the census ink curl and brighten and vanish—and had then done something far more radical, though no one watching would have known it. He had opened a fresh ledger sheet, ruled it in his neat hand, and entered a corrected record: *Crane, Orsena. Witness, Republic of Vessany. Deposition lodged under seal, Harbor Archive.* Dated. Signed. Filed in sequence where the burned flag used to be. Paper as sanctuary. If they took the ledgers, they would find her named, protected, witnessed. If they took the ledgers, they would also find him.

He had expected to feel afraid afterward. What he felt instead was lightness, terrifying and complete, the way the rigging must feel when the last knot is cast off.

Now he stood in the cooperage doorway on Coal Quay with a census ledger under his arm and watched Renwick's men deploy through the chandlery district, and he understood the geometry of the morning at a glance because geometry was his whole life: they were sweeping toward the archive from the east and the south, closing on the clerk's door, and somewhere inside that closing net—or very soon to be—was Orsena Crane.

He had tried to warn her. He had left word at the tavern in the only cipher they had between them, a chalk mark on the third barrel from the door that meant *the historians have your name, do not come by day.* But Bess's tavern had been quiet in the wrong way when he passed it, its windows dark at an hour when Bess was always scraping the griddle, and the chalk mark had already been scrubbed away by someone's careful hand, and Emrys had stood in the street doing arithmetic of dread and arriving at a sum he could not put down.

A sergeant found him there. Slate-gray coat, red piping, a face built for following orders and occasionally questioning them. "You. Census?"

"Vale. Harbor district." He produced his credentials with the steady hand of eleven years' practice, and hated how useful practice was.

"You'll know the buildings," the sergeant said. "Renwick wants a local man. We're looking for a fugitive, ex-republican courier, may be making for the archive." A printed likeness appeared between them, and Emrys made himself look at it with the mild professional interest of a man comparing a sketch to a ledger. The jaw was too soft. Whoever had drawn her had drawn the woman and missed the man entirely, which meant she had perhaps an hour of anonymity left in the world's eyes, and no more.

"I know the buildings," Emrys said.

They moved west first, away from the water, and every step cost him something he could not itemize. He gave the sergeant accurate answers about street names—he would not be caught in a falsifiable error; that was the whole art of misdirection, he realized, the same art as the census, the same art as the lie in the ledger: precision everywhere except the single point where it mattered. When the sergeant asked which route a courier would take from the tavern district to the archive, Emrys walked him mentally through the map and chose the truth's twin brother.

"The fish-market lanes are being swept already," he said. "If she's watchful—and your likeness suggests she has cause—she'll avoid them. The coal quay runs parallel to the harbor road. Covered wagons load there till midmorning. If I were moving unseen with something to deliver, I'd take the quay, come at the archive from the water side."

It was even true. That was the beauty and horror of it. There was a real path by the coal quay; she *could* have taken it. He was simply betting her life that she hadn't, or that if she had, the wager would buy her minutes, and minutes were all the deposit needed. Timestamp and seal. He had thought about it all night, lying dressed on his bed watching the ceiling, and had reduced her whole chance to those two words. Everything else—her safety, her freedom, whatever unspoken thing had grown between them on the frozen packet and refused to die in the warm city—everything else could survive later, or not. But the timestamp had to happen within the hour.

"Show us the quay," the sergeant said.

Emrys showed him the quay.

It was a good performance, and he despised himself a little for how good it was. He pointed out sight lines and doorways with a clerk's exactitude; he noted which warehouses had back exits; he suggested, gravely, that the coal heaps themselves should be searched, and watched six men begin kicking apart forty tons of black shale while the real door of the real archive stood open and unguarded four streets east. The sergeant thanked him. The word landed on Emrys like a fine ash.

Then, at the quay's end, where the coal dust met the ice-littered water, he heard it: the long crack of the river letting go, louder than yesterday, closer, a sound that ran up through the soles of his boots and settled in his teeth. Down the channel, a stretch of ice the size of a threshing floor tilted, sighed, and slid beneath the gray water. The soldiers stopped to look. Even the gulls went quiet.

"Thaw's early," the sergeant said. "Command wants crossings sealed by tomorrow."

*By tomorrow.* Emrys kept his face arranged into clerical sympathy while something in his chest began to run—not his body; his body stayed perfectly still, hands folded on the ledger, the picture of a minor official waiting to be dismissed—but some engine in him had thrown its full weight against the bars. Tomorrow the archive might be requisitioned entire, crated, carried off into the victors' vaults where sealed testimony could sleep for fifty years. Today, or nothing.

"You can find your own way back to the district office," the sergeant said, already turning away. "Renwick may want you later."

"He knows where to find me," Emrys said, and walked off the quay at a pace precisely calibrated not to attract pursuit, holding his ledger like a man carrying his own heart's evidence, and turned east the moment the first warehouse swallowed him from view.

* * *

The buttress shadow bought Orsen ten minutes and no more. Renwick's men finished loading the wagon—records, she realized; they were carting records *out* of the archive, preemptively, sealing history before it could be deposited—and the civilian in the wool coat said something she could not hear, and two men posted themselves by the clerk's door while the rest pulled away toward the harbor road. Toward the coal quay, she thought, with a cold drop of comprehension. Sweeps converging on the water side. Someone had told them the water side.

Which meant Emrys had told them the water side, which meant Emrys was buying her the land side, which meant—

She stopped the thought before it could become hope, because hope was heavy and she needed her hands free. But her feet had already decided. She crossed the salt-house yard in the wagon's dusty wake, twelve steps that lasted a year, and reached the north wall where the clerk's door waited, iron-banded, unguarded now, its lintel carved with the republic's old motto in letters worn nearly to rumor: *LET IT BE KNOWN.*

Her hand was on the latch when the whistles sounded again—closer, wrong-directioned, coming not from the water side but from behind her, from the fish market, and she understood that the geometry had shifted, that Renwick was cleverer than the map, that maybe no one had bought her anything at all.

Inside the door: a dim passage smelling of paper and lamp oil, stairs going down toward the deposition hall, and a bell rope by the threshold with a small brass plate—*WITNESSES RING ONCE.*

Orsen slipped inside, pulled the rope, and heard, far below, through floors of stone and shelved truth, a single clear toll answer her—as the door at her back shuddered once, softly, against its frame, like a knuckle on wood.
