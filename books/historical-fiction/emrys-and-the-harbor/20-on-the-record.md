## 20. On the Record

The harbor archive smelled of tallow and old salt, of paper that had drunk a century of sea air through its pores. Orsen moved between the shelves in the dark like a woman wading through kelp, one hand trailing the spines of ledgers she would never have time to read. Somewhere above her, boots crossed the floorboards of the reading room—two sets, then three, then silence. The agents were sweeping toward the coal quay. Emrys had bought her that much. She repeated it to herself like a prayer with no god attached: *down the coal quay, down the coal quay.* Every minute they spent pulling apart crates of brine-cured herring was a minute the deposition office stayed empty ahead of her.

She found it at the end of the western aisle: a narrow room with a clerk's desk, an inkwell, a lamp with oil still in its reservoir, and a brass plate screwed to the door—*DEPOSITIONS & OATHS, ASSISTANT REGISTRAR*. The registrar himself had gone home hours ago, or fled with the other officials when word came that victors' men were walking the district. But the office did not run on the man. It ran on the book.

The book lay open on the desk, as if it had been waiting.

Orsen set down the dispatches—wrapped now in oilcloth, tied with the cord she had worn around her waist since Vessany—and unwrapped them for the last time. Her fingers were steady. That surprised her. All night on the rooftops, all day in doorways, her hands had trembled like a drunkard's, and now, at the very threshold of the thing she had crossed a frozen country to do, they went quiet as two stones.

She had drafted the testimony twice: once in cipher, once in plain script, by the light of a shuttered lantern in the map room three aisles back. The cipher copy lay folded against her heart. The plain copy she took out now and read again from the top, though she knew every line the way one knows the lines of one's own face.

*I, Orsena Mirren Crane, born in the parish of Vellichor-in-the-Reeds, Republic of Vessany, do swear and depose as follows—*

The words *born* and *Orsena* sat together on the first line, and she had looked at them for a long time before she could leave them there. Twenty years of hiding in the space between those two names, and here they were, stitched into a single sentence, waiting to become permanent.

Boots again, somewhere far off—a door slamming, voices muffled by shelving and distance. Not here. Not yet.

She turned up the lamp.

* * *

The assistant registrar arrived at half past four in the morning, which Orsen learned later was because he had been summoned by his own night clerk, who had found the deposition office lamplit and occupied and had not been brave enough—or perhaps had simply been wise enough—to come in alone. He was a small man, gray at the temples, with the ink-stained cuffs of someone whose whole life was conducted at a desk, and he stopped dead in the doorway when he saw her, one hand going to his collar.

"You can't be in—" he began, and then he saw the dispatches on the desk, and the oath book open beside them, and something in his face changed channels entirely. "Those are sealed materials."

"They are," Orsen said. "They're also evidence of high treason committed by the ministers of the Republic of Vessany against their own peace terms, against their own army, and against eleven thousand people in the river districts whose evacuation orders were sold to the enemy six days before the armistice." She laid her hand flat on the plain-script testimony. "I've come to put it on the record. I believe this office is still neutral territory under the Harbor Accords."

The registrar's eyes went to the brass plate on his own door, as if confirming where he stood. Outside, distantly, came the sound of the fish-market bell—not the hour, someone was ringing it hard and fast—and beneath it, fainter, shouting.

"The Accords are being renegotiated," he said carefully. "As of Tuesday."

"Then I've come at the right time."

He should have called for the watch. Later—he would tell her this much later, in the way people confess small mercies as though they were larger ones—he had thought about it. He had thought about the watch, and about the agents moving through the district, and about the fact that in nine years behind this desk he had sworn in fishermen's widows and cargo disputes and one memorable case of a lighthouse keeper's parrot entered as witness to smuggling, and never once, not once, had anyone come in at four in the morning with treason in a satchel and asked, politely, to be heard.

"Sit down," he said.

"I'll stand," Orsen said. "I've been sitting in hiding for a week."

Something almost like a smile touched his mouth and withdrew. He settled behind his desk, drew the oath book toward him, uncapped the ink. His hands, she noticed, were not entirely steady either, and this steadied hers.

"Name," he said, "for the record."

And there it was. The question she had spent twenty years answering wrong on purpose.

"Crane," she said. "Orsena Mirren Crane."

His pen paused above the page. He looked up at her—at the close-cropped hair, the man's coat, the jaw she had bound every morning since she was nineteen—and whatever he saw, he wrote what she had told him. The nib scratched. The sound filled the little room like rain.

"Residence?"

"Last lawful residence, the city of Vessany. Present residence—" and here she almost laughed, because what was the truth but absurd? "—this building. Since roughly midnight."

"Occupation."

"Courier," she said. "Formerly of the Foreign Ministry of Vessany. Formerly," she added, because formerly had begun to apply to nearly everything she was.

He wrote it. Then he set down the pen, folded his hands, and said, in the flat, careful voice of a man stepping onto ice and testing it with each footfall: "Do you understand that a deposition, once sworn and sealed, enters the permanent register of this archive; that it cannot be withdrawn, amended, or destroyed except by order of the full registry court; and that under Article Fourteen of the Harbor Accords, sworn testimony deposited in a neutral registry is protected material, privileged against seizure by any signatory power?"

"I understand," Orsen said, "better than you know."

"Do you swear, before this registrar and this record, that the testimony you are about to give is true, complete to the best of your knowledge, and given freely?"

From somewhere beyond the walls came running feet, and a voice calling *the quay, the coal quay, check the coal quay*, and Orsen thought of Emrys walking them there with his ledger held out like a lantern, leading wolves away from the door with nothing but paperwork and nerve. She thought of Bess, whose betrayal had put the wolves on her trail at all. She thought of the republic coming down behind her on the Meridian's stern rail, its colors folding into the fog like something drowning.

"I so swear," she said.

"Then begin at the beginning," the registrar said, "and speak clearly. The record hears everything once."

* * *

She began with the peace terms.

She spoke of the winter negotiations at Corvel House, where she had carried documents between the ministers' wing and the cipher room, and how the drafts that reached the table were never quite the drafts that left the cipher room. She spoke of Minister Halvenne's private correspondence—the pages sewn into her coat lining now, exhibit by exhibit as the registrar tagged them—letters in which the evacuation corridors of the eastern river districts were priced, haggled over, and sold, so that the armistice line could be drawn where certain men's warehouses and mills would fall safely on the victors' side. Eleven thousand people had been moved out of the corridors' protection and into the path of the advance. The ministers had called it *strategic realignment*. In the letters they called it what it was: *consideration rendered*.

Her voice held. She had expected it to break—had rehearsed this moment in the dark of the map room and assumed grief would take her at the throat—but instead it came out level and clear, the courier's voice she had trained herself into, the voice that delivered what it carried without spilling anything.

She gave dates. She gave names. When she reached the part where the names became dangerous—Halvenne, Corvel, the deputy cipher chief whose initials appeared on the routing slips—she heard the fish-market bell stop, and in the new silence the sound of her own pulse.

"And your means of knowledge," the registrar said, not looking up. "For each document cited."

"Personal custody," she said. "Continuous, from the fall of the cipher room to this desk." She untied the oilcloth and pushed the dispatches across to him, one bundle, forty-one pages, the weight of a sleeping cat. "I deliver them into the registry's protection, per the deposit provisions of Article Fourteen, to be held unopened pending certification."

The registrar drew the bundle toward him and then, for a moment, simply rested his hand on it, as though feeling for a heartbeat.

Outside, the noise had changed key. The shouting had organized itself. Boots, many boots, in the street now rather than scattered through the district, and a voice with the practiced carrying power of official authority calling something about the archive being closed, the archive being requisitioned, all persons to remain where—

The door at the end of the western aisle banged open hard enough to shelve-rattle the whole wall.

Renwick came down the aisle at a pace just short of running, coat flying, two agents behind him, and Orsen watched him arrive and understood in one cold flash exactly what she was looking at: a historian who had stopped being a historian somewhere between the harbor records office and tonight. His face had the terrible openness of a man who believes he is still in command of events.

"The hall is closed," the registrar said, rising, and his voice cracked only slightly. "This is an active deposition under seal. Article Fourteen."

"There is no Article Fourteen anymore," Renwick said. "There hasn't been since Tuesday." But his eyes had already gone past the registrar to the desk—to the oath book, open, the ink still wet on the heading; to the dispatches, tagged and numbered; to Orsen, standing with her hands loose at her sides—and Orsen watched the arithmetic happen behind his face, item by item, and watched him arrive at the sum too late.

"When," Renwick said quietly, "was it entered?"

The registrar looked at the wall clock. So did Orsen. The second hand climbed with obscene serenity.

"Entered and sworn as of twenty-two minutes past four o'clock," the registrar said. "Sealed as of—" he checked, lifted the wax, let it fall red and final onto the fold, pressed his stamp into it, "—thirty-one minutes past. The record is closed, sir. You are welcome to file a request for certified access, in duplicate, through the ordinary channels."

One of the agents laughed—a single startled syllable, immediately strangled. Renwick did not laugh. He stood very still in the aisle between the shelves, and Orsen saw something move through his face that she recognized, because she had felt it herself on the Meridian's deck watching the colors come down: the sight of a thing ending that cannot be ended slowly, only all at once.

"You understand what you've done," he said. To her, not to the registrar. "You've handed them a weapon. Everything in those pages will be used—it will be quoted, excerpted, bent—by whoever finds it convenient. Truth doesn't stay true once it's in a ledger. It becomes material."

"Yes," Orsen said. "That's why it goes in a ledger, and not in a fire. Material can be reexamined. Ash can't."

"It will cost you," Renwick said. "You know that. Your name is on the first line. There is no version of tomorrow in which you are safe."

"No," Orsen agreed, and heard, to her own surprise, no fear in her voice at all. "But there is a version in which I am *true*, and I find I've stopped being willing to trade the second for the first."

Somewhere outside, the boots were turning. A whistle sounded, doubled, and Renwick's agents looked to him, and he looked at Orsen for a long moment—long enough that she understood the look was the beginning of the next chapter of this, whatever it would be—and then he turned and went back up the aisle, slower than he had come, and the outer door closed behind him with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

The registrar sat down heavily. His hand, when he reached for the oath book to make the final attestation entry, was shaking now, all the shaking arriving late, the way it does.

"Witness signature," he said. "Below the seal. Your mark, madam, for the permanent record."

Orsen took the pen. Her hand was steady again—steady as stone, steady as the harbor water in windless weather—and she signed, in a script she had not written in twenty years, *Orsena M. Crane*, and the registrar blotted it, and closed the book, and the little room went quiet enough that she could hear the river working at the ice outside, cracking it, groaning, beginning at last to let go.

It was done. Timestamped, sealed, witnessed, permanent. Eleven thousand people had their account on the record, and so did the woman she had buried in a border town two decades ago, resurrected in ink at four in the morning by her own hand.

She sat down, finally, on the hard clerk's chair, and discovered that triumph, when it actually arrives, feels almost exactly like exhaustion wearing a borrowed coat.

The outer door opened again, softly this time, and a voice she knew better than any voice in the world said, from the mouth of the western aisle:

"The coal quay didn't hold them long."

Emrys came down between the shelves with snow in his hair and a bruise darkening along one cheekbone, holding his census ledger against his chest the way a soldier carries a folded flag, and Orsen rose to meet him, and behind them both the river ice groaned and split, wide awake, all the way to the sea.
