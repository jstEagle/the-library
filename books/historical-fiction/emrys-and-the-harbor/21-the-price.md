## 21. The Price

They held her for eleven days in a room above the deposition hall that had once been a chart-room, its walls still hung with brass instruments nobody had thought to remove—sextants, sounding leads, a barometer whose needle had given up somewhere around the fall of Vessany and now rested at *storm*. Orsen counted the days by the light through the harbor-facing window, which arrived each morning a little earlier, a little whiter, the way spring arrives in a northern port: not as warmth but as argument.

She was not mistreated. That was the strangest part, the part she kept turning over like a coin of uncertain currency. She was brought tea in the mornings, thick and bitter, in a chipped enamel cup. A soldier with a farmer's broken knuckles stood guard outside her door and sometimes, when he thought she couldn't hear, hummed songs that were not victors' songs at all but old river ballads, the kind Vessany's bargemen had sung before the war made singing complicated. She was questioned twice a day by men who called themselves readers rather than interrogators, as if renaming the work might lighten it.

They wanted one thing: the original dispatches. And they could not have them, because the dispatches no longer existed as loose documents. They existed as testimony—hers—sworn, transcribed, sealed, timestamped, and entered into the public record of the Harbor Archive under the name **Orsena Crane, courier in the service of the Republic of Vessany, witness to the sale of the peace terms**. The assistant registrar, a small precise woman named Ilse Vantor who smelled of ink and cold air, had performed the sealing with a ceremony so brief it was nearly invisible: wax, stamp, ledger line, done. Eleven seconds, perhaps. Eleven seconds against eleven days of questioning, and the eleven seconds won every time.

"You understand," said the senior reader on the fourth day—a soft-spoken man with the pale, unlined face of someone who worked indoors among other people's words—"that the record cannot be unsealed without your consent or a judicial order. And a judicial order requires grounds. And grounds require..." He gestured vaguely, a man describing a door in a house that had burned down. "...a state of affairs that does not currently obtain."

"Then I don't understand what you're keeping me for," Orsen said.

"For assessment." He folded his hands. "The record protects you from disappearing. It does not protect you from being *read*."

She learned to give them nothing in the interviews that was not already in the deposition, word for word, page for page. When they asked about Emrys Vale she said he was a census taker who had asked her questions required by his office. When they asked about Bess she said she had drunk one cup of cider in a tavern and been told the courier route was closed. When they asked how she had come to be carrying the dispatches at all, she recited the account she had written at three in the morning in the archive's back room, her hand cramping around the pen, grief and purpose indistinguishable in her chest—and she watched the readers' faces as they listened to their own government's treachery read back to them in a woman's steady voice, and she understood that some of them had not known, and that knowing now was a wound they would carry home.

That was the strange arithmetic of it. They had power over her body—her room, her door, her guard, her days—but she had power over the story, and the story was sealed where no fire could reach it and no editor could soften it. Renwick's histories would say what Renwick's histories said. The archive said what it said. Somewhere in the harbor records office, in a drawer that would outlast everyone in this building, her mother's name and her father's name and her own true name sat together on a line of clerk's script, and no one could take the line out without leaving a hole shaped exactly like it.

On the eleventh day, Ilse Vantor came up herself, keys chiming at her belt.

"You're released," she said. "Detention lifted. Not cleared—there will be a review, eventually, when the provisional authority decides what 'eventually' means. But the record stands, and holding a sworn witness past the term of protective custody is beginning to embarrass people who prefer not to be embarrassed." She paused at the door. "For what it's worth—which is nothing, which is less than nothing—I read the whole deposition. Twice." Her jaw did something small and hard. "My father ran grain barges on the Vess. You've made an old woman's winter interesting, Crane."

Then the door was open, and the stairs were there, and the cold came up to meet her like water.

* * *

Emrys heard it first as rumor, the way everything traveled in Harborton that winter—as a murmur in a queue, a correction on a manifest, a sentence half-heard between two dockhands sharing bread. *The courier's out. They let the courier go.* By then the story had already begun to shed particulars and grow fur, the way stories do in port cities. Some versions had her escaping across the ice at night. Some had her executed and the execution covered up. One, which he overheard outside the requisitioned records office where he had gone to surrender his remaining ledgers, insisted she was seven feet tall and had fought off six agents with a boat hook.

He knew better. What he knew was smaller and heavier: that on the morning after the deposition hall, he had walked into Renwick's office as agreed, and lied, and been caught lying, and been suspended for it—and that the lie had bought exactly enough time.

They had given him a hearing that lasted forty minutes. A junior magistrate with tired eyes, a stenographer who yawned, Renwick sitting at the back with his hands folded and his expression arranged into regret. The evidence was thin and Emrys made it thinner. Yes, he had removed a page from a working ledger. No, he could not produce it. Yes, he understood the gravity. His explanation—that he had spoiled the page in ordinary course, spilled ink, mis-entered a name—was transparent, and transparency was all it needed to be. Falsification of census records carried penalties up to two years' labor service. But prosecuting a census taker whose flagged entry pointed directly at a sealed public deposition containing proof that the peace terms had been sold—that was a different kind of paper trail, and the provisional authority wanted no part of it while negotiations with the neutral powers dragged on. So: suspension pending investigation. Surrender of credentials. Report weekly to the harbor office. A mark on his file that would follow him like a smell.

"You understand what you cost us," Renwick had said afterward, in the corridor, quietly, almost gently—the gentleness worse than anger. "Not the page. The *principle*. A record is only worth what a man will not do to it."

"Yes," Emrys had said. "I found that out too."

And now it was the last week of the thaw, and the river was breaking up.

* * *

The quay at low tide was a country of ruined geometry. Ice that had lain solid and trustworthy for four months had spent three days cracking itself into plates and slabs and shards, grinding against the pilings with a sound like slow applause, like teeth, like a door being forced somewhere far under the world. The packet boats were idle; nothing with a hull its owner loved went into that water yet. Gulls stood on the floes with the patient insolence of landlords. The air smelled of thawed rope, fish, coal smoke, and the green nothing-scent underneath all of it that meant the year was turning whether anyone's papers were in order or not.

Orsen came down the quay steps slowly, because her legs had forgotten long walking in the chart-room, and stopped when she saw him.

He was standing at the ice-choked edge with his hands in the pockets of a coat too thin for the wind, hatless, looking out at the grinding floes the way he had once looked at his ledgers—with total, doomed attention, as if the river might be reconciled if only he read it carefully enough. He had heard her boots on the boards. She knew he had, because his shoulders changed, a settling, a readiness, before he turned.

"Census taker," she said.

"Suspended census taker." He came toward her, and stopped at the distance strangers keep, and then, deliberately, closed it to the distance friends do. "They took my book away. Did you know that? Twenty-two years of manifests and household counts and death registers, and they gave me a receipt."

"What will you do?"

"Report weekly. Answer questions. Wait." He smiled, and it was a real smile, tired at the roots. "I'm very good at waiting. It's most of what counting is."

The floes ground against the pilings. A gull screamed somewhere upstream and was answered.

"They'll clear you," she said. "Eventually. Renwick needs quiet more than he needs your head. The record embarrassed them, Emrys. Your little page embarrassed them twice—once for what it proved and once because a *clerk* proved it. Men like that forgive anything except being made ridiculous."

"And you?" He looked at her hands, then quickly away, as though he had caught himself at something intimate. "What will you do? There's a review. There's always a review."

"I stay." She said it and heard it land in her own chest like a stone dropped down a well—she heard how deep it went before it struck water. "I can't run anymore. Running was the whole shape of my life for six years, and I did it well, and it made me no one. The record says who I am. Orsena Crane, of Vessany, witness. If I run, I make the record a lie, and I didn't pay—" her voice caught on something and she let it catch; there was no one left to hide the catching from, "—I didn't pay what I paid to make it a lie. Let them review me. Let them watch me buy herring and mend nets and grow old in a boarding house. Every gray hair will be testimony."

He nodded slowly. Out on the water a slab the size of a chapel floor tilted, rose, and slid under with a sound like a sigh, and neither of them looked at it, because they were looking at each other.

"I want to tell you something," Emrys said, "and I've rehearsed it badly, which you'll appreciate, because I never rehearse anything. In my profession you don't rehearse. You observe, you record, you attest." He took a breath. "When I flagged your entry, I told myself it was procedure. A gap in the registry is a gap in the registry; the state has an interest; I have a duty. And then I burned the page. And the strange thing—the thing I've been carrying around for three weeks like a stone in my boot—is that burning it felt exactly like recording it. Exactly the same. Same certainty, same steadiness of hand. Because both times I was attesting to the same thing. To *you*. To what's true." He shook his head at himself. "I don't know what that makes me. A bad official. Probably a bad citizen of whatever we're citizens of now. But I know it makes me yours, in whatever fashion you'll have me, and you may have me in none, and I'd still say it, because it's on the record now—" the smile again, crooked, "—and I've developed strong feelings about records."

The wind came off the floes and pushed her hair across her mouth, and she pushed it back with a hand that was not quite steady, and did not try to make it steady.

"When I was seventeen," she said, "I promised myself I would never be known. Known was dangerous. Known was a name on a list. I kept that promise for nine years and it kept me alive and it starved me to a rind." She looked at the grinding ice, at the black water opening between the plates like pages parting. "You're the only person alive who knows the whole of it, and you paid your livelihood to keep it, and I find—" now her voice did steady, found a level it hadn't had since the republic's colors came down, "—I find I don't want to go back to starving."

"Then don't," he said simply.

Neither promised. Promises were currency and they had both learned what currencies do in collapsed states. Instead he offered his arm—formally, almost comically, a gentleman's gesture performed on a rotten quay in a defeated wind—and she took it, and they walked the length of the ice-choked waterfront without speaking, past the idle packets and the gulls and the pilings groaning under the season's great change, two people whose names were both, now, in drawers in the harbor archive, entered, attested, sealed against forgetting.

At the top of the quay steps, where the road forked toward the boarding houses and the harbor office, they stopped. He would go left, to report; she would go right, to lodge, to buy herring, to begin the long unglamorous labor of staying.

"A letter came for me this morning," Emrys said. "From the archive. Registrar Vantor's office. My suspension is under review, and they request—" he unfolded the paper, though he plainly knew it by heart, "—they request my attendance, as a person with relevant knowledge, at a preliminary inquiry concerning certain *historical materials recently deposited*, and whether any further depositions might be... encouraged." He looked up. "Renwick isn't finished. He means to write the war properly this time, and he wants witnesses, and he's decided witnesses are easier than archives."

Orsen watched the floes turn in the gray water below them, plate over plate, the river remembering it was a river.

"Then he'll want me too," she said. "Good. Let him come to the record. This time I'll be the one asking questions."
