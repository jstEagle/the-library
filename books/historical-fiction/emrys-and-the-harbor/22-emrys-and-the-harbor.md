## 22. Emrys and the Harbor

The harbor had a new clock now. It hung from an iron bracket above the registrar's office, painted the gray of a gull's wing, and it kept better time than any clock Emrys Vale had known in his years of service. He checked it out of habit, though he was early—he was always early—then climbed the worn steps to the records room with his ledger under his arm and his coat still smelling of river fog.

Nine years since the ice broke. Nine years since he had stood on that quay with his hands shaking and his conscience burning like a coal in his pocket, and watched a woman in a man's greatcoat walk into the deposition hall carrying nothing but her own name.

He had been restored to a modest post. Modest was the word for it—the assistant registrarship had gone to another man during the investigation, and when Emrys returned from his suspension it was to the same desk, the same ledger stands, the same smell of ink and damp stone. The new state had needed clerks more than it had needed martyrs, and so he had been neither punished nor honored, merely reabsorbed, the way water is reabsorbed into a river after a flood. His file still carried a note about irregularities in the census of a certain winter. No one read old notes anymore. Paper aged faster than grudges.

But the harbor remembered him in its own way. The younger clerks came to him with questions of procedure, because Emrys Vale knew where every body was buried in the archive—not the dead, but the documents: which shelf held the manifests of the last packet season, which drawer the exemption papers of men who had never existed, which folio contained a deposition sworn aloud at half past four in the afternoon while victors' agents hammered at the door.

That deposition had a number now. Deposition 4471-C, Harbor Archive, Harborton. He could recite the number the way other men recited psalms.

* * *

The book arrived on a Tuesday, in a crate of new acquisitions from the southern presses, wrapped in brown paper and smelling of glue. *The Fall of Vessany: A Reckoning*, by Marta Ilves, formerly of the University of Corven. Revisionist history, the catalog slip said, as though revision were a disease rather than a duty. Emrys signed for it because signing for things was what he did, and because no one else in the reading room would touch a book with Vessany in the title. For years after the war, the name alone had been enough to make officials look away, the way one looks away from a beggar whose suffering implicates everyone who passes.

He took it to his desk at the end of the gallery, beneath the tall windows where the light fell in long gray columns, and he opened it not expecting anything. He had learned to expect nothing. Hope was a habit he had broken like pipe-smoking, painfully and with relapses.

Ilves wrote well. That surprised him; he had expected either propaganda or pedantry, and instead found a mind working through the rubble like a patient salvage diver. She wrote of the ministers who had sold the peace terms while their republic starved. She wrote of the courier networks, rolled up one by one. And then, on page three hundred and eleven, in a chapter titled *The Paper Witnesses*, he read:

> "The fullest surviving account of the betrayal comes not from any minister's memoir or victor's dispatch but from the sworn testimony of a single witness, deposited at Harborton under the name Orsena Crane—a woman who had lived for years as a man named Orsen Crane, carrying the republic's last dispatches sewn into her coat. Her deposition, timestamped hours before the crossings were sealed, names the signatories of the secret terms and describes, in plain language that no historian has improved upon, what it cost to carry truth through a country that had decided not to survive. 'I write this,' she says, in the final paragraph, 'not because I expect to be believed, but because being believed was never the point. The record exists. I existed.'"

Emrys read the paragraph twice. Then a third time. Around him the reading room went about its business—pages turning, a clerk sneezing in the dust, somewhere far off the harbor bell tolling for a fog that hadn't yet arrived. He sat very still, the way he had once sat very still over a ledger page, feeling the weight of small paper's power over lives.

*I existed.*

She had told him once, on the frozen waystation, in half-truths, that the worst loneliness was not having no one—it was having no one who knew. That night she had been a rumor wearing a borrowed name. Now she was a footnote in a university press, cited by scholars, quoted in seminars she would never attend. Somewhere in the world, students were reading her words without knowing her face, and that was its own kind of justice, and its own kind of cruelty.

He turned the page. There was a note appended, in smaller type:

> "Deposition 4471-C remains open to public inspection at the Harborton Harbor Archive. The witness's subsequent fate is unknown. Records indicate detention, release, and departure northward in the spring following the sealing of the crossings."

Northward. He had known that much; he had stood on the quay and watched her go, or rather watched the boat go, because she had already disappeared into its hold before the lines were cast. Neither promising what they could not give—that was what they had done, standing in the ice-choked silence, and he had kept the promise of no promises faithfully for nine years.

He closed the book gently, as though it might bruise.

* * *

Spring came late that year, and when it came it came all at once, the way it always did on the coast—one week of mud, and then the lilts along the seawall exploding purple overnight, and the herring boats going out at four in the morning with their lamps swinging. Emrys had taken to walking the quay on Sundays, an old man's habit arrived prematurely, watching the ice go out of the river even though there had been little ice that year. Some winters the river barely froze at all now. The climate of the world, like the politics of it, seemed to be softening at the edges.

It was on such a walk, in the third week of April, that the letter found him.

Not at his lodging—the landlady said a boy had come asking for the registrar, been told the registrar was at church or the pub or wherever landladies suppose registrars go, and left the letter wedged in the door frame. Emrys found it that evening, a thin envelope, cheap paper, addressed in a hand he recognized instantly and would have recognized across forty years and an ocean: the small, deliberate script of someone who had learned to write in cipher first and in letters second, each character formed as though it were evidence.

His own name on the front: *E. Vale, Registrar's Office, Harborton*. No return address. A postmark from a town he had to look up in the atlas—Freehaven, in the northern republics, a place that appeared on maps only recently, since the borders had been redrawn again.

He did not open it on the street. He carried it up to his rooms, set it on the table, lit his lamp, made tea he did not drink, and then, having delayed as long as dignity allowed, broke the seal.

> "Dear Mr. Vale,
>
> You will forgive the delay. Nine years is not so long a time as it feels; I am told historians now consider it a reasonable interval between publication and correspondence.
>
> I am alive. I thought you should hear it from me and not from a footnote.
>
> My name is Orsena Crane. You are one of perhaps four people living who know what that sentence costs me to write. I use it openly here—in Freehaven they know me as the woman who deposed the ministers' treason, and knowing it has bought me a kind of citizenship that my birth country never offered and my adopted country tried to hang me for. I keep ledgers for the harbor cooperative. Ironic, I know. I am good at it. You taught me something about what paper can hold.
>
> I read Ilves's book. Someone sent it to me anonymously—I suspect Bess, who survived everything including her own treachery, and who has retired to keep a tavern two streets from mine, where we do not speak of the war and speak of almost nothing else. We are, I think, the only two people who remember certain evenings, and that makes us companions of a stubborn kind.
>
> I want you to know that I have thought often of that night in the deposition hall. Of the hammering on the door. Of your voice saying, 'The record is sealed,' in the tone of a man announcing weather. I have told that story many times, and in every telling, you appear. In some tellings you are braver than you were. In most you are exactly as brave as you were, which is to say afraid and doing it anyway. I have come to believe that is the only courage worth naming.
>
> I burned my forged papers the first spring here. I keep my true ones in a tin on the mantel. I am known. Do you understand what that means, for someone like me? Not safe—I am not sure safety was ever on offer, for either of us. But known. When I walk into the cooperative hall, people say, 'There is Orsena Crane.' When I die, the register will say my right name. That is not nothing. That may be everything.
>
> The historians want me to write a memoir. I have begun it, God help me. It is terrible so far—all chronology and no blood. But I find that when I cannot get the words right, I think of you sitting in the census office with your ledger, recording ordinary people accurately, as though accuracy were a form of love. Perhaps it is. Perhaps that is the whole of what I learned in those years: that to be recorded truly is to be loved in the only way that lasts.
>
> Come north, if you ever tire of the harbor. The river here does freeze, properly, and in winter the whole town goes quiet and white, and there is work for a careful man. Freehaven needs registrars. Freehaven needs everything.
>
> If you do not come, write anyway. Tell me the harbor is still there. Tell me the gulls are still insolent. Tell me something ordinary, so I will know the world held.
>
> Yours in the record,
>
> Orsena Crane"

Emrys read the letter four times. Then he folded it along its original creases and set it on the table, and he took off his glasses, and for a long while he simply sat in the lamplight while the tea went cold and the harbor bell tolled the hour across the water.

Belonging achieved not through safety, but through being known. He thought of her phrase from the deposition—I existed—and understood, finally, that it had never been a lament. It had been a description of the only home she had ever wanted, and he had helped build it, one timestamped page at a time.

He slept badly, which was usual, and woke early, which was also usual, and dressed in his good coat, which was not.

* * *

The registrar's office opened at eight. At ten past eight, Emrys Vale submitted a letter of request for extended leave, citing family matters in the northern republics. The assistant registrar—young, earnest, terrified of him in the way juniors are terrified of men who know where everything is filed—stammered that he would need approval from the harbor commission.

"Then obtain it," Emrys said pleasantly, and went back to his desk.

At noon he walked down to the quay one last time. The tide was high; the fishing boats knocked their hulls against the pilings like patient horses. Gulls wheeled and screamed their insolence at the sky, exactly as instructed. He stood a long moment at the spot—there was no marker, but he knew it to the paving stone—where a woman had once walked into a hall with nothing but her name, and he had stood aside to let history pass through him like wind through a doorway.

He had spent nine years believing that act had cost him everything worth having. Standing on the sun-warmed stones, he understood that it had cost him only the life he had been pretending to live—which was, he supposed, the same bargain she had made, and in the same currency.

That evening, packing by lamplight, he came upon the old census ledger from that winter, bottom of a trunk, spine cracked. He turned to the flagged page—or where the flagged page had been. In its place, in his own younger hand, was the corrected entry, the one he had written while the thaw cracked the river outside: *Crane, Orsena. Witness. Recorded.*

He had forgotten he had kept the ledger. He had forgotten the entry survived. He sat on the edge of the bed holding it, laughing a little, helplessly, at the persistence of paper—how it waited in trunks and archives and footnotes, patient as seed, until the season of its planting came around.

He packed the ledger. Some documents belonged in archives; others belonged wherever the truth in them was actually known.

The commission approved his leave in eleven days, with a note questioning the urgency. He did not answer the note. On the morning of departure the harbor wore its spring fog like a shawl, and the northern boat blew its whistle twice, and Emrys Vale stood at the rail with one book, one letter, one ledger, and no promises to give anyone—which meant, at last, that he was free to keep one.

The boat cast off. The harbor fell away behind him, gray and kind and full of eyes, and ahead the river ran north toward the ice, toward a white and quiet town where a woman kept ledgers under her own name and a tavern-keeper poured drinks for the only two people who remembered.

He took out the letter and read the postscript he had missed the first four times, hidden below the signature in that small deliberate hand:

*"P.S. — The cooperative needs a registrar by midsummer. I may have mentioned your name. I may have mentioned it accurately."*

Emrys laughed aloud, startling the gulls, and the river carried him north.
