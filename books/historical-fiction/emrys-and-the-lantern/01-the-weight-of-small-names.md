## 1. The Weight of Small Names

The snow came down that January like ash from a distant burning, fine and grey and patient, settling on the slate roofs of Halloran Park until the whole estate seemed to have been dipped in candle tallow. Cyrus Thornbury had learned to read weather by the way it fell. Rain meant hurry; fog meant watchfulness; snow like this — slow, deliberate, indifferent to everything beneath it — snow meant the world had decided to forget what it was carrying, and she had best do the same for a few hours.

She carried the basin of water against her hip with both hands, the way the army had taught her, because a nurse's hands were never free and never idle and never, ever noticed. That was the first lesson of surviving seven years among men who outranked you by every measure the world kept count of: be useful, be quiet, be furniture. A chair is not asked questions. A lamp is not suspected.

The workshop had been converted three winters past from its old purpose — carriages, harness, the smell of linseed oil still ghosting under the newer smells of carbolic and blood — into something no one at the estate called a hospital aloud, because calling it a hospital would make it one, and then there would be forms, and inspectors, and names in official books. Better it remain *the workshop*, where six convalescent men lay in iron cots between the carriage bays, and where Cyrus moved among them each evening with her lamp and her basin and her perfect, unremarkable silence.

"Miss Thornbury." Private Alder, nearest the window, youngest of them and therefore the one who pretended hardest not to be afraid. His shoulder had been shattered at Badajoz and mended crooked, and he would carry the crookedness all his life in place of a wife or a trade. "Snow's stopped the post again, they say."

"Post will come when the roads decide," she said, wringing the cloth. The water stung her knuckles where the cold had split them. She wound wool around those splits each night; she did not mention them; mentioning them would make them a subject, and subjects drew eyes. "Give me your arm."

He gave it. She washed the wound with the gentleness that was her only extravagance, the one thing she spent lavishly and hid completely, and while she worked he talked, as they all talked when her hands were on them, the way men confess to a river. He talked about his mother in Reading, about a dog named Bouncer, about the sergeant who had pulled him out of the trench at Badajoz and caught the ball that should have been his.

"What was his name?" Cyrus asked. She asked this question perhaps forty times a day, in exactly this tone — mild, half-attentive, a nurse's idle courtesy. It was the most important question she asked anyone, and she had taught herself to ask it so lightly that no one ever thought to wonder why.

"Hollis," Alder said. "Sergeant Hollis. Tom Hollis." He paused, watching her hands. "They put him in the dispatches, didn't they? I'd have thought. Man like that."

"Undoubtedly," said Cyrus.

She dried his arm. She straightened the blanket. She lifted her lamp and moved to the next cot, and if her mind had already begun composing the entry — *Hollis, Thomas, sergeant, 44th, fell at Badajoz, 6 April, saving Pvt. Alder, J.; no mention in dispatches; recommended twice by Capt. ___ (name withheld by officer's order)* — nothing of that showed in her face. Nothing ever showed in her face. It was the face of a woman of thirty-one years who looked older when she was tired and younger when she was interested, which meant it looked older nearly always, and it was a face people forgot within an hour of seeing it. She had verified this. She had tested it deliberately, once, standing beside a colonel while a clerk described her to him — *the nurse, sir, dark-haired, plain, quiet* — and the colonel had looked directly at her and greeted her as though meeting her for the first time. She had curtsied as though it were the first time too.

Invisibility was not a curse. She had stopped believing it was a curse somewhere around the second year of the war, in a tent that smelled of rot and rain, when she understood that the invisible were the only ones left holding anything true.

The last cot held Corporal Meech, who slept badly and woke screaming and apologized for it each time, and after him came the part of the evening that belonged to no one but Cyrus. She banked the stove. She checked the lanterns. She told the night orderly — a groom named Pettifer who owed his position to Lord Halloran's charity and his silence to Cyrus's discretion regarding the brandy — that she would lock up.

Then she sat at the small desk in the tack room, among the smell of leather and old horse sweat, and opened the ledger.

It was not a beautiful book. It had been a workshop ledger originally, its first third filled with entries in a coachman's hand recording axle grease and buckskin, and Cyrus had bought it for twopence when the estate cleared its stores. The remaining two-thirds were hers. She wrote small, tight, upright — a hand that wasted no ink — and she wrote in a code of her own devising that was barely a code at all: ordinary words in ordinary sequence, so innocent that any man flipping through it would see a woman's fussy household accounts. *Eggs, sixpence.* *Soap, from Mrs. Barrow.* But the eggs were men, and the soap was regiments, and the sums were dates, and only Cyrus could read the market prices of the dead.

Tonight's entries:

*Sgt. Hollis, T., 44th Foot. Fell Badajoz, 6 April 1812, drawing fire from the sap. Recommended for commendation by Lt. Farrow. Recommendation withdrawn — reason unrecorded. Name absent from London Gazette, 19 May 1812. Widow: none known. Mother living, Hereford, address unknown.*

She knew the shape of these entries the way a priest knows the liturgy. Name, rank, manner of death, the truth of it — which was almost never the version in the dispatches — and then the long catalogue of what the world had done with the name afterward, which was almost always nothing.

There were two hundred and eleven names now. She did not need to count. She felt the weight of them the way she felt the weight of the basin, distributed correctly across her body, carried without complaint.

She was finishing the Hollis entry when she heard the footsteps, and her hand did not shake and her page did not turn, because seven years had trained even her reflexes into furniture. But some animal part of her, low in the stomach, went flat and waiting.

It was only Emrys Halloran.

The younger son crossed the workshop floor beyond the tack-room door with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, moving through the blue hour of the unlit hall like someone who had not yet decided whether to exist that day. He was twenty-three and had his father's height and none of his father's certainty; he stopped at the window bay where the recovered Crimean lantern sat in pieces on the workbench — Cyrus had watched the crate arrive in November, watched him begin the restoration with the fierce privacy of a man doing penance for something that wasn't his fault — and then he saw her lamplight under the tack-room door, and stood a moment in the dark as if considering knocking.

He did not knock. He never knocked. He turned and went back toward the house, and Cyrus let out a breath she had been holding since Badajoz.

She blew out her own lamp, folded the ledger into the canvas satchel she carried everywhere — the satchel was as unremarkable as she was, patched at one corner, smelling faintly of carbolic — and let herself out into the snow.

The walk to the dower cottage where she lodged took eleven minutes along the garden wall. She used the time as she always did, letting the faces of the day file past her inward eye: Alder and his crooked shoulder; Meech waking at four with the scream strangled halfway, because even his nightmares had learned discretion around her; Sergeant Hollis, whom she had never met, dying at Badajoz for a captain's reputation.

Her brother came last, as he always did, and stayed longest.

Edwin Thornbury had fallen at Corunna in the retreat, in the ice and the dark, when Cyrus was nineteen and still a girl who signed her letters with flourishes. No one had seen him fall. No one had buried him, so far as the records showed, or if someone had buried him, no one had written down where, and the name Edwin Thornbury appeared in no dispatch, no muster roll correction, no parish register across the whole length of that terrible winter road. He had simply been subtracted. Her mother had died still writing letters to the War Office, still receiving the same courteous non-answers, and Cyrus had gone out to Portugal as a nurse the following spring telling herself it was for the wages, and had stood in the first field hospital of her life understanding, with a clarity that has never since left her, that the difference between her brother and the men around her was nothing at all except that these men were still, for the moment, being counted.

Someone had to keep the other book. The real one. The one the guns wrote and the gentlemen declined to copy fair.

She reached the cottage, let herself in, fed the stove a knot of peat, and set the satchel on the table. Snow ticked at the shuttered window. From the direction of the great house came the sound of the Halloran dinner party — laughter, a snatch of some drawing-room song, Lord Halloran's voice raised warmly over it all, spending charm the way he spent money, which was to say generously and slightly beyond his means. Cyrus had served at enough of those dinners to know the family the way she knew the wounded: by their hands and their silences. Emrys would not be at table tonight. Emrys was avoiding his father's talk of the regimental office the way a man avoids a debt collector, and Cyrus noted this without judging it. Avoidance was a strategy she had helped invent.

She ate bread and cold mutton standing up. Then she brought the ledger to the candle, meaning to transcribe the morning's dispensary notes before sleep, and found herself instead turning backward through the pages — an occupation she allowed herself rarely, because the ledger was a working instrument and not a reliquary, and sentiment, she had long ago ruled, was a luxury for women who did not keep accounts.

But tonight the snow made her sentimental, and she turned to the earliest pages, her first crude entries from the tent at Balaclava, and read the name at the head of them all.

*Quill, Daniel, sergeant, light company. Fell 25 October, holding the ground before the fourth gun. The maneuver that saved the company was his; claimed in dispatches by Capt., later Col., V—. Widow: Martha, residing —*

Cyrus's pen had run dry that day, seven years ago. She remembered it: dipping, finding the nib bare, cutting a new point with shaking fingers while the wounded groaned around her and Colonel Vane — then Captain Vane, already smooth, already rising — dictated his glorious account to a clerk ten feet away, wearing Quill's maneuver like a stolen coat.

She had finished the entry. She had started a war of her own that night, a war fought entirely in ink, entirely alone, entirely in rooms like this one, and she had won every engagement so far, if winning meant that two hundred and eleven dead men existed somewhere besides her memory.

The candle guttered. Cyrus pressed her thumb flat against the page, over the letters *V—*, and felt the old grief and the older anger sit down together inside her like two tired soldiers sharing a fire.

Outside, far off down the park drive, she heard wheels. Odd, at this hour, in this snow. She rose and moved the shutter an inch and looked out, and saw a carriage laboring up the drive toward the great house, lamps swinging, a courier's horse ahead of it — and behind the carriage glass, briefly lit by the passing flame of a stable lantern, a face she knew.

Seven years older. Greyer at the temples. Smiling at something, or at everything.

Colonel Vane had come to Halloran Park, unannounced, in the dead of winter, and Cyrus Thornbury stood in the dark of her cottage with her thumb still resting on his initial, and understood that the quiet she had built her whole life inside of had just, without warning, ended.
