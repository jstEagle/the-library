## 2. A Letter Under Seal

The frost came early that year, and it came like a debt collector.

Cyrus Thornbury felt it in her hands before she saw it anywhere—knuckles stiffening inside her gloves as she crossed the kitchen court before dawn, breath hanging about her face in a small private cloud that the wind took and spent at once. The Halloran estate in December was a study in greys: grey sky pressed down on grey slate, grey stone walls furred with rime, the yew walk so dark it seemed cut from the air itself with shears. Only the workshop windows held colour, the low amber of lamps lit against the early dark, where wounded men slept two and three to a bed that had once been meant for finer things.

Seven years she had walked this ground. Seven years since she had come down from the transports with a crate of bandages and a brother's name sewn into her hem, and found that England did not know what to do with a woman who had learned to hold a man's jaw while he screamed. The estate had known what to do with her, or at least had not minded pretending to. Clerk to the workshop, they called her—keeper of stores, keeper of lists, keeper of the small unglamorous arithmetic of splints and laudanum and straw. It was work no one envied and therefore no one watched, and Cyrus had built her whole life upon that principle: that which is unnoticed survives.

She let herself into the workshop by the side door, the one whose hinges she had oiled herself so that no one would hear her coming or going at odd hours, and stood a moment in the warmth letting her fingers thaw. The smell of the place was winter and men: carbolic, wet wool, tallow, the sweetish undertone of wounds healing badly and wounds healing well, which were not so different to the nose as one might hope. Forty-one beds. Thirty-eight occupied now, the influenza having taken its toll among the convalescents in November, though mercifully only three to the ground, and those three named in her ledger with their regiments and their mothers' villages, because no dispatch would carry them further than *died of fever, regretted*.

Regretted by whom? she had written once, in the margin, in a hand smaller than her small hand already was, and then struck it through, because even margins could be searched.

"Miss Thornbury." Private Oakes, propped on his elbow in the third bed, his shoulder bound and his eyes too bright with the restlessness of a man healing faster than his patience. "You've a face on you this morning."

"I have the same face I have every morning, Private."

"No, miss. There's weather coming behind it."

She gave him the look she kept for such observations—the flat, mild look of a woman who has been told her fortune by dying men and does not care for the trade—and moved on down the aisle with the medicine tray, counting pulse after pulse under her fingertips the way other women counted beads. Corporal Bell, fever down. Gunner Meech, fever up, lungs rattling like a dropped coin-box. Young Whitlock asleep with his fist curled against his mouth, nineteen years old and missing two fingers and old enough now never to be young again.

It was in this rhythm, this familiar tide of small necessities, that the morning broke. And it broke, as bad mornings do, with something ordinary: the sound of hooves on gravel, and the bell at the gate.

* * *

The post came late that day, delayed by ice on the north road, and it came with one piece out of the common run. Cyrus saw it first because she was the one in the hall, returning from the dispensary cupboard with a keyring in her pocket, when Reeves the groom stamped in with the mail-bag and a look of self-importance he wore like a livery.

"Sealed, this'un," he said, holding up a letter between two fingers as though it might be hot. "For his lordship personal. Feller rode it up from the crossroads himself, wouldn't trust it to the bag."

It lay then upon the salver in the morning room for the better part of two hours, and Cyrus passed it four times in the course of her duties, and on the fourth passing she stopped.

Not because of the seal—that heavy black wax, stamped with a crest she did not recognise, some merchant concern or government office, the kind of thing that arrived twice a year and made Lord Halloran drink an extra glass of port. She had seen seals enough. What stopped her was the direction.

The hand on the face of the envelope was narrow and upright, each letter drawn with a kind of clerkly precision that nonetheless betrayed effort, as if the writer's fingers had once been surer than they were now. The ink was iron-gall, browned at the edges where it had pooled. And Cyrus knew that hand. She knew it the way one knows a voice heard across a ward at night—a voice attached to no particular face, only to a season of them.

She had seen that hand writing on hospital returns at Scutari, seven years ago, in the winter the snow came through the broken windows and froze the wash-water in its basins. A supply officer's hand, filling out requisitions in triplicate, complaining in a dry voice about the state of the ledgers. She had stood beside that man at a table strewn with forms while he copied figures, and she had looked at those figures longer than he knew, and afterward she had gone back to her cot and opened a schoolboy's exercise book and begun, in a hand she made deliberately unlike any she used in daylight, to write down what the figures said and what they left unsaid.

Colonel Vane's hand. Or a hand trained by Vane, or trained alongside him—one of that office of neat men who moved paper where other men moved guns.

Cyrus stood very still in the morning room, the keyring quiet in her pocket. The letter lay on the salver beneath the mounted stag's antlers, white as a patch of uncovered skin. She had no right to touch it. She had less right to read it. She had delivered a thousand letters in seven years without so much as tilting an envelope toward the light, because invisibility was her discipline the way drill was a soldier's, and discipline is only tested when no one is watching, which is to say always.

But the name on the front was Lord Halloran's, and the hand was Crimea's, and Crimea did not write to country gentlemen in December without cause.

She picked it up. Only to carry it, she told herself. Only to take it where it ought to go.

The study door stood open—Reeves had announced the letter, and Lord Halloran was expecting it—and Cyrus crossed the Turkey carpet with the envelope balanced on her flat palm like a dish of something volatile. Lord Halloran sat behind the desk in the grey light from the tall window, a big man going soft at the edges, his reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair. He took the letter without looking at her; she was furniture, and had spent years becoming so, and there were mornings the erasure stung and mornings it was balm.

This morning it stung, and she did not examine why.

He cracked the seal with his thumb. And here was the failure—not of discipline exactly but of luck, which is discipline's disreputable cousin. As he drew the single sheet from the envelope the light caught it, and Cyrus, standing at the correct respectful distance with her hands folded, saw the page upside down across six feet of cold air. She saw perhaps nine lines in a slanting script much hastier than the address. Most of it was nothing—a jumble of words her eye refused to hold, the way an ear refuses a language heard once. But one phrase leapt off the leaf entire, black and upright as a fence rail:

*...in settlement of the Thornbury account...*

Lord Halloran read the letter through once quickly, then again slowly, and his face did something complicated that he would not have permitted in company. His thumb pressed the edge of the desk until the nail went white. Then he looked up, and Cyrus arranged her features into their working blankness an instant before his gaze arrived.

"That will be all," he said. His voice was level, but levelness maintained is audible, like a rope held taut.

"Yes, my lord."

She closed the door softly behind her and stood in the corridor among the dark portraits of Hallorans who had never had to hold their faces still, and discovered that her heart was going like a fist against a locked door.

*The Thornbury account.*

Her surname. Her father's surname, dead these twenty years; her brother's surname, dead these seven and unnamed. She had worn it quietly all her life, a plain serviceable thing, and in seven years at Halloran no one had ever once used it back to her except the housekeeper on quarter-day, when wages were entered in the book. *Thornbury.* She had half persuaded herself the name had gone dormant in her like a seed in frozen ground.

And now it appeared in a letter in Vane's hand—or Vane's office's hand—written to Lord Halloran concerning an *account*, which was a word for money, and money was a word for everything else.

Someone remembered her name. Someone had gone looking for it, or someone had stumbled on it, and either possibility was worse than the other. Cyrus began walking—down the corridor, along the gallery, past the long windows where the frost ferns grew on the glass in patterns like drowned forests—walking without destination, which she never did, because a woman with nowhere particular to be is a woman noticed.

She thought: it is coincidence. Some army creditor chasing old debts, some quartermaster's clerk unpicking a knotted account, and *Thornbury* merely a word in the ledger, a firm, a shipper, a supplier of canvas and lint—there had been Thornburys in trade, surely, somewhere, sometime. She thought it hard, the way one shouts into wind.

Then she thought of the hand. Narrow, upright, precise, tired. Writing requisitions at Scutari while the snow came through the glass. And she remembered—she had tried for seven years to remember less, but memory keeps its own ledger and charges interest—she remembered that the same hand had signed the return on which Sergeant Quill's final manoeuvre was recorded as the initiative of a captain who had been, at the hour in question, asleep in his tent with a bottle for a pillow. The captain got a commendation. Quill got a burial pit with no marker and a widow in a Norfolk village who would wait two years for a pension that arrived owing eleven months' arrears.

Cyrus had written it all down. Not the accusation—she had no proof, only sight and certainty—but the facts: dates, quantities, signatures, the strange arithmetic of a consignment of army blankets that arrived at the hospital numbered four hundred and was entered in the regimental books as six hundred and fifty. She had written it in her disguised hand, in her hidden book, and hidden it, and told no one, because telling would have cost her position, and position was what kept her near enough to see, and seeing was all she had to set against the silence.

Now the past had addressed an envelope, sealed it in wax, and sent it riding up the icy north road to the house where she swept the crumbs of her own invisibility.

* * *

That evening the household dined early and strangely. Cyrus served the second course herself, which was not her office but which no one remarked upon, because the footman was ill and the housekeeper flustered and everyone at the long table too occupied with their own unease to notice who carried the dishes. Lord Halloran drank more than usual and spoke less. Mrs. Halloran had retired upstairs with a megrim, or with the wisdom to be elsewhere. Emrys was present, the younger son home from town, and Cyrus observed him observing his father with that particular vigilance the young reserve for the people they love and distrust in equal measure—watching the wine consumed, the silences lengthening, the letters that arrive and change the weather of a house.

Twice Lord Halloran glanced toward the sideboard where Cyrus stood, and both times his gaze snagged on her a fraction longer than it ever had in seven years, as though the letter had taught him to see her, or as though he were wondering how much she had seen.

She kept her eyes down and her hands steady and poured the claret without a tremor, and inside herself she was running through the wards at Scutari with the wind tearing the canvas and men calling out in the dark for water, for mothers, for names.

Afterward, in the scullery, with the last of the washing-up steam rising around her like a curtain, she allowed herself the indulgence of shaking. Just her hands, just briefly, gripped on the edge of the copper. Then she dried them, took up her candle, and climbed the back stair to her narrow room under the eaves, where the cold lived like a lodger.

She did not light the lamp. She sat on the bed in the dark and listened to the house settle around her, beam by beam, the way old houses talk in frost, and she turned the phrase over and over until it lost its gloss and showed its teeth.

*Settlement.* Accounts are settled when they are finished. Debts are settled when they are paid. Witnesses are settled when they are silenced, and ledgers are settled when they are burned.

Whoever wrote that letter knew the word *Thornbury* in connection with Lord Halloran's affairs. Either the writer believed there was an account between the families—which was nonsense, unless the writer knew what Cyrus knew, which would mean her secret had a crack in it she had never found—or the writer wanted Lord Halloran to believe it. Wanted him to look sideways at his plain, quiet workshop clerk and wonder what papers she kept. Wanted the name itself to do its work, moving through the house like smoke, finding the flammable places.

Or—and this thought arrived last and stayed longest—wanted *her* to know she was found. Because she had read the phrase. She was almost certain he had angled the page without meaning to, or meaning to; certain men in Vane's orbit did nothing without meaning to. Nine lines shown to a servant across six feet of carpet. A message wrapped inside a letter inside an envelope inside a seal, and the true recipient not the man in the study at all.

Fear, Cyrus had learned, was not the enemy. Fear was a sense, like sight; you did not blind yourself to be brave. The enemy was what fear made you want to do, which was always the same thing: hide, run, burn your own papers, become smaller. Seven years ago a frightened version of her had nearly fed the exercise book to the stove. She had held it over the coals and then taken it back, because the names inside it were all that remained alive of people everyone else had finished grieving.

She rose, lit the stub of candle at the banked embers, and knelt to pull the loose board at the floor's edge. The ledger lay where it always lay, wrapped in oilcloth, a schoolboy's book grown thick with pasted-in pages and marginal entries, seven winters of the erased. Her hands, steadier now, unwrapped it and turned to the pages marked Crimea, winter, the supply returns, the blankets numbered four hundred and sold as six hundred and fifty, the signature she knew.

At the bottom of the earliest relevant entry, beneath the date, she added a line in her smallest hand:

*A letter comes to this house in a hand I know. It speaks my name. Begin counting the days.*

Then she wrapped the book away again, replaced the board, scattered the dust evenly with the side of her palm, and blew out the candle. She lay in the dark listening to the frost tightening its grip on the eaves, and she thought of the workshop below with its thirty-eight sleeping men, and of the boy Whitlock with his curled fist, and of the lamplight that burned past midnight in the corner where young Mr. Emrys worked over some salvaged piece of metalwork she had glimpsed but never asked about, because asking questions was a way of being seen.

Down the corridor a clock struck two. In the morning she would be plain Miss Thornbury again, keys in her pocket, face like furniture, invisible as survival required.

But tonight the phrase lay in her chest like swallowed ice: *the Thornbury account.* Somewhere south of the frost line, in London perhaps, a narrow upright hand had dipped a pen, and her seven quiet years had begun, very softly, to come undone.
