## 3. The Workshop at Dusk

The lantern came back from the Crimea in a crate of things nobody wanted.

Emrys Halloran remembered the afternoon it arrived—October, two years past, when the estate had still smelled of harvest and his father had still spoken of the war as a noble necessity rather than a debt collector. The crate had been addressed to no one, marked only with a quartermaster's chalk: *effects, unclaimed.* Inside, packed in straw gone grey and damp, lay the ordinary debris of men who would not be coming home: a spoon bent double, a boot with the lace still knotted, three Bibles water-swollen into bricks of paper, and a field lantern crushed along one side as though a horse had stood on it, its glass panes clouded white with something Emrys chose not to think about too closely.

He had asked his father's steward if he might keep it. The steward—a careful man who measured every word against the possibility of later embarrassment—had looked at the crate and then at Emrys and said only, "Burn the rest."

Emrys did not burn the rest. He burned nothing. He carried the lantern to the workshop that cold afternoon and set it on the long bench beneath the north window, and there it had sat for two years while he learned, slowly and without instruction, how to bring dead things back to their purposes.

Now, on an evening in early December 1813, with dusk falling blue and early through the frost-ferned glass, he sat hunched over that same bench with a jeweller's loupe pressed to his eye, teasing the last stubborn fleck of corrosion from the lantern's hinge pin. The workshop around him was warm enough—the great iron stove ticked and breathed at his back—but the light was failing fast, and he worked by feel now more than sight, his fingers reading the small metal landscape the way a blind man reads a face.

The hinge pin was brass, original, and worth saving. That was the whole argument of his life at present, conducted silently and mostly against himself: that small things were worth saving. His father believed otherwise. His father had lately developed a talent for looking at the workshop—all forty feet of it, with its benches and its lathes and its racks of fine tools accumulated over three generations of Hallorans who preferred making to managing—and seeing only so much salvage.

"Regimental office," Emrys said aloud to the empty room, mimicking his father's clipped tone, the vowels flattened as though pressed under a weight. "'A man your age should be doing something useful, Emrys. Figures. Correspondence. The estate cannot run on sentiment.'" He flexed the pin between finger and thumb, testing. It was sound. He set it down on a square of chamois with the tenderness of a priest laying out a relic. "Sentiment is precisely what runs it, Father. Sentiment and debt. I've seen the figures."

He hadn't, not properly. He had seen only the edges of them: the way Lord Halloran's hand shook now when he signed things; the way the steward's lips went thin over the ledgers; the way his mother had stopped ordering new curtains for the drawing room and begun instead having the old ones turned, their faded sides folded inward like faces refusing the window. There was a language of money that families spoke in gestures rather than sums, and Emrys had become fluent in it against his will.

His father wanted him in the regimental office because his elder brother, Geoffrey, was in the regiment itself, somewhere in the Peninsula, writing letters that arrived at widening intervals and said less each time. And because Colonel Vane was expected.

Emrys's jaw tightened at the thought. Vane had dined here before—twice, in the years since the war—and both times Emrys had watched the colonel move through his father's house like a man taking inventory with his eyes while his mouth praised the port. There was something in Vane's manner of attention that Emrys distrusted instinctively: he listened the way a magistrate listens, kindly and completely, and you told him everything, and afterward you could not remember having decided to.

*He'll want the workshop,* Emrys thought, returning the loupe to his eye. *He'll look at all this iron and glass and see what it would fetch.*

The lamp on the bench guttered. Emrys trimmed the wick, and in the steadied light the lantern's body gave up another of its secrets: a line of engraving along the base plate, nearly effaced by corrosion, filled with black grime that no solvent would touch. He had been working at it for a week with a needle and patience. Tonight the letters came clear at last under the loupe, one after another, like a name being spoken very slowly:

**Q-U-I-L-L**

He sat back.

Quill. Sergeant Quill—he had found the surname months ago in the same crate, scratched inside a Bible's warped cover alongside a date Emrys could not read. A sergeant, then. Some sergeant's lantern, carried through whatever Balaclava had been, surviving whatever Balaclava had done, shipped home in a box of effects with a bent spoon and knotted bootlaces because there had been no one to give it to.

*No one,* Emrys thought. Or someone who didn't know it existed. Or someone who knew and couldn't afford the passage of grief across an ocean of paperwork.

His breath fogged faintly in the cooling air. The stove needed feeding. He ignored it. He was looking at the four letters—QUILL, ground deep by some loving or furious hand, perhaps the sergeant's own, perhaps a comrade's—and feeling the peculiar vertigo that came over him sometimes in this room, the sense that the objects he restored were not objects at all but held breath. Everything in this workshop had once mattered to somebody. That was the entire secret of the place, and the entire reason his father's new pragmatism felt to Emrys like a species of blasphemy.

He took up his burnishing tool and began, very gently, to clean the recessed letters, working the grime out grain by grain so that he might fill the engraving with dark wax and make the name permanent again. The work steadied him, as it always did. Outside, the last light drained from the sky and the frost began its nightly campaign against the windowpanes, building its silent white architecture in the corners of the glass.

It was full dark when he noticed the other light.

* * *

There were only two buildings between the house and the home farm that still showed windows after ten o'clock: the stable, where the head groom sat up with a mare in foal, and the converted workshop block at the estate's eastern end, where the estate's clerks kept accounts by day. Emrys knew this the way he knew the geography of his own restlessness—which was to say, precisely, and from many hours of observation through sleepless windows.

For the better part of a year, he had noticed that one particular window on the upper floor of the workshop block burned late. Not every night. Often enough. And always the same window: second from the left, the one belonging to the little alcove room where the estate's records clerk worked days amid her ledgers and her files.

Miss Thornbury. Cyrus Thornbury. Even her name was a kind of contradiction—Cyrus, a man's name, given he'd overheard her explain once to a puzzled housemaid, because her father had wanted a son and got her instead, and had the grace or the grief to love her anyway.

She frightened him slightly, Miss Thornbury. Not in any way he could have defended. She was small, quiet, exact; she had come to the estate some years ago with a recommendation that mentioned nursing in the army hospitals and said nothing else about anything; and she possessed the unnerving faculty of seeming to know things she could not possibly have been told. Once, when Emrys had been convalescing from a fever, she had appeared at his door with willow bark tea and the information—not a question, never a question—that his brother's last letter was three weeks late and that silence from the Peninsula usually meant nothing at all. She had been right, of course. Geoffrey's letter had come the following week. But Emrys had never asked how she knew to comfort him precisely then, and she had never offered the mechanism, and between those two silences a certain careful distance had established itself, courteous and load-bearing, like a wall holding up a roof.

He saw her rarely and knew her barely, and yet he thought of her lamplight as the estate's other heartbeat. When he woke at three in the morning with the particular dread that had been visiting him lately—nameless, financial, atmospheric, the dread of a household living beyond its means in every sense—he would sometimes look toward the eastern block, and if that window glowed he felt less alone in his wakefulness. Someone else was up. Someone else declined to sleep either.

Tonight the light was burning. He saw it when he crossed the yard for coal for the stove, the bucket handle biting cold into his fingers, his breath pluming. Second window from the left, upper floor, burning steady behind its curtain of cheap calico. Eleven o'clock, near enough. What clerk kept accounts at eleven o'clock at night? None that his father paid, surely; the estate's arithmetic was done by five and the clerks gone home to the village hours since.

Unless she wasn't doing the estate's arithmetic.

The thought arrived uninvited and stayed. Emrys stood in the yard with his coal bucket and considered the window, and the window considered him back, steady and yellow and unrevealing. He thought: *she writes letters at night.* He thought: *she reads.* He thought, with a small cold slide of sensation he did not immediately identify and then did: *she watches this house the way I watch it. From the edges. Counting.*

What a person counted at midnight, in a clerk's alcove, in a household sliding quietly toward ruin, in the same season a colonel prepared to visit and a father prepared to sell the workshop—well. There were answers and answers.

He went back inside. He fed the stove. He did not go to bed, because going to bed meant lying in the dark listening to the house settle and hearing in every creak the sound of money leaving. Instead he returned to the bench and worked another hour on the lantern, cleaning the last of the grime from Quill's name, and when he finally snuffed his lamp and shrugged into his coat, the yard clock was striking one.

He paused in the doorway, key in the lock, and looked east.

Still burning.

On impulse—he would blame the hour later, the strange honesty of one o'clock in the morning—Emrys crossed the frosted yard instead of turning toward the house. His boots crunched. The cold had that particular stillness it achieved only well past midnight, a held-breath quality, as though the whole landscape were listening. Smoke curled thin from the workshop block's chimney; she kept a fire too, then. He climbed the outer stair to the upper floor—it was a workman's stair, iron-railed, built for clerks hauling boxes—and stopped at the door with light under it.

For a long moment he simply stood there, feeling foolish. What would he say? *I couldn't help noticing we are both awake, Miss Thornbury, and I wondered if you were also afraid, and if so, of what?* One did not say such things. One especially did not say such things to a woman one had exchanged perhaps four hundred words with in two years.

But under his feet, through the door, he could hear the small industrious sounds of another person alone: the scratch of a nib, the turn of a page, once the soft scrape of a chair. Ordinary sounds. Companionable sounds. Sounds that made the night feel, briefly, less like a country he was the sole inhabitant of.

He raised his hand to knock—

—and stopped, because through the door, in the pause between page-turns, he heard her voice. Low. Not speaking to anyone; speaking to herself, or to the page, in the murmuring half-audible way of a person reading aloud names from a list. He caught only fragments through the oak: "...Harper, private..." ...a pause, the scratch of the pen... "...fourth of October..." Another pause. Another name.

Emrys lowered his hand.

She wasn't doing accounts. Accounts had numbers; this had names and dates, recited like a litany, like a roll call of some regiment he had never heard of. And there was something in her voice as she spoke them—something he recognized without wanting to, because he heard it in his own voice when he talked to the broken lanterns and the crushed instruments and the water-swollen Bibles. Reverence, maybe. Or grief being made useful.

*She's keeping a muster,* he thought, stepping back from the door. *Of whom?*

He retreated down the iron stair more quietly than he'd come up, his mind moving fast now, fitting things together with the same care he brought to a seized hinge: a nurse from the army hospitals; a woman who knew when letters were late; a light that burned past midnight; a litany of names and dates receded into the dark. His father wanted him counting figures in the regimental office. Perhaps the figures had already been counted—by someone paid almost nothing, lodged above the workshop, working nights, writing down what the officers' dispatches left out.

At the bottom of the stair he stopped and looked up once at her window, gold against the black.

Then he turned for the house, and behind him the light burned on, and neither of them slept, and the frost came down on the estate of Halloran like a slow white sentence being passed.

In the morning, he would learn, a letter had already arrived under seal for his father—had arrived, in fact, that very evening, and lay waiting on the hall table in the dark, its wax unbroken, its address written in a hand that would make a certain quiet clerk go pale when she saw it.

But that was morning. Tonight there was only the yard, the frost, the two lights—one dying as he reached his own door, one burning on above the workshop—and the name QUILL, which he would spend the next weeks bringing back out of the dark, one grain at a time.
