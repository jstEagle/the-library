## 5. Vane Comes to Dinner

The invitation arrived on Tuesday and the colonel on Friday, which gave my father three days to rehearse his hospitality and me three days to dread it.

I heard the carriage before I saw it — not the light rattle of a farmer's trap but the deliberate, weighted roll of a well-sprung traveling coach, four horses at a pace just slow enough to be admired from the windows. By the time it came up the lime avenue the frost had hardened the ruts to iron, and the wheels rang against them like something being struck in warning. I stood at the workshop door with solder smoke still on my sleeves and watched it approach, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt almost like prophecy: *this is not a visit. This is an inspection.*

Colonel Ambrose Vane stepped down onto our gravel in a coat of blue so dark it drank the afternoon light. He was perhaps fifty, built long and spare as a churchyard yew, with silver at his temples arranged — I am certain this was arrangement — in two precise wings above his ears. His face was the kind that photographs poorly in memory because nothing about it commits. He smiled before he spoke, and he spoke before my father reached him, and somehow by the time they shook hands the colonel had already made the gravel, the frost, the house, and my father himself seem pleased to receive him.

"Lord Halloran. You are kindness itself to have me." The voice was warm, low, unhurried. "The country air here — do you smell it? Even winter smells of *order* in a place like this."

My father laughed. My father, who had spent November snapping at the footmen and December not sleeping, actually laughed, and the sound came out of him like a man remembering a language he'd learned young and half forgotten.

"Colonel. You flatter the mud."

"I never flatter mud, my lord. Mud is honest. It is only men who make things dishonest by describing them." And then, turning, extending one gloved hand toward me without looking away from my father's face, "And this will be the younger son. Mr. Halloran. They tell me you have a talent for mechanisms."

"They tell me nothing," I said, "because no one asks me anything."

The smile deepened by a fraction of an inch. "Then we shall remedy that. I ask questions professionally."

* * *

Dinner was laid for seven o'clock in the small dining room, the one with the Fenland landscape my grandfather bought to prove he could afford better landscapes than he deserved. My mother had died the year before Balaclava; there was no mistress to sit opposite, so the table was arranged with my father at the head, the colonel at his right hand, myself at the foot, and Vane's aide-de-camp — a smooth, red-haired captain named Pryce who said perhaps forty words all evening and remembered every one — filling the fourth chair.

I noticed Cyrus before the first course was carried in.

She did not serve us herself; that was not her place anymore. Seven years ago she had been hands and basins and bandages, and now she was ledgers and stores and the workshop's quiet accounts, a clerk's position my father had given her when she came back from the war with a limp she had never explained and references no one had ever questioned. But she appeared in the doorway twice during the meal, once to speak to Mrs. Beddoe about the wine and once to bring a fresh decanter herself when the girl carrying it fumbled on the threshold — and both times I found myself watching her because watching Cyrus was like watching water find its level. Nothing wasted. Nothing visible.

It was the second time that struck me. She crossed the room through a conversation about army provisioning — the very subject, as it happened, around which the whole evening was quietly rotating — and set the decanter beside the colonel's elbow with such perfect economy that no one paused their talk. Her face was not blank exactly. Blankness has a texture, a kind of held emptiness, like a room with the furniture taken out. Hers was furnished. There was attention in it, and patience, and something underneath both that I might have called watchfulness if I had been a man given to reading faces, which until that winter I had not been.

She looked at Colonel Vane the way I look at a mechanism that hums wrong but runs anyway.

* * *

The colonel began gently. That was the artistry of it. He talked first of roads, then of the fen drainage commission, then of a mutual acquaintance whose gout had driven him to Bath, and only when the soup had gone and the turbot had come did he lay his knife and fork down parallel on his plate — a small, tidy gesture, like a man clearing a desk — and say:

"I find myself lately much occupied with the settling of accounts, Lord Halloran. Old ones. The War Office has developed, in its dotage, a passion for tidiness. Commissariat papers from the late campaign are being reviewed. Reconciled. Names matched to requisitions, requisitions to sums." He took up a spoonful of the white soup my cook had over-sweetened and consumed it without appearing to notice it at all. "One hears extraordinary things. Sums that walked off. Goods invoiced twice. Men paid who never existed." A mild pause, the length of a held breath. "And sums that were paid out honestly, by honest men, and later — misunderstood."

My father's hand had stopped halfway to his glass.

"You interest yourself in bookkeeping now, Colonel?" His voice was easy. His knuckles were not. I had grown up watching those hands sign checks and deeds and the occasional furious letter, and I knew the difference between his ease and his ease-when-cornered the way one knows a dog's tail from its hackles.

"A hobby that became a profession," Vane said pleasantly. "The war taught me that battles are won by quartermasters, my lord, whatever the gazettes may say. And that wars are *lost* the same way. Paper kills more reputations than shot does." He turned the full mild weight of his attention across the table to me. "Does your son keep your books, Lord Halloran?"

"My son keeps lanterns," my father said, too quickly.

"How useful a trade." Vane did not smile this time; he performed a smile's architecture without inhabiting it. "Light, Mr. Halloran, is a great revealer. A candle in the wrong window has undone more conspiracies than all the constables in England. You would know, I imagine — you work with glass and flame. What does one see through good clear glass?"

"Whatever is behind it," I said.

"Just so." He raised his glass a half-inch toward me, a toast to the correctness of the answer. "Just so."

Captain Pryce wrote something in a pocketbook. I could not swear to it. I saw his hand move near his breast and afterward the pocketbook was in his hand rather than his pocket, and that was enough.

* * *

Between the roast and the sweet my father made his one error of the evening, and I watched it happen the way one watches a cart wheel begin to sink into soft ground — slowly, obviously, and with no power at all to stop it.

Vane had drifted, with terrible casualness, back to the commissariat. "There was a convoy, I recall — the autumn of 'twelve, or perhaps the spring after — grain and tentage out of a depot I shall not name, drawn under papers signed by a civilian agent of some standing. A gentleman of property. The kind of man who lends his name to things because his name is worth lending." He buttered a piece of bread he did not eat. "The trail of that paperwork passes, my lord, through some very respectable drawing rooms. Through this county. One might almost say — through estates like this one."

"Then whoever drew those papers should answer for them," my father said, and his voice cracked on the last word like ice giving way underfoot, and in the silence after it every candle flame in the room seemed to lean.

He recovered. He was capable, my father, of recoveries that would have done a stage actor credit. He laughed, waved his hand, spoke of the wretched business being seven years cold, of clerks who misfile and auditors who invent sins to justify their salaries. But the crack had been heard. Vane heard it the way I imagine a physician hears the wrong sound in a chest — not with alarm, but with confirmation.

"There is nothing colder than old paper, my lord," the colonel said, gentle as a confessor. "Until someone warms it up."

* * *

They went through to the drawing room for barely half an hour — my father pleading an early night with a fluency that fooled nobody — and when the colonel's coach pulled away into the dark I went out through the side door into the yard, ostensibly to bank the workshop stove, actually to breathe air that had not been poured through a sieve of courtesy.

The night was hard and still, stars in their winter numbers, the lime avenue a black corridor of frost. From the drive I could hear the coach's wheels diminishing, unhurried. He had nowhere to rush to. Men who intend to return do not rush away.

When I turned back toward the house, the windows were going out one by one as the household closed down for the night — the drawing room, the hall, the dining room where the footmen were clearing. But one window on the ground floor still burned steady: the estate office, where a single lamp sat, and behind it, bent over papers, the unmistakable silhouette of Cyrus Thornbury.

I stood in the cold longer than I meant to, watching her shadow move across the ledger pages she kept — everyone knew she kept them, though no one had ever been asked to see them; she had that kind of authority, the kind that comes from being overlooked so thoroughly and so long that overlooking her had become policy. She worked the way she served. No motion wasted. No motion visible.

What did she see tonight?

That was the question that stayed with me while the yard frost climbed my boots and the stove ticked itself cold behind me. She had stood within arm's reach of Ambrose Vane with a decanter in her hands and a face like still water, and I had seen — or thought I had seen — the moment the decanter passed the colonel's shoulder, the smallest hesitation, a quarter-beat of stillness in her wrists, as if the name on her tongue had tasted something.

She had known him. Or known *of* him. Or known something the rest of us were only beginning to feel the shape of, the way cattle feel weather coming across the fen before the sky shows it.

I came back inside. The corridor to my rooms passed the estate office door, and against all sense — I had no plan, no pretext, nothing but the restlessness the whole evening had poured into me — I slowed as I came level with it. The lamp still burned. Through the gap where the door had failed to meet its frame, I could see her: pen moving, page turning, the light making a small gold country of the desktop while the rest of the room fell away into dark.

She was writing fast. Faster than accounting requires.

And then she stopped — abruptly, mid-line — and lifted her head, and looked directly at the gap in the door, directly at me, though the passage behind me was unlit and I could not have been visible unless she had heard my boot on the boards and known precisely where the sound placed me.

Her expression did not change. That was the unsettling part. Most people, caught, show a flicker — guilt, apology, the reflexive mask of innocence. She showed nothing at all, only that furnished stillness, and then she inclined her head a fraction, the way one acknowledges a passerby on a road, and returned to her page.

As if I were no more remarkable than frost.

I lay awake most of that night listening to the house settle — beams contracting, a door somewhere breathing shut — and turning over the evening like a coin I could not read. *Sums that walked off. Papers signed by a gentleman of property. Estates like this one.* The colonel had not accused us. Accusation is blunt, and Vane was not a blunt instrument; he was a fine blade laid flat along the throat, warm from his own hand, patient. He would return. Men who intend to return do not rush away.

Near dawn I rose and lit the workshop lamp and took up the Crimean lantern again, the one I had been restoring through all these bad weeks — Quill's name faint under the grime of years on its brass collar — and worked at it with hands that needed occupation. Somewhere past midnight I realized the lamp oil was low, and that I had been thinking, the entire time, not of solder seams but of a decanter passing a colonel's shoulder and the quarter-second of stillness in a serving woman's wrists.

In the morning, Mrs. Beddoe told me over breakfast that Miss Thornbury had asked whether the colonel would be staying the night, and whether his coach had turned toward the London road or the coast road.

No reason for her to ask. Clerks do not need to know which road inspectors take home.

Unless they are counting how long they have.
