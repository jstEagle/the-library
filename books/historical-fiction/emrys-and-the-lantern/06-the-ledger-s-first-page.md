## 6. The Ledger's First Page

The house slept, but the workshop did not.

Cyrus had learned long ago that a candle carried through the servants' passages at half past one in the morning drew no more attention than a mouse behind the wainscoting—less, if the mouse was careful. She was always careful. Seven years of carefulness had worn a groove in her, smooth as the channel a river cuts through limestone, and she moved along it now without thinking: past the stillroom where the fire had been banked to embers, past the linen store with its smell of starch and lavender and cold stone, up the narrow stair that creaked only on the fourth step if you put your weight on the left side of the tread.

Her room at the top of the east wing was barely furnished—a bed, a washstand, a chair, a chest. The window gave onto the frozen kitchen garden, where the bean poles stood like a congregation of thin black ghosts waiting for a sermon. Frost had made ferns on the inside of the glass. She did not light her candle until the shutters were closed; she never forgot, not once in seven years, though there had been no one to see for most of them.

The ledger lived under the loose floorboard beside the chest, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. She knelt, lifted the board—its edge had rubbed a small bald place in the floor's wax that she could find blind—and drew the book out into her lap like something breathing.

It had begun as nothing. That was the strange thing, the thing she could never explain to anyone who had not stood where she had stood: the ledger had begun as nothing at all, as a convenience, a habit, the way a woman might keep count of stitches. A name written down so it would not be forgotten by accident. Then another. Then the forgetting itself became visible to her, became a shape, a country with borders, and she understood that she was keeping not a list but a map—the map of everyone the army had decided not to see.

She turned to the first page.

The paper had gone soft at the corners, tea-coloured with age and handling. Her hand was younger there, quicker, less certain of its own authority. The ink had faded from iron-brown toward the colour of dried blood, which was either an irony or a truth depending on the hour, and this hour—it was nearly two now, and the frost-ferns on the shutter seams let through threads of moonlight—this hour it felt like truth.

*Thomas Quill, sergeant, 33rd Foot. Died 19th October, before Sebastopol. His manoeuvre at the redoubt saved two guns and cost him his life. Captain —* and here the young Cyrus had crossed out a name, pressed so hard the nib had torn the paper, *— reported the action as his own in dispatch. No pension issued to widow. Widow: Martha Quill, Brompton Lane, near Aldershot.*

She had not read that page in years. She did not need to; it lived behind her eyes the way a burn lives in skin. But tonight she read it slowly, word by word, because the sealed letter had cracked something open in her and she wanted—I need to know when it began, she thought. I need to know how far back the rot goes.

And there it was. Not on the first page. On the third.

She had documented supply transactions the way other women noted recipes—not from ambition but because numbers were the language of the war's true story, and nobody else was listening. *Received at Balaklava, 22nd October: 40 tents returned on the books as serviceable. Condition upon inspection by nursing staff: rotten, unlivable, four men sheltered under them subsequently fevered.* And beneath that, in her smaller, later hand—the hand of a woman who had learned that names were currency and omissions were purchases:

*Requisition signed: Col. H. Vane.*

She sat very still.

The frost ticked somewhere in the window frame. Below, in the depths of the house, a door settled against its frame with a sigh, the ordinary sound of an ordinary sleeping house, and Cyrus did not hear it, because she had gone somewhere else entirely—to a tent that smelled of iodine and wet wool, to a table where a tall officer with a voice like warmed wine had signed papers without reading them, his glove resting on the stack, saying to his adjutant, *the tents will do, the tents will do, we are not housing ladies*—and she had been folding bandages six feet away, invisible in her grey dress and her grey silence, writing nothing down because she owned nothing to write with and had not yet thought to steal both pencil and privacy.

But she had remembered. Memory was the first ledger. Paper came after.

She turned pages with fingers that had gone strangely cold. October bled into November. November into the winter of the siege. And there—second entry, the one about the hospital lime, ordered short and billed in full, *requisition approved Col. H. Vane*—and there again, in February, the shipment of quinine that arrived as chalk dust and glucose, forty pounds of medicine on the manifest and forty pounds of nothing in the crates, *inspected and passed: H. Vane.* Three transactions. Three separate seasons of theft, each one small enough to drown in the general ocean of wartime waste, each one documented by a woman whom no officer ever once looked at directly while she wrote.

Three entries, threaded through seven years of pages like wire through cloth.

She had never connected them before. That was the terrible admission, and she made it to herself honestly, kneeling on the cold boards with the book open across her knees: she had recorded them singly, as she had recorded everything singly—one death, one fraud, one omission at a time—because the whole shape of it would have been unbearable, and because she had not known then what she knew now, which was that Colonel Henry Vane had come to dine at Halloran Hall eleven days ago, and had smiled at her across the soup, and had said, *I believe we may have met, Mrs. Thornbury. Crimea has a way of scattering its people.*

He had recognized her. He had looked at her plain grey dress and her plain grey face and he had seen—not a nurse. A witness.

* * *

She did not remember lighting the second candle. It stood guttering beside her on the floor, and the first had drowned in its own wax, and time had come apart at the seams the way it did sometimes, hours folding like linen. Her knees ached. The frost on the shutters had grown while she read, thickening, the glass going milky, and the room was bitter as a grave.

Think, she told herself, in the flat nurse's tone she used on herself when panic presented its symptoms. Diagnose. Do not weep over the wound until you have cleaned it.

Facts.

Fact the first: the sealed letter, delivered Tuesday, addressed to Lord Halloran in a hand she had almost recognized—that slanted, hurried script, the descenders bitten off short, a clerk's hand trained to speed and disguise at once. She had glimpsed one line as she carried the thing on its silver salver, one phrase only, caught sideways like a fish beneath the ice: *the Thornbury account.*

For five days she had believed the letter named her. Five days she had walked the corridors with her ears ringing, waiting for the summons, the constable, the end of everything she had built out of silence. She had examined her own record for crimes—there were none, none but the ledger itself, which in the wrong hands was not evidence but confession, proof that a civilian woman had kept unauthorized accounts of army property, army personnel, army shame. Forgery, they would call it. Falsification. There were men hanging in the fleet prison for less than what her pages contained, though what her pages contained was only the truth, and the truth had never yet been a defence in front of a tribunal that preferred comfort.

Fact the second: Vane had dined. Vane had asked, gently, charmingly, about the family's wartime finances—about the contracts Lord Halloran had brokered, the commissariat arrangements, the loans. Emrys had watched her watching, she knew; she had felt his gaze on her like sun through glass, heat without touch. The boy noticed things. That would matter later or it would not.

Fact the third—and here she made herself look at it straight, because a nurse who flinched from a wound made it worse—fact the third was that the letter's phrase had not frightened her because it accused her. It had frightened her because it *named* her. *The Thornbury account.* Not an accusation at all, perhaps. An inventory item. Something listed. Something being located.

Bait.

She said the word aloud, and it fogged in the freezing air and vanished, and she hated how small her voice sounded, and she said it again, louder, because fear shrinks in a loud room.

"Bait."

Consider it as she would consider a case of fever. A man falls ill three days after eating at a certain table. The foolish physician blames the last meal. The wise one asks where else the man has eaten for a fortnight. So: the letter arrives at Halloran Hall accusing the family of conspiracy in an officer's embezzlement scheme—an accusation that, if pressed, would ruin the Hallorans, seize the estate, scatter every servant Cyrus had spent seven years protecting and recording. Who profits? Whoever holds the counter-evidence. Whoever can appear, at the crisis, as the family's saviour—or whoever can use the threat of ruin to purchase silence, signatures, favourable testimony. Whoever wants the estate's records opened, inventoried, and then burned under the respectable cover of settling debts.

Whoever is already inside the sheepfold, dining on roast pheasant and asking after the accounts.

She had watched Vane sign away medicine for chalk and shelter for fever. She had watched him take a dead sergeant's manoeuvre and wear it like a medal. She had written all of it down, alone, in secret, believing herself invisible—and she had made one error, the single error of her long careful career, and the error was this: she had assumed that invisibility was a property of herself rather than a gift granted by the indifference of others. Vane had dined with officers for thirty years. Vane knew precisely what a quiet woman with a memory was worth, and precisely how to price her.

*The Thornbury account.* He had not needed to find her ledger. He had needed only to make her believe he might, and watch her run, and follow her running to whatever she treasured enough to protect.

Or worse—he had needed her to run to Lord Halloran, to confess, to place herself in the family's hands, so that when the accusation broke she would be discovered inside the household like a spy in a besieged city, and every page of her ledger would become evidence not of Vane's thefts but of the Hallorans' desperation to manufacture innocence. Her book, her beautiful terrible book, turned against the innocent. Her seven years of small names sharpened into a blade held at the throat of the only house that had ever given her a bed and a wage and the dignity of being unnoticed.

She closed the ledger.

Her hands were shaking now, properly, the fine tremor of exhaustion and cold, and she sat on the floor of her narrow room and let them shake, because there was no one to see, and that was the whole of her life in a sentence: there was no one to see.

* * *

Dawn found her still at the floorboard, but dressed, composed, the ledger wrapped and rehidden and the floorboard's bald patch dusted with wax shavings to hide the rubbing. She had spent the last dark hours doing what she had always done, the only thing that had never failed her: she had worked.

Three pages copied, in a hand deliberately altered—she could write four different hands, a legacy of copying letters for illiterate soldiers dying faster than their pens could learn—and those copies folded into the lining of her winter cloak, because a book burns but a seam must be picked. If Vane wanted the ledger, he should have it, eventually, in pieces, in places he did not know to search. The rest she would deal with as dawn dealt with night: not all at once, but edge by edge.

She went down through the waking house with her tray and her blank face. Scullery fires being lit. The smell of blacking and bread. In the entrance hall the post had been laid out, and she paused—not pausing, merely walking slowly past, the way an invisible woman walks past everything—and saw among the bills a letter in the bitten-off, slanted hand.

Addressed to her.

*Mrs. C. Thornbury, Workshop Clerk, Halloran Hall.*

No seal broken. Black wax, unmarked. She stood holding it and heard, distantly, the kitchenmaid singing to the kettle, and the frost letting go of the windowpanes in slow ticking drops, and her own heart, which had gone very quiet and very fast, like a creature cornered that has decided not to scream.

So. The bait had not been the first letter at all. The first letter had been the net thrown wide over the Hallorans. This—this was the hook, drawn in close, set gently for the fish.

She did not open it in the hall. She did not open it in her room, where the floorboard knew her secrets. She carried it down through the yard, breath smoking, past the workshop where a lamp still burned in the deep window—Emrys, working again at some hour no reasonable man kept, and she felt the pull of that lit window like warmth from a door left open in winter, and resented the feeling, and did not examine it—and out beyond the icehouse to the bare field where the beech hedge ran, and there, with the hedge at her back and no window facing her, she broke the black wax with her thumb.

One line, in the clerk's bitten hand:

*You are remembered. Bring the account you keep, or it will be brought for you.*

No signature. No address. Only, pressed faintly into the paper below the words where the pen had rested a moment too long, the ghost impression of something written on the sheet above it in the original draft—she tilted the page to the low sun and read the shadow of a phrase, half-formed, half-struck through by whoever had drafted and redrafted this elegant little threat:

*—as Quill was rememb—*

Cyrus stood in the frozen field with the paper trembling in her hands, and the fear that had walked beside her for five days changed its clothing entirely. This was no longer about her safety. This was Vane reaching back across seven years to the very first entry in her book, the dead sergeant, the widow in Brompton Lane—telling her, in the politest possible terms, that he knew what she knew, knew she knew it, and had begun, already, to tidy the living witnesses.

Martha Quill. Brompton Lane. A day's travel, no more.

Cyrus put the letter inside her bodice, next to her heart where the paper warmed against her skin, and turned back toward the house, walking fast, an invisible woman moving at the speed of someone who has finally understood that she is seen.
