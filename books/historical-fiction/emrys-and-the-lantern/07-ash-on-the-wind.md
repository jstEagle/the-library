## 7. Ash on the Wind

The smell woke him before the bells did.

Emrys Halloran had fallen asleep over his workbench again, cheek against the cool grain of an unfinished lantern frame, and for a moment he hung between dreams and waking with the scent of smoke in his nostrils and no name for it. Woodsmoke was ordinary. Every hearth in the house breathed it through December. But this was different—sharper, greasier, carrying a sweetness beneath it like burnt sugar and scorched hair—and by the time his mind assembled the word *wrong*, his body was already moving, boots half-laced, coat flung over bare shoulders, out into a night that had turned the colour of iron.

The eastern sky glowed.

Not dawn. It was past two o'clock; he knew it from the case clock in the corridor, whose ticking he had counted himself to sleep against. The glow sat low along the roofline of the old record wing, where his grandfather's papers and the estate accounts slept in rows of cedar cabinets that smelled of dust and camphor and forty years of rain. The glow pulsed once, twice, like something breathing under the shingles, and then a tongue of flame uncurled from the guttering as if it had been waiting all this time merely for someone to watch.

Emrys ran.

The gravel took his ankles at every stride. Behind him he heard doors opening, voices unraveling from sleep—the cook's high cry, the gardener's dog going mad at its chain—and ahead of him, above him, the fire found its voice: a dry roar, then a crack like a felled oak, then glass giving way somewhere in the dark of the wing with a sound almost gentle, almost musical.

*The ledgers.*

He could not have said why that thought arrived first, ahead of even the servants, ahead of any calculation about the house itself. But it was there, urgent and cold in his chest: the record room held everything his father would not speak of—the wartime supply contracts, the treasury correspondence, seven years of figures in Lord Halloran's cramped and careful hand. Figures that Colonel Vane had asked after so politely over the dinner table, turning his wineglass by its stem, saying *how curious*, saying *these sums seem larger than the regimental returns suggest*. Figures that were, as of this burning night, feeding themselves to the wind.

* * *

They fought it for three hours and lost.

There was never truly a chance of saving the wing; by the time the bucket chains formed, the fire had gotten into the joists and was travelling overhead like something alive, dropping embers behind them so that men fighting flames at their backs turned to find new fires already lit where they had stood. Emrys hauled water until his palms split and his shoulders burned worse than his lungs, until the frost on the flagstones melted and refroze into a slick that put old Hobbs the gardener on his hip, gasping. Twice he went inside—once for the silver, because his father shouted for the silver, and once, on his own impulse, for nothing he could afterwards explain—and came out coughing black, eyes streaming, the heat pressing against him like a living weight.

Dawn came grey and ashamed over the wreckage. The east wing was a shell, its windows blind with soot, snow falling softly into the burned-out rafters and hissing where it landed. Half the roof was gone. The other half sagged like a tired shoulder. And yet the fire had stopped, almost politely, at the line of the old party wall, as though it had been instructed to take only what it was given and touch nothing more.

That was the thought Emrys could not shake, standing ankle-deep in slush while the household milled around him grey-faced and silent. Fires were greedy things. He had seen a hayrick burn in '08 and watched the flames leap a country lane to take the opposite hedge. This fire had been *tidy*. It had begun in the one room of the whole estate where paper lived, and it had eaten precisely that, and it had died at the wall like a servant reaching the end of his errand.

His father did not say any of this. Lord Halloran stood before the smoking ruin with his hands clasped behind his back in the attitude he used at gravesides, very straight, very pale, and said only, "Thank God it did not reach the house. Thank God no one is hurt." And then, after a silence in which the snow ticked down through the broken rafters: "See to it the workshop is cleared today. All of it. There's paper enough lying about that place to feed another fire."

"The workshop, Father?" Emrys kept his voice level with an effort. "What has the workshop to do with—"

"Useless paper." His father's mouth made the words as though they tasted of ash. "Old drawings, patterns, correspondence nobody wants. I want it out. Burned or carted off, I don't care which, but gone." He turned away, and Emrys saw—for only a moment, only in profile—that his father's hands, clasped tight behind him, were shaking.

* * *

By noon the yard below the workshop windows had filled with crates and the smell of wet char still drifting from the east wing. Emrys watched from the lantern bench, ostensibly sorting brass fittings, actually watching the servants carry out armfuls of his father's history. There was more of it than he had guessed. Drawing rolls tied with faded ribbon. Boxes of invoices going back to his grandfather's day. A crate of letters stamped with the War Office seal that made his stomach tighten when he glimpsed the direction labels—*Halloran & Sons, Provisioners*—the family firm in its brief, profitable, shadowed prime.

Seven years ago, when he had still been at school and the war was still feeding itself, Halloran & Sons had supplied the Crimea. His father had grown grey over those ledgers; his mother had grown quiet. Emrys had understood none of it then. He understood perhaps a little more now—not the arithmetic, but the shape of it, the way a man's posture changes when he carries something heavy in a place no one can see.

"Mr. Emrys?"

It was Mrs. Prewitt the housekeeper, wind-reddened, holding out a small box with both hands as if it were hot. "Begging your pardon, sir, but Mr. Hobbs pulled this from under the stair in the east passage, where the fire didn't get. Your father said anything salvaged was to go to you, being as you've got the key to the workshop store."

Emrys set down his fittings and looked at the box. It was cedar, small, iron-cornered, heavier than its size suggested. The lid was scored with a long dark smear where something burning had brushed across it.

"Did he say that? My father said it was to come to me?"

"He said—" Mrs. Prewitt frowned, reconstructing. "He said, *anything with the Halloran mark goes to Mr. Emrys, and nothing else comes indoors at all.* That's the whole of it, sir."

"Then this has the mark?"

She turned the box so he could see the underside, and there, branded into the wood in letters softened by decades of handling: *H&S — 1811*.

He carried it into the lantern workshop and set it on the bench among the brass and glass, and for a long while he did not open it. Outside, the work of clearing went on. He heard crates thump onto cart beds, heard Hobbs grumble, heard the scrape of the great doors. Through the frosted window he watched a man he did not recognize—a broad fellow in a labourer's smock who had not been hired for any work Emrys knew of—stand at the edge of the yard doing nothing at all, simply watching the house, his breath rising in slow unhurried plumes. When Emrys shifted to see him better, the man pushed off the fence post and walked away toward the lodgings road without hurry, like someone whose errand had been accomplished.

Emrys opened the box.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that crackled with age, lay bundles of correspondence and two slim ledgers, and beneath them a leather wallet containing what he at first took for receipts but which proved, when he smoothed them on the bench under the good north light, to be something else entirely: duplicates. Supply dockets, each in duplicate, each bearing two signatures—one his father's, unmistakable even in the smallest sample, and one belonging to a hand he did not know, angular, confident, signed *R. Vane*.

Colonel Vane's name, on provisioning papers dated 1810, 1811, 1812, for quantities of hardtack, fodder, and medical stores that made no sense beside the numbers written in the margins—numbers crossed out, corrected, corrected again, as though someone had argued with the figures and lost.

Emrys sat down slowly on the stool. The workshop was very quiet. Frost ferned the corners of the window glass; the restored Crimean lantern hung above him on its chain, dark, patient, its engraved plate catching what little light there was. He thought of Vane at the dinner table, turning his wineglass, saying *how curious*. He thought of his father's shaking hands behind his back. He thought of the fire stopping at the party wall like a servant at a threshold.

And he thought—suddenly, unwelcome, complete—of Cyrus Thornbury, and her lamp burning past midnight in the corner of the converted ward, and the way she moved through the house like a woman reading a page no one else could see.

* * *

He hid the box badly at first, and then well.

His first instinct was the floor cavity under the drying rack, which every boy of the household had used for contraband tobacco and stolen apples, but he abandoned it within the hour, imagining his father—or worse, the broad-shouldered stranger—kneeling in the dust with a candle. In the end he took the cedar box apart in his mind the way he would take apart a mechanism: where would no one ever look? Not where things were hidden, but where things were *kept*. Hidden places invited searching. Kept places did not.

So he emptied a crate of genuine junk—cracked panes, a bent hinge, three years of failed wick-dippings—wrote *GLASS & SCRAP, TO GO* in chalk on its side, and buried the oilcloth bundles at the bottom beneath the broken glass, where any searcher's fingers would learn caution quickly. Then he stacked the crate in the corner behind the grinding bench, under the shelf of unfinished frames, in plain sight of anyone who entered, labelled as rubbish, and left it.

It was not courage. He examined the feeling carefully while he worked, because it seemed important to know what one was dealing with. It was not the thrill of conspiracy; his heart was too heavy for thrills. It was nearer to the sensation of catching a child about to step into a frozen pond—you did not weigh whether you were loyal to the child or to the ice. Something in this house was drowning, had been drowning for years, and everyone had agreed not to look at the water.

His father included. Perhaps especially his father. Emrys turned the thought over and it cut him coming and going: that Lord Halloran might have ordered the workshop cleared not out of grief or tidiness but out of fear—that "useless paper" was a man naming, aloud, the thing he could not afford to let exist. And that the fire last night might not have been sent against the Hallorans at all, but *for* them—sent to consume what might otherwise damn them, by someone who considered that a kindness, or an investment.

If Vane had done it, then Vane was not merely asking questions anymore. Questions had become matches.

* * *

The afternoon wore down towards a sunset the colour of a healing bruise. The clearing was finished; carts rolled out the gate with their loads of ribbon-tied drawing rolls, and Emrys signed the manifest with a hand that wanted very much not to be his. Mrs. Prewitt brought tea to the workshop and found him still at the bench, pretending to fit glass into a frame he had no intention of finishing.

"There's talk below stairs, Mr. Emrys," she said, folding her hands. "About last night. Cook says she saw a man on the east lawn before the flames showed—before, mind, an hour before—and Hobbs says he heard a cart on the back lane at midnight, though the gate log shows nothing. And the stable lad swears he saw sparks fall upward." She said it flatly, the way servants report the impossible: they had learned that impossible things, reported plainly, were safest.

"What does my father say to all this?"

"Your lordship's father says it was defective flues and a dry season." Her mouth pressed thin. "In December, sir. With the ground soaked and the cedar green as grass."

"And what do you say, Mrs. Prewitt?"

She looked out the frosted window at the black skeleton of the east wing, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I say that whoever wrote the ledger of this house had best keep it close, sir, because the counting is not done." Then she straightened, resumed her housekeeper's face, and took the tray away.

*Ledger.* The word rang in him strangely, like a bell struck in another room.

He waited until full dark, until the household settled and only the wind moved, and then he took a candle and went down the servants' corridor to the converted ward, telling himself he was checking on the convalescent stove, which had a temper in cold weather. The stove was fine. Its iron sides ticked gently. And beyond it, in the small alcove that served as the nursing office, the lamp was burning—past eleven now, as it had burned the night before, and the night before that—throwing a bar of warm gold up the whitewashed wall.

Cyrus Thornbury sat with her back to him, a thin upright figure in grey, her pen moving in small steady strokes across a page. She did not startle when he spoke; perhaps she had heard his tread from the stair and identified it, the way she seemed to identify everything.

"You should be asleep, Mr. Halloran," she said, without turning. "You fought a fire today. Or it fought you."

"I could ask the same. You keep hours no nurse is paid for."

"My hours are my own affair." She capped the inkwell with a click that closed the conversation like a door, and laid a blotter over the page with the smoothness of long habit—but not before he had seen, in the instant the lamp caught it, not figures but *names*. Columns of names, some struck through, some starred, dates marching down the margin in that same small steady hand.

He should have left it there. Every instinct of his upbringing told him to leave it there: one did not look under servants' blotters, one did not see what the invisible kept. But the cedar box sat twenty feet behind him in the workshop, holding Vane's signature in triplicate, and his father's hands had shaken in the snow, and somewhere out on the lodgings road a broad-shouldered man was sleeping soundly after a job well done.

"Cyrus," he said—and it was the first time he had ever used her name aloud rather than her title, and they both felt it land. "Last night, before the fire, did you see anyone on the east lawn?"

For a long moment she regarded him across the lamplight, and her face did what it always did: arranged itself into perfect blankness, the face of a woman who had learned that expression was armour. But her eyes stayed on his a heartbeat too long, and in that heartbeat he saw the other thing underneath—the same cold alertness he had felt in his own chest since dawn, the animal knowledge of being hunted.

"Yes," she said quietly. "I did. And I think, Mr. Halloran, that we had better talk—not here, and not tonight. Tomorrow, after the lamps are out, in the lantern workshop. Bring whatever it is you've hidden behind the grinding bench."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere above them the wind took a loose slate and worried it, and Emrys understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, that he had never once been the only person in this house keeping watch in the dark.

"I'll bring the box," he said.

"No," said Cyrus Thornbury, and for the first time since he had known her, she almost smiled—an expression like light moving under ice. "Bring nothing. Just yourself. What I have to show you will make sense of yours."
