## 8. An Alliance of Insomniacs

The frost came early that November, silvering the workshop windows from the inside where breath collected, and Emrys Halloran found he could no longer sleep in his own bed. He told himself it was the cold, that the fire in his room drew poorly, that the wind off the fen had a new note in it like a finger run round the rim of a glass. But the truth was simpler and less comfortable: since the fire in the record room—since the smell of scorched vellum that had seeped into the corridors and would not wash out—he woke at odd hours with his heart going like a fist on a door, certain the house was burning again.

Three nights after the fire, he gave up pretending. He pulled on his coat over his nightshirt, took a candle, and went down through the sleeping house, past the record room with its blackened door and its smell of wet ash, out across the yard where the frost crunched underfoot like sugar, toward the workshop.

He meant only to sit with the lantern. The Crimean field piece had become his obsession—the small brass lamp engraved with Sergeant Quill's name, its glass panes clouded, its wick mechanism seized with seven years of rust. Working on it steadied him. There was an order to restoration that life did not offer: you could see what was broken, and you could mend it, or learn why you could not.

But when he rounded the corner of the workshop, he stopped in the shadow of the wall.

There was light inside. Not his bench lamp, which he always trimmed low and shaded. A proper light—a lantern's steady gold—burning behind the far partition where the estate clerks kept the household accounts. And beneath it, unmistakable even through the frosted pane, someone sat bent over papers.

He knew who it would be before he reasoned it out. Cyrus Thornbury. The nurse-clerk who moved through Halloran House like a draft of air, who appeared at elbows with laudanum or ledgers and vanished before thanks could settle on anyone's tongue. His father called her indispensable. The steward called her punctual to the minute. No one, as far as Emrys knew, had ever once described what she looked like when she thought herself unobserved.

She was unobserved now—or believed so—and through the glass he watched her for a long moment without meaning to. She sat very straight, her dark hair pinned carelessly, strands coming loose at her temples, and she wrote with a quick, economical hand, pausing now and then to hold a page close to the flame as though checking it against something invisible. On the table beside her lay documents he recognized: the surviving ledgers from the record room, the scorched ones, the ones his father had ordered cleared out with the rest of the *useless paper*. Someone had carried them here instead. Someone had decided they were not useless.

His first thought was duty: tell the steward, tell his father. His second thought arrived hard on the first's heels and overtook it: *my father ordered these burned.* And his third thought, arriving quietest of all but staying longest, was that he himself had rescued a box of papers from that same room and hidden it not twenty feet from where this woman now sat, in the cavity behind the lantern bench, and that he had felt, doing it, more honest than he had felt in years.

So instead of going to the house door and raising the household, Emrys Halloran lifted the latch and walked in out of the cold.

* * *

Cyrus heard the latch and did not startle—that was the training of Crimea in her, the reflex of a woman who had learned that visible fear invited wolves—but her pen stopped mid-stroke, leaving a small ink blot like a period at the end of nothing. She knew the footsteps. She had catalogued every footstep in that house the way she catalogued names: Emrys Halloran walked heel-first, deliberate, a man who wanted to be heard by doors and not surprised behind them.

She closed the ledger she was copying from and folded her hands on top of it, and when he came round the partition she looked up at him with the smooth blankness she wore for Colonel Vane, for dinner guests, for the world.

"Mr. Halloran," she said. "You'll want a shawl. This end of the building holds the damp."

"You're burning my father's oil," he said, "and copying documents my father ordered destroyed."

"Yes."

It was, she would think later, the first time in seven years she had answered a direct question from a Halloran without softening it into something deniable. The word simply came out, plain as a nail driven flush. Perhaps it was the hour. Perhaps it was the smell of ash still on his coat, or the fact that he stood there in his nightshirt and greatcoat looking less like the master's son than like some boy caught out of bed, and yet his eyes were older than his face, and they were not accusing her. They were asking.

Emrys pulled the second stool from under the bench, set it down across the table from her, and sat.

"You've been at my box," he said.

"I don't know what you mean."

"The box behind the lantern bench. I put it there myself, ten days ago. It's been moved—not much, an inch, but I notice inches." He nodded at the scorched ledgers. "You found it. You've been reading what I saved."

Cyrus considered denial, weighed it, and set it aside the way she set aside a blunt instrument—useful elsewhere, not here. "I found it the morning after you hid it," she said. "You crossed the yard in your dressing gown with a box under your arm and you looked over your shoulder twice. You look over your shoulder like a man who has never done it before. Anyone watching would have known."

"Did you watch?"

"Someone should," she said, "since you did it so badly."

For a moment neither spoke. The lantern between them hissed faintly, its restored wick giving a cleaner light than any lamp in the main house. Emrys found himself staring at the flame because it was easier than deciding how to feel about being seen—truly seen—for perhaps the first time since he'd come home from buying his commission and hating every day of it.

"What is it you're copying?" he asked.

She turned the ledger she had been working from so he could read it. He expected household accounts, and at first glance that was what it resembled—columns, dates, sums. Then he saw the names running down the margins in her tight hand, and saw that the columns were not sums at all but entries: *requisitioned, delivered, signed for*, and beside several signatures small annotations. *Not his hand. Clerk's habit of looping the F. Cross-checked against pay book.* Another: *Goods signed for 14th; wagon recorded as returned 9th. Five days unaccounted.*

"These are army records," he said slowly. "From Crimea."

"Some of them. Some from the regimental office here, which is the same animal in a warmer stable." She kept her voice level, but her thumb moved along the ledger's edge, a small protective gesture she did not seem aware of. "Your father ordered all paper cleared from the record room. Most of it will be burned with the autumn rubbish, as instructed. Some of it I have borrowed."

"Borrowed," Emrys repeated. "And the annotations?"

"Are mine."

"Seven years' worth?" He tipped his head toward the shelf where, among the estate ledgers, a row of plain bound volumes stood with numbered spines—volumes he had taken for inventories his whole adult life.

Cyrus said nothing, which was answer enough.

* * *

He should have been angry, he supposed later. A servant—no, not quite a servant, something between clerk and ghost—had been secretly compiling a private archive of his family's affairs, and by rights he ought to have demanded it, locked it away, questioned her. Instead what Emrys felt, sitting in the damp cold of the workshop at two in the morning with frost flowering up the windowpanes, was something closer to relief, and that frightened him more than discovery ever could.

Because he had been alone with it. Since the fire, since Vane's dinner, since his father had grown brittle and strange and started locking the study door—a thing no Halloran had ever done—Emrys had been carrying the knowledge alone that something was rotten and that everyone else in the house had agreed, silently, collectively, to walk past it with their eyes forward. His mother went to Bath and stayed there. His sister wrote cheerful letters. His father drank two glasses of port instead of one and answered questions before they were finished. And Emrys lay awake listening to the wind and thinking: *either I am mad, or I am the only one awake in a burning house.*

Now here was another soul awake.

"Why?" he asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "Why do it? Seven years of—what is this, exactly? Names, signatures, wagons that went missing. Who are you writing it for? No one will ever read it."

"I read it," Cyrus said.

"That isn't an answer."

"It is the only true one I have tonight, Mr. Halloran." She rose, stiff from hours on the stool, and went to the small stove at the wall, feeding it a knot of kindling with hands that moved on their own knowledge. He watched her back while she worked. When she spoke again she did not turn around. "You have brothers-in-arms. Men who slept beside you and would take a bullet meant for you. You know what that is worth."

"I never saw action," he said. "I was commissioned late. By the time I sailed it was over."

"Mm." She struck a match; sulfur flared; the stove caught with a soft whump. "Then let me describe it differently. Imagine a man dies for a maneuver he devised himself, and afterward a captain writes the dispatch and puts the maneuver in his own mouth. Imagine the widow receives a letter praising the captain's brilliance, and her husband's name is not in the letter at all. Not in the dispatch. Not in the casualty list correctly rendered—one clerk's error, one name transposed, and no one corrects it because correcting it inconveniences the promotion board." She closed the stove door and straightened. "Imagine it happening not once but everywhere, constantly, because paper is written by the winners and the dead cannot complain. That is who I write for. Not for readers. For the men who cannot."

"Sergeant Quill," Emrys said quietly.

Her eyes came to him sharply, the blankness gone entirely, and what lay underneath was raw and old. "Who told you that name?"

"No one. It's engraved on the lantern." He gestured to the workbench, where the brass field piece sat half-restored, its glass panes laid out like extracted teeth. "*J. Quill, Sgt., 17th.* I bought it from a peddler at the Michaelmas fair. Or he sold it to me—I never quite worked out which of us was more determined. I've been restoring it. I don't know why. It seemed like it mattered."

For a long moment Cyrus looked at the lantern, and Emrys watched something move across her face like weather—grief, recognition, and beneath them both a wariness that did not fade so much as choose, deliberately, to stand down.

"He held the battery road at a place nobody at home has ever heard of," she said. "He'd been a sergeant eleven years. He had a wife named Martha and a way of whistling through his teeth when the shells came in that made the younger men calmer, God knows how. And when he died, Captain—now Colonel—Vane wrote home describing *his own* dispositions, his own coolness, his own genius, and Quill appeared nowhere in three pages except as 'losses were light.'" Her voice stayed even throughout, which somehow made it worse. "I was holding the man's hand when he died, Mr. Halloran. He asked me to write to Martha. He asked me to make sure the letter got there. And I stood in the tent afterward and listened to Vane dictate that dispatch, and I understood that if I left it to the army, Martha Quill would live and die without knowing her husband had been anything at all."

"So you began writing it down."

"At first only his name. Then others. Then—" she made a small gesture at the shelves, the numbered spines, seven years of it, "—it became a habit, and then a discipline, and then the only prayer I know."

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind had dropped, and the silence of frozen ground pressed against the windows. Emrys became suddenly, acutely conscious that he was sitting in his nightshirt talking to a woman whose name he had known for seven years and whose face he realized he had never once properly looked at until tonight.

"There was a letter," he said. "A month ago. Sealed, for my father. You carried it in. I saw your face when you handed it over. You went white as whey and then made yourself ordinary again inside a breath. What was in it?"

"Why do you ask?"

"Because my father has been afraid ever since. Because Colonel Vane dined with us and smiled at everyone and asked questions about our finances that no guest asks. Because the record room burned ten days later, and fires do not happen in rooms where nothing is feared." He leaned forward. "Miss Thornbury. Cyrus. I am lying awake every night in that house feeling the ground tilt, and I am so tired of being the only person awake. Tell me what you know, or tell me to go to bed. One or the other. But stop deciding for me what I'm permitted to see. I've had seven years of that from my own family."

* * *

Cyrus studied him across the lantern light, and what she felt was not trust—it was too early for trust, and trust had gotten people killed before—but recognition. That was more dangerous, in its way. Recognition was how conspiracies began: not with oaths, but with two exhausted people seeing their own sleeplessness mirrored in another face and thinking, *ah, at last, a witness.*

She thought about Vane's name threaded through those old supply transactions. She thought about the phrase glimpsed under the seal—*the Thornbury account*—and the cold that had walked her spine ever since. She thought about Lord Halloran locking his study door, about the fire that had burned hottest precisely where the oldest records lay, about a hired man's silhouette she had seen once at dusk near the stables, walking with a soldier's roll of the shoulders.

If Vane was moving against the Hallorans, then this man—this tired, decent, badly-dressed man in his nightshirt—was already in the flood's path whether he understood it or not. Protecting him by silence had worked for seven years. Silence had also, she was beginning to suspect, arranged the very flood.

"If I tell you," she said, "you cannot unknow it. And you cannot tell your father. Do you understand me? Whatever you decide after, you must not carry it to Lord Halloran. Not yet. Perhaps not ever."

"That's a strange condition."

"It's the only safe one. Your father may be innocent, or frightened, or foolish, or bought—I don't yet know which, and until I do, everything he learns, Vane learns. Fear talks, Mr. Halloran. Fear talks in its sleep." She held his eyes. "Can you keep a thing from your own father? Think before you answer. It is a harder vow than most marriages."

Emrys thought of the box he had already hidden without being asked. Of the study door he had noticed locked and said nothing. Of the slow, sick certainty that his father's new brittleness was guilt wearing a waistcoat.

"Yes," he said. "I can."

So she told him. Not everything—she kept the ledger itself out of his reach and kept the phrase *the Thornbury account* to herself like a stone swallowed—but enough. Vane's visit. The probing questions dressed as reminiscence. The fire, and its convenient placement. The letter in a handwriting she knew from field hospitals, a clerk's hand, and the way Lord Halloran had aged a year in the reading of one page.

When she finished, Emrys sat with his elbows on his knees and his head bowed, and for a while he said nothing at all, and Cyrus found the silence companionable, which alarmed her more than anything else that night.

"The box I hid," he said finally. "It's correspondence mostly. My father's letters from 1811 and 1812, some bills of exchange, a promissory note I didn't understand. I hid it because it felt wrong to let it burn, not because I understood any of it." He looked up. "I don't suppose you'd understand it either."

"I'd like to try," Cyrus said.

"And if we find something in it that damns my family?"

"Then we find it," she said. "Better us than Vane."

He nodded slowly, and then, absurdly, yawned—a great unguarded yawn that cracked his composure open like ice in spring—and had the grace to look embarrassed. "Forgive me. It's past three."

"Go to bed, Mr. Halloran."

"Will you be long?"

"I'm always long," she said. "It's the whole of my method."

He rose, hesitated at the edge of the lamplight like a man reluctant to leave a lit window for a dark road, and then said, not quite meeting her eye: "Tomorrow night, if you're here again—I could bring the box. We could go through it together. Two pairs of eyes. Mine at least are rested."

It was not an oath. It was barely an arrangement. But Cyrus heard in it the thing she had not heard in seven years: someone offering to stand watch beside her.

"Bring candles," she said. "And knock. I startle badly at latches."

He almost smiled—the first smile she had seen on a Halloran in months—and then he went out into the blue-black cold, his candle bobbing away across the yard toward the sleeping house. Cyrus sat alone with the lantern and the ledgers and the tick of the stove, and she found that her hand, when she took up the pen again, was not entirely steady.

She copied one more entry before snuffing the light. In the morning, she would go to the village—to Martha Quill, who had sent word last week, timid as a bird, that a colonel had come asking questions about *the Thornbury woman.*

Whatever was coming, it had already reached past her to the people she had tried hardest to protect.
