## 10. Orders to Clear the Grounds

The frost came early that November, and with it the smell of coal smoke and cut peat that meant winter had settled over the estate like a guest who intended to stay. Emrys Halloran felt the season in his hands first—the lantern workshop kept its own climate, colder than the house by half, and the fine work he did there demanded fingers warmed twice an hour over the brazier before they could be trusted with solder.

He was warming them now, watching the flame in the little iron grate the way other men watched cards or horses, when he heard the gravel crunch of his father's boots on the path outside.

That was wrong in itself. Lord Halloran did not come to the workshop. In the four years since Emrys had claimed the disused coach-house as his own, his father had entered it exactly once, pronounced it *a peculiar use of a gentleman's son's time*, and never returned. But boots were boots, and the crunch stopped at the door, and the door opened on a figure Emrys took a full three seconds to recognize, because his father stood stooped these days, as though the house's troubles had been hung about his neck like yokes of wet wood.

"Your mother said I'd find you here." His father's voice had the flat carefulness of a man reading from papers he hadn't chosen. "Dress for dinner tomorrow. We have a guest."

"We have guests every week."

"This one stays."

Emrys set down the soldering iron. On the bench before him lay the Crimean lantern, its brass frame half-disassembled, each piece laid out on white flannel like bones prepared for burial. Colonel Vane's visit two nights past still sat in his memory uneasily—a dinner of probing questions dressed as compliments, Cyrus moving through the room with her face wiped clean as slate, his father's laugh growing shorter as the evening went on.

"The colonel," Emrys said. It wasn't a question.

"The colonel has raised certain concerns about the estate's finances. Concerns I intend to address." His father's eyes went past him, around the workshop—the racks of glass, the drawers of fittings, the half-restored lamps waiting like patients. "In fact, I intend to address more than concerns. Come to the morning room at ten. There is something the whole household should hear."

* * *

The morning room at Halloran Park had been designed to flatter its occupants. Its windows faced south, catching light even in the dead months; its walls were paneled in honey-colored oak that made every voice sound warmer and wiser than it was. Emrys had always suspected his grandfather had chosen the room for negotiations precisely because no one could say anything harsh in it and mean it.

At ten o'clock the next morning it held nearly everyone: his mother upright in her chair with her sewing untouched in her lap; the steward Mr. Prewitt with his ledger open and his face carefully empty; the housekeeper Mrs. Saxon; a cluster of senior servants standing along the wall with the particular stillness of people who have learned that announcements in this room are rarely good. And near the back, half in shadow where the window-light failed, Cyrus Thornbury, in her grey dress, her hands folded, her expression the same smooth blank she wore at every gathering—and her eyes, Emrys noticed, not blank at all. Her eyes moved from his father's face to the papers in his hand to the window, once, as if checking the distance to it, and then returned to perfect repose.

His father stood by the mantel. He did not look at anyone in particular while he spoke, which Emrys had learned long ago was how the man delivered news he was ashamed of.

"You will all be aware," Lord Halloran began, "that the war years placed strains upon this estate, as upon every house in the county. You will also be aware that we have done our best to meet those strains honorably." A pause, arranged like furniture. "Colonel Vane, who honors us with his presence again this evening, has been good enough to review our accounts and advise upon economies. His counsel has been... clarifying. It has become clear to me that certain outbuildings and their contents represent a liability we can no longer responsibly carry."

He looked down at the paper, and Emrys watched his father's jaw tighten before the words came.

"The workshop—the old coach-house—is to be demolished. Beginning after the thaw, in early spring. The grounds are to be cleared, and the contents disposed of. By fire, where disposal by sale is impracticable." The paper made a small sound in his grip, like a bird shifting. "Wages for those employed there will be honored through February. That is all."

Silence took the room the way cold water takes a lung.

Mrs. Saxon's hand found her apron. Mr. Prewitt closed his ledger very softly, as though the noise might break something. Along the wall, one of the grooms—a young man Emrys knew mostly as a whistling presence at the stable door—looked at the floor as though the boards had spoken his dismissal personally.

And Cyrus Thornbury did not move at all. But Emrys, watching her because some instinct he didn't yet understand made him watch her, saw her thumb press once against her own wrist, hard, beneath the fold of her sleeve. A small motion. The kind of motion a nurse makes to keep herself steady during a bad night.

His father left the room without meeting anyone's eyes. His mother followed a moment later with her chin high and her knuckles white around nothing.

* * *

Emrys found Prewitt afterward in the steward's office, a low room smelling of ink and apple stored too long, and shut the door behind him.

"Tell me it isn't true."

"It's true." Prewitt didn't look up from the column of figures he was ruling. He was sixty, dry as September grass, and had served two generations of Hallorans with the loyalty of a man who had decided long ago that families were like weather—one endured them. "Papers were signed Tuesday. Vane brought a surveyor with him. They walked the grounds for an hour."

"A surveyor. For a coach-house?"

"For the coach-house, the tack room, the records annex, and the lean-to." Prewitt ruled another line. "They measured the record room twice."

Something turned over in Emrys's chest—not yet fear, but its cousin. "Why would a man measure a room twice unless—"

"Mr. Emrys." Prewitt set down his rule. For the first time he looked up, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "I keep the accounts of this estate. It is my work and I am not ashamed of it. But I will tell you what your father will not. The debts are real. They are also not so large as all this. A house could settle them by selling the Yorkshire land, or the London lease, or half a dozen other things that would pain no one. Instead we are burning buildings." He picked his rule back up. "When a man burns a thing, Mr. Emrys, ask yourself what was in it."

* * *

Emrys spent the afternoon in the workshop and got almost nothing done.

The lantern lay in its pieces on the flannel, Quill's name etched inside the base plate—*SERGT. J. QUILL, 93RD*—and Emrys looked at it and thought about fires. Not the tidy spring bonfire his father had announced, all order and permission, but the other fire, six weeks ago, the one that had climbed the east wall of the records annex in the dark hours and died only because the wind turned. Officially, an overheated flue. Unofficially, nobody official had ever said anything at all. Scorched ledgers had been carried out in baskets, and his father had ordered the workshop cleared of *useless paper* the following day, and Emrys had rescued one box and hidden it behind the glass racks where he was looking at it now.

A box of shipping manifests. Supply requisitions. Dull papers, dry as sand—and yet someone had wanted them ash badly enough to come at night with pitch and a ladder.

He thought about Vane walking the grounds, measuring the record room twice. He thought about his father's paper trembling in his hand. He thought about the phrase Emrys had heard through a door he should not have been near, three nights ago, his father's voice low and Vane's voice smoother still: *the accounts will speak for themselves, my lord, once the accounts are all that remain.*

Once the accounts are all that remain.

He stood very still in the middle of his workshop, among the lamps and the glass and the smell of flux and cold brass, and let the thought finish forming, ugly and simple as a knot coming undone.

It was not economy. Economy sold things; it did not burn them. It was not even shame, though his father had shame enough to spare. It was erasure. Somewhere in those buildings—in the manifests, in the requisitions, in the duplicates and triplicates that quartermasters' clerks had copied out in trip-wire handwriting seven years ago—was the arithmetic of what had actually happened to the Halloran money. Where it had gone. Through whose hands. Into whose pocket. Papers that could hang a man, or save one, depending on who was holding them when the magistrate asked his questions.

And the sealed letter—that accusation naming the family conspirators in an officer's embezzlement—sat in his mind now like a card turning over. He had assumed it was an attack. What if it was bait? What if the accusation mattered less than the response it provoked—driving his father into Vane's arms, driving the estate toward demolition, driving everything flammable toward the flame?

Vane was not clearing debts. Vane was clearing ground. And the clock, which had been ticking all along in a language Emrys couldn't read, had just become legible: spring. The thaw. Ten weeks, perhaps twelve, and then the walls came down and the smoke went up and everything anyone might ever have known would be smoke too.

* * *

He did not tell his father any of this. There was no version of telling that did not require first asking the question underneath it—*Father, what did you sign, and to whom do you owe it?*—and he was not sure the household would survive his father's answer. So instead he did what Halloran men apparently did: he carried it alone, and worked with his hands, and told himself that industry was a form of courage.

He lasted until midnight.

Then he banked the brazier, pulled his coat on, and went out into the frozen dark to look at what he was supposed to let burn.

The grounds at night were another country. Frost furred the yew hedges silver; his breath went up in flags; the gravel had gone stiff and rang oddly underfoot. He walked the long axis of the yard, past the tack room with its shuttered windows, past the records annex with its new scorch shadow still printed faintly on the eastern stone like a bruise healing yellow. He put his hand flat against the annex door. Locked, of course, but locks were arguments, not walls.

Inside—somewhere in there—were seven years of paper. Requisitions his father had signed without reading. Counter-signatures in a hand Emrys did not know. Deliveries recorded and deliveries unrecorded. If you knew how to read them—and someone in this house knew how to read them better than anyone, someone whose lamplight he had watched burn past midnight through the servants' wing window for two years now—they would tell a story. Perhaps the story that would save his family. Perhaps the story that would end it. He was honest enough, standing in the cold with his hand on the latch, to admit he no longer knew which he feared more.

A light moved in the upper window of the servants' stair.

Small. Steady. Candle-flame colored, in a house that had gone dark everywhere else an hour ago. It crossed, paused, crossed back—the rhythm of a person pacing a small room, or sitting at a small table, doing small patient work that could not wait for morning.

Cyrus Thornbury. Awake again, at the hour when the honest world slept, in the week the axe had been announced.

Emrys stood in the frost with the scorched annex at his shoulder and the lit window before him, and understood, with the plainness of a key turning, that whatever was coming for his house in the spring had already begun arriving through that window, page by page, night by night—and that if he wanted to know what the fire was meant to destroy, he would have to stop watching her lamp from across the yard, and knock.

He started toward the door before he could think better of it.
