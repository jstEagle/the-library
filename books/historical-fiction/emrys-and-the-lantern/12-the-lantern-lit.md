## 12. The Lantern Lit

The workshop after midnight was a country with its own laws. Emrys Halloran had learned them the way one learns a foreign coast—by wreck and repetition—and by now he moved through the dark between benches and lathes without a candle, reading the room by memory alone: the ridge of the planing bench under his knuckles, the iron mouth of the forge gone cold, the great saw blade hanging on its peg like a moon that had given up shining.

He lit no lamp at first. He sat instead on his father's old stool at the far bench and let the dark settle around him, because in the dark he could pretend the house behind him was only a house, and not a thing being quietly taken apart by men who smiled while they did it.

Three days since the fire in the record room. Two days since his father had stood in the yard with his coat collar up against the wind and ordered the workshop cleared of useless paper, his voice pitched carefully flat, the voice of a man who has learned that loud grief attracts vultures. One day since Emrys had carried a box of scorched ledgers out through the frost-fog and hidden them beneath the floor of the lantern cabinet, where no one would look, because no one thought them worth looking for.

*Useless paper.* His father's words turned over in him like a stone in a boot.

He struck a match at last and touched it to the wick of the small oil lamp he kept for close work, and the light made its little island on the bench, and inside that island lay the lantern.

It was an ugly thing, honestly considered. A field piece, army pattern, built to be carried and dropped and carried again—tinned sheet iron gone dull as pewter, a hinged door dented along one edge where something worse than a drop had found it, wire handle mended once long ago with a clumsy soldier's splice. It had come to the workshop in a crate of effects purchased at auction from a decommissioned depot, one item among forty, and any other man would have melted it down or let it rust. But Emrys had lifted it out of the straw and seen, punched into the base plate in letters small enough to miss, a name.

QUILL. SGT. J. And beneath it, in a second hand, thinner, hurried: *Balaklava, Oct. 25.*

He had spent three weeks of evenings on it now, and tonight—if his hands held and the solder behaved—it would be finished. He did not fully understand why it mattered. That was a lie he permitted himself; he understood perfectly well. It mattered because everything else in his life had become a matter of ledgers and debts and his father's grey face at dinner, and here was one object in which repair was still possible, in which a broken thing could be made whole by patience alone, and no colonel's signature required.

He began with the glass.

The original panes had been horn, cracked and clouded; he had cut new ones from a sheet of window glass requisitioned—no, *borrowed*, he corrected himself wryly, borrowed indefinitely—from the glazier's offcut pile. Four panes, each a little imperfect, each holding a faint green tint at the edges like ice over water. He set them into their channels with putty worked soft between his fingers until it took the warmth of his skin. The cold of the workshop had stiffened his hands; he blew on them, flexed them, began again.

Outside, the wind came up from the fen, moving through the bare elms with a sound like a crowd very far away remembering something. Somewhere in the house a floorboard ticked as timber contracted. His mother had been dead eleven years, but there were still nights when the house sounded like her walking through it, and he had stopped telling himself this was foolish and simply let it be true.

* * *

The soldering went badly, then well, then badly again, which was the usual weather of it. He re-tinned the seam along the roof cap, clamping the pieces with a hand-vice padded in leather, feeding the iron into the joint and watching the silver run like something alive seeking the lowest ground. The smell of flux and hot metal rose around him—the smell of every good evening of his life, if he were honest, more honest than gunpowder or church incense or the perfume of any woman he'd ever been introduced to.

His mind, as it always did during the long middle hours of work, wandered where it should not.

It went first to Colonel Vane at his father's table, all teeth and ease, asking questions shaped like compliments. *And how does the estate manage, these days? Such difficult times for men of property.* Emrys had watched Cyrus Thornbury pour the wine that night—watched her the way you watch a heron at the water's edge, aware of some stillness in her that was not emptiness but aim. She had served Vane his soup with a blankness so complete it was itself a kind of statement, though what it stated Emrys could not have said. Her eyes never rested anywhere longer than courtesy demanded. Except once. Once, when Vane had laughed at some joke about army accounts, her gaze had flicked to his face and stayed there a half-second too long, and Emrys, catching it, had felt the temperature of the room change the way it changes before snow.

Then his mind went to the lamplight. Because Cyrus's window burned past midnight—had burned past midnight for weeks now—and Emrys, himself a creature of the small hours, had begun to keep a kind of unofficial watch on it, the way sailors keep a light on an unfriendly coast. He told himself he was merely curious. He told himself a clerk burning oil was no business of his. But there was a box of papers hidden under his own floor that said otherwise, and there was a fire in the record room that said otherwise, and there was the phrase he'd heard his father mutter over the sealed letter—*the Thornbury account*—which Emrys had carried around for days now like a splinter working toward the surface.

Thornbury. Cyrus Thornbury. The account. Whose account? Accounted how, and to whom?

He wiped the solder seam with a rag and inspected it by lamplight, turning the little roof cap this way and that, hunting flaws. Sound. He set it aside and took up the base plate, and there was the name again, and he found himself doing what he did every night now: pressing his thumb into the punched letters as though he could read the man through the metal.

Sergeant J. Quill. He had asked the regimental agent in town about Quills and been met with shrugs; the muster rolls for that autumn were in London, and London was a swamp he did not care to enter just yet. So Quill remained a shape without features—a man who had carried this lamp, whose hands had polished these dents smooth or made them, who had perhaps scratched the date himself with a knife point the day of the great cavalry action everyone talked about and nobody explained. Balaklava, Oct. 25. A date that meant thunder and blunder and six hundred rides into the mouths of guns, according to the newspapers. What it meant to the man who owned this lantern, Emrys could not know. But he suspected the newspapers had left things out. Newspapers always left things out. Dispatches left things out. Officers, he was beginning to suspect, left out entire regiments of human beings when it suited the shape of the story they wished to tell.

He thought of Cyrus's ledger—he had seen it only once, glimpsed, a fat book bound in grey cloth that she kept close as a prayer book—and wondered what names filled it. He wondered whether Quill was in it.

* * *

Toward two o'clock the frost reached in under the door and laid its silver breath along the flagstones, and Emrys rose to warm his hands at the banked forge, raking the coals idly, and on impulse shoveled a little life back into them. Not to work by—only to see by, a low red glow, enough to take the ache from his fingers. Fire was a servant here, contained in iron, obedient to bellows and shovel. It occurred to him, standing in that glow, that somewhere in the dark a mile away the record room still smelled of the other kind of fire, the disobedient kind, and that his father had begun sleeping with the key to his study under his pillow like a man in a besieged city.

*They will burn it all,* he thought, with the calm of a man stating a fact he has been avoiding for weeks. *Whatever they are after, they will burn it all rather than let it be read.*

And the workshop—his workshop, the only room in England that had ever felt designed for him—sat directly in the path of that intention, full of shavings and oil and paper patterns, flammable as a hayrick, and guarded by nothing but the assumption that no one would bother.

He went back to the bench.

The last task was the burner. The original wick-tube had crushed flat; he had fabricated a new one from brass tube, reamed to fit, and tonight he fitted it, seated the burner plate, trimmed a fresh wick from cotton tape, and filled the reservoir with the good camphene he kept for fine illumination—not the rank tallow-smoke of common lamps but a clean bright flame, white-hearted. Then he sat a moment with his hand resting on the tin body, the way you rest a hand on a horse's neck before mounting.

"All right," he said aloud, to the lantern, to Quill, to the empty room. "Let's see if we've done you justice."

He opened the little hinged door. He touched the match to the wick.

The flame took, guttered, steadied. Light bloomed inside the four panes—warm at the core, clearer at the edges, throwing the shadow of the wire frame across the ceiling in a geometry like a window seen in a dream. The dent in the door caught the light and held it, a bright crescent, a scar made ornamental. And the name—when Emrys tilted the lamp to look—the name at the base caught fire in its punched hollows, QUILL, SGT. J., legible as it had not been in years, legible as it would be to anyone who bent to look.

Emrys sat back on the stool and looked at it for a long time.

It was such a small thing. That was what undid him, a little: the smallness. All that effort—all those evenings of flux and file-work and cut fingers—for the sake of making visible the name of a dead man on an object worth perhaps two shillings. No magistrate would thank him. No newspaper would print it. If Colonel Vane walked through this workshop tomorrow he would see a battered army lantern, note its condition as fair, and pass on. And yet Emrys felt in his chest a pressure close to tears, and understood that he had not repaired a lantern at all; he had performed a resurrection in miniature. He had said to the dark: *this man existed. He carried light. Someone bothered to punch his name into the thing he used to see by, and I refuse to let that be nothing.*

Somewhere above him, faintly, a floorboard ticked again. Or footsteps. He listened. Silence. But his heart had begun to beat faster, and not from the lantern.

Because a thought had arrived, fully formed, the way dangerous thoughts do—as obvious as if it had always been there:

*Names are the whole war.*

Vane traded in the absence of names—in omissions, in unrecorded transactions, in men erased from rolls and debts recorded under other men's seals. The letter accused the Hallorans in ink; the fires destroyed paper; the demolition order would destroy wood and iron and everything written on either. Every weapon in this quiet campaign was aimed at making things unreadable. And what had he just spent three weeks doing? Making a name readable. Punching it back into the world. It was nothing. It was everything. It was the same act as Cyrus's ledger, done in tin and glass instead of ink, and the recognition of that kinship landed in him with the weight of a vow.

He would hang it in the window.

The thought came slyly, dressed as innocence. After all, why not? A restored piece, displayed—perfectly natural, a craftsman's pride, nothing anyone could name as rebellion. And yet he knew, knew in his blood, exactly what it was: a light in a window facing the road, facing Vane's lodgings in the village, facing whatever hired eyes watched the estate these nights. A light with a dead sergeant's name glowing in its base. *Come and see what survived your fires. Come and read what cannot be unwritten.*

His father would call it madness. Provocation. His father, if he saw it, would order it taken down within the hour, would wrap it in sacking and bury it in the deepest drawer, the way he had buried everything else—his debts, his fear, the sealed letter, whatever truth had made his hands shake at dinner. And that was precisely why it must go up. Someone in this house had to stop hiding things.

Emrys stood, lifted the lantern by its mended handle—it burned steady, barely swaying—and crossed to the great window at the front of the workshop, the one that looked down the carriage drive through the bare trees toward the gate and the road beyond. Frost furred the lower panes in fern-patterns. He scraped a clear space with his thumbnail, set the lantern on the sill, adjusted its angle so the flame drew evenly, and stepped back.

From outside—anyone passing on the road in this blue-black hour—there would be only a square of gold in a dark wall. Nothing remarkable. But step closer, bend to the glass, and the name was there, burning quietly in the base of the light: proof that the forgotten kept their names if even one person refused to forget them.

"Goodnight, Sergeant," Emrys said softly.

The flame answered with the smallest movement, like a nod.

He stayed a while longer, banking the forge, capping the flux jar, winding the solder wire onto its spool—putting the room in order the way he always did, an inheritance from no one, simply his own instinct that a workshop should end each night ready to begin again. Then he trimmed the wick a final half-inch so it would burn down safely till morning, and gathered his coat, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch.

Through the frosted pane behind him, the light threw his shadow long across the benches—stretched thin and tall, a stranger's silhouette, someone he might yet become.

He looked out across the yard toward the house. And stopped.

Cyrus's window was dark.

Not dimmed—dark, the curtain drawn, no glow behind it at all, for the first time in weeks. At two in the morning, at the hour when her lamp was as certain as sunrise, the room stood black and silent. Emrys felt the frost walk up the back of his neck, entirely unrelated to the weather. People did not break habits like hers; her midnight light had been as fixed as the North Star, and stars did not go out on their own.

He stood in the doorway of his workshop a long moment, the lantern burning gold at his back and his own shadow thrown out ahead of him across the frozen yard, pointing like a compass needle toward the dark window where Cyrus Thornbury should have been awake—and was not.

Then the curtain twitched. Just slightly. Just enough that he could not tell whether she had looked out—or whether someone else had made her move.
