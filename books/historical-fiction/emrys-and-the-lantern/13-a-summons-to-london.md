## 13. A Summons to London

The order came on a Tuesday, in the space between the second bell and the lighting of the kitchen fires—a time of day that belonged to no one, when the house held its breath and even the mice were still.

I was at the washstand in the servants' hall, chapping my hands warm before the meagre fire, when Mrs. Beddoe appeared in the doorway with a look on her face I had learned to read the way sailors read weather. She carried a folded card on a small salver, as though the thing might bite her through the china.

"He wants you in the study," she said. "Himself. Before he's dressed."

"Before he's dressed," I repeated, and dried my hands slowly. Lord Halloran did not summon anyone before he was dressed. Lord Halloran conducted his household the way he conducted his correspondence—by rule, by hour, by the tidy arithmetic of precedence. A summons issued in his dressing gown was a summons made in haste, and haste in that house had become, over these past months, a synonym for fear.

I climbed the back stairs with my candle guttering in the draught, past the linen room where Martha Quill's mended sheets lay stacked like snowdrifts, past the narrow window where the fen showed grey and flat as a dead man's palm. The house creaked around me in its old familiar voice. Seven years I had walked these corridors, light-footed, unseen, a woman shaped like a shadow among shadows—and I had thought myself safe in that invisibility. But the sealed letter had taught me otherwise. Somewhere out in the wide cold world, someone remembered my name. *The Thornbury account.* The words had lodged under my ribs like a fishhook, and every stair I climbed seemed to set them deeper.

The study door stood ajar. I knocked once, softly, and entered without waiting to be bid, because that was our custom—he preferred not to say aloud that a servant might come in, and I preferred not to make him.

He sat behind his desk in his nightshirt and banyan, and I saw at once that he had not slept. The skin beneath his eyes had gone the colour of old tallow, and his hands, usually so careful with his pen, lay flat on the blotter as if he did not quite trust them to move. On the desk before him: the sealed letter, opened now, its wax broken into red crumbs. Beside it, a card with writing on one side only. And a glass of something amber, untouched, sweating rings onto the mahogany.

"Cyrus." He said my name the way he said little else these days—flatly, wearily, as though the syllables cost him coin. "Sit."

There was no chair for servants in the study. There never had been. I remained standing, which he knew I would do, which is perhaps why he offered.

"You will accompany me to London," he said. "Thursday. We take the early coach from March and change at Ely. Vane has requested—" a small, bitter movement of his mouth, "—*requested* an interview. Certain questions have been raised about the estate's wartime accounts. He has been kind enough to suggest I answer them in person, at his club, before things proceed to more formal channels." He picked up the card and put it down again, and I watched his fingers tremble, very slightly, before they stilled. "Formal channels. As though this were a drainage dispute."

I said nothing. In seven years I had learned that silence, properly deployed, was worth ten replies.

"He asks for you by name." Lord Halloran looked up then, and his eyes—his son's eyes, though Emrys's held none of this drowned grey colour—fixed on me with an expression I could not immediately name, and then could: it was suspicion wearing the mask of inquiry, and beneath both, fear. "Colonel Vane remembers you, Cyrus. From the hospitals. He says—" he glanced at the card, though he plainly knew the words by heart, "—he says your recollection of certain supply transactions would be invaluable to his inquiry."

The fire popped in the grate. Somewhere below, the kitchen bell rang for the scullery girl, and the sound rose through the floorboards like something drowning.

"And what would you have me recall, my lord?" I asked quietly.

It was the right question. I watched him weigh it, watch the calculation move behind his face—the same calculation, I suspected, that had kept him upright through seven years of debts contracted in dark rooms with men whose names he never spoke at table. He wanted to tell me what to remember. He wanted, I think, far more than that; but the words he chose were careful, and carelessness would have told me less.

"I would have you recall the truth," he said. "Whatever portion of it serves."

"Truths are not cut to measure, my lord."

"No." He almost smiled. It was the saddest expression I have ever seen on a living face. "No, they are not. That is rather the whole of my difficulty." He drew a breath, squared the papers on his desk as though ordering them could order the world. "We leave Thursday. Pack for a week. Mrs. Beddoe will see you have proper clothes—you will want something respectable. You will be seen." He said the last as if it were a sentence handed down by a court, and perhaps it was. Then, as I reached the door: "Cyrus."

I turned.

"Vane is not a good man." His voice had dropped, become something private and hoarse. "I have known him since before he was a colonel, and I am telling you—he is not a good man, and he is very clever, and he will look at you and know exactly what you are worth to him. Whatever he offers you, whatever he threatens you with—remember that you serve this house, and this house has been good to you."

*This house has been good to you.* It was not a lie. It was also, I understood with a cold clarity that surprised even me, not entirely true. Goodness, in that house, had always been mixed with debt, as water is mixed with milk, until neither could be told apart. But I bowed my head and said, "Yes, my lord," because that was what the woman I had spent seven years building would say, and I went out, and closed the door softly behind me, and stood in the corridor with my heart hammering against my ribs like a bird against a window.

*Walking into the lion's mouth,* I thought. *And the lion has asked for me by name.*

* * *

Two days. I counted them the way a condemned woman counts her mornings, and I used them the way I had learned to use everything: thoroughly, silently, without waste.

By day I was Cyrus Thornbury, clerk of the workshop, nurse of record, a woman who fetched and copied and mended and asked nothing. I inventoried Emrys's stores of brass and glass while he worked at his bench with his sleeves rolled, the smell of solder and beeswax thick in the lamplit air, and I let him talk—about the lantern he was restoring, about the fen, about anything but the letter and the colonel and the coach that waited for Thursday morning—and I answered in monosyllables and felt the ledger's weight everywhere, in every drawer, in the lining of my own skull.

By night I sewed.

My room sat at the end of the east passage, above the workshop, small and clean and cold, with a window that looked out over the black water of the drainage cut. I had moved the ledger after the pages went missing—I no longer trusted walls that other people's hands had touched—but on those two nights I brought it out from its hiding place and worked by the light of a single stub of candle, shielded by a screen, with a shawl over my shoulders and my door locked and my ears trained on the sounds of the house.

I did not take all of it. The whole book was too thick, too stiff; it would show under any skirt, however full, and a woman caught carrying her own testimony loses the use of it forever. So I chose. God forgive me, I chose, sitting cross-legged on the floor with the ledger open across my knees, reading seven years of the dead and deciding which of them could travel and which must stay behind in the dark.

I took the Crimea pages first. The entries from the spring of '09: Sergeant Quill's flanking manoeuvre at the river crossing, performed in his thirty-first year with forty men and no artillery support, credited within the week to Captain—now Colonel—Vane. My own hand, dated the third of April, witnessed by no one because there was never anyone left alive to witness anything, except a nursing woman who wrote at night. I took the supply ledgers' ghosts: three transactions, 1809 and 1810, in which grain and medical stores billed to the regiment simply ceased to exist between the quartermaster's book and the Treasury's, and the difference in price—eight hundred pounds, eleven hundred, six hundred and forty—flowed nowhere that any honest account could find. I took the names of the two clerks who had signed the bills of lading, one of whom I had nursed through fever in the winter of '10 and who had talked, delirious and frightened, of signatures he'd been made to forge.

And I took, because I could not in conscience leave it, the page I had never meant anyone to see: the entry concerning this house. Lord Halloran's debts. The sums drawn against the estate's future, funnelled through channels I had traced only partly, into hands I had named only tentatively, in my smallest, most private script. The page that indicted the man I was about to travel to London to defend.

I sat for a long time with that page in my lap, the candle burning low, the fen wind worrying at the window latch. Loyalty said: leave it. Leave it here in the wall of the lantern workshop, wrapped in oilcloth, where no magistrate and no colonel and no coach-searching footpad will ever find it. Truth said: you did not spend seven years writing the truth down in order to start editing it now, at the first moment it becomes dangerous to the people who feed you.

Truth won, as truth had always won with me, expensively.

I unpicked the hems of two petticoats—the heavy winter ones, wool-stiffened, the kind no man's eye lingers on—and I cut the chosen pages free with my sewing scissors, close and neat, twenty-one leaves in all. Each one I folded twice and oiled slightly with tallow against damp. Then I sewed. I am quick with a needle; field nursing teaches a woman to stitch flesh and cloth with equal economy, and I ran my seams fine and even, anchoring the paper flat against the panels of the petticoats where they would lie against hip and thigh, distributed so that no one place bulged. Over each seam I laid a row of ordinary running stitches in ordinary thread, the kind of mending any lady's maid might mock and no one would examine. When it was done, the petticoats hung on the chair back looking like nothing at all—grey wool, grey stitching, grey woman's grey underclothes.

But when I put one on, in the black hours before Wednesday's dawn, and stood before my little cracked mirror, I felt them. Every step pressed the pages against my body like a second skin of paper and ink, like letters written directly on the flesh. Twenty-one leaves. Dead men's names and living men's crimes, riding against my thighs as I walked. I thought: if the coach overturns, if I drown, some stranger may someday find me and wonder why the madwoman sewed documents into her skirts. I thought: this is what it costs to carry the truth. You wear it where everyone can see and no one can look.

I blew out the candle and lay down on my bed fully dressed, and did not sleep so much as hold still until morning.

* * *

Wednesday evening, Emrys found me at the workbench, checking the last of the inventory against his lists, and he did something he had never done in front of the other staff: he dismissed them. Asked Hobb and the boy for the lamp-oil keys and sent them up to supper, and stood in the doorway until their footsteps faded on the stairs.

Then he came and sat on the stool opposite me, across the bench, with the restored lantern between us—Quill's lantern, its new-ground glass panes throwing soft lozenges of lamplight across the scarred wood—and he said, without preamble:

"My father is taking you to London."

"Yes, sir."

"To Vane."

"Yes, sir."

He turned the lantern slowly on its base, and the light swung around the workshop and came back, and in its turning I watched his face, which had his father's bones and none of his father's habit of concealment. He was afraid. He was twenty-six years old and afraid, and trying not to show it, and failing, because he had never learned the trick of it that I had learned at fifteen.

"She won't tell me what she knows," he said, to the lantern, as though it were the stubborn party. "She copies things at midnight and hides them in walls and looks through people as if they were windows, and now she's going to London to sit across a table from a man I've heard my father mention only in his sleep. And I'm to stay here and mind the brass."

"Someone should mind the brass," I said.

His mouth twisted. "Cyrus." My name, plain, no honorific, the way only he and Martha ever said it. "If it goes badly—if Vane is what I think he is—there'll be nobody here to watch your back. Do you understand? He collects people. Father won't say it outright but I've seen his face. Vane collects people and uses them until they're used up, and then—" He stopped. He had been going to say *then they fall from bridges*, or something near it, and he was too decent to finish.

I closed the inventory book and folded my hands upon it, and I looked at this young man—this insomniac, this mender of broken light, this master's son who had chosen truth over blood before ever I asked him to—and something in my chest, long packed away in cotton wool, ached sharply.

"Mr. Halloran," I said. "Emrys. I have been walking into rooms where I was not welcome since before you could ride a horse. I survived Balaclava, and the fever tents, and seven years of being invisible in your father's house, which is a harder war than any of them. If Colonel Vane thinks he will collect me the way he collects clerks and widows and frightened lords, he has underestimated how much of me there is to collect." I rose, and gathered my shawl, and at the door I paused, because I am not made of stone no matter how carefully I have built myself to appear so. "Mind the lantern," I said. "Keep it lit in the window. If I am not back in a fortnight, burn nothing, sign nothing, and trust the wall."

"The wall," he repeated.

"The wall knows," I said, and went out into the frozen dark, and did not let myself hear whatever he called after me.

* * *

We left at half past five on Thursday morning, in sleet, the coach lamps swinging yellow holes in the blackness. Lord Halloran sat with his back to the horses and did not speak for the first twenty miles. I sat opposite, hands folded, petticoats heavy with their secret cargo, and watched the fen slide past the frosted glass—black drains, white fields, the skeletal pumps, a heron standing in the shallows like something that had died standing and not yet noticed.

At Ely we changed coaches, and in the yard of the Lamb Hotel, warming ourselves before the taproom fire while they switched the horses, I felt rather than saw the man.

He stood at the edge of the yard under the gateway arch, collar up, hat low, unhurried, a gentleman's greatcoat on a working man's frame. He was not looking at us. He was looking with tremendous interest at the off-side leader's near hind shoe, and no man on earth stares at a horse's hoof for four minutes unless he is watching something else entirely. When I turned my head he moved off, casual as smoke, toward the London road.

"My lord," I said quietly. "Do not turn around. We are followed."

Lord Halloran's teacup, halfway to his lips, stopped. I will grant him this: he did not turn. He set the cup down with a click that sounded very loud in the warm little room, and looked into the fire, and said, in a voice stripped of everything but arithmetic:

"One man?"

"One that I can see."

"Then there are two." He straightened his cuffs, a small precise gesture, the last furniture of the man he had been before Vane's letter arrived. "Vane likes to know where his pieces are on the board. Finish your tea, Cyrus. It is a long way to London, and I suspect neither of us will sleep much when we arrive."

We rode the rest of the way in silence, the wheels hammering the frost-broken road, the fen giving way by degrees to hedgerow and coppice, coppice to pasture, pasture to the brick sprawl of the northern suburbs. Night came down over London in a fall of gaslight and soot-smell, thousands of windows burning, thousands of names being traded behind them, and somewhere in that glittering animal of a city, Colonel Vane sat at his club with his excellent wine and his excellent patience, waiting for us to be delivered to him.

The coach slowed at last into the inn yard off Bishopsgate, into torchlight and shouting ostlers and the press of strangers. Lord Halloran descended first, stiff and grey, and handed me down like a lady, which I was not and had never pretended to be. As my boots touched the cobbles, a link-boy stepped out of the crowd to light our way to the waiting carriage—a boy of perhaps twelve, quick-eyed, ragged, holding his flame high.

And pinned inside the brim of his cap, where only I could see it, glinted a silver regimental badge. The badge of Vane's old corps.

"Welcome to London, miss," the boy said cheerfully, and touched his cap, and led us into the dark.
