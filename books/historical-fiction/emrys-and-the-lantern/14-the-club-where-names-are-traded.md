## 14. The Club Where Names Are Traded

London in February was a city of smoke and appetite. Cyrus had forgotten, in seven years of country air and lamp-blackened workshops, how the capital wore its filth like velvet—how the fog came off the river yellowed at the edges, smelling of coal and tallow and the sour breath of ten thousand chimneys, and how men walked through it as though it were an ornament arranged for their benefit rather than a consequence of their commerce.

She had been in the city four days, lodged at the sign of the Copper Kettle in Fetter Lane, a chandler's boarding house chosen for its anonymity: clerks, widows of modest means, a dissenting minister who coughed through his sermons to himself at supper. No one asked her business because no one wished to trade their own. It was exactly what she required.

Lord Halloran had installed himself at Brooks's, among his creditors and his former brother-officers, performing prosperity with the desperate fluency of a man reading from a script whose pages were being torn out ahead of him. He had given Cyrus her instructions on the second evening—a brief, embarrassed interview conducted while he adjusted his cravat three times before the glass.

"You will call on my man of business, Mr. Prosser, in Lincoln's Inn Fields, each morning. You will carry him whatever papers I send down by porter. And you will—" here he had paused, and something had moved behind his eyes that might have been shame or might have been calculation; with Lord Halloran the two had long since worn grooves into each other— "you will keep clear of White's, of Boodle's, of the Colonel. If Vane learns you are in town before I have answered his inquiries, everything becomes twice as difficult."

*Yes, my lord,* she had said, in the voice she kept for such occasions, the voice of smooth blank paper. *Of course, my lord.*

She had gone directly from his lodgings to Boodle's, stood across St. James's Street in the shadow of Hatchards' window for forty minutes in a thin rain, and watched Colonel Sir Reginald Vane arrive at half past six, handed from his carriage like a relic being translated, and received at the door by a servant who bowed as though bowing were a science.

That had been two nights ago. Tonight she meant to go closer.

Not inside—that was beyond even her arts, or would require arts she did not care to purchase. But the club's windows fronted the street, and behind those windows, in the long room where the members dined, there were tables set near the glass. She had learned this much from the pot-boy of the Copper Kettle, who had formerly worked in the kitchens of a club not three doors down and who talked, when warmed by small beer and the flattery of a woman who listened, of nothing so much as the architecture of privilege: which rooms looked onto which streets, which tables caught the light, which members took the corner seats because they had secrets and which took the window seats because they wanted to be seen having them.

"Colonel Vane," the pot-boy had said, when she mentioned the name lightly, as one mentions weather, "takes the window table. Always has. Man likes an audience even when he thinks no one's watching." He had laughed at his own contradiction without noticing it.

So tonight Cyrus dressed herself in the grey wool of a respectable upper servant, pinned her plainest cap low over her brow, took up a covered basket as though returning from an errand, and made her way through the fog toward St. James's Street with her heart beating its slow, practised rhythm—the rhythm of invisibility, learned in hospital tents where the wrong face at the wrong cot could mean a surgeon's notice and a surgeon's notice could mean anything at all.

The street was lamplit and busy despite the hour. Clinkers ran with trays of glasses; a chairmen's brawl was dissolving outside the Thatched House; somewhere a fiddler played badly in a doorway, and the fog ate the sound and gave back only the scrape of the bow. Cyrus crossed at the far end and let herself drift along the pavement opposite, unhurried, a woman with an errand, until she came level with the club's great windows—and stopped, as if to adjust the shawl over her basket, angled just so, her face turned toward the glass.

Inside, the long room blazed with candles. Perhaps twenty gentlemen sat at cloth-covered tables, white shirt-fronts gleaming like moths against the dark panelled walls. Port decanters moved in slow orbits. The noise within came muffled through the glass—a wash of voices, the chime of silver, laughter released at intervals like clockwork.

And there, at the window table, exactly where the pot-boy had promised: Colonel Sir Reginald Vane.

Seven years had refined him rather than aged him. He had been broad then, in Crimea, a big man grown heavier with rank, red-faced over maps, generous with brandy to those who brought him good news and glacial to those who did not. Now the breadth had settled into thickness carried well by tailoring, and the redness had cooled to a permanent healthy glow, the complexion of a man who breakfasted on kidneys and contempt. He sat with his back very straight, his hands folded beside a glass of claret he seemed barely to touch, and he was smiling at his companion in the way he had once smiled at quartermasters presenting requisitions—with perfect courtesy laid over perfect appraisal, the smile of a man weighing meat.

Cyrus did not know the companion. That was the first thing she understood, standing in the fog with rain gathering on her lashes: she had expected Vane alone, Vane in conversation with some lordling, and instead there was a second man at the table, slight and sandy and nervous in a way that even at thirty feet and through glass declared itself in the set of the shoulders. He was younger than Vane by fifteen years, perhaps more, dressed expensively but without conviction—a coat cut for another man's posture. His hands kept leaving his wineglass and returning to it. He leaned forward when Vane spoke, and drew back when Vane paused, like a man rowing in another's boat.

Vane said something. The clerk—for he had the look of clerkship, ink-stained at the soul if not the cuff—produced a leather folio from beneath his chair and opened it flat upon the tablecloth, angling it, after a glance left and right, so that only Vane could read it.

Cyrus's breath slowed. This was the old discipline coming forward unbidden: the field-nurse's stillness, the capacity to stand at a bedside for an hour motionless, become furniture, become part of the ward's silence, while life and death negotiated themselves inches away. Her body remembered how to be unnoticed even as her mind raced ahead.

Vane read. Then he took up a pen—his own, drawn from his breast pocket, and Cyrus noted that, noted it hard: a man who trusted no other nib—and bent over the folio. From the angle of his shoulder she could see it was writing, not signing. Long strokes. Several lines. The clerk watched, lips moving slightly, as though following a form he knew and feared.

Then the pen stopped, and Vane turned the folio around, and pushed it across the table with two fingers, and the clerk signed beneath whatever had been written, his signature quick and cramped and somehow ashamed even at that distance, the way a man signs when he is signing something he will later wish to deny knowing.

Papers changed hands. The folio vanished. Vane raised his glass a fraction—the toast of conspirators, courteous, final—and said something that made the clerk laugh, high and unhappy, the laugh of a man laughing because refusal was no longer on any menu set before him.

And then Cyrus saw Vane slide a single card across the tablecloth, face down, with one finger, and the clerk take it up quickly and put it away in his breast, and press his hand briefly against the wool as though the card were warm.

A name. A direction. An instruction, or an introduction, or a payment rendered in the only currency Vane dealt in besides money: access.

*Who are you?* she thought at the sandy man, with an intensity that must have thinned the fog between them. *What have you signed? What have you been told to carry, and to whom?*

As if the thought had travelled—as if intent could cross glass—Vane lifted his head.

It was nothing dramatic. He did not startle or turn fully; he simply raised his eyes from his companion and looked toward the window, along the line that every man who takes a window table knows by instinct is the line of his audience. Cyrus felt the look pass over her and past her and through her, and she did the only thing invisibility allowed: she became entirely ordinary. She bent to her basket, retied a ribbon that needed no tying, straightened, shifted the basket to her other arm, and walked on up the street at the pace of a woman late for a kitchen and expecting a scolding. She did not look back. Looking back is the whole art of being seen.

She turned into Jermyn Street, walked its length, turned again, and only then permitted herself to lean against the cold brick of an arcade and breathe.

He had looked. Of course he had looked. A man engaged in forgery at a club window does not grow careless—he grows vigilant, and vigilance sweeps the street every few minutes the way a lighthouse sweeps water. She had known this. She had positioned herself accordingly, in the grey dress, in the fog, among the clinkers and chairmen. And yet her hands were not steady, and she made herself stand in the cold until they were, because the next part required steadiness, and patience, and the particular cruelty of waiting.

For she had seen something else, in the moment before Vane looked up. When the clerk had put away the card, he had also taken from his folio one last paper—not signed, merely shown—and Vane had glanced at it and nodded once. And the paper, in the candlelight behind the glass, had borne a direction written large enough to catch her eye, the way directions do when one has spent years copying them from letters and discharge papers and the backs of unpaid promissory notes.

*Cornhill.* Not a number she could read. But Cornhill—the heart of the city's paper trade, where scriveners kept their stalls and copyists worked by the dozen and a man could buy a hand as easily as a hat.

* * *

She went back the next night. And the next.

This was the discipline her ledger had taught her: that truth does not announce itself; it accumulates, entry by entry, date by date, the way sediment makes stone. On the first night Vane dined alone with a general and talked of nothing she could hear. On the second he did not appear at all, and she stood in the rain until midnight feeling foolish, and went home with wet hems and a fever tickling at the edge of her throat, and told herself that foolishness and diligence were often indistinguishable until afterward.

On the third night, the sandy clerk returned.

He arrived alone, at half past eight, and was admitted with less ceremony than before—which told her something; membership by proxy, tolerated presence, a man used at the door but not welcomed by it. Vane was already at the window table. There was a third man this time, heavy, florid, wearing spectacles he removed the instant he sat down and polished throughout the interview as though his hands required employment to keep his conscience quiet. The three heads bent together. The folio appeared. Papers moved in small, controlled migrations across the tablecloth, each one passed under the cover of reaching for a decanter, and Cyrus, watching from the bookshop shadows with her basket of stones—she had added weight to make the errand credible, and now her arm ached with the honesty of her disguise—understood that she was watching not a meeting but a transaction, and that transactions of this kind have a grammar, and that grammar can be learned.

Vane wrote. The clerk witnessed. The spectacled man supplied, from a fat pocketbook, signatures—actual signatures, practiced, fluent, four different names executed in four different hands on four different slips, and Cyrus felt the floor of the world tilt gently beneath her, because she recognized the exercise. She had seen it before. Seven years ago, in a tent that smelled of carbolic and rot, she had watched a captain practise a dead sergeant's hand in a dispatch-book margin, over and over, until the dead man's name flowed from a living stranger's fingers as smoothly as water.

Vane was doing it for profit. The captain had done it for glory. Both had done it for the same reason underneath, which was the belief that names belong to whoever can use them best, and that the men whose names they are have no further use for anything.

The interview ended near eleven. Vane rose first, buttoned his coat with the satisfaction of a man closing a till, and left by the club's side entrance into Jermyn Street, where his carriage waited. The spectacled man departed minutes later in the opposite direction. The sandy clerk remained alone at the table, ordered nothing further, sat with his untouched glass for a long quarter-hour—Cyrus counted it against the church bells of St. James's, which gave the quarter with a reluctance audible even through fog—and then gathered himself and came out through the main door into the street, pulling on gloves as he came, moving fast, like a man anxious to be anywhere other than where he had been.

He turned east. Cyrus followed.

She had followed men before—in Crimea, a quartermaster's runner she had suspected of diverting laudanum—and she knew the mechanics: keep the head down, keep to the kerb's shadow, match pace by sound rather than sight, use the shop windows' reflections and the fog's charity. The clerk walked badly for a man with secrets, too quickly and too straight, telegraphing his route half a street ahead, and he led her down Jermyn Street, across the Haymarket, and into Pall Mall eastward, where the lamps burned fewer and the fog lay thicker between them, before turning north at last into the mouth of a narrow court behind Cornhill.

There he stopped, under a bracketed lamp, and took out the card.

Cyrus flattened herself into a doorway twenty yards back, among crates of tallow, and watched him hold the card up to the light and read it again, though plainly he knew what it said—read it the way one reads a sentence one has been sentenced to, checking it for errors, for mercy, for a flaw in the wording that might release him. His lips moved. The fog stole the words. But she had spent years reading lips across sickrooms, catching the murmured last things men said to pillows when they believed no witness remained, and she caught the shape of it, repeated twice, the way a frightened man repeats a name to fix it in memory:

*Aldous Finch.*

Then the clerk—Finch, if the card named him; or Finch if it did not, and the name belonged to someone else entirely, someone the card directed him toward—folded the card away, climbed three steps to a door set between a stationer's and a notary's, let himself in with a key, and disappeared.

Cyrus stood in the doorway among the tallow crates for a long time after, feeling the cold come up through her boots and the rain settle into her wool, and assembled the night the way she assembled all her nights, piece by piece, date by date, in the order that made stone.

A colonel who forged. A clerk who witnessed. A spectacled man who supplied four fluent hands. A card passed under a tablecloth. A name spoken twice to the fog. A house behind Cornhill, between a stationer's and a notary's, where a frightened man locked himself in with whatever he had signed.

She walked home through the emptying streets with her basket of stones, and at the Copper Kettle she paid the maid extra to bring her a candle that would burn past midnight and to say, if anyone asked, that the quiet lady in the end room had retired days ago to the country. Then she locked her door, wedged a chair beneath the handle—it had become a habit in London, where she trusted nothing—and drew the ledger from its hiding place beneath the false bottom of her sewing box.

By candlelight she wrote it all: the dates, the hours, the table by the window, the folio, the four signatures in four hands, the card, the court off Cornhill, the door between the stationer's and the notary's. And beneath it, underlined once in her small, ruled, nurse's hand, the name her ears had pulled out of the fog:

*Aldous Finch — clerk. Present at three transactions observed. Witnessed the colonel's hand. Carries his card. Afraid.*

She sat looking at the entry for a long while, the candle guttering, the fog pressing at the window like a face. Aldous Finch. Was he the author of the sealed letter—the hand she had recognized from her nursing days, the hand that had written *the Thornbury account*? Or was he merely the next link, another instrument, another name borrowed and used? She did not know. But she knew fear when she catalogued it, and she had written *Afraid* about him the way she had once written *Feverish* about soldiers: not as diagnosis but as prognosis. Men that afraid were either victims already, or witnesses soon to be disposed of.

And a disposed witness, she had learned in seven years of recording omissions, leaves behind him one thing always: paper. Men like Vane destroyed men, but never quite managed to destroy the trail of signatures, drafts, copies, and receipts that men trail behind them like sledges through snow.

She closed the ledger, banked the candle, and lay in the dark listening to London breathe.

Tomorrow, she decided, in the borderland between waking and sleep, tomorrow she would find where Aldous Finch worshipped, or drank, or lodged, or all three. Tomorrow she would begin learning the face that went with the hand.

She fell asleep with the name sitting in her mind like a coin held in a closed fist—warm, and edged, and waiting to be spent.

Three days later, she would learn that Finch had been asking questions of his own. About her.
