## 15. Finch's Confession, Half-Spoken

London in February was a country unto itself—a grey republic of chimneys, its air thick enough to chew, its streets running with soot and the melt of dirty snow. Emrys Halloran had been raised on the Halloran estate's clean winds and the green hush of the parkland, and for three days now the city had pressed upon him like a thumb upon a bruise. He wore his second-best coat because his best marked him as a gentleman, and a gentleman alone in St. James's at dusk was either a fool or a mark. He wished to be neither. He wished only to find Aldous Finch.

He had the name from Cyrus's letter, which had reached him by the slow grace of the mail coach two days after he himself had left home—crossing on the road, they later learned, like two thoughts passing in a corridor and never meeting.

* * *

The letter lay now in his breast pocket, read so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth. He took it out that evening in his lodgings off Chancery Lane, a room rented under a name not quite his own, and read it again by the light of a tallow candle that guttered more than it burned.

*My dear Mr. Halloran,—for I suppose I must learn to write to you rather than about you, and find the habit stranger than I expected.*

*London is loud. I had forgotten how loud, or perhaps I never knew it, having come to it before as a nurse and seen only its hospitals, which are quiet in the way that graveyards are quiet. His lordship is lodged at the Clarendon and spends his evenings being polite to men who are counting his debts behind their smiles. I am lodged one floor above the kitchens, which suits me. I can hear everything and be seen by no one. You know how I prize that arrangement.*

*I have news, but I will give it to you plainly, as you asked, though plainness frightens me a little. Tonight I followed him. The clerk from the letter—I have his face now. He is small and neat and walks like a man rehearsing his steps, and he drinks at a club off Jermyn Street where the windows are fogged from inside. Through the glass I watched him sign something for another man, and the way he signed it—the flourish first, the name after—told me he has done it many times. He writes other people's names better than his own.*

*His name is Finch. Aldous Finch. He wrote the sealed letter; I am nearly certain. The hand matches, down to the peculiar way he makes his f, like a fence post leaning.*

*Go carefully. If he wrote it once to order, he can unwrite it—but a man who sells names will not give one back for free, or for safe.*

*Do not tell his lordship any of this. I mean that quite seriously.*

*Yrs., C. Thornbury*

*P.S.—The mutton here is terrible. I would trade my penknife for one of Mrs. Hobday's pies. Do not send one; it would arrive as a science experiment.*

Emrys found, as always, that he read her postscripts twice. It was the only place her humour lived openly; everywhere else in the letter it hid, the way the woman herself hid, folded into the seams of the household.

* * *

Finding Finch proved easier than Emrys feared and harder than he hoped. The club off Jermyn Street would not admit him—his coat was good but his accent was county, and county did not open these doors—and so he did what he had learned to do in the lantern workshop, where patience was half the craft: he waited. He took a room across the street at a coffee-house whose window gave onto the club's entrance, drank coffee until his hands trembled, and watched the door through two long February evenings while sleet came down like thrown gravel.

On the third evening, at a quarter past nine, Aldous Finch came out into the cold, pulled his collar up with both hands like a man shutting a gate behind him, and turned toward St. James's Square.

Emrys followed.

The streets were slick with ice beneath new snow, and Finch walked quickly, head down, past shuttered milliners and a watchman warming himself at a brazier, past the dark bulk of the square's railings where bare trees stood like prisoners taken against their will. At the corner by the Norris statue Finch stopped—stopped so abruptly that Emrys nearly walked into his back—and said, without turning, in a thin voice pitched precisely between politeness and panic:

"You have followed me three nights, sir. Either say your business or let me fetch a constable, and God knows I do not wish to fetch a constable."

"I want to talk about a letter," Emrys said. "One you wrote."

Finch turned then. Up close he was smaller than the window-glass had made him—a narrow man of maybe five-and-forty, with ink-stained cuffs just visible at his wrists and eyes the colour of weak tea, quick and unhappy. He looked at Emrys the way a man looks at a bill he cannot pay.

"Not here," he said. "Not here."

* * *

They ended in the back parlour of a chandler's shop near the Haymarket, a place of tallow smell and dripping umbrellas, where the proprietor charged twopence for candles nobody bought and asked no questions of gentlemen who wanted a table. A single dip burned between them. Rain had begun again outside, soft against the shutters, patient as arithmetic.

"Brandy?" Emrys offered.

"No." Finch folded his hands on the table, unfolded them, refolded them. "No spirits, if you don't mind. I need my wits about me, such as they are." A pause. "You are the Halloran boy. The younger one."

"You know that."

"I know that." Finch laughed once, a sound with no floor under it. "I know a great deal, Mr. Halloran. That is my profession. Knowing things is not the crime; writing them down for other men—that is where the money is, and also the sin, depending on your church." He looked at the candle flame as though it owed him an apology. "You want to know who dictated the letter."

"I want to know who dictated the letter."

"And if I told you"—Finch's voice dropped to hardly more than the rain—"if I told you, what would you do? Go to a magistrate? Which magistrate? Half the benches in this city dine with the men you'd be accusing. Go to the papers? The papers eat from the same hand." He shook his head slowly. "There is a version of this conversation, Mr. Halloran, where you leave here knowing nothing, and in six months you've forgotten my face, and we are both of us alive and moderately comfortable. I would very much prefer that version. I have read it over several times. It does not survive contact with you."

"Then let me try another version," Emrys said quietly. "A version where you tell me the truth, and I keep your name out of it entirely. You weren't the author—you were the pen. Pens aren't hanged. Men are."

"That is exactly the distinction," Finch said, with sudden bitter precision, "that keeps pens employed."

For a long moment neither spoke. The rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog dreamed audibly.

Then Finch sagged, all at once, like a puppet with a cut string, and rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, and when he lowered them he looked ten years older than the man who had crossed St. James's Square.

"It was dictated," he admitted. "Every word. I sat in a room in—no. Not the room." He caught himself, jaw tightening. "I sat in a room. There was a fire, and there was brandy I was afraid to drink, and there was a man on the far side of the desk who spoke the whole accusation to me sentence by sentence, and I wrote it down in a fair hand, because a fair hand is what I am paid for. The names of the Hallorans. The sums. The dates. None of it mine. Some of it, I think—" he hesitated, and his tea-coloured eyes flickered up, terrified and honest in equal measure— "some of it, I believe, was true. That is the devil of it. They mix it, you see. Nine lies and a truth, like watering wine. The truth is what makes the lies go down."

"What was true?"

"The debts," Finch whispered. "Your father's debts. The rest was built on top of them, brick by brick, until the house looked ready to fall on its own."

Emrys felt the words land somewhere beneath his ribs. He had known—Cyrus's letters, the fires, the shape of his father's midnight vigils had told him—but knowing a thing in the marrow is different from hearing it spoken by a stranger over a tallow candle.

"The man who dictated," Emrys said. "Give me his name."

And here Finch broke.

It was not dramatic. That was the terrible thing—it was the smallest possible breaking, a man simply reaching the end of what his nerves would carry. He stood up from the table so fast his chair skidded, went to the shuttered window, stood with his back to Emrys and his hand pressed flat against the wood as though holding the city out.

"No," he said. "No, no, no."

"He'll destroy you anyway," Emrys said, rising. "When you're spent. When your hand shakes too badly or your memory grows inconvenient. You must know that. Men like him use pens until they blunt them, and then—"

"You don't understand what he *has*." Finch turned, and his face was wet—not tears, or not only tears; sweat stood on his brow despite the cold room. "He has copies. Of things I have written. Fifteen years of things, Mr. Halloran, every forgery, every favour, every signature that was never mine to make—all of it copied fair and filed away, and he showed me the file. He showed me the file and he smiled, and he said, *'Aldous, you are the most reliable man I know,'* and I understood that it was both a compliment and a coffin being measured." His breath came short. "If I give you his name and it reaches him—and it will reach him, it always reaches him—then one morning I will not wake on the riverbank near where I fell. Or I will, and it will be called what the other thing was called. What happened to—" He stopped himself, lips going white.

"To Finch?" Emrys asked softly. "To whom?"

But Finch only shook his head, shaking now visibly, hands, shoulders, the fine tremor of a mechanism overloaded, and Emrys understood suddenly that he was watching a man who had already fallen from a bridge in his imagination a hundred times, rehearsing it nightly, waiting only for the courage or the order.

"Sit down," Emrys said, and steered him back to the chair, and poured him water from the jug despite the refusal of brandy. Finch drank it in small desperate swallows.

"I wrote it," Finch said at last, staring at the empty glass. "That much I will swear to anyone you like, because it is true and it costs me nothing—I wrote it. Dictated, yes, but my hand, my ink, my seal. If that is all you ever have, take it. Take it to your magistrate and hang the letter around my neck if you must. But the name of the mouth it came from—" He pressed his lips together. "I cannot. I am sorry. God help me, I am sorry, but I cannot."

* * *

He could, however, almost.

In the quarter hour that remained before Finch insisted, standing, on ending the interview, he gave Emrys the crumbs a frightened man scatters without realizing—the room had a marble mantel; the brandy had been French, not government-issue rum, which narrowed the class of host; the dictating man had used a word, *repeatedly*, a particular word, and Finch mimed it without naming it: a crisp downward chop of the hand, and the syllables *quite so*, *quite so*, delivered in a tone of polished, unhurried command.

And then, at the chandler's door, pulling on his gloves with fingers that would not quite obey him, Finch said the last thing he said:

"There is a draft."

Emrys went still. "A draft?"

"Of the confession. Mine. Not the letter—the other document. The one I began writing at night, for myself, in case—" He did not finish the case. "It exists. I could not burn it. Isn't that absurd? Fifteen years of burning other men's paper, and I couldn't burn four pages of my own." He managed something that was almost a smile and was mostly grief. "I put it where I put things I want found by the right person and no other. She'll understand. The widow. Quill. Tell her—tell her it's in the book of hours, where the poor always look first."

"Mr. Finch—"

But the door had already closed behind him, and Emrys stood in the chandler's parlour among the smell of tallow and rain, listening to Finch's footsteps diminish up the Haymarket—quick, quick, quick, like a heart trying to outrun what it knew.

* * *

*My dear Mr. Halloran,*

*You will think I have gone mad, writing twice in one week, but the post leaves tomorrow and sleep has declined to visit, so we may as well be honest with each other, which I find increasingly difficult not to be.*

*I saw him leave the chandler's. Yes—I was across the street, because I am constitutionally incapable of allowing other people to take risks unsupervised; it is the nurse in me, and I have stopped apologizing for her. You talked to him for the better part of an hour, which is longer than I hoped and shorter than you needed, and when he came out he was walking like a man who has handed over a parcel of explosives and is not certain of the fuse's length.*

*So: whatever he gave you, guard it. And whatever he refused you, do not press him again—not yet. Frightened men can be visited twice; terrified men can only be visited once.*

*A piece of my own news, since yours will be wanting company. Tonight his lordship introduced me to a Colonel Vane at the Clarendon as "our Miss Thornbury, who kept the books through the war years," and Vane took my hand and held it one moment longer than courtesy requires and said, "Ah. The record-keeper. How useful those people are." As though I were a spade, or a well. I curtsied. I am quite proud of the curtsy; it contained entire libraries of what I did not say.*

*He remembers me. I am sure of it now, beyond fear, into something almost like certainty, which is colder. Seven years ago a sergeant died holding a line that saved four hundred men, and Vane wore the credit like a borrowed coat, and I wrote the truth down because no one else would, and I have been waiting since without admitting I was waiting. Well. The waiting may be over. Whether that is deliverance or catastrophe I decline, tonight, to guess.*

*Come home when you have what you came for. Or don't come home—but then write, and write plainly, and remember that a letter can be read by others, so write plainly in the way that means something only to me.*

*The mutton remains terrible.*

*Yrs., C. T.*

Emrys folded the letter along its softened creases and set it in the candlelight and watched the flame awhile. Outside, the rain had thickened toward sleet again; the window ran with water, and the lamps of Chancery Lane blurred into smears of gold, the whole city dissolving and reforming, dissolving and reforming.

*Quite so,* he thought. *Marble mantel. French brandy.* A short list, and every item on it pointing toward one man—and yet Finch's terror pointed there too, more eloquently than any crumb, terror of that specific texture being reserved, in Emrys's experience, for predators and their keepers.

There was a draft, hidden where the poor look first. In a hymnal, perhaps—in a book of hours. With Martha Quill, back home, a day's ride away.

He snuffed the candle and began, in the dark, to pack.
