## 16. The Price of Protection

The summons came at the hour when the house was least itself—that grey interval between the last of the daylight and the lighting of the lamps, when the corridors of Halloran Park seemed to hold their breath, and every sound carried further than it ought. A footman Cyrus had known for six years, a boy called Peters who had once wept over a dead hound and whom she had entered in her ledger under *Peters, Thomas, groom's apprentice, honest*, came to her room with a candle he did not need and a message he clearly did not wish to deliver.

*His lordship asks you to attend him in the study. At your convenience.*

At your convenience. No lord in England said *at your convenience* to a clerk unless something was very wrong or very delicate, and Cyrus had learned long ago that the two wore the same coat. She put down her mending—stockings, always stockings; the army taught a woman nothing so thoroughly as the mending of stockings—and looked at herself a moment in the small mirror above the washstand. The face that looked back was the face she had spent seven years perfecting: calm, unremarkable, a face made for passing through rooms without being remembered in them. It would not do to wear anything else tonight.

She followed Peters down the west corridor. The house smelled of beeswax and cold stone and, beneath it, faintly, the char-ghost of the record-room fire—a smell that had worked its way into the plaster itself, into the grain of the banisters, so that no one who walked these halls could any longer forget what had been tried here. Someone had meant to burn the Hallorans' history out of existence, and though the flames had been beaten back, their breath remained, soaked into the very fabric like smoke into wool.

Cyrus passed the door of the workshop corridor and felt, as she always did now, the small hard weight of the ledger against her awareness—not on her person, never again on her person in daylight, but bricked behind the lathes where she had hidden it, patient as a seed. *I know where you sleep,* she thought at it, the way one might think at an old friend across a crowded church.

The study door stood ajar. Lamplight lay along the carpet like spilled honey.

* * *

Lord Halloran was standing at the window with his back to the room, and Cyrus saw at once—he saw everything at once; it was her curse and her craft—that he had aged a decade since the harvest. His shoulders, once the shoulders of a man who had carried a plough over wet ground well into his forties, had begun to curl inward, folding around some private wound the way a man folds around a cracked rib. On the desk behind him lay papers arranged with terrible neatness: the neatness of a man who has spent the afternoon putting his affairs into order because he no longer trusts himself to do it quickly.

"You'll take wine?" he asked, without turning.

"No, my lord. Thank you."

"Then sit, Miss Thornbury, if you please." He turned then, and gestured to the chair opposite the desk—the guest's chair, the chair no servant ever sat in—and waited until she had lowered herself onto its edge before resuming his place behind the desk. He did not sit so much as settle, like ballast shifting.

For a while neither spoke. The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere upstairs a door closed; somewhere below, the cook's voice rose and fell in argument with the scullery girl about the state of a copper pot. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a country that still believed itself at peace.

"I have been going through the accounts," Lord Halloran said at last. "All of them. Seven years of them." He laid one hand flat on the neat papers. "Do you know what I found, Miss Thornbury?"

"Figures, my lord."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face and died. "Gaps," he said. "Gaps dressed up as figures. Payments to men whose names I cannot find. Receipts for goods that never reached this estate. Debts I do not remember contracting, in hands I almost—but not quite—recognise." His fingers pressed the paper flat, whitening at the knuckle. "And threaded through all of it, always, the same name. Vane."

Cyrus kept her breathing even. In her mind's eye, unbidden, a ledger page opened: three transactions, dated years ago, each one initialed in a colonel's confident hand. She said nothing. Silence was the oldest instrument she owned, and the truest.

"He has been feeding on this family since before Emrys could shave," Lord Halloran went on, and now the composure cracked enough to let something raw show through. "Every loan a kindness. Every kindness a hook. And I took the bait like a carp in a mill pond, because a gentleman does not ask impertinent questions of a brother officer, and by the time asking became necessary, asking had become ruinous." He looked up at her, and his eyes—his son's eyes, but dimmed, banked, like coals left overnight—held hers with an effort. "You were there, they tell me. Crimea. You served with the medical trains."

"With the nurses, my lord. Behind the lines."

"Behind the lines is where everything happens that gentlemen later deny." He rose abruptly and crossed to the decanter after all, pouring himself a measure of claret he did not drink, only held, watching the colour of it move. "Miss Thornbury, I will not insult you by pretending this conversation is casual. You have kept this household's records for six years. You are discreet—you are famously discreet; my steward swears you could keep the secret of your own christening. And I find myself in need of discretion more than I have ever needed it in my life."

He set the glass down untasted.

"There is a ship," he said. "Out of Hull, sailing for Hamburg on the first fair wind after Michaelmas. There is money—my money, what remains of it that Vane cannot trace—to buy you passage and set you up comfortably wherever you choose to land. Amsterdam. Lübeck. Anywhere the English law's long arm grows tired before it arrives." He drew a folded paper from beneath the others and laid it on the desk between them, squared to the edge, precise as everything else in this dying room. "In exchange, you surrender to me tonight any papers, notes, copies, or records in your keeping that concern this family or its finances. All of them. Every scrap."

* * *

The clock on the mantel counted four seconds before Cyrus understood that she had stopped breathing, and resumed.

She had rehearsed for many questions in the weeks since the fire. She had rehearsed for Vane's men searching her room. She had rehearsed for arrest, for accusation, for the moment—she felt it coming the way one feels weather in an old wound—when she would be asked to choose between the book and her own neck. But she had never rehearsed for this: for kindness. For a good man, drowning quietly in debts he had been tricked into signing, offering her salvation in exchange for the very thing that might save him.

Because that was what the ledger was. However much Lord Halloran did not yet know it, however much he hoped to bury his own shame along with the evidence, those pages sewn behind brick and lath contained the only complete account of Vane's method—the dates, the sums, the initials, the names of the clerks and quartermasters through whom the money ran like water through a broken pipe. Burned or surrendered, they would leave the Hallorans naked before a man who had already sent fire to their gates.

And if she gave them up, she would be complicit in her own silencing. Again. For how long had silence been her trade? Seven years of recording privately what she dared not say publicly; seven years of writing names no one would read. She had told herself it was enough—that memory hoarded was a kind of justice deferred. But deferred justice, she had learned at a bedside in Crimea watching a sergeant bleed out for another man's glory, is only injustice wearing a patient face.

"My lord," she said, and heard her own voice arrive steady, which astonished her, "may I ask why?"

"Why?" He laughed, short and without humour. "Because I am trying to save your life, woman. Because the next fire may not be content with ledgers. Because Vane has dined at my table and smiled at my wife and asked, oh so gently, whether I employ anyone who served in the Crimea—and I am not a fool, whatever my creditors believe. He is looking for you. Whatever you wrote down over there or over here, he wants it gone, and everyone who touched it gone with it." He leaned forward, both hands on the desk now, and for a moment the old strength surfaced in him, the ploughman's stubbornness. "I will not have another death on this estate's conscience. Take the ship, Miss Thornbury. Take the money. Leave me the paper, and I will see it burned myself, tonight, with my own hands, and we will both sleep better."

*And the lie*, she thought, *is that you believe burning it saves anyone. You think it buys peace. It only postpones the slaughter.*

She looked at the folded paper on the desk—the terms of her exile, written in a hand that shook only slightly, at the bottom, where the signature would go. She looked at the man behind it, who had once carried her bodily through floodwater when the mill stream burst its banks, who paid for Martha Quill's coal every winter without knowing he did it, because he paid the parish rate and never once complained of it aloud. She respected him. That was the unbearable part. If he had been a fool or a tyrant she could have refused him cleanly. Instead he was a decent man trying to buy her safety with the rope intended for his own neck, and she was about to look him in the eye and lie to him.

Seven years she had guarded her truthfulness like the last dry match in a wet campaign. She had lied to officers, yes—in the way one lies to the enemy, by omission, by the careful blankness of a face. But never like this. Never warmly. Never to a man she served, over his own hearth, with his own claret untouched between them.

She felt the guilt arrive even before the words did, sharp and bright as a lancet. And then—this was the strange thing, the thing she would carry with her down every road she ever walked afterward—she felt the guilt turn in her hand and become a tool. Guilt sharpened into resolve. If she must be false to a good man, let the falsehood serve the truth. Let the lie protect the very evidence that would one day clear him.

"Lord Halloran," she said gently, "you overestimate me."

He frowned. "I don't think so."

"I kept accounts for this estate. Figures in and figures out, wages, stores, the workshop inventory. Those are your steward's property and remain in his office, every page of them, and he may burn them tomorrow if it pleases you." She paused, and let a weariness into her voice that was not entirely manufactured—there was real weariness underneath, years of it, and it lent the performance a truth no invented feeling could. "But papers concerning the Crimea—names I wrote down, things I saw—those are mine, my lord, and they concern no one in this house. They concern dead men and unpaid widows, and Colonel Vane's ambition, which is precisely why he wants them. I burned most of them myself after the fire in your record room. What little remains I can put in your hands within the hour, if you wish it."

It was masterly. She heard it come out of her mouth smooth as poured cream, each clause load-bearing, none of it contradictable except by search—and search was exactly what she must now invite, deflect, survive. She watched the relief move across his face like cloud-shadow over a field, and hated herself for the skill of it, and knew, simultaneously, with a clarity that felt almost holy, that she would do it again, and again, as many times as the book required.

"You burned them," he repeated slowly. "After the fire."

"A clerk who keeps private notes learns caution, my lord. Caution arrived late, but it arrived."

He sat back. For a long moment he studied her the way one studies a map of country one thought one knew—and Cyrus held the gaze, blank and open, giving him nothing but the tired honesty of a woman who had seen too much. It was the oldest trick in her repertoire: to seem simple. To be, in the eyes of power, furniture.

"God forgive me for asking it of you at all," he said at last, and the words dropped into the room like stones into a well. "Keep what remains, then—or burn it, whichever lets you sleep. But the offer stands. The ship sails from Hull after Michaelmas. If Vane comes within a mile of you, Miss Thornbury—if you so much as see his carriage on the north road—you take the money and you go, and you do not write to tell us you arrived safely. Do you understand? Not a word. Your safety matters more to this family than our name, and I will not argue the point."

*Your safety matters more than our name.* She rose, because sitting made the tears likelier and tears were ammunition she could not afford to spend. She inclined her head.

"That is generous beyond my deserving, my lord."

"It is less than you deserve and more than I can afford, which is the story of my entire life." He pushed the folded paper toward her. "Take the terms. Keep them where you keep your other secrets."

She took the paper. Their fingers did not touch; she saw to that. And then, at the door, with her hand on the latch, she committed the second sin of the evening—she turned back, and offered him, unprompted, one more piece of the smooth cream.

"My lord. If I might say one thing without being presumed impertinent."

"Say anything you like. You've earned it."

"Whatever Colonel Vane offers you—forgiveness, silence, accommodation—remember that a man who sets fire to a house has already decided what he intends for the people inside it. Bargaining with him afterward only teaches him where you sleep." She said it plainly, a nurse's plainness, the tone she had used to tell fathers truths about sons. "I have seen his signature before, sir. Not on documents. On the way documents fail to match the men who signed them."

He stared at her. Something moved behind his eyes—the first crack of true understanding, perhaps, the first cold draft through the sealed door of his denial. He did not answer, and she did not wait for one.

* * *

The corridor was dark. Her candle had burned low in the holder, and the flame bent and guttered in the draught from the ill-fitting window at the passage end, throwing her shadow enormous and wavering up the wall—a giantess made of a small woman and a trembling light.

Cyrus walked slowly. Behind her, the study door closed on a good man arranging his own surrender, and ahead of her, past the linen store, past the boot room, the workshop corridor waited with its smell of oil and cut wood and lamp-black, and inside the wall cavity, behind the third lathe below the shelf where Emrys Halloran's unfinished lantern stood waiting for its glass, the ledger lay in the dark with all its small patient names.

She stopped at the turning. She listened the way she had learned to listen in hospital tents—for the wrong silence, the silence with a shape in it. The house breathed. Somewhere below, the copper-pot argument had ended in victory for one party or the other. Nothing stirred that should not.

But her room had been entered once already. Pages had been taken and returned by hands she could not yet name. Three people had access. Three people, and one of them had given her terms tonight in a shaking hand, and one of them was a boy who chose truth over blood, and one of them—

She counted again, and the counting frightened her, because it always came out the same: three doors into that room, and she had bolted only two.

Cyrus pinched out her candle rather than let it gutter, and stood a moment in the full dark, letting her eyes remember how to see. The folded paper with the ship's terms she slid inside her bodice, against her heart, where lies and promises could keep each other company. Then she moved toward the workshop, silent on the stone, a woman made entirely of caution arriving late—past the linen store, past the boot room, toward the wall where her whole truth slept like a buried spring, waiting for someone with the courage to dig.

Tomorrow she would visit Martha Quill. Tomorrow she would begin collecting the living. Tonight, in the dark, she permitted herself ten seconds of grief for the honest woman she had just murdered in the study—the Cyrus Thornbury who could not lie—and then she knelt at the loose brick, and began to work it free.
