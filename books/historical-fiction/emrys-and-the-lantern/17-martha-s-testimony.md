## 17. Martha's Testimony

The road to the Quill cottage ran along the fen's edge, where the land gave itself over to reeds and the pale stubble of cut rushes. Frost had come in the night and not yet lifted; every fence post wore a rind of white, and the ditches lay still as poured pewter. I walked with my collar up and my ledger against my ribs, wrapped in oilcloth as it always was now, and I confess I walked slowly—not from the cold, though the cold was honest enough, but because I was rehearsing a lie.

Or rather, rehearsing the shape of a promise that had no honest way of being kept.

Martha Quill lived at the end of that road in a cottage no larger than a hayrick, its thatch patched with sacking, its garden rows turned and sleeping under their blanket of frost-killed straw. Smoke rose thin and grey from the chimney. A goat regarded me from behind a hurdle with the flat yellow indifference of its kind. I had been here once before, three weeks past, when I had gone to ask what Colonel Vane wanted with *the Thornbury woman*, and I remembered how she had stood in her doorway with her hands folded like a woman waiting for bad news she had long since learned to expect on schedule.

I knocked. The door opened before my knuckles fell a second time, which told me she had seen me coming down the lane—there was little else to watch on that road but crows and weather—and that she had decided, in the interval, whether or not to let me in.

"Cyrus Thornbury," she said. Not a greeting. A confirmation, as though she were checking me against some description she'd been given.

"Mrs. Quill."

"You've come again." She stepped back anyway, and I followed her inside.

* * *

The cottage interior was clean the way only poor and proud houses are clean: everything scrubbed to bareness, nothing spare, the single room arranged with a soldier's economy. A fire in the small grate. A table scrubbed white. On the wall above the hearth hung the one indulgence—a framed sampler, faded rose-pink wool on linen, reading *God Is Our Refuge and Our Strength*, stitched in a careful child's hand. Beside it, smaller, hung a portrait miniature in a cracked case: a young man with sandy whiskers and a look of patient good humour, painted by someone who had worked cheaply and quickly and gotten the mouth right but not the eyes.

I knew those eyes better than the painter had. Sergeant Daniel Quill had died at the age of thirty-one in a dressing station outside Balaclava, his hand in mine, while Captain—by then, Major—Vane rode away from the position he should never have abandoned and toward the commendation he had not earned. The maneuver that saved two hundred men that night had been Quill's: the repositioning of the supply waggons through the ravine under covering dark, an improvisation no officer had ordered and none would claim. Vane claimed it. Dispatches recorded it under his signature. Daniel Quill entered history as *N.C.O., name unrecorded*, and I entered my own ledger on the strength of that omission, writing his name in full on the first page with ink I borrowed from the surgeon's desk.

That was seven years ago. Seven years, and here sat his widow in a patched-thatch cottage, pouring tea into cups that did not match because matching cups cost money.

"Sit," she said. "You'll take tea. It's only bread with it today; there's butter when the market's kinder."

I took the chair opposite hers. "You needn't feed me, Mrs. Quill."

"I know what I needn't do." Her voice was mild but it had iron under it; I remembered that from our first meeting too. "Sit and drink your tea like a Christian."

So we sat and drank tea like Christians, and spoke of small things—the frost, the goat, the price of coal—while I searched for the door into what I had come to say. She let me search. She was a woman who understood that people came to her door carrying things they needed help setting down, and she had the patience of all those who have buried someone and must now outlast everything else.

Finally I set down my cup. "Mrs. Quill. I'm going to ask you something, and before I ask it I want you to understand that you may refuse me, and refuse me freely, and I will never think less of you for it. I want you to know that first, because what comes after isn't fair."

She folded her hands on the scrubbed table, exactly as she'd done in her doorway. "Say it."

"The magistrate's hearing is coming. Against Lord Halloran's enemies—the true conspirators. There will be testimony given, and sworn statements taken, and I intend to put before the court certain records I've kept. Records concerning the Crimea. Concerning supply transactions that were falsified, and credit that was stolen, and men who were written out of accounts they had earned their place in." I drew breath. "Your husband's name is among them."

Something moved behind her face—quick, suppressed, like a bird striking a window from within.

"I am asking," I said, "whether you would be willing to swear to what you saw and heard in the year before he died. You were there. You kept the mess accounts; you copied the requisitions. You told me once that you saw the original of the ravine order in Sergeant Quill's own hand, before it vanished into dispatches wearing another man's signature. If you swore to that—if you stood in a courtroom and said it—it would count for more than all my pages together. My book can be called a forgery. Your memory cannot."

The fire ticked in the grate. Outside, the goat said something contemptuous to the frost.

"And if I refuse?" she asked quietly.

"Then you refuse. Nothing follows. No one learns from me what you know or where you are."

"And if I agree?"

And there it was: the question I had walked three miles rehearsing, and for which I had found no honest answer.

* * *

I want to set down plainly what happened in my chest in that moment, because it is the thing I am least proud of in this whole affair, prouder though I may later be of what stood beside it.

If Martha Quill testified, she would make herself visible to Colonel Ambrose Vane. Everything I knew of him—seven years of knowing him, first at a distance across a dressing station, then closer through paper and rumour—told me what visibility meant to such a man. He had already visited her once, in this very cottage, asking after *the Thornbury woman*. That visit had been polite. Vane's visits were always polite. But politeness from a man like that is a surveyor's work: he measures the ground before deciding where to build. And Finch—who had written the letter that started all of this, who had confessed to Emrys that the accusation against the Hallorans was dictated to him—Finch had fallen from a London bridge into black February water, and the coroner's verdict had been delivered with a straight face, and everyone who mattered had nodded and moved on.

If she testified, they might come for her the way they had come for Finch. Or they might come slower: the rent raised, the charity withdrawn, the parish officers suddenly attentive to the condition of her cottage, the goat impounded for straying. Ruin has many doors and all of them open politely.

Could I guarantee her safety? Could I stand in her kitchen, looking at the sampler her daughter had stitched before the fever took the girl two winters after her father died—I learned that on my first visit; there was a second frame beneath the bed, she'd shown me, wrapped in cloth—and say to her, *yes, Mrs. Quill, if you stand up in an open courtroom and put your dead husband's stolen honour back into the light, I will keep you from harm*?

No man could honestly say that. No woman either. I could not even guarantee my own safety; there was, by then, very likely a warrant being drawn somewhere with my name on it, for Vane had threatened precisely that—forgery of army records, a hanging matter, or near enough. To promise Martha Quill protection I did not possess, could not possess, from an enemy who killed at a distance through other people's hands—that was not protection. It was recruitment dressed in kinder clothes.

And yet.

I looked at the miniature above the hearth, with its patient painted smile, and I thought: *she has carried him for seven years in a cracked frame on a wall in a fen cottage, and no one has ever once said his name aloud to her in company.* I thought about what names weigh, and who bears them, and how long.

"Mrs. Quill," I said, "if you agree, I will tell you honestly what I can and cannot do. I owe you that much before anything else."

Her eyebrows rose slightly. Whatever answer she had braced for, it was not this.

"I cannot guarantee your safety," I said. "I won't pretend otherwise. The men we would be standing against have money and office and no scruple about how either is used. They have already silenced one witness—I believe you know to whom I refer. If you testify, you will be at risk. Real risk, of the kind I cannot ward off, because I am a clerk with a ledger and no authority anywhere, and the law that ought to shield you is the very instrument they hold. Any person who tells you different is lying to recruit you."

The fire ticked. She did not move.

"What I *can* do is this. I can tell you the whole truth of the danger before you choose, and let you choose with open eyes—which is more than anyone gave your husband when he was ordered into a ravine by a man who then stole the ordering of it. I can place your testimony so it does the most damage in the least exposure—one appearance, sworn statement lodged with the magistrate beforehand, witnesses to the swearing. I can see that whatever small means exist for your keeping flow to you first and fastest. And I can promise—" and here my voice did something I had not planned; it went rough, and I let it, because roughness is the only honesty left when honesty runs out of guarantees— "I can promise that you will not stand alone in that room. Whatever happens after, you will not be the only name spoken. I will be beside you, and my book beside me, and if they take one of us they must account for both."

Silence, then. Long enough that I heard the wind change among the reeds outside, and the goat complain again, and somewhere far off the fen pump working its slow iron heart.

Martha Quill looked at the miniature above her hearth for a while. Then she looked at me, and her eyes—Daniel's eyes, the ones the cheap painter hadn't gotten right, because no painter gets that kind of patience right—were dry and very steady.

"You're telling me you can't protect me," she said.

"Yes."

"That they might ruin me, or worse, and that you can't stop it."

"Yes."

"That you'll stand beside me while it happens, if it happens."

"If I'm not already taken," I said. "They're likely to take me first."

To my astonishment she smiled then—a small, crooked, tired thing, but a true smile, and I saw in it the ghost of the girl who had married a sergeant for love against her mother's counsel, and followed the drum, and kept the mess accounts in a tent through a winter of mud and typhus because someone had to and she was the someone.

"Well," she said. "That's the most honest offer anyone's made me since Daniel died. Everyone else promises safety and delivers nothing but the promise. You promise nothing and deliver yourself. I'll swear it, Cyrus Thornbury. I'll swear what I saw. God help us both, but I'll swear it."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon, as much as could be worked without pen and oath. I asked, and she answered, and I committed her answers to memory rather than paper—paper could be stolen, burned, torn from walls; memory travelled lighter and could not be seized without seizing the woman herself, which was precisely what we hoped to forestall by lodging her sworn statement early.

She had joined Daniel in the Crimea in the spring of '55, one of the army wives permitted to follow; she had nursed alongside the paid nurses and the sisters, and kept the company's small books, and so had handled documents that never reached an officer's eye unless it suited them. She described the requisition ledgers: the columns in which grain that had been issued appeared as sold, the boots that arrived rotten and were invoiced as sound, the sums that flowed upward into accounts nobody named. She described the evening Daniel had come to her tent with a copy of the ravine order in his own hand—he was a careful man, she said, careful the way soldiers become when they learn that carelessness around paperwork kills faster than cannon—and how he had said, half laughing, *if ever I'm written out of this war, Marty, this bit of paper writes me back in.*

The paper had vanished after his death. His kit was inventoried by a captain's clerk and the document was never listed. She had asked after it twice and been told, both times, that such papers were regimental property and beyond a widow's claim.

"He knew," she said, staring into the grate. "Near the end. Not that night—he wasn't lucid—but days before, he said to me, *Vane's counting on me dying quiet, Marty. Men who steal from the dead count on the dead staying dead.*" She turned to me. "Well. He didn't stay dead, did he? Not altogether. Here he sits in my kitchen, same as ever, wanting his supper."

I wrote nothing down, and forgot nothing.

It was nearly dusk when we finished, and she fed me bread and butter after all—the market had been kind—and walked me out into the blue cold of the fen evening. The frost had hardened; our breath hung. At the gate she stopped, hands folded again, and said the thing I had been waiting for without knowing I waited:

"You said they might take you first."

"It's possible. Likely, even, if Vane moves before the hearing."

"Then hear me." Her voice dropped low and even. "When my Daniel lay dying, a nurse sat with him through the last of it so that I, arriving too late from the base hospital, would not find him alone. I never learned that nurse's name. They don't write such names in any book. But I have wondered about her for seven years, and I have thought, sometimes, that if ever she came to my door needing anything—a crust, a coat, a lie told for her, a truth—I would pay a seven-year debt without asking the sum." She looked at me directly. "It was you. I knew it the moment you stood in my doorway last month. Don't ask me how I knew. Wives know."

I had not known that. I had sat with many dying men, and held many hands, and I had never known which hand belonged to whom afterward; invisibility was my method and my shelter, and I had believed it total. It seems nothing is total. It seems that even the unseen leave prints somewhere, in someone's memory, waiting.

"So," said Martha Quill, opening the gate for me, "you'll not be lecturing me about risk, Cyrus Thornbury, as if I hadn't already buried everything risk could take. I've stood at gravesides twice—husband and child—and the ground is ground, however you arrive at it. What I couldn't survive is standing idle again. Swear your promises or don't; I've accepted worse terms from better liars. Just come back for me when the day comes, and we'll walk into that courthouse side by side, and between us we'll say Daniel Quill's name so loud that Vane hears it in whatever fine room he's drinking in."

I took her hand. Hers was small and hard as knotwood.

"I'll come back," I said. "That I can promise absolutely, because it depends on no one but me."

"Do," she said. "And Miss Thornbury—when you write his name in your book again, write the date he was born too. The fourth of March, 1824, at Walsall. Nobody's written it anywhere these seven years but me."

* * *

I walked back along the fen road in full dark, the lantern of the hall showing far off across the fields like a low star, and the whole way I conducted an argument with myself that ended in defeat for both sides.

For I had promised her nothing, and in promising nothing I had promised everything: myself, my presence, my body placed between her and whatever came. If the warrant for my arrest came before the hearing—as Vane intended, I did not doubt—then my promise would break on the first stone of it. She would walk into that courtroom alone after all, or not at all, and the difference between recruiting her and betraying her would turn out to be a matter of days and luck, not principle. Every honest bone in me knew this. And every honest bone in me also knew that I would do it again, and had already done it, and would keep doing it, because the alternative was to leave the forgotten unforgotten-but-unavenged forever, and there is a word for a person who protects herself by leaving everyone else unprotected, and I refused—refuse still—to be that word.

Emrys had said something to me once, on the night we kept watch together over the workshop, copying pages while the storm tried the shutters: *the truth doesn't defend itself; it just lies there until somebody picks it up and bleeds for it.* I had thought it a young man's dramatics then. Walking the frozen fen with Martha Quill's promise in my keeping and my own conscience doing double duty as executioner, I understood it as simple arithmetic. Truth plus testimony equals justice. Testimony costs blood. Someone pays. I had simply spent seven years hoping the invoice would find another address.

It had found mine.

The hall's lights grew as I came up the drive, and I saw that one window on the ground floor burned brighter than the rest—the study, and a figure moving behind the glass that could only be Emrys, pacing, pausing, pacing again. Waiting up. Something in his posture, even at that distance, told me the news was not good news; insomniacs learn to read each other's silhouettes the way sailors read weather.

He met me in the entrance hall before I had my cloak fully off, lamplight behind him, and his face confirmed what the window had hinted: the colour drained from it, the jaw set hard.

"Cyrus," he said. "There's a rider just come from London. It's about the charges against you."
