## 18. Fire in the Outbuildings

The smell woke him before the bell.

Emrys Halloran had spent enough evenings in the workshop to know every odour of the estate after dark—linseed oil and cold iron, the wet-wool sweetness of the stable, the tallow-and-char ghost of his own lamp. So when a new note threaded through his sleep, thin and acrid, like hair singed close to the scalp, his body understood it before his mind did. He was out of bed with his boots unlaced, dragging on his coat, and only in the doorway, seeing the orange bloom against the study curtains' edge, did the word arrive: *fire*.

The stables were burning.

He crossed the yard at a run, frost-slick cobbles threatening to take his legs from under him, and the heat met him halfway—a wall of it rolling off the west end of the building where the records loft sat above the stalls. The old account room. His grandfather's ledgers. Every lease, every bill of sale, every receipt going back sixty years, stacked in oaken chests that now fed the flames like they had been waiting all their lives to burn.

The bell in the clock tower was ringing—Hobbs, the under-groom, hauling the rope with both arms, its clangour tumbling over the sleeping grounds. Servants spilled from their quarters in nightshirts and greatcoats, buckets already moving down the line from the pump, hands passing hands in the old rhythm of estate catastrophe. Emrys seized a bucket and joined the chain without thinking, water slopping ice against his wrists, and all the while he stared past the labour at the loft window glowing like the eye of a furnace.

"It's gone up too fast," Hobbs gasped between pulls when Emrys reached him. "Too fast, Mr. Emrys. A hay fire smoulders first—you smell it an hour before you see it. This went up like gunpowder."

"Like what?"

"Like something was *in* it, sir. Pardon me saying." The groom's face was soot-streaked, his eyes wide and white in the firelight. "There were men about the yard past midnight. I heard 'em talking low by the gate, thought it was Mr. Danvers come home late. It weren't Danvers."

Emrys handed off his empty bucket and stood a moment in the roaring cold, watching the roof timbers begin to sag. Too fast. Of course too fast. Spirits, or pitch, or both—the workmen who had come sniffing at the workshop last month, the same quiet menace wearing different coats. First the record room in the house, saved by luck and a stone floor. Now this.

They were not trying to frighten anyone. They were deleting something.

"The accounts," he said. "The wartime accounts—where did my father keep them after the record room fire?"

Hobbs shrugged helplessly. "Moved things about, sir. I couldn't say."

But Emrys could guess, and the guessing turned his stomach. After the first fire his father had shifted everything he considered vital into the one place no one would think to search for financial papers: the stables themselves, tucked into a locked chest beneath the tack room floor, because who burns a record twice in the same week? Who imagines a man would move his secrets into a barn?

Emrys looked at the flames and knew, with sick certainty, exactly which chest was feeding them now.

* * *

By three in the morning the fire was beaten down to a smoking skeleton. The west wing of the stable had collapsed into its own cellar of char; the east end, the horses long since led out to the paddock, stood blackened but upright. Snow began falling softly into the wreckage, hissing where it landed, and the servants stood about in a weary ring, steaming like cattle, no one willing to be the first to go back to bed.

And then, cutting across the park at speed from the direction of the road, a figure.

Emrys saw him first from the corner of his eye—a man running bent low along the hedgerow, not toward the fire as everyone else came, but *past* it, angling for the gap in the wall where the bridle path let out onto the turnpike. He moved like a man with payment waiting at the end of his errand.

"Hoy!" Emrys shouted, and broke from the line.

The man glanced back once. Firelight caught him briefly—a hard-boned fellow in a dark coat, cap pulled low—and then he was through the gap and gone, footsteps fading up the frozen lane toward the village. Toward the coaching road. Toward, Emrys would have wagered his last shilling, the lodgings Colonel Vane kept at the George while he conducted his leisurely inspection of the district's accounts.

Danvers came puffing up beside him, the estate's head man, breathing smoke and fury. "I know that one," he said grimly. "Seen him about since Vane's party arrived. Stood at the bar of the George two nights back, drinking with the colonel's man Finch—no—" he corrected himself, frowning, "not Finch. The other one. Quiet fellow, pays in guineas, asks after the family."

"Asks after us."

"Asks after the *papers*, Mr. Emrys. That's what I heard said, before I left off listening."

Emrys stood in the falling snow, hands blistered, throat raw, and felt the shape of the thing settle over him at last. Not misfortune. Not debt collectors' spite. Architecture. A design, drafted in London, executed by hired hands, aimed with precision at paper—always at paper. And behind the design, patient and smiling at dinner tables, a colonel of the British Army who wanted something in these records never to see daylight again.

Behind him, a door slammed. His father had come out at last.

Lord Halloran crossed the yard slowly, dressing-gown under a heavy coat, grey-faced in the fire's dying light. He did not look at the ruin. He did not look at the servants. He walked to the collapsed west wing, stopped at the rope barrier of steaming timber, and stood there so long that the household began, one by one, to drift away and leave him alone with it.

Except his son.

"I know what was in that chest," Emrys said quietly.

His father did not move. "Then you know more than I wished anyone to know."

"The wartime supply accounts. Vane's transactions. Whatever you took out of the house after the first fire."

Stillness. Then Lord Halloran's shoulders—those shoulders that had carried the estate through his mother's death and two wars and seven lean years—dropped, all at once, like a structure finally admitting what the fire had done to its timbers.

"Yes," he said.

* * *

They did not speak in the yard. His father walked back to the house with a gait Emrys had never seen in him—not the brisk stride of the magistrate's guest and county notable, but the small, careful steps of a very tired man crossing ice he no longer trusted. In the study the embers of yesterday's fire had gone to ash, and Lord Halloran did not ring for more coal. He sat behind his desk without lighting a lamp, so that Emrys stood over him in the dark, snow melting off his boots onto the carpet, and waited.

"There is a chest missing," his father said at last, to the desk rather than to him. "From the tack room floor. It was gone when we broke open the ruins. Either it burned entirely—unlikely; there'd be iron fittings left—or someone took it out ahead of the flames."

"You think your own fire was set to cover a theft?"

"I think," his father said, "that Colonel Vane now has, or soon will have, whatever those papers showed about our dealings. And that he will use them however suits him best." A pause. The wind found the broken window of some distant outbuilding and moaned through it. "You are twenty-four years old, Emrys, and you have asked me three times this winter why a colonel takes such interest in a country estate's finances. I have lied to you twice and deflected you once. I find I have no appetite left for any of it."

Emrys pulled a chair around and sat facing him. "Tell me."

The confession, when it came, came flatly, without excuse—a recitation learned in sleepless nights and delivered now like a man reading his own indictment.

"It began in 'eight, the year after Waterloo. You remember the state of us. Your grandfather's debts, the mortgages, the settlements due on your sister's marriage. I went up to London to arrange another loan, and I was introduced—to whom it hardly matters—to a group of men who had grown rich on army contracts and found peace inconvenient to their incomes. Vane was their creature then, not yet their master. Quartermaster contracts. Commissariat supplies. Names on paper, goods that existed mostly on paper, invoices passed back and forth until the money had been washed white and divided."

"And my name—"

"Your name was on two of the partnerships. Yours and your brother's." Their eyes met in the dark, and Emrys felt the words land somewhere beneath his ribs. "It protected me. Sons of a peer make convenient signatories; no one examines a boy of nineteen for a transaction dated when he was at school. I told myself it was nothing—that you'd never be called to account for paper you never touched. I told myself a great many things."

Emrys sat very still. Outside, faintly, the last of the stable bell was still tolling, Hobbs unable somehow to stop.

"The accounts Cyrus keeps—" he began.

"Are more honest than any I paid for," his father said, with the ghost of something that was almost a smile and was mostly grief. "Yes. I've known for years that she knows. Or suspects. She has that way of knowing things without ever saying them—did you never wonder why I keep a battlefield nurse as clerk of the workshop, a woman who could have her pick of positions? Part guilt. Part fear. She was at Balaclava, Emrys. She saw where the money went and who it never reached. If she ever wrote down everything she saw..." He trailed off.

"She has written it down."

Lord Halloran raised his head. "What?"

"I can't say how I know. But she has. Seven years of it, I believe. Every omitted name, every transaction that didn't add." Emrys watched his father's face in the ember-light and made his choice with his eyes open. "And I won't help you take it from her."

For a moment something moved across the old man's features—anger, perhaps, or the reflex of a lifetime's authority—and then it collapsed into something worse: relief. A terrible, naked relief, as though his son had just taken from him a weight he had lacked the strength to put down himself.

"No," his father said quietly. "No, I don't suppose you will." He pressed both hands flat on the desk, as if to steady himself against a swell. "Do you understand what comes now? Vane has the papers or will have them. He has witnesses he owns. He can ruin us by spring without breaking his stride—and he will enjoy it. Everything I did, I did to preserve this house, and I have delivered it instead to a man who will pull it down stone by stone and call it justice."

"Unless we give him something else to look at. Unless there's proof of what he did that isn't ours to lose."

His father looked at him then, really looked, and for the first time in Emrys's memory there was no rank in it—only a father and a son in a dark room, measuring each other across a gulf of silence seven years wide.

"You speak as though you have such proof."

"I speak as though I intend to find it."

Lord Halloran nodded slowly, and turned his face away toward the black window, where snow was still falling on the ashes of the stables. When he spoke again his voice was so low Emrys had to lean forward to catch it.

"When I took those partnership papers out of the house after the first fire—there was one document among them that wasn't mine. A letter, unsigned, in a hand I didn't know, setting out terms. Terms for framing a man. For making a scapegoat of some officer whose name meant nothing to me." His hands tightened on the desk's edge. "I told myself it was none of my affair. I filed it with the rest and burned nothing and asked nothing, because asking might have cost me my arrangement. That letter, Emrys—it named the method. Dates, postings, the movement of funds. If Vane wrote it, then somewhere there is a counterpart. An original. Something in his hand."

Emrys felt the hair rise along his arms. "Father. Whose name was on that letter's scheme?"

His father was silent a long moment. The fire's last breath moved the curtain.

"A captain," he said at last. "A dead sergeant. And a nurse who saw too much."

Emrys rose. Somewhere across the frozen park, in a workshop lit past midnight, that nurse was keeping her vigil—and now he knew, beyond all doubting, why she wrote in the dark, and what the dark had been trying to bury.

"We're past choosing sides," he said. "We're only choosing which truth survives us."

And he went out into the snow, toward the light still burning in the workshop window.
