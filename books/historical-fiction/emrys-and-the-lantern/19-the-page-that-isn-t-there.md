## 19. The Page That Isn't There

The candle had burned to a stub when Cyrus returned to her room, and the first wrongness she noticed was the smell.

Not smoke, not tallow, not the lavender she tucked among her linens against damp—something else. Something faint and metallic, like a coin held too long in the mouth. She stood in the doorway with her hand still on the latch and let the room speak to her the way a sickroom speaks to a nurse: not in words, but in the arrangement of small things that had shifted by an inch, or a breath, or a deliberate hand.

Her shawl lay over the chair back at an angle she had not left it in. The angle mattered; she had folded it wet-weather inward for seven years and never once thought about it until this moment, when it faced the window instead of the door. Her writing box sat square on the desk where she always left it askew, canted toward the light, because a clerk learns early which objects invite attention and which deflect it. Someone had squared it. Someone had wanted it tidy—or had wanted her to think no one had touched it at all.

Cyrus did not move. She counted her own heartbeats the way she counted a fever patient's pulse: steadily, without alarm, because alarm was a luxury that got people killed. Outside, the January wind worried the shutters of the east range, and somewhere below, in the stable yard, a dog turned three times and settled. The house breathed around her. The house had been breathing around her all evening while another pair of hands opened her things.

She crossed to the desk and lifted the lid of the writing box with two fingers, as if it might be hot.

The ledger was there. Of course it was there—she had locked it, and the lock was cheap, and any nail knife in this house could have persuaded it. The ledger lay on top of her spare spectacles and the bundle of letters tied in ribbon, exactly as she had left it, except—

Except it was thinner.

She knew its weight the way a mother knows the weight of a sleeping child. Seven years of carrying it from garret to garret, from hospital tent to servants' attic, wrapped in oilcloth through two Channel crossings and one winter in a baggage wagon outside Balaclava. She knew how it sat in the hand. And it sat wrong now. Lighter. Offended somehow, as if the book itself knew it had been diminished.

She set it on the desk beneath the candle and opened it with the care of a woman opening a wound.

The pages fell open naturally to the middle years—Crimea, the winter entries, her hand younger and harder pressed into the paper. She turned backward, entry by entry, name by name, and told herself the whole way that she was mistaken, that grief and fear made arithmetic liars of everyone, that the book could not be lighter because books do not bleed.

Then she reached the spring of 1811 and found the gap.

Three leaves. Torn close to the binding, but torn hurriedly near the bottom—a ragged triangle of paper still clinging to the gutter like a shred of skin. She knew what those pages had held. She had written them by the light of a shielded lamp in the loft above the old stables, her breath fogging, her fingers stiff around the pen: three transactions, dated and witnessed, involving army supply contracts routed through a merchant house in Canterbury. Three entries in which the name *Vane* appeared—not accused, not editorialized, only recorded, the way she recorded everything, with dates and figures and the flat fidelity of a mirror. Entries that proved a colonel had moved government money through ghost invoices before he was ever a colonel, before anyone thought to watch him.

Entries that would hang him.

And someone had taken them.

* * *

She did not cry out. She had learned in Crimea that crying out gathers listeners, and listeners gather conclusions, and conclusions in a house like this one travel faster than truth. Instead she stood very still in the cold center of her room and let the paranoia come, because it was going to come whether she invited it or not, and it was better to examine it than to be examined by it.

Only three people had access.

That was the arithmetic, and it was worse than any wound she had dressed. The east attic corridor was reached by one stair, past Mrs. Rook the housekeeper's room—and Mrs. Rook slept like the dead and rose before dawn to a routine so fixed that her absence from it would have rung through the house like a dropped tray. So: Mrs. Rook, unlikely. Which left the servants' side door, unlocked since the storm broke the latch in November. Which left hands that knew this house well enough to climb a dark stair without waking a floorboard's particular voice.

Lord Halloran. Emrys. And herself.

Herself, dismissed. She had been in the dining room until midnight, mending table linen under Mrs. Rook's eye, under six witnesses' eyes. Unless six witnesses lied, unless she had climbed her own stair in some other hour of some other day and torn out her own testimony—but no. She was many things, and tired was all of them, but she was not a woman who forgot the state of her own book.

Which left father or son.

Or someone either of them had admitted past the usual walls. Vane's people came and went now like weather. The inspector kept a man in the village; she had seen him twice at the lane's end, hatless in the cold, watching the chimneys smoke. Perhaps the stair had carried stranger feet than hers. Perhaps the latchless door had done its quiet work. But the thought did not comfort her, because the torn pages were not random theft. Whoever had entered had known *which* pages. Out of seven years of names and dates and figures, they had cut out precisely the three leaves that named Vane—and nothing else. A servant rummaging for blackmail would have taken more. A son protecting his father would have taken the Halloran debt entries too, and those remained, she checked them twice with shaking fingers, intact as scripture.

No. This was surgical. This was a reader of ledgers. This was a man who had spent his life reading other men's accounts and knew exactly which line undid him.

But Vane had not slept under this roof since the dinner.

Had he?

Cyrus sat down slowly on the edge of her bed, and the mattress complained, and the sound seemed enormous in the little room. She thought of the colonel's hands—dry, white, precise—turning the pages of estate accounts at that terrible dinner, smiling at Lord Halloran across the roast, saying *you keep thorough records, my lord, thorough records are a patriot's duty.* She thought of the half hour Lord Halloran had vanished from his own table, pleading letters from London, and how the conversation downstairs had continued without missing a beat while upstairs something rustled like mice in the walls.

He had gone up. He had gone up during dinner, and the attics were ten steps from the family corridor, and Mrs. Rook had been below supervising the second course.

The knowledge arrived not as suspicion but as recognition, and recognition is the cruelest form of betrayal, because you cannot un-know what you have known all along. Lord Halloran was not a wicked man. That was the whole horror of it. He was a frightened one—a proud, drowning gentleman who owed Colonel Vane more than the estate was worth, who had watched his record room nearly burn and ordered his workshop cleared of paper with a voice that shook, who was now trying to buy his family's survival with whatever currency lay nearest to hand. And the nearest currency was hers. The pages that proved Vane's guilt were also pages that proved Halloran's debts, and a drowning man will tear the ledger out of anyone's hands if the water is high enough at his chin.

She had recorded his debts herself. She had softened them, even—omitted two smaller loans entirely, out of loyalty, out of the old habit of protecting the house that fed her. And this was her repayment: three pages gone, and with them the only independent proof that Vane's current accusations were built on his own older crimes.

Without those pages, her ledger became a weapon pointed the wrong way. Without those pages, every surviving entry read as evidence *against* the Hallorans—their debts, their irregular payments, their names threaded through suspicious sums—while the man who orchestrated it all stood clean, his own history amputated from the record. Whoever tore those leaves had not destroyed evidence. He had *reaimed* it.

She put her hand flat on the book as though it were a patient's forehead.

"I'm sorry," she whispered, and heard how mad it sounded, apologizing to paper. But the words were not for the paper. They were for the sergeant whose maneuver a captain had stolen, for Martha Quill waiting in her poverty for a justice Cyrus was suddenly no longer certain she could deliver, for her brother lying under his nameless stone these eleven years. All of them lived in this book. All of them had just been robbed.

* * *

She did not sleep. Sleep was for people whose rooms kept their secrets.

Somewhere past three she lit a fresh candle, took out needle and thread and the roll of waxed linen she kept for bandaging, and began to sew. Not petticoats this time—she had smuggled pages to London that way once already and would not repeat a trick that had survived only because no one thought to look. Tonight she made a different thing: a flat case of waxed linen, barely thicker than a psalter, sized to the ledger exactly, with loops of waxed thread at the corners. Then she went down the back stair with the candle shielded, through the scullery where the embers breathed their orange ruin, out into the yard where the cold hit her face like open water.

The workshop stood dark at the range's end, its windows blind except one—and even in her fear, even with her heart drumming the old campaign rhythm, she noticed that one window was not quite dark. A lantern glowed behind the glass at the far bench, low and steady, and a shape bent over it that could only be Emrys, still at Balaclava's broken lantern this late, or again at it, keeping his own insomniac vigil a corridor away from hers all these weeks while they circled the same unease like dogs around a bone neither would claim first.

She did not go to him. Not tonight. Tonight she had arithmetic to finish, and the arithmetic said trust no one, and the arithmetic did not yet know what to make of a young man who restored dead men's lamps.

The workshop's outer wall on the north side was stone, double-thick where the old kiln flue had once run, and years ago—her first winter here, stocking wood against a hard frost—she had noticed the cavity behind a loose block near the foundation, dry, dark, deep enough for a book. She pried the block now with the flat of a fire iron, working it the way she worked a splinter from flesh, patiently, feeling the mortar grit give. Behind it the wall exhaled a smell of old lime and dust. She wrapped the ledger in its new linen case, bound it with the waxed thread, and slid it into the dark.

For a moment her hands hovered at the mouth of the cavity, unwilling to withdraw. Seven years the book had slept beside her head. Seven years it had been the last thing she touched at night and the first at waking, closer to her than any living person, and now it would lie alone inside a cold wall with only mortar for company while she walked back to her room and lay down beside its absence.

"You'll be safer there," she told it softly. "Safer than with me."

It was true, and it was also a confession. Because the wall was only stone, and stone can be broken, and the real question—the question that had followed her down the stairs like footsteps—was not where to hide the book but whom she could no longer trust to stand between it and the men who wanted it. She had spent seven years invisible, and invisibility had been armor. But armor only works while the war stays outside the walls. The war was inside now. It had climbed her stairs in soft shoes during a dinner party, and it had used her own lock's weakness, and it wore, very likely, the tired, honorable face of the master of the house.

She seated the block back into its socket, swept the mortar dust with the side of her boot, and looked up once at the workshop window.

The lantern-glow beyond the glass had not moved. If Emrys saw her—a clerk crossing the yard at four in the morning with a fire iron—he would ask questions she was not ready to answer. She raised no light of her own. She simply stood in the blue-black cold and watched his shadow bend over his work, and felt the full weight of the choice ahead settle onto her shoulders like a pack: to protect the house, or to protect the truth, knowing the house had already chosen to protect itself.

Tomorrow she would need to know which pages Lord Halloran took, and why only those, and whether he acted from panic or instruction—and whether the word *instruction* led, as it led everywhere now, back to Colonel Vane.

Tomorrow she would begin watching the watchers.

She turned toward her stair, and behind her, in the workshop window, the lantern flame dipped once—as if the house itself had drawn breath—as Emrys's shadow lifted, and turned, and looked directly out at the yard where she stood.
