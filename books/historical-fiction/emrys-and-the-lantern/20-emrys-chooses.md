## 20. Emrys Chooses

The house had a different sound at three in the morning, and Emrys had learned to read it the way a sailor reads weather.

By day, Halloran Court creaked and muttered with the business of living—servants' feet on the back stairs, the kitchen's clatter rolling up through the floorboards like distant surf, his father's voice somewhere behind a door, always behind a door now, pitched low in conversation with men whose names were never offered. But in the small hours the house went quiet in a way that was not peace. It was the quiet of held breath. Of a thing listening for itself.

Emrys moved through it with his boots in his hand.

He had told himself a dozen stories on the way down from his room—thirst, restlessness, the need to check the workshop stove—and he had let each story go as quickly as it came. There was no use lying to himself in the dark. He was going to his father's study because he could not stop thinking about the pages that were missing from Cyrus's ledger, and because some instinct older than courtesy had been whispering to him for two days that his father knew where they were.

The corridor outside the study smelled of last night's fire, banked low, and beneath it the faint sweetness of the brandy his father had taken to drinking in quantities Emrys preferred not to measure. A sliver of light lay along the floorboards under the door.

His heart stopped, then started again, harder.

Light. At three in the morning. His father was awake—or had left the lamp burning when he finally surrendered to sleep, which would be its own kind of answer.

Emrys stood in the dark with his stockinged feet cold against the wood and made himself breathe. He thought of Cyrus in the lantern-light of the workshop, her voice steady as a plumb line: *Someone has been in my room. Only three people had access.* Himself. The maid-of-all-work, Tilly, who could barely reach the high shelf where the ledger lived. And Lord Halloran, who had keys to every door in this house and had stopped looking his son in the eye since the second fire.

*He wouldn't,* Emrys had told her then, and believed it, because believing it was easier than the alternative.

But belief was a luxury the house no longer sold.

He turned the handle slowly. The door gave without a sound—oiled recently, he noted, another small strangeness in a house full of them—and he slipped inside.

* * *

The study was not as he remembered it from childhood.

That study had been a bright room once, or bright by the standards of his father's taste: the tall window onto the park, the globe with its continents worn to brass at the equator where young Emrys had spun it and dreamed of anywhere but here. Now the curtains hung drawn even against the moonless night, and the globe stood in the corner like an old dog banished from the hearth. Papers covered the desk in drifts—not the tidy correspondence of an estate managed well, but the chaotic sediment of an estate managed desperately. Bills. Dunning letters. A lawyer's card laid face-down like a card thrown down in anger.

And there, in the wing chair beside the dead fire, sat his father.

Asleep. Or near enough. Lord Halloran's head had sunk to his chest, his dressing gown open at the throat, one hand still curled around a glass that had surrendered its last inch to evaporation. The lamp on the desk burned low and yellow, and in its light Emrys saw his father properly for the first time in months—not the brittle patriarch barking orders about demolition schedules, not the smooth host performing confidence for Colonel Vane, but something smaller. An old man sitting up late with his ruin, the way a farmer sits up with a sick animal, unwilling to leave it and unable to help it.

Emrys felt the sight of him like a blow under the ribs. Love and fury arrived together, as they always did now, indistinguishable in the dark.

He almost turned back. That was the truth he would carry afterward: he almost turned back. His hand was actually raised—to touch his father's shoulder, to wake him, to say *Papa, tell me what is happening, tell me all of it*—when his eye caught the gleam of leather on the desk's far corner.

A folio. Small, dark, bound in the plain calf that stationers sold by the dozen. It did not belong on that desk among the bills and lawyers' cards. It belonged, he knew with sick certainty, in a wall cavity in the lantern workshop, wrapped in oilcloth, kept by a woman who trusted exactly no one.

Emrys crossed the room without deciding to. His body made the choice his conscience had been circling for days.

The folio was not locked, not tied, only closed. Inside, in Cyrus's small, upright, relentless hand, were pages he had seen before and pages he had not—the entries she had shown him by lantern light during their long nights of copying, and others she had kept back, and he understood now why she had kept them back, because these were worse. These were names he knew. Supply contracts dated seven years past, figures copied in a clerk's fair script, marginal notes in Cyrus's tighter hand: *payment received against goods never delivered — signature matches*. And beneath one entry, underlined twice, a name that stopped his breath:

*Halloran, E. — countersigned.*

Not his own initials. His grandfather's. But the name was the name. The family name, threaded through Vane's early schemes like a stitch through cloth, and if you pulled it—if Vane pulled it—the whole garment would come apart, and everyone wearing it would stand naked in a courtroom.

His father stirred in the chair. Emrys froze, folio half-open, a thief caught by nothing but his own heartbeat.

"No," Lord Halloran murmured, to no one, to someone long gone or not yet come. "No, I can explain the discrepancy. If you will simply—" His voice trailed into breathing. Even asleep he was negotiating. Even asleep he was bargaining.

And there it was, the whole shape of it, laid bare in his father's unconscious muttering: Lord Halloran had taken the pages not to destroy them. Destroying them would have been simpler, safer, and his father was many things now but never simple where risk could be traded. He had taken them because they were *leverage*. Because a man drowning will grab at anything, even the hand that pushed him. He meant to sit across a table from Colonel Vane with these pages between them and say, in effect: *burn my debts, and my family's name stays buried with yours.*

It might even work. That was the terrible part. Vane was vulnerable too; the ledger touched them both. Two conspirators, each holding a knife at the other's throat, might agree to stand very still.

And Cyrus would hang for it. Not literally—her offense was forgery of army records, and the gallows for that was unlikely, though unlikely was not impossible—but she would be ruined, transported perhaps, her name dragged through whatever mud Vane chose to pour, while the Hallorans bought back their respectability with pages stolen out of her room while she slept.

Emrys stood in the lamplight holding the evidence of everything, and felt the two halves of his life pull apart like ice on a thawing river.

* * *

He had loved his father without complication for twenty-three years. That was worth saying honestly, because honesty was about to become expensive. As a boy he had thought Gerald Halloran the finest man in England—had watched him settle disputes among tenants with patient good humor, had seen him ride out in the worst weather of the year to sit with a dying shepherd because no one else would, and had built his whole idea of what a man should be out of those materials. Even the war years, even the debts that had begun to show at the seams like straining canvas—even those he had forgiven, because the war forgave nothing and no one, and his father had come home from it diminished and had still, every day, tried.

But trying was not the same as being good, and Emrys was beginning to understand that the difference between them was the entire distance he now found himself crossing, folio in hand, from the sleeping man in the chair toward the door.

Because here was the arithmetic, brutal and simple: if he put the folio back and walked away, he preserved his father's chance—slim, squalid, but real—of buying peace. And he condemned Cyrus to be the price of it. She would take the fall without complaint; that was her way, invisibility unto death. She would go wherever they sent her and start a new ledger on whatever paper she could beg, and she would never say a word against any Halloran, and she would be right not to, and the injustice of it would sit in Emrys's chest for the rest of his life like a swallowed coal.

Or he could do what he was already doing. Could carry the pages back to her and let his father wake to find his last bargaining chip gone, his position collapsed, his choices narrowed to confession or destruction. Could choose, in effect, a woman who had served his family seven faithful years over the blood that had made him.

It did not feel like a choice. That frightened him most of all. He had expected the moment of decision to arrive with trumpets, some clean crisis of conscience he could point to afterward and say *there, that was when I became someone new.* Instead it felt like noticing something that had been true for a long time. Truth over blood. As if the words had been waiting in him all along, patient as seeds, needing only this dark room and his father's broken muttering to put out roots.

He closed the folio softly. Then, because he was not yet a complete traitor, because love does not switch off like a lamp, he crossed to the wing chair and looked down at his father's face for a long moment—the deepened lines, the grey stubble, the glass loose in his fingers—and eased the glass free and set it on the desk where it could not spill or cut him. He fetched the blanket from the chest beneath the window and laid it over his father's knees, the way you cover someone you are leaving, or betraying, or both.

"Papa," he whispered, though the man could not hear him. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But she didn't make this mess, and I won't let her pay for it."

Lord Halloran's face worked once, as if the words had reached him underwater, and settled again into sleep.

Emrys took the folio and went out, drawing the door shut behind him with the same soundless care, and stood a moment in the corridor letting his eyes adjust to the greater dark. Somewhere below, the house ticked and settled. Somewhere beyond the park, past the frozen gardens and the black line of the fen, London existed, where Vane was awake too, no doubt, in some warm club, moving pieces on a board only he could see entirely. Let him look. Let him search. From tonight there would be one piece fewer where he expected to find it.

* * *

The path to the workshop was two hundred yards of gravel and frost-killed grass, and Emrys walked it with his shoes in one hand and the folio buttoned inside his coat, feeling absurd and mortal and more awake than he had been since the first fire. The cold went through his stockings and set his feet aching, then numb, and he barely noticed. Above him the sky had cleared to that hard winter black salted with stars, and the whole estate lay silvered and silent, beautiful the way a battlefield is sometimes beautiful afterward, when the smoke has gone and the cost has not yet been counted.

The workshop door was locked, as agreed. He knocked the pattern they had chosen—two, pause, three—and waited, breath smoking, until the bolt drew back and Cyrus stood in the gap with the rebuilt lantern held high, its flame steadying as the door opened wide enough to admit the air.

She looked at his face, then at his coat, where the folio's corner showed above the buttonhole, and something in her expression closed and opened at the same time—a flinch and a settling, grief and relief arriving in the same instant.

"You found them," she said.

"My father has them." He stepped inside, and she shut the world out behind him. "Had them." He unpinned the folio and held it out. "Has. Had. They're yours."

She took it the way other people take up infants, both hands, careful of the head, and carried it to the workbench without a word. In the lantern's light she opened it and turned pages, checking, counting, her lips moving slightly over dates and sums. Emrys watched her and understood that she was not verifying the pages. She was verifying herself—reassuring herself that the record had come home whole, that the thing she had built out of seven years of small stubborn acts had not been unmade while she slept.

"He means to trade them," Emrys said. "To Vane. My debts for his silence. Our name for yours."

"I know." She did not look up. "I'd have known within the week anyway. Vane's man was seen at the Cross Keys asking after the London coach schedule. Someone is planning to travel soon, and it isn't us." She smoothed the page flat with two fingers, and her voice, when it came again, was quieter. "You'll have broken your father's heart to bring me these."

"His heart was broken before I touched it. Vane broke it, and the debts broke it, and he broke it himself, worst of all. All I did was keep one small part of him from breaking something else." Emrys heard his own voice come out harder than he intended, and made himself soften it, because she of all people deserved precision rather than noise. "Cyrus. Listen to me. I want to say it plainly, once, so we never have to dance around it. I chose. Tonight, in that room, with him asleep not four feet away—I chose you and the truth over him and the lie. And I will not pretend afterward that it cost me nothing, because it cost me everything, but it was mine to spend and I've spent it. So don't protect me from knowing what I've done. That's all I ask."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lantern hissed faintly on its wick. Outside, wind moved through the bare elms with a sound like slow water.

Then she closed the folio and looked up at him, and in the lantern-light her face was stripped of every professional blankness she owned, and what was underneath was not softness exactly—it was something older and tougher than softness—but it was offered, wholly, for the first time.

"Then we're past the point where either of us can turn back," she said. "Good. I've been carrying this book alone for seven years, Mr. Halloran, and alone is a habit, not a virtue. Sit down. There's things in those pages you haven't seen, and if we're going to survive what comes next, you'd best see them now—because tomorrow morning, your father is going to discover his bargaining chip is gone, and when he does, there is only one man in England he'll believe took it."

Emrys felt the cold of the walk return to him all at once, settling deep.

"And when he asks me," he said slowly, "I'm going to tell him the truth."

"Yes," said Cyrus, and set the lantern between them, and opened the book. "That's exactly what frightens me."
