## 21. Vane's Offer

The summons came the way frost comes—not announced, simply found in the morning, silvering everything it touched.

Cyrus was in the stillroom, crushing dried comfrey between her palms for a poultice one of the gardeners would need by afternoon, when Mrs. Beddow appeared in the doorway with a card on a small salver, as though delivering it to a lady of the house rather than to a nurse-clerk whose hands smelled of herbs and lamp oil.

"Colonel Vane," Mrs. Beddow said, and her voice had the particular flatness she reserved for things she did not wish to think about aloud. "He's asking you to walk with him. The grounds. He says—" she glanced at the card, though she must have known its contents already— "he says he finds conversation easier out of doors."

*Out of doors.* Cyrus set down the mortar. Out of doors there were no witnesses close enough to hear, no walls to hold an echo, no servant passing with linens who might later be asked what she'd seen. Out of doors was where men went to say things they wanted never said back.

"Where is Lord Halloran?" she asked.

"Gone to Ashcombe about the timber valuation. Mr. Emrys rode with him." Mrs. Beddow's eyes flicked up, quick and questioning, then down again. She was a good woman; she knew when not to ask. "The colonel's waiting at the south terrace."

Cyrus wiped her hands on her apron, took up her shawl because the morning had teeth in it, and went.

* * *

Vane stood at the balustrade looking out over the park as if the land were already his—she noticed that first, before his smile, before his greeting. He stood the way surveyors stand, measuring. He had traded his uniform for plain dark wool, which somehow made him worse; out of regimentals he was only a man of middle years with grey coming in at his temples and a face built for reasonableness, the kind of face juries trusted and widows trusted and clerks trusted right up until the ledgers were balanced against them.

"Miss Thornbury." He inclined his head. "Thank you for indulging an old soldier's whim."

"Colonel." She did not curtsey. He noted it; the noting moved behind his eyes like a fish under ice.

They walked. The gravel spoke beneath their boots, and beyond it the park lay brown and silver under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Rooks argued in the elms. Somewhere a gate hinge cried out and was ignored.

"You've been with this family seven years," Vane said. It was not a question, so she did not answer it. "Seven years. That's longer than most marriages endure, and with considerably less complaint, I'm told. Remarkable constancy in a woman who could have taken a comfortable post in any hospital in London."

"Hospitals in London smell worse," Cyrus said.

He laughed—a real laugh, brief and warm, and that was worst of all, because it reminded her that men like him were not monsters. Monsters could be fought. Men like him were merely reasonable.

"They told me you were direct." They passed the yew walk, its branches dripping from the night's rain. "I'll be direct in return, then. I admire you, Miss Thornbury. I've admired you since Balaclava. You have a gift for order. For accuracy." A pause, measured to the half-second. "For record-keeping."

The word fell between them and lay there, small and enormous.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Don't you?" He stopped walking. The path bent ahead toward the ha-ha, where the park dropped away into fields, and beyond the fields the fen stretched grey to the horizon—the same fen, she thought absurdly, across which she might someday run if it came to running. "Come. Let us dispense with the theater. It insults us both."

He turned to face her fully, and she saw that the warmth had been folded away somewhere, put aside like a coat no longer needed. What remained was patient and cold and entirely awake.

"A ledger," he said quietly. "Kept over seven years. Names, dates, transactions. Supply movements. Signatures observed and remembered. I know it exists, Miss Thornbury, because I have spent those same seven years learning what happened to certain documents that should have been destroyed, and every thread ends near a woman who was always present and never mentioned. You made yourself smoke, Miss Thornbury. Smoke drifts, but it leaves a residue. I have simply followed the residue."

The rooks went on arguing in the elms. Her heartbeat felt very loud, very slow, like footsteps on stairs in a sleeping house.

"If such a book existed," she said, "and I am not granting that it does—"

"You are. Your hands have gone still. Hands go still when a person stops performing and starts calculating. Yours calculated the moment I said *ledger*, which tells me the term was correct." He smiled again, thin as paper. "I mean you no harm. Quite the opposite. I have come to make you a proposal, and I would ask you to hear all of it before you decide anything. May we walk? Standing still invites observation, and I promised you privacy."

She walked. It was that or stand rooted like one of the yews, and she had survived too long to let a man see her root.

* * *

"The Halloran family," Vane began, resuming his reasonable tone, "is in difficulties. You know this better than most; you keep their accounts' ugly cousins, I imagine—the deferred bills, the creditors' letters. Lord Halloran borrowed heavily during the war years to fund ventures I need not detail, and those ventures were managed by men who did not always act with the family's interests at heart. There is evidence—growing evidence—that funds intended for supply contracts were diverted. That signatures were affixed to documents the signatories never saw. That the name Halloran has been used, shall we say, as a vessel for other men's cargo."

*My ledger says otherwise,* she thought. *Three entries, 1809 through 1811. Vane, initialled V.R., countersigning grain requisitions that never reached any depot. Vane's hand on the transport vouchers for a hospital shipment that arrived as empty crates. I wrote your name in my own blood-ink before you ever learned to spell it in theirs.*

"And the letter," she said aloud. "The sealed letter that came in October. Was that also part of the evidence growing?"

Something flickered across his face—pleasure, she realized with a lurch of nausea. Pleasure that she had shown she knew of it.

"You saw the direction," he said. "Of course you did. You see everything; it is your defining virtue and, I suspect, your defining burden." They rounded the ice pond, black glass under a skin of fog. "Yes. That letter was mine, in substance if not in ink. It names this house as conspirator in the embezzlement of some eleven thousand pounds of army funds. It will reach the magistrate within the fortnight unless circumstances alter."

"And what circumstances would alter it?"

They had come to the boathouse, shuttered for winter, its boards silvered with weather. Vane leaned against the wall, folding his arms, arranging himself into the posture of a man settling in for honest negotiation—and Cyrus understood suddenly why he had chosen this spot. The boathouse. The one place on the grounds far enough from the house that even Emrys's window could not overlook it. He had planned the geography of this conversation down to the drip-line of the eaves.

"Surrender the ledger," he said. "All of it, or at minimum every page bearing dates before 1812. Give it to me freely, tonight, and the letter goes into my stove by morning. No magistrate. No trial. No scandal. The Hallorans keep their name, their house, their standing. The young man keeps his prospects—which I understand are currently of some importance to him, given a certain attachment he's been forming." He watched her carefully as he said it, and she kept her face as blank as a closed door, hating herself for the way her breath caught anyway. "Everyone walks away whole, Miss Thornbury. That is my offer. That is the *reasonable* offer."

"And if I refuse?"

The fog had thickened around the pond. When he answered, his voice was softer, almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened her more than any threat shouted could have.

"Then I proceed. I proceed with the letter, and the Halloran name goes into the dock beside mine—or rather, beside the men I will permit to stand accused in my place. And separately—quite separately, though the two matters will unfortunately become entangled in the public mind—I proceed against you." He straightened, brushing something invisible from his sleeve. "You held a post with the army medical staff, Miss Thornbury. You recorded casualty returns. Regimental documentation, signed under authority. Now—I am given to understand that certain of those returns contain discrepancies. Dates altered. Entries amended after the fact. A sergeant's death recorded on one day in one document and another day in another. Small things. Trifles, in themselves. But forgery of military records is not a trifle. It is a felony." He let the word sit in the cold air. "It carries the same penalty as murder, Miss Thornbury. Hanging."

*He knows about Thomas,* she thought, and the knowledge went through her like bay water through a wound. Not Thomas her brother—Thomas dead these twelve years and unnamed—but the other thing: the entry she had altered in 1810, one date shifted by two days to protect a young lieutenant who had frozen with fear and left wounded men untended while another man covered for him. One date. Two days. Written in mercy, and now hanging from her neck like a stone.

"You falsified nothing yourself," she said. "You'd manufacture the discrepancies. You'd forge the proof of forgery."

"I would produce documents," Vane said mildly. "What the documents prove will be decided by a jury of respectable men who have never buried a soldier and cannot read a field return without yawning. Juries believe uniforms, Miss Thornbury. I have worn mine for thirty years." He paused, and added, almost kindly: "And you—you will be the strange woman who kept secret books about her betters, hoarding names like a magpie hoards buttons. Do you truly believe an assize jury will trouble itself over whether your alterations were merciful?"

The fog pressed close. She became aware of her own hands, hanging at her sides, and forced them to relax.

"Why me?" she asked. "You could burn the workshop. You nearly burned the record room. You have hired men and fire and the patience of seven years. Why come to me with offers?"

"Because fires leave questions," Vane said. "Questions lead to inquiries, and inquiries are imprecise instruments—they break things I do not wish broken. And because—" here he turned to look at her directly, and for one unguarded instant she saw something almost like respect in his face— "because you are the only person in England holding a true account of certain transactions, and true accounts are dangerous things, Miss Thornbury, but they are also valuable. In the wrong hands yours destroys me. In mine, it ceases to exist, and everyone it could harm is spared. Think of it as triage. You of all people understand triage. Some limbs must come off so the patient lives."

*He is going to amputate the past,* she thought, *and call it medicine.*

* * *

They walked back. He talked of indifferent things—the weather, the state of the roads, the melancholy decline of the wool trade—and she answered in monosyllables, and beneath the surface of the talk her mind ran its cold arithmetic.

If she gave him the ledger, the Hallorans were safe—for now. But Vane did not forgive debts; he harvested them. The entries in her book were the only proof that Lord Halloran's borrowing had fed Vane's early schemes, that the family's shame was partly another man's design. Burn the ledger and Vane owned the past entire. He would own Emrys's future too, dangling it forever the way he dangled the letter now. Safety purchased from a man like this was not safety; it was a lease, renewable at his pleasure.

If she refused, the letter went to the magistrate. Lord Halloran, brittle and frightened and guilty in the small ways and innocent in the large ones, would stand in the dock. And she would stand in another one, perhaps, with her single altered date exhibited like a severed hand.

Unless—

*Unless the truth comes out first. Unless the whole truth, all at once, in daylight, before he can burn or bury or buy it.*

Which meant testimony. Which meant witnesses. Which meant Martha Quill saying aloud what she'd seen in Crimea. Which meant moving fast, faster than reasonable, faster than a careful invisible woman had ever permitted herself to move.

At the terrace steps Vane stopped and offered his hand—not to shake, she saw, but palm upward, open, waiting. The gesture of a man accepting a delivery.

"You needn't answer today," he said. "Tonight will do. I shall be at the Woolpack Inn until Thursday; send word by nine o'clock, either word. But do send word, Miss Thornbury. Silence, in our present circumstances, would have to be understood as refusal—and refusals oblige me to begin." His eyes went past her, up toward the house, to the tall window of the workshop where a restored lantern sometimes glowed past midnight. "Consider what you're protecting. Consider what it weighs. And consider, above all, how much lighter everything becomes when heavy things are finally put down."

He raised his hat, turned, and went down the drive whistling something tuneless and old, a soldier's marching song from another war, and Cyrus stood on the terrace steps watching him diminish along the avenue of bare elms until the fog took him.

* * *

She did not go inside at once. She walked instead around the east wing, past the kitchen gardens with their rotted bean-poles, to the workshop, and stood outside its door a long moment with her hand on the iron latch, listening to the silence within.

Inside, on the shelf where he kept his files, Emrys's tools hung in patient order. Inside, on the bench, half-restored, the Crimean lantern waited—Quill's name chased into its brass collar, the sergeant Vane had buried twice, once in a shallow grave and once in someone else's glory. And inside the wall cavity behind the third panel, wrapped in oilcloth, the ledger lay where she had hidden it three nights ago, seven years of small names sleeping in the dark like seeds.

*Triage,* Vane had said.

Her brother's face rose before her—Thomas at fourteen, laughing, mud to his knees—unmarked grave, unnamed stone, the omission that had made her. Every name in the book was a brick laid against that first forgetting. Hand the bricks over and the wall came down. All of it. Everything she had built in the dark.

But if she kept them, the Hallorans fell, and Emrys with them—he who had chosen truth over blood, who had returned her stolen pages without his father's knowing, who hung lamps in windows for dead sergeants. She could not carry both weights. She was one small woman with one pair of hands.

No. That was his arithmetic, and it was a lie, the way reasonable lies are lies. There was a third road. It ran through a courtroom and a magistrate and a jury of respectable men, and it was narrow, and it was likely fatal, and it required what she had never once risked in seven years of invisibility: her own name, spoken aloud, in front of everyone.

Tonight, Vane expected an answer by nine o'clock.

Cyrus lifted the latch and stepped into the workshop's lamplit dimness, and pulled the door shut behind her, and did not send word to the Woolpack Inn at all.

Instead she took down the oilcloth-wrapped bundle from the wall, cleared a space on Emrys's bench, and uncapped her ink. If the reckoning was coming, it would find the ledger finished—every page copied, every entry doubled, the truth made into two truths that no fire and no bargain could ever again reduce to one.

Outside, the fog thickened over the park, and somewhere down the darkening road a man whistled, waiting for an answer that was already, quietly, irrevocably no.
