## 22. The Night Watch

The storm came up from the west an hour after dusk, the way the autumn storms did, shouldering across the fen with a sound like a great door dragging on its hinges. Cyrus heard it first as a pressure in her ears—the same animal instinct that had warned her of shellfire at Balaclava—and then as rain arriving in sheets against the workshop's north-facing glass, so hard and so continuous that it seemed less like weather than like a decision the sky had made about them.

She had lit the lantern before the last light failed. It sat in the center of the long workbench now, its restored panes throwing a warm amber ring onto the scarred oak, and within that ring lay everything they had: her ledger, opened to the middle years; a sheaf of good paper Emrys had bought in Norwich under a bookseller's indifferent eye; three pens, two knives for cutting nibs, a sanding dish, and a bottle of ink wrapped in flannel against the cold.

Outside, the wind found the gaps in the weatherboarding and played them like a broken organ. Inside, the smell was of linseed oil, cut glass, old solder, and the particular sweetness of heated metal—Emrys's smell, she thought, though she would never have said so. The smell of a man who spent his nights making broken things give light again.

"We should begin," she said. "If we mean to finish by morning."

Emrys looked up from the window, where he had been watching the yard dissolve into water and darkness. His shirt-sleeves were rolled to the elbow; there was ink already on his right hand, a smudge he did not yet know about, high on his cheekbone where he had pushed back his hair. He looked, she thought, very young for a man whose father was in a magistrate's custody and whose house was dying around him. And also not young at all. Seven weeks ago he had been only the younger son who kept strange hours. Now he was head of a disgraced name, and the strangeness of his hours had become strategy.

"Finish by morning," he repeated, as if testing whether the words could bear weight. He came away from the glass and sat down opposite her, and the lantern between them made two small fires of their eyes. "Miss Thornbury, I have read perhaps a quarter of what you've written, and it took me three nights and cost me more sleep than I can afford. You propose to copy all of it."

"I propose to copy the parts that matter. Which is most of it." She uncapped the ink and set out their papers in two stacks, one before each of them. "You take the supply transactions and the signatures—I taught you the hand-analysis last week, and you learn faster than the corporals did. I take the names and the dates. We cross-copy the entries that touch both."

"And when our copies disagree?"

"They won't. If they do, mine is correct, and you may write me a furious note in the margin."

Something moved through him that was nearly laughter—she saw it break the surface of his exhaustion and sink again—and then he drew the first page toward him and bent over it, and for a long while there was no sound in the workshop but the storm and the scratch of two pens moving in time, like oars.

* * *

They had agreed to keep watch in shifts, but the agreement dissolved almost immediately, because neither of them could work without the other within reach of a question. *Is this Vane's hand or Finch's? What does this abbreviation mean? Why is this entry dated twice?* So they sat knee-to-knee across the bench through the first hour, heads bowed toward each other over the ledger like two people reading one scripture, and the intimacy of it frightened Cyrus more than the storm did.

Not the nearness itself—she had tended men at arm's length and closer through fever and amputation; the body held no terrors for her. It was the being *seen*. For seven years her survival had rested on the assumption that nobody watched her, that a nurse-turned-clerk with a plain face and quieter manners passed beneath notice the way furniture passes beneath notice. Vane had ended that assumption forever. But Emrys ended something else, something older: he read her pages not as evidence to be used but as grief to be understood, and every time he paused over a name—a private, a farrier, a boy of sixteen listed as *drowned, unnamed*—and looked up at her with that grave attention, she felt the ledger's seven-year silence cracking open inside her chest.

"You knew all of them," he said once, past midnight, his pen resting. Rain hammered the roof above them like fists.

"I wrote them down. Knowing is different from writing. Writing is only—" She searched for the word. "Witness. Knowing is the wound."

"Tell me one."

It was not a demand. It was an offering, and she understood that if she declined it he would never ask again. Outside, the wind rose to a shriek and fell away, and in the falling silence she heard herself say:

"Sergeant Quill. You know that one already—your lantern carries his name. But you don't know that he died holding a position he had no orders to hold, because Captain Vane ordered the withdrawal and told no one below the rank of lieutenant. Quill held because no one told him to go. Twenty-two minutes. When they found him, his hand was still on the fuse. And Vane wrote to London describing the maneuver as his own improvisation, executed under fire, and was commended for it." Her voice stayed level; she had practiced levelness the way other women practiced music. "Martha Quill received a widow's portion calculated on eight months' service. The clerk who computed it signed himself *per procurationem, C. Thornbury*. My hand. I have hated my own handwriting since."

Emrys said nothing for a moment. Then, quietly: "That's why you began."

"That's why I began. Everything after was only the habit of it growing roots."

He reached across the bench—not to touch her, she noted, not quite, but to turn the ledger so that the current page faced her rather than him, an act of courtesy so precise it undid her more than any grasp would have. "Then let us make certain the habit survives us," he said, and wet his pen, and returned to work.

* * *

The candle-clock burned down. The storm changed registers—now a steady drumming roar, now sudden lulls so profound they could hear the fen drains running loud in the dark, now ragged salvos of rain like grapeshot. Somewhere past two in the morning the wind found a loose pane high in the gable and set it chattering in its frame, a thin metallic tooth-chatter that went on and on until Emrys rose without a word, fetched a strip of leather and a tack from his tool wall, climbed onto a crate and mended it by feel and lantern-glow while the rain ran down his wrists.

Cyrus watched him do it and thought, with a clarity that felt almost clinical: *I could love this man.* Not *I do*—she was too honest with herself, too well-trained in evidence, to leap from a mended window to a whole architecture of feeling. But the capacity was there, discovered suddenly in herself like a room in a house she had believed fully mapped, and it terrified her precisely because it arrived now, under siege, when everything either of them touched was already promised to destruction. Love formed in a burning building is made of the same material as panic. She had seen soldiers mistake one for the other in Crimea, and she had buried the consequences.

So when he dropped down from the crate, shaking rain from his cuffs, and caught her looking at him, she did the safe thing. She slid a copied page across the bench.

"Your entry for March 1811," she said. "The flour contracts. Check the second signature against your father's correspondence file—it isn't his hand, whatever the date says."

The moment folded itself away, unspoken, into the general silence of unfinished things. Later, both of them would carry the knowledge of it separately into their separate watches of the night, the way soldiers carried unspent cartridges.

* * *

By three o'clock the fatigue came for them, and it came differently for each. Emrys's pen began to slow, his letters widening like a man walking downhill in the dark; twice he caught himself mid-yawn with the startled expression of someone ambushed by his own body. Cyrus, who had stood thirty-six-hour vigils over dying men and considered sleep a country she visited rarely and without enthusiasm, felt instead the cold arrive—that deep bone-cold of the marsh country in autumn that no coat defeats—and she got up, stiffly, to feed the stove in the corner.

There was no coal left worth the name. She fed it offcuts of pine and splints of broken glass-crating, and the fire took them greedily, throwing jumping shadows up the tool wall, and she crouched there longer than the fire required, letting the heat find her hands.

"Cyrus."

She turned. He had used her Christian name—her given name, which she had surrendered to him only once, drunk on nothing stronger than relief, the night after the stable records burned. Hearing it in the storm-dark did something to the air between them.

"You should sleep an hour," he said. "I'll watch the door and keep copying the simple entries. Dates and totals. Even I cannot ruin those."

"And leave you alone with the original? You might discover how easy forgery is after all, Mr. Halloran, and then where would we be?"

"Convicted, probably. Both of us." But he smiled when he said it, and the smile in the lantern-light was the first entirely unguarded thing she had ever seen him make. It changed his face completely—made it younger and stranger and much more dangerous to her peace. "Sit by the fire at least. Your hands have gone white."

She sat. She did not admit that her hands had gone numb. There are limits to what even the honest will confess, and she had learned hers early.

For a while neither spoke. The fire cracked and settled. The storm leaned on the workshop and was refused, leaned again, was refused. And then Emrys, still writing, said—not looking up, addressing the page as much as her—

"When this is over. Whatever 'over' means, whichever way it falls. If the ledger does what we hope and Vane is broken open in a courtroom—if all these names get read aloud and the dead get their due—" His pen stopped. He stared at the half-finished line. "What becomes of you? Where does a woman go, afterward, who has done what you're doing? They will not thank you. Thanking you would require admitting they needed you, and the entire edifice rests on pretending you were never there."

Cyrus looked at the fire. The truthful answer—*I do not know; I have never planned past the next name*—rose to her lips and she swallowed it, because some part of her wanted to give him something truer than truth, and she distrusted that part profoundly.

"There is always another ledger," she said. "There are erased people everywhere, Mr. Halloran. I have grown competent at finding them."

"That isn't an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

He let it lie. That was the thing about him, she thought—Emrys, the insomniac, the mender of lanterns, the son who had chosen a stranger's truth over his own father's bargain. He let things lie when lying was the kinder act. He knew the difference between silence as concealment and silence as shelter, and it occurred to her, watching his pen move again in the amber light, that this knowledge was rarer in men than courage, and worth more.

"If it were permitted," he said, very quietly, to the page, to the fire, to no one, "if the world were arranged otherwise—"

The wind chose that instant to fling a branch against the north wall, a crack like a musket, and they were both on their feet before thought caught up with body—he reaching for the poker, she for the knife she kept sewn into her sleeve—and for a heartbeat they stood facing the dark doorway together, shoulder against shoulder, breathing fast, the whole future balanced on a stick of fen-wood thrown by a gust.

Nothing came through the door but rain.

"It is only the storm," Cyrus said, when her pulse allowed speech. But she noticed that neither of them stepped apart, and she noticed that she did not want to be the one to step first, and she filed the noticing away with all the other evidences she was not yet prepared to enter into any record.

* * *

Toward dawn the storm exhausted itself the way storms do, going from violence to sullen persistence to a fine, drifting, grey rain that turned the first light milky. The fen beyond the windows emerged piece by piece from the dark—the black line of the hedge, the drowned silver of the pasture, the distant estate house with its blind lit windows where the household, scattered and fearful, was waking to another day of disgrace.

On the bench lay forty-one sheets of new paper, filled edge to edge in two hands: the duplicate record, complete but for the final cross-checks. Beside it, the original ledger, closed now, its worn boards beaded with damp from the air.

Emrys divided the copies into three bundles with a care that bordered on ceremony. "One goes to Martha Quill, sewn into a mattress or a hem, wherever she judges safest. One goes into the church at Aldermere, behind the vault stones—Father Loxley owes my mother a debt of memory and will keep his mouth shut. The third—" he weighed it in his hand "—stays with you. You're the only one of us who can testify to it, so it should live beside the witness."

"And the original?"

"The original goes back in the wall." He glanced toward the cavity behind the lantern-rack, where a panel his grandfather had built for smuggled brandy still swung on greased hinges. "The oldest hiding place in the house. Perhaps the oldest things deserve the oldest protections."

They worked in silence then: wrapping bundles in oilcloth, warming wax, pressing seals made from a plain die—no crest, no initial, nothing that could be traced. When it was done the workshop looked exactly as it had the evening before, innocent of everything, and Cyrus felt the strangest vertigo, standing in a room that contained the ruin of one man and the redemption of several, and gave no sign of it whatsoever.

Emrys banked the fire. She trimmed the wick and shuttered the lantern, and its circle of amber shrank to a thread and went out, leaving them in the grey pre-dawn with the rain whispering on the roof.

At the door they paused, as they always did now—an awkward, necessary arithmetic about who leaves first, whose absence must be invisible to whom. He put his hand on the latch and did not lift it.

"Cyrus." Her name again, in daylight's antechamber, sounded different than it had in the dark—more perilous, because meant. "Last night, before the branch struck the wall. I was about to say something."

"I know."

"It was not about ledgers."

"I know that too."

He nodded slowly, and she watched him decide—she could see the decision happening, item by item, like a clerk closing accounts—to defer it. To carry it forward, unspent, into whatever came next. "After the assizes," he said. "When we know what world we're standing in. Ask me then."

"And if the world we're standing in has no place for the answer?"

"Then I'll ask anyway," said Emrys Halloran, "and we shall build one."

He lifted the latch and went out into the thin rain, crossing the yard toward the house with his collar up and his stride long, and Cyrus stood in the doorway watching until the mist took him. Then she gathered her bundle of copies, bound it beneath her shawl against her heart, and went the opposite way, along the flooded path toward the lane, alone with the storm's leavings and the weight of paper and the sentence she had not let him finish.

She had reached the gate before she saw the rider.

He stood his horse at the bend of the lane, motionless in the drizzle, too far off to be a farmhand and too patient to be harmless—a dark shape that had clearly been waiting for exactly this lightening of the sky, and that turned, unhurried, to watch her as she stopped.

In his hand, glinting dully through the rain, was a folded paper. And he raised it—not waving, only showing it to her, the way a man shows a warrant—and Cyrus understood, with the cold certainty of the ledger-keeper who knows an entry is about to be written, that the night's work had come too late, and that the world had already begun to move against them.
