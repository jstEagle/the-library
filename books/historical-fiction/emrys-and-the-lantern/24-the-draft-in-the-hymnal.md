## 24. The Draft in the Hymnal

The frost had come to the fen country overnight, and it came the way grief comes—not all at once but in a slow silvering of everything Cyrus had taken for green. She walked the lane from the coach stop to Martha Quill's cottage with her breath hanging before her like something she could not put down, and the hedges stood beaded and still, and the whole world seemed to have agreed to hold itself quietly for fear of breaking.

She had not slept. Finch was nine days dead—Aldous Finch, clerk, forger, coward, and, in the end, perhaps something braver than any of them had allowed—and his death walked beside her the whole way, keeping pace with her steps as patiently as a dog. He had fallen from Westminster Bridge on a Tuesday evening with his hat still on and his gloves still on and his boots polished, because that was the sort of man he had been: tidy even at the end, considerate even in dying, leaving no scene anyone would have to clean up but the river's own.

*He fell*, the newspapers said. *His own hand is supposed*, said the coroner's men who were not looking very hard.

Cyrus knew better, and knowing better was its own kind of weight, because she had spoken with him. She had sat across from him in the smoke of a cheap coffee-house while Emrys Halloran kept watch by the door, and Finch had twisted his gloves in his hands and told them, in a voice barely above the hiss of the brazier, that the letter accusing the Hallorans had been *dictated* to him—that he had copied out another man's lies in his own careful clerk's hand and sealed it and sent it, and that he could not say whose hand had held the pen above his shoulder because to say it would be to die, and he was not ready to die, not yet, not quite yet.

But he had given them something. He had given them a look—a flick of the eyes toward Martha Quill's direction, eastward, fenward, when Cyrus had mentioned where she lodged. And he had said, in the last minute before he fled the coffee-house like a man late for his own funeral: *"I was raised on scripture, Mrs. Thornbury. Whatever else I am, I am that. A man keeps his promises where he made them."*

At the time it had sounded like nothing. A sentiment. A sad little flourish from a sad little man.

Now it sounded like a map.

* * *

Martha Quill was in the garden when Cyrus came through the gate, though garden was a generous word for it in February—a scraped square of frozen earth where last summer's bean poles leaned together like tired old men and the kale stood black-green and frosted in ragged rows. Martha's hands were bare despite the cold, red-knuckled, working at the frozen ground with a hand-fork as though winter were simply an obstacle to be reasoned with. She had been a soldier's wife. She did not wait for seasons; she negotiated with them.

"You've brought the cold in with you," Martha said without turning. "I felt the gate."

"I've brought worse than that, I'm afraid."

"Then you'd best bring it to the fire."

The cottage was one room and a loft, scrubbed white, smelling of peat smoke and tallow and the faint clean ghost of soap. On the mantel stood the things that constituted Martha Quill's whole visible estate of memory: a daguerreotype case too old to be a daguerreotype and too new to be anything else, holding instead a lock of hair tied with faded ribbon; a small brass button, cavalry, polished so often the face of it had gone blind; and a book.

Cyrus saw the book before Martha closed the door behind them. She saw it the way a drowning woman sees a plank—not with decision but with the whole body at once.

It was a hymnal. Small, bound in dark cloth gone shiny at the corners from handling, its spine creased into three soft valleys from years of being held open in two hands. *Select Hymns for the Use of Camp and Garrison*, the gilt lettering read, most of the gilt worn away, so that only *Hymns for the Use* remained legible, which struck Cyrus as accidentally honest—the way worn things always were.

"Aldous Finch gave you that," Cyrus said. It was not a question. Her heart had begun to work strangely, hard and shallow, like a bird caught indoors.

Martha followed her gaze and something moved across her weathered face—not surprise, exactly. More like the weary recognition of a debt come due at last.

"He did," Martha said. "Eleven years ago, when the regiment shipped out. He was chaplain's assistant then, before they made him a clerk proper. He came round the married quarters the night before we sailed—or before *they* sailed, I should say; wives don't sail, wives are left—and he put this in my hands and said—" Martha stopped. Her jaw worked. "He said, 'Keep this where you keep what matters, Mrs. Quill, and one day it may matter more than you'd wish.' I thought him a strange young man then. I think him a stranger dead one."

"When did you last hear from him?"

"Three weeks past. A letter, unsigned, saying only: *'Look in Job. Forgive me.'*" Martha's voice flattened. "I thought him deranged with guilt over some debt. I wrote back asking what he meant. No answer came. Then the papers came, and the bridge, and I understood no answer ever would."

"Job," Cyrus repeated.

"Chapter and verse he didn't say. There are—" Martha almost smiled, and it was terrible, the smile of a woman who has spent eleven years learning that hope is just grief wearing its good coat. "There are forty-two chapters of Job, Mrs. Thornbury. I've been through every one of them twice since the news. There's nothing hidden in Job. Nothing but suffering, and the wind off the wilderness, and a man losing everything and being told by his friends it must be his own fault." She looked up sharply, suddenly fierce. "I know that book. I've *lived* that book. If Aldous Finch hid something in it, it isn't in Job. It's near it."

Cyrus went very still. Outside, the wind moved along the hedge line with a sound like fabric tearing slowly.

"Where is it near?" she asked quietly.

Martha crossed the room. She took down the hymnal with both hands, the way one takes down an urn, and set it on the table under the window where the grey light fell strongest. The pages, when she opened them, exhaled a faint dry sweetness—pressed flowers once, long since crumbled to rust-colored ghosts between the leaves. And there, in the back, after the last hymn and before the blank flyleaf, were printed the Apocrypha readings and the Psalms appointed for burial, and between the burial psalms and the binding—

Martha's thumbnail found the join. The back board of the book had been opened and re-laid, expertly, by someone who understood bindings: a clerk's neatness, a forger's precision. Beneath the cloth, in a cavity no thicker than two fingers, lay folded papers.

Neither of them breathed for a moment. The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere beyond the wall a robin delivered its thin winter testimony and was done.

"Take it," Martha said, before Cyrus had even asked. "Whatever it is, take it out of my house today. I've carried it eleven years without knowing I carried it. I'll not carry it a twelfth."

* * *

They read it at the table, side by side, like two women at a deathbed.

There were four sheets, folded once each, written in the small upright hand Cyrus had seen on the sealed letter and recognized from the first—Finch's hand, unmistakable, the hand of a man who had been trained until his personality disappeared into his penmanship. But where the sealed letter had been smooth, confident, official, these pages were rough with revision. Lines struck through and rewritten. Margins crowded with corrections in a smaller, tighter script, as though the truth had started as a whisper and grown louder with every draft. Words blotched where water—or something water-like—had fallen on them and been blotted in haste.

It was dated. That was the first thing Cyrus noticed, and the thing that made her eyes sting: he had dated it, the eighth of January, ten days before he died. He had known. Perhaps not the hour or the bridge, but the shape of it. Men like Vane did not leave loose threads dangling, and Finch had known himself to be thread.

Then she began at the beginning, and read aloud, because some documents demand air, and this was one.

*I, Aldous Nathaniel Finch, clerk of the Treasury pay office, being of sound mind if unsound circumstances, do set down what I have done and at whose instruction, that if I should come to a sudden end it will not be believed that I died of accident.*

In the autumn of 1811, the draft stated, Colonel Edmund Vane had approached him through an intermediary—a treasury under-clerk named Prewitt, whose name appeared twice more in the pages, always spelled slightly differently, as though Finch could not bear to write it identically twice—and had laid before him a scheme dressed as an administrative necessity. Wartime supply contracts, funnelled through shell purveyors; invoices drawn against regiments disbanded or destroyed; the difference between the sum authorized and the sum actually expended flowing upward into accounts Finch described only as *the usual places*, with the bitterness of a man who had audited those places himself.

Prewitt, the second name. And a third: *Harlbeck, of the Commissariat board, who signs whatever is placed before him and reads none of it.*

Three names. Vane above them, Prewitt and Harlbeck beneath, and beneath them all, doing the writing, the sealing, the disappearing—Aldous Nathaniel Finch, who had wanted, Cyrus thought as she read, only what most damned men want: to matter somewhere, to be necessary to someone, even if the someone was a predator.

And then, halfway down the second sheet, the handwriting changed character entirely. The clerk's composure broke. The letters pressed harder into the paper.

*It was never meant to touch the Hallorans.* 

Cyrus stopped reading aloud there. Her voice had failed her.

*I said so. I told Prewitt the estate accounts were clean—I had seen them, in the ordinary course, and they were cleaner than most, because the old lord is vain and vain men keep straight books out of pride if nothing holier. But Vane required a scapegoat of consequence, a family whose ruin would frighten the others into silence, and the Hallorans had one fatal quality: debts. Debts contracted quietly, to Vane himself, years before, in the lean seasons. Vane holds their paper. He means to hold their necks.*

*The letter I sealed and sent was composed for me. I was instructed to write "the Thornbury account" in the third paragraph myself—it amused someone to include it, I believe, though I did not then understand the joke. I understand it now. There is a woman. There is always a woman with a ledger, in every regiment, in every war; the officers laugh about them over port. Vane has known of her since Crimea. He does not fear her evidence. He intends to purchase it, or poison it, or hang it.*

*If I am dead, let whoever reads this go to the widow Quill. I have left with her what I could not leave anywhere Vane might search. She does not know she guards it. God forgive me for using her ignorance, and forgive her the danger it brings, and forgive us all, who served mammon and called it His Majesty.*

Below, in the crowded margin, in that tight final script, one last addition:

*The samples of "forged army records" prepared against her—against the Thornbury woman—are held at Prewitt's chambers, Lincoln's Inn, third floor, in a deed-box marked DUNSTABLE. They are forgeries. I watched Prewitt make them. My hand helped with two. That is the sin I cannot die without naming.*
