## 25. Demolition Day Announced

The notice came on a Tuesday, pinned to the estate gate with four nails, as though the paper itself might otherwise escape.

Emrys found it on his way back from the village, his boots crusted with frozen mud and his collar pulled high against a wind that came off the fens like something with intentions. He saw the knot of grooms gathered at the gate before he understood what they were looking at—a small congregation, hats in hands, reading in silence the way men read anything bearing an official seal.

He shouldered through them.

*Notice of Demolition and Clearance.* The words sat in a printed hand so clean it seemed to have been written by no one at all, by the law itself, indifferent as weather. *By order of His Majesty's Magistrate for the District, pursuant to proceedings in bankruptcy against the estate of the late partnership Halloran & Sons, the outbuildings known as the East Workshop, together with all sheds, stores, and appurtenances, shall be cleared of contents and demolished within ten days of this notice, such works to commence on the morning of the —*

The date struck him first as an abstraction, then as arithmetic. Ten days. He counted backwards from it automatically, the way he had once counted the days of a fever, and arrived at a morning three weeks after Lady Day, when the frost would still be in the ground but the light would be lengthening, when the workmen would come with hammers and carts and burn what could not be sold.

Ten days to empty a building that held eleven years of his father's ambitions, seven years of his own patient work, and—if Cyrus was right about what she kept hidden—the only true account of certain events that powerful men very much wished had never happened at all.

One of the grooms, old Hobbs, shifted beside him. "Begging your pardon, Mr. Emrys, but does that mean our positions—"

"It means nothing yet," Emrys said, and heard how thin the comfort was even as he offered it. "Nothing yet."

He walked back up the drive slowly, the notice folded into his coat like a coal he could not put down. The elms along the avenue stood bare and black against a sky the color of pewter, and somewhere behind the house a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, which amounted to the same thing this winter.

His father was waiting in the study. Emrys knew it without being told; he could feel the house's attention gathered there, the way water feels the pull toward a drain. He went in without knocking.

Lord Halloran stood at the window with his back to the door, and Emrys saw at once that his father had already read the notice—that he had probably ridden to the gate himself at dawn, or sent someone, because a man who owes money learns to smell paper like a shark smells blood. He had aged another year since autumn. His dressing gown was buttoned wrong at the throat.

"You've seen it," Emrys said.

"I've seen it." His father did not turn. "Ten days. They gave your grandfather forty years to build that workshop and they give me ten days to bury it."

"The demolition isn't the point." Emrys laid the notice on the desk, squaring its corners out of a nervousness he resented in himself. "Vane asked for this. You know that as well as I do. A bankruptcy clearance doesn't require a magistrate's order unless someone has made representations—unless someone wants the grounds cleared before any official surveyor sets foot here and finds things that ought not to be found."

"What things?" His father turned then, and his face was a terrible thing to look at—not defiant, not ashamed, but hollowed, a man standing in the ruin of a house that still stood around him. "What is it you think I've done, Emrys? That's what you're asking, under all your careful words. Say it plainly if you're going to say it at all."

Emrys held his gaze. Seven years of ledgers and half-truths stood between them; a box hidden in the lantern workshop; a nurse who copied documents by night; debts whose shape his father had finally, in the aftermath of the stable fire, begun to confess. "I think you borrowed from a man who never intended to be repaid in money," Emrys said quietly. "And I think the demolition is his way of burning the evidence of his own signature."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The fire in the grate had burned down to a red argument among the coals. Then Lord Halloran sat, heavily, in the chair behind the desk—the chair his grandfather had sat in, and his father—and put his face briefly into his hands.

"There's a letter coming," he said. "From Vane's man. Arranging an inspection."

"An inspection of what?"

"Of the estate's papers. Everything relating to the wartime contracts. He'll come with a clerk and a solicitor, all propriety, and he will read every page we still possess." His father lowered his hands. His eyes were dry and very tired. "He wrote me yesterday. He suggests Thursday week. He suggests it, Emrys, the way a wolf suggests supper."

Thursday week. Emrys did the arithmetic again and felt the floor tilt beneath the numbers. The demolition fell on the Saturday after. Which meant Vane would walk through the record room, cataloguing exactly what survived the fires, exactly what remained to incriminate whom—and two days later the workmen would arrive, and whatever he had noted and wished gone would go into the fires legally, officially, with the magistrate's own seal upon the order.

No room left for accident. No room left to hide anything. Vane had arranged it so that his inspection and the destruction would arrive in the same week, close enough that one could serve the other, far enough apart that no honest fool could prove the connection.

"He's not coming to inspect," Emrys said. "He's coming to take inventory of what he needs to erase."

"Yes," said his father simply. "I believe that is precisely what he is doing."

* * *

The rest of that day moved through Emrys like weather through a broken window.

He walked the workshop twice without knowing fully why, his hand trailing along the benches where he had learned planing and joinery at nine years old, where the smell of linseed oil and iron filings had been as constant to his childhood as his mother's voice. The place looked different now that he knew its death date. Every object acquired the sharp, sad brightness of a thing already mourned: the lathes under their dust sheets like shrouded guests; the racks of chisels with their handles worn to the color of honey by three generations of hands; the long window at the eastern end where, only weeks ago, he had hung the restored Crimean lantern, Quill's name reground into its glass panes, and lit it against the dark as a small and possibly foolish act of defiance.

It still hung there. It still caught what little light the day allowed and threw it back in a soft amber circle onto the floorboards. He stood beneath it a while, absurdly, the way a man stands at a graveside.

*Ten days.* After that, the window would be rubble in a cart, and the lantern with it, unless—

Unless. Everything now came down to unless.

He heard her before he saw her, because he had learned to hear her: the particular quality of Cyrus Thornbury's silence, which was not absence but presence held very carefully, like a full cup carried across a crowded room. She was at the far bench, ostensibly sorting inventory slips, her hands moving over the papers with the mechanical diligence she wore like armor. Her lamplight, he remembered, had burned past midnight more nights than not, these last weeks. He wondered how many hours of sleep she permitted herself, and suspected the answer would frighten him.

"You've seen the notice," he said. Not a question.

Her hands stilled on the papers—one heartbeat, two—then resumed. "I heard the grooms talking at the gate this morning. News travels faster than magistrates."

"Vane inspects Thursday week. The demolition follows on Saturday."

She looked up at that. In the gray light her eyes were the color of wet slate, and there was something in them he had not seen before—or rather something he had seen and misread: not fear exactly, but the concentrated stillness of a person calculating distances, counting exits, measuring the time between now and the end of something. "So whatever he cannot explain away on Thursday," she said slowly, "he burns on Saturday. Under color of law. With witnesses."

"Yes."

"And your father means to let him."

"My father—" Emrys began, and stopped, because he did not know how to finish the sentence honestly. My father means to bargain. My father took pages from your ledger hoping to buy his way free, though he doesn't know I've returned them. My father is a man drowning who will grab at anything, including us. What he said instead was: "My father means to survive. Those are not always the same as meaning well."

Cyrus set down her pen. She aligned it with the edge of the bench, a small precise gesture he had watched her make a hundred times, and he understood suddenly that the precision was not tidiness—it was how she kept herself from shaking, or shouting, or both. Seven years of holding steady in rooms where men decided other men's lives.

"Then Thursday week," she said, "is either the end of it or the beginning. Depending on what we do with the ten days before it."

*We*. He noticed the word. He was fairly sure she noticed that he noticed. Neither of them took it back.

* * *

That evening the household assembled for what his father called, with bitter formality, *a family consultation*, though family had narrowed to father, son, and the aunt who managed the kitchens and pretended not to understand politics. Cyrus served the tea. She had a gift, Emrys had long observed, of being present and unremarked simultaneously—a servant's invisibility worn so naturally that strangers assumed it was her nature rather than her discipline. Only now, watching her pour, did he understand how much labor that invisibility cost, and what a weapon it had secretly become.

His father laid the notice on the table between the sconces.

"The workshop goes in ten days," he said. "There is no appeal; I have inquired. Vane inspects on the Thursday prior. I intend to cooperate fully with the inspection, answer every question, and trust that a man may be ruined honestly if he must be ruined at all."

"That's not what Vane intends," Emrys said.

"No," his father agreed, and there was a terrible mildness in it. "I imagine not."

Aunt Beatrice set down her cup with a click. "Then burn the papers yourselves," she said flatly. "Tonight. All of them. If the man wants evidence destroyed, oblige him early and deprive him of the theater."

The silence that followed had weight. Emrys watched Cyrus, who had gone to stand by the sideboard with the teapot, her face perfectly composed, perfectly blank—and understood that she was the only person in the room who knew why they must not burn everything, and could say nothing without undoing herself. Some of those papers were crimes. Others were exonerations. The fire would not distinguish, and neither, in his father's panic, would the household.

"And destroy the proof of my innocence along with his guilt?" his father said at last, wearily, and Emrys could have kissed the old man for the accidental accuracy of it. "No. Whatever else is true, I am not the forger. I am not the thief. Let him come Thursday and read what he likes. A clear conscience survives an inspection."

*A clear conscience.* Emrys looked at his father's gray face and thought: you are not clear and you know it, and yet you cling to that phrase like a plank, because the alternative is admitting you handed a wolf the keys yourself. He loved his father in that moment, and despaired of him in the same breath, and could not have separated the two feelings with a knife.

Cyrus refilled the cups. When she reached his father, her hand did not tremble at all.

* * *

He found her afterward in the corridor, of course. Or perhaps she found him; the distinction had stopped mattering weeks ago. The lamps along the passage had been economized down to every third bracket, and they stood together in a pool of shadow between lights, speaking low, as conspirators speak, though what they conspired toward was merely the truth.

"He'll find nothing Thursday," Cyrus murmured. "I've seen to that. What remains in the record room is dross—I've spent a month making certain of it. But the demolition order changes things. Ten days is not a month. There are pages still to copy, and people still to reach, and now every errand I run past the gate is watched by men who report to somebody."

"Then don't run the errands. I'll go to the village. I'll go wherever needs going."

"To Martha Quill's?" she said softly, and he flinched, because yes—to Martha Quill's, to the widow whose testimony might hang a colonel, whose name he barely dared think aloud. "You'd carry messages to her through a village where Vane's hired men drink at the Bear and ask questions of anyone foolish enough to answer?"

"Then tell me what to do, Miss Thornbury." It came out harder than he intended, edged with a frustration that was mostly fear—for her, for the book sewn somewhere into her existence, for the whole fragile architecture of names and dates they were trying to raise against the weight of the law itself. "You see everything. You've seen everything for seven years while the rest of us blundered about in daylight. Tell me what ten days buys us."

For a moment she said nothing. The wind pressed at the tall windows along the passage, rattling a latch somewhere, and in the dimness her face was unreadable, a page closed.

"Ten days," she said at last, "buys us enough time to finish the duplicate ledger. It buys us Thursday—because Vane must inspect a record room, and while he inspects ours, his own lodgings sit half-empty and his clerk Aldous Finch's papers sit wherever Finch kept them, and dead men's hymnals sit in widows' houses waiting to be read properly. It buys us the gap between his inspection and the wreckers' hammers—two days, maybe less, in which whatever he means to disappear can be disappeared, or saved, depending on who moves first." She paused. "And it buys us the tenth day itself. Because on the morning they come to tear down the workshop, I will need to be standing inside it, taking the last thing from the wall, and someone will need to keep the workmen off me while I do it."

The image arrived whole and unwelcome: Cyrus prying a board from a wall cavity while cartmen shouted and a foreman threatened, the lantern glass above her, the ceiling of everything they'd built coming down around the last true record in England.

"That someone will be me," Emrys said.

"I didn't ask you to—"

"No," he said. "You didn't. You never ask. That's rather the whole problem with you, Miss Thornbury—you'd carry every burden alone until it killed you, and call it discretion." He heard his own voice shake and did not trouble to steady it. "The tenth day is mine. You keep your eyes on the book; I'll keep theirs on me."

Something moved in her expression then—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. Pity, or gratitude, or that unnamed thing between them that lived in lamplight and unfinished sentences and neither of them would feed nor starve.

"Ten days," she said quietly, as though sealing it.

"Ten days."

* * *

He did not sleep. He gave up pretending around two in the morning and went down through the sleeping house, past his father's door where no light showed but where he would have wager the estate the old man sat awake in the dark, and out across the yard to the workshop, where the cold hit his lungs like deep water.

The lantern still burned in the east window. He had taken, some weeks ago, to leaving a small oil flame in it overnight—wasteful, indefensible, necessary. Its glow lay on the frost-silvered cobbles in a long amber lane, the only warm color in a world of iron and gray.

He worked. It was what his hands knew how to do when his mind refused their proper business. He took up the half-restored campaign clock from the rear bench and set himself to its escapement, the tiny geometry of wheels and pallets, and let the work hold him the way prayer holds some men. Outside, the fen wind rose and fell, testing the shutters, patient as a creditor.

Sometime before dawn he became aware of movement beyond the frosted window—a figure crossing the yard from the house, wrapped in a dark cloak, moving quickly and without light. He knew the walk before he knew the person: unhurried urgency, a body that had learned to cross hospital wards at night without waking the dying.

Cyrus, heading for the gate road, in the hour when the watchmen of the world are drowsiest and the roads belong to whoever has business on them.

He was moving before he had decided to move, snatching his coat, taking the yard at a half-run with the frost biting through his boots. By the time he reached the gate she was already beyond it, a dark shape diminishing along the white verge of the north road—and she was not going toward the village. The village lay east. The north road ran toward the fen, toward the old parish ground where the paupers and the unmarked were buried, toward whatever errand she had kept seven years and chosen, of all the ten days remaining, to spend this one on.

He stood at the gate a long moment, his breath smoking, the demolition notice nailed to the wood just above his head like a sentence passed on them all.

Then he stepped through, and followed her into the dark.
