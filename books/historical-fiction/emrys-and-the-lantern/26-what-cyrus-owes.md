## 26. What Cyrus Owes

The frost had come down hard in the night, and the churchyard at St. Aldhelm's-in-the-Marsh wore it like a second shroud. Every blade of grass stood sheathed in glass; the yew trees dripped slow silver from their black boughs; and the gravel path crunched beneath Cyrus's boots with a sound like small bones being counted. She had risen before the household stirred, taken bread and a heel of cheese in her apron pocket, and slipped out through the garden gate while the sky was still the color of pewter and old ash.

She had not been here in four years.

The lie she told herself on the walk—that she had simply not had time—dissolved somewhere past the second milestone, when the fen opened out flat and pale on either side of the raised road, and she could no longer pretend that distance was anything but choice. Four years of choice. Seven years, if she were honest, and honesty was the whole errand of this day.

Thomas Thornbury had been dead eleven years, and he had no stone.

There had never been money for one. There had been, in the years after Balaclava and the fever camps, barely money for bread, and Cyrus had learned quickly which griefs a working woman was permitted to carry publicly and which had to be folded away like good linen against a day that might never come. A name carved in granite cost two guineas at the mason's in Market Haddley. Two guineas was a winter's candles. Two guineas was medicine when the coughing season came to the estate's lower cottages. So Thomas lay somewhere in this ground—third row from the lych-gate, the sexton had told her once, near the pauper's strip where no one paid for permanence—and the grass had long since forgotten even the shape of the digging.

Cyrus stopped at the gate and stood very still, letting the cold find its way under her cloak collar, her cuffs, the seams of her gloves. She wanted to feel what she supposed she ought to feel. What came instead was the old nurse's habit: an inventory. Her breath fogging. Her fingers stiffening at the knuckles. The ledger's weight against her ribs where it rode in a satchel beneath her cloak—for she did not leave it behind anymore, not since she had found those pages torn, not since she understood that a book could be hunted like any living thing.

*Third row from the lych-gate.* She counted the rows as she walked, and the counting was a kind of prayer, though she had not prayed properly since the hospital tents at Balaclava, where prayer had seemed a luxury of officers and the dying were given instead morphine, water, and her hand.

Here. Or near enough here. The sexton was dead himself now, and the man who kept the burial book in the vestry had shown her the entry only once, years ago, and entries in parish books were thin things—a date, a cause, *fever*, a Christian name and surname and nothing between them. No age. No trade. No word of who had loved him or how badly.

Cyrus knelt on the frozen ground, and the cold came up through her skirts like water.

"I brought you nothing," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the empty yard, flattened by the frost-white silence. "I know that's the first thing you'd note. Thomas Thornbury, who could smell a excuse at forty paces."

The yews dripped. Somewhere beyond the wall, a cart creaked along the fen road and faded.

She had meant to prepare a speech. She had composed fragments of it for years without admitting that was what she was doing—in the dark of the workshop after the last lamp was trimmed, walking the estate paths with her ledgers balanced on her forearm like a tray, phrases surfacing and sinking again. *Forgive me. I kept your name alive in ink. I made a religion of other people's names because I could not save yours.* All of it true, and all of it, she understood now, a kind of cowardice dressed in humility. She had built a monument out of secrecy and called it devotion.

"Here is what happened," she said instead, and told him.

She told him about the hospital tent and the captain—Halloran's cousin's man, that was the irony no one but she appreciated—who had stood over Sergeant Quill's position in the dispatch and claimed the maneuver as his own inspiration while Quill's body went into the ground with a number for a headstone. She told him how she had stood at the edge of that conversation with a basin in her hands and said nothing, and how the nothing had weighed more than the basin ever had, so that by evening she had found herself with a blank exercise book and a stub of pencil, writing down *Quill, Sergeant Elias, 17th Foot, devised the withdrawal at the Kerevan postern, credit given to Captain ——,* and how the pencil had shaken and the writing had not.

"You'd have laughed at me," she said. "You always said I kept accounts like a Jew of the old stories, remembering every farthing. You didn't mean it kindly, the last time. We'd quarreled over the fare money and you said I would sooner forgive God than a debt." Her throat tightened around the memory—the last quarrel, conducted half across a dockside in Portsmouth, both of them too proud to say the ordinary words that brothers and sisters say, believing there would be later. There is always later, until there is only the parish register and the line that says *fever.*

"I have kept seven years of debts," she told the ground. "Every one of them owed to somebody."

* * *

The sun rose while she spoke, though the fen fog held it to a pale coin, and by degrees the churchyard lost its glass sheen and became merely grey and wet. Cyrus got up stiffly, knees protesting, and walked the boundary of the pauper's strip because she could not bear to choose one patch of earth and call it him. She touched three headstones in passing—not his, none of them his, but neighbors of a sort, men and women who slept in the cheapest beds in God's house—and at the wall she stopped and took out the ledger.

It was a plain thing still, though the spine had been mended twice with linen thread and beeswax, and the corners worn round as river stones. She had carried it through seven winters and one war's long tail. It contained, by her own last count, four hundred and eleven names. Some were full entries—dates, regiments, deeds witnessed, wages unpaid, signatures compared and found false. Some were only names and a question mark, recorded because a dying man in a cot by the draughty end of the ward had said his name twice and she had not known what else to do with it but keep it.

Four hundred and eleven names, and not one of them hers. Not one of them his.

That omission had been the design, once. The front page of the ledger—the page every thief, inspector, or curious master's son would open first—was blank. Deliberately blank. Let them look and find nothing worth taking. Let them think the book a shopkeeper's inventory, dull as ditchwater. Invisibility had kept her safe through Vane's dinners and Lord Halloran's scrutiny, through the fires and the searches, through the long years of being the woman nobody saw because she arranged herself so carefully to be unseen. The blank first page was the lock on the whole house of her secret work.

And she had come here, kneeling in the frost above her brother, to break it.

Cyrus opened the ledger against the churchyard wall. Her breath made small ghosts over the paper. From her reticule she took the penknife, the traveling ink bottle wrapped in flannel against the cold, and the good pen—the one Emrys had given her without comment last spring, saying only that a clerk deserved a pen that didn't scratch, and she had understood it for exactly the kindness it was.

Her hand hesitated over the blankness. Ink in cold weather moved like syrup; the first stroke would go down heavy and blot. She knew this. Still she hesitated, because the hesitation was itself the last door, and doors, once opened, admit whatever is waiting outside as well as in.

If she wrote the names, the ledger confessed itself. No longer a clerk's inventory but a testimony. And a testimony demanded things of its author—it demanded she be findable, accountable, present. It demanded she stand somewhere in daylight and answer. She thought of Vane's smooth voice at dinner, probing the family's finances with a fish-knife gentleness; she thought of the scorched record room and the hired man running toward Vane's lodgings; she thought of Finch's letter, *the Thornbury account,* and how the name had found her after all these years of perfect quiet. They are coming regardless, she thought. The hunters do not spare the hidden. They simply take longer to flush.

And meanwhile the dead waited without stones, and the living waited without witnesses, and she had mistaken patience for virtue for seven years running.

"No longer," she said—to Thomas, to the yews, to herself, she did not sort out which.

She warmed the ink bottle briefly between her palms, uncorked it, and wrote.

The nib bit. The letters came out uneven in the cold, and she let them. On the front page, beneath nothing, above everything, in her own careful hand:

*Thomas William Thornbury. Born Deptford, March 1791. Private, 44th Foot. Died of camp fever at Scutari, December 1804 by the reckoning they gave him, though he was twenty-three by God's count and mine. Brother to the keeper of this book. He has no stone. This page is his stone.*

Then, beneath, without letting herself stop:

*Cyrus Anne Thornbury. Keeper of this record. Witness to all herein. Accountable before God and the magistrate for every entry, every omission, and every silence.*

Her signature. Her name in a place where anyone might read it. She sat back against the wall and looked at the two lines together, and something loosened in her chest that had been bound since Portsmouth docks—grief, or shame, or simply the muscular effort of holding herself invisible, year upon year, the way one holds a stomach wound.

The omissions, though. That was the wound that would not close so easily. She turned the pages slowly, past Quill, past the corporal Marsh she must soon find, past the quartermaster's clerk Dawes whose discharge papers had been falsified twice over—and stopped, as she had known she would stop, at the knowledge she had not written anywhere at all.

Lord Halloran's debts. The sums that had flowed, in the early years, through channels that led—if a person followed them patiently, as she had, alone, at midnight—toward Colonel Vane's rising fortunes. She had documented the supply frauds meticulously. She had never once set down the name of the man whose borrowed money had fed them at the start, because that man had taken her in off the roads when she came back from the war with nothing but a nurse's skill and a temper, had given a woman without references a post in his workshop, had been, in his brittle and distracted way, decent to her. She had protected him with silence the way she now suspected he had tried to protect his family with the torn pages from her room.

Her loyalty had a price and the price was truth, and today, at her brother's grave, she could no longer tell which of them the debt belonged to.

"Seven years I've been paying interest," she murmured. "You'd have something sharp to say about that too."

The fog was lifting off the fen. Beyond the wall the world reassembled itself in increments: a drainage mill's sails turning far off, black rooks dropping out of the yew, the road shining wet where the frost surrendered. Cyrus closed the ledger and held it a moment against her chest, feeling its changed weight—which was no weight at all, and all of it.

She would copy the front page. She would hide duplicates as she and Emrys had planned, ink pressed to duplicate sheets in the storm-watched nights to come. But the original would travel openly in her hands now, and if anyone asked her what the book was, she would tell them. That was the vow of the morning, sworn on frozen ground with no stone to witness it but the ones that weren't his: she was done hiding. Whatever came of visibility—Vane's attention, the magistrate's, the household's, Emrys's—she would meet it standing in her own name.

She re-corked the ink. As she bent to gather her things, movement caught her eye at the lych-gate.

A horseman had drawn up there—a big man on a bay hunter, hunched in a greatcoat too fine for the fen roads, talking to the new sexton, who pointed. Toward her. The rider turned in the saddle, and even at seventy yards, even hooded against the damp, Cyrus knew the set of those shoulders and the unhurried confidence of the seat.

Colonel Vane had come to a country churchyard at dawn, and the sexton was pointing him toward the woman kneeling at a pauper's grave.

Cyrus did not run. Running was hiding with hurry in it. She put the ledger into her satchel, buttoned her cloak with fingers that no longer shook, and remained exactly where she was—upright, visible, her brother's name drying on the first page—while the Colonel opened the gate and began, politely, to cross the frozen ground toward her.
