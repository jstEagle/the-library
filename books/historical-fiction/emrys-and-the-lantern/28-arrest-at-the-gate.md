## 28. Arrest at the Gate

The frost came early that morning, and with it the horses.

I heard them before I saw them—hooves on the gravel drive, four sets of them at a trot, which was itself an answer. Nobody rides at a trot up the Halloran drive who means courtesy. The milk-girl had not yet come; the kitchen yard lay grey and empty; and I stood at my window with the curtain drawn back two inches, watching constables dismount in the blue half-light like crows settling on a field.

There were six of them. Two wore the dark coats of the county force; the others were hired men in good boots, which told me their wages had been paid recently and from London. A seventh figure remained mounted at the gate, wrapped to the chin in a travelling cloak, and even at that distance I knew the set of his shoulders—the way Colonel Vane sat a horse as though the horse ought to be grateful.

My hands were steady. That surprised me. Seven years of tending men whose arms had been taken off at the elbow had taught my hands a kind of treachery: they went calm precisely when everything else was going to ruin. I dressed without hurry. I pinned my hair. I took the ledger from its place inside the lining of my second-best trunk—no longer under the floor of my room; that hiding place had been betrayed weeks ago—and bound it flat against my ribs with a linen bandage, where it rode warm and small as a second heart.

Then I went down to open the door before they could break it.

* * *

The household woke in stages, the way a body wakes to pain.

First Mrs. Prewitt the housekeeper, drawn into the hall in her shawl, her mouth opening and closing on questions she could not shape. Then the footmen, then the girls from the laundry, gathering on the stairs like starlings, each one holding some small object she had grabbed without thinking—a brush, a spoon, a folded cloth—as though the day required props.

Lord Halloran came down last, and he came down as a man comes down to his own funeral: shaved, coat buttoned, every detail correct. He must have heard the hooves too. He must have lain in bed for the ten minutes between them and his dressing, deciding how a gentleman receives ruin.

"Mr. Brack," he said to the senior constable, and his voice held. "You are on my ground at a strange hour."

"Sir." Brack was young through the cheeks and old through the eyes, the kind of man who has served warrants on neighbours before and will again. He unfolded a paper with both hands, carefully, as if it might go off. "Warrant issued out of the sessions at Norwich. Thomas Halloran, of Halloran Park—you are charged upon sworn information with fraudulent conversion of regimental funds, embezzlement of monies entrusted to your office as contractor of supply, and conspiracy with persons unknown to falsify accounts of the same."

The words fell into the hall like stones into a well. I stood at the edge of the group, in my grey dress, in my grey position, invisible as I have always been, and I watched the paper tremble very slightly in Brack's hands and thought: *sworn information*. Someone had sworn. Vane had put his name to lies with all the solemnity of a christening.

"I will want to see the information," Lord Halloran said. "The name of the party swearing it."

"You'll see it at the assizes, sir. I'm instructed—" Brack cleared his throat. "I'm instructed you're to come now, quietly, no carriage of your own. The gig is provided."

"The gig." Lord Halloran said the word as though tasting something spoiled. Then he straightened, and for one moment the old man I had served for seven years stood fully upright inside the ruined one. "Very well. Mrs. Prewitt, my grey greatcoat. Not the good one—it will be confiscated eventually, and I'll not have Vane's people wearing it."

He almost smiled. It was the worst thing I had seen all morning.

* * *

They did not let him linger, but they could not prevent him crossing the hall, and it was there—at the foot of the stairs, with the household arranged around him like mourners at a graveside too damp for digging—that he saw me.

I do not know what he read in my face. I had meant to arrange it into nothing; I had seven years' practice at nothing. But perhaps nothing, at close range, has its own texture. He stopped. The constables shifted. And Lord Halloran looked at me across the width of his own entrance hall the way a man looks at the last lit window of a village he is being marched out of.

"Miss Thornbury," he said. "See to it the workshop accounts are kept current. Whoever inherits the mess will want them legible."

It was a message and a farewell and an apology, all three folded flat, and I understood that he knew—I could not say how, but I knew he knew—that there were papers somewhere in this house worth keeping legible. Whether he had taken my pages hoping to bargain and repented of it, or whether he only guessed at the shape of what I carried against my ribs, I could not tell. His eyes rested on me one heartbeat longer than propriety allowed either direction.

"Yes, my lord," I said.

Then they walked him out. Through the gate, past the lodge, onto the gig like a poacher, and the horse turned its head against the cold and the wheels began to turn, and Emrys came running down the drive in his shirtsleeves with his collar open as though he had dressed in mid-fall and not finished, shouting something—"Father! Father, I'll ride to Norwich, I'll—" until one of the hired men turned his horse across the road and Emrys stopped as though he had struck glass.

I watched from the gate. That is what I did that morning; I am a woman made for watching gates. The gig went small down the frost-white avenue of elms, and the mist took it the way water takes ink, and the sound of the wheels lasted long after the sight of them was gone.

Beside me, Emrys stood shaking. Not weeping—shaking, the fine continuous tremor of a man whose body has been told to run at something and refused permission. His breath made small torn clouds.

"They didn't even let him take his own coat," he said. "Cyrus. They didn't let him take—"

"No," I said. "They didn't."

That was all we said. Some griefs are too wide for words to bridge at the first hour of the day; you can only stand at opposite rims and look down together.

* * *

A household does not scatter all at once. It scatters the way ice goes off a pond—from the edges inward, quietly, in the night, so that by morning the thing that held everyone up is simply not there anymore.

By noon the first notice was pinned inside the servants' hall: wages suspended until matters were resolved, any servant wishing to seek other situations would receive a character and a fortnight's pay from the estate steward—if the steward could be found, which by evening he could not. By two o'clock the laundry girls had packed. By four, one of the grooms was loading another man's trunks onto a cart and did not come back from the errand. Mrs. Prewitt moved through the corridors with a bunch of keys that jangled less every hour, because each ring held fewer rooms she still controlled.

I stayed. Of course I stayed. Invisibility has this advantage over loyalty: no one thinks to order you out.

But I felt it, all that day, the strangest sensation of my life—the sensation of ceasing to be unseen *against my will*. For seven years my safety had lived in the fact that no one important remembered I existed. Now the household was emptying, and every departure made me more visible, the way the last candle in a chandelier throws more shadow than all the rest. By evening there were perhaps nine of us left in a house built for thirty, and our footsteps echoed in the service passages, and the silence had a new quality—not the silence of work, which is full, but the silence of abandonment, which has teeth.

Emrys did not leave either. He spent the day writing letters—at least twenty, I counted the wax—and by lamplight in the study he burned half of them unopened replies or unsent, I never learned which, feeding them to the fire page by page with the deliberation of a man choosing which parts of himself to keep.

I brought him tea at nine because someone had to, and because bringing tea is what I do when I cannot bring anything else.

"My father took your pages," he said, without turning from the fire.

I stood very still with the tray. "Sir—"

"Don't 'sir' me today, Cyrus, I couldn't bear the weight of it." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I found where he'd hidden them. After the arrest I went to the study to burn things and there they were, sewn into the map-case lining. I know what he meant to do—trade them to Vane for the debts, buy himself out of the noose with your seven years." He looked up at me then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level. "I've already given them back. Yesterday. Before the constables came. You didn't know?"

"I didn't know," I said, and the ledger stirred against my ribs like something alive.

"Well." He took the tea. His hand steadied around the cup as though the warmth gave orders to the rest of him. "Then we are both of us poorer in fathers than we were last week, and neither of us can afford to lose the other. Go and sleep, Cyrus. Tomorrow we find out what Vane does to a family once its head is in Norwich gaol, and you will want your wits about you more than your sorrow."

I went. But I did not sleep.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I did instead, lying dressed on top of the blankets with one candle lit, doing what I have always done when the world becomes unbearable: I counted names.

In my ledger there are entries, dated and witnessed, of supply transactions from the winter of 1807—three of them, each carrying Vane's signature or Vane's initials or the initials of the treasury clerks who did his signing for him. I wrote those entries when I was nobody, a nurse with cold hands in a hut at Balaclava's aftermath, writing in a stolen interval because a sergeant had died unnamed and I could not bear it. They seemed harmless then. Ink against ink. A private book against the empire's public forgetting.

And those same entries—those three lines of mine—are the true pattern underneath the false one Vane has now sworn to. He has not invented the fraud. He has *reversed* it. Every transaction I recorded as his theft, he has re-dated, re-signed, re-addressed so that the money's road runs through Halloran hands instead of his own. He needed a ledger's worth of proof to hang on someone, and he could not use mine, so he built himself a copy with the names changed—the way a forger copies a painting and sells it as the original.

The household was destroyed today by omissions. That is the thought that would not leave me. Omissions I recorded faithfully, omissions that were true—but truth, in the wrong arrangement, is only another kind of weapon. Vane had not needed to forge much. He had needed only to take what was real and point it in a different direction, and gravity and the courts did the rest.

I got up. I lit the second candle. I unwound the linen from my ribs and opened the ledger to the front page, where my brother's name now stands written in full—*Josiah Thornbury, private, 33rd Foot, died of fever at Scutari, buried without marker*—and beneath it my own name, which I wrote there three weeks ago when I finally stopped hiding from myself.

And beside both, in the margin, I added a line in my smallest hand:

*Thomas Halloran arrested this day upon sworn falsehood. Present, and witness to it: C. Thornbury.*

Because that is what my book is for. It is not a weapon and it is not a shield. It is a witness. And a witness that stays silent at an arrest is no better than the dispatches that leave the dead sergeant out.

* * *

Dawn the next day brought the second blow, and it came, as blows do, in the shape of ordinary paper.

A notice nailed to the workshop door by a boy paid a shilling to ride out and vanish again: *By order of the creditors' committee, sealed inventory to be taken of all workshops, outbuildings, records, and effects on the Halloran estate. All persons found interfering with said inventory liable to prosecution.* Beneath it, the date—four days hence—and beneath that, the signature of the committee's inspector.

*A. Vane, Col.*

Four days. He would come himself, with his clerks, with his warrant and his hired boots, and he would walk through the workshop where Quill's lantern hangs in the window, and he would run his gloved finger along the shelves, and anything made of paper would go into his crates and come out at the assizes as evidence of a fraud he had authored.

I stood in the yard reading the notice while the frost smoked off the paddock fence, and behind me I heard Emrys stop at the workshop threshold. He had been up most of the night; I could see it in the set of his shoulders. But his voice, when it came, was quiet and terribly awake.

"He'll burn the records legally now," Emrys said. "No need for fire. He'll just carry them off in daylight and call it inventory." He looked at the lantern in the window—Quill's lantern, reground glass and new solder, burning even in daylight because neither of us had the heart to snuff it. "Cyrus. Whatever it is you've been carrying against your ribs since yesterday morning—and don't look at me like that, I've watched you strap it in twice—I need to know how fast you can copy."

I turned. The yard was empty. The house behind us stood with most of its shutters closed, breathing smoke from one chimney only, a great grey patient animal waiting to be put down.

"Fast enough," I said. "If the light holds."

"It'll hold," said Emrys, and pushed open the workshop door, and reached inside to turn the wick of the lantern higher.
