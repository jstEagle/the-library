## 29. Emrys Stands Alone

The house had a new silence in it, the kind that comes after a door has been shut for the last time.

Emrys walked the corridors at dawn because he could not sleep and because walking was the only act left to him that required no decision. His father's study stood open, drawers pulled out like tongues, the constables having taken what they wished two days past. The portrait of his grandfather still hung above the cold hearth, though someone—Vane's men, perhaps, or the bailiffs—had knocked it crooked on its wire, so that the old man now leaned eternally into the wall as if listening for something buried inside it.

Head of a disgraced house. He turned the phrase over as he walked, testing its weight the way he might test a hinge, and found it did not yet fit him, the way a dead man's coat does not fit until you have worn it through a season of rain.

The servants were going. Mrs. Prewitt had wept in the kitchen and packed her mother's china; the stable boys were already gone, their loft straw still holding the shapes of their sleeping. Cook stayed another week out of loyalty or inertia—he could not tell which, and suspected she could not either. By spring there would be no one. By spring, if Vane had his way, there would be no workshop either, no walls at all where a Halloran had ever kept anything worth keeping.

He ended, as he always ended, at the workshop door.

* * *

Inside, the morning light came gray and thin through the window where the lantern used to hang. Its bracket stood empty now, a small ring of clean wood amid the grime, like the mark left on a wrist by a watch worn for years. The shattered glass had been swept into a corner—his own blood had dried brown on the workbench where he'd gripped it after the workmen's cudgel found his shoulder—but Cyrus had not let him touch the frame itself. She had gathered the broken pieces in her apron like a bird gathering nest-material, saying nothing, and carried them somewhere he did not know.

She was not here this morning. He heard her upstairs instead, footsteps crossing and recrossing the floor of her room, the particular rhythm of a person pacing with a book in her hands.

Emrys stood in the middle of his father's workshop—the workshop that had been his grandfather's, that would be rubble by summer—and understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming on a trough, what he had to do.

She would not leave. He knew that already. But he owed it to whatever remained of himself to try, because the alternative was to stand at the assizes and watch her be taken the way his father had been taken, and he could not—his body refused it before his mind had finished the thought—could not carry that second weight on top of the first.

* * *

He found her at noon, or she permitted herself to be found at noon, sitting on the mounting-block by the paddock fence with the ledger open on her knees. The wind came flat off the fen and moved the dead grass in long silver strokes. She had wrapped herself in the gray shawl that made her, as always, nearly invisible against any winter landscape—a talent he had come to understand was not accident but architecture, built stone by stone over years of needing not to be seen.

"Cyrus."

She did not look up from the page. "Your shoulder should be bound tighter. You keep letting it sag."

"It's bound."

"Badly." She turned a page. The paper made a sound like a wing.

He sat down on the block beside her, the wood biting cold through his coat. For a while neither spoke. A crow dropped out of the pale sky onto the fence post, considered them with one flat eye, and left again, deciding they were nothing worth its attention.

"I have been doing sums," Emrys said at last. "It is the only kind of arithmetic I was ever good at. What a thing costs." He watched the fen rather than her face. "If you go north—your sister's people, or whatever name you choose on the road—you could be beyond a warrant's reach in nine days. Ten, in bad weather. The ledger goes with you, sewn into your petticoats the way you took it to London. It stays whole. It stays *yours*. And when the time is right—years from now, when Vane is old and comfortable and has stopped watching—someone publishes it. Every entry. Every name."

"And your father rots on a convict hulk in the meantime," Cyrus said quietly, "for debts that were half manufactured and half his own foolishness, while Vane sits in a good chair by a good fire."

"Someone survives," Emrys said. "That is not nothing. I have learned recently exactly how much it is not nothing."

She closed the ledger then, both hands flat upon its cover as if stilling something beneath it, and turned to look at him. Her eyes were the color of rain over chalk, and they held no softness that he could see—only that weary, terrible competence he had admired from across rooms for seven years without once daring to name.

"You have been rehearsing that speech," she said.

"Since dawn."

"Was it well received?"

"It hasn't finished being delivered."

"No," she said. "It hasn't." She looked back out at the fen. "Ask me properly, then. Not the clever version. The true one underneath it."

The wind moved between them. Somewhere behind the house a door banged—Cook, or the wind, or the house itself loosening joint by joint toward ruin.

"The true version," Emrys said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, roughened at the bottom, "is that I cannot do this without you. Not the trial. Not—" He stopped, started again. "I watched my father be taken through our own gate and I stood on the step like a boy because there was nothing in my hands. No proof. No witness. Nothing but my word that we are more sinned against than sinning, and my word is worth precisely what the world now thinks a Halloran's word is worth. You are the only thing in England that can make those men listen. And if you run, they will hunt you, and if they catch you the ledger dies with you, and if they don't catch you the ledger dies anyway, because a book in hiding is just paper. So the true version, Cyrus Thornbury, is that I am asking you to save yourself so that I don't have to watch you hang, and I know—I *know*—it is the sensible thing, and I hate every syllable of it, and I would rather burn the ledger myself than hand you over to the road alone."

Silence. The grass bowed and rose, bowed and rose.

"That was better," Cyrus said. "The true version." She unfolded her hands from the ledger's cover. "Now hear mine."

* * *

"Seven years ago," she said, "in the hospital tent at Balaclava, a sergeant named Thomas Quill maneuvered four hundred men out of a ravine that a captain had marched them into drunk. Quill died doing it. The captain wrote the dispatch. I stood three feet from the table while he wrote it, holding a basin, invisible, the way I was always invisible, and I watched him write *I ordered* where the truth was *he acted*, and something broke in me that has never mended and never needed to." Her thumb moved along the ledger's edge, an old caress. "So I began this book. Not as rebellion—that came later. At first it was only grief with nowhere to go. A dead man's name written down by somebody, *anybody*, so that the earth would hold it twice: once in the ground and once in ink."

"I know why you began it."

"Do you?" She turned her face to him fully then, and he saw for the first time how tired she was—not the tiredness of sleepless nights, which heals, but the tiredness of years, which only ends. "Then you know this also. A record is not a testimony, Emrys. They are two different animals that must hunt together or starve apart. Testimony without the book is hearsay—my word, Martha's word, the corporal's word, all of it floating loose, and Vane will swat each voice down separately the way you swat flies, because none of us carries rank or property or even, God help us, respectable sex. But the book without testimony—" she lifted it slightly, let it fall back to her knee "—the book without testimony is theft. Stolen documents, unprovenanced, in the hands of persons unknown. Vane would not even need to forge anything. He would simply say, *where did this woman get these papers*, and the question itself would be the verdict. I would be a thief before the magistrate finished his breakfast."

"So you stay. And when you take the stand, they take you."

"They may."

"They *will*. There is a warrant drawn, Cyrus. Vane told you himself what he would charge you with, and he is a man who keeps his promises to his enemies if he keeps them to no one else."

"Yes," she said. "He is. That is, in its rotten way, to his credit."

Emrys stood up. The cold had got into him somewhere during her speech and he could not find where; his hands wanted to shake and he forbade them. "Then I am asking you to let me carry half of it. Whatever half can be carried. If you testify, I stand beside you. If they arrest you after, I follow the assizes to wherever they send you and I—" his voice cracked and he hated it "—I will be there. At every gate. However long."

Cyrus rose too, slowly, the ledger hugged now against her chest like a child or a scripture, and looked up at him, and something in her expression shifted—the frost of it thinning, just at the edges, the way the fen ice thinned at the edges first though the middle stayed black and deep.

"That," she said, "is the most foolish offer anyone has ever made me. And I have received several."

"Cyrus—"

"Hear the rest." Her voice gentled, which frightened him more than anger would have. "You are offering to stand beside me. I am telling you what standing beside me costs. Vane does not stop at the witness. He ruined Finch. He ruined your father. He burned two buildings and struck a Halloran over the head in broad daylight because no one would swear to seeing it. If I testify, everything you have left—this house, whatever name remains to it, your own freedom—goes onto the table with mine. I did not spend seven years learning to be invisible so that I could watch the last visible thing I care about be destroyed for my sake."

The words *the last visible thing I care about* fell between them and lay there, and neither of them picked it up, and neither of them stepped around it. The wind filled the space where the sentence should have gone next.

"Then we are at an impasse," Emrys said finally. "Each of us willing to be ruined, neither willing to permit it."

A sound escaped her—not quite a laugh, but the place where a laugh had once lived before grief moved in. "We are at an impasse," she agreed. "Unless."

"Unless?"

"Unless we stop speaking of it as *mine*." She held the ledger out—not handing it over, exactly, but presenting it, the way a soldier presents arms, the way a witness presents evidence. "This book names four hundred and eleven people, Emrys. Dead sergeants. Unpaid widows. Clerks who signed what they were told to sign and hated themselves in private. Men your father cheated and men your father saved and men neither of them ever knew. For seven years it has been my book, my secret, my burden, because secrecy was how I survived and I did not know any other way to keep a thing safe. That time is finished. Secrecy is what Vane feeds on—one woman alone with a book, easily discredited, easily arrested, easily erased. He has beaten every opponent who stood singly." Her eyes were very steady. "He has never once faced two."

Emrys looked at the ledger between her hands. Under the gray sky its binding showed the wear of seven years—corners rounded, spine creased like a psalter, one water-stain blooming across the lower cover from a Crimean night he would never ask her to describe twice.

"You are proposing a pact," he said slowly.

"I am proposing that you learn this book. Every entry. Every date. Every cross-reference to Finch's draft and Martha's knowledge and the corporal's discharge papers—so that if they take me on the courthouse steps, you can finish the testimony. Not read it. *Be* it. A record needs two bodies now, not one: one to speak and one to remain." Her jaw set. "They can arrest the speaker. They cannot arrest the memory if the memory walks out of the courtroom wearing their own name."

For a long moment Emrys could not speak. The fen wind pushed at them both, patient as tide, and somewhere far off across the flat land a church bell was striking the hour with its old cracked note, one toll missing, as it had been missing for thirty years, and no one had ever fixed it because everyone had grown used to the gap.

"If they take you," he said, "and I finish it—they will take me too. You understand that. It isn't safety I'm buying. It's only continuation."

"It is only ever continuation," Cyrus said. "That is the whole of what any of us buys. Quill continued in four hundred living men. Finch continues in a hymnal. Your father will continue, God help him, in whatever restitution he manages before he dies." She opened the ledger at last, to the first page, and turned it toward him so that he saw, in her small upright hand, the columns of dates and names marching away into the years like soldiers who had finally been given their orders. "Take it."

He took it. It weighed less than he expected and more than paper should.

"Now," she said, and there was something almost light in her voice, something that might, in a kinder decade, have become mischief, "begin at the beginning, and tell me what you see."

Emrys bent his head over the first entry and began, haltingly, to read aloud, and Cyrus listened with her eyes closed and the wind pulling at her shawl, correcting him only twice, and between them the book passed—for the first time in seven years—out of one keeping and into two.

They were still reading when the light failed, and it was Cyrus who noticed it first: a rider on the London road, moving fast toward the estate, carrying something white in his hand that flapped like a gull in the wind.

"A summons," she said quietly, opening her eyes. "Or a warrant. By tomorrow we shall know which."

Emrys closed the ledger and held it against his chest, and together they turned to watch the rider come.
