## 30. Gathering Witnesses

The morning after Emrys stood alone in the ruined great hall of his family's name, I took the ledger from its hiding place and read it not as a record but as a summons.

Seven years of entries, and I had always understood them as a private act—a debt paid to the dead who had died without names and to the living who had been paid without wages, without credit, without mention. A ledger was a confession kept in secret; that had been its whole virtue. But a ledger in a courtroom was something else entirely. A ledger in a courtroom needed mouths. It needed hands to hold it up and voices to say *I saw this, I was there, this name is true*. Ink alone could be called forgery. Ink believed belonged only to the living.

So I made a list on the back page, beneath my brother's name and mine, written now in full where once I would have hidden them both: three people still breathing whom the book could vouch for, and who could vouch for the book.

Martha Quill first, because she had already said yes once, badly, with her eyes.

* * *

I walked to her cottage in the grey hour when the fen mist had not yet decided whether to be fog or simply cold air standing still. The path was frozen mud ridged like old candle wax, and my boots broke through the crust at every second step, so that the walk sounded—crunch, sink, crunch, sink—like some enormous slow animal following me. I did not mind. I have spent my life preferring sounds that mean no harm.

Martha was already out, of course. Widows of sergeants do not lie abed. She was at the pump in her yard, breaking the ice skin with the heel of her hand, and when she saw me she did not smile, which I respected. We had passed the point where smiles did work.

"You've come about the swearing," she said.

"I've come about nothing else."

She pumped water into a chipped jug and carried it inside, and I followed, because following is what I know how to do. The cottage smelled of peat smoke and last night's cabbage and the particular dry sweetness of wool dried too near a fire. On the mantel stood the hymnal—the one Finch had given her, though Martha did not yet know what slept inside its boards, and I had not told her, and would not until I must. She poured two cups of tea so weak it was more warm water than anything, and we sat, and for a while neither of us drank, because sitting with your hands around a cup is different from drinking out of it, and we both wanted the difference.

"They say Lord Halloran will hang," she said at last. "Or be transported. My Tom said once that transportation's worse. Hanging takes a minute. Transportation takes everything slowly."

"Lord Halloran will not hang for embezzlement. He may lose what he has. Those are different wounds."

"And Colonel Vane?"

"Colonel Vane," I said, "is not yet wounded at all. That is why I've come."

I opened the ledger on her table. I had thought about this part all the way down the frozen path—how much to show, how much to withhold—and in the end I showed her everything, because Martha Quill had earned the whole truth by living through half of it. I turned to the entries from the winter of 1811, the field hospital at the forward post, and I read her the line about Sergeant Thomas Quill, 33rd Foot, killed in the rearguard action at the ford, his maneuver credited in dispatches to Captain—then Major—Vane. I read her the follow-up entry, dated four days later, noting that the widow's pension claim had been refused on grounds of insufficient witness.

"Insufficient witness," Martha repeated. Her voice did not rise. It went flat and quiet, the way ice goes flat before it bears weight or breaks. "There were witnesses. There were eleven men who saw Tom hold the ford so the wagons could cross. Eleven men, and every one of them dead or scattered or afraid."

"One of them is in my ledger," I said. "Corporal Samuel Reyes, discharged with a ruined knee, last known address a lodging house in Ely. And there is a clerk, Nathaniel Pryce, quartermaster's office, who signed the requisition that shows Vane drew double rations and powder against Quill's company for three months after Quill was dead. Paper doesn't scatter. Paper waits."

Martha looked at the ledger a long time. In the small light from the window her face was the face I imagine on all the women history steps over—not bitter, exactly; used up in the way a tool is used up, honestly worn to its purpose. Then she put one finger, careful as a communicant, on Tom's name.

"If I stand up in a courtroom," she said, "and say what I saw—which I didn't see, mind; I was in England when it happened; I can only swear to what he wrote me, and to what Vane said to my face last month—"

"What he said to your face is evidence. He came asking about 'the Thornbury woman.' That is a man moving pieces, and men who move pieces can be shown the board."

"And if I stand up, they'll come for me the way they came for Mr. Finch." She said the name evenly. She knew of Finch's death; everyone in the village knew, in the way villages know things, by a kind of osmosis of dread. "Bridge. Fall. Suicide, they'll write. I can't swim, Mrs.—Cyrus. I never learned."

I had promised her safety once, dishonestly. I would not compound it now.

"No," I said. "I cannot keep you safe. If you testify, you will be at risk, and I will not be able to prevent what a determined man sends against you. What I can promise is this: you will not be invisible. Whatever happens, your name will be in the record beside Tom's, spoken aloud, entered into law. They will not be able to erase you afterward without erasing the verdict too. Erasure is what Vane does best. Testimony is the one thing that makes it expensive."

She was silent for the length of two breaths, three. Outside, a wren made its small mechanical noise and stopped.

"Tom used to say," she finally said, "that a soldier's real fear isn't dying. It's dying and nobody writing it down." She lifted her cup and drank the pale tea as if it were wine at a covenant. "Then I'll write myself down. Tell me when and where, and I'll be there with my bonnet on."

Walking back through the lifting mist, I found my hands were shaking—not with fear, I realized, but with the strange exertion of having asked someone for something and been believed. It is an effort I am out of practice at. Invisibility asks nothing of anyone. It only takes.

* * *

Corporal Samuel Reyes was harder.

Ely on the second day was all wet slate roofs and church bells, and the lodging house where the ledger said he lived turned out to be a yard behind a cooperage, a building leaning at the angle of a man listening hard. The landlady, a woman with flour on her forearms and suspicion in every line of her, told me Reyes was out—out meaning anywhere, meaning gone, meaning don't ask—and that gentlemen had already come asking after him twice this month.

"Gentlemen?" I said.

"Well-dressed ones. Army manner. Asked was he the same Reyes that served in the 33rd. Said they'd a pension matter to settle." She sniffed. "Sam went to the fen for a fortnight when the second one came. Pension matters, he says, come in envelopes. Men in good coats don't bring pensions door to door."

So I waited. I had learned waiting in hospital tents, where it is the entire profession: you wait beside men who are either dying or recovering and you cannot hurry either. I took a room in the town at a rate that pained me, and each morning I walked past the cooperage yard, and on the third morning a man was chopping kindling there with a leg planted wrong, knee locked straight, the whole swing of the axe coming from hip and shoulder because the knee would not bend.

He watched me come up the path without stopping his work. He had a face like a closed door in a wall of closed doors.

"Mrs. Thornbury," I said. "Nurse, 33rd. Crimea. I changed the dressing on your knee at Balaclava, November of '11, and you told me I pulled the lint gentler than the chaplain pulls sin."

The axe stopped. For a moment nothing in him moved except his eyes, which traveled from my face to the satchel at my side and back, doing arithmetic.

"They said you might come," he said at last. "After the gentlemen came. I thought: if she's alive, and if she still keeps that book, she'll come. Or I thought maybe I only hoped it, which is a different thing." He set the axe down against the block with great care, as if it were a sleeping child. "Come inside where the wind can't hear us."

His room was one bed, one chair, one stove, and a wall hung with a soldier's small museum: a bayonet, a folded flag too faded to identify, a wooden crucifix carved from shell-fragment wood. He lowered himself onto the bed with the stiff precision of a man for whom sitting down is a negotiated settlement, and I took the chair, and I put the ledger open between us.

"Reyes," I said, "at the ford you were the senior surviving NCO. You ordered the withdrawal after Quill fell. You reported the maneuver to Captain Vane in person, and Vane's dispatch credited himself. You signed a statement saying so, and then you withdrew the statement, and then you were promoted out of the regiment to a garrison post so far from anyone who remembered the ford that the memory itself lost its witnesses."

A muscle moved along his jaw. "You've got all that in there."

"Dated. In my hand. I wrote it the week it happened."

"Then you've got the part I'm ashamed of, too." He looked at the crucifix on the wall rather than at me. "I withdrew the statement because Vane sent for me and put a letter on the table from the paymaster showing my mother's debts—her lodgings, her burial club arrears—and he cleared them. All of them. While I sat there. Then he said, 'Statements are heavy things, Corporal. Heavy things need strong backs.' And I had seen what happened to men with weak backs. So I swapped my witness for my mother's grave being paid for." He turned and looked at me directly, and his eyes were the flat grey of gun metal. "That's what you'll get if I stand in your courtroom. You'll get a man who sold the truth once and can be told he'll sell it again. Vane's counsel will make me a liar in front of the magistrate before the first hour's done."

"He will try," I said. "And here is what answers him." I turned the pages to the entry he feared and read it back to him—all of it, including the part where I had written, in my own hand, *R. withdrew statement under pressure of financial inducement; R.'s original account consistent with three other accounts taken independently; the withdrawal changes nothing about what R. saw.* "I testified to your weakness seven years ago, Corporal, because a record that only holds clean hands proves nothing. It is the dirty testimony that convicts. Let them call you bought. You were bought—that is the truth, and the truth is the only weapon in this war that doesn't misfire."

For a long while he sat with his straight leg stretched out and his hands loose between his knees, and the stove ticked, and somewhere below us the coopers drove hoops with rhythmic muffled blows like a distant artillery practice.

"The ford," he said quietly. "It was Tom Quill's idea. Reroute the wagons over the shingle while we held the causeway. Vane was a mile back with the staff. When he rode up, the fight was done, and Quill was done, and Vane looked at the ground and looked at us and I watched him decide, right there, that the ground would remember him instead." He flexed his bad knee slowly, wincing. "Eleven years I've let that sit in me like shot. Doctors won't cut near it. Maybe a courtroom's the only forceps left."

"When I send for you—"

"I'll come." He said it fast, before courage could reconsider. Then, slower: "Send word through the cooper. Not by post."

I left Ely with my heart beating oddly high, the way it used to after a night when every man on the ward survived.

* * *

Nathaniel Pryce was the hardest of the three, because Pryce was not brave and did not pretend otherwise, and because I found him not in poverty but in fear of falling into it, which is a different engine entirely.

He lived now in March, in a brick terrace house with clean windows and a maid-of-all-work, having married above his station and spent seven years paying for it. He met me at the door himself, recognized me in one horrified glance, and pulled me into the parlor with the urgency of a man rescuing a brand from a carpet.

"Cyrus Thornbury," he whispered, as if the walls had ears retained by counsel. "In my house. Do you know what it means, that men from London have already been to my office? Asking about old requisitions? My wife thinks I've been gambling. I nearly let her think it."

"Better than letting her think the truth?" I asked, and had the grace to feel the cruelty of it even as I said it.

He sat down heavily on his own good sofa and put his face in his hands. He had grown soft and pink in civilian life, but his fingers still had the clerk's look—ink-stained at the edges of the nails, precise even in despair. "You don't understand the position of a man like me," he said through his hands. "I sign things. That is my whole life; I sign things that other men compose. Seven years ago I composed one thing of my own—one requisition, altered at Vane's instruction, initialing for stores drawn against a dead man's company—and I have been paying for that signature ever since, in installments of fear. And now you want me to carry it into a court and set it on a table."

"Yes."

"They will take my position. My pension contributions. My wife's brother stands surety for our lease—"

"Pryce." I leaned forward. I did not touch him; I have learned that frightened men flinch from hands. "I am going to tell you what you are, and you may believe it or not, but it is in the ledger either way. You are the only living man outside Vane's circle who knows the exact shape of the forgery—who initialed what, against whose name, on whose order. Finch is dead. The other clerks are bought or terrified. If you do not testify, Vane's version of that requisition becomes the only version, and my ledger becomes an accusation without corroboration, and the Hallorans drown on the strength of paper only you can contradict." I let that sit. Then, because he was still trembling, I added the only honest coin I had left: "I cannot promise you'll keep your place. I can promise you'll keep your soul, which is the item you've been mortgaging these seven years at ruinous interest."

He laughed then—a short, awful, genuine laugh, the laugh of a man caught out by his own conscience arriving late. "Ruinous interest," he repeated. "Yes. That's precisely the terms." He dropped his hands and looked at me, and behind the fear his clerk's mind was already working—I could see it, the little machine of columns and dates that had made him valuable to Vane in the first place. "The requisition of January '11. I kept a file copy. Against every rule, I kept a file copy, because I was frightened, and frightened clerks keep copies. It's in the house somewhere. If—" he swallowed "—if I'm to hang myself, I'll hang myself with documents."

"Three days hence," I said. "The magistrate's hearing at the assize courthouse. Martha Quill will swear to Vane's visit. Corporal Reyes will swear to the ford. You will lay the requisition on the table beside my ledger, and the three together will be a rope with three strands, and no counsel in England can untie it in an afternoon."

"And when Vane comes for me afterward?"

"Then he comes for all of us," I said, "in front of the magistrate, in daylight, on the record. Which is the only ground where we outnumber him."

He saw me to the door himself, glancing up and down the street like a smuggler, and just before I stepped out he said, quietly, without looking at me: "My wife will need to know something true. I've been composing lies for her all week. It's strange—this morning I couldn't remember which ones."

"That's how it starts," I said. "The truth. It starts with forgetting how the lies went."

* * *

I walked out of March in the last copper light of the third day, and the fen lay flat and gleaming on either side of the road like a sheet of hammered pewter, and I felt the strangest sensation I have felt since Balaclava: that I was not alone in knowing something.

All my life the knowledge has sat in me like lead sewn into a coat hem—heavy, hidden, mine alone to carry. Now it was distributed. A widow with her bonnet and her grief. A corporal with his straight leg and his shame converted at last to purpose. A clerk composing honesty for his wife as practice for a magistrate. Three ordinary lives, bent toward one courtroom, and my little book the hinge of all of them. The invisible, assembling into a chorus. I hummed—actually hummed, three bars of some hospital hymn—walking the darkening road with my satchel light and my heart not light but steady, steady as a pulse that intends to go on.

It was full dark when I came up the drive to the Halloran estate, and the first thing I saw was the workshop window, and the lantern burning in it—Emrys's defiance, Quill's name in reground glass—and beneath it a rider's horse tied at the gate, lathered, head drooping, saddle dark with road mud. The rider himself was in the kitchen doorway talking low and fast to Emrys, and though I was still forty yards off and the night had no moon, I knew the posture of bad news the way I once knew the smell of gangrene before the wound showed.

Emrys turned and saw me across the yard. Even at that distance I could see him weigh something—the old courtesy, perhaps, the instinct to spare me until morning—and discard it. He crossed the gravel quickly, hatless, holding a folded newspaper.

"Cyrus." His breath smoked. "This came on the evening coach. A warrant's been drawn. In London." He held the paper out, and his hand was not quite steady. "It has your name on it. Forgery of army records."
