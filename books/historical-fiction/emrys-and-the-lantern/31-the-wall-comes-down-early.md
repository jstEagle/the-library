## 31. The Wall Comes Down Early

The frost had come down hard in the night, and by first light the whole estate wore a skin of white glass. Emrys woke to a sound he could not at first name—a deep, rhythmic knocking, patient as a heartbeat—and lay still in the grey half-dark of his room while his mind assembled it from its parts: iron on oak. A knocker. Not the front door's silver one that the vicar used, but something heavier, blunter, the kind of instrument men carried when they intended to enter whether they were admitted or not.

He was dressed before he had finished thinking about it, coat over nightshirt, boots unlaced, and he took the back stairs three at a time with the cold pressing against him like water. Through the kitchen window, in the blue hour before sunrise, he saw them: six men gathered at the gate of the yard, breath rising from their collars in pale plumes, a waggon behind them loaded with pick-irons, crowbars, and coils of rope. Two lanterns swung from the waggon's rail, throwing long shivering shadows across the frost.

They had not been expected until Thursday. He knew this because he had counted the days himself, marked them in pencil on the frame of his workbench like a condemned man marking a cell wall. Ten days, the magistrate's notice had said. They were giving him three.

One of the men saw him at the window and nudged another. Six faces turned upward toward the glass, and Emrys understood, with the peculiar clarity that arrives only at moments of ruin, that this was not carelessness. This was design. Vane wanted the workshop down before anyone official could ride out to see it standing—before any magistrate's man could walk its floor and wonder why an honest family would burn paper twice and then pull down the building that held it.

He went out through the yard door without stopping to decide what he would say, because there was nothing to say that would matter, and words spent on men who arrived with pick-irons before dawn were words wasted.

* * *

The frost cracked under his boots like thin crockery. The air smelled of iron and old smoke—the stable records had burned nine days since, and the smell had worked itself into everything, into the wood of the fences, into his own hair, he suspected, so that he carried the fire with him now wherever he went.

The foreman was a broad man with a face weathered to the colour of a saddle and eyes that did their work quickly and looked away. He held a folded paper in one mittened hand and extended it as Emrys came near, the way a man offers a document he hopes will end the conversation for him.

"Notice," the foreman said. "Signed Tuesday last. Magistrate's order for demolition of the outbuildings, east range." He cleared his throat. "Dated for today."

"It is dated for Thursday." Emrys did not take the paper. "I have read the notice. I have it pinned above my bench."

"Amended," the foreman said, and there was, beneath the flatness of it, something almost apologetic. Almost. "New date. New signature. We're paid from first light, Mr. Halloran, and paid double if we're done by dark. There's men behind us want the work, if we won't."

Emrys looked past him at the others. They were not villains. That was the thing he had never learned how to hold: they were never villains. They were men with winter coats gone thin at the elbows and children who needed bread, standing in the cold before dawn because someone had offered them double wages, and the thing being pulled down was only a building, and buildings were only buildings unless you knew what was inside the walls.

Behind him, above the workshop door, the lantern burned in its window—the Crimean field piece he had spent months restoring, hung there weeks ago in open defiance of his father's orders, Quill's name ground into the glass panel so that the flame behind it lit the letters from within like a relic in a church niche. He heard the men notice it. One of them, younger, tilted his head at it and said nothing.

"You'll have to wait for the magistrate's man," Emrys said. His voice surprised him by being steady. "There's an inspection scheduled. There's evidence in that building pertinent to a trial."

"Evidence," the foreman repeated, tasting the word. "Begging your pardon, sir, but the paper says otherwise. The paper says a derelict structure, unsafe, to come down for materials." He folded the notice away into his coat. "I've got a paper too. Papers are all we've got between us this morning, and mine's got the bigger seal."

For a moment Emrys felt the old uselessness rise in him, the sensation that had governed most of his life—that the world was conducted entirely in papers and seals and signatures, and that a man who could not produce the right one simply ceased to exist, the way the sergeants in Cyrus's ledger had ceased to exist, written out by other men's pens.

Then he thought of her, somewhere in the house behind him—he prayed she was still in the house—and thought of what was inside the east wall of the workshop, behind the third lath from the corner, wrapped in oilcloth: seven years of names, the only true account of what had happened to those men, hidden in a cavity no wider than a hymnal is thick.

They were going to take the wall down in the next hour. If she did not get to it first, it would go into the waggon with the rest of the rubble, and by evening it would be ash on the wind, and every name in it would die a second time.

"No," Emrys said.

The foreman sighed, and it was the sigh of a man who has seen this before and knows how it goes. "Sir—"

"I said no." Emrys walked past him, past the waggon, past the men with their irons, and put his back against the workshop door. The wood was bitter cold through his coat. "You can write in your book that you were obstructed. You can tell whoever sent you that the son stood in the doorway. But you are not coming through this door while I am standing in it."

Nobody moved. The lanterns hissed. Somewhere beyond the hedge a robin was singing, absurdly, in the dark, the way robins do before the light comes, claiming territory it could not yet see.

"Mr. Halloran," the foreman said, gently now, the gentleness of a man handling something breakable, "there's six of us, and there's one of you, and the day's already started."

"Yes," Emrys said. "I'd noticed."

* * *

It went slowly at first, which he had not expected. He had expected to be moved immediately, handled like furniture, and instead the men stood shifting their feet and looking at the frost and at each other, and the youngest one said, quietly, not to anyone in particular: "That's Quill's name. On the lamp. In the window."

Emrys turned his head. "You know it?"

"My grandfather served with a Quill. Thirtieth Foot. Died Crimea." The young man's jaw worked. "Never got a stone. Gran keeps his likeness on the mantel and says his name to it every Sunday, so it shouldn't be lost." He looked at the glowing letters in the glass a moment longer, then down at his boots.

The foreman said, "Tom," in a voice meant to end things, but the word hung there in the freezing air and did its own work. These were men whose fathers and brothers had marched off under officers' signatures, and some of them had come home and some had not, and the ones who had not come home had mostly not come home *anywhere*—no grave, no marker, no line in any book—so that their families kept them alive the way poor people always have: by speaking names aloud to empty rooms.

Emrys stood against the door and let the silence do what it would. His heart was going hard enough that he could feel it in his teeth. Behind him, through the door, he could hear the workshop's familiar sounds—the tick of cooling metal, the settle of old timber—sounds he had slept beside all his life without knowing he was listening, and which he was hearing now, he realised, perhaps for the last time.

A light appeared at an upper window of the house. Then another, moving along the gallery. Someone was awake. Someone was coming down.

Not quickly enough.

"Right," the foreman said at last, and the word fell like a gate. "I'm sorry for it, sir. Genuinely." And he made a small motion with two fingers, and two of the men took Emrys by the arms—not brutal, not yet, merely firm, the way you move a beast or a drunkard—and drew him aside from the door, and the foreman himself put his shoulder to it and the lock, which had never been strong, gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"No—" Emrys wrenched free, and this time when he planted himself it was in the doorway itself, inside the frame, filling it with his body, and he heard his own voice go high and strange: "There is a true record in this building. Do you hear me? Whatever they told you is in these papers—whatever story they bought—it isn't the truth. The truth is in here, and they are paying you double wages to burn it before anyone can read it."

"Sir," the foreman said wearily, "I don't get paid to read."

And then the light came.

It came around the corner of the stable block, small and yellow and steady, swinging at the end of an arm, and Emrys knew the rhythm of that swing the way he knew his own heartbeat, because he had watched it from his window past midnight for years without once letting himself understand why he watched. Cyrus came fast across the frozen yard with her cloak flying and the lantern held high, and her face above it was perfectly blank—the serving-woman's blankness, the nurse's blankness, the face she wore when the world was at its worst and she intended to survive it—but her eyes found Emrys in the doorway and passed over him with one flicker of acknowledgment, and then she was past him, through the door, into the dark of the workshop, and the door closed behind her, and he heard the bar drop.

"Here now—" the foreman began.

"She's removing personal effects," Emrys said, and blessed whatever instinct put the lie in his mouth so smoothly. "Women's things. Medicines. You can't mean to put workmen among them."

"I mean to put a pick through that wall within the quarter-hour," the foreman said, "because that's what I'm paid for," and he signalled again, and this time four men came at once, and Emrys learned in the next minute exactly how little dignity matters against four pairs of hands.

They did not beat him, quite. They hauled him backward off the step and he fought them, unreasoningly, uselessly, and got one arm loose and drove an elbow into somebody's ribs and was spun around by the shoulders, and someone's fist—accidental or not, he would never be sure—took him across the left cheekbone, and the world went white and sideways and he sat down hard on the frozen cobbles with the taste of copper filling his mouth. Blood came warm over his lip and froze at his chin even as he wiped at it.

Through the ringing in his ears he heard the bar lifted. He heard boots on the workshop floor. He heard the foreman say, "Wall first. Third bay. Mark it," and the tap of a chalk line snapped taut, and the small shriek of chalk on plaster, and he thought—*she needs more time, God, she needs more time*—and got a knee under himself and rose.

He did not remember deciding to rise. He simply found himself upright, swaying, blood running freely now, planting himself between the workmen and the east wall with his arms out, and he heard himself saying, over and over, words he could not afterwards recall, only the fact of them, the stubborn animal repetition of them, while the men closed in and the first crowbar came up onto a shoulder ready to swing.

Someone shouted. It might have been him; it might have been one of theirs. The crowbar came down—not at him, at the wall, at the third lath from the corner—and the first blow bit plaster, and the second blew a hole through the lathwork, and Emrys threw himself against the nearest man's back and was thrown off, and went down again, and this time a knee arrived in his back and held him to the cobbles with the frost burning through his shirt and his cheek pressed to the ground, so that he saw the whole of it sideways and upside down: the boots crossing the yard, the crowbar rising and falling, the wall of his childhood opening like a wound—

—and then a hand came out of the gap in the wall.

Small, quick, certain. It withdrew holding a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, no bigger than a Bible and no less terrible, and it vanished again, and the whole transaction took perhaps four seconds, and of the six men in the yard not one of them understood what had been taken out of that wall or why a woman's arm reaching through broken lathwork should make the youngest workman, Tom, stop mid-swing and cross himself with his mitten still on.

"The lamp," Emrys said into the cobblestones. His voice came out ruined. "Leave the lamp. Take whatever else you like—take the roof—but leave the lamp in the window."

Whether it was the blood on his face, or the name in the glass, or simple human fatigue with the whole rotten morning, something shifted in the yard. The knee lifted off his back. The foreman stood looking up at the little lantern with its flame guttering behind Quill's engraved name, and his weathered face did something complicated and unhappy, and then he said, in a voice pitched for the record rather than the moment:

"Structural hazard. Lamp's part of the fixture. Has to come down with the rest."

They did it carefully, which was somehow worse than doing it savagely. One man climbed and unhooked it and passed it down, and another reached for it, gloved, and the frost had made the iron hook slick, and it slipped—slipped between two sets of hands with the slow, horrible deliberation of things that fall precisely when falling matters least—and struck the cobbles.

The glass burst. All of it, all at once, the panes and the ground-glass chimney and the engraved panel where a dead sergeant's name had been ground letter by letter by a man who owed him that much, and the flame died in the same instant, and the pieces skated glittering across the frost in the new light of the risen sun, catching fire briefly as the daylight touched them, so that for one moment the yard seemed strewn with embers.

Emrys lay on the stones with his blood freezing to the frost and did not move. Above him the sky had gone the colour of a blade's edge, and the robin was still singing, and the men were already at the wall, and the sound of it coming down was the sound of a great tree felled, groaning, splitting, giving up at last.

But the bundle was gone from the wall. The bundle was under Cyrus's cloak, going somewhere he could not see, and he pressed his split cheek against the frozen ground and laughed—one short, broken sound that nobody heard—because the symbol had shattered on the cobbles and the truth had walked out of the yard alive, and he knew, lying there, that this was the entire arithmetic of his life henceforth: the glass breaks; the name survives.

* * *

They let him sit against the fence while the work proceeded. It was, he supposed, a kind of mercy, or perhaps only indifference; a bleeding man out of the way is a bleeding man dealt with. Through the morning the workshop came apart in stages, the roof first, then the rafters, then the walls he had known board by board, and the smell of old oil and iron and beeswax rose out of the opened building like the smell of a body when the chest is opened, and he sat with his chin on his fists and watched seventeen years of his life sorted into piles: salvage, scrap, burn.

Cyrus did not reappear. He had not expected her to. She would be moving through the house now in her perfect blankness, ledger secured where no search would think to look, wearing invisibility like armour, and the thought steadied him in a way that nothing else that morning managed to do.

Near noon a carriage came up the drive—too fine for workmen, too plain for gentry—and Emrys's stomach dropped before he even saw the colours of the man who stepped down from it, because he knew that tread on the gravel, that unhurried, proprietary tread, the tread of a man arriving to inspect his purchase.

Colonel Vane came through the gate with a clerk scurrying behind him carrying a leather case, and surveyed the half-demolished building with an expression of mild satisfaction, as a man surveys a field he has had drained. Then his gaze travelled along the fence and found Emrys sitting in the wreckage-dust with the blood dried black on his cheek, and Vane smiled the smile Emrys had watched him deploy at his father's dinner table, all warmth on the surface and nothing beneath it but measurement.

"Mr. Halloran." Vane clicked his tongue, softly, sympathetically. "You really ought to have that seen to." He glanced at the scattered glass, the dead lantern's brass skeleton on the cobbles, and back at Emrys, and his voice dropped into something almost intimate. "You know, it occurs to me that your Miss Thornbury was not at her duties this morning. A servant gone missing on a working day—that's the sort of irregularity a magistrate likes documented. Wherever might she have got to?"

Emrys looked up at him through the blood-crusted swelling of his eye and said nothing at all, and behind his ribs his heart hammered out the only answer that mattered: *gone, ahead of you, already gone.*
