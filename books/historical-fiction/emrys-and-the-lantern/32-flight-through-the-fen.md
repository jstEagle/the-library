## 32. Flight Through the Fen

The frost had come down hard in the night, and the fen wore it like a second skin—white and brittle and treacherous as a liar's promise.

We left before the light did. Cyrus Thornbury, who had spent seven years learning to move through houses unseen, now moved across open country with nothing to hide behind but reeds and darkness, and found she missed walls the way a fish misses water. The ledger was bound against my ribs beneath my cloak, sewn where I had once sewn dispatch pages into petticoat hems; Finch's draft lay inside the hymnal, wrapped in oilcloth, riding against Emrys's chest as though he were carrying another man's heart in place of his own. Perhaps he was.

Behind us, on the rise above the workshop, torches moved. Not many. Four men could do this work; five at most. Vane had never needed an army. He had only ever needed patience, money, and the certainty that no one would stand in his way—and for thirty years the world had obliged him.

"They'll take the road," Emrys murmured at my shoulder, his breath ghosting silver. "It's faster."

"The road has eyes." I pointed east, where the fen lay flat and grey-white to the horizon, stitched with drainage cuts and sheep tracks and the ghosts of old peat workings. "We go the way nobody goes. The way I went when I was collecting names nobody wanted."

He looked at me then—a long look, the kind he'd been giving me more often since the storm night, since we'd sat shoulder to shoulder over the copying until the candles drowned in their own wax. "You know this ground?"

"I know every ditch between here and Ely," I said. "I walked it the winter Martha Quill's rent fell due and her landlord wanted paying in ways she couldn't pay. Nobody follows a servant girl into the fen. They think the fen eats us all eventually."

* * *

Dawn came up the colour of skimmed milk, and with it came the sound that turned my blood to ice water: dogs.

Not hounds in full cry—this wasn't a fox hunt—but two low, methodical voices of animals working scent along the road we had refused to take, and then swinging off it. Vane's men had thought of dogs. Of course they had. A man who steals signatures thinks of everything a signature can be traced by, including footprints and sweat and fear.

"Down," I said, and pulled Emrys into the reed bed as the first grey light showed us the flat beyond: two figures and two shapes moving low and fast, quartering the ground a half-mile back, coming on with the terrible unhurried confidence of things that know the quarry is tired.

We crouched in the frozen reeds while my knees screamed and my breath wanted to tear itself out in sobs, and I made it small and silent because I had learned long ago how to make pain small and silent. Beside me Emrys held utterly still except for his eyes, which tracked the hunters the way a hare tracks a hawk—not in fear, I realized, but in calculation. He had changed since his father's arrest. Something soft in him had been rendered down to sinew.

"They're casting toward the cut," he breathed when the shapes had passed behind a stand of black poplars. "They expect us to go around the water."

"They're right. Sensible people go around the water."

"Cyrus." He said my name—he had earned it, these last weeks, from *miss* to *Thornbury* to *Cyrus*, each step a small treason—and there was something almost like humour in his face, bloody-lipped and frost-burned as it was. "How much of this fen freezes solid, and how much only pretends?"

I looked at him and understood what he was asking, and hated him for it, and loved him for it, all in the same cold instant.

"The drains freeze at the edges and lie underneath," I said. "The cuts are deeper. The old peat workings—" I stopped.

"The old peat workings are bottomless," he finished. "But the ice on top looks the same as the ice everywhere else."

"Yes."

"And they don't know this ground."

"No," I said. "But neither, Emrys, do we. Not well enough for this."

"We have the book," he said simply. "They want the book. Everything else is arithmetic."

* * *

We went out onto the ice at mid-morning, under a sky so pale and wide it seemed the fen had swallowed the world and left nothing above us but its own reflection. The surface crackled under our boots like breaking crockery, and every crack went through me like a hand closing on my throat.

I had crossed frozen drains before—servants' shortcuts, unremarked and unrecorded, the geography of invisibility. But always in shallow places, always knowing the mud waited three feet down to catch you like a soft dark palm. This stretch, where the old workings flooded together into a broad mere masked by snow and reeds, was different country. Here the water remembered being deep. Here the ice was a rumour rather than a fact.

Emrys went first, testing each stride with the iron pry-bar he'd carried from the workshop—the one piece of salvage he'd refused to leave, saying a man should carry one tool of his trade even into exile. When the bar rang hollow he shifted our line; when it bit and held he waved me forward. We moved like this, call and answer, ring and hold, an inching procession across a white table laid over a black throat.

Behind us, faint on the wind, the dogs gave tongue.

Closer now. Close enough that I could hear the men's voices, thin and carried: *"There—see the reeds broke—"*

"They've found where we came up," Emrys said quietly. He didn't turn. "Keep walking. Even pace. If we run, the ice will know."

That was the truth of the fen, and he had learned it in ten minutes: the ice knew everything. It knew haste. It knew weight gathered on one foot. It knew fear, somehow, the way animals know a slaughterhouse. So we walked, evenly, absurdly, like Sunday strollers, while behind us men began to run.

The crack when it came was not loud. That was the horror of it—it was intimate, a sound under my feet and nowhere else, like something clearing its throat directly beneath my boot sole. Then the whole world tilted, very slightly, the way a dream tilts, and the reeds ahead of Emrys shivered though there was no wind.

"Hold still," I whispered.

The crack travelled. I heard it go—away from us, mercifully, out toward the middle of the mere, a long ripping whisper like a page being torn slowly from a book. Then silence, and the dogs, and the running men.

And then, forty yards ahead, right in our path, the ice spoke again.

"Off the line," Emrys said. "Bear left. There—where the reeds are thick. Reeds mean shallows."

Reeds meant shallows, usually. I followed him leftward, and the dogs came on, and the world narrowed to the next stride and the next.

I never saw the place where the ice ended. I only knew that my next step, the step I had already begun, met no resistance at all—and that Emrys's hand closed on my cloak and hurled me sideways with a strength he had never shown me before, and that I struck the ice flat and slid, and behind me, where I should have been, the black water opened its mouth.

The ledger hit the edge of the hole.

I heard it happen before I understood it—that soft, awful slap of oilcloth and board on wet ice, the slide, the tipping balance of everything I had written for seven years sliding toward the gap where the fen kept its secrets. Emrys dropped to his belly at the broken edge, one arm plunged to the shoulder into the freezing water, and hauled, and the water hauled back.

"No—" My voice came out cracked in two. I flung myself flat and caught his ankle, and the cold came up through my sleeves and into my bones like the fen reaching for both of us at once. His other hand came out of the water holding the ledger, streaming, dripping, alive—and then his body jerked backward, dragged, and I understood with a clarity that had nothing to do with thought that the water had him now, that his coat had filled like a sail, that the fen had decided to keep one of us after all and preferred the warm one.

"*Let go of the book,*" I gasped.

"No," he said.

It was the most ordinary word. Flat, calm, stubborn as his father and nothing like him at all. His chin was on the ice, his cheek against it, frost and water and blood from his split lip all mingling, and his arms did not loosen.

So I did what nurses do. I did not think about the future or the arithmetic or the men crashing through the reeds behind us. I put my whole life's knowledge of bodies and weight and leverage into one act: I got my knees under me, hooked his arm, and threw myself backward away from the hole with everything I had, screaming—not words, just sound, the sound I had never once let out in seven years of silence.

The ice groaned. Held. Groaned again. And Emrys came out of the water the way a birth happens—all at once, violent, inevitable—landing on the white surface with the ledger still locked against his chest, both of us soaked, both of us shaking, the hole closing its black eye behind us and going still.

For a moment neither of us moved. Steam rose off his shoulders into the bitter air. His teeth were chattering so hard I could see his jaw jumping.

"You nearly drowned," I said stupidly.

"Not yet," he said. "Though I take your point about sensible people."

Then the dogs burst from the reed line, two hundred yards off, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

We ran then. Properly ran, ice be damned, because the ice between us and the hunters was thicker here near the shore and because terror is its own kind of arithmetic. Once the surface boomed under us like a drum and I felt the crack race past my boots and away, and Emrys laughed—a wild, gasping, half-drowned laugh—and we kept running.

We came off the mere through a screen of dead reeds and dropped into a peat cutting overgrown with birch scrub, and there we crouched among the roots while the pursuit reached the shore of the mere and stopped.

I watched them through the stems. Two men and the dogs, standing at the broken hole, at our tracks, at the wide white expanse beyond. One of them knelt and put his hand flat on the ice. The other turned his head slowly, reading the fen the way Vane read a room.

"They know we crossed," Emrys breathed against my ear. His lips were blue. "They'll go round. North shore. Twenty minutes, maybe less."

"Then we spend twenty minutes well." I was already working at his coat. Wet wool kills faster than water does; I had seen men die of it by inches in Crimea, good men who'd stood in a wet trench and simply cooled, like lamps going out. "Boots off. Coat off. Shirt wrung. Now, and don't be modest, I've seen worse than you in far worse states."

He obeyed, shuddering, while I stripped off my own sodden cloak and dug into the bundle on my back. Martha Quill had packed it—I had not asked her to, she had simply known, the way women who have buried soldiers know—and inside, wrapped in flannel, were a dry shift, a wool blanket, and a stub of bread and cheese that I could have wept over. I got the blanket around Emrys's shoulders and rubbed his arms and back with rough quick strokes, nurse's hands, no ceremony, and somewhere in the middle of it he caught one of my hands and held it against his chest, over his hammering heart, and said nothing at all.

I let it stay there for three breaths. I counted them. Then I took my hand back, because twenty minutes is twenty minutes.

"Finch's draft," I said. "Tell me it stayed dry."

He unwrapped the hymnal from his shirt with fingers that shook, and the oilcloth had done its work; the pages inside were damp only at the corners, Finch's cramped confession intact, the names standing up out of the paper as if they knew they'd been rescued. *Colonel H. Vane. Aldous Finch, clerk. R. Pettifer, clerk.* Three names in a dead man's hand.

The ledger was worse. Water had found the boards, wicked up into the outer quire. I opened it with the care of a woman opening a wound, and there they were—my dates, my names, Sergeant Quill's maneuver credited to Captain Vane, dated in my own hand seven years ago—the ink feathered at the edges but legible, legible, thank God, legible.

"It will warp as it dries," I said. My voice did something strange on the last word. "Pages will cockle. A magistrate might say—"

"A magistrate might say a great deal," Emrys said. "But every name is still there, Cyrus. You wrote them too deep to drown." He looked at the open page, at Quill's entry, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and rough. "He nearly took it instead of me, you know. The water wanted it more."

"Books float," I said. "Men sink."

"That's not what you were afraid of."

No. It was not. I had stood at the edge of that hole with my whole life's testimony sliding into black water and discovered that what tore in me was not for the pages at all. But there were dogs on the far shore beginning to swing north, and some confessions, like some entries, must wait for a safer page.

* * *

We moved again within the hour, south and east, away from the road and the north shore and the patient arithmetic of Vane's men, along a raised causeway Martha had told me of—an old drover's track, stone-cored, running above the bog like a spine. The afternoon came down grey and cold and clean, and the fen opened around us, vast and indifferent, taking no sides.

Emrys walked beside me under the blanket with the ledger and the hymnal distributed through our clothes again, close against the skin, where a body's heat could keep them the way a heart keeps blood. Twice he stumbled with exhaustion and twice I steadied him, and the second time he stayed leaning on my arm a moment longer than balance required, and neither of us remarked upon it.

Near dusk we saw, far behind, two figures rounding the northern shore of the mere, small as punctuation. They had lost the scent at the water, and the water was not giving it back. But they were still coming. Men paid by Vane did not stop coming; that was the whole of his genius, that he bought persistence.

"There's a turf-cutter's hut past the second sluice," I said. "Martha knows it. She'll bring food and word of the magistrate's court. From there—" I stopped. From there lay London, and witnesses, and a courtroom, and the end of hiding. From there I would have to stand up in front of strangers and read aloud every name I had ever written, and be seen, wholly and permanently, which was the thing I had spent seven years learning never to be.

Emrys watched me not finish the sentence, and nodded slowly, and finished it for me in his own fashion.

"From there we stop running," he said. "You testify without the book, it's hearsay. The book without your voice is theft. Both, or nothing." He smiled, blue-lipped, exhausted, certain. "Both, Cyrus."

Ahead of us the causeway ran on into the failing light, and behind us the fen held our pursuers and our tracks and the hole in the ice where I had learned, finally, in cold water and one desperate scream, exactly what I was unwilling to lose. Somewhere beyond the horizon, Vane believed his men would return with a burning in their report and my ledger in their hands.

Let him believe it. Tomorrow we would be in Ely. And by the time Colonel Vane learned that the book had crossed his fen and slipped through his hired hands like a name through a clerk's fingers, I would already be sitting down by candlelight in a turf-cutter's hut, drying the pages one by one, rehearsing the reading of them aloud—starting, as I had started seven years ago, with a sergeant named Quill, whom the water had tried to claim twice, and failed.
