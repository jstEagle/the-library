## 33. Sanctuary and Strategy

The cottage stood at the end of a lane that was not so much a lane as an apology for one—a rutted track half-drowned in frozen mud, hedged with blackthorn, invisible from the turnpike unless a traveler already knew it was there. Cyrus had known it was there. She had carried the knowledge of it for seven years the way she carried everything else: folded small, kept dark, brought out only when needed.

Now she stood before its door with fen water frozen in the hems of her skirts and her whole body still shaking from the crossing of the drainage cut, and she could not make her fist close enough to knock. It was Emrys who lifted the latch—Emrys, whose lips were blue beneath the split at his brow, who had gone into the icy water to his shoulders to save a book he had once been ordered to burn.

Martha Quill opened the door before either of them could announce themselves. She was a broad woman gone narrow with grief and hard years, her hands scarred by laundry work, her eyes the flat grey of the February sky. She looked at Cyrus first, then at Emrys, then at the oilcloth bundle under his arm, and something moved across her face like wind over standing water.

"You came," she said. "I told myself you wouldn't, and you came."

"We were followed," Cyrus said. "Perhaps not yet. Perhaps tomorrow."

"Then don't stand on my step being perhapsed." Martha pulled them both inside by their sleeves, as if they were strays, as if she had done it a hundred times before—as perhaps she had, in another life, dragging wounded men out of tent-weather and into lamplight. "There's soup. There's bread. There's one bed and a loft and I'll thank neither of you to comment on the arrangement. Sit down before you fall down, and give me that bundle before you drip on it."

Emrys did not give her the bundle. He held it a moment longer, this young man with his ruined cuffs and his father's name gone rotten in his mouth, and Cyrus watched him weigh it—the instinct of a gentleman surrendering nothing into working hands against the newer, harder-won knowledge that some things survive precisely because they are entrusted.

He gave it to her. Martha set it on the table with the reverence of a woman who had buried a husband whose name had been stolen, and unwrapped the oilcloth, and looked down at the ledger as though it might look back.

"Well," she said quietly. "All those names. I thought I was the only one who remembered them."

* * *

The cottage had two rooms and a lean-to, and for three days it became a war office, a scriptorium, a chapel. The table was scrubbed white with sand and lye until the grain stood up pale as bone, and upon it they laid out the evidence of seven years as a surgeon lays out instruments: the ledger open, its pages softened with handling; Finch's confession draft, rescued from the hymnal, its ink browned but legible; sheets of cheap paper on which Cyrus copied entries in duplicate, her pen scratching through the long evenings like a mouse in the wainscoting.

Cyrus worked as she had always worked, which was to say invisibly even in plain sight—head bowed, hands quick, speaking only when spoken to. But the habit chafed now. She caught herself looking up. She caught herself answering questions no one had asked her, offering opinions, correcting Emrys's arithmetic about dates and pay scales with a firmness that would once have cost her her position. Seven years of silence were coming apart in her like wet rope, strand by strand, and she did not know whether what lay beneath was strength or simply all the noise she had swallowed.

It was Martha who organized them. This surprised Cyrus, though perhaps it should not have; a sergeant's widow learns the shapes of campaigns. Martha had no head for ledgers but an unerring instinct for sequence—what must be proved first, what a magistrate would ask second, where the soft places in a man's testimony lay that opposing counsel would go at like a terrier at a rat.

"He'll say you're a liar," Martha said, on the second evening, jerking her chin at Cyrus across the table. "That's how it goes. First he'll say you're a liar, then he'll say you're a woman and can't read, then he'll say the book's forged, and if all that fails he'll find some poor fool to swear he saw you write it different." She knotted her hands together. "They did less than that to hang Ned's corporal at Corunna, and nobody spoke for him at all."

"Then we must be certain no page contradicts another," Cyrus said. "And every date must be accounted for."

"And your hand must be known," Emrys said. He had been quiet by the fire, feeding it splinters of an old chair rather than burn good coal, saving everything the way soldiers save everything. "Not just legible. Known. They must believe the ledger is yours the way your face is yours."

"It is," Cyrus said, and heard how the words had changed weight since she had written her own name on the front page beside her brother's. "But belief is not proof. Proof is—" She stopped. Looked at the hymnal.

* * *

They came to the hymnal on the third morning, because everything else had been done and none of them wanted to say aloud that the last piece was the piece that mattered most.

Finch's confession named Colonel Vane. It named two treasury clerks. It described, in a clerk's cramped and careful hand, the dictation of the letter that had accused the Hallorans—the letter that had begun everything, that had come to the estate under seal in a hand Cyrus had recognized from her nursing days. What it lacked was any guarantee that Aldous Finch had ever existed at all, now that Aldous Finch had fallen from a London bridge with his coat buttoned and his pockets full of stones. Dead men confess anything. Vane need only assert that the draft was a fabrication planted among them, and the document that should have been ammunition became a noose.

"He wrote in margins," Cyrus had told them in the fen, gasping, soaked, clutching the book above the black water. "When we nursed together—he couldn't help himself. Margins, flyleaves, anything. He annotated the world."

So now Martha fetched the hymnal down from where it had lived for six years atop her wardrobe, wrapped in a stocking against moths and memory. It was a small volume, its boards warped by damp, *A Selection of Psalms and Hymns* stamped in faded gold. She set it on the table and did not open it at once. Her thumb moved over the cover as if reading braille.

"He gave me this the week after they told me Ned was dead," she said. "Official notice. Killed in action, no effects returned, no particulars. A printed form. He stood in my doorway holding this book like it was hot, this little grey man who'd cried at the sight of gangrene, and he said—" Her voice caught, steadied itself with the practiced motion of a woman who had steadied it ten thousand times. "He said, 'Mrs. Quill, the forms are wrong. The form says he fell. He didn't fall. He held the line.' And then he went away quick, before I could ask him what he meant, and I never asked, and he never said, and now he's dead too."

She opened the hymnal.

The margins were full.

Small, crabbed, upright letters ran along every psalm, dense as stitchwork—dates, cross-references, and beneath several of the numbers, in a hand identical to the confession draft, fragments of accounting. Cyrus bent close, her breath fogging the cold page, and read: figures for requisitioned stores that matched, entry for entry, columns she had recorded in Crimea seven years ago. Supplies drawn in Sergeant Quill's name after Quill was dead. Payments signed in his hand by a man whose hand it was not. Vane's early thefts, annotated by the clerk who had forged them, hidden in the only scripture Aldous Finch trusted.

"Oh," Martha said softly. "Oh, you clever, frightened man."

Emrys laid the confession draft beside the open hymnal, and the three of them leaned over both documents in the grey window light, comparing stroke by stroke. The *f*s with their crossed tails. The way Finch made his sevens with a bar through them, always, even in haste—even, apparently, in fear. The particular cramped *th* that looked like a man hunching his shoulders against weather.

"It matches," Emrys said at last. His voice was rough. He had been struck by a workman's stave two days ago and it showed in everything now—in the bruise mapping his temple, in the careful way he held his head, in a new economy about him, as if words cost blood. "Every character. Any examiner of handwriting will see it in a moment."

"But will they know it's *his*?" Martha asked. "Excuse me, Mr. Halloran, but a clever lawyer says: anyone can copy a hand. Says maybe the nurse copied it herself, being clever and frightened too."

Cyrus had already thought of this. It was the answer she had carried across the fen along with the ledger, the one thing the water had not touched.

"The hymnal is dated," she said. "Here—flyleaf, in his hand: 'For Mrs. Quill, with respect and sympathy, A. F., Christmas 1812.' Six years ago. Before any of this. Before Vane needed a frame, before the Hallorans were accused, before I was anything but a clerk in a workshop. If the marginal notes are in the same hand as the confession—and they are—then either Finch wrote both, or someone taught themselves to forge him years in advance, in secret, for a purpose no one could have foreseen." She closed the hymnal gently, as one closes a coffin. "Vane is ruthless, but he is not prophetic. No magistrate will believe he planned this in 1812. The past is the one place he cannot follow us."

Martha looked at the book a long moment. Then she took off her apron, folded it, and set the hymnal inside it, wrapping it the way she had once wrapped her husband's medals.

"Ned used to sing," she said, to no one in particular. "Badly. Out of tune and loud and proud of it. I never knew which psalms were his favorites till now." Her jaw tightened. "Well. He'll testify too, then, won't he. All of them will. Every name in that book of yours—they'll all get their say at last."

* * *

On the fourth day they rehearsed.

It was Emrys's idea—or rather, it began as his idea and became Martha's practice and Cyrus's ordeal. He played the part of counsel for the crown, pacing the tiny room with the firelight throwing his shadow enormous against the rafters, firing questions across the table in a voice pitched to carry to a courtroom he had never entered but understood the way gentry understand courtrooms: as architecture built to intimidate.

"State your name and station for the record."

"Cyrus Thornbury. Formerly nursing sister, regimental hospital, Crimea. Presently clerk to the estate workshop of Halloran Grange."

"And this book, Mrs. Thornbury. You claim it records the names of soldiers omitted from official dispatches. On whose authority?"

"On mine."

"On *yours*. A nurse. An unlettered woman keeping accounts of officers' conduct. Why should this court receive it?"

"Because the court has received nothing else." She heard her own voice level and clear and marveled at it. "Seven years of dispatches have erased these men. If my authority is insufficient, then let the court examine the entries against pay muster rolls, against quartermaster returns, against the signatures of men still living who will swear to what they saw. My book does not replace the record, sir. It repairs it."

Emrys stopped pacing. Something crossed his face—not triumph, not quite admiration, but the expression of a man discovering that a tool he had underestimated could cut stone.

"She'll try to rattle you," he said. "Vane's counsel. He'll bring up Crimea. He'll suggest your judgment was—affected. By what you saw. By grief, if he learns about your brother."

"He knows about my brother," Cyrus said quietly. "Or Vane does. He has known for years. That is why he fears the ledger and not merely the law."

The room went still. Even the fire seemed to hold its crackling.

"Why?" Emrys asked. "Why would he—"

"Because my brother died carrying orders Vane falsified. Because I was there when the surviving sergeant tried to report it and was silenced. Because everything in that ledger begins with the same wound." Cyrus found she was gripping the table edge and did not remember putting her hand there. "You asked once, Mr. Halloran, why I recorded names. Here is the rehearsal you cannot stage: when they ask me that in court, I must answer without weeping, and I do not yet know if I can."

Martha rose without a word and put a hand on each of their shoulders—one on Cyrus's, one on Emrys's, pressing them both down into their chairs as though they were children, as though they were patients, as though tenderness were a discipline she had never lost.

"Then we practice that too," she said. "Again. From the top. And when your voice breaks, Mrs. Thornbury, you let it break, and you wait, and you go on. Juries forgive weeping. What they don't forgive is stopping."

* * *

The nights were the hardest.

The loft was warm and smelled of apples and old straw, and Cyrus lay in it listening to the house settle, to the wind worrying the thatch, to Emrys below keeping his vigil by the banked fire because he could not sleep and would not pretend. Sometimes they spoke through the floorboards, low, in the register people use for truths too heavy to lift to full voice.

"I keep thinking about the lantern," he said, on the fifth night. "Quill's lantern. It shattered when they struck me. Glass everywhere in the mud like frost."

"You'll grind new glass. You restored it once from wreckage."

"That's not what I mean." A pause. The fire ticked. "I mean—I stood in front of that wall and let a man break my head rather than move aside, and I felt *proud*, Cyrus. Proud to be bleeding for a book. And then in the water today—all that black water, and all I could think was, if I drown, Father hangs alone and the book drowns with me, and pride seemed like such a stupid thing to have brought." His laugh was small and terrible. "I spent twenty-six years learning to be a Halloran. It turns out what I wanted was to be useful."

"You were useful before you were brave," Cyrus said. "You returned my stolen pages when keeping them might have bought your father's safety. Don't undervalue that. Everything else—this, the fen, the broken head—that was courage. That was choice. But that night in your father's study, choosing truth over blood—" She stopped, hearing what she was saying, hearing the intimacy she had walked into. "Few men manage it once. You managed it without applause."

Silence from below. Long enough that she thought he had fallen asleep. Then:

"There's something I almost said. In the workshop. During the storm."

"I know," Cyrus said. "I almost said something too."

"We shouldn't. Not now. Not with everything—" 

"No. Not now." She stared at the rafters, at the pale shapes of hung apple baskets swaying faintly in the draught, and felt the almost-words settle back down into that place inside her where she kept everything—folded small, kept dark. But it was different now, keeping them. Once the darkness had been hiding. Now it was sheltering. She could not have explained the difference to anyone. Perhaps, someday, to him.

"Goodnight, Emrys."

"Goodnight, Cyrus." And then, softer, when he thought she could not hear: "Whatever happens—we finish it. Whatever happens."

* * *

On the sixth morning the frost on the window lay thick as wool, and Martha sent Emrys to the village for salt and candles with strict instructions to speak to no one, and Cyrus sat alone with Martha at the table, cross-referencing the final column of entries against the hymnal notes, when Martha laid down her pen and looked at her with those flat grey fenland eyes.

"They'll come for you before the assizes," Martha said. "You know that."

"I've assumed it."

"Not assumed—*known*. I watch you work, Mrs. Thornbury. You leave yourself exits. Which door, which window, what lies between here and the marsh. A woman who plans her escape while she rehearses her testimony isn't sure she'll live to give it." Martha turned the hymnal's wrapped bulk slowly on the table, as if it were a compass needle finding north. "Ned's cousin's boy works the toll road out of Ely. He came by yesterday while you were sleeping. There's a warrant drawn in London. For forgery of army records. Against Cyrus Thornbury, nursing sister. They've had samples copied—your hand, writing what you never wrote. Vane's been preparing this since autumn."

Cyrus sat very still. Outside, a curlew called across the frozen fields, that long rising note the fens made even in winter, and she thought how strange it was that the world continued its ordinary announcements while a man in London signed papers turning her seven years of truth into seven years of crime.

"If they take me before I testify," she said at last, "the ledger becomes stolen property. Confiscated. Discredited. And the Hallorans—"

"Hang alone. Yes." Martha's voice was granite. "So we don't wait for them to come polite with their warrant. We go now, today, to the magistrate at Ely ourselves. You walk in the front door, book in hand, and you surrender before they can arrest you—because a woman who gives herself up is a witness, and a woman dragged in is a prisoner, and the difference is everything." She pushed back her chair and stood, and took down her shawl and her husband's old cloak from the pegs by the door, and Cyrus saw that Martha had packed them days ago.

"You knew before I arrived," Cyrus said slowly. "The toll-boy's news—you expected it."

"I raised four sons in poverty and buried three," Martha said. "I expect everything." She held out the heavy cloak. "Get your book, Mrs. Thornbury. Get your hymnal. The assizes won't come to us, and God help us, we're done hiding."

Through the window, far off across the white fields, Cyrus saw a figure coming fast along the towpath—Emrys, running, hatless, his arms waving before he was near enough for any message to be heard.

Something was behind him on the road. Something with wheels.
