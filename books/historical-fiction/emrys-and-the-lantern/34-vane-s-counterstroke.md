## 34. Vane's Counterstroke

The warrant came on a Tuesday, carried by a boy too young to know what he carried.

Cyrus saw him first from the kitchen window—a thin figure in an oversized coat picking his way up the frozen drive, one hand raised against the wind that came off the fen in long grey shoves. Behind him, at the gate, a mounted man waited with the stillness of someone paid to observe rather than deliver. She noted the horse before she noted the boy. It was a cavalry remount, army-bred, and it stood in the manner of horses whose riders had learned to watch without appearing to.

She had been grinding dried yarrow for Martha Quill's chest complaint. Her hands continued the work while her mind ran ahead, the way they had learned to do in the tents at Balaclava, where a surgeon's shout might mean anything and the body learned to keep ladling broth while the soul braced itself. *This is it*, she thought, with a calm that frightened her more than fear would have. *He has finally written my name down.*

"Miss Thornbury?" The boy's voice cracked on the second syllable. He held out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with legal string, and would not meet her eyes. "From the magistrate's office at Ely. You're to sign."

She signed. The name looked strange there—*Cyrus Thornbury* in her small, careful hand, the same hand that had filled forty ledger pages with other people's names—and she thought: *how odd, that I should be asked to authenticate myself in order to be accused.*

The boy left. The rider at the gate turned his horse with deliberate slowness and followed him back down the drive, and Cyrus stood in the doorway holding the packet as though it were something dead that she had been asked to identify.

She did not open it until she was alone in the linen room, with the door shut and the bolt drawn, though a bolt would stop no one who truly meant to come through. The oilcloth crackled. Inside lay three documents and a single sheet of charges, written in the florid clerk's hand that seemed to flourish wherever official cruelty was done.

The charge was forgery of army records. The documents were exhibits.

She spread them on the folding table between stacks of pillowcases and read them twice, three times, her lips moving without sound. They were muster rolls—or pieces of them, torn from larger pages, dated to the winter of 1809–10, when she had served at the general hospital in Colchester before ever crossing the sea. Her name appeared on each. Not merely as witness. As *author*. *Entries made and certified by C. Thornbury, nurse and recordist*, the endorsements read, and beneath each endorsement sat a signature that was almost hers.

Almost.

Whoever had practiced her hand had understood its bones but not its breath. The capital T was correct; the loop of the *y* descended properly; the spacing was disciplined, even prim. But her signature always leaned—had leaned since girlhood, since she'd first signed her brother's letters for him when his own writing tired—leaned forward into the page like a person walking into wind. These signatures stood upright. They leaned nowhere. They were the signatures of a woman who had never walked into anything.

*He has never actually seen me write*, she realized, and the realization arrived cold and complete, like water finding a seam. *Seven years of watching me, of noting my habits, of tracing my movements from regiment to estate—and he has only ever seen my hand secondhand, in copies, in reports. He knows what I recorded. He does not know how.*

It was a flaw. It was perhaps the only flaw. And flaws in Vane's workmanship were rare enough that she felt something dangerous kindle in her chest—not hope exactly, but the raw material of it, the ore before smelting.

Because everything else was exact. The dates matched entries she had genuinely made. The hospital stamps were right; she recognized the faded iron-cross of the Colchester registry, the slight double-strike where the clerk's hand had slipped. Whoever assembled these forgeries had possessed access to genuine papers from which to work. Which meant Vane had gotten hold of fragments of the true records—perhaps through the same channels through which he had once falsified supply ledgers, perhaps through Aldous Finch before Finch died, perhaps through some other clerk whose name she did not yet know—and had built his accusation out of true bones and false flesh.

That was what made it artful, and what made it terrible. Any magistrate summoned to compare these pages against authentic examples would find the dates corroborated, the stamps corroborated, the paper itself of the correct age and provenance. Only the hand would fail to convince—if anyone thought to look closely, if anyone alive knew her writing well enough to swear to its lean.

And who knew her writing? Emrys, who had watched her copy by lantern light through half a winter. Martha, who owned letters. Perhaps no one else in England.

*He means to make me the counterfeit*, she thought, folding the papers along their original creases with hands that would not hurry. *He has spent years making himself the author of other men's deeds, and now he will spend his last credit making me the author of my own erasure. If I am a forger, then every page in my ledger is suspect. Every date. Every name. The widows become inventions. The dead sergeants become fictions told by a convicted criminal.*

It was not merely a charge against her. It was a counter-ledger, kept in reverse—a book in which truth could be unwritten by discrediting the woman who wrote it.

*

She found Emrys in the workshop, which had become in recent weeks a strange sanctuary, half-ruin and half-arsenal. The demolition crews had not returned since the morning he'd stood bleeding before them; the magistrate's inquiry had frozen their work, at least for now, and the building sat in its own wreckage like a wounded animal in cover. The rebuilt lantern hung in the window—Emrys had reground the glass himself over eleven nights, working with a jeweler's patience—and its panes threw soft amber diamonds across the floor, across the crates of rescued papers, across the long workbench where the copied ledger pages lay sorted and bundled in oilcloth like field dressings.

Emrys looked up when she came in. He had grown thin since his father's arrest; the fine bones of the Halloran face stood out in him now, and there was a new stillness in the way he held himself, the stillness of a man who had stopped asking whether he was equal to what was required and simply begun doing it.

"You've had word," he said. Not a question. He read her the way she read ledgers, line by line, and she supposed she had known that about him for some time without admitting it.

She laid the charge sheet and the forged exhibits on the bench among the true ones, and watched his face while he read. He went very pale, very quietly, in the Halloran manner—but his hands, resting flat on the wood, did not move at all.

"He's built it well," Emrys said at last.

"He's built it nearly well." She showed him the signature. "Look at the lean. My hand leans into the page. His version stands straight. I have never in my life written a letter that stood straight."

Something moved through his exhaustion—brief, fierce, quickly mastered. "Then we put you on the stand and let the court watch you sign your name twenty times."

"And when I finish signing," she said gently, "they arrest me. That is the design, Emrys. The warrant is already issued. It is not conditional. Whether the forgery is proved or disproved, I am taken the moment I place myself within reach of the court. He does not need me convicted. He needs only me silenced and discredited. A witness in the dock awaiting trial can testify to nothing further; a witness in flight testifies to nothing at all."

The lantern light moved across his face. Outside, the wind worried at the broken shutters with a sound like slow tearing.

"Then you run," he said. "Take the book. Take the hymnal draft. Take the north road tonight—I'll give you money, I'll give you the horse—you can be in Lincolnshire by Friday and Scotland by Sunday, and write your testimony from a distance where his writ doesn't—"

"No."

The word came out quieter than she intended, and firmer than she had planned. She heard in it her own voice from seven years ago, from a tent in the Crimea, refusing a surgeon's order to abandon a man who would certainly die. She had been wrong then—the man had died anyway, slowly, in the night, holding her hand—but she had never regretted the refusal, because some refusals are not about outcomes.

"Listen to me." She took the folded charge sheet and set it deliberately atop the stack of true ledger pages, forgery and truth lying together like two claims on the same ground. "If I run, what happens here?"

He was silent.

"I'll tell you what happens, because I've watched it happen before, in other counties, to other families whose names are in my book. Vane arrives with his sworn information and his doctored samples and his tame clerks, and the assizes proceed without me. Martha Quill takes the stand, and counsel for the crown asks her how she knows the entries are genuine, and she says *because Miss Thornbury wrote them and showed them to me*, and counsel says *ah, the forger herself*, and everything Martha swears to becomes evidence of the conspiracy rather than evidence against it. The corporal—same. The quartermaster's clerk—same. Every living witness I gathered is anchored to my credibility, and my credibility is the thing he has just mined under." She paused. "And your father hangs for conspiracy. Debt fraud may cost him the estate; conspiracy will cost him more than the estate."

"They won't hang him," Emrys said, but his voice lacked conviction even as it defended.

"They don't need to hang him. Transportation is hanging by slower methods, and Vane knows it, and your father's health is not—" She stopped herself. Some truths are unkind in the saying even when necessary. "The point stands. My flight is his victory. My testimony is his risk. That is why he filed these charges now, this week, before the trial—he is not trying to punish me, Emrys. He is trying to make my presence impossible. It is the same strategy as burning the workshop, only aimed at a person instead of a building."

"Then what is the strategy against it?" His voice had gone rough. "You walk into that courtroom and surrender yourself, and whatever happens with Vane, you are taken. Exile at best. Prison. Possibly worse—they hanged people for falsifying army records, Cyrus, that statute isn't decorative. You would be giving him exactly the arrest he wants, only on our terms instead of his. Is that the arithmetic? Is that the trade?"

"It is the trade," she said. "And I have been walking toward it since Colchester, if I am honest, though I didn't know the shape of the door until today."

She turned to the window. The fen lay flat and silvered under the dying light, ditches running like veins of mercury toward the horizon, and somewhere out there, she knew, men were being paid to watch this house, this door, this window. She had spent her whole adult life learning how not to be seen, and she had been good at it—good as invisibility must sometimes be, good the way a scar is good, proof of survival but proof of wounding too. And now the skill was useless, or worse than useless, because the whole design of the coming trial depended on visibility. On standing up. On names spoken aloud in a room full of strangers.

*Thomas Thornbury*, she thought. *Private, Third Foot. Died of fever at Walcheren, 1809. Buried without marker. Never named in any dispatch.* She had written him into the ledger's front page six weeks ago, her own name beside his, and since that day she had felt the old grief change its weight—no lighter, but differently carried, the way a load shifts when you finally lift it correctly.

"The trap closes on him too," she said aloud, thinking it through as much as saying it. "Do you see that? He has committed himself. To prove me a forger, he must produce these samples and stand behind them. To stand behind them, he must swear to their provenance—which means swearing, before a magistrate, that these papers passed through legitimate channels. But those channels are *his* channels. The clerks, the registries, the chain of custody—every link he names is a link in the embezzlement scheme, and every link is a man who can be found, questioned, and turned. He has handed us a map of his own network, dressed as evidence." She smiled, and was aware that the smile had something wolfish in it, and did not apologize for it. "Aldous Finch fell from a bridge rather than name Vane. But Finch is not the only clerk in England who has taken Vane's coin, and not all of them love him enough for bridges."

Emrys stared at her. Then, slowly, he began to laugh—not mirth, nothing so clean, but the release of pressure that laughter sometimes performs for terror, and when it finished he pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes and said, "You are the most frightening person I have ever met, and I grew up with Colonel Vane at my father's dinner table."

"You flatter me. Vane frightens me considerably more than I frighten him. But—" she touched the forged signature, the upright, windless letters of a hand that had never existed, "—he made an error. He has made very few, which is why this one matters so much. He forged a woman he has only ever observed from a distance, and he forgot that a signature is not decoration. It is a gait. It is the way a particular body moves through the world. Mine leans, because I have always leaned. Because I learned to write by keeping a dying boy's letters going, and I have been walking into wind ever since." She drew a breath. "We need a handwriting expert. Before the trial, within days. Someone who can compare these samples against genuine examples of my hand from Colchester—hospital correspondence, requisitions, anything surviving in the registry—and swear to the difference. Do you know anyone with standing?"

"I know everyone Father knows," Emrys said grimly, "which is a catalogue of debtors and drunkards. But—" he paused, and she watched an idea arrive in him the way weather arrives over the fen, visible from a long way off, "—there's a solicitor in Ely. Perrin. Old, half-deaf, scrupulous to the point of disease. He handled Grandmother Halloran's estate and hated every minute of it, which recommends him. He owes us nothing, which is better. He cannot be accused of being ours."

"Send to him tomorrow. Quietly—don't use the drive, the boy who brought the warrant was followed, and whoever follows boys can follow letters. Go yourself, if you can. You're not under warrant. Yet." She let the last word sit between them without softening it, because they had agreed weeks ago to stop pretending, and pretense between them now would be a kind of disrespect.

"And you?" he said. "What do you do, while I'm riding to Ely to beg the help of a man who hates his work?"

Cyrus looked at the workbench, at the true pages and the false ones lying together, and made her decision the way she had made all the important decisions of her life: without ceremony, in silence, in lamplight.

"I write," she said. "Tonight, while my hand is angry. I take a fair-copy page and I sign it forty times, leaning, and I date it, and I ask Martha to witness it, and the corporal after her, and I build a specimen of my own hand so fresh and so witnessed that when Perrin lays it beside Vane's upright little ghosts, the difference will declare itself without a single word of testimony from me." She was already pulling the copying paper toward her as she spoke, already seating herself at the bench, already dipping the pen. "He forged a woman he never bothered to know. Very well. Tomorrow the court shall meet the original."

Emrys watched her a moment longer. Whatever he was feeling, he folded it away carefully, as she had seen him fold away everything since his father's arrest—grief, tenderness, the unfinished sentences of that storm-watched night, all put aside like linens out of season.

"Cyrus." At the door he stopped, half-turned, the lantern light catching only his shoulder and the edge of his jaw. "When you take the stand—when they take you afterward—I'll be outside the bar. And whatever the verdict, whatever they do with you—" His voice caught, steadied. "I'll be the one waiting when they bring you out. However long. Wherever they take you. That is not a promise I'm asking you to believe. It's one I'm informing you of."

He did not wait for her answer. She heard his boots on the frozen mud outside, heard the stable door, heard his horse go out into the dark toward Ely.

Cyrus bent over the page and wrote her name.

It leaned into the wind. It had always leaned into the wind. Forty times she wrote it, and the candle burned lower, and out across the black fen the watchers watched, and in Ely a warrant lay in a drawer with her true name upon it, and none of it—not the warrant, not the watchers, not the long arm of Colonel Vane reaching from London to crush a nurse with a notebook—none of it could alter the simple physical fact of how Cyrus Thornbury's hand moved across a page.

But three days later, when the assizes opened and the crown's counsel rose to produce his exhibits, the courtroom doors opened behind him—and the woman herself walked in, unbidden, unbailiffed, ledger in hand, to surrender.

Her trial would wait. Lord Halloran's would not.
