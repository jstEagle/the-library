## 35. The Assizes Open

The assize court at Lent had swallowed half the county, and it seemed to Emrys that every soul who came had brought the cold in with them. The corridor outside Court Two smelled of wet wool and tallow and the particular staleness of buildings that had heard too many men condemned. He stood beneath the high arched window with his back to the light, because if he faced the glass he would see his own reflection—pale, unshaven since the arrest, wearing his dead mother's mourning black because nothing else in the house fit the occasion—and he could not bear the arithmetic of what that reflection contained: a son, a name, a house falling.

They had barred him at the door. Not rudely; that was the worst of it. The usher had touched his sleeve with something approaching kindness and said, *Kin of the accused are not admitted while evidence is given, sir. You understand. Prejudice to the jury, you see.* And Emrys did see. He understood perfectly, the way a man understands a lock turning from the wrong side. His father's trial was proceeding forty feet away through two inches of oak, and he was to learn of it secondhand, by rumor and echo, like a servant hearing gossip through a pantry door.

So he listened to doors.

It was astonishing, he thought, how much a door could tell you if you gave it your whole attention. The great double doors of Court Two were old, their seals long perished, and they breathed with the room behind them. When the crowd shifted, when counsel rose, when laughter—God help them, there had been laughter once already, at some clerk's mispronounced name—the sound came through the grain of the wood low and muffled, a sea against a hull. And above all of it, when the courtroom quieted to that particular hush reserved for the reading of charges, Emrys could hear Colonel Sir Ambrose Vane's voice.

He would have known it anywhere now. Seven years ago he had heard it across a parade ground at Woolwich, bright and commanding, the voice of a man who believed the world was arranged for his convenience and had so far been proved right. It had not changed. That was the obscenity of it. Men's voices broke and aged and wore thin; Vane's rang the same as ever, polished as regimental brass, each syllable set down like a coin on a counter.

*My lord, the Crown will demonstrate that the accused, Nathaniel Halloran, did over a period of six years conspire with persons unknown to divert sums designated for the provisioning of His Majesty's forces—sums which passed through accounts controlled by the accused—into private hands.*

Persons unknown. Emrys pressed his palm flat against the oak and felt the words vibrate faintly under his fingers, or imagined he did. Persons unknown. There was a person known, all right. There was a person whose name Cyrus Thornbury had written in her ledger beside three supply transactions dated before Emrys had finished growing his first beard, and that person was presently on his feet in the well of the court being addressed as *my learned friend* by men who dined with him.

* * *

The morning had begun badly and grown worse by increments, the way disasters do—not a collapse but a settling, stone by stone into place.

He had risen before dawn at the house on Bedford Square where the family was lodging in exile, three rooms rented under a name that still opened no doors but at least paid its bills. His father had been awake too, sitting fully dressed by the window, watching the carts come up from Covent Garden through the dirty glass. They had not spoken since the gate. There was nothing left to say that the constables had not already said with their hands on his father's arms.

His father had dressed carefully for the trial. Emrys had watched him knot his cravat twice, undoing it both times, and on the third attempt his hands had shaken so that the white cloth sat crooked at his throat all day, and Emrys had not told him. Some mercies are small enough to be cruel.

At the courthouse steps the reporters had found them—or found him, rather, for the crowd parted around his father as around a man already dead. *Son of the accused?* A pencil appeared. *Will you say the charges are false, sir? Will you defend your father's name?*

And Emrys, who had rehearsed dignity on the walk over, had said: *I will defend it better than these proceedings intend to.* Which was true, and undignified, and would be printed by noon with the word *hysterical* attached to it, he had no doubt. A boy in the crowd had laughed. Emrys had looked at the boy until the laughing stopped, and then he had gone inside to be barred from the only room in England that mattered.

Cyrus had been there. That was the one mercy the morning held. She stood at the edge of the colonnade in her grey cloak, hood back, face bare—which meant she had stopped hiding, which meant she had decided something, and the look of her decided face steadied him more than any speech could have. She carried her satchel. He knew what rode inside it now, sewn into the lining of everything she owned, distributed like seed corn against famine: seven years of names.

*You shouldn't be seen with me,* he'd said.

*I've been invisible for seven years,* she answered. *One morning standing in plain sight won't kill me.* Then, quieter: *Listen to everything. Even the parts that hurt. We'll need all of it.*

We. She said we now without flinching, and he clung to it through the hours that followed the way a drowning man clings to driftwood that is, admittedly, quite small.

* * *

By mid-morning the doors had taught him the shape of the Crown's case, and the shape was beautiful, and that was the terror of it.

Vane performed magnificently. There was no other word. Emrys had expected bluster—he had braced himself for the bullying colonel, the man who cornered nurses in corridors and offered ruin with a smile—and instead he heard, filtering through the oak, something far more dangerous: restraint. Measured tones. A reluctance almost tender, as though it pained Vane to speak ill of a family he had once counted among his friends.

*It grieves me, my lord. It grieves me beyond expression. I knew the Halloran estate in happier days. I dined at that table.* A pause, expertly measured. Through the door Emrys could hear the gallery shifting, leaning in, women's skirts rustling. *But grief must not stand between justice and the Crown's soldiers, who bled at Crimea while their provisions were sold from under them.*

A murmur ran the length of the corridor, passing Emrys like a wind through wheat. Somewhere behind him a woman said *shameful* in a voice thick with feeling, and he realized with a lurch that she was weeping for the soldiers, for the poor robbed soldiers, weeping for the very fiction Vane had built out of his own crimes.

That was the architecture of it. Emrys began to map it as he listened, because mapping was all he had, and fury needed somewhere to go or it would eat him alive from the inside. Vane had constructed a story in which the embezzlement was real—of course it was real, the money had moved, Emrys's own father had signed the instruments—but the guilt had been transposed, note for note, onto the one man whose debts made him plausible. Lord Halloran had borrowed from Vane to cover early losses. Vane had then used Halloran's signature to move the stolen sums, and now presented those same signatures as proof of Halloran's thieving. It was elegant. It was nearly airtight. Every document the Crown produced would be genuine; only its meaning was forged, and meaning left no fingerprints.

*Exhibit eleven.* Paper rustled somewhere in the vast room, and Emrys heard his father's name read aloud—*Nathaniel Halloran*—in Vane's level voice, and heard the jury foreman grunt acknowledgment, and understood suddenly and completely what a name sounded like when it was being taken apart by strangers. Like a hinge. Like a small dry creak, repeated.

He walked to the end of the corridor. He walked back. He had been pacing for an hour and knew it and could not stop.

An elderly barrister waiting for his own case took pity on him around noon, offering a share of a bench near the fire. *You're the Halloran lad,* the man said—not unkindly. *Bad business. Though between us, Vane's case looks strong as church brick. Treasury papers, sworn inventories, a quartermaster's clerk giving evidence—"*

*A clerk,* Emrys said. *Which clerk?*

The barrister shrugged. *Some fellow brought down special from London. Finch, or Fitch—no, they're saying the fellow's dead now, drowned off the bridge in January. Sad business too. They've his deposition, though. Taken before.*

The corridor went very quiet for Emrys then, quieter than any courtroom. Finch. Aldous Finch, who had written the sealed letter naming the Hallorans; who had confessed to Emrys in a Soho coffee-house with his hands shaking around the cup, saying *I was made to write it, I was made to*; who had fallen from a bridge three weeks later while the coroner wrote *misadventure* in a fair round hand. And now his ghost was testifying. Vane had taken the dead man's fear while it was fresh, bottled it as a deposition, and poured it out this morning for a jury that would never know the taste of it.

*Sir,* Emrys said, and his voice surprised him with its calm, *that deposition—who administered it? Who witnessed the signature?*

*Why, I couldn't say. Vane's people, presumably. He's conducting the prosecution personally, extraordinary thing, but he has the Home Office ear since the Crimea, and—"*

Emrys thanked him and turned back to the doors, and stood there with his hands closed at his sides, nails drawing blood from his own palms, because if he did not give the fury somewhere physical to live it would make him scream in a public corridor.

* * *

In the afternoon the witnesses began, and the afternoon was worse than the morning, because the morning had been paper and the afternoon was flesh.

First the treasury men, smooth and sorrowful, confirming ledgers Emrys had never seen but recognized anyway—they bore the same institutional handwriting as the estate's own ruined books, the same columns, the same little ticks of verification that meant nothing and certified everything. Then a shipping agent. Then a bank man. Each one placed a stone in the wall. None of them lied, precisely. That was Vane's genius: he never asked a man to lie. He asked only questions whose honest answers, arranged in the right order, built the lie for him.

And then, through the breathing of the doors, Emrys heard a voice he knew.

Martha Quill.

She had promised. God help her, Cyrus had gone to her cottage and asked her to stand up in open court, and Martha—who had buried a husband whose name was stolen before his body was cold—had said yes knowing what it might cost her. But Emrys heard her voice crack on the third question, heard Vane's tone shift into something silken and solicitous, the good colonel comforting the poor widow even as he dismantled her.

*You state, Mrs. Quill, that you observed irregularities in the supply returns at the field hospital in the winter of '55.*

*I saw crates marked full going out empty, sir. I said so.*

*To whom did you report this?*

*To nobody, sir. A woman like me reports to nobody.*

*Indeed. And yet you now come forward, seven years on, at the instance of—forgive me—a servant in the Halloran household. A woman recently implicated in matters of forgery, is that not so? There is a warrant discussed, I believe. Might it be that your testimony was—shall we say—encouraged?*

*I wasn't encouraged. I was asked.*

*Asked by a woman facing charges of falsifying army records, to swear documents concerning army records. My lord, I press nothing further. The jury will draw its conclusions.*

The silence that followed lasted four heartbeats. Emrys counted them against his own pulse. Then the doors exhaled the sound of Martha Quill stepping down, small quick footsteps, a door at the far side of the court opening and shutting on winter air—and she was out, she was safe, she was walking past him in the corridor with her chin up and her eyes bright and terrible, and she did not stop, only gripped his wrist once, hard, as she went by, as though to say: *still standing, both of us.*

But the damage was done. He could hear it in the room's new weather. Cyrus had warned him: testimony without the book is hearsay. Martha's word, unwitnessed, seven years old, delivered by a widow against a decorated colonel—it hadn't merely failed. Vane had turned it. He had made her into evidence of *his* theory: that the Hallorans were coaching witnesses, manufacturing testimony through their servants. Every ally they gathered, Vane would bend into a prop for his own case. The chorus Cyrus was assembling would be recast, voice by voice, as the conspiracy's choir.

Emrys slid down the wall beside the doors until he was sitting on the cold flagstones, and the usher coming down the corridor pretended not to see him, and he sat there in the posture of a beggar and let the fury finish sharpening itself into something with an edge and a purpose.

Because here was the thing the fury had finally shown him, clear as ice on black water: they could not win this by gathering strength. Every witness was a liability, every day another exhibit. The only weapon in England that could cut Vane was lying thirty feet away in a grey cloak, in a satchel, in seven years of ink—and it could not be entered, it could not be read, it could not save anyone until someone stood up in that room and put their own liberty on the scale beside it.

She had told him what she intended. He had argued. He had said *wait*, and *there must be another way*, and *let me do it*. She had answered with that decided face: *They'll never take your testimony—you're kin. They'll never believe mine unless I make them. The book needs a voice, Emrys, and the voice needs to be worth hanging.*

Through the doors, Vane's voice was closing the day, warm and reasonable, thanking the jury for their patience, promising tomorrow the final accounting, the complete picture, the last proofs of the Halloran guilt. Applause—actual applause, quickly suppressed—from somewhere in the gallery.

The great doors swung open at last as the court rose, and heat and lamplight and the smell of the crowd rolled out over Emrys where he sat on the floor, and through the widening gap he saw Vane coming down the aisle: powdered, immaculate, bowing to acquaintances, collecting congratulations like ripe fruit, his eye sweeping the corridor—and stopping, briefly, on Emrys among the feet and the shadows.

Vane smiled. It was not a cruel smile; cruelty requires interest. It was the smile a man gives a settled account.

Then the crowd closed, and the doors swung shut again, and Emrys got slowly to his feet, and went to find Cyrus—because tomorrow, when the Crown rested its perfect case, the defense would call its first witness, and he already knew the name on the list, and he already knew what it would cost.
