## 36. Cyrus Takes the Stand

The morning I gave myself to the court, frost had come early to London, and it lay along the railings of the Old Bailey like sugar that would never melt. I had dressed with care, which is to say plainly: grey wool, black bonnet, no ribbons. A woman who means to be believed must first be seen clearly, and clarity begins with the absence of ornament. In my left hand I carried the ledger wrapped in linen. Against my ribs, beneath my stays, I had sewn the duplicate pages Emrys and I had copied through two nights of storm—insurance against confiscation, against fire, against the small accidents that had lately been happening to men who knew too much.

Martha Quill walked with me from the lodging house. She had insisted. Her hands were folded over her muff, and she said nothing for most of the way, and then at the corner where we had to wait for a cart of cabbages to pass, she squeezed my arm hard enough to bruise.

"I used to pray he'd be remembered," she said. "Just remembered. By anyone. I never thought it would come to a courtroom."

"Memory doesn't always look like what we expect," I said.

"No," she agreed. "Sometimes it looks like a woman in a plain bonnet."

* * *

I did not go in through the public door. I went to the officer at the side entrance and asked for him by his office—Mr. Prosser, clerk of indictments—and I laid my warrant on his counter and said, in a voice pitched not to carry, "I am Cyrus Thornbury. There is a charge outstanding against me for forgery of army records. I am here to answer it."

He looked at the warrant, then at me, and something moved behind his eyes—the particular calculation of a man deciding whether he has been handed a live coal or a dead one.

"You understand you'll be taken into custody?"

"Immediately after I testify. Not before. The defence will want me heard while Lord Halloran's trial stands open, and the crown—" I let the smallest pause fall, "—the crown will want to hear what I have to say about Colonel Vane before his counsel can object that the witness is unavailable."

It was, I confess, a lie of emphasis. They could have taken me at once and questioned me later. But Prosser was a man of ledgers himself; I saw the orderliness of my argument land on him like arithmetic done correctly, and correctness, in that building, was the only currency that never devalued. He sent a runner to the barrister chambers. Within the hour I was sitting in a cold anteroom drinking tea I could not taste, waiting to be called.

Through the wall I could hear the trial going on without me. Vane's voice came through the stones sometimes—not the words, only its shape, that measured parade-ground cadence that could make any sentence sound like an order already obeyed. I had heard it across a dressing station seven years ago, saying *Sergeant Quill acted under my direction*, when Quill lay three days dead and his maneuver had won a pass that Vane's report would claim forever. I closed my eyes and listened to the voice as if it were weather. Weather passes.

* * *

When they called me, the courtroom changed.

I felt it before I understood it—a settling, a leaning-forward, the collective intake of a crowd that has grown bored with lawyers and scents blood or story. The gallery rustled. At the defence table Lord Halloran turned, and his face when he saw me was terrible to witness: hope and shame fighting openly, an old man watching the servant he had tried to buy off walk toward the witness box with the thing he'd paid her to surrender.

Emrys was not inside. Barred as kin, he waited beyond the doors, and I thought of him there—ear pressed near the wood, the way he had listened all his life to conversations that happened over his head. I would speak loudly enough for doors.

The oath was administered. I laid the ledger on the rail, unwrapped the linen, and opened it to the front page.

"Cyrus Thornbury," the clerk prompted. "State your name and occupation for the record."

"I am Cyrus Thornbury. Formerly nurse and dressmaker of the 33rd Regiment hospital at Balaclava, latterly workshop clerk to the Halloran estate." I paused, and made myself say the rest, because it belonged on the record too. "Keeper, these seven years, of a private account of persons omitted from official returns."

Counsel for the prosecution stirred. "My lord, this document has not been entered into evidence, and the witness faces charges concerning precisely such records—"

"My lord," said the defence, rising, "if the crown maintains the book is forged, it can hardly fear having it examined. Forgery is proved by comparison. Let it be compared."

There was a silence in which I watched the judge decide. He was old, dry as August grass, and he looked at the ledger longer than he looked at anyone.

"The book will be produced," he said. "Witness, you will speak to entries you personally made. Dates, hands, particulars. We will waste no time on sentiment."

"No, my lord," I said, though sentiment was the whole engine of what I intended, wearing the clothes of fact.

* * *

They passed the ledger down. I watched hands I did not know turn pages I had filled through fevers and snow and grief, and something in my chest pulled like a stitch tearing—but slowly, so that I could keep standing. The prosecution's expert examined dates, ink, watermarks, the progression of my handwriting from the shaking entries of 1806 to the steady ones of the present. Then the crown's own man compared samples of regimental returns Vane had filed against entries in my book, and I watched him find, entry by entry, that where Vane's reports recorded supplies drawn and signed, my book recorded names—the men who had actually drawn them, in their own voices, dictated to me at their bedsides because their fingers were gone or bandaged.

The expert looked up once, quickly, the way a man looks up when the ground shifts.

"Read the entry of the fourteenth of January, 1807," the defence directed me. "The one regarding supply of greatcoats, Second Battalion."

I found it. My finger knew the page before my eyes did; seven years of rereading had worn grooves.

"'Fourteen January, 1807,'" I read. "'Requisition of ninety-eight greatcoats signed in regimental return as received by Sergeant T. Quill, 33rd. Sergeant Quill died the twelfth of September previous. Signature compared against his mark in the regimental pay book: not his hand. Coats traced to sale in Canterbury, Michaelmas fair, vendor unnamed. Witnessed by me: Private Aldous Finch, quartermaster's clerk, who stated he was ordered to carry the signature.'"

The courtroom had begun to be quiet. Not the ordinary quiet of proceedings—I knew that quiet, its shuffling patience. This was another kind. It was the silence of people hearing something they recognized and had never expected to hear named aloud: the smallness of the thefts, the smallness of the stolen.

"Read the next one," someone said softly, from the gallery, and the judge let it stand.

"'Second February, 1807. Widow's pension claimed and drawn quarterly for Martha Quill, husband deceased in service. No payment ever reached the widow. Addressed to an agent in Chatham. Widow located by me at Deal, destitute, sewing for her bread.'" I looked up. "Martha Quill is present in this courtroom, my lord. She will swear to it herself."

She was, and I saw her in the third row, chin lifted, hands still around that muff as if it were a railing she held in a high wind.

I read on. I had planned to select six entries—enough, I thought, to make the pattern visible. But once I began I could not stop at six, because every page had a face attached, and I had promised those faces. The corporal whose discharge papers were sold to a healthier man. The drummer boy marked *deserted* who had in truth died of flux and been buried under another boy's number, so that his mother in Shropshire spent nine years writing to a son who had never lived past the winter. The quartermaster's clerk who refused a falsification and was posted, within the month, to a station so unhealthy it amounted to a sentence of death—my own brother among the names he had dictated to me before the posting took effect, his last gift being the habit of noticing.

Each entry bore a date. Each date was in my hand. Each name I spoke settled into the room like a coin dropped into a deep well, and the silence grew so complete that I could hear the clock above the door, and my own breath, and once, very faintly, a woman in the gallery weeping without sobbing—the mother from Shropshire, I learned afterward, who had come on faith and a rumour.

Vane sat at the crown's table in his best coat, silver-haired, magnificent, and I did not look at him while I read. But I felt him listening, and I understood—for the first time fully, with something close to pity—that a man who builds his life on other people's erasure must spend that life terrified of paper. Every name was a splinter working loose. Every date was proof the erasure had required hands, and hands leave prints.

When I reached the entry concerning the letter under seal—Finch's letter, dictated, accusing the Hallorans—the prosecution rose to object that this touched matters outside the present indictment.

"My lord," I said, before the judge could rule, "Aldous Finch fell from Blackfriars Bridge in November. He cannot speak. What he wrote survives, and it survives in a hymnal given to Martha Quill in his own hand, naming the man who dictated it. I do not ask the court to take my word. I ask it to take his handwriting, which is in evidence, and compare it against the signatures in this book that same hand confessed to forging."

The judge looked at me for a long moment over his spectacles.

"You are yourself charged, madam, with forging army records."

"Yes, my lord."

"And you stand there and offer a book of your own keeping as the measure of another man's forgeries."

"I do, my lord. Because a true record indicts the false one, and there is no other kind of proof that carries the dead in it. If your lordship finds my book forged, then hang me for it, and Vane walks. But weigh it first against the returns it contradicts, entry by entry, date by date. Forgery flatters power. Mine does nothing but trouble it. That is how your lordship may know which is which."

The words came out of me steadier than anything I had ever spoken, and I knew as they left that they were the reason I had been put on the earth—to say exactly that, in exactly that room, to exactly that silence.

The judge's mouth compressed. It might have been the suppression of a smile.

"The court will compare," he said. "The witness will stand down. And the warrant—" he glanced at the officer by the wall, and the whole room breathed in together, "—will be executed at the conclusion of these examinations."

Which meant: after. Which meant they needed me still. Which meant, for one more day, I was worth more free than taken, and both Vane and the law knew it.

I gathered my ledger. As I stepped down, passing the crown's table, Vane leaned slightly toward me—no motion anyone else would see, only a tilting of the head, a voice pitched under the court's murmur like a knife slid flat between floorboards.

"You have hanged yourself cleverly, Mrs. Thornbury. But books burn, and widows tire, and sons inherit their fathers' debts. Count on nothing lasting."

"Colonel," I said, in just as low a register, not stopping, "you have forgotten the one thing my book was written to teach. Small things last. You built everything large enough to be seen falling."

Then I was past him, and the doors opened, and Emrys stood on the other side of them, white-faced, having caught every word through the wood as I had meant him to, and behind him the corridor was full of people who had been unable to get seats—clerks, soldiers' mothers, a discharged corporal with his cap in his hands—all of them looking at the ledger under my arm the way starving people look through a window at bread.

And down the corridor, pushing through them two at a time with his hat crushed in his fist and terror written across him in a hand larger than any forgery, came the treasury clerk who had sat beside Vane at his club—come, by the look of him, to trade his own ruin for someone else's before the day was out.
