## 37. The Unraveling

The courtroom on the third day smelled of tallow and wet wool, of the crowd pressed shoulder to shoulder beneath the high windows, and beneath all of it, faintly, of old stone and older judgments—the smell of a building that had heard every kind of lie and had learned, perhaps, to wait for the truth the way a spider waits at the center of its web.

Martha Quill went up the steps to the witness box with her chin lifted and her hands folded, as though she were entering church. She wore her good grey dress, the one with the mended hem, and she had brushed her hair back under a plain cap so that her face showed clear and unadorned. Cyrus, seated among the witnesses waiting to be called, watched her climb those steps and thought: *she is more frightened than any wounded man I ever tended, and she is doing it anyway.*

The barrister for the crown, a spare man named Kettering with ink-stained cuffs and a voice like a dry leaf, inclined his head to her.

"State your name for the court."

"Martha Quill. Widow." A pause. "Widow of Sergeant Thomas Quill, late of the 33rd Regiment of Foot."

"Mrs. Quill, you were present in Crimea during the winter actions of the supply retreat. Is that correct?"

"I was. I followed the drum, as they say. I did the washing and the nursing when there was no one better." Her voice was steady, but Cyrus could see the effort of steadiness in the tendons of her wrists. "Which was most times."

"You have heard testimony this week regarding a maneuver at the bridge over the Alma road—a withdrawal conducted under fire that saved some forty men of the light company. Who performed that maneuver?"

Martha turned her head, slowly, deliberately, and found Colonel Vane where he sat at the counsel table. He had dressed carefully for this day: dark coat, silver at his collar, his face arranged into an expression of patient, faintly bored dignity. He looked like a man listening to weather.

"Thomas Quill performed it," Martha said. "My husband. And he died performing it, and he died without his name on anything, because the report that went to London carried another gentleman's name instead."

A stirring ran through the gallery like wind through wheat.

"And how do you know this," Kettering said, "when the official record carries Colonel Vane's name?"

"Because my husband told me before he died. Because the men who came back told me. Because—" and here her voice did not shake, which was its own kind of miracle—"*she* told me." She pointed, one work-worn finger, across the courtroom to where Cyrus sat. "That woman wrote it down. Seven years ago she wrote it down, and she sent me word of it, and she has never once let me forget what my Thomas did. The army forgot him inside a week. That woman remembered him longer than England has remembered its own dead."

Cyrus felt every eye in the room turn upon her, and understood—for the first time in her life, fully understood—what it was to be seen. It was not warmth. It was weight. She kept her hands still in her lap and bore it.

Kettering produced a paper. "I show you a page copied from a private ledger, entry dated the twelfth of February, seven years past. Read it aloud, if you would."

Martha put on her spectacles. She read slowly, in the careful voice of a woman who had learned her letters late and never taken them for granted:

"'Sergeant T. Quill, 33rd Foot, fell at the Alma road bridge, the eleventh inst., directing the withdrawal of the light company after Captain—' " she stopped. Looked up. Looked at Vane again. "It names him. It says *Captain Vane claimed the maneuver in his dispatch of the same date.* It says the men of the company confirmed it and were reprimanded for saying so."

"Thank you, Mrs. Quill."

Vane's own counsel rose to cross-examine, a heavy-browed man who had spent two days bludgeoning witnesses into hesitancy. He began now with courtesy, which was worse. "Mrs. Quill, this ledger—it is a private book, kept by a servant. Unwitnessed. Uncountersigned. You understand that any person might write anything in a private book?"

"I keep accounts myself," Martha said placidly. "Eggs, butter, what I owe the miller. If I wrote that the miller owed me sixpence and he'd never seen the book, you'd say the book proves nothing. But if I wrote it seven years running, and never once wrote sixpence he didn't owe, you might begin to think the book knew something." A ripple of laughter moved through the gallery and was suppressed. "Sir, I've read that ledger entire. There are three hundred names in it. Three hundred soldiers and servants and widows that the army's fine dispatches left out. You tell me how a forger profits from writing down dead men nobody wants to claim."

The heavy-browed counsel had no answer for that, or none he wished to speak aloud, and sat down.

* * *

Corporal Ede testified after the noon recess. He was a narrow man now, thirty years old and bent as if still ducking under tent ropes, his discharge papers folded in his coat pocket like a talisman he no longer quite believed in. His name appeared eleven times in Cyrus's ledger; Cyrus had found him in November, living above a farrier's shop in Maidstone, and had watched his face when she said, *I wrote down what happened at Balaclava, corporal. All of it. Your part included.*

He had wept then. On the stand he did not weep. He spoke in a flat soldier's voice about supply convoys that arrived short-weighed, about requisitions signed by officers who had never stood at the depot, about the day he was ordered to sign a receipt for four hundred blankets that had been, so far as he ever saw, three hundred and twelve—and the other eighty-eight sold out the back of the camp while their colonel dined with a treasury man newly arrived from home.

"Who was the treasury man?" Kettering asked.

"A clerk, sir. Aldous Finch."

The name dropped into the courtroom like a stone into a well. Everyone had heard of Finch by now—the man who had fallen from Westminster Bridge in December, the man whose suicide had closed one door and opened a dozen darker questions. At the defense table, Lord Halloran's barrister leaned forward. Across the room, Vane did not move at all, and the not-moving was itself eloquent.

"And this Finch," Kettering continued, "did you see his hand? His writing?"

"Saw him write the receipt himself, sir. Big looping letters, like a schoolmaster's. He wrote my name in it wrong twice and laughed about it."

Kettering nodded to an usher, who brought the hymnal.
