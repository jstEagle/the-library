## 38. Verdicts

The assizes sat in the county hall like a great grey animal grown old in its own importance, and for three days it had fed on the Halloran name. Emrys had not been permitted inside—kin of the accused were barred from the gallery until verdict, lest their presence sway the jury or their grief disturb the dignity of the Crown—and so he had spent those days in the corridor outside Court Two, listening to doors. It was a strange apprenticeship, learning a trial by its sounds: the murmur before a witness entered, the creak of the dock, the particular hush that fell when Vane rose to speak, a hush like snow settling on a field, beautiful and cold and covering everything.

He learned his father's voice through oak panels too. He heard it crack on the second day, when the prosecution read aloud the ledger of debts—the true ledger, the one Lord Halloran had kept in his study with the key around his neck like a reliquary of shame—and heard him say, *yes, my hand, yes, my debt*, and nothing more. He did not blame Vane. He did not accuse. He simply admitted what he was, and Emrys, standing in the corridor with his back against cold stone, felt something in his chest break and set at once, the way a bone will knit crooked if no one sets it properly.

On the third day they let the family in.

* * *

The courtroom smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular staleness of justice administered in a building that had heard too much. Emrys took his seat in the gallery between his aunt and the empty place where his mother should have been—she had died believing her husband an honest man, and perhaps that was a mercy, though he could not yet decide—and looked down at the well of the court as at a stage seen from a high pew.

His father stood small in the dock. That was the first cruelty of the architecture: the dock was built to diminish, and it worked. A man who had commanded rooms all his life, who had filled doorways with his shoulders and his voice, now seemed folded into himself, a coat left on a hook.

And there, two rows behind the barristers, sat Cyrus Thornbury.

She wore her grey dress, the plain one, the one she had worn to tend men whose names officers forgot, and her hands were folded in her lap as if she were waiting for a kettle to boil. Only Emrys knew what those hands had done in the past fortnight—had copied, sewn, carried, sworn, surrendered. Only he knew that beneath the bench where she sat, in the custody of the court's clerk, lay seven years of small names in a ledger bound in brown cloth, each page dated in her careful hand, each entry a stone lifted off something buried.

Vane sat at the prosecution's table in his inspector's best, and he was magnificent. Emrys hated how magnificent. The Colonel had a voice like cutlery laid on linen—all polish and precision—and he deployed it now in service of the Crown's case against the Halloran debts, painting Lord Halloran as a speculator who had dressed speculation in regimental colours, who had borrowed against contracts he meant to default, whose workshop full of "patriotic manufacture" was a theatre set with nothing behind the boards.

It was even true. That was the unbearable architecture of the whole affair: some of it was true. His father *had* borrowed badly. His father *had* signed papers whose arithmetic made no sense, because Vane's early money had come wrapped in exactly this kind of plausible paper, and by the time the sums turned poisonous the antidote and the disease shared one signature.

But embezzlement—the officer's funds diverted, the conspiracy with treasury clerks—that was Vane's own reflection in the glass, held up and reversed. And on that question the jury would have to weigh Cyrus's ledger against the word of a decorated colonel.

* * *

They brought the verdicts after four o'clock, when the light through the high windows had gone the colour of weak tea and every candle in the room had been lit, so that faces flickered between gold and shadow like coins being turned over.

The foreman was a corn factor with a face like a closed ledger himself. He stood, unfolded his paper, and read in a flat Midlands voice that made each phrase sound like a line item.

On the counts of fraudulent borrowing and debt concealment: guilty.

Emrys heard his aunt's breath go out of her. In the dock, his father did not move at all—which was somehow worse than collapsing. He had known. Emrys realized, watching that still grey profile, that his father had asked for this verdict in the only prayer he had left. Guilt on his own terms, in his own ledger, paid in his own coin. There was a terrible dignity in it, and a terrible waste.

But on the counts of conspiracy to divert officer's funds, on receiving stolen monies of the regiment, on the forged instruments of the winter contracts: not guilty. Not proven. The words fell one by one, and with each one Vane's face performed a small funeral—grief, regret, the sorrow of a public servant betrayed by a friend—while behind his eyes Emrys watched the arithmetic run the other way.

The sentence came swiftly: restitution in full, a fine that would strip the estate to its fields and fences, and eighteen months' imprisonment—suspended in part on account of age and cooperation, but imprisonment nonetheless. His father would go to the gaol at York, not the hulks. Small mercies. They were going to have to learn to count those.

As the constables moved to take him down, Lord Halloran turned—not to the gallery, not to his son, but to the third row, where a woman in a grey dress sat with folded hands—and he inclined his head to her. A bow. Shallow, formal, unmistakable. The whole courtroom saw it and did not understand it.

Emrys understood it. It was the bow of a man acknowledging the person who had told the truth about him when lying would have been easier. Her ledger did not spare him; it named his debts alongside Vane's thefts, dated them, cross-referenced them, refused to sort them into comfortable piles. She had convicted his father as surely as she had unmasked the Colonel, and his father had bowed to her for it.

*Not proven*, Emrys thought, watching them lead his father out through the door beneath the judge's seat. *Not the whole truth either.* Justice, it turned out, was a ledger too, and every entry in it cost someone.

* * *

There was no recess worth the name. The court rolled on like a cart downhill, and the next matter was called: *the Crown against Colonel Ambrose Vane*, and then, bundled into the same hour like an afterthought, *the Crown's information against Miss Cyrus Thornbury, charge of forgery of army records*—to be heard together, said the clerk, given the common evidence.

Vane's counsel rose first, and for an hour the Colonel's magnificence was disassembled in public, plank by plank, and it was Cyrus's doing.

She took the stand a second time—she had testified already in the Halloran matter, and the room knew her now, this quiet grey woman with the voice that never rose—and she answered questions as if she were taking a pulse. Yes, she had served in Crimea. Yes, she had kept a private record. Yes, entry forty-one, dated the twelfth of October, 1806, recorded a requisition for winter blankets signed *A. Vane, Col.* and countersigned by a clerk, *A. Finch*—and yes, the same requisition appeared in the regimental books with a different date and a different sum, and yes, she had compared them, page against page, because comparing things was what she did, it was the whole of her profession, you learned to notice when a wound reported and a wound suffered did not agree.

The ledger itself was passed up to the bench, and Emrys watched the judge handle it. That was the moment he would remember all his life, he thought afterward: the judge—a dry old eagle with dust in the creases of his wig—turning the brown-cloth book over in his hands, opening it near the front, reading. Reading longer than judges read. Reading the way one reads a letter from home.

The entries were not arguments. That was their power. They were not accusations; they were observations. *Sgt. J. Quill, 3rd Regt of Foot, died of wounds received in the sortie, 14th June. Credit for the night manoeuvre given in dispatches to Capt. —. No pension applied for. Widow: Martha Quill, presently of —.* Names, dates, sums, silences. Seven years of them. When the prosecuting counsel for Vane tried to characterize the ledger as the fanciful compilation of a woman of no standing, the judge looked at him over his spectacles and said, "It is dated, sir, in a consistent hand, contemporaneous with the events described, and corroborated in six instances by witnesses this court has already heard. What precisely would you call it, if not a record?"

Counsel had no good answer, and everyone in the room knew it, and the silence that followed had a different quality than Vane's silences. It was the silence of a thing being decided.

Then they brought out the hymnal.

Martha Quill herself carried it to the witness table, a small dark book with a cracked spine, and she held it the way one holds a sleeping child. Aldous Finch had given it to her years ago—this was established plainly, in her flat northern voice, while Vane's counsel shifted like a man sitting on something hot—and inside it, in margins crowded close as ivy on a wall, Finch had written his drafts: the confession he never dared sign, the account of letters dictated to him, signatures pressed into his hand, sums routed through his clerkship at the treasury's pleasure. And among the dictated letters, named again and again in Finch's cramped script: *by direction of Col. V.*

They matched the hand. That was the technical miracle Emrys only half followed—a treasury clerk produced under promise of leniency identified samples of Finch's official writing; an expert in questioned documents, summoned from London at ruinous expense that Emrys suspected Cyrus had arranged through channels he would learn about later, compared them under a lens and pronounced the hymnal margins genuine beyond reasonable doubt. But the miracle that mattered was simpler: a dead man's small voice, hidden in a songbook, saying what he had been too afraid to say alive. They had silenced Finch on a bridge in London, and here he was anyway, testifying in ink.

Vane's composure went the way of ice on a spring river—not all at once, but in long cracking shifts that everyone could hear. First he corrected counsel's errors with a smile. Then he stopped smiling. Then, when the expert held up the lens-work for the jury and the fourth matching signature was read aloud, Vane leaned toward his solicitor and was heard—clearly, terribly heard in the hushed room—to say, "This is nonsense, I'll have the man's commission for—" and stopped himself, too late, in a silence you could have measured with calipers.

The jury did not take long. On the charges of forgery of public instruments and uttering the same: guilty. On the charge of procuring the death of Aldous Finch—murder, in the law's cold phrasing, though everyone understood it for what it was: a push on a parapet contracted at distance—guilty of manslaughter, the lesser finding, the compromise verdict, because the hired man had fled and could not be produced and juries are cautious creatures. But guilty enough. Guilty beyond redemption.

When the foreman read it, Vane stood very straight, and Emrys had to grant him this: he did not wilt. He received the sentence—transportation for the forgeries, and the murder matter remanded for further examination that everyone in the room knew was a formality preceding the rope—with the rigid courtesy of a man reviewing troops that had mutinied. Only once did he look into the gallery, and his eyes found not Emrys but Cyrus, and what passed across his face was not hatred. It was bewilderment. He could not understand, to the end, how the invisible woman had beaten him. He had fought armies and treasuries and men, and he had been undone by a clerk with a notebook, and the shape of that would follow him to the end of his days, whatever end it was.

* * *

Which left her.

"The Crown's information against Cyrus Thornbury," the clerk read, and Emrys felt the whole room lean, imperceptibly, toward the grey dress in the third row.

The charge was forgery of army records: that she had fabricated entries, altered dates, invented transactions to suit a private grudge. It was Vane's counterstroke, filed weeks ago with doctored exemplars, and it had hung over her like weather ever since—an arrest warrant drawn and waiting, stayed only by the court's indulgence while the greater matters burned.

She stood. She did not go to the dock. She stood where she was, in the aisle, and the judge beckoned her forward, and she came to the rail and put both hands on it, and Emrys saw that her knuckles were white and her face was perfectly calm, which was the most frightening combination he knew.

The warrant was read. The doctored exemplars—two pages purportedly showing her hand altering sums—were produced, and the questioned-documents man was recalled, and he examined them, and examined her ledger, and examined the letters she had written from London which the court had subpoenaed, and then he did something Emrys did not expect.

He laughed. Softly, apologetically, into the hush.

"My lord," he said, "whoever prepared these exemplars studied a hand of this general character, but they have not studied *this* hand. The lady's numerals are formed with a nurse's habits—she writes figures the way one writes doses, legible in bad light, in haste, by tired eyes. Every digit in her genuine work is open at the loop. These forgeries are closed-looped throughout. No one writes eight thousand pages—" he caught himself, smiled again, "—eight hundred pages, my lord, over seven years, in one style and then suddenly another for two entries that happen to incriminate a colonel. The alterations are not hers. Indeed," and here he paused, delicately, "the exemplars appear themselves to be traced from a copybook hand, imperfectly."

The judge looked at the pages. Looked at Cyrus. Looked at the ledger, which lay on the bench before him still, worn brown cloth gone soft as old leather at the corners.

"Miss Thornbury," he said. "You have heard yourself called a falsifier of soldiers' records. What do you say?"

Cyrus's voice, when it came, was quiet enough that the room had to stop breathing to hold it.

"I say, my lord, that I have written down eleven hundred and forty-two names of persons whom the official record omits. Some of them reflect badly upon me. Some reflect badly upon men I have loved and served. If I had meant to forge, my lord, I would have begun by forging myself innocent."

The silence afterward lasted a long time. Then the judge said, "The information is dismissed. The book is certified a true record, and shall be so enrolled." And he put his hand flat on the cover, briefly, almost gently, as a man touches the shoulder of someone he has wronged.

* * *

Afterward the corridor was chaos—bailiffs and barristers and newspaper men with pencils, and somewhere his aunt weeping in the practical way of women who have managed estates through worse—but Emrys found Cyrus at last at the top of the steps, in the thin gold end-of-day light, holding her ledger against her chest with both arms as though the wind might otherwise take it.

"It's done," he said, because there was nothing adequate and that was nearly true.

"It's done," she agreed. Her eyes were on the square below, where Vane was being handed into a closed carriage between two constables; a crowd had gathered, and someone threw mud, and it missed, and Vane did not flinch. "Partial," she said. "My lord Halloran goes to York for debts that half began as someone else's design. Martha Quill gets a pension petition that may take years. Finch is still dead." She turned to look at Emrys, and for once her face was entirely unguarded, and he saw how tired she was—seven years tired, all of it surfacing at once now that the wall had come down. "But it's real. I keep telling myself it's real."

"It's real," he said.

Below, the carriage carrying Vane lurched into motion, and the crowd parted for it, and closed again, indifferent, already forgetting.

Cyrus looked down at the ledger in her arms, and then out at the road leading north from the assize town—the long road, the fen road, the one that went away from everything—and Emrys saw her look at it a moment too long, the way a man looks at a door he is deciding whether to walk through.

"Cyrus," he started, and did not know how to finish it—*stay*, or *thank you*, or *what happens to us now*—and while he stood there failing, a clerk came out onto the steps behind them with a paper in his hand and an odd expression on his face.

"Miss Thornbury," the clerk said. "Forgive me. There's a matter the magistrate asked me to mention privately, before you leave the town." He glanced at Emrys, then back. "It concerns your brother. The record office has found something. A muster roll—from before the war you served in. They've found his name."
