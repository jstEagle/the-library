## 39. The Lantern Rehung

The frost came out of the ground in March the way a secret leaves a body—slowly, painfully, leaving everything beneath it changed. By April the fen had turned the colour of new copper, and the ditches ran loud with water that had been silent and lethal all winter. Cyrus Thornbury walked out to the workshop in the early morning because she could not sleep past dawn anymore; seven years of night tending had rewired her body so thoroughly that sleep now ended at first light whether she wished it or not. She had learned not to fight it. She brought her ledger instead.

She did not carry it everywhere out of fear. That habit had died in the courtroom, or rather it had transformed—the book no longer needed hiding, only keeping. She carried it now the way a curate carries scripture: from duty, from comfort, from the simple fact that it was hers and had been longer than anything else she possessed except her own hands.

The workshop stood exactly where it had always stood, which still struck her as a small miracle each time she saw it. She had expected, for so many months, to come round the corner of the walled garden and find a black rectangle of ash and rubble where the building had been—a scar with a fence around it and men in Vane's employ standing guard over nothing. Instead: grey stone, slate roof, four tall windows catching the low sun. The demolition order had died with the old magistrate's appointment, swept aside by the new man before he had even finished unpacking his chambers. He was said to be a plain fellow who disliked spectacle and hated waste, and Cyrus had decided within a week of his ruling that she could have loved him for both reasons.

The workmen's scaffolding still clung to the east wall where the fire of November had scorched the stone, but the scaffolding was being taken down plank by plank this week. Repair, not razing. It made a different sound entirely—hammers pulling nails rather than driving them—and Cyrus found she could stand and listen to it without her chest tightening.

Inside, the workshop smelled of linseed oil and glass dust and the green wet breath of spring coming through the propped door. Emrys Halloran was already at the long bench, sleeves rolled, a cloth in one hand and the lantern laid out before him on a bed of felt like a patient prepared for surgery.

He did not look up when she entered, but he spoke, which was its own kind of greeting. "The glass came back from York on Tuesday."

"And?"

"And I've been sitting here for half an hour afraid to unwrap it." He said it lightly, but his thumb kept moving over the sacking bundle in small circles, the way a man strokes a horse he is about to mount after a fall.

Cyrus set her ledger on the corner of the bench, took off her outer apron, and hung it on the peg by the door—an act of domesticity so ordinary it startled her. A year ago she would not have dared remove so much as her shawl in the presence of the master's son. Now she stood in the workshop in her grey working dress with the morning behind her and felt, absurdly, at home.

"Then let us look at it together," she said, "and be afraid in company. It goes better that way. I've found most things do."

Emrys smiled—not the quick social smile he wore downstairs among the creditors and the lawyers, but the slower one, the one that reached his eyes and stayed a moment after his mouth finished moving. He unwrapped the bundle.

The pane emerged from the sacking like something surfacing from dark water. It was perhaps eight inches square, crown glass, slightly uneven in the old way, and through its centre ran the name.

*SERGEANT THOMAS QUILL — 33rd FOOT — HE KEPT THE LIGHT.*

The letters had been ground into the glass by some Crimea regimental engraver with more patience than skill, and the original pane had shattered at Balaclava, been gathered up shard by shard by a dying man's comrades, shipped home in an officer's trunk, sold, resold, and finally bought at auction by Emrys Halloran two winters ago for more money than he confessed to anyone. The fire in the outbuildings had cracked the frame and clouded two panes, but the name-pane had survived intact—only crazed along one edge like ice on a puddle.

Until the accident. Until the night the scaffolding plank gave way under the workman and the whole lot came down against the east wall, and Emrys, running toward the crash, heard glass break inside the workshop and knew, before he opened the door, precisely what had broken.

That had been six weeks ago. He had sent the pane to a glass-house in York with a letter Cyrus had helped him compose, asking not for repair—for the grinding was a lost art, the man who'd done the original long dead—but for *regrinding*: a new pane cut from the old, the letters re-cut fresh into new glass, following the surviving original line for line. The letter had asked whether such a thing could even be done. The reply from York had been one sentence: *If the name matters enough, it can be done.*

Now the name lay between them on the felt, re-cut in clean bright strokes, and Cyrus read it aloud because it seemed required—because names unspoken were only half-kept.

"Sergeant Thomas Quill," she said. "Thirty-third Foot. He kept the light."

Emrys's voice joined hers on the last phrase, quietly, as if they were finishing a grace.

* * *

They worked through the morning assembling the lantern, and the work had the quality of certain kinds of prayer: repetitive, exacting, requiring the whole of the hands so that the mind might go where it needed. Cyrus held pieces steady while Emrys fitted them, and the brass frame—rebuilt by the York smiths from the fire-warped original—came together joint by joint until the thing stood upright again on the bench, a lantern once more and not a box of fragments.

It was a Crimean field lantern, the kind hung at dressing stations so the surgeons could work through the night. Quill had carried it. Quill had filled it and trimmed it and shielded its flame with his own body against the wind on the plateau above the harbour, and he had died doing something else entirely—something clever and brave and wholly his own—which a captain had then written up in dispatches under his own name. Cyrus had watched that theft happen from three feet away, holding a basin, unable to speak, seven years and a lifetime ago. The ledger began that same night, on a page torn from the back of a supply manifest, in pencil, by candlelight, with Quill's full name and rank and the words *the manoeuvre was his.*

"You're thinking about the beginning," Emrys said. It was not a question. He had learned to read the particular stillness she fell into when the past arrived uninvited.

"I'm thinking that he would find this very funny," Cyrus said. "A gentleman's son spending a fortnight's income and a season's labour on a lamp. He'd say—" she searched for the cadence of him, the broad Yorkshire burr she had not heard since 1811 and could not forget, "he'd say there was a deal easier way to waste money, sir, and most of 'em more pleasant."

Emrys laughed, and the laugh moved through the workshop and out the open door into the garden where the bees were working the currant flowers, and Cyrus thought: this is what a house sounds like when it is healing. She had heard houses die—in Crimea, in field hospitals requisitioned out of farmhouses, in the estate's own record room after the second fire. She knew the difference in the acoustics.

Lord Halloran's footsteps sounded on the gravel path outside—slower now than a year ago, with a drag at the left foot that had appeared during the months of the trial and never left. Cyrus and Emrys exchanged a glance and continued working. There was a new etiquette in the household regarding the father: no falling silent when he entered rooms, no abrupt changes of subject. Silence, they had all learned, was where shame bred.

He came through the door stooping slightly, though the lintel did not require it, as if he had grown used to making himself smaller. He wore a coat brushed shiny at the cuffs. Everything about him was brushed shiny at the edges now—the diminished man, the convicted man, spared conspiracy but convicted of debt fraud, his estates encumbered, his name in the London papers beside the word *fraud* in small type. He had aged a decade in a year. But his eyes, when they found the lantern on the bench, were clear.

"That's it, then," he said. "The Quill piece."

"The Quill piece," Emrys confirmed. "We hang it today, if the fittings hold."

His father nodded slowly. Then he did the thing that Cyrus still found hardest to watch, harder than the trial, harder than the arrest at the gate: he apologised again. Not in words—he had worn out words months ago—but in the small currency of acts. He crossed to the bench and picked up one of the brass cross-pieces and turned it over, examining the joinery with genuine attention, and said, "York does honest work. Better than I deserved from any of mine," and set it down carefully, and asked whether there was anything wanted from the house, and went out again down the path, dragging his left foot, toward the study where the restitution ledgers lay open every day from nine until dusk.

Restitution. It was the court's word and the new magistrate's condition, and Lord Halloran had seized upon it the way a drowning man seizes rope—not as punishment, Cyrus suspected, but as penance, which is a different thing altogether and easier to bear. Every week he sat with his clerks and paid out, in ordered instalments, to the tradesmen and tenant farmers and retired servants his borrowing had quietly bled. Some of the names on his list were in her ledger too. She had checked, in February, alone in her room, comparing column to column with a candle burning low—and had found that the two books agreed, hers and his, entry for entry, debt for debt, as if truth were a country both maps described accurately from opposite shores.

She had wept then, briefly and without drama, and washed her face, and gone down to supper.

* * *

By late afternoon the fittings were proved and the moment came that could not be postponed any longer without cowardice. The window in question was the great south-facing one of the workshop, above the bench where Quill's lantern had spent two winters being restored—the window that faced the road, the village, the church tower, the whole passing world. Hanging a lamp in a window was a signal older than language. Sailors' wives did it. Watchmen's households did it. It meant: someone here is awake; someone here remembers; there is a light kept against your coming home.

Emrys climbed the ladder. Cyrus steadied it at the base, her hands on the wood, looking up at him against the pale spring sky through the windowpanes. He fitted the bracket first, then lifted the lantern—empty of oil, glass only, a reliquary rather than a lamp—and settled it onto the hook, and adjusted it twice, three times, until it hung true and plumb.

Through the glass, from inside, the name read backward. From the road, from the world, it would read forward: legible, public, permanent.

"There," Emrys said, from the top of the ladder. And then, quieter, as if the word cost him: "There."

He came down and they stood together in the failing light and looked at it. Outside, the sun was dropping behind the elms and the workshop window had begun, faintly, to catch the last of it, so that the name seemed lit from beyond—as if the light came not from their side of the glass but from somewhere behind the sky, through the name, outward.

"He never saw England again," Cyrus said. "He never saw anything again, properly. They put him in the ground at Catania with no marker, because the burial detail's ink had run in the rain and nobody thought to rewrite it. I wrote his name in my book that night partly because—" She stopped. Seven years of practice at invisibility, and still the truest sentences caught in her throat. "Partly because I couldn't bear that the rain had him and I didn't."

Emrys said nothing for a while. Then: "Now everyone who walks the Alcester road will have him. Carters. Children. The post-boy. People who'll never know what Balaclava was."

"That's the point of it," Cyrus said. "Memory shouldn't require credentials."

They stood until the light failed entirely, and then Emrys lit the workshop lamps and the real work of the evening resumed—there was always real work now, records to sort and copy, the surviving scorched ledgers of the record room transcribed page by careful page into fair copies, because the family's history had nearly burned once and would not be permitted to burn again. Cyrus took up her pen. The scratch of nibs filled the warm room, and the bees had gone to bed, and somewhere down the corridor Lord Halloran closed his study door on another day of paying back what could be paid back.

At nine o'clock, footsore and ink-stained, Cyrus gathered her things. At the door she paused and looked back once at the dark window, where the lantern hung invisible against the night—and yet she found she could feel it there, the way one feels a picture on a wall in a familiar room. Present. Kept.

Tomorrow, she thought, she would write to Martha Quill. Martha had not yet seen it. Martha, who lived now in the cottage the estate had settled on her—small restitution of the family's own initiative, unprompted by any court—who came on Thursdays and argued politics with Emrys over tea as if she had known him all her life. Martha should see her husband's name shining in a window. Martha should see it first of anyone, before the carters and the children and the post-boy.

Yes. Tomorrow, the letter. She had almost reached the turning of the stair when the knock came at the front door—firm, official, wrong for the hour—and the housekeeper's hurried feet crossed the hall below, and a voice Cyrus did not know asked, in the flat tone of men who deliver what cannot be refused, whether Miss Thornbury was in the house.

Her hand found the banister in the dark.

Down in the hall, lamplight bloomed, and the housekeeper called up the stair, uncertain, apologetic: "Miss? Miss Thornbury—there are gentlemen below. They're asking for you by name."
