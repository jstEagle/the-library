## 1. The Weight of Paper

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width, and Ivo Fairweather was measuring it the way they measured everything now: by how much could be carried.

A candle stub, two fingers tall. A knife with a cracked handle that fit the palm like an old promise. Three pens — no, two; the third had splayed its nib beyond rescue an hour ago. A change of linen rolled tight as a wound dressing. Six silver coins sewn into a coat hem, which was not many coins and rather more coat than winter strictly required. And a spoon. The spoon had come with them eight years ago, when they'd arrived in Havelmark with nothing but a name nobody had bothered to give them and the clothes Master Aldous had said were *adequate, barely*. The spoon was the only thing from before that Ivo had kept.

Everything on the table fit inside one satchel with room left over for bread, if there were bread.

Everything except the atlas.

Upstairs, the floorboards creaked their slow, uneven music: Aldous pacing, or trying to, because Aldous had never in his life been able to sit still while he still had breath to spend, and apparently did not intend to learn now, at the end. The creak went three steps toward the window. Stopped. Went back toward the bed. Ivo counted the intervals without meaning to. They had gotten very good, this past month, at counting things without meaning to — heartbeats between coughs, hours between the doctor's visits, drops of ink left in a bottle.

Ivo dipped the pen and returned to the last plate.

It was the finest work of their life, which was a bitter joke, because it was also the only work of their life that would hang them if anyone saw it done well. The plate showed the river country east of Havelmark as the imperial archives recorded it in the year 1712 — and beside those lines, in Aldous's cramped red ink, the same country as it stood in 1746. Between the two lay a border that had moved like a snake through grass, patient and quiet, sliding a mile here, half a mile there. And where the border slid, villages vanished. Not burned. Not emptied. Simply *unrecorded*, until a clerk in Vienna could look at his beautiful official map and say, truthfully, that no such places existed.

Ivo laid the final stroke along the old riverbed at Doleny Ford, blew gently on the wet line, and sat back.

Their hands were steady. That surprised them. All month the hands had trembled at odd moments — cracking eggs, lacing boots — as if the body knew something the mind refused to read aloud. But tonight the hands were a craftsman's hands, and so Ivo let them work, because work was the only prayer either of them knew.

From above: three creaks, then silence, longer than usual.

Ivo's pen hovered.

Then the silence broke into that particular ragged cough, and Ivo exhaled, and hated themselves a little for exhaling in relief rather than grief, and climbed the stairs with the candle.

* * *

Master Aldous Kray was sitting on the edge of his bed fully dressed, boots included, as though he had somewhere to be. He was seventy-one years old and looked carved out of something that had once been a tree and had since given up on the pretense. His eyes found Ivo in the doorway and brightened with the sharp, mischievous light that had survived everything else the years had taken.

"Ah," he said. "Good. You're still here. I half feared you'd have the sense to run."

"You half fear everything," Ivo said. "You told me the frost would kill the roses and I should half fear it, and the roses came back fine."

"The roses," Aldous said gravely, "are braver than both of us." He patted the bed beside him. "Sit. Tell me about the plate."

"It's done."

Something crossed the old man's face — triumph and terror sharing a bench, elbowing each other for room. "Done," he repeated. "Thirty-one years. Done." He laughed, which became a cough, which Ivo waited out with a hand on his back, feeling the ribs beneath like the staves of a barrel. "Well. Don't look at me like a nursemaid. Look at me like an heir. Because that's what you are tonight, whether you like it or not, and I intend to make the inheritance properly heavy before I go. Sit."

Ivo sat.

"Do you know," Aldous said, "when I copied those archive folios — young man, quick hands, nobody ever counts the pages a quick hand copies — I thought I was stealing maps. Paper. That's all paper is, Ivo, until someone bleeds on it, and then it's something else entirely." He turned his head and looked at the window, where the frost had grown its own white borders across the glass. "Three villages. Do you understand yet what that means? Not destroyed. Erased. A grandmother in Doleny Ford can hold her granddaughter's hand and point across the river and say *my mother was born there* and be told, by every map, every ledger, every honest clerk, that there is no *there* there. The crown didn't kill them. It simply stopped remembering them. And a thing the empire stops remembering—" he tapped the floorboard with his boot, twice, like a judge's gavel "—was never alive at all. Legally speaking, we are all of us free to do anything whatsoever to a place that does not exist."

"And so you made it exist again," Ivo said quietly.

"I made a *record*. Whether it exists again depends entirely on who carries it to Vienna." Aldous's eyes came back around, and for a moment the mischief drained out of them and left something older and rawer underneath. "That's the part I cannot do, boy-girl. That's yours."

"Why mine?"

"Because you're nobody." Aldous said it with terrible tenderness, the way other men said *beloved*. "No family. No guild mark. No name worth recording — I've checked, believe me, I've spent thirty years making sure of it. The empire hunts documents by following the men who matter. You are a person the ledgers will look straight through. I bought your invisibility cheap and it turns out to be the most valuable thing I own." He smiled, thin and crooked. "Forgive me. Most masters leave their apprentices a set of tools."

"You left me a death warrant with engravings."

"I left you the *truth*, which has always weighed approximately the same." He reached under the pillow — Ivo started forward but Aldous waved him off — and produced not a weapon but a small brass key, worn smooth, and pressed it into Ivo's palm. The metal was warm from his skin and Ivo found they couldn't look away from it. "The strongbox key. There's coin in it, and letters, and the commission from the guild in Vienna that makes this whole enterprise legal as sunrise, provided the plates reach them. Voss—"

He stopped. His jaw worked.

"Voss?" Ivo said.

"Nobody," Aldous said, too quickly and too evenly, which from Aldous was as good as a shout. "If inspectors come, there'll be a man named Voss leading them. Be polite. He's very fond of being liked." He closed Ivo's fingers over the key with both of his dry, cold hands. "Now go downstairs and tell me honestly: can it be carried? The atlas entire. Plates, proofs, marginalia. Can one person carry it?"

Ivo thought of the table below. Of the satchel with room left over for bread.

"No," they said. "Not quickly. Not quietly. Not through a checkpoint. The plates alone, wrapped and boxed, are half a man's load, and any box that size gets opened."

Aldous nodded slowly, unsurprised, and somehow this was worse than argument. "No," he agreed. "It can't be carried. So it must be *hidden*, and hidden things are lost things, eventually. Unless—" and here the mischief crept back into the corners of his mouth, exhausted but stubborn, like a cat coming home in bad weather, "—unless it isn't carried by a person at all. Unless it travels the way everything travels through this town, downriver, among things nobody thinks to inspect. But that's tomorrow's problem, and tomorrow is somebody else's. Tonight, apprentice, I want you to sleep. Actually sleep. You've inked my life's confession and you're swaying like a mast in weather."

"I'm fine."

"You're a liar for the first time in your life and you're bad at it. Bed."

Ivo rose. At the door they stopped, because there was a question they'd been carrying for a month, and it had grown heavier than the atlas.

"Master. Why me? Truly. There are men in this town with names and wagons and reasons to hate the crown. Why hand it to a ghost?"

Aldous lay back against the pillows and looked at the ceiling, and for a long moment Ivo thought he might not answer, or had already gone wherever he was going.

"Because ghosts," he said finally, "are the only people the empire has never once managed to catch."

* * *

Downstairs, Ivo did not sleep.

They unpacked the satchel instead and repacked it, twice, weighing each item in their hands like a merchant pricing goods: candle stub, knife, two pens, spoon, six coins in a hem. A life measured in portable things. They had thought themselves poor and discovered, holding each small object, that they had simply traveled light — that everything they actually owned fit in two hands and everything that owned *them* weighed forty pounds of engraved copper and could not cross a bridge without being seen.

Outside, the river muttered under its first skin of ice, and beyond it the bridge — the bridge that had already closed to carts this week, that would close to everything soon, that was closing even now like a fist deciding to become a wall. Winter was shutting the door. And somewhere north of the frost line, riding behind relays of horses, imperial writs were doing the same arithmetic Ivo had just done, arriving at the same conclusion: *close the bridge, seal the town, search every paper.*

Every paper.

Ivo looked at the atlas lying open on the worktable, all its erased villages shining faintly in the candlelight, and understood — with the sick, clear calm that comes just before panic — that invisibility had never been theirs. It had been issued to them, like a license, by people too important to bother looking. And licenses could be revoked by the first inspector with a good memory and a better eye.

Someone knocked on the street door.

One knock. Soft. Then nothing.

Ivo stood very still in the cold workshop, one hand flat on forty pounds of evidence, and listened to the ice forming on the river, and waited to see if the knock would come again.
