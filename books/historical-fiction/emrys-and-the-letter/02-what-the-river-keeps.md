## 2. What the River Keeps

The ground in the churchyard took the spade badly that morning, the way it always did in late autumn, half-thawed and sulking, and Marta Yarrow dug her husband's grave herself because no one in this town had ever done anything for her that she hadn't first done for them.

Six men offered. She refused all six, and by noon the whole town knew it, and by evening the whole town had opinions about it, which was the closest thing to affection this place knew how to give.

Tomas had been a ferryman for thirty-one years and a liar for thirty-two, if you counted the day they met, when he told her the river was calm. It had not been calm. It had nearly drowned them both, which was how he'd decided she was the one. He'd gone in the end the way he'd lived — on the water, in October, when the current turned mean and the light went early. They found the boat snagged two miles down. They found him a day after that.

Marta stood at the edge of the hole she'd made and looked at him and thought, with the strange practicality that grief had left her, that he was lighter now than he'd ever been. Tomas had been a big man, big hands, big laugh, big appetites, and the river had taken all of it and left her something small and hard and portable, like a stone you keep in your pocket to remind yourself the world has edges.

The priest said his words. The townspeople said theirs. Old Ferenc wept openly, which was not remarkable, since Ferenc wept at christenings, weddings, and once, memorably, at the opening of a new bake oven. Marta did not weep. She had done her weeping in the night, alone, into the sleeve of a coat that still smelled of tar and tobacco, and she did not intend to make a public exhibition of what was hers.

It was while the priest droned that she noticed the apprentice.

The Aldous boy — or girl, or whatever it was; the town had argued about it for six years and settled on *the apprentice*, which solved nothing and pleased everyone — stood at the churchyard wall, apart from the others, and was not watching the burial.

The apprentice was watching the bridge.

Everyone else's eyes went to the grave, as eyes should. The apprentice's went to the bridge — the old wooden span across the river, the one that froze shut every winter and turned this town from a place on the way to somewhere into a place at the end of nothing. The apprentice watched it the way a debtor watches a creditor's door. Watched it, Marta noted, with a little counting motion of the lips. Measuring. Timing. Doing sums about it.

Marta had buried a husband, and so she was permitted, by the ancient law of widows, to observe anything she liked without explanation. She used the license freely. She watched the apprentice watch the bridge for the better part of the eulogy, and by the time the priest said *amen*, she had assembled a small and disagreeable picture.

The apprentice came to the river landing every Thursday to collect a parcel of paper and ink from the barge — everyone knew that, Aldous sent his boy for supplies the way other men sent for bread. But lately the Thursday visits had changed. The apprentice had started lingering. Asking, in that careful, colorless voice, when the last barge would run. Whether the ferry still crossed after the first ice. Whether the bridge toll was collected in winter, or whether the soldiers simply came and stood there and were cold.

Soldiers. The apprentice had asked about soldiers twice now, and both times had gone pale as a fish belly when Marta answered, which was answer enough.

Marta Yarrow was not an imaginative woman, and she was proud of it. Imagination was what happened to people who didn't pay attention. But she paid attention the way Tomas had read water — by the set of the current, by what moved underneath — and what moved underneath this town this autumn was something she could taste in the well water. Master Aldous had not left his house in a month. The shutters stayed closed. The parcels of paper kept coming, bigger parcels, and the apprentice's eyes had developed the look of a person carrying water in both hands across a frozen yard — everything held, nothing spilled, and God help anyone who jostled the elbow.

Something was in that house. Something that had to do with the bridge, and the soldiers, and the winter coming down the valley like a debt collector.

Marta had her own arithmetic. It went: *Tomas. The ferry. The toll the crown put on the crossing in '39 that drowned the ferry trade. The village upriver that stopped appearing on the tax rolls, and the priest who stopped writing back, and the year the maps changed and no one in this town said one word about it out loud.*

She had been keeping that arithmetic for eleven years, and it did not balance, and she had stopped expecting it to.

But she knew a fellow bookkeeper when she saw one.

* * *

She found the apprentice three days later at the landing, doing what the apprentice did, which was standing in the exact posture of a person who has nowhere to be and is therefore watching everywhere at once.

"You," Marta said.

The apprentice turned. Up close, the face was still a puzzle — young and old at once, all the softness worn off early, the way a coin goes smooth. "Widow Yarrow. I'm — I'm sorry. About your husband."

"Are you." Marta looked at the river. "The river took him. Are you sorry at the river?"

"I — no. Yes. I don't know how to answer that."

"Good. Don't answer things you don't know. It's a habit that gets people hanged." She let that sit in the cold air between them for exactly as long as it needed to. "You mend nets?"

The apprentice blinked. "Do I — no?"

"Can you learn?"

"I can learn most things with my hands."

"I know. I've watched you carry Master Aldous's parcels. You carry them like they're sleeping children. Hands like that can learn a net." She turned and started up the bank, and did not look back, because looking back was asking, and she was not asking. "Tomorrow. Dawn. The shed by my house. Bring your own knife."

Behind her, after four full steps of silence: "Why?"

Marta stopped. She had prepared for *yes* and for *no* and for the apprentice's particular flavor of *I don't understand*, which was really a *no* wearing a scholar's coat. She had not prepared for *why*, asked plainly, the way a person asks when they have spent their whole life being given reasons instead of questions.

"Because my nets have holes in them," she said, without turning, "and because you stand at my husband's funeral watching the bridge like a man doing sums, and I want to know what the sums come to. One of us is going to learn something, apprentice. I've decided it'll be both of us."

She walked home. She did not look back. But she heard the small footsteps follow, and she counted them, and there were more of them than she expected, and they were quicker than she expected too, and by the time she reached her gate she had already begun — against every instinct she owned — to think of the sound as *company*.

* * *

The apprentice came at dawn, with a knife, and stood in the shed doorway with the expression of a person entering a church of the wrong religion.

"Sit," Marta said, and pointed at the stool. "Net first. Watch."

She took a torn net from the pile — a good net, a net Tomas had made, with a tear in it shaped like the argument they'd had the night before he died, though she'd have denied that under oath — and she showed the apprentice the mesh, and the shuttle, and the way the knot sat in the hand.

"You don't pull," she said. "Everyone pulls. Pulling is what hands want to do. You *set* the knot and let the line lie where it wants. The river does the pulling. Your job is only to make sure that when it pulls, nothing comes undone."

The apprentice's hands took the shuttle. They were, as promised, good hands — quick, precise, a little too careful, the hands of someone who had learned early that breaking a thing meant paying for it.

"You've mended before," Marta said. "Not nets."

"Paper. Sometimes leather. Master Aldous's books, when the bindings go."

"Mm. And when you mend a book, do you read it?"

The shuttle stopped. Just for a heartbeat — a stutter in the rhythm, small as a skipped stitch. "I mend the binding," the apprentice said. "The pages are the master's business."

"Of course they are." Marta took up another net and worked beside them, and for a while the shed held only the sound of line and knot, and the river beyond the wall doing its low winter muttering.

She let the silence do its work. Silence was a net too. You set it and waited, and things swam into it on their own.

"I knew a man from Dolni Vrata," she said at last, conversationally, the way you'd mention weather. "Up the valley. Village on the old maps. My husband's mother was born there. He used to say the plums from Dolni Vrata were the sweetest in the empire, and the empire agreed, right up until the empire decided the village wasn't in it anymore. New border came through in '36. Redrew the line. Dolni Vrata went from *here* to *not here* in the space of one surveyor's afternoon. The people didn't move. The map did." She set a knot. "Funny thing. The tax collectors found the village again within the year. Taxes travel better than people do."

The apprentice had gone very still. Stillness, Marta thought, was the loudest sound there was.

"That's an old story," the apprentice said carefully.

"It's an old story." She looked up. The apprentice's face had done what faces do — closed like a shop shutter, everything valuable moved inside. But the hands had not been told. The hands were strangling a net knot that wanted nothing more than to lie flat, pulling, pulling, exactly the way beginners pull.

"You're tearing my net," Marta said.

The apprentice looked down, and set the knot properly, and breathed.

"Master Aldous is dying," the apprentice said, suddenly, to the net, in a voice like a confession dropped in an empty church. "He is dying, and there is — there is work. Unfinished work. And the bridge closes soon, and there are going to be men here, and I don't know what to do, and I have never in my life said any of that out loud, and I would like it very much if you pretended I didn't."

Marta set down her shuttle.

The apprentice's shoulders had a curve she recognized — the curve of someone who had carried a thing so long they'd forgotten their spine was supposed to be straight. She had worn that curve herself for eleven years. She knew its exact weight, because she'd weighed it every morning, alone, at this same hour, in this same cold.

"Apprentice," she said. "What's your name?"

The question landed harder than any interrogation. The apprentice looked up, genuinely lost. "I — Fairweather. Ivo Fairweather."

"No." Marta shook her head. "That's a name written in a ledger somewhere. I mean *your* name. The one you'll have when this is over. Because I've watched you for six years, Ivo Fairweather, and I've never once seen you eat at anyone's table, or borrow a coat, or get drunk at the harvest fire, or be anyone's anything. You've been *the apprentice* so long you've started to believe that's a person." She stood, and took the mended net out of the shaking hands, and inspected it, and found it, to her private fury, nearly perfect. "In this town, that ends. Whatever you're carrying, you'll carry it better fed. That's not kindness. That's tactics."

"It feels like kindness," the apprentice said, in a small voice, like a person touching a stove they'd been told was cold.

"It'll pass." Marta hung the net on the wall, next to Tomas's old ones, and did not examine why she'd put it there, among the kept things. "Come inside. There's soup. And while you eat, you're going to tell me what's in Master Aldous's house that has you watching my bridge like it owes you money."

"I never said there was anything—"

"You said it just now, with your whole face. Eat your soup."

Outside, far up the valley, the first real cold of the season was walking down out of the mountains, patient as an inspector, and the river beneath the ice went on keeping what it kept — the boat, the man, the year the map changed — and holding it, the way it held everything, until the spring came to give it back.
