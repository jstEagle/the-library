## 3. Orders Come with the Frost

The cold came down out of the mountains the way Master Aldous had always said a good map should arrive: without apology, covering everything at once, changing nothing it touched and yet changing the meaning of all of it. Ivo woke to a workshop where the ink pot had frozen overnight, a small disc of black ice skinned over like a pond, and stood holding it in both hands for longer than was practical, thinking about how many things in that room were only waiting for warmth to become useful again.

Upstairs, the master coughed twice and then didn't.

Ivo counted to thirty, the way you learn to. Then came the rustle — the particular dry whisper of an old man rearranging himself among blankets — and the counting could stop. It had become a liturgy: the silence, the count, the rustle. Someday, Ivo knew, there would be no rustle, and they had not yet decided what a person did with their hands on such a morning.

They breathed on the ink pot until the skin softened, and went back to work.

The last plate was nearly done. It was the one Aldous had called *the honest one* — the river from its source down to the bridge, drawn as it actually lay, not as the crown's surveyors had declared it to lie eleven years ago. Side by side, the two lines told the whole story without a single word of explanation. One line passed east of the marshes. The other cut straight through three villages as though they had never existed, which was, Ivo now understood, precisely the point.

Ivo laid down the final stroke of the eastern bank, blotted it, and sat back, and felt the strange weightlessness that follows finished work — followed, in this case, by the far heavier weight of *what now?*

Aldous had answered that question forty times in the past month, each answer different, each delivered in the voice of a man arguing with his own death about scheduling. *Take it to Graz. Take it to the guild by the lake road. Burn it and live.* The last instruction had been given twice, which Ivo had learned meant *do not do this.*

What it came down to, Ivo thought, measuring the atlas the way they measured everything — in what could be carried — was this: the plates weighed less than a sack of flour. They fit inside a coat. A person could walk out of town with them under one arm and no one would look twice, because no one ever looked twice at Ivo Fairweather.

That had been the whole of their life's strategy, come to find out. Invisibility. The apprentice of no family and no name worth recording, the boy-or-girl-or-neither whom innkeepers forgot mid-sentence, whom muster officers waved past because writing down *whatever that is* seemed like effort. For nineteen years this had felt like a misfortune. Now, holding nine copper plates wrapped in oilcloth, Ivo discovered it had all along been something else entirely.

It had been permission. Granted by other people, without their knowing, revocable at any moment.

The realization arrived with a sensation like biting foil — bright, wrong, metallic. Because invisibility was only safety so long as nobody was *looking*. And somewhere upriver, in whatever cold office produced documents with seals on them, someone had begun to look.

* * *

The writs arrived on a Tuesday, carried by a trooper who did not dismount, which everyone understood to be its own kind of message.

There were three of them, nailed to the market cross in the order of increasing badness. The first announced that the bridge would close to traffic at month's end, the season's ice having made crossing hazardous — a fiction, since the bridge had stood through worse winters than anyone alive could name, but a convenient one, because a closed bridge was a strainer, and rivers with strainers caught whatever floated downstream.

The second announced the appointment of an imperial inspector for the district, name of Voss, who would arrive before the frost set hard and whose authority would be, the wording made clear, limited chiefly by his own judgment.

The third was the one people stood in front of longest. All papers in the town and surrounding parishes — deeds, leases, ledgers, letters, charts, surveys — would be produced for examination. Any document bearing upon the boundaries of His Imperial Majesty's dominions was to be surrendered forthwith. Penalties for concealment followed, in the manner of such documents, after a paragraph break, in a list, like a menu.

The trooper rode off. The town read the notice, and then did what towns do: it talked about the weather.

"Early frost," said the tanner, squinting at the mountains as if he personally found them suspicious.

"Coldest November in years," agreed the smith, who knew perfectly well it was not.

Ivo stood at the edge of the crowd with their collar up and read the third writ four times. *Charts. Surveys.* Somebody in Vienna had noticed that a cartographer had gone missing from his post, along with certain materials, and had done what bureaucracies do when embarrassed: widened the net until it included everything, on the principle that if you examine every paper in the world, one of them must eventually confess.

That night, upstairs, Ivo read the list of penalties aloud, because Aldous had asked, and because lying to a dying man about the news seemed like a cruelty with no dignity in it.

"Sedition," Aldous said, when the reading was done. He pronounced it comfortably, the way another man might say *Tuesday*. "Well. I have been called worse by better." He was propped on pillows, gray as his own paper, but his eyes still worked the way they always had, moving over things and finding their errors. "You've finished the last plate."

"Yes."

"And?"

"And it's true," Ivo said. "Both lines. Anyone who looks will see it."

"That is the entire difficulty with truth, my dear. It insists on being visible." He closed his eyes. When he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and precise, the drafting voice, the one he'd used for instructions that were not suggestions. "Not the guild here. Not the church. Vienna. The guild in Vienna has men who owe me favors and men who hate me, and the haters are more reliable, because the indebted can be bought and the spiteful cannot. You will take it to Vienna."

"The bridge closes in twelve days."

"The bridge closing is for carts and cowards. The river does not close." He began to cough, and waved off the end of the sentence, and did not finish it, and Ivo lay awake half the night assembling the unfinished half out of spare parts: *the river does not close, therefore you will go by water, therefore you will need someone who knows the water.*

Which was a thought about Marta Yarrow, and Ivo turned away from it firmly, the way one turns away from a door one is not ready to open.

* * *

For three days, Ivo did what apprentices do, which is to say they became aggressively ordinary. They swept the workshop. They fetched ice from the river to keep the ink. They bought bread and salt pork and let the shopkeeper talk at length about her nephew in Pressburg, and nodded in the right places, and was almost — almost — able to believe that the world's attention, having arrived in the valley on horseback, might ride on through without stopping.

Then, on the fourth day, the surveyor's case came back to them.

It belonged to Herr Brandl, the tax assessor, and it had been broken for two years — the lock sprung, one hinge cracked, the whole thing demoted from carrying instruments to carrying odds and ends under Ivo's bed, where Brandl had shoved it during a visit and forgotten it. Ivo pulled it out to make room for firewood, opened it, and stopped.

Inside, in the lid, was a shallow false bottom — a tray for fine instruments, felt-lined, that lifted out on two brass tabs. Beneath it: a space. Not large. Perhaps the depth of two fingers, the width of the case, the length of a forearm.

Ivo measured the space against the plates in their mind, the way they measured everything, and found that the plates would fit exactly, the way things fit when a careful person designed a container for hiding valuable instruments from casual eyes.

Someone had built this case for precisely this purpose once already. Ivo did not know whose, or when, or what had been hidden in it before, and found that not knowing was somehow worse than knowing. The world, it appeared, had been conducting this same argument for a very long time, and had left tools lying around for whoever came next.

They took the case to the bench. They cleaned two years of dust out of the tray. And then they sat for a long while with the oilcloth bundle in their hands, not opening it, having the conversation with themselves that they had been avoiding since the writs went up at the cross.

Because hiding the plates was not the same as finishing them. Finishing was craft; any apprentice could finish work and hand it to the master. Hiding was a decision. Hiding meant that when the inspector Voss came through town examining papers, and eventually came — everyone knew he would come — to the little map-shop at the end of the lane, Ivo would stand in front of him and answer questions, and some of the answers would be untrue.

Ivo had never lied. Not from virtue especially, though the master had drummed into them that a mapmaker's first instrument was honesty — *a false line kills a traveler, my dear; there is no sin in our trade except the false line.* Mostly Ivo hadn't lied because nobody had ever asked them anything worth lying about. Invisibility required no testimony.

But that was the sick clarity of it, sitting there in the cold with the bundle: invisibility had never been theirs. It was a loan. It was something other people extended to the unimportant, and the writ nailed to the cross had cancelled it. From the day Voss arrived, Ivo Fairweather would be examined. Papers, premises, person. The only question left was what the examination would find — a frightened apprentice with nine plates of sedition, or a person who had chosen, deliberately, with open eyes, to stand in front of the truth and be mistaken for empty air.

One of those was a life. The other was just a very long habit of not being seen.

"All right," Ivo said aloud, to the case, to the plates, to the master sleeping upstairs. Their breath fogged. "All right."

They fitted the plates into the false bottom, one by one, each settling onto the felt with a sound too soft to hear. Nine plates. Three villages. Two lines of river, one true and one invented, and the difference between them equal to everything Ivo had ever owned and several things they hadn't known they owned until now.

The tray went back. The lid closed. The broken lock hung open, because a broken case that looked broken invited no questions, and Ivo slid it under the bed where Brandl had left it, among the odds and ends, invisible.

Then they went upstairs with the master's supper, and Aldous looked at them across the bowl for a long moment with his drafting-eyes, and said:

"You've hidden them somewhere clever."

"I haven't hidden anything."

And there it was. The first lie of Ivo Fairweather's life, delivered evenly, in a steady voice, to a dying man who had taught them that a false line kills a traveler.

Aldous smiled — slowly, the way ice forms — and closed his eyes, and said, "Good. You'll do," which was either forgiveness or enrollment, and Ivo genuinely could not tell which, and suspected it might be both.

* * *

The frost hardened that night for good. By morning the ruts in the lane had gone to iron, and smoke stood straight up from every chimney as if the whole town were saluting, and word came down from the bridge that a rider had been seen on the far bank at dawn, waiting for the ferry, wearing the gray coat of the imperial service and watching the town across the water with the patience of a man who intended to be owed nothing and to collect everything.

Marta put him across at midmorning, it was said. Six strokes of the oar, the crossing she could do asleep. But everyone agreed she had taken her time about it, and had looked him over thoroughly, and had said something to him at the landing that made the inspector smile.

Ivo heard all this secondhand at the bakery, standing in a queue of neighbors, holding bread they had no appetite for, feeling the weight under their bed a mile away the way you feel a tooth.

So it begins, they thought.

And then, because thoughts in a small town travel faster than riders: *so it begins, and Marta Yarrow has already met him, and Marta Yarrow watches the bridge too carefully, and sooner or later — probably sooner — one of us is going to have to say something true to the other.*

The gray coat turned up the lane toward the inn, unhurried, reading the town as he went.

Toward, Ivo noticed with a lurch, the end of the lane where the map-shop stood.
