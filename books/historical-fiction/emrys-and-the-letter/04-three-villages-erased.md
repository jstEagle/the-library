## 4. Three Villages, Erased

The frost came in through the workshop window in the night and drew its own maps on the panes — delicate, branching, entirely honest about where it had been. Ivo thought, watching it melt in the grey of morning, that the crown's mapmakers could have learned something from frost. Frost never pretended a river had moved.

They had not slept well. The plates sat in the surveyor's case beneath the loose floorboard where they had hidden them three nights ago, and every hour of the night Ivo had lain awake listening to the house settle around that spot as though the whole building were leaning down to look at it. Now, with the inspectors still days away by the best count — no one knew the worst count — there was nothing to do but wait, and waiting had turned out to be a kind of work, and Ivo was bad at it.

So instead they did the thing they had been avoiding since Master Aldous died: they read the marginalia.

There was more of it than any sane atlas would need. Aldous had written on everything. He had written in the gutters of the pages, in the margins, along the spines, once — memorably — across his own thumb in ink he then transferred to the corner of a chart of the Danube bend, so that his fingerprint survived in the archive long after the rest of him would not. In Vienna they had called him *the Marginal*, half affectionately. Ivo had always assumed the notes were habits. Old men's mutterings put down on paper because an old man's hand needed somewhere to go while his mind worked.

Now, with the workshop cold and the ink long dried and nobody left alive to explain himself, Ivo sat down with the draft copies — the ones that would pass inspection, the harmless ones — and read every word.

* * *

It began small. A note beside a river fork: *"Third survey shows west channel. First and second show east. Ask why the river moved for no one else."* Beside a stretch of road: *"This road appears on no earlier chart, yet the milestone is older than my father."* Beside a bridge: *"Bridge rebuilt 1731. On paper, built 1734. Three years the stone stood unbuilt."*

Ivo read these and felt the particular chill of a thing that has been in the room all along.

Aldous had been a careful man, which meant he had been a suspicious one; carelessness and suspicion are the same muscle worked in different directions. For thirty years he had copied, corrected, and collated the empire's charts, and for thirty years he had noticed — and noted — every place where the official record disagreed with the ground it claimed to describe. Rivers nudged. Roads rerouted. Boundary stones that appeared in surveys years after travelers had already been walking past them. And always, always, the corrections ran in one direction: away from the crown's convenience, toward somebody's loss.

By midmorning Ivo had filled four sheets of their own paper with transcriptions, and the pattern had stopped being a pattern and become a confession.

Three villages. That was what it came down to, when you laid the drafts side by side and followed Aldous's furious little arrows. Three villages on the frontier stretch — Dolen, Vresova, and a third whose name Aldous had written and struck through and written again, as if he could not bear the permanence of either act — existed in the surveys of 1712 and 1719 and 1726, and then did not exist. Not destroyed. Not depopulated, at least not in any record that survived. Simply *removed*. The border redrawn in a smooth, economical curve so that the villages fell outside it, and the tax rolls redrawn to match, and the parish registers quietly reassigned, until by 1740 a man could stand in the market square of Dolen and be told, courteously, by officials consulting accurate documents, that he was standing nowhere at all.

*"They have not burned anything,"* Aldous had written in the margin of the final plate, in a hand gone shaky with something that was not age. *"That is the genius of it. Fire leaves ash, and ash can testify. They have used ink. Ink dries clean."*

Ivo set the papers down. Their hands were steady — Ivo's hands were always steady, it was the one inheritance from no one that had served them well — but behind the steadiness something was rearranging itself, the way furniture rearranges in a dark room when you finally understand its shape.

Master Aldous had spent thirty years gathering proof and perhaps two years knowing he was dying, and he had told Ivo almost none of it. Not out of distrust. Out of the same arithmetic Ivo used themselves: *what can be carried.* A dead master's secrets were heavy. An apprentice who did not know them could be questioned safely; an apprentice who did know them could only be killed or broken. So Aldous had let Ivo ink the plates without ever saying what the plates accused, and had gone upstairs to die whispering to a God who had presumably already read the marginalia.

And Ivo had carried the thing anyway, unknowing, like a courier handed a sealed letter — and now the seal was off, and the letter was addressed, in part, to someone Ivo knew.

Vresova. The word sat in the middle of the third sheet of notes, circled twice in Aldous's hand. Marta's husband, Joren, had come from Vresova. Ivo knew this the way one knows things in small towns: not by being told, but by absorbing it, the way plaster absorbs damp. Joren the ferryman, dead this autumn, had spoken of Vresova the way men speak of childhoods — casually, in fragments, a name dropped among others. Marta had never spoken of it at all.

Ivo looked at the case under the floorboard, and at the notes, and at the window where the frost had finished melting and left the glass merely dirty and true.

Then they put on their coat, which was not theirs, and went down to the river.

* * *

Marta's boathouse smelled of tar and cold water and, faintly, of the onions she fried in self-defense against the loneliness. She was mending a net when Ivo arrived, her hands moving with the blind competence of someone who could do the work in her sleep and often did, and she did not look up.

"You walk like a man coming to confess," she said. "But you're not a man, so I don't know what to call it."

"A woman coming to confess," Ivo said. "If the shoe fits."

"The shoes don't fit either. Those are Joren's." But she tipped her chin at the stool across from her, which was as close to welcome as Marta issued in writing, and Ivo sat.

For a while there was only the sound of the net cord running through her fingers and the river doing its patient winter work underneath the floorboards. Ivo had learned in the past weeks that Marta's silences were not empty; they were rooms she kept for people until they found something worth putting in them. Ivo opened their satchel and took out the third sheet of notes, and put it in the room.

"What's this?"

"Master Aldous's notes. From the atlas." Ivo's voice did something awkward in the middle of the sentence, and they started over. "I hid the plates before I read them properly. It seemed — safer not to know what I was carrying. That was cowardice dressed up as caution, probably. Both of us are well supplied with that."

"Don't preach at me about caution on my own floor," Marta said, but without heat, and unfolded the page.

Ivo watched her read. It took less time than Ivo expected, because Marta read the way she did everything — quickly, without ceremony, missing nothing — and then it took a very long time, because she went back to the beginning and read it again, and this time her hands had stopped moving on the net, and the net lay across her lap like something asleep.

"Vresova," she said at last. Just the name. The river said it back to her under the boards.

"Yes."

"He knew." Her voice was flat and terrible and very quiet. "Your old master knew what they'd done. Knew before Joren died. Before—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Joren spent nine years petitioning. Nine years. Letters to the chancery, to the bishop, to anyone whose title sounded like it might have ears. And they wrote back — oh, they wrote beautifully — *the matter has been reviewed and the records found correct.* Correct. He died believing he'd failed to prove what everyone told him wasn't there. And this grumpy old man in his tower sat on the proof in green ink."

Ivo flinched. "He wasn't sitting on it. He was dying, and he was afraid, and he—"

"I know what he was," Marta snapped. Then, quieter, with the exhaustion of a woman arguing with the truth: "I know. Joren met him once. Years ago, at the customs house. Came home saying the imperial cartographer had asked him strange questions about ferry routes near Vresova, and had listened — actually listened — like a man taking dictation from his own conscience." She pressed the heel of her hand against one eye. "I thought Joren exaggerated. Men exaggerate the people who listen to them."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a dog was conducting its usual negotiations with the ice.

"I didn't come to defend him," Ivo said. "I came because I read those notes last night and I couldn't breathe, and then I thought about you, and I couldn't breathe differently. The atlas proves it. All of it. Dolen, Vresova, Hrast — the borders, the rolls, the dates. Enough for the guild in Vienna to take apart everything the chancery has signed for twenty years. That's why the inspectors are coming. That's why the bridge closes. They aren't hunting a book, Marta. They're hunting an apology the empire refuses to make, and your village is half of what it apologizes for."

Marta was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, the fury had settled into something older and more useful, like sediment settling into riverbed.

"My mother is buried in Vresova," she said. "Or rather — she is buried in a churchyard that the records say belongs to a parish eleven miles east, in a village that doesn't exist, over a grave that doesn't exist, and every year I have paid a burial tax for the privilege of visiting nowhere." She laughed, short and hard, without a scrap of humor in it. "Joren used to say we should just move the stones ourselves, one mile east every year, and let the clerks chase the cemetery across the map. I always thought it was a joke. Your master thought it was evidence."

"He thought everything was evidence. It made him very tiring at dinner."

That startled a real laugh out of her — brief, rusty, surprised at itself — and something in the boathouse shifted, some beam easing in the cold. Marta looked at Ivo properly then, for the first time since they'd sat down, with the assessing gaze she usually spent on weather.

"You're going to try to move it downriver," she said. Not a question. "The atlas. Past the checkpoints, past whatever inspector they've sent. Alone, presumably, in that coat that fits you like a sail, because you've had three weeks to ask anyone in this town for help and you haven't managed to ask."

"It seemed unkind," Ivo said, "to make other people carry my master's ghost."

"It seemed unkind," Marta repeated, with wonder. "You sit in my boathouse eating my onions and telling me my mother's grave is in your satchel, and you're worried about *imposing*." She shook her head slowly, and then, briskly, in the voice she used for launching boats, she said: "Right. Here is what happens now. You will stop carrying this alone, because alone you will fail, and when you fail they will find everything, and they will burn the notes and the plates and possibly you. The river goes down to the Miller's Bend, and the miller owes me, and the ice won't hold a skiff for another fortnight at least, which gives us time if we're clever and none at all if we're proud. Do you understand? We. I've decided there's a we. You weren't consulted."

Ivo found, somewhat to their surprise, that their eyes were stinging, and blamed the onion smoke, which was not present.

"No," they managed. "I wasn't consulted. Thank you."

"Don't thank me." Marta folded Aldous's note with great care, as though it were a letter from the living, and tucked it into her bodice. "Thank the river. It keeps what people throw away, and it carries what matters, and it has been waiting a long time for its turn at the chancery." She stood, and reached for her coat, and paused. "First thing tomorrow, you'll tell me exactly where the plates are hidden, how many there are, and what your old master would have done next. All of it, Ivo Fairweather. No more sealed letters."

"There is one other thing," Ivo said, and hesitated, because saying it aloud would change the shape of the danger. "The inspector they're sending. Master Aldous got a letter from him, months ago. Friendly. Almost fond. He signed it *your devoted former student, Voss.*"

Marta went very still.

"Well," she said. "Isn't that convenient. A man who loved him." Her eyes met Ivo's, and in them was the cold bright calculation of a woman reading winter water. "Because the most dangerous kind of hunter, apprentice, is the one who hopes you'll run — and the most hopeful kind of all is the one who taught your master everything he knew about hiding."
