## 5. The Inspector's Patience

Inspector Voss arrived on a Tuesday, which pleased him, because Tuesdays were unremarkable days and he preferred to begin his work while everyone was still bored.

He came by coach with two clerks and a lacquered box of writs, and he stepped down onto the frozen mud of the river town in good boots that immediately regretted it. He was fifty-one years old, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that innkeepers trusted on sight — which is to say, a face that had practiced being trusted for a very long time. He paid for his rooms in advance. He complimented the stew. He asked after the health of the landlord's mother by name, having read her name in the parish register before he'd crossed the threshold, and the landlord glowed like a stove.

*There*, Voss thought, hanging his coat to dry. *One door opened. Thirty-nine to go.*

He had been sent because an atlas had gone missing from the imperial archives, or rather, because plates from an atlas had gone missing, or rather — if one believed the memorandum, which Voss did not, memoranda being written by men who had never held the thing described — because *nothing* had gone missing at all, merely been misfiled, and would Inspector Voss please confirm this while conducting a routine review of papers in the district during his already-scheduled circuit.

Voss had spent eleven years in Vienna's archive division. He knew what misfiled meant. Misfiled meant someone had carried it out under their coat, and the someone was almost always the person who loved it most.

And he knew, better than anyone living, whose coat that would have been.

* * *

He began, as he began everything, with tea.

The town had perhaps four hundred souls, one bridge, one church, one ferry landing going soft with disuse since the frost closed the crossing, and one mapmaker's workshop with dark windows. He did not go to the workshop first. That was the trick of it — everyone expected the inspector to go straight for the interesting building, and expectation made people arrange their faces. Voss wanted faces in their natural state.

So he interviewed the priest about baptismal records. He interviewed the midwife, who told him everything about everyone within twenty minutes and nothing useful within forty. He interviewed the miller, whose handshake was too warm and whose eyes went to his son when Voss asked how many strangers had passed through since autumn — a small tell, a flicker like a fish under ice, and Voss noted it without hooking it. A fish flickering under ice could be left there. Fish kept.

He drank a great deal of tea. He praised the town's bread to three separate households and received three different recipes, all claimed to be identical. By Thursday he knew which families feuded over a fence line, which widow watered down her wine, and which of the bridge guards owed money to which of the ferrymen — and he had not yet asked a single question about maps.

On Friday he asked about the ferryman who had died in November.

"Good man," said the innkeeper, refilling Voss's cup without being asked. "Quiet. Came from upriver originally. Never talked much about it."

"Where upriver?"

The innkeeper shrugged with the whole upper half of his body, a gesture that meant *I know and I will not say and we both understand each other.* "People come here to be from somewhere else," he said. "River town rule. You don't ask where somebody washed up from."

"How philosophical," Voss said warmly. "And his widow?"

"Marta. Keeps to herself. Mends nets now." A pause, the particular pause of a man deciding whether the inspector counted as town or crown. "She'll talk to you the way the ice talks. Slow, and then all at once, and then you're through."

"I look forward to it," said Voss, who did not, particularly. Widows who kept to themselves were either innocent or dangerous, and the innocent ones wasted his mornings.

He walked down to the landing that afternoon. The river wore its winter skin, grey-white, sullen, moving somewhere underneath. Marta Yarrow knelt among nets spread over the frozen shingle like the skeletons of enormous birds, her fingers moving through knots without looking at them.

"Frau Yarrow," he said. "Inspector Voss. I'm reviewing papers in the district."

"I heard." She didn't rise. She tied off a knot, inspected it, and only then looked up. Her eyes were the exact color of the river in February, which is to say they gave nothing back. "You'll want to see my husband's ferry license. It's expired, but he's dead, so I imagine the empire has bigger worries."

"It's in order?"

"It's in a box. The box is in my house. My house is where it always is." She went back to the net. "You can come tomorrow morning if you want the box. I don't do business after dark in winter. Fingers get stupid."

"May I ask how your husband died?"

"You may ask." Another knot. "The river took his joints all the years he worked it, and then his heart followed the same road. There. Now you've asked and I've answered and neither of us enjoyed it."

Voss smiled — genuinely, which was rare enough that he noticed it himself — and wished her a good evening. Walking back up the shingle, he reflected that she had answered every question truthfully and told him absolutely nothing, and that this was not a skill one learned from mending nets.

*Someone washed up here*, he thought, *and someone else knows it.*

* * *

The interviews proceeded in alphabetical order, more or less, because alphabetical order offended no one and let him watch the whole town flinch in sequence as his list crept toward their names. He sat in the church hall with a small table and two chairs and a clerk who took notes, and he asked each citizen the same gentle questions: their trade, their family, any correspondence received from outside the district, any documents in their keeping bearing imperial seals.

To the last question, nearly everyone said no. Nearly everyone said it too quickly. Voss nodded at each of them as though *no* were the most satisfying answer ever given him, and privately tallied the lies, which stacked up like cordwood: the smith had a grandfather's land grant with a seal on it; the schoolmistress had letters from a cousin in Buda; the priest, bless him, had a whole cabinet of dispensations and was sweating like a cheese in summer.

Everyone owned paper with seals. Everyone feared paper with seals. This was the empire's entire genius, Voss thought sometimes, and its entire weakness: it ruled by documents, and therefore could be wounded by them, and every soul in every town carried a small secret archive under the floorboards.

He reached the letter *F* on the ninth day.

"Fairweather," said the clerk, and the hall went quiet in a way Voss felt along his skin before he understood it — the particular hush of people watching something they'd decided to care about.

The apprentice came in and sat down.

Voss had seen them before, of course; he saw everyone before he met them. A slight figure in a coat two sizes wrong, ink-stained fingers, hair cut by someone in a hurry. Young — hard to say how young, nineteen or twenty-five, the agelessness of people who had stopped expecting to be looked at. They sat the way people sit who have practiced being unremarkable: hands folded, spine apologetic, eyes politely empty.

"Good morning," Voss said. "You are Ivo Fairweather."

"Yes, sir."

"Apprentice to Master Aldous Reiner, cartographer, deceased this past autumn."

"Yes, sir."

"And you remain at the workshop."

"I keep it," Ivo said. "Someone has to sweep."

Voss smiled at that and let the silence do some work. Silence was his best tool; he'd bought it cheap and it never wore out. The apprentice bore it well — too well? No. Well, in the way of someone for whom silences had always been the natural weather.

"Tell me about the master's work," Voss said. "His late work. The style of it."

A pause. Small. Half a breath longer than innocence required.

"He drew coastlines," Ivo said. "Rivers. Hills with little hatch marks. He liked rivers best. He said borders were gossip and rivers were fact."

"Did he." Voss leaned back. "That sounds like him."

Something moved behind the apprentice's eyes — the flicker of a person hearing a stranger quote their dead. "You knew him, sir?"

"We were acquainted. In Vienna. Years ago." Voss turned his cup slowly on the table, watching the apprentice watch it, which was instructive: Ivo tracked the cup the way a draftsman tracks a line, measuring, not coveting. "He had a hand you could recognize anywhere. His hills leaned east. His letters had tails on them like tadpoles. If he drew you a map of heaven, you'd know it was his before you found your own house on it."

"That's true," Ivo said quietly. "He used to joke that his maps should sign themselves."

"And now he's gone, and his workshop remains, and someone sweeps it." Voss folded his hands. "When a master dies, the apprentice inherits the tools. Has anything of his come into your keeping, Fairweather? Papers, plates, instruments?"

"No, sir."

"No?"

"The guild took his effects for debt. What was left was furniture mostly. And the broom." The ghost of humor, quickly smoothed. "I bought the broom myself, sir. It wasn't inherited."

Voss laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and heard the town's collective exhale behind it. Charming them was easy; charming this one was work, and the work was the pleasure. Because here was the thing, the thing that had kept him awake half the night in his good bed at the inn:

He had seen Aldous's late drawings. Not the missing atlas — the exercises, the marginalia, the practice plates the old man had shipped to Vienna under cover of correspondence in the years before the theft, back when Voss still wrote him letters and told himself the friendship was ordinary. He had seen the hand. And just now, when he'd mentioned tadpole-tailed letters, the apprentice had not looked confused. The apprentice had looked *recognized* — the small involuntary brightness of someone hearing their own secret described by a stranger.

Which meant one of two things. Either Ivo Fairweather had studied the master's hand so devotedly that it lived in their fingers — or the hand in the missing atlas was not entirely the master's.

*The plates were finished by someone*, Voss thought. Archives did not smuggle themselves out under coats. Dying men did not hide atlases without help. Somewhere in this town was a second draftsman who drew hills that leaned east, and Voss was sitting across a table from them, drinking their tea.

"Thank you, Fairweather," he said pleasantly. "You've been very helpful."

"Sir." The apprentice rose, bowed precisely, and left — unhurried, which was either excellent discipline or excellent instinct, and Voss honestly could not tell which, and the not-telling delighted him the way a difficult chart delights a cartographer.

* * *

That evening he wrote his interim report by candlelight and stopped, pen hovering, at the line *no documents of relevance located.*

It was true. It was also, he suspected, false, and the difference between those two things was currently sweeping a workshop across the square, or burying itself deeper, because his interview today had been a bell rung over deep water, and fish that hear bells stop rising.

He thought about Vienna. About a younger Aldous Reiner laughing at some archive dinner, saying, *You enforce the map, Elias; I make it; between us we'll never agree on where we live.* About the last letter Aldous had written him, four years ago, which Voss still kept in a drawer he did not open in daylight: *They are redrawing the frontier, my friend, and erasing places that exist, and I find I cannot be the man who holds the pen and says nothing.* Voss had reported nothing. Had filed the letter as personal correspondence, category: sentiment. It was possibly the only dishonest filing of his career, and he had told himself it proved his loyalty lay with the institution and not the man.

Now the institution had sent him here to retrieve whatever the man had done about it.

He dipped the pen and wrote instead: *Inhabitants cooperative. Bridge closed per seasonal writ. Review continuing.* Then he sat back and admitted the thing he had been circling for nine days, the thing with teeth:

He did not especially want to find the atlas.

No — more precise than that. He wanted to find it *first*. Wanted to hold it before the clerks did, wanted to read the erased villages in Aldous's leaning eastern hills, wanted to know whether his friend had been right before the empire decided whether he had been. And if the atlas should pass through some cold checkpoint and vanish into a river or a fire — well. Paper was fragile. Rivers were hungry. Accidents happened, particularly to inspectors who looked away at crucial moments.

But that was sentiment, and sentiment was a leak, and he was too old to pretend otherwise.

Across the square, the workshop window went dark. Voss watched it a while, the way one watches a line on a chart that runs off the edge of the known page.

Then he put on his coat, crossed the square, and knocked — gently, twice — on the workshop door.
