## 6. Nets and Names

The town decided Ivo Fairweather belonged to it the way a river decides to freeze: gradually, then all at once, and without consulting anyone.

Marta watched it happen from her doorway, hands in a basket of netting, and could not have said when it began. Perhaps with old Ferenc, who had stopped calling Ivo "the apprentice" and started calling them "the young one from Aldous's," which was not much of an improvement but was at least warmer in tone. Perhaps with the baker's wife, who left the heel of a dark loaf on the workshop step every second morning as if she had simply miscounted. Perhaps — and Marta suspected this was the true beginning, though no one would admit it — with the coat.

The coat had belonged to János Brenner, who had died of his lungs two winters past and left it hanging on a hook in the tavern like an unclaimed opinion. It was grey wool, patched at both elbows, too big for Ivo by exactly the amount that made it look borrowed rather than given. One evening Ivo came into the tavern with frost in their hair and stood shivering near the stove, and Brenner's widow had looked at the hook, and then at Ivo, and said nothing at all — which in this town was practically a speech. The coat had been around Ivo's shoulders ever since, and no one had remarked upon it, because remarking would have made it a thing that happened, and things that happened required reasons, and reasons were scarce that winter.

Marta herself had done her part, though she'd have denied it under oath. She had started paying Ivo in soup instead of coin, because coin invited questions about what exactly mending nets earned, and soup invited none. She had also started expecting them at her table on Sundays, and had stopped pretending this was for the nets' benefit.

"You missed a knot," she said now, not looking up.

"I didn't."

"Then you missed it twice, because I saw it yesterday and it's still there."

Ivo leaned over the net spread across her floor and found it — a slipknot where there should have been a proper hitch, the kind of error that would hold until precisely the wrong moment. They fixed it in silence. Their hands had gotten good, Marta noted. Six weeks ago they'd handled netting like it was something that might apologize back. Now their fingers moved with the small confidence of someone who had been told, at least once, that they'd done a thing well.

"The river's going to lock up soon," she said.

"How can you tell?"

"Listen."

Ivo listened. To Marta the river in late December made three sounds, and only one of them was water. There was the ordinary murmur, which meant nothing. There was a low knocking, like a fist on a door far away, which meant ice was forming somewhere upstream in the shallows and testing its strength. And there was a third sound, rarer, a long groan that came at night — the sound of the river settling its accounts. She had heard the knocking for two days now.

"When it freezes properly, the bridge closes," Ivo said.

"The bridge closed weeks ago. Officially." Marta pulled a length of cord tight. "Unofficially, the ferry still runs while there's open channel. When the channel goes, we're an island. Whatever comes next comes across the ice or not at all."

Ivo's hands stilled on the net. They had gotten better at hiding it, but Marta had learned their tells the way she'd once learned her husband's: the pause before answering when the answer would be a lie, the way they counted things — steps, coins, words — as if measurement were a kind of prayer. She did not ask what they carried or why the inspectors' arrival had made the whole town walk softly. She had her own arithmetic. She knew Aldous had hidden something, because Aldous had been the sort of man who hid things the way other men coughed — constantly, and with growing difficulty.

But there was a thing she had not told anyone, and lately it had begun to feel like a stone carried in one boot, always noticed, never addressed.

* * *

She waited until Sunday, when the table was full and the stew had done its work loosening everyone. This was how her mother had taught her to say difficult things: sideways, over food, so they arrived dressed as conversation.

There were five of them that night. Ivo. The miller's boy Tomas, who came for the food and stayed for the arguing. Widow Patak, who was mostly deaf and therefore the safest confidante in town. And old Ferenc, who brought a bottle of plum brandy he claimed had been waiting for an occasion, and who considered any Sunday sufficient occasion.

"So," Marta said, ladling out the last of the stew, "the young one from Aldous's has been asking after my husband."

Ivo went very still. It was true; they had asked, once, carefully, weeks ago — *what was your husband's family name?* — and she had answered with the name and nothing more, and they had not pressed. But she had seen where their eyes went when they said it. Everyone in her village knew that particular stillness. It was what people did when a name brushed against a wound.

"I was," Ivo admitted. "I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize for asking after Dániel. Apologize if you stop." She set down the ladle. "His family came from Zselickislak. That's the village. Or it was."

"It was?" Tomas asked, mouth full.

"Is," Marta corrected herself, and then, because lying to the half-deaf seemed pointless, "or will be again, if certain papers say what certain people hope they say. That's all I'll give you for free, Tomas, so eat."

Widow Patak leaned toward Ferenc. "What did she say?"

"She said the stew needs salt," Ferenc said comfortably.

Marta turned to Ivo, whose face had gone through several weathers in quick succession. "You understand now," she said quietly. "Why I asked you here. Why I watch the bridge with you. Dániel's people weren't moved. They weren't paid. They were drawn off the map. A clerk in Vienna put a line through three villages and a river shifted on paper, and forty families became a rumor, and the rumor thinned, and now the only place my husband's village exists is in the heads of old women and—" she nodded at Ivo— "in whatever your master spent his last years bleeding onto copper."

Ivo set down their spoon with the care of someone handling evidence.

"I've read the marginalia," they said. "All of it. I should have read it sooner. I was afraid of what it would make me responsible for."

"And?"

"And Master Aldous wrote, beside the survey of Zselickislak: *They did not burn it. Burning leaves ash. They simply declined to remember it.*" Ivo's voice caught slightly. "He underlined it twice. He underlined very little."

The table was quiet. Even Tomas stopped chewing.

"My mother-in-law used to say the village bell rang clearer than the town's," Marta said, because grief needed somewhere to go and hers had always gone into stories. "Dániel believed they'd fix it someday. Believed it the way some men believe in weather. Then the fever took him, and I thought — well. I thought the map had won. Paper beats memory, if the paper lives long enough and the memory doesn't."

She looked at Ivo directly then.

"You're carrying something. I've known since the first week, when you watched the bridge like a man watches a debt collector. I won't ask what. But I'll tell you what I know, and what I know is rivers and winters and which millers can be trusted and which soldiers take bribes and which take offense. That's worth more than a strongbox. Strongboxes get found."

Ivo looked at her for a long moment — and Marta saw something happen in their face that she recognized from her own mirror: the moment a person stops being alone on purpose.

"Sunday after next," Ivo said finally, "the channel will be thick enough to walk, but thin enough that no inspector will expect anyone to try. There's a stretch below the willows where the current keeps it honest. If someone wanted to move something downriver before the deep cold, that's when."

"Then Sunday after next," Marta said, "someone had best teach you the winter moods of the river. Because whoever moves it will need to know the difference between ice that holds and ice that merely promises."

* * *

They went down to the bank the next morning, before light, when the frost stood in the reeds like a congregation.

"Three kinds of winter ice on this river," Marta said, breath smoking. "Black ice, milk ice, and liar's ice. Black ice you can trust with a horse. Milk ice holds a man if he spreads his weight and prays. Liar's ice looks like milk ice but hums when the wind crosses it — you hear that singing, you go back, no matter what you're carrying."

"And if what I'm carrying can't go back?"

"Then you wait for it to stop singing. Ice is patient. So are dead men's maps." She crouched and tapped the frozen mud with her boot heel. "Learn this bank. In the dark, every willow looks like every other willow, except that one—" she pointed— "which leans like my aunt Ilonka after mass. You steer by crooked things. Straight things lie; they all look alike."

Ivo laughed — actually laughed, a short startled sound, as if surprised to find it in stock. Marta found she liked the sound more than was convenient.

"You're smiling," Ivo accused.

"I'm teaching. Teaching is serious work."

"You're smiling *because* of the aunt."

"I'm smiling because six weeks ago you couldn't tell a net-hitch from a hole, and now you're going to help me commit crimes against the crown across a frozen river, and somehow that feels like the most sensible arrangement I've made since Dániel died." She straightened. "That's enough river for today. Come up to the house. There's bread."

At the door Ivo hesitated, and she saw them do their counting — the measuring of portable things, the inventory they performed whenever kindness approached.

"Marta," they said. "Why me? You owe me nothing. I came here with no family, no name worth recording—"

"There it is," Marta said. "No name worth recording." She opened the door and the warmth rolled out, and the smell of bread, and the sound of the kettle she'd left on the hob. "You know what this town called me for the first year after Dániel died? The ferryman's widow. As if widowhood were my trade, like smithing. As if I'd been born to it." She stepped inside and held the door. "Nobody gets to decide what they're worth by what they came with, child. You're worth what you mend, and what you carry, and whose table you sit at. Now sit down before the bread goes cold, because I didn't bake it to watch you stand in my doorway taking inventory of your own unworthiness."

Ivo sat. And it seemed to Marta, watching them break the bread with hands ink-stained and net-scabbed and entirely their own, that the apprentice had gone out of them somewhere along the way — that the town had quietly renamed them, the way it renamed everything it kept, and Ivo had not yet noticed, and would fight it when they did, and lose, which was the best kind of belonging: the kind you couldn't refuse.

Outside, far off, the river knocked twice against the shore and fell silent.

Marta paused with her cup halfway to her mouth. Two nights ago the knocking had come at long intervals, distant, tentative. This was closer. This was sooner.

The river was locking early this year. Earlier than anyone expected.

Whatever Ivo meant to move downriver, they would have less time for it than Sunday after next — and Marta, who had spent a year learning to live slowly, felt the old urgency rise in her chest like water finding its level.

"Eat quickly," she said.
