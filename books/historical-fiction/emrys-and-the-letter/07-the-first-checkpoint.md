## 7. The First Checkpoint

The ice spoke first.

I had been listening to it for three nights from my window above the workshop — a low groaning, like a giant turning over in bed, then a high thin singing as the river's skin stretched and cracked and stretched again. Master Aldous would have called it the river arguing with itself. Marta called it the ice getting its nerve up.

"It'll hold a cart by Friday," she said, stamping her boots on the ferry landing. "Which means we go tonight, while it still holds nothing but us and a skiff and whatever God thinks He's doing."

We loaded in the dark behind her woodshed: two poles, a lantern with the wick trimmed to a coward's flame, and the surveyor's case with its false bottom, in which forty copper plates lay wrapped in oilcloth like a litter of sleeping animals. The decoy case — the one with three years of honest surveying notes and a very dull compass — rode under my arm where any inspector could see it. My arms shook so badly setting the plates in that Marta put one hand flat on the case and said nothing until I stopped.

"They're only paper," I said.

"They're heavier than paper," she said, which was true, and kinder than comfort.

I had measured my whole life in what could be carried. A master's tools: four pounds. A box of inks: one. Every book I owned fit inside another book. But the atlas — forty plates of proof that the crown had redrawn the border to make three villages vanish off the face of the record as neatly as wiping a slate — could not be carried anywhere safe. It could only be carried *forward*, which is a different direction entirely, and one I had never traveled.

Marta took the bow pole. I took the stern. We pushed off into a river the color of iron shavings, and the town fell away behind us — the bridge with its new chain across it, the customs house where a lamp burned late because Inspector Voss kept late hours now, the church spire standing up against the stars like a pin holding the world to the sky.

"You row like a man stirring soup," Marta observed.

"I was apprenticed to a mapmaker, not a waterman."

"Then think of the river as paper and the pole as your pen." She demonstrated: a smooth stroke, no splash, the blade entering and leaving the water as politely as a guest. "In and out quiet. Nobody likes a sloppy hand."

So I drew long, careful lines down the dark page of the river, and after a while my arms found the rhythm of it, and the cold became a thing I carried instead of a thing that carried me. Above the eastern bank the hills were white and moonless. Somewhere back there, in country I had never seen, lay the ghost-villages. Dolena. Kriva Potok. And the third, the one whose name I did not yet know out loud but whose name I had seen written in Aldous's cramped marginalia, over and over, like a prayer or an accusation: *Vesseny*. Marta's husband's village. I knew now why she had watched me so carefully all those weeks at the nets. She hadn't been looking for an apprentice. She'd been looking for her dead.

* * *

The first checkpoint was not a checkpoint at all, properly speaking — just a bend below the old lime kilns where the river narrowed and the crown had planted a hut, a brazier, and two bored soldiers whose breath rose in the lamplight like the souls of small regrets.

"We pass it how?" I whispered. We had shipped the poles and were letting the current carry us, which felt like trusting a stranger.

"With paperwork," Marta said, and produced from her coat the bill of lading for a shipment of millstones bound downstream to Brenner's Mill — a shipment that existed, technically, in the same way that a promise exists. "You are my man. You say little. You have a bad cough. Nod when I touch my elbow."

"I don't have a cough."

"You do now."

The soldiers came down to the water's edge with their lantern. The younger one looked about fourteen and was losing an argument with his own boots. The older one held out a mittened hand for the papers and read them with the slow dignity of a man who could not read well and wished to enjoy it anyway.

"Millstones," he said. "Heavy cargo for a skiff this size."

"Heavy is the point," Marta said pleasantly. "Light cargo floats fast, and fast cargo gets searched. Mine crawls. Nobody robs a snail."

This philosophy appeared to move him. He handed back the bill of lading and then leaned toward the skiff, squinting at me. "And him?"

"My sister's boy," Marta said, without a flicker. "Sick lungs. Going to the mill air. It's drier."

"Mill air," the soldier repeated, as if tasting it. Then, because fourteen-year-olds are honest by accident, the young one piped up: "Uncle says the inspector's got everyone's papers pulled apart back in town. Like washing day."

"And does your uncle wash mine too?" Marta asked.

"He's looking for something specific. Papers that shouldn't exist." The boy shrugged. "He said maps lie worst when they're true. Whatever that means."

Marta touched her elbow. I nodded. It was, I believe, the most convincing nod of my life.

We slid past the brazier's orange circle, and the soldiers shrank behind us into two smudges of light, and the river took us the way rivers take everything — without opinion. For perhaps half a mile neither of us spoke. Then Marta laughed, a low sound like water under ice.

"A bad cough," she said. "You've the acting talent of a fencepost."

"I nodded."

"You nodded like a man being hanged who'd changed his mind at the last minute." She shipped her pole. "But it served. First one's past, mapmaker."

Triumph is a strange animal. It came up in me all at once, warm and ridiculous — we had done it, actually done it, the plates were *past*, Voss could search every drawer in the town and find nothing but honest ink — and I laughed out loud into the freezing dark, and Marta laughed too, and for one whole minute we were two fools in a boat grinning at the river like it had told us a joke.

Then the far bank showed a light. One lamp, swinging, at the mill landing. And beneath the triumph, quietly, the terror arrived to take its seat.

* * *

Brenner the miller met us at the sluice gate with a lantern in one fist and a finger across his lips. He was a wide, floury man with kind eyes and the handshake of someone used to catching things that didn't want to be caught.

"In," he mouthed, and walked us through the mill's guts — the great stones silent for winter, the floor soft with dust, the wheel dripping icicles in the dark outside — up to a room above the meal bins where a stove ticked and two chairs waited like old friends.

We gave him the case. He weighed it in his hands, all forty plates, and his face did something complicated — grief and fury sharing a chair.

"So it's real," he said. "Aldous always said the crown doesn't erase people with fire. Fire makes martyrs. Erase the *map* and in fifty years nobody remembers there was ever anything to mourn." He set the case down gently, the way you set down a sleeping child. "It goes to Vienna Thursday, with the flour barges. Kessler's man meets it at the guild house. After that it isn't ours to save anymore."

"And us?" I asked.

"You'll sleep here tonight and walk back tomorrow as ordinary as bread." He smiled. "The inspector's men don't come to mills. Too much noise, not enough wine."

We ate bread and hard sausage and drank tea that tasted of the stove, and Brenner talked about his son Jozsi, who ran the ledgers, a clever lad, he said, clever as a cartload of monkeys, and would I care to see the mill accounts sometime, since I had such a fine hand? I said I would, because I was tired and warm and stupid with relief, and because I had spent my whole life believing that being useful was the same as being safe.

Marta slept by the stove. I lay on a pallet of sacks and listened to the wheel drip and thought: *first one passed.* Two more crossings. The plates were past the soldiers, past the chain, past the searching. In three days they would be Kessler's problem, then the guild's, then the empire's, and Ivo Fairweather, apprentice of no family, could go back to being invisible.

I fell asleep believing it, which is the last kind thing I can say about that night.

* * *

I woke to Marta shaking my shoulder in the grey before dawn, and her face told me everything before her mouth could catch up.

"Horses," she said. "Two hours ago. On the road."

We came down the stair into the smell of cold flour, and Brenner stood at the window with his back rigid, and outside, riding up the mill road with the sun just clearing the hills, came four riders in imperial grey, moving at the unhurried pace of men who already knew what they would find.

"The strongbox," Brenner said quietly, without turning. "A rider came through yesterday evening. Word from the town." He swallowed. "They opened Aldous's strongbox, son. Under the workshop floor."

The floor seemed to tilt. That box had been empty for six days — I had emptied it myself, sweating, cursing, prying up boards while the frost climbed the windows. Empty. Nothing in it but the smell of paper and a pressed violet Aldous had kept for thirty years, which I had not been able to make myself throw away.

An empty strongbox proves there was something once.

"He knows," I said. My voice sounded far away, like it belonged to somebody standing out in the cold. "Not what. Not where. But he knows there was a box, and now he knows it's empty, and empty boxes mean the thing is *moving*."

"Yes," said Marta. She was already wrapping the decoy case in sacking, her hands steady, her eyes very bright. "And he'll ride ahead of us now, not behind. Every crossing between here and Vienna will be waiting for two travelers with a surveyor's case and a story about sick lungs."

Downstairs, boots on the millstone floor. A voice — courteous, unhurried, terribly familiar even through the floorboards — asking Brenner's wife whether the mill took in lodgers in winter, and whether she might be so kind as to fetch her husband, and whether anyone else happened to be staying in the house this morning.

Jozsi's voice answered, light and helpful. Clever as a cartload of monkeys.

Marta looked at me, and I looked at the coffin.

It stood in the corner of the mill room where Brenner stored it — a plain coffin, unfinished, waiting on custom, wide enough and long enough for a surveyor's case wrapped in sacking to lie within it as if it were the dearest thing in the world. Which, I suppose, it was.

"There's no way back," Marta said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't despair either; it was simply the truth, laid on the table like a tool. "Whatever happens next, there's no going back to being invisible."

The stairs creaked under polite boots. I picked up the coffin lid.

"No," I agreed. "There isn't."
