## 8. Betrayal at the Mill

Marta had never trusted a boy who smiled before breakfast.

Young Anton, the miller's son, had been smiling since dawn — smiling at the soldiers, smiling at the frost, smiling at her in a way that made her want to box his ears and ask what his mother had fed him. He was nineteen and had the particular talent of boys that age: he could look innocent while doing arithmetic. And she could see the sum running behind his eyes. A case of papers. An imperial reward. A way out of flour dust and his father's shadow.

She said nothing to Ivo. She simply moved the coffin forward in her mind, from third position to first, and began arranging things accordingly.

* * *

The plan had been simple enough, as plans made by tired people in cold rooms usually were. The decoy case — the broken surveyor's case with its false bottom and its bundle of Aldous's harmless field sketches — would travel openly downriver with the miller's grain barge, loud as a church bell, obvious as sin. Let Voss seize it if he wanted it. Let him spend a week puzzling over contour lines of hills that didn't exist and riverbanks drawn with loving precision and no intelligence whatsoever.

The real plates would go another way entirely. Downriver there was a village called Szentkereszt — one of the three, though not Marta's own — where an old guild courier named Bence waited for whatever came to him. And what came to him, this week, would come in a coffin. Old Pál the gravedigger owed her, or felt he did, or simply could not refuse a widow who looked at him the way Marta looked at people when she wanted something. Inside the coffin, beneath a decent layer of straw and above a very real corpse — old Widow Kerekes, God rest her, who had died loudly and inconveniently on exactly the right night — lay the atlas, wrapped in oilcloth, flat as regret.

"It is," Marta had told Ivo, "the most honest lie we have ever told. She really is dead. He really is going to the churchyard."

Ivo had not laughed. Ivo had gone very pale and very still, the way they did, and then had said, quietly, "She carried things too, then."

That was the trouble with Ivo. Everything became ceremony. Everything became weight. Marta carried things the way you carry firewood — because it was cold and someone had to. Ivo carried things the way you carry relics.

Well. Someone had to do that too, or nothing meant anything.

* * *

The betrayal, when it came, was almost gentle in its stupidity.

Anton told the soldiers about the surveyor's case. Of course he did. That was the whole point of the decoy — it was designed to be found by exactly such a boy, so that finding it would feel like his own cleverness rather than her design. What Marta had not accounted for was the way Anton's face fell when the soldiers opened it and found only drawings.

She watched from the mill doorway, arms crossed against the cold, as Corporal something-or-other lifted out a sketch of a hillside and turned it upside down, twice, as though the hill might reveal ambitions if properly oriented.

"There's nothing here," the corporal said.

"There has to be," Anton said, and his voice cracked on it, and Marta felt — briefly, unwillingly — sorry for him. He had sold a secret and been handed landscapes. He had betrayed a town for pictures of grass.

"Boy," the corporal said, not unkindly, "if this is what treason looks like, the empire is safer than anyone thinks."

But behind the soldiers stood a carriage, and out of the carriage came Inspector Voss, and the temperature of the morning dropped in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

He was dressed plainly, which she had learned was its own kind of arrogance — powerful men dressed plainly the way rivers ran quietly over deep places. He crossed the mill yard, took the sketch from the corporal's hands, and studied it for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was a genuine smile, which was the worst thing about it.

"Aldous's hand," he said softly. "Hatching like this. He never could leave a hill alone." He looked up, and his gaze went past the soldiers, past Anton, past the miller wringing his cap, and settled — she had known it would — on the doorway where Marta stood.

"Good morning, Widow Yarrow," he called. "I wonder — is there a funeral today?"

* * *

There was. There is always a funeral, in winter, in a river town; death keeps its own schedule and waits for no inspector. Widow Kerekes's coffin came out of the chapel at noon with six bearers under it and the whole town trailing after, because everyone who had ever borrowed her marmalade recipe or argued with her about fence lines turned out for Katalin Kerekes, and that was nearly everyone.

Voss stood at the chapel gate. He let the coffin pass. He even bowed his head, briefly, and Marta thought: he knew Aldous, and Aldous is buried two months now, and perhaps grief makes courteous men of inspectors for whole seconds at a time.

Then he stepped into the road and put one gloved hand, very lightly, on the coffin lid.

"I'll walk with her a way," he said. "I find funerals clarifying."

So the procession went down to the churchyard with an inspector among its mourners like a stone in a loaf of bread, and Marta walked behind it watching his hand rest on that lid and thinking, with a calm that frightened her: *he knows. He doesn't know what he knows, but he knows.* You couldn't prove anything by opening a coffin in front of forty witnesses without cause, and he had no cause, only instinct, and instinct was not admissible. But he could stand there. He could make them all carry their fear past him in slow steps. It was a cruelty of remarkable economy.

At the graveside, the priest spoke. The bearers lowered her. The first spade of earth hit the lid — hit the straw, hit the oilcloth, hit the true borders of three villages — and Marta discovered she had stopped breathing somewhere around the second psalm.

And then Voss turned away from the grave, and found Ivo standing at its edge, and did not turn away again.

* * *

Marta got close enough to hear it. She would not have described it as eavesdropping. Eavesdropping implied she might have felt ashamed.

"Young Fairweather," Voss said. "Or should I say — apprentice of Master Aldous. Though 'apprentice' seems a thin word. Those plates in the surveyor's case were practice work, weren't they? Studies. The hand that drew them learned on something better."

Ivo said nothing. The snow ticked down through the bare branches.

"I loved him," Voss said, and the words came out with the strange flatness of a thing rehearsed for thirty years and never once spoken aloud. "In Vienna, when we were young men. He used to say I would end up drawing lines for whoever paid me, and I said he would end up starving for whoever didn't, and we were both correct, and I have thought about it every day since." He paused. "He is dead now, and the dead cannot be prosecuted, only slandered. Give me his confession, Ivo. Tell me where the atlas is, and I will write it all down as his obsession, his bitterness, an old man's spite against the crown that passed him over. Absolution for him. Safety for you. No one in this town need ever know your name was in it at all."

And there it was, laid out on the frozen ground between them like merchandise: a clean escape, bought with a dead man's reputation.

Ivo looked at Voss for a long time. Marta watched their face and saw something she had not seen there before — not fear, and not courage either, but a kind of terrible attention, the look of someone measuring a distance they already knew the length of.

"You want me to tell you he was wrong," Ivo said quietly. "That he drew lies."

"I want you to be able to sleep in a warm room again."

"He slept badly anyway." Almost a smile. "He said conscience kept his hands steady."

Voss's jaw tightened. "You are throwing away your life for ink."

"I am carrying it," Ivo said. "There's a difference. He taught me that everything worth keeping can be carried — but he was wrong about half of it. Some things you don't carry anywhere. You just hold still, and let them hold still with you, and wait."

Something moved across the inspector's face — recognition, maybe, or the ghost of a younger man who had once argued about lines with Aldous in a Vienna garret. He looked at the grave. He looked at his own gloved hands.

"The coffin goes in the ground," he said at last, heavily. "Everything in this churchyard stays in this churchyard. That is how churchyards work."

And he walked away across the snow, and did not order the grave opened.

* * *

They filled it in themselves, afterward — Marta and Ivo and old Pál, three shovels working in the blue dusk. When the last of it was done and the marker set straight, Marta put her hand on Ivo's shoulder, and felt them trembling under her palm like a plucked string.

"He offered you the world," she said.

"He offered me a smaller cell with a lock on the inside." Ivo knelt and pressed the snow flat over the mound with both hands, careful, the way they did everything. "Marta — Anton sold us. I saw it in his face at the mill. He didn't know what he was selling, but he'll know next time. Next time he'll look harder."

"Next time," Marta said, "there won't be a coffin."

"No," Ivo agreed. "The ice will be thick enough to walk soon. Three checkpoints between here and Bence, and Voss knows our faces now, and every soldier on the river knows to open anything that looks like it holds paper." They stood, and brushed off their knees, and looked down at the grave that had already given up its secret and moved on. "We're out of clever places to hide it."

Marta looked at the churchyard, at the town lights coming on beyond the wall, at the river lying black and patient under its first skin of ice — and she thought of her husband, who had ferried people across that water for thirty years and never once asked what they carried.

"No," she said slowly. "But we've still got a river. And rivers, my love, don't hide anything. They just refuse to stop moving."

Downstream, far off, the ice groaned — a long, deep sound, like something enormous turning over in its sleep — and by morning the patrols would report the crossing at Ferenc's Bend frozen solid, and passable, and watched.

Which meant the third checkpoint would be walked across, on foot, in full daylight, by whoever was bold enough or desperate enough to try it.

"We leave tomorrow," Marta said.
