## 9. The Last Crossing

The river froze the way old men die — slowly, complaining, and then all at once.

Ivo Fairweather stood at the edge of it in the blue hour before dawn and listened to the ice groan beneath its skin of snow, a sound like a door being opened somewhere far away in a house where no one lived anymore. Three weeks they had come this way, Marta ahead of them with her lantern shuttered to a coin of light, and three times they had passed a checkpoint by some mixture of luck, paperwork, and Marta's genius for looking like exactly what she was: a widow hauling nothing worth inspecting.

But the third checkpoint was different. The third checkpoint was Voss.

"He'll be there," Marta had said last night, packing the plates into the false bottom of a fish crate with hands that did not shake, which Ivo had always envied and now understood was not calm at all but practice. "He stopped pretending courtesy at the mill. Men like that don't go back to pretending. They just get quieter."

"Then we shouldn't go," Ivo had said, and heard how young their own voice sounded.

Marta had looked up at them across the candle, and for once she hadn't corrected them or mocked them or done any of the hundred small things she did instead of being gentle. She'd said only: "Aldous didn't spend thirty years on those plates so you could keep them under your bed like love letters. And my husband didn't drown within sight of his own village so the crown could pretend the village never breathed." She'd tied the knot, blown out the candle. "We go. That's the whole of the plan. We go, and we don't stop going."

So they went.

The checkpoint sat where the river narrowed between two bluffs, a wooden barrier across the towpath, a brazier throwing orange light against the frost, and four soldiers whose breath rose around them like slow ghosts. And apart from them, slightly upwind, hands clasped behind his back as though he were admiring the view rather than waiting for it — Inspector Voss, in a coat too fine for the weather and a hat pulled low anyway.

"There he is," Marta murmured. "Right on schedule. He's been expecting us since the mill."

"How can you know that?"

"Because he's alone. A man who wants an arrest brings soldiers close. A man who wants a conversation sends them to the far side of the fire." She shifted the fish crate on her shoulder. "Walk like the ice is bad. It is. Let him see you're frightened. Frightened people are boring."

"I'm not frightened," Ivo said, which was a lie, and both of them knew it, and something almost like a smile passed over Marta's face before she killed it.

They came down onto the ice road together, boots crunching, and the soldiers at the barrier straightened, and Voss turned.

Up close, in the brazier light, he looked older than he had in the workshop that first terrible day of questions. There were lines around his eyes that hadn't been inked into Ivo's memory, and his breath came out in silver plumes, and he looked — Ivo searched for the word and disliked every candidate — *tired*. Tired the way Aldous had been tired, in the last month, when the coughing came and the maps still needed finishing.

"The fishmonger's apprentice," Voss said pleasantly. "And the ferryman's widow. Out early."

"Fish don't wait," Marta said.

"No. They don't. Nothing waits, that's the tragedy of the trade." His eyes went past Marta to Ivo, and stayed there, and Ivo felt the look the way one feels a hand on the back of the neck. "You've been busy, I think. Since the mill. Since the coffin we were kind enough not to open until it was already in the ground, at which point it contained, as I recall, a very well-dressed deceased gentleman and no surveyor's plates whatsoever."

"The dead deserve dignity," Marta said.

"The dead deserve whatever the living say they do. That's the whole of history, madam; you'd know." He stepped closer, and the soldiers behind him made a small show of studying the river. "Master Fairweather. May I call you that? You signed the guild petition that way, after all. Bold, signing anything. Your master would have told you never to sign anything."

"He told me never to draw what isn't true," Ivo said. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Everything else was negotiable."

Something moved through Voss's face then — quickly, like a fish under ice — and was gone. "Yes," he said quietly. "He would have said that. He said it to me once, in Vienna, in a year you cannot imagine and I will not describe, over a table covered in border commissions, and I laughed at him. I was young and the crown paid for my wine." He looked out at the river. "I have thought about that laugh more often than any laugh I have enjoyed."

The ice groaned beneath them, long and low, and somewhere downstream something cracked like a rifle shot.

"You know what's in the crate," Ivo said.

"I know what was in the atlas. I've known since the second day, when I saw your linework in the margins of your master's ledger. He had a way of drawing rivers — a little hesitation at the bends, as if the river might change its mind. You inherited it. You couldn't help inheriting it. Hands are honest; that's why we hang people by evidence of them." He turned back. "Three villages. Szelk, Drenov, and the ferry town. Erased from the record by a stroke of red ink in an archive your master bled to reach. Do you know what happens if those plates reach Vienna? Not to the empire — the empire will shrug, empires are built to shrug. To *you*. To the widow. To every soul in this town who helped you. There are gallows on the frontier for less, and I have signed the orders for less, and I would prefer — I find I would genuinely prefer — not to sign another."

"Then let us pass," Marta said.

"I cannot let you pass. If I let you pass, I am complicit, and complicity discovered ends with me in the same cell as you." He said it without heat, the way a man explains arithmetic. Then he paused, and the wind moved his coat, and for a moment Inspector Voss looked like nothing so much as a man standing at a grave deciding whether to say the thing carved inside him or the thing carved on the stone.

"But," he said.

The single word dropped between them like a stone through ice.

"Aldous owed me nothing," Voss said. "That is not true. Aldous owed me everything and paid none of it, because I never asked, because asking would have required admitting what he was to me, and I was a career man, and careers do not survive admissions." His jaw worked. "Thirty years ago he stood in a doorway in Vienna holding a portfolio he should have burned, and when the raid came up the stairs he put it in my hands and walked toward the noise, and I stood there like a fool holding another man's death, and he came back down alive and took it from me and never spoke of it again. Never thanked me. Never acknowledged it. As if mercy were simply a thing one does, like breathing, requiring no record." He laughed — a short, awful, genuine sound. "I have hated him for it for thirty years. I have hated him more these last months, chasing his ghost up a frozen river. And now he is dead, and I find the debt is still outstanding, and debts to the dead accrue interest."

The ice cracked again, closer this time. Under their feet Ivo felt it shudder — a long rolling tremor, the river turning over in its sleep.

"Inspector," one of the soldiers called, uncertain. "Sir? Is everything—"

"It is an inspection, corporal. Stand where you are."

Voss looked at Ivo one last time. And then he did the thing Ivo would carry for the rest of their life, longer than any map, longer than any name: he looked away. Deliberately. Turned his whole body toward the upstream dark, hands clasped behind him, and studied the horizon as though the fate of the empire were written there in frost.

"The ice is breaking up early this year," he said, to no one, to the river, to the night. "It would be irresponsible of me to have anyone attempt a crossing in these conditions. Anyone at all. If persons unknown were to slip past my checkpoint while my attention was elsewhere, I would have no choice but to report that they vanished — drowned, most likely, poor souls, the river keeps what it keeps." A pause. His shoulders did not move. "You have perhaps ninety seconds before I remember how to be thorough. The courier waits at the willow bend, the one with the split trunk. Guild men keep worse hours than fish."

Marta was already moving. She pressed the fish crate into Ivo's arms — *you carry it, you carried it here* — and took Ivo's sleeve, and they went, fast and low along the ice road, past the barrier, past the soldiers who had discovered a sudden fascination with the brazier, past the inspector who did not turn, whose breath rose steadily into the dark like smoke from a fire that had finally, after thirty years, been allowed to go out.

At the willow bend a figure detached itself from the shadow of the split trunk — a compact man in a plain coat, snow in his eyebrows, with the patient hands of someone who had waited in many cold places. He said nothing. He held out a leather satchel, and Ivo opened the fish crate, and lifted out the plates — Aldous's plates, thirty years of a dead man's stubbornness, each one wrapped in oilcloth, each one lighter than a loaf of bread and heavier than a cathedral — and laid them in the satchel one by one, counting, the way Master Aldous had taught them to count, *twelve, thirteen, fourteen*, the last plate the one with the three villages drawn back into existence in careful, hesitant ink.

"Fifteen," Ivo whispered. "All fifteen."

The courier buckled the satchel shut, nodded once, and was gone into the trees, and that was all. No ceremony. Evidence travels best without witnesses, and mercy travels best without thanks.

They were halfway back to the barrier when the river gave up its pretense.

The crack ran under their feet like lightning made solid, and then the whole world tilted — the ice road lifting, splitting, a black seam opening wide enough to swallow the moon's reflection — and Ivo heard Marta shout their name, actually their name, *Ivo*, the first time she had ever spent it, and they threw themselves flat and slid, the crate gone, everything gone except their grip on her wrist and her grip on theirs, and the cold came up through the broken ice like a hand.

Behind them, faintly, they heard Voss shouting to his soldiers — *the ice! get back from the edge!* — a commander's voice doing a commander's work, buying cover for them even now, or perhaps simply meaning it.

They crawled. Hand over hand, belly to the groaning ice, Marta cursing with magnificent fluency in three languages, until the bank took them back and they lay in the snow gasping like landed fish, alive, empty-handed, and free.

After a long time Marta started laughing — silent, shaking laughter into her scarf — and Ivo joined her, and neither of them could have explained it to a priest.

"Well," she managed at last. "The river kept what it kept."

Downstream, at the willow bend, a small light moved away through the trees toward Vienna, carrying fifteen plates and three villages and a dead man's name, and Ivo Fairweather, who had measured their whole life in what could be carried, lay in the snow with nothing left to carry at all — and found, to their astonishment, that they had never felt lighter.

Marta sat up, brushed the snow from her hair, and looked at them with an expression Ivo had never seen her wear: unguarded, unarmored, almost young.

"Come on," she said. "We're expected at the Halbers' table, and if we're late again old Halber will tell the story about the eel, and I refuse to hear it twice in one week."

They got up together and walked home along the waking river, and behind them the ice went on breaking, all the way to the sea.
