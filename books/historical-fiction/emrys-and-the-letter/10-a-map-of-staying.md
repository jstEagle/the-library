## 10. A Map of Staying

The letter came on a Tuesday, which mattered to no one but the post rider, who complained the whole way down the hill that the mud had eaten one of his boots and he'd finished the route in a borrowed clog.

Ivo was mending a net on Marta's porch when he heard him coming — not because the rider was loud, though he was, but because Ivo had learned over the winter that every arrival in this town announced itself: the church bell's particular enthusiasm for weddings versus funerals, the dogs' opinion of strangers, the way Halber cleared his throat three times before saying anything of consequence. The river had taught Marta to read water. It seemed Ivo had been learning to read a town.

"Fairweather!" the rider called, using the name as if it had always belonged to someone. "From Vienna! Wax seal and everything — you'd think it was money."

It was not money. It was the guild's crest pressed into green wax, and inside, two pages in a secretary's careful hand.

Ivo read the first line and then sat down on the porch step without meaning to.

*The true borders have been published.*

Marta appeared in the doorway with a mallet in one hand and her sleeves rolled, having never in her life stopped working merely because something important might be happening. She read over Ivo's shoulder — she read quickly, with the impatience of a woman who had taught herself letters from shipping manifests — and then she went very still.

"Read the rest," she said, in the voice she used when the river was lying to her.

Ivo read the rest aloud, because some things needed air. The guild had authenticated the plates. The survey had been entered into the imperial record under protest, then under seal, then — after certain ministers discovered they could be heroes as cheaply as villains — under celebration. The three villages were being restored to the rolls: Drenov, where Marta had been born; Kesselmark, where her Jozef had grown up hauling timber; and little Vražda Ford, whose name would need explaining to every clerk in Vienna for the next hundred years. Compensation commissions were forming. A court date existed. Names were being written back into the world.

And at the bottom, in a different hand — smaller, more human — a postscript:

*The guild extends an invitation to the draftsman of record. There is a position in the Vienna archive. There is also, I am told, a medal. Come claim both, and come see what your master's work has done. — F., who owes you more than postage.*

Ivo lowered the letter. The net lay half-mended across their lap, and beyond it the river ran high and brown with spring, carrying ice-rot and willow catkins and one indignant duck.

"Well," said Marta.

"Well," said Ivo.

"You'll go, then."

It was not a question, but it was shaped like one, the way a door is shaped like a wall until you push it.

* * *

They had always assumed they would go. That had been the whole arithmetic of the winter: carry the thing through, deliver it, survive, and then — Vienna. Guild halls with warm floors. Archives where paper was respected instead of feared. A life measured in proper instruments, in brass and vellum, in a name spoken by people who mattered. Ivo had imagined it the way one imagines heaven: frequently, vaguely, and without any real desire to pack for it.

Now the letter sat on the table between them, and Ivo looked around Marta's kitchen and found the arithmetic had changed.

It was not a fine room. The plaster had given up near the window, and the shelf of Jozef's old river books leaned like a drunk, and there was a burn on the table from the night the candle had gone over during the argument about whether Ivo was going to die foolishly at the third checkpoint (Marta had won the argument; Ivo still maintained a technical loss). But there were three coats on the pegs now, one of them borrowed so long it had simply become Ivo's. There was a stool pulled up to the table that no one called anything but *your stool*. There was a jar on the sill where Halber's wife kept the wild garlic she pretended not to give them, and a chipped mug that had migrated, sometime in March, from *guest* to *kept*.

"I could write to them," Ivo said slowly, testing the thought the way you test spring ice. "Decline."

Marta did not look up from the bread she was not really kneading. "You'd decline Vienna."

"I'd decline the archive." Ivo turned the mug a quarter-turn on the table, a habit they'd caught from her without noticing. "The medal can go hang. But the work — Marta, nobody has ever surveyed this stretch properly. Not the real borders, not since the redraw. Every map in every office still shows villages that aren't there and omits ones that are. Somebody with the plates, with Aldous's methods, with—" they gestured helplessly at the window, the town, the whole visible world "—with *here*. Somebody should draw what's actually true."

"Somebody," Marta agreed. "In Vienna."

"No." And then, with the strange lightness that comes after a weight is finally set down: "Here. From here. The measurements start at your porch, technically, if we use your fencepost as the datum. I've already thought about it. The fencepost is very reliable. It has stood through worse than us."

Marta set down the dough. For a moment Ivo thought she might cry, which would have been catastrophic for both of them, and so was relieved when instead she said, gruffly:

"That fencepost leans."

"It leans *consistently*. That's all any of us do."

She laughed — short, surprised out of her — and wiped her hands on her apron, and looked at Ivo with the expression that had, over the winter, replaced all the guarded ones: the one that assessed, decided, and admitted nothing further.

"You'll want paper," she said. "A lot of paper. You'll bankrupt us in paper."

"Halber knows a supplier in Pressburg."

"Halber knows everyone in Pressburg. Half of them owe him money and the other half wish they didn't." She picked the letter back up, read the postscript again, and something moved through her face — grief, old and worn smooth now, like a stone the river had finished with. "Drenov's going back on the maps."

"Yes."

"He used to say it wouldn't matter. Jozef. Said the crown could erase a village faster than ink could save it." She set the letter down gently, as if it were one of Aldous's plates. "He'd have loved being wrong. He was wrong about almost everything else; it would have balanced the ledger."

Ivo reached across the table and took her flour-dusted hand, and she let them, and neither of them said anything else about it, because between them things never needed saying twice. That was the discovery of the year, really: that love — and Ivo had finally stopped flinching from the word — was less like carrying and more like standing. You did not transport it anywhere. You held still and let it accumulate.

* * *

Spring came on the way springs do in river towns: all at once, then reluctantly, then all at once again. The bridge reopened to pilgrims and peddlers and one furious imperial auditor who arrived to investigate the investigation and left within the week, defeated by Halber's hospitality and the town's collective, serene inability to remember anything useful.

Voss did not return. But in April a parcel came, forwarded twice, wrapped in oilcloth against the water. Inside was a drafting instrument of Viennese make — the finest Ivo had ever held — and no note except a single card bearing Voss's name and, beneath it, four words in a small precise hand:

*A debt, fully paid.*

Ivo kept the card pinned above the desk. Some debts made better ornaments than ledgers.

The new map grew slowly across the long table by the window. It was not a grand commission; the guild had offered one and Ivo had countered with something smaller and truer, and to everyone's surprise — most of all the guild's — the guild had agreed: a full survey of the restored borderlands, drawn locally, signed *I. Fairweather, draftsman of record*, the title sitting under Ivo's name like a bird deciding whether to stay.

But the first sheet — the one Ivo worked on in the evenings, by candlelight, for no archive at all — was a different kind of map entirely.

It was the town. Every street, every leaning roofline, the well with its crooked winch, the mill (under new management, the old management being in prison), the churchyard with its two new graves and one very old one that Ivo visited on Sundays to report progress to Master Aldous, who had wanted, more than vindication, Ivo now understood — to be *of use*, and finally was.

And around the edges of the map, where proper cartographers put compass roses and scale bars, Ivo put names. All the names. Old Halber, *who tells the eel story weekly.* Halber's wife, *who gives garlic and denies it.* The ferryman's grave by the willow, *Jozef, who was right about the river and wrong about ink.* Marta — and here Ivo's pen had hovered a long time before writing simply *Marta Yarrow Fairweather, who keeps the house, the nets, and me,* and then had gone on before courage could fail.

They had married in March, quietly, at the church, with the entire town in attendance pretending to be there for other reasons. Yarrow had been Aldous's surname, unclaimed by any blood relation, and Ivo had taken it the way one takes up a tool from a beloved dead hand — and Marta had added hers to it without ceremony, the way she did everything, and the clerk had written both names in the register, and that was that. Identity, it turned out, was not carried. It was built, plank by plank, at tables, in kitchens, on porches, by people who stayed.

* * *

On the evening the last plate of the border survey went to the courier — south this time, openly, with receipts — Marta set the table for supper and then paused, holding a second plate, looking at the cupboard as if it had asked her a question.

"What?" said Ivo, sharpening a pencil.

"We only ever use two."

"So?"

"So." She took down a third plate — the good blue one, saved for occasions — and set it at Ivo's place, and then stood a fourth beside it, empty, facing the chair where nobody sat yet. She did not explain. She had never once explained herself and clearly did not intend to begin.

Ivo looked at the plates. At the fourth one, patient and blank as fresh paper.

"The house is small," Ivo said carefully.

"The house is *ours*," Marta said, "and small is not the same as full."

Outside, the river ran high and bright with spring, carrying everything downstream except the things that chose to hold still. Ivo dipped the pen, wrote the final marginal note on the town map — *survey continues; additions expected* — and went in to supper.

Two plates for tonight. Room, at the table, for whatever came next.
