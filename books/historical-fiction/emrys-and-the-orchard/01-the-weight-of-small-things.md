## 1. The Weight of Small Things

By the time the bells of Saint Ferro rang the half-hour before dawn, Orsen Vasquez had already weighed eleven crates of Vellish cedar oil with nothing but their two hands, and had been proved wrong on none of them.

That was the trick nobody at the Halloway Trading Company had ever quite caught onto: that a person could learn weight the way other people learned prayers. You held a crate long enough, hefted it once, twice, let your wrists do the arithmetic—and you knew. Forty pounds and a suspicion. Sixty-two if the seals had leaked and the oil had gone thick in the cold. The company paid three coppers an hour for men with scales and five for men who could read ledgers, but they had never thought to pay anything at all for a girl-shaped nobody of nineteen years whose hands were worth more than both.

Orsen did not point this out. Pointing things out was how you got noticed, and getting noticed, in the tenements of Lower Saltrow, was how you got dead, or worse—indentured further.

"Vasquez!" bellowed Foreman Hobb from somewhere amid the forest of crates, his voice arriving the way weather arrives, from all directions at once and none of them pleasant. "Manifest says twelve."

"Eleven," Orsen called back, not looking up. "The twelfth is on the *Merrow's Debt*, still floating, unless it sank overnight, in which case it's twelve but wet."

There was a pause while Hobb consulted someone smarter than him, which took a while, and then: "*Fine.* Eleven."

Orsen permitted themselves one small breath of satisfaction—barely a breath, more the memory of one—and moved to the next crate.

*Carry little,* their father had taught them, back when their father's chest still worked like a chest instead of like a bellows full of gravel. *Want less. Walk through the world like a shadow at noon, mija, and no one will ever put a hand on you.* Papa Vasquez had been a pearl diver for thirty-one years, and pearl divers understood invisibility better than any priest understood grace. The sea only noticed you when you wanted something from it. Want nothing, take nothing that isn't given, and it would let you pass.

The warehouse was the same sea, only drier and worse-smelling.

It was a cathedral of commerce, if cathedrals smelled of tallow and tar and the particular sweetness of cedar oil soaking into pine. Rafters went up and up into gloom where the rope loft hung like a gray cloud snagged on the architecture—a great hammock of coiled hemp and sisal that swayed, very slightly, when the harbor wind found the loading doors. Crates stood in streets and avenues, whole neighborhoods of them, labeled in the tidy clerk-hand of the Halloway Trading Company: *SALT PORK TO PORT AMBROSE. CEDAR OIL, FINE, TO —* and then a blank, because the fine people of Port Ambrose presumably did not wish their names associated with cargo.

Orsen knew every aisle. Knew which floorboards near the east doors sang when stepped on (useful, if you ever needed to know someone was coming), knew which clerk signed with a flourish and which with a scrawl, knew that the third candle on the tally desk always guttered around nine bells because the wick was cut crooked and no one would admit to cutting it.

Most of all, Orsen knew faces.

This was not a skill they had chosen. It had simply grown in them, the way calluses grow, from years of standing still while important people walked past. A warehouse saw everyone eventually: merchants wrapped in wool worth more than Saltrow entire; captains with rope-burn voices; clerks with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that never went lower than your collarbone, because looking lower meant admitting you'd seen a person. Orsen watched them all from behind crates and clipboards, and memorized them the way other folk memorized hymns—the merchant with the gold tooth who cheated on weights and then complained about cheaters; the captain who wept quietly in his rum; the young clerk, Merrit, who was kind to the dock boys and therefore doomed to go nowhere in this company.

Faces were currency. You collected them and spent them never.

Because here was the second lesson, carved just beneath the first: some faces you must never acknowledge. The ones that came through the private door at the harbor side, past the guard who pretended to sharpen his knife. The ones that arrived in carriages with the crests painted over. Whatever those faces came to buy or sell or bury, Orsen looked at the floor, looked at the manifest, looked anywhere that was nowhere, and let them pass like weather.

Shadow at noon, Papa said. Shadow at noon.

* * *

At eight bells the sky over Vellamare went from slate to silver, and the warehouse filled with the clatter of its morning population: forty-odd loaders, six tallymen, a cook named Berta whose chowder was rumored to contain at least one item that had once been alive, and Mr. Pennhallow Quill.

Quill was junior auditor, which was a way of saying he counted what other people lost, and he did it with the serene contentment of a man who had never once doubted that the world was a ledger and he had been born holding the pen. He was perhaps thirty-five, narrow as a ruled line, with spectacles he cleaned at moments of maximum condescension and a voice pitched exactly between kindness and threat.

"Vasquez." He materialized beside the cedar oil the way cold air materializes under a door. "The Merrow's Debt manifest."

"Eleven crates landed. Twelve shipped."

"And the discrepancy?"

"Wet, sir. Or drowned." Orsen kept their eyes at the respectful middle distance—his shoulder, roughly. "Captain Tull said the crate shifted in the swell off the point. He'll file the loss notice."

"Mm." Quill wrote something. He wrote everything; Orsen suspected he wrote his dreams down, itemized, with fines assessed against himself for the improper ones. "Tull has filed four loss notices this quarter. Curious, how the sea grows so selective around Captain Tull."

"Yes, sir."

"You agree with me, Vasquez?"

"I agree the sea is strange, sir."

Something flickered at the corner of Quill's mouth—not a smile, but a cousin of one, twice removed. He cleaned his spectacles with two precise strokes, which Orsen had learned meant he was either pleased or preparing to be displeased, and in Quill those were often the same weather. "Your indenture review is next month. Seven years served." He tucked his book away. "Time does accumulate, doesn't it? Like interest."

He glided off to ruin someone else's morning, leaving Orsen holding a cedar crate and a feeling they couldn't name yet, a small cold coin dropped into a pocket they didn't know they had.

Seven years. It didn't feel like seven years. It felt like one very long year repeated seven times, each repetition costing slightly more than the last. Nine years old when Papa's lungs gave out and the Halloway Company had offered the only charity Lower Saltrow recognized: a contract. Room, board, and a wage of almost nothing, in exchange for—well, the contract had said *a term of seven years' faithful service*, and Orsen had been too young and too scared to ask what happened after the seven years if you weren't faithful enough, or if *faithful* turned out to mean whatever the man holding the ledger said it meant.

They hefted the crate. Forty-one pounds. Heavy for cedar oil.

Somebody had watered somebody's shipment, and it wasn't going to be Orsen's problem, because noticing things out loud was not in Orsen's job description. Noticing things silently was the whole of it.

* * *

Midmorning brought the faces.

They came through the private door at the harbor side, three of them, moving through the aisles like a cold current through warm water: a woman in gray silk with a face like a locked drawer, a guard pretending to be a servant, and a young man Orsen had never seen before—young, rich in the bone-deep bored way, his coat worth more than the warehouse roof.

The clerk assigned to them was Aldous Crane, senior tallyman, a man who could smell money through oak. Orsen was stacking salt-pork barrels twenty feet away, close enough to see, far enough to be furniture.

The transaction was quiet and fast. A sealed strongbox passed from the young man to Crane. A different box—long, narrow, heavy in a wrong way—passed back. Papers were signed without being read by anyone who mattered. And through all of it the woman in gray watched the room, her eyes sliding across loaders and tallymen like light across water, touching everything, catching on nothing.

Her gaze reached Orsen.

Orsen looked at the barrel. Counted staves. Thought about absolutely nothing in the loudest possible silence.

The moment stretched—one heartbeat, two—and then the current moved on and out the private door, and the warehouse exhaled, and Crane began telling an anecdote about the young man's father that was funny only if you'd never met poverty.

Orsen finished counting staves. Sixty-two. Good oak.

That was the whole art, right there. The woman in gray had looked, and Orsen had not been there to be found. Seven years of practice, and it worked like clockwork, and someday—someday soon, after the review, when the contract was done—it would buy a little room above the harbor with a window facing west, and Orsen would stand in that room and be invisible entirely for themselves alone.

They allowed themselves to want this for precisely four seconds. Then they put the wanting away, carefully, in the place where the other wants lived, and went back to work.

Want less. Carry little. There'd be time for windows later, if the arithmetic ever came out kind.

* * *

Lunch was chowder on the loading dock with the loaders, who had adopted Orsen years ago the way harbors adopt gulls—without ceremony and without any illusion of ownership either direction.

"—and I says to him, I says, *that's not a rat, that's my lunch,*" Dov was saying. Dov was built like a capstan and had the wit of one, which everyone agreed affectionately, including eventually Dov. "And Berta says, *then you eat like a rat.*"

"She's not wrong," said Fen, a wiry old loader missing two fingers to a winch and most of his teeth to everything else. He nudged Orsen with an elbow. "You're quiet today, Vasquez. Quieter. Which for you is practically a séance."

"I'm eating."

"He's scheming," Dov decided. "Vasquez schemes. That's how they get the good jobs. Weighing things with their hands. Witchcraft."

"It's practice," Orsen said. "Same as you throwing bales. My hands are just rude about it."

Fen laughed, a sound like gravel approving of more gravel. "Rude hands. Ha. Mark me—" he pointed his stump of a spoon at the harbor, where the ships rode at anchor in the pale morning light, masts thick as a drowned forest—"mark me, this lot ends soon anyway. Third ship this month come in short-handed. Captains grumbling at the hiring hall. Men don't like the routes anymore. Something's wrong on the water, and when the water's wrong, the warehouses empty out, and when the warehouses empty—" he made a gesture that encompassed firing, hunger, and the general behavior of employers, all in one rotation of his wrist.

"Doom and gloom," said Dov comfortably, into his chowder.

"Aye, and doom keeps better hours than optimism," Fen said. "You'll see."

Orsen said nothing, but privately filed Fen's words next to the other small things they'd noticed lately: the hiring hall busier than usual, and quieter; the tallymen whispering behind the manifest room door; the way the company had started buying up dried fish and hardtack in quantities that had nothing to do with any normal season's orders. Companies were like animals before storms. They got restless. They ate everything they could reach.

Then again, Fen read doom into weather and fish prices alike. The world ended roughly four times a year in Fen's telling, and had so far failed to show up for any of them.

The bell rang. Back to work.

* * *

The afternoon passed in weights and names.

A shipment of Ambrosian wool, soft as apology, sixty pounds to the bale and lying about it by three. A case of glassware from the Glassreach, which Orsen carried like a sleeping animal. Barrels of tar. Boxes of paper. And once, carried through the public doors by a harried midwife's assistant, a small wrapped bundle that made Orsen's arms remember, suddenly and uselessly, the weight of a thing they'd never held.

Just cloth and wood, actually. A cradle part. They weighed it by habit—nine pounds—and despised themselves mildly for the flicker of something underneath the number.

At four bells, Hobb found them in the wool aisle with news delivered in his customary style, which was to say loudly and aimed generally at the region containing Orsen. "Quill wants the loft inventory done tonight. Rope, canvas, sacking. Full count. Says the auditor wants it before month's end."

"The loft inventory hasn't been wrong since spring," Orsen said.

"Don't care. Auditor wants it. You know how he is." Hobb lowered his voice into the register he believed was confidential, a register audible in Port Ambrose. "Word is there's changes coming. Big ones. Reorganization. So make the count clean, eh? Don't give him a thread to pull."

Changes coming. Everyone said it lately, in the tone of people describing weather they could feel in their knees but couldn't see yet. Orsen climbed the ladder to the rope loft as the light through the high windows went amber, and worked along the swaying gray hammock of coil and canvas, counting.

It was peaceful up here, actually. That was the secret nobody knew: the loft was the best place in the warehouse, warm with trapped heat, smelling of hemp and dust, rocking faintly with the wind off the water like the whole loft was a boat that had decided to stay ashore. From up here you could see the whole cathedral of crates below, the tiny figures of loaders and clerks moving their tiny important loads, and beyond the open doors the harbor itself—gray-green water, forest of masts, the far hills where the terraced gardens climbed, and somewhere past the point, invisible from here but permanent in Orsen's mind, the shallow reef where the old orchard lay drowned.

Mama had described it a hundred times, in the evenings, when the tenement was loud and the lamp was cheap. An orchard from before the seawall, she said, from her grandmother's grandmother's day, when the sea had come up slow and swallowed the hillside whole. Apple trees still standing down there in the green dark, she said, branches soft with weed, and the pearls settled in the roots like fruit that had finally learned patience. She'd dove there. She'd sworn the apples still showed white sometimes, deep down, when the light hit right.

She'd died diving there. But that was a thought for a different hour, with different armor on.

Orsen counted rope. Four hundred and eleven coils of hemp, thirty-eight of sisal, canvas in bundles, sacking in taller stacks near the north wall where the shadows pooled deepest. Two hundred and six. Two hundred and—

A sound.

Small. Very small. From the north wall, from behind the tallest stack of sacking, where the shadows pooled.

Orsen went still, one hand on the hemp, and listened the way pearl divers listen—with the whole body, with the blood.

There it was again. Soft as a sigh. Soft as—Orsen's mind supplied the word before they could stop it—soft as a breath. A small breath. Far too small to belong to anyone with business in a rope loft at four bells, or in a rope loft at any bells, or in the world at all, really, at that size.

The warehouse noise continued faithfully below—Hobb's distant bellowing, a winch complaining, the ordinary music of commerce. Up here, in the warm and the dust, in the amber failing light, the stack of sacking by the north wall sat perfectly innocent and perfectly silent, and Orsen stood in the swaying gray hammock of rope with their heart conducting an unauthorized percussion section, remembering all at once and much too late that they had counted this loft twice a year for seven years and had never, not once, heard it breathe.

They should have called for Hobb. That was the procedure. Strange sounds in the inventory were reported, noted, fined against someone's wages, resolved.

Orsen looked at the ladder. Looked at the sacking.

Stepped, silently, toward the sacking instead.
