## 2. Salt in the Tenement

The walk from the Halloway warehouse to the tenements took eleven minutes if you didn't dawdle, and Orsen Vasquez never dawdled. Dawdling cost candle-oil, and candle-oil cost coin, and coin was the one thing the company paid in smallest amounts and counted most carefully.

They climbed the outside stair with a paper of salted cod under one arm and a twist of honey candy hidden deep in their pocket. The stair groaned its familiar complaints—third step, fifth step, the whole seventh flight threatening retirement—and Orsen patted the rail the way you'd pat an old mule. Everything in Vellamare that had survived this long deserved a little respect.

Their father's room was on the top floor, which meant it was cheapest and dampest and had the best view, though Tomás Vasquez could no longer climb to the window to enjoy it. Orsen let themselves in without knocking, because knocking would wake him if he'd finally managed to sleep, and not knocking would wake him anyway, so really the choice was ceremonial.

"Orsen." His voice came out like sand poured through a sieve. He was propped against the wall on his pallet, blanket over his legs, hands resting on his chest as if he were already being laid out. "You're late."

"I'm never late."

"You're always exactly as late as the company allows, which is a kind of lateness." He coughed, wet and long, and turned his head to spare them the worst of it. "Sit. Tell me what the sea did today."

"The sea did nothing. The sea stayed where it was put. I weighed forty barrels of salted herring and one crate of something Mr. Pell refused to label, and I memorized the face of a man who tried to pass off river stones as pearls."

Tomás smiled, and for a moment the diver's face surfaced through the wreck of the clerk's—the broad cheekbones, the eyes gone pale as sea glass from years of staring into deep water. "River stones. In *our* harbor. Did he think we'd forgotten what pearls look like?"

"He thought I'd forgotten. That's different."

"That is very different." Their father nodded slowly, gravely, as though Orsen had said something profound instead of merely true. "Your mother used to say the same. 'They don't lie because they believe the lie,' she'd say. 'They lie because they believe *you*.'"

Orsen sat on the upturned crate that served as a second chair and began wrapping the cod in cloth for tomorrow's stew. Their hands liked having work. Idle hands started thinking, and thinking, in Orsen's experience, mostly led to wanting, and wanting was a debt you couldn't audit your way out of.

"How are your lungs?" they asked, which was a foolish question, and they both knew it, and he answered it anyway because that was the ritual.

"Wet," he said cheerfully. "Like everything else in this city. But I dreamed about her last night. Your mother. She was diving under the orchard again."

"I know."

"You can't know."

"You dream about it every time the weather turns. It's been turning for three days." Orsen kept their eyes on the fish. "Tell me the part about the branches."

He told it, then. He always told it, and Orsen always listened as though hearing it fresh, because some stories weren't for information—they were for keeping a person alive, and Tomás Vasquez had been living on this one for twenty years like a man living on hardtack.

"She dove down past where the light gives up," he said, his voice finding an old rhythm, almost singing, "and there it all was, still standing. Apple trees in rows, white with blossom even underwater, and the blossoms drifting up when she moved through them, like snow falling the wrong direction. And the fish swimming between the branches like birds that had given up on the sky and found something better." He paused for breath, spent it on a cough, went on. "And she said—" here he always lowered his voice, as if the company might be listening even now, even for this— "she said the fruit down there never rots. It just waits. Ripe forever, waiting for somebody brave enough to go down and pick it."

"And the pearl?"

"And the pearl." He closed his eyes. "In the crook of the lowest branch. Big as a quail's egg. She saw it every dive and left it every time, because—" 

"Because taking it would mean the orchard wasn't waiting anymore."

"Because some things you leave so there's always a reason to come back." He opened his eyes and looked at Orsen with that terrible gentle attention fathers have, the kind that sees straight through a person to whatever they're carrying. "You've got flour on your sleeve."

"It's herring dust."

"It's flour. You've been baking again. For whom?"

"For nobody."

"Nobody eats well lately, then." He smiled, and let it go, because he was a man who understood that some gifts had to be smuggled in plain sight or the recipient would refuse delivery.

* * *

Marisol came up the stairs two at a time, which was how Marisol did everything, including things that should have been done slowly, like growing up and forgiving people.

She was nineteen and worked the mending tables at the sail loft, and she had her mother's black hair and her father's temper and a hunger in her that Orsen recognized the way you recognize weather coming over water. She burst into the room smelling of tar and cheap soap and stopped dead when she saw the honey-shaped bulge in Orsen's pocket.

"You bought candy."

"I bought nothing."

"There's a bulge the exact shape of honey drops in your left pocket, Orsen. I inventory fabric for a living. I notice bulges."

"Then you also noticed I denied buying any, and we can move on with our evening."

Tomás laughed until he coughed, which was the family way of laughing. Marisol fished the candy out of Orsen's pocket with the speed of a gull taking bread, distributed one drop to each parent-figure present, and put the second-to-last one in her own mouth while looking directly at Orsen, daring commentary.

"You're thin," she said around the candy.

"I'm efficient."

"You're *thin*, and you're gray, and you smell like the inside of a warehouse, and you gave Father your supper again, didn't you? Don't lie. There's flour on your sleeve and no flour in this house."

Orsen looked down at their sleeve. Betrayed by baked goods. This was why invisibility required constant maintenance.

"I ate," they said. "I eat constantly. I'm the best-fed invisible man in Vellamare."

"You're not a man yet, you're a *clerk's shadow*—" Marisol caught herself, but too late; the word was out and doing its work. She sat down hard on the windowsill and stared out at the harbor rather than at either of them. Below, the Halloway warehouses lined the quay like a row of closed fists, and the company flag hung limp in the windless evening, red and gold, patient as a spider.

And for a moment—for three paragraphs' worth of a moment, if moments could be measured that way—we'll stand where Marisol stands, because love strained by scarcity has two ends of the rope, and only one end gets to do the talking at supper.

Here is what Marisol saw when she looked at her sibling: she saw a person who had learned to make themselves small so thoroughly that smallness had begun to look like contentment. She saw someone who weighed cargo by hand and memorized faces and called survival a philosophy. And she saw—this was the part that made her teeth ache—someone the company had *won*. Not beaten. Won. Orsen spoke of Mr. Quill the auditor with a careful neutrality that was worse than hatred. Orsen brought home wages calculated to the copper and apologized for the arithmetic as though the arithmetic were weather. Once, when Marisol had suggested—with heat—that the warehouse workers might band together and demand better quarantine rations, Orsen had looked at her with genuine fear and said, *"Don't say things like that where walls can hear,"* and Marisol had understood that her sibling's spine hadn't broken; it had been *inventoried*, entered into a ledger, and filed.

And here is what Marisol wanted, in the order she wanted it: passage abroad, on a real ship, to a coast where the air didn't taste of brine and debt. A house with a door that locked from the inside. Her father breathing easy in dry mountain air. And her sibling standing upright beside her—not bent toward the company like a plant toward a window, but *upright*, the way their mother must have stood before the sea took her.

She knew Orsen loved them. That was never in question. Orsen loved the way ballast loves: silently, at the bottom of everything, holding the ship steady and getting no credit and going nowhere itself. And that was precisely the problem. Ballast doesn't get a berth. Ballast stays with the hull.

"Marisol." Orsen's voice, careful. "You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The one where you're about to say something true."

"Somebody in this family has to." She turned from the window. "There's a ship. The *Cormorant's Grace*, out of the northern ports. They take passengers if you can pay the passage, and I've been counting, and if I save everything for a year—everything—and if Father's medicine costs what it costs and not what it will cost—"

"A year," Tomás said softly.

"A year," Marisol said, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated that it cracked, because cracking was weakness and weakness was a luxury. "A year is nothing. A year is *nothing* compared to—"

"To whether I'm alive at the end of it," their father finished, gently, and the room went quiet the way rooms go quiet after a wave passes over a boat.

Orsen said nothing at all, which Marisol noted, catalogued, and resented.

* * *

By full dark, Tomás slept, propped high so the fluid wouldn't drown him from the inside—a pearl diver, dying of water, and the sea not even having the decency to be present. Marisol and Orsen sat on the landing outside, sharing the last honey drop by splitting it with a thumbnail, because neither would take the larger half and the negotiation had reached stalemate.

"You could ask Quill," Marisol said. "For a berth. When the company charters its ships—you know they charter ships, everyone knows, the favored families get lists—"

"Favored families get lists," Orsen agreed. "We are not a favored family. We are a favored *line item*."

"Then ask anyway. You've been useful for nine years. Nine years, Orsen. Usefulness has to buy *something*."

Orsen turned the candy fragment over in their palm. From the landing they could see the warehouse roofs, and beyond them the dark water, and somewhere out on that water, the drowned orchard that wasn't there anymore except in a dying man's mouth.

"Usefulness buys more usefulness," they said. "That's the whole trick of it. You carry one barrel, and then you're the one who carries barrels, and then carrying barrels is your name, and one day you look up and you're not Orsen Vasquez at all, you're *the one who carries barrels*, and nobody asks what happened to the other one."

"That's the saddest thing you've ever said, and you once described your own wage as 'a rounding error with ambitions.'"

That startled a laugh out of Orsen—short, rusty, real. Marisol stored the sound away the way she stored everything: proof, evidence, ammunition for later.

"I mean it, though," she said, quieter now. "I'm going to get us on a ship. All three of us. I don't care what it costs or who I have to charm or swindle. Mother got taken by the water. I'm not letting the rest of us get taken by paperwork."

Orsen looked at their sister—at the set of her jaw, which was their mother's jaw, at the fury in her hands, which was their mother's hands—and felt the old fear rise, the fear that lived in them like groundwater: *she wants too much, and wanting too much is how the company finds your soft places.*

But they said none of it. Instead they pressed the last crumb of honey drop into her palm and stood, brushing herring dust—or flour—from their sleeve.

"Go to bed, Mari. Dream about mountains."

"Dream about *berths*," she corrected. "Big ones. With our names on them."

Orsen went down the groaning stairs and along the quay road, back toward the warehouse district, where a cot waited in the ropewalk loft and a night's sleep waited on top of it, thin as communion wafer. The lamps of Vellamare burned low behind them. Ahead, the Halloway warehouse squatted against the stars, vast and blind, its doors locked, its ledgers dreaming their tidy dreams of debts and fines and faces to forget.

Orsen let themselves in through the coal hatch, as they did every night, and was halfway across the dark floor of the counting room when they stopped.

There was a sound. Small. Rhythmic. Coming from the direction of the loading door.

Not a rat. Rats didn't cry.

Orsen stood very still in the dark, holding their breath the way their father held his before a dive, and listened.
