## 3. Instructions to Forget

The sound came again — a thin, hitching wail that rose and fell like wind through a keyhole.

Orsen stood in the dark of the counting room with their lamp unlit and their heart doing something entirely unscheduled. Thirteen years in the Halloway warehouse had taught them every noise the building made: the groan of the tide against the pilings, the tick of the copper scales settling, old Fenwick's cough from the night watchman's chair by the stove. This was none of these. This was new. New things, in Orsen's experience, were almost always trouble wearing an interesting hat.

They lit the lamp anyway, cupping the flame, and followed the sound across the floor to the loading door.

The bundle sat just inside the threshold, on the top step where the rain could not reach it. A basket, woven reed, the kind fishwives used for eels. A blanket folded over whatever was inside, and tucked into the knot of it, a letter sealed with plain wax — no crest, no ribbon, which told Orsen more than any signature would have. Important people wanted their letters known. Unimportant ones wanted them forgotten before the ink dried.

The bundle cried again, furiously now, as though offended by the delay.

"Alright," Orsen said to it, feeling foolish and also somehow obligated. "Alright, I'm coming."

They crouched and lifted a corner of the blanket. Two furious dark eyes stared back at them from a face no bigger than their palm — red with outrage, damp at the temples, with a whorl of black hair standing straight up as if the child had been startled by her own arrival into the world and never quite recovered.

An infant. At the loading door of a trading company. In the middle of a Tuesday.

Orsen looked around the empty dock, out at the harbor lights bobbing in the dark, half expecting someone to be watching, waiting to laugh. Nobody. Just the water slapping the pilings and the baby glaring up at them with the conviction that all her problems were now, officially, Orsen's fault.

* * *

By the time the first grey light came through the high windows, Orsen had learned several things.

First: the baby was a girl. Second: she was perhaps three months old, though Orsen's expertise in such matters was limited to what one could glean over tenement fences. Third: she had lungs that would have done credit to a foghorn, and she used them liberally whenever she was set down, which meant Orsen spent the better part of two hours rocking her in the crook of one arm while reading the sealed letter one-handed by lamplight.

The letter was brief. Clerks' hands always were; they were paid by brevity, in a sense.

*To the night man at Halloway's:*

*The bearer is to be taken to the Sisters at Foundling Steps this morning. No inquiry is to be made. The child is to be given no name and remembered by no one. Forget the face.*

*Settlement enclosed. Speak of this to no one, including yourself.*

There was no settlement enclosed. There was nothing enclosed at all, except the baby, who had by then fallen asleep against Orsen's collarbone with one small fist knotted in their shirt as though she'd read the letter too and intended to contest its terms.

"Forget the face," Orsen murmured.

The face in question was pressed into their neck, breathing in little sanded hitches, one cheek round as a dinner roll. It was, Orsen reflected, a remarkably forgettable face, in the way that fire is forgettable, or the tide.

They should have taken her to the Sisters. That was the whole of the instruction, and Orsen's entire method of survival rested upon doing exactly what instructions said, precisely, invisibly, without leaving so much as a fingerprint of personality on anything. Carry little. Want less. Be unseen. They had built thirteen years on that foundation, and the foundation had held through fines, through beatings, through winters when the coal ration went to favored men and Orsen simply got colder and said nothing.

But the Sisters at Foundling Steps took coin for their charity — everyone knew it, though nobody said it above a whisper. And the plague rumors had been thickening all month, and when sickness came to Vellamare, the foundling home was always first to fill and first to bury. And the baby had not let go of Orsen's shirt.

"I'm thinking about logistics," Orsen told her sternly. "Nothing else. Logistics."

She slept on, unconvinced.

* * *

Morning brought Mr. Pellowe, the junior clerk, who arrived at seven sharp with his ledgers under his arm and his expression already arranged into its usual look of a man smelling something faintly disappointing.

"You're early," Pellowe said.

"I'm always early."

"Don't be clever, Vasquez." He set his ledgers down on the counting table and paused. His nose twitched, quick as a rat's. "What's that smell?"

Orsen considered. The smell was sour milk and wet wool, because they had fed the baby — there had been a jar of goat's milk in the provisioners' stores, logged for the crew of the *Cormorant*, and Orsen had decided, in a moment of unprecedented criminality, to borrow rather than steal, meaning they fully intended to log it back somehow someday.

"Wet wool," Orsen said. "Rained in through the coal hatch."

Pellowe sniffed again, failed to find anything worth pursuing, and moved on to the day's manifests. He was, in fairness, not stupid — merely economical, the way so many company men were economical, spending attention only where profit might follow. A crying infant in a rope loft was not profitable. Therefore it did not exist.

"There'll be a ship in Thursday," he said, running a finger down the column. "Yellow flag off the point yesterday. Captain'll want quick turnaround or quarantine, and Quill wants it to be quick. Double shifts till then."

"Yes, sir."

"And Vasquez—" Pellowe hesitated in a manner so unusual that Orsen actually looked up. The clerk was staring at the top step of the loading door, where the reed basket still sat, empty now, rain-damp, unmistakably a basket. "That yours?"

"It washed up," Orsen said. "I was going to burn it."

Something crossed Pellowe's face — not suspicion, exactly, but the memory of something. He knew whose basket it was. Of course he knew. He had probably carried it here himself, or watched it carried, or signed the paper that ordered it carried. But knowing cost him something, and company men never spent what they didn't have to.

"Burn it, then," he said, and opened his ledger, and that was the end of it.

Orsen burned the basket that afternoon, in the small stove by the watchman's chair, feeding the reeds in piece by piece until there was nothing left but a smell of scorched grass and a handful of pale ash. It felt less like destroying evidence than like attending a very small funeral. The baby — hidden since dawn in the rope loft above the storeroom, wrapped in a bolt of second-grade canvas that Orsen had "damaged" and therefore had every right to repurpose — slept through the whole ceremony, which Orsen felt showed either great wisdom or great indifference, and at three months old those amounted to the same thing.

* * *

The rope loft was, Orsen thought, nearly perfect.

It ran the length of the storeroom, a raftered shelf six feet deep hung with coils of hemp and manila, reached by a ladder bolted to the wall behind the sailcloth racks. Nobody went up there. Ropes didn't need inventorying twice a year, and the loft was dusty, dim, and smelled of tar — all things that discouraged visitors and, apparently, encouraged sleep, because the baby had gone down in a nest of canvas between two coils of three-inch hemp as contentedly as though she'd been born to warehouse life.

Which was, Orsen supposed grimly, not far wrong.

They climbed up during the lunch hour, when the floor below emptied, and checked on her. She was awake, working on her own fist with an air of serious scholarship. When Orsen's head rose above the loft floor, she stopped, stared, and produced — unmistakably, deliberately — a smile.

It was a gummy, lopsided, entirely corrupt smile, the smile of a moneylender, of a girl who already knew how the world worked and meant to get the best of it.

"No," Orsen said.

The smile widened.

"That's not going to work on me. I've seen professionals." Orsen settled cross-legged beside her, careful of the canvas, and offered one finger, which was seized and gummed with tremendous authority. "I'm invisible. Thirteen years. Do you know how hard that is? I once stood in this room for four hours while two foremen argued about whether I'd gone home, and neither of them asked me. That takes discipline. That takes *practice*. And you—"

The baby bit his finger. Gently, toothlessly, but with clear intent.

"—you've been here one day," Orsen finished, "and already you've got me stealing milk and burning evidence."

Somewhere below, the door banged, and voices came in — dockhands back early from the midday meal, grumbling about the yellow flag and the price of flour. Orsen flattened themselves against the loft boards without a sound, breath shallow, finger still held captive in a grip surprisingly strong for something that weighed less than a sack of rice. Below, boots crossed the floor. A coil of rope was taken down from directly overhead, its hook shrieking faintly, and Orsen lay nose to nose with the baby, who gazed up at them with wide, delighted eyes, clearly under the impression that this was a game invented entirely for her benefit.

*Don't,* Orsen communicated silently. *Don't you dare.*

She dared. She laughed — a single bright syllable of a laugh, *hah*, like the first note of some instrument nobody had named yet — and Orsen clapped a hand gently over it, and below, the boots paused.

"What was that?"

"What was what?"

"That noise."

"Rats," said the other voice comfortably. "Big ones, too. Heard 'em last week up in the rafters."

The boots moved on. The door banged again. Orsen lay there a long moment, heart hammering, and then lifted their hand from the baby's mouth, and she looked up at them with such perfect, unearned trust that something happened in their chest — a crack, fine as a hairline fracture in porcelain, quiet enough that they could pretend afterward they hadn't heard it.

"Rats," Orsen whispered. "You're going to get me killed, and they'll call it vermin control."

* * *

That evening they inventoried their situation the way they inventoried cargo, by hand, honestly, item by item, because self-deception was a luxury for people with margins.

Assets: one goat's milk jar (borrowed), half a bolt of damaged canvas (pre-damaged), a hot water bottle stolen from the infirmary cabinet in a spirit of outright larceny they chose not to examine, and a knowledge of the warehouse's rhythms so complete it bordered on the eerie. Liabilities: one infant, unlogged, unnamed, illegal in every direction; one junior clerk who had seen a basket; one Mr. Quill, senior auditor, who missed nothing that touched the ledgers, and milk was in the ledgers; and the fact that Orsen Vasquez, invisible for thirteen years, had now smiled at a baby in broad daylight where God and the rafters could see.

And beneath all of it, unlogged, unpriced, the true liability: that when the baby gripped their finger, Orsen did not want to let go.

Their father had a saying, back in the tenement, back when his lungs still allowed sayings: *the sea takes what you love best, so love in pencil.* Their mother had loved in ink. Everyone in the Saltrow tenement agreed it was the sea that took her for it.

Orsen sat in the loft after midnight, the baby asleep in the crook of their arm, the harbor lights shivering beyond the high windows, and understood with terrible clarity that they had already written her name somewhere inside themselves, in ink, in a hand that could not be undone.

They just hadn't decided what the name was yet.

Below, in the counting room, the manifest for Thursday's ship lay open on the table — the *Cormorant*, quick turnaround, quarantine pending. And pinned beneath the paperweight, fresh from Quill's office, a notice in neat clerical script:

*All irregularities in stores to be reported. All discrepancies will be accounted. Nothing leaves this warehouse unnoticed.*

Orsen looked at the notice. Then at the sleeping weight against their chest, breathing in little tides.

"Noticed," Orsen whispered, "is a matter of who's looking."

And in the morning, when Pellowe asked why the night man smelled of goat's milk, Orsen would lie for the first time in thirteen years — fluently, easily, terribly well — and would begin, in the same breath, choosing a name.
