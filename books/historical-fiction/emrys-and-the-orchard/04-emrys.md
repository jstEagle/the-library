## 4. Emrys

The rope loft smelled of hemp and dust and, increasingly, of baby—that warm, milky, unmistakable smell that no amount of careful sweeping could erase. Orsen had learned this on the third night, crouched in the dark with a scrap of soap and a wet rag, scrubbing at the boards where the child slept, and understanding with a cold slide in their stomach that they could scrub until the wood wore through and the smell would still be there, waiting for the next nose that came along.

"You," Orsen told the bundle quietly, "are very bad at being invisible."

The bundle sneezed in response.

Three nights now. Three nights since the clerk had set the basket down at the warehouse door with the note pinned to the blanket—*forget the face*—and walked away into the fog without so much as a backward glance. Three nights of Orsen meaning, every single morning, to do something about it. Carry her to the Sisters at the almshouse. Leave her on a church step where the parish had a duty and a budget and no Halloway ledgers involved. Any of the reasonable things. The invisible things.

And every single morning, Orsen would climb the loft ladder at first light with milk stolen from the company stores diluted in a flask, and the child would fix them with that ferocious unblinking stare—the one that seemed to say she had considered the matter of Orsen Vasquez at length and found them adequate—and the morning's reasonable plan would dissolve like salt in rain.

Tonight was the fourth night, and Orsen had made a decision, though they were trying not to call it one.

They sat cross-legged on an old sail canvas with the infant propped against their knee, feeding her from the flask's spout with the patience of someone who had learned that haste spilled more than it saved. Below them, the warehouse ticked and settled. Somewhere out in the harbor a bell buoy clanged its slow idiot rhythm. The child drank with an urgency that bordered on insult, small hands kneading at Orsen's sleeve as if the sleeve itself might produce supper.

"Slow down. Nobody's taking it from you."

Nobody is taking anything from you, Orsen thought, and heard how the sentence sounded, and hated themselves a little for how good it sounded.

She finished with a sigh that was frankly theatrical for a creature three months old at most, and fixed Orsen with the stare again.

"We have to discuss your name," Orsen said.

Because that was the decision. You couldn't keep calling her *the bundle*, or *the cargo*, or—God help them both—*little thief*, which was what Orsen had taken to muttering every time they palmed a wedge of cheese or a cup of milk off the tally-sheets. A nameless thing could be handed over, forgotten, filed away. A named thing was a different animal entirely. Orsen knew the arithmetic of names better than most; they'd watched the company reduce whole crews to column entries, watched men who'd been *Tomaso, who sang, who had a laugh like a door hinge* become *Lot 44, damaged in transit*. Names were the difference between a person and a line.

And still, God help them, Orsen wanted to give her one.

"Emrys," Orsen said, and stopped, because saying it aloud in the dark made it real.

Their mother had talked about the orchard the way other people talked about heaven. Orsen had been seven, maybe eight—old enough to remember, too young to have saved any of it but the words. She'd sit on the tenement steps after a diving run, still dripping, lungs rattling like a loose window in a gale, and she'd tell it: an orchard off the coast of some country she'd never named, drowned centuries ago when the sea took the low fields, and the trees still standing down there in the green dark, still bearing fruit every year, pale fruit that no one picked because no one could stay down long enough. Apples of moonlight, she called them. She said when you dove deep enough that the light went strange and silver, you could almost see them at the edge of everything—a whole orchard holding its breath under the water, patient, waiting for someone with better lungs.

*Emrys,* she'd said the place was called. She'd spelled it in the air with a wet finger, twice, because Orsen kept getting the middle wrong.

The sea took her two winters later. Took her the way the sea does—between one morning and the next, between one breath and the lack of it, off the pearl beds, with her last pearl still sewn into her sash because she refused, absolutely refused, to sell it while she lived. Father never said why. Orsen had stopped asking.

"Emrys," Orsen said again, to the child this time. "It's a drowned place. It shouldn't suit you. And yet."

The child kicked one leg, striking the flask, nearly upsetting it, and Orsen caught it and laughed before they could stop themselves—a real laugh, rusty, startling them both.

"Well. Clearly you have opinions. That settles it."

* * *

The trouble with a secret that breathes, Orsen reflected over the following weeks, is that it breathes audibly.

The loft above the rope store was tolerable by day—workers came and went on the main floor, pulleys screamed, crates slammed, and a baby's fussing drowned easily in all that. But nights were another matter. Nights the warehouse emptied, leaving one watchman, old Berrin, who did his rounds at the top of every hour and otherwise slept in his chair by the door with his hat over his face and a snore like a saw working green wood. Between rounds there was sometimes half an hour of quiet, and in half an hour of quiet, Emrys could go from sleeping angel to outraged duchess in the space of a single hiccup.

Orsen developed systems.

There was the shawl-wrap, learned from watching tenement mothers, that kept her snug and mostly silent. There was the walking circuit—forty paces the length of the loft, forty back, with just enough bounce at the turn to settle her—or else the sitting hold, her head against Orsen's chest, where apparently a heartbeat was persuasive company. There was the song, which was less a song than a hummed fragment of some pearl-diver's shanty their mother used to sing, all about a mermaid with expensive taste, and which Orsen hummed badly and privately and would have denied under oath.

And there was the milk problem, which was the problem that could hang them.

Company stores ran thin in winter even in good years, and the tally-clerks counted everything twice. But cows were kept at the yard behind the counting-house, and the dairy ledger, Orsen had discovered, was kept by a man named Pellow who was old, half-blind in low light, and constitutionally incapable of suspecting anyone whose collar was buttoned correctly. Orsen's collar was always buttoned correctly. Every evening, in the shuffle of the closing inventory, Orsen would carry past the dairy shelf a crate of something legitimate, and return carrying the same crate with a stoppered jug nested invisibly inside among the goods. Half a pint a night. Sometimes less. Never enough that a shelf looked touched.

"Carry little, want less," Orsen murmured to Emrys during one such transfer, tucking the jug into the canvas sling they'd rigged. "That's the whole philosophy. I want exactly one impossible thing now, which ruins the arithmetic, so don't tell anyone."

Emrys, wrapped and riding against Orsen's chest like contraband—which, in fairness, she was—gripped a fistful of shirt and drooled with tremendous dignity.

The near-discoveries accumulated the way debts do: slowly, then all at once.

There was the Tuesday the foreman sent Orsen up to the loft for the spare block-and-tackle an hour earlier than usual, and Orsen, heart slamming, reached the ladder first and spent ninety unbearable seconds above doing loud, unnecessary things with rope while below them Emrys woke and thought seriously about crying—and then, mercifully, decided instead to watch the swaying shadows with wide interest. Orsen descended with the tackle and legs like watered ink.

There was the afternoon Pellow himself wandered back to the rope store chasing a runaway invoice, and stood directly beneath the loft discussing freight rates with a junior clerk while Emrys, awake in her crate-nest, held perfectly, unnaturally still—as if she understood. Orsen, sorting rope two aisles away, had never believed in the child's intelligence more fervently, nor felt more doomed by it. Clever children, their father liked to say, are the Lord's joke on the poor.

And there was the night Berrin's round came early.

Orsen heard the boots on the stair with perhaps thirty seconds' warning—thirty seconds in which to smother a flask, tuck a child under canvas, arrange themselves innocently amid the coils. They managed the flask. They managed the canvas. What they could not manage was Emrys's fist, which had claimed a handful of Orsen's shirt-front and declined to release it, so that when Berrin's lantern swung up over the loft rail, what he saw was Orsen Vasquez, model indenture, sitting alone in the dark—leaning slightly, at an angle no spine would choose, toward an empty corner.

"Vasquez." Berrin squinted. "What in the harbormaster's name are you doing up here at this hour?"

"Inventory," Orsen said. "Rope doesn't count itself."

"Rope counts itself less at midnight than any hour I know." Berrin came two steps onto the loft, lantern raised, and Orsen felt the moment stretch like bad rigging under load. Under the canvas, very softly, Emrys made a sound—not a cry, thank every saint in the calendar, but a small sleepy grunt, the sound of a dream being rearranged.

Berrin paused. His eyes were not good. His ears, unfortunately, were excellent.

"What was that?"

"Rats," said Orsen, with the serene confidence of a liar of four weeks' standing. "Big ones, up here. Been meaning to report it."

Berrin considered this. Berrin considered rats the way other men considered the weather—one of life's established facts, somebody else's problem. He grunted, lowered the lantern, and delivered a short homily on the subject of reporting infestations to clerks who wouldn't listen, then shuffled back down the stair, his own ankles apparently more precious to him than any rat.

Orsen sat in the dark afterward for a long time, listening to their own pulse, feeling Emrys's grip slowly relax in sleep.

"That," they whispered, "was the last time I lie well. I'm not built for it. My face does things."

Her breathing evened out against their chest. Outside, the bell buoy clanged. Rain started somewhere over the harbor and came on across the roofs in a soft rush, and Orsen found, to their mild horror, that they were humming the mermaid song again, rocking slightly, entirely without instruction from the rational part of themselves.

*Dangerous*, the rational part observed. *This is what dangerous looks like. It looks like this exact moment.*

"I know," Orsen told it, and kept humming.

* * *

The trouble was that Orsen had spent nine years perfecting the art of not wanting things, and the art was failing.

Not-wanting was simple, really. You looked at the world the way you looked at cargo: everything weighed, everything temporary, nothing yours to keep. Wanting was how the company got its hooks in—you wanted a coat without patches, you wanted Sundays, you wanted your indenture shortened, and every want was a handle by which you could be lifted and carried wherever the ledger required. So Orsen carried little, wanted less, kept their eyes down and their hands quick and their heart, such as it was, locked in a strongbox somewhere behind the ribs.

And now here was this small tyrant in a rope loft, weighing perhaps eleven pounds, who had seized the strongbox key in her fat little fist along with everything else she'd grabbed, and Orsen could feel the wanting spreading through them like warmth through cold hands.

They caught themselves doing unforgivable things. Counting her fingers for the ninth time. Waking early to watch her sleep instead of sleeping, which any sensible person would call theft of the only property Orsen owned, namely rest. Saving, from the ruined ends of rope destined for the scrap bin, a soft unraveled strand to tie around one tiny wrist—because the strand was red-dyed at the core, and the red showed, and Orsen wanted her to have one thing in the world that was hers and not gray.

Worse: they had begun to imagine futures. This was the true corruption. For nine years Orsen's imagination had ended neatly at the close of each shift. Now it wandered. It wandered down the years to a girl running along the quay with the red string still on her wrist, grown tall, grown laughing, grown free—free, which was a word Orsen had long ago filed under *not for our kind* alongside *Sunday* and *enough*.

Freedom was arithmetic in Vellamare. Orsen knew that. Knew it the way you knew the tide tables—by hard memorization, by watching it ignore your preferences entirely. Their own indenture had seven years left on paper, and everyone knew the paper lied in the company's favor; fines appeared like barnacles, each one adding months. Seven years was a lifetime to keep a secret that breathed and grew and would, eventually, learn to talk.

"Seven years," Orsen told her one night, her asleep on their chest, the rain going soft on the roof. "By then you'll be asking questions. Who's this grumpy person, you'll say. Why do we live in a rope loft. And I'll say, hush, eat your stolen cheese."

She sighed in her sleep, deeply, like a dowager disagreeing with the menu.

But it was the mornings that undid Orsen most. Dawn coming gray through the loading doors, the harbor turning silver, and Emrys waking cheerful—cheerful, of all the unreasonable traits—with a gummy smile of such total, undeserved confidence that Orsen felt like a man handed a purse he knew must be stolen goods.

One morning in particular. The air had a bite to it; frost furred the edges of the high windows. Orsen fed her, changed her with the practiced speed of long practice at scandalized efficiency, and then, instead of settling her back in the nest, held her up to the window ledge where she could see the harbor—because lately she liked that, lately she tracked the gulls with her whole face, mouth open, as if personally auditing their flight.

"There," Orsen said, pointing with her own small hand wrapped in theirs. "Water. That's what took my mother, that water, so give it the respect of a healthy suspicion. And out past the point—" they hesitated, then finished it, because they had never said it aloud to anyone in eight years— "out past the point there's supposed to be an orchard. Underneath. Trees full of pale apples nobody picks. My mother swore it. She dove for pearls and she said the deep places keep the best things, and she'd know, because she was one."

Emrys grabbed Orsen's finger with the crushing, solemn grip of the very young, and stared at the sea, and gurgled.

"Yes," Orsen agreed. "Exactly."

Below, the loading doors rumbled—the day shift arriving, the world resuming its demands. Orsen tucked her into the nest, kissed the top of a head fine-fuzzed with dark hair (an act they would deny in any court), and climbed down into the daylight and the noise and the smell of tar and honest work, wearing their invisible face, carrying their enormous secret, lighter than they had ever been and more heavily burdened than they had ever been, both at once, which they were beginning to understand was simply what love weighed.

They had just clocked in—had just taken up their post at the tally desk, pencil ready, face arranged into its usual pleasant nothing—when they saw Mr. Quill coming across the warehouse floor with a ledger under his arm and his little eyes already fixed on Orsen like two pins finding cloth.

"Vasquez," said Quill pleasantly. "A word about your accounts."

Behind Quill, at the far end of the aisle, the dairy shelf sat in the lamplight. Orsen did not look at it. Orsen did not look anywhere at all.

"My accounts, sir," they said, and heard their own voice come out level, deferential, invisible—and understood, with a lurch, that the lying had only just begun.
