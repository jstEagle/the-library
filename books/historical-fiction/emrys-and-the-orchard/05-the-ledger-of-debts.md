## 5. The Ledger of Debts

There were three kinds of silence in the Halloway warehouse, and Orsen Vasquez knew all of them by heart.

The first was the morning quiet, before the doors rolled open—the hush of ropes and salt-stiff canvas, the creak of the building settling on its pilings like an old man finding a comfortable chair. The second was the working silence, which wasn't silent at all but was made entirely of useful noise: the knock of crate against crate, the tally-clerk's chalk, somebody always whistling badly near the tar vats. The third kind was the dangerous one.

The third kind came when Mr. Quill took off his coat.

Orsen learned it was happening before anyone told them. The dockhands stopped whistling. Tally moved from his usual slouch to something almost upright. Even the rats seemed to conduct themselves with more dignity along the top beams. Orsen was coiling hemp line in the west bay when the third silence arrived, rolling in ahead of its owner like weather off the harbor.

"Vasquez."

Orsen set down the coil, wiped both hands on their apron—twice, because Mr. Quill noticed things—and turned. "Sir."

Quill stood in the doorway between the counting office and the floor, holding a ledger under one arm the way another man might hold a sleeping cat: carefully, and with the certainty that it could turn on him at any moment. He was a narrow man, gray as unpolished pewter, with small quick eyes that had never once in Orsen's memory looked surprised. Surprise, Orsen suspected, would require first having been wrong about something, and Mr. Quill had arranged his life so that this never happened.

"Counting office," Quill said. "Now."

"Yes, sir."

Orsen followed him across the floor. Behind them, Tally caught Orsen's eye and mouthed something that was either *good luck* or *you're done for*, which in the Halloway warehouse amounted to much the same blessing.

* * *

The counting office smelled of ink, lamp oil, and the particular staleness of paper that had been argued over for years. A single window let in a bar of harbor light that fell across the desk like something laid out for inspection. Quill's clerk—a nervous boy called Fenwick whose collar was always too big for him—pulled out a chair for Orsen and then retreated to the corner as though hoping to be mistaken for furniture.

"Sit," said Quill.

Orsen sat.

The chair was the wrong height. Everything in the counting office was the wrong height for someone who had grown up hauling cargo instead of sitting behind it, and Orsen suspected this too was deliberate. You did not negotiate comfortably with a man whose feet didn't reach the floor.

"You are aware," Quill began, opening the ledger, "of the terms of your indenture."

"Nine years, sir. Entered at age nine." Orsen kept their eyes on a middle distance just above Quill's left shoulder. It was a trick they'd learned early: look attentive without looking defiant, respectful without looking interested. "Service ends at majority."

"Mm." Quill turned a page. The sound it made was very soft and very final, like ice shifting. "That is what you are *aware* of. What I have here—" he rotated the ledger so it faced Orsen, and tapped one gray fingertip against a column of figures, "—is what is *recorded*."

The column ran down the page in Quill's own cramped, immaculate hand. Each entry had a date, a fine, and a reason. Orsen read them slowly, because reading fast felt like flinching.

*Broke two crates. Fine: three months.*

Orsen remembered those crates. They had been broken by the crane operator, a drunk named Pellworthy who was Quill's cousin by marriage. Orsen had been standing underneath them.

*Mishandled silk. Fine: four months.* The silk had arrived water-damaged from Valencia. Everyone had seen the water damage. Everyone had signed the manifest saying so. Everyone except the person whose signature ended up on the correction.

*Unaccounted absence, one evening. Fine: six months.* That was the night Marisol had fever, and Father couldn't get up off the cot, and Orsen had run home through the rain and back again before dawn, arriving with wet boots and dry hands and exactly enough time to begin the morning shift.

*Insubordination.* No detail attached. Fine: eight months. Orsen could not, for the life of them, remember committing insubordination, which meant only that they had failed to notice doing it, which was somehow worse.

Page after page. The column went down, and down, and then turned over onto a second page, and a third, and each entry was three months or five months or eight months, and each one had been entered into the ledger in the same tidy hand within days of the offense, and not once—not once—in eleven years had anyone shown Orsen the arithmetic.

"Do you understand what you are looking at?" Quill asked.

Orsen did some quick, terrible math instead of answering. Three plus four plus six plus eight—

"I believe," Orsen said, and their voice came out level, which surprised them, "that my indenture now ends some years past my majority."

"Four years past," said Quill pleasantly. "At present count. There is also the matter of the lamp oil you requisitioned in excess during the winter of the flood year, the rope lost in transit under your watch, and—" he flipped a page with something close to fondness, "—an adjustment I made myself last Tuesday, for untidiness of your work station."

"Tidiness," Orsen repeated.

"This is a place of order, Vasquez." Quill closed the ledger and folded his hands on top of it, and for a moment he looked less like an auditor and more like a priest at the altar of a very small, very mean god. "Everything here is counted. Every hour, every length of rope, every breath of company air you've drawn since you were small enough to fit inside a shipping crate. And every debt carries interest." He smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which made it worse; it was the smile of a man showing you how the world worked, and being patient about it, because he genuinely believed you'd thank him eventually. "Your time is money. It has always been money. The only question ever in dispute is whose pocket the money sits in."

Fenwick, in the corner, made a sound like a swallowed cough and became extremely fascinated by the ceiling.

Orsen sat very still.

There was a moment—or there was in Orsen's chest, where moments sometimes went to be alone—a moment of pure white fury, hot and clean and rising like floodwater. Eleven years. Eleven years of carrying twice their weight, of learning every knot and tide table, of being *useful* precisely so that no one would ever look too closely, and the whole time the ledger had been growing quietly in the dark like mold behind a wall. Freedom hadn't been taken from them. It had never existed. It was arithmetic designed against them, sums run backward so that the answer was always more service, always more Orsen, forever and until further notice.

They thought, briefly and vividly, of tipping the desk over.

Then they thought of Father's lungs, and the physic he needed and could not afford, and Marisol's face when she talked about ships, and the fact that a debt-crippled indentured worker could still walk home at night, while a discharged troublemaker walked nowhere except into the gutter. Fury was a luxury. Orsen had never been able to afford luxuries.

So they did what they had trained themselves to do since the age of nine. They got smaller.

"I see, sir," Orsen said quietly.

"Do you?" Quill studied them, and those small quick eyes moved over Orsen's face like fingers checking a crate for weaknesses. "Most people, when shown their true accounts, become agitated. They raise their voices. They say foolish things about fairness, as though fairness were a currency accepted anywhere in this hemisphere." He leaned back. "You're not raising your voice, Vasquez."

"No, sir. Raising it wouldn't shorten the ledger."

Something flickered across Quill's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under a dock. Approval, possibly. Or disappointment. With Quill it was difficult to tell whether he preferred his subordinates clever or broken, or whether the ideal specimen was somehow both.

"Precisely," he said. "Which brings me to why we're speaking."

He rose, went to the window, and stood looking out at the harbor with his hands clasped behind his back, delivering his next words to the water rather than to Orsen, which Orsen understood was a courtesy in the way that being shot quickly was a courtesy.

"The company values diligence. The company rewards it. There is a position opening—cargo master's assistant, with a wage attached, and certain... adjustments available to one's ledger. Diligent service can be credited, Vasquez. Fines forgiven. Years struck through." He turned his head slightly, so that one gray eye found Orsen over his shoulder. "I keep my eye on the ones who keep their heads. You've kept yours remarkably well for a long time. That has not escaped me."

"Thank you, sir," said Orsen, in the tone of a person who has been handed a live coal and told it is a gift.

"You'll hear more soon. Dismissed."

Orsen stood, bowed the exact amount required—not a hair more—and walked to the door with their spine straight and their steps even and their hands perfectly, deliberately loose at their sides.

"Vasquez."

Orsen stopped. Did not turn. Turning eagerly was for dogs.

"One more thing." Quill's voice had changed—lighter, almost idle, which in the counting office was the sound of a trap being oiled. "The milk."

The word landed in the room and sat there.

"We go through forty measures a week," Quill continued, conversationally, examining his fingernails. "Forty, give or take. This week's delivery, per Fenwick's tally, shows forty-one ordered, thirty-nine received, and the dairy swears they sent the full amount." He turned around fully now, and his face was pleasant and empty as a locked box. "Shrinkage happens, of course. Rats. Spillage. Carelessness." A pause, measured to the drop. "Though I confess I've never known a rat that drank from a specific measure on a specific day of the week, week after week, always on the nights you close the west bay."

Somewhere behind Orsen's ribs, a small, hidden heart that was not their own nearly stopped.

But Orsen's face was a warehouse too, and nothing was stored where it could be seen. They allowed themselves one blink, one small frown of a man doing arithmetic, and said:

"I'll ask the night boys, sir. If someone's skimming, they'll slip."

"Mm," said Quill. "Do that." He returned to his ledger. "Rats are such a nuisance. One likes to catch them early. Before they grow."

"Yes, sir."

Orsen walked out of the counting office at a steady pace, down the stairs, across the floor, past Tally—who looked up, read everything and nothing in Orsen's face, and wisely said nothing—and out into the yard, where the afternoon light lay flat and ordinary on the crates, and gulls screamed over the roofs as though the world had not just rearranged itself.

Only when Orsen reached the shadow between the salt sheds did they stop, put one hand against the rough boards, and breathe.

*He doesn't know,* Orsen told themselves. *He suspects everything. That's different. Suspecting everything is simply his profession, the way weighing cargo is mine. A man who weighs everything trusts nothing, and a man who trusts nothing accuses nobody in particular.*

It helped. A little. In the way that bailing helped against a rising sea.

Because here was the thing Orsen now understood, standing in the salt-shed shadow with their heart still drumming: Quill had shown them the ledger today for a reason. Men like Quill did nothing for one reason. He had shown them the debt to demonstrate that Orsen belonged to him, body and hours and future, down to the last struck-through year—and then he had dangled forgiveness like bait on a hook, and then, just to complete the lesson, he had let one gray eye drift across the subject of the milk.

Three moves. Debt, reward, threat. Quill wasn't auditing an indenture. He was auditing *Orsen*—testing what they wanted, what they feared, and how much pressure each would take before something cracked.

And somewhere upstairs, in a rope loft that smelled of hemp and warm sleep, a small person who officially did not exist was waking up hungry.

*Before they grow,* Quill had said.

Orsen pushed off the shed wall, straightened their apron, and walked back toward the west bay wearing the face of a person thinking about rope.

Inside, beneath the calm, a new ledger had opened—one that no auditor would ever be permitted to see. And its first entry, written in something colder than ink, read:

*Mr. Quill. Owes: four years. Interest: compounding.*

Above the harbor, far out past the breakwater, the light was beginning to change. A wind had come around to the east, smelling wrong—of sickness and tar and distant, rotting things—and old divers like Father always said you could taste a bad season on the wind three days before the flags went up.

Orsen tasted it now, and did not yet know what it was.

But it was coming.
