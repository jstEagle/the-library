## 6. Plague Flags

The yellow flag went up on a Tuesday morning, and Orsen Vasquez learned about it the way they learned about most things—last, and from the sound of other people being afraid.

They were in the counting room, weighing bolts of Ondrish linen by hand, when the shouting started on the quay. Not the good kind of shouting, the kind that meant a catch had come in fat or a crate had gone overboard and someone's pay was about to be docked. This was the other kind. The flat, spreading kind. The kind that moved through a crowd like water through cloth.

Orsen set down the linen, brushed the lint from their palms, and did what they always did: made themselves small and went to look.

The *Cormorant's Grace* sat at anchor beyond the mole, riding low in the water, her decks crowded with passengers who were not being allowed to disembark. From her mainmast flew a square of yellow cloth so bright it looked like a wound against the grey sky. Even from the warehouse steps Orsen could read the message plain as an invoice: sickness aboard, keep away, we are alone out here.

"Well," said Tam Broadfoot beside them, a stevedore with forearms like mooring posts and a habit of stating the obvious with great reverence. "That's not good."

"No," Orsen agreed.

"Yellow flag means plague."

"It does."

"My cousin had plague once. Well—not plague plague. He said it was plague but my aunt says it was bad shellfish." Tam scratched his jaw. "Either way he turned colors I don't care to describe before breakfast."

Orsen wasn't listening anymore. They were watching the harbor office, where a runner had just gone in at a sprint, and watching Mr. Quill appear at the upper window of the company house with his ledger under his arm the way other men carried Bibles or weapons—which, in Quill's case, amounted to the same thing. Even at this distance, Orsen could see him smiling.

Quill was the company auditor, a narrow man assembled entirely of angles and arithmetic, who wore his hair slicked so flat it looked painted on. He smiled the way other men frowned: often, and to effect. Orsen had spent nine years learning which smiles cost what. This one, they suspected, would cost Vellamare dearly.

* * *

By noon, the harbor chain was up.

Orsen had seen it lowered exactly twice in their life—for festivals—and now watched it cranked into place by soldiers who wouldn't look at the fishermen shouting from the water. No ship in. No ship out. Every hull in the roadstead frozen where it floated, including the *Grace*, including her hundred-odd souls, including whatever she carried below decks besides passengers.

The chain was the harbor saying: *the world is closed.*

By one o'clock, the grain merchants had doubled their prices. By two, the chandlers had followed. By three there was a fistfight outside Pell's Provisioners over the last barrel of salt pork, and Pell himself stood in his doorway charging admission to watch. Orsen bought what they could carry—a sack of meal, dried cod, a twist of honey candy for Marisol that cost triple what it should have—and paid without blinking, because blinking was a luxury and they'd learned long ago to want less.

*Carry little, want less, be seen never.* It had been a good philosophy. It had kept them alive through two cholera scares, a dock fire, and their entire childhood. But standing in the provisioner's queue behind a woman quietly weeping because she couldn't afford flour anymore, Orsen felt the philosophy creak like old rigging. There was a new weight in the loft above the rope room. A small weight. Six pounds, give or take, as of last week's secret measuring against the grain scales after hours. And small weights changed all the arithmetic.

Emrys needed milk. Milk was about to become currency.

Orsen walked home through streets already transforming. Shutters closing. Preachers on corners announcing the end of days with the cheerful confidence of men who had been wrong about it every previous year and would be wrong again. A boy selling "plague charms" made of knotted string at four coppers each, doing brisk trade. The tenement district smelled of fear, which is to say, of too many people cooking too little food behind closed doors.

Their father was propped in his chair by the window, working to breathe the way other men worked at oars.

"There's my invisible child," he wheezed, pleased. "I heard the chain go up. Heard it from here. Like a bell with no tongue in it."

"It's quarantine," Orsen said, setting down the food. "A ship came in with the flag."

"Mm." Their father looked out the window at the harbor, at the masts standing idle like a forest struck dead. Forty years diving for pearls, and the sea had taken his lungs trade by trade, breath by breath, until now he paid for every word with three more. He spent them anyway. He always had. "Your mother used to say you could tell the weather by how the gulls sat. Plague weather, she'd have called this. Gulls all hunched up like disapproving grandmothers."

Marisol came in from the washroom with her sleeves rolled and her temper already rolled tighter. Nineteen years old and built entirely of plans, Marisol moved through the tenement like a woman walking through a room she intended to leave.

"They're saying it's the fever," she said. "The real one. The kind that empties quarters."

"They say everything," Orsen said.

"The chandlers doubled prices in one morning, Orsen. One morning. That's not rumor, that's arithmetic, and I can do arithmetic." She wrung out a rag and snapped it flat. "And there's worse talk. Down at the wash-house. They're saying the Halloway Company has berths reserved."

Orsen kept their hands very still on the meal sack.

"Reserved berths," Marisol repeated, turning. "Evacuation ships. Company families only. That's the talk. So I'm asking you, brother, since you're *inside*—" the word came out barbed, "—whether it's true."

"I weigh linen," Orsen said mildly. "Linen doesn't discuss shipping manifests with me."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That thing where you turn into a locked drawer." Marisol flung the rag onto the basin. "I know you know. You always know. You knew about the wage cut before Father did. You knew which clerk to bring the petition to. You know things, and you hoard them like pearls, and you hand them out drop by drop when it suits you, and I am *tired*, Orsen. I am tired of watching you be loyal to people who would ship us off this rock as ballast if it profited them."

"Marisol—" their father began.

"No. He needs to hear it." She crossed her arms. Her hands were red from the wash-water; she had been washing other people's linens since she was eleven so that Orsen's indenture fines could be paid down, though neither of them ever said so aloud. "You think if you're useful enough, quiet enough, they'll take care of you. That's not loyalty, it's hope wearing a work apron. And hope doesn't get berths, Orsen. Names on paper get berths. Bloodlines get berths. Whose name is on their lists? Tell me that."

Orsen looked at their sister—at the fury in her, which was only love that had nowhere to spend itself—and felt the truth settle in their chest like cold ballast: they didn't know whose names were on the lists. But they intended to find out.

"I'll find out," they said quietly.

Marisol blinked. Whatever she'd expected—the locked drawer again, the deflections—it hadn't been surrender.

"When?" she demanded.

"Soon."

She held their gaze a moment longer, then picked her rag back up, wrung it, and returned to the washing, which was as close to peace as the two of them had managed in years. Their father caught Orsen's eye on the way out and gave the smallest nod, the kind that said *well handled* and also *be careful*, because fathers could say two things at once with half a breath.

* * *

Back at the warehouse that evening, the rope loft was dim and smelled of hemp and dust and, faintly, of the small sour-milk sweetness that Orsen had spent two weeks pretending not to notice whenever anyone stood too close.

Emrys was awake, which was either wonderful or terrible depending entirely on whether she intended to be loud about it.

"Hush now," Orsen murmured, easing into the loft's blind corner where the lamplight from below couldn't reach. "Hush, little one. Quiet as cargo."

Emrys considered this. She was at the age—nearly five months—where she regarded the world with a sort of appalled fascination, and Orsen with a trust that terrified them more than any auditor. She grabbed a fistful of Orsen's shirt collar and pulled, and made a noise that was less a cry than a statement of intent.

"All right, all right." Orsen fished out the day's prize: a cup of milk bought from a dairymaid at the market gate, paid for double and accepted without question, because Orsen Vasquez buying milk was unremarkable, forgettable, a nothing transaction between nobodies. Invisibility had its uses. "Here. Before you wake the entire customs service."

Feeding an infant secretly in a rope loft during quarantine while the world outside priced itself into madness was, Orsen reflected, not a life anyone would have designed on purpose. And yet Emrys drank with such grave, single-minded determination, such tiny furious competence, that Orsen had to press their lips together to keep from laughing aloud, and the laughing feeling rose up through the fear like a bubble through deep water.

Below, voices. Orsen went stone-still, cup balanced, infant stilled mid-sip as if she too understood the rules.

"—berth lists posted tomorrow, that's what Rennick told me." Two clerks crossing the ground floor, lantern light sliding along the seams of the floorboards overhead. "Company families first. Favored lines. Then skilled men, maybe, if there's room."

"And the rest of Vellamare?"

"The rest of Vellamare has Vellamare, don't they?"

Laughter, fading. A door. Then nothing but the creak of the building settling and the far-off slap of harbor water against pilings.

Orsen let out a slow breath. Emrys finished her milk and then, with the impeccable timing available only to infants and cats, fell asleep against Orsen's chest, one small hand still knotted in their collar, holding on with the absolute confidence of someone who had never yet been dropped.

Orsen sat in the dark and thought about names on paper.

Tomorrow the lists would be posted, and Orsen Vasquez—nine years indentured, invisible, indispensable—would almost certainly not be on them. Marisol might be, conditionally, the way a tool is conditionally included in an inventory. Their father, whose lungs were worth less than the paper recording his debts, would not. And the six-pound weight asleep on their chest existed nowhere in any ledger in the building, which meant that as far as the Halloway Trading Company was concerned, Emrys could not be saved because Emrys had never existed at all.

Dread was an old acquaintance. Orsen knew its texture the way they knew wet rope. But tonight the dread did something new. Tonight it cooled, and hardened, and took an edge.

They looked down at the sleeping face they had been ordered to forget—the face they had instead memorized better than their own hands—and made the kind of promise that doesn't announce itself, doesn't swell with music, simply arrives fully formed the way a fact arrives. Not *I hope*. Not *if there's any way*. Just:

*You will be on a ship. Whatever it costs, whoever pays it—you will be on the water before the yellow flag comes down.*

Then Orsen laid the child gently in the padded crate that served as a cradle, climbed down into the lamplit ordinary world, and went looking for the manifest book that listed every berth the company controlled.

Finding it would be easy. Orsen knew exactly where Quill kept it.

Reading it unseen—that was tomorrow's problem.

And tomorrow, the lists would go up.
