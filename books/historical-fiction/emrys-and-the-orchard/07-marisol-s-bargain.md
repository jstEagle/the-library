## 7. Marisol's Bargain

Marisol had decided, somewhere between her father's third coughing fit and the fourth day of bread gone stale before she could even afford it fresh, that her brother was hiding something.

She was not a suspicious woman by nature. Suspicion cost energy, and energy was a currency the Vasquez family had never possessed in surplus. But she had grown up in a tenement where every secret had a smell—gin behind the water barrels, dice under the floorboards, a married man's cologne on her neighbor Petrona's shawls—and Orsen had started to smell like a secret. Not gin, not dice. Something sweeter.

Milk.

There was no milk in the house. There had been no milk since the quarantine flags went up and prices climbed like sailors up a rigging. Yet twice this week Orsen had come home with a wet stain blooming on the front of their coat, and when she'd asked about it, they'd looked at her with the particular blankness of a person who has spent nine years practicing not having a face.

"You spilled something," Marisol said.

"Oil," said Orsen.

"It smells like milk."

"Sweet oil."

"Orsen." She crossed her arms. "Nobody sells sweet oil that smells like milk and costs nothing, because you haven't spent a copper all week, I count your wages down to the farthing because *someone* in this family has to be good at sums—"

"I have to go back for the night shift," Orsen said, and kissed the top of her head as if that were an answer, which it was not, and left, which was worse.

Their father coughed from his corner—a long, wet sound like a rope being hauled through sand—and Marisol went to prop him up against the wall, and thought: *I am going to find out what my brother is carrying.*

* * *

It took her three days, because Orsen was careful, but Orsen had taught her everything they knew about being careful, and Marisol had always been a better student than teacher.

She followed them at a distance on the fourth evening, hood up, past the chandlers' row and the shuttered fish market, to the Halloway warehouse—that great gray beast of a building crouched at the harbor's edge like something that had crawled out of the sea and decided to stay and make money of it. She knew the building from stories. She knew it mostly from the way Orsen's shoulders changed shape whenever it came into view, growing both broader and smaller at once, as though the company took up room inside them.

There was a delivery door on the north side, half-hidden behind stacked crates of salt cod. Orsen glanced around—badly; they were a warehouse clerk, not a spy, and glancing around badly is one of the few honest things a person can do—and slipped inside.

Marisol counted to two hundred, then found the gap in the boards where the cod-smell escaped, and put her eye to it.

What she saw made her forget, for one full moment, every grievance she had ever logged against her brother, and Marisol kept grievances the way other people kept buttons: carefully, sorted by size.

Orsen sat on an overturned crate in some back corner among the coils of rope, holding a baby.

Not near a baby. Not minding a baby. *Holding* one, tucked against their chest in a sling made of sacking, rocking it with the awkward rhythm of a person who had learned to rock a baby by imitating the motion of waves and gotten it almost right. The baby had a fistful of Orsen's collar and a face like a furious red moon, and she was making a noise halfway between a hiccup and a complaint, and Orsen—her brother, who had once gone three days without speaking during a wage dispute out of pure stubbornness—was talking to her.

"—and *that*," Orsen was saying, in the low voice they used only for Father's fevers, "is why you never let go of the rope, no matter what anyone tells you about letting go of the rope. Are you listening? You are not listening. You have an entire hand in your own mouth."

The baby gurgled, triumphant.

"Yes," Orsen agreed gravely. "That's exactly what I said."

Marisol's first feeling was so large it didn't have a name. It had pieces of anger in it, and pieces of grief, and somewhere underneath, absurdly, a kind of fierce pride—as if her brother had finally done something with his whole heart instead of his careful hands, even if the whole heart was going to get them all hanged.

Because she could do the arithmetic too. A company baby meant a company crime. A company crime meant the magistrate, and the magistrate meant the docks, and the docks meant a rope, and ropes were the one commodity in Vellamare that never ran short.

She should have walked away. She should have gone home and waited and confronted Orsen like a sensible woman, over tea, with leverage.

Instead she tapped on the boards.

* * *

To give Orsen credit—and Marisol was trying, in these new strange minutes, to give Orsen credit—they did not scream. They went very still, the way a rabbit goes still, and then they crossed the room and opened the door with the expression of a person opening the door to their own execution and hoping to hurry the axeman along.

"Before you say anything," Orsen began.

"A *baby*, Orsen."

"Not loud. She's very quiet, actually, she's—"

"She is currently chewing your button with the focus of a woman who means to swallow it." Marisol stepped inside and shut the door behind her. Up close the baby was smaller than she'd looked through the crack, and lighter, and there was a raw look to her wrists where someone had tied off the swaddling too tight, and Marisol felt the large nameless feeling rearrange itself into something sharper and more useful. "Whose is she?"

"I don't know. A clerk left her. There was a note." Orsen's jaw did the thing it did when they were swallowing words. "'Forget the face.'"

"And instead you—" Marisol gestured at the rope loft, the stolen milk smell, the entire catastrophe of her brother. "You *kept* her. In a *warehouse*. During a *quarantine*."

"There wasn't anywhere else."

"There is never anywhere else! That is the entire condition of our lives!" Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that it cracked, because Orsen flinched, and flinching was supposed to be her weapon, not their wound. She breathed. She looked at the baby again, because looking at the baby was easier than looking at her brother. "How old?"

"Eight weeks. Perhaps nine."

"She's small for it."

"They're all small for it, I think. I asked the fishwife who sells me the milk and she said hers were all small for it too."

"You *asked the fishwife*—"

"I asked her nothing! I bought milk and listened to her talk, which is different, and she thinks I have a sick wife at home, which is also different."

For a moment—absurdly, horribly—they almost laughed, both of them, the way they had laughed as children at funerals and evictions, and the baby chose that moment to spit the button out and fix Marisol with a stare of such frank, unblinking appraisal that Marisol felt thoroughly assessed and found insufficient.

"What's her name?" she asked, though she already knew, from the set of Orsen's mouth, that there was one.

"Emrys."

"The orchard." Marisol blinked. "The drowned one. Mother's story." Their mother had died diving when Marisol was four, and all Marisol had left of her was a handful of borrowed memories and one story, told so often by their father that it had worn smooth as a sea glass: the orchard beneath the water, trees still standing in the green dark, apples that divers were forbidden to pick because the drowned kept their harvest. "You gave her Mother's story for a name."

"It was the truest thing I had to give her."

And there it was. That was the trouble with Orsen, Marisol thought, and always had been. They carried everything so quietly that when something real finally fell out of them, it landed like an anchor.

* * *

Here is what Marisol had learned in twenty-two years of the tenement: love does not survive scarcity. Love gets rationed like everything else, cut thinner each year, until one day you look across a table at your own blood and feel mostly arithmetic.

But watching Orsen tuck Emrys back into the sling with those careful hands—hands that could weigh cargo to the ounce without scales—she felt something in her chest refuse the arithmetic. Which annoyed her considerably.

So she did what she always did with feelings too large to hold: she turned them into terms.

"I want passage," she said.

Orsen looked up.

"There are berth lists, Orsen. Don't look surprised—I work laundry for company wives. They gossip like kettles. There's an evacuation coming, favored families only, and my name isn't anywhere on anything except a rent ledger and a death register waiting to be filled in." She held up a finger before Orsen could speak. "I'm not asking how you know about ships. I'm telling you what I want. When you run whatever scheme this becomes—and it will become a scheme, I've seen your face do sums—you put me on a ship. That's my price. That's all of it."

"And you'll keep her secret."

"I'll do more than keep it." Marisol pulled off her shawl and sat down on the crate Orsen had vacated, and held out her arms for the baby with the confidence of a woman who had raised half the tenement's children and buried two of her own before they drew breath. Emrys landed in her arms, considered her, and grabbed her braid with both fists, which Marisol chose to interpret as approval. "I'll help. Feedings, cover stories, washing. I'll learn the guard rotations if I have to. But I'm not doing it out of the goodness of my heart, because my heart hasn't been good since I was nineteen and it stopped being fed regularly. I'm doing it because we're partners now, Orsen, and partners trade."

Something moved across her brother's face—not hurt, exactly. Recognition. Marisol had learned her hardness somewhere, after all, and she watched Orsen recognize the source of it, the way you recognize your own handwriting in someone else's letter.

"That's fair," Orsen said quietly.

"It is entirely unfair," Marisol said. "That's what makes it fair. Fairness in this city is just unfairness split properly." She bounced the baby, who made a sound like a small bell. "One more term. If it comes to it—if it comes to a pier, a boat, a choice—she goes first. Before me. Before you. Before Father, though God forgive me for saying it, because Father would say it himself."

"Marisol—"

"She's the only one of us who hasn't been owned by somebody yet." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "Let's keep it that way as long as the world allows."

Orsen was silent a long moment. Out beyond the walls, the harbor bells rang the quarantine hour, flat and slow, like a heartbeat heard through water.

"Partners, then," Orsen said at last, and put out their hand, and Marisol shook it, and it was done: an alliance built on leverage and sealed over a stolen bundle, which was more than most marriages in Vellamare were built on.

"Now," she said, settling Emrys against her shoulder, "you're going to tell me everything. Where the milk comes from, where the note went, who else knows, and what in the name of the drowned you intend to do in six weeks when she has teeth and opinions."

"She already has opinions."

"Then you'd best start telling me how you plan to survive them."

They talked until the lamp burned low—Orsen in their flat careful voice, Marisol interrupting with questions that got harder as the night went on—and it was nearly dawn when Marisol, gathering her shawl to slip home before the streets woke, paused at the door.

"One thing more," she said. "The auditor. Quill. The company wives say he counts everything. Sugar lumps. Candle stubs. *People.*"

"I know," said Orsen. "He audited my indenture last month. He knows my debts to the farthing."

"Yes, well." Marisol pulled up her hood. "I've met men like that in laundries. Men who count. And I'll tell you what counting men always know, Orsen, free of charge: they know when something's missing from the ledger long before they say so. They're not blind. They're *waiting*."

She left then, with the baby's warmth still ghosting her shoulder and her brother's silence following her out the door, and she did not see—or perhaps, being Marisol, she saw perfectly well and filed it away—the figure at the end of the lane in a company coat, standing beneath the quarantine flag with a little notebook in his hands, writing something down.
