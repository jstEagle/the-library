## 8. Quill's Eye

There were three kinds of watching in the Halloway warehouse, and Orsen Vasquez had catalogued them the way other people catalogued constellations.

The first kind was the foreman's glance—broad, incurious, a man counting heads the way he counted barrels, satisfied so long as nothing appeared to be missing. The second kind was the dockhands' sideways attention, which had nothing to do with work and everything to do with who owed whom a drink. The third kind—the dangerous kind—was Mr. Quill's.

Quill did not watch with his eyes. That was the trick of it. He watched with his ledger.

Orsen learned this on a gray Thursday when the auditor appeared in the counting room doorway holding a sheaf of paper as though it were a wounded bird he intended to question. Quill was not a large man. He had the build of a ruled line—thin, straight, and ruled by someone else—and his eyes were the pale washed-out blue of a receipt that had been through the laundry. He wore his coat buttoned to the throat even in the warehouse heat, as if perspiration were a form of theft.

"Vasquez," he said, in the tone of a man confirming an entry rather than greeting a person.

"Mr. Quill." Orsen set down the crate of indigo they'd been weighing and gave him the particular bow reserved for men whose pens could shorten your life. "Something I can carry for you?"

"You can answer for something that isn't being carried." Quill stepped closer, boots precise on the boards. "Milk."

For one heartbeat—one long, cold heartbeat in which Orsen's entire future presented itself for review—Orsen felt nothing at all, which was the old survival trick: be empty, be smooth, be a crate with no markings. Then the training took over, and what rose to their face instead was mild, honest confusion.

"Milk, sir?"

"The company purchases milk for the clerks' tea. Four pails a week from the Aldous farm. Dock records show deliveries on Tuesday and Friday. The kitchen shows consumption of—" Quill consulted his paper, though Orsen would have bet the whole warehouse he knew the figure by heart— "considerably less than four pails' worth of tea. Someone is carrying it off before it reaches the pot."

* * *

Here is what Orsen had done, and how badly they had done it.

Emrys was seven months old now, which meant her appetite had outgrown what a bribed kitchen girl could skim unnoticed. Orsen had been taking more. Taking it earlier. Taking it with the confidence of a man who has crossed the same tightrope so many times he's stopped looking down. Two nights past, they had walked out of the dairy alcove with a full pail under their coat just as Tam Weller turned the corner with a lantern, and Orsen had converted the moment into a yawn and a remark about the weather, and Tam had laughed and moved along—but Tam had *seen*, or half-seen, and half-seen things travel to Mr. Quill the way water travels to the sea.

Orsen had known this reckoning would come. What Orsen had not expected was its shape.

"I don't take milk, sir," Orsen said now, and heard their own voice do something new. It stayed level. It stayed soft. It carried precisely the weight of a man mildly inconvenienced by an absurd accusation. "Can't abide it. Never could. My sister will tell you—I'd give my portion away as a boy, and she'd scold me for it."

Quill studied them. It was like being studied by an abacus.

"The records suggest otherwise."

"Records also suggested my indenture ended two years ago, sir, and yet here I am." Orsen said it gently, almost kindly, the way you'd hand back a dropped coin—and watched something flicker behind those laundry-blue eyes. It was a reckless thing to say. It was also, Orsen realized with a strange lurch, exactly the right thing to say, because it reminded Quill that Orsen knew about the arithmetic, and that knowing made Orsen either a threat or a candidate.

The silence stretched. Somewhere beyond the wall, a crane groaned through the fog, hauling cargo nobody would ever be able to afford.

"Walk with me," Quill said.

* * *

They walked the length of the upper gallery, above the warehouse floor, where the crates below looked like a city seen from a cliff—streets of tea and silk and salt, all of it owned by men who had never lifted any of it. Quill walked slowly, hands folded behind him, and let the silence do his talking until it became unbearable enough that a lesser man would have filled it with excuses.

Orsen filled it with nothing. Carry little. Want less. Be invisible.

"You've been with us eleven years," Quill said at last. "By my figures, nine."

"Figures differ, sir."

"Figures are the only things in this world that don't lie, Vasquez. Men lie around them the way rivers lie around stones—the water moves, the stone stays put." He stopped at the gallery rail and looked down at the floor, and Orsen watched him locate, without appearing to look, the rope loft hatch. "A man in your position has two roads. He can keep being a stone, and be ground down by the river until there's nothing left but sand. Or he can learn to move with the water."

"Is that a promotion offer, sir? Only I've never been promoted before, and I'd hate to miss the feeling."

It was a joke. A small, warm, human joke, the kind Orsen had spent eleven years never making in front of auditors—and Quill did not laugh, but he did something worse. His mouth bent at one corner, very slightly, the way paper bends before it tears.

"There is a berth list," Quill said.

The fog seemed to press closer against the gallery windows.

"Plague flags went up on the Marisol-bound lanes yesterday, as I'm sure you know. The harbor will close within the fortnight. When it does, the company evacuates its personnel. Essential personnel. Clerks, factors, their families." He turned those pale eyes on Orsen, and for the first time they contained something warmer than arithmetic—a heat, low and banked, like a stove left deliberately lit. "There is a third road, Vasquez. Between stone and sand. The road of the man who becomes useful in a new way. Who notices things. Milk, for instance. Who takes it, where it goes, what else leaves this warehouse by unmarked doors. There are men in this company—men above me—who would pay a great deal for eyes like yours. And they reward good eyes with paper. Berth paper. For a man, his sister, and—"

He paused. Just a pause. Half a breath. A door opened a crack and shut again.

"—whoever else constitutes family these days."

* * *

Later, Orsen could not have said how they got through the rest of the day. Their hands weighed indigo and counted bolts and signed chits, and all the while some quiet clerk in the back office of their mind was screaming.

*He knows.* Not certainly—not proof, not yet—but knows the way you know weather coming off the water: pressure in the joints, taste of copper. Quill had dangled a berth and a name Orsen had never spoken aloud in the same sentence, and then watched to see which way the eyes went. Orsen's eyes had gone nowhere. Orsen's eyes had gone nowhere because for eleven years Orsen's eyes had gone nowhere, and that habit—that beautiful, suffocating, survival-bought habit of perfect blankness—had saved them.

But it had cost something too. Walking home through the tenement streets, past shuttered shops and a fishwife arguing prices with a woman whose face said she hadn't eaten in two days, Orsen felt the shape of the temptation settling into them the way rain settles into cloth. Not dramatic. Not evil. Just *reasonable*. That was the horror of it. Quill wasn't offering wickedness; he was offering sense. Inform. Point. Name the men who pilfer and the women who smuggle and the dockhands who skim, most of whom were only doing what Orsen did—taking small things from a company that had taken everything—and in exchange, paper. A berth. Marisol on the water. Emrys—

And there it was. The trap wasn't the informing. The trap was that every road Orsen could imagine led through someone else's ruin, and Quill had simply offered to pave the shortest one.

At the tenement, their father was asleep sitting up, which was how his lungs preferred him now, each breath arriving like a laborer late to work. Marisol was mending a sail-cloth jacket by tallow light, and she looked up and read Orsen's face in three seconds flat, because sisters are better auditors than Quill would ever be.

"What did he want?"

"How do you know it was a he and a he wanted anything?"

"Because you've got your honest face on," Marisol said, threading her needle, "and you only wear that after somebody's tried to buy you."

Orsen sat down heavily on the crate that served as a second chair. "He offered a berth. All of us. For names." A pause. "He mentioned the baby, Mari. Didn't say the word. But he said it."

Marisol's needle stopped. For a moment the tallow flame was the loudest thing in the room.

Then she set down the jacket, and her face—her dear, sharp, hungry face—did something Orsen had feared and understood for years: it began, quietly, to calculate.

"Ors. Listen to me. Names aren't souls. The men he wants—they're thieves the way we're thieves, yes, but they're not *family*, are they?" Her voice was low and urgent and terribly sensible. "You give him five names, none of them ours, and Emrys sleeps on a ship instead of a rope loft. You give him nothing, and you're gambling her lungs against strangers' freedom. Tell me the arithmetic favors mercy. Tell me, and I'll believe you. I'll even try."

"I need to think."

"You always need to think. It's your best quality and your worst one." She picked the needle back up, and softer: "Think fast, Ors. The harbor closes in a fortnight, and that man doesn't bait hooks for fun."

* * *

That night, in the rope loft, Orsen fed Emrys by the light of a stolen candle stub, and watched her grip one finger with her whole small fist as though the finger were property she intended to hold in perpetuity.

"He knows about you," Orsen told her gravely. "Or nearly. Which means you, little orchard, are currently worth three berths, and I am currently worth nothing, and between us we make a poor bargain for everybody except Mr. Quill."

Emrys blew a bubble of milk and regarded Orsen with the serene contempt of the well-fed.

"Yeah," Orsen agreed. "I don't like his terms either."

They should have slept. Instead Orsen lay in the rope-dark running the ledger Quill had handed them, adding columns, and discovered somewhere past midnight that they had begun—without deciding it—to compose lies. Not the old lies, the small gray lies of invisibility, the *no sir, can't abide milk* lies. New ones. Elaborate ones. Lies with architecture. If Quill pressed, Orsen could tell him about pilferage in the west annex, plausible, deniable, pointing at no living soul—or at one soul, a dead-end name, Tam Weller maybe, no, not Tam, Tam had a daughter—

Orsen caught themselves pricing people. Weighing them by hand, the way the company had taught them to weigh cargo, and finding that some heads were heavier than others, and hating the scale, and continuing to use it anyway, because Emrys stirred in her sleep and needed feeding again and the harbor was closing and the fortnight was already bleeding down to days.

Corruption, it turned out, did not arrive like a storm. It arrived like a clerk: punctual, polite, offering you a pen.

In the morning there was a note folded into Orsen's time-sheet, written in a hand so fine it might have been engraved:

*My office. Eleven o'clock. Bring your answer—or bring me something better than one.*

Below it, in the same precise script, a single line that had not been in the spoken offer, and that changed the weight of everything:

*I will expect news from Pier Nine.*
