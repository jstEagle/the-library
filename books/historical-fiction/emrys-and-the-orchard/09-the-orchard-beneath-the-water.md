## 9. The Orchard Beneath the Water

The fever had been on Father for three days when it broke—or rather, when it changed shape. Marisol described it first: "He's not raving anymore, but he's talking like a man at the bottom of a well who's finally decided the well is worth describing." She said this with her arms crossed and her jaw set, which was how she said most things lately, but there was worry underneath it, the way there was salt underneath seawater.

Orsen came after the evening shift, carrying what could be carried without comment: a heel of bread, a twist of dried figs, a stopper of the cheap barley spirits that Halloway's sold its dockhands at prices designed to keep them grateful for the privilege of buying it. The tenement stairs still smelled of low tide and boiled cabbage, and the fourth step still groaned like an old man asked to rise, and Orsen still stepped over it out of habit, though these days Father's own groans made the stair's seem companionable.

The room at the top was small enough that Orsen knew every inch of it by feel: the drip in the corner that never quite froze even in winter, the loose brick where their mother's one surviving shell had been hidden for years until Marisol found it and wore it, the bed that took up nearly half the floor and now held their father propped against pillows gone thin as communion wafers.

"Orsen," Father said, and his voice was clear. That alone frightened Orsen more than the raving had. Fever-haze voices you could dismiss. Clear ones meant the man beneath the fever had come back to make decisions.

"I'm here."

"Sit. Sit, sit. You stand like a clerk. Clerk's spine." He waved a hand that trembled but did not wander. "Your sister has been feeding me lies about how much food costs so I'll eat less of it. Tell her the bread is stale and she may have my share, if it makes her feel generous."

"It's fresh," Orsen said.

"Then lie anyway. Kindness is mostly lying done gently." But he took the bread, and ate some of it with a deliberateness that seemed almost ceremonial, and looked out the one window, where the harbor lay flat and pewter-colored under the last light.

"The yellow flag went up today," Orsen said carefully. They hadn't meant to say it. It came out the way confessions do, sideways, while the mouth was busy with something else.

Father nodded slowly, as if he'd ordered it himself. "Third time in my life. When I was a boy they burned the fishing fleet. My grandfather stood on the quay and wept for boats. Not people—boats. He'd lost two sons to the sea by then and cried only for the boats." A pause. "I used to think that was terrible. Now I think maybe he just knew what he could afford to grieve."

"Father—"

"You're going to ask me about your mother."

It wasn't a question, but Orsen answered it as one. "No."

"Liar," he said fondly. "You've been asking since you were six. You just do it silently, with your whole face. Your mother did that too. The silent questions. Drove the pearl brokers mad—they liked buyers who begged." He shifted against the pillows, and winced, and continued through the wince as though pain were a toll he'd long ago stopped disputing. "Marisol got the loud questions from me. Balance in all things."

From the corner, Marisol, who had been mending the same sock for an hour without making a stitch, said: "I'm going to fetch water. Which is code, apparently, for leaving you two alone, because everyone in this family thinks grief works better without witnesses." She set down the sock, kissed the top of Father's head, squeezed Orsen's shoulder hard enough to mean something by it, and was gone down the groaning stairs.

* * *

When she was out of earshot, Father patted the edge of the bed, and Orsen sat, and for a moment neither spoke. Below them the tenement performed its evening orchestra—a baby crying two floors down, a couple arguing amiably about cardamom, someone practicing a fiddle badly enough that it circled around to being moving.

"She grew up on the east shore," Father began, in the voice he saved for stories, which was lower than his speaking voice and slower, as though words were pearls he was stringing one at a time and didn't dare drop. "Before the seawall. Back when the orchard district ran all the way down to the waterline, before the harbor masters decided the sea needed managing."

"The drowned orchard," Orsen said quietly. They had heard fragments their whole life—enough to know the shape of the story but never the whole of it. Their mother had told it once, they understood, in full, the night before she died, and Father had kept it like a diver keeps air.

"The drowned orchard." He smiled at the ceiling. "There was a flood when she was a girl—the great wash, the old people called it—and the water came up over the terraces and took the low fields and the pear trees and half a village, and when it receded, the orchard stayed under. Sixty feet down, right off what's now the shipping channel. On a still day, the old men swore, you could look over the rail of a boat and see the trees. Standing up straight. Still bearing, some of them, in their way—white ghost-blossoms in spring, drifting up like snow falling the wrong direction."

"That's not possible."

"Of course it's not possible. But she saw it, and I believed her, and that's the whole difference between her life and mine. I believed what was possible and it drowned me slowly. She believed what she saw and it killed her quickly, and honestly, Orsen—" he turned his head, and his eyes were fever-bright but level, "—honestly, I've never been certain hers was the worse bargain."

Orsen said nothing. The fiddler below had given up, or died; the silence felt earned.

"She dove for pearls. Not the way the company divers dive, with their suits and their lines and their ledgers—she dove free. Breath and stubbornness. Her father had been an orchard man; the water took the trees and gave him nothing, so she went down to visit what it had taken and came back up with pearls in her cheeks like a hamster, grinning." He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and Orsen held the cup to his mouth, and when he'd recovered he waved off sympathy with two fingers. "She claimed the orchard paid rent. That the trees sent her gifts for visiting. The brokers said the beds off the old district were exhausted—picked clean for a generation—but she always found them. Always. I dove beside her for five years and never matched her take. She'd surface, spit out her mouthpiece, and say, *They like me better. I'm fruit folk.*"

"What was her name?" Orsen asked, as they had asked a hundred times in silence.

And Father, for the hundredth time in Orsen's memory, did not quite answer. He looked out at the darkening harbor instead, and his face did something complicated, and he said: "Her name isn't the part you need. Names are handles. What you need is the pearl."

* * *

He told it then, all of it, in the order he'd kept locked for twenty years, and Orsen understood suddenly why he'd never told it sober, or healthy, or in daylight: because told properly, it required him to be honest about himself, and honesty was a cold swim he'd avoided his whole life.

"There was one pearl she wouldn't sell. Every diver has one—it's practically doctrine, like sailors' tattoos. But hers wasn't superstition. She found it the year we married, deep under the orchard, wedged in the roots of the biggest tree. Big as a quail's egg, gray-green, with a light inside it like the moon behind cloud. Brokers offered her sums that would have bought us this building and the one next to it. She laughed at them. Offered more. Laughed harder." His hands worked on the blanket. "I begged her, near the end. We had you coming, and no savings, and my lungs were already starting their long betrayal, and I stood in this room—I think this very room—and told her a pearl was a pearl and love was love and one could be converted into the other at fair market rate."

He stopped. Orsen waited. Somewhere below, the cardamom argument had resolved into reconciliation, evidenced by silence.

"She looked at me the way you look at weather," Father said. "Like something happening to other people. And she said: *It isn't mine to sell. It's the orchard's. I'm only holding it.* And I said, *The orchard is sixty feet underwater and doesn't have pockets,* and she said—and Orsen, write this somewhere inside yourself, because it is the only inheritance I have to give you besides debt—she said: *"*

He closed his eyes to get it exactly right.

"*Everything the water takes, it keeps safe. Everything it keeps safe, somebody has to remember how to find.*"

* * *

"She died that autumn," he went on, quieter. "A cold snap, a long bottom, a cramp. The men on the boat said she surfaced twice and went down a third time on purpose, which nobody believed, because going down on purpose is madness. Except I believed it. Because the season before, she'd shown me where she kept it—not the pearl, the *place*—told me if anything happened, it belonged to the orchard and I should put it back. Put it *back*, Orsen. Twenty years of brokers' offers, and her dying wish was a refund."

"Did you?"

"I did not." His voice cracked, and he let it. "I kept it. Through the worst years, when you were indentured and I couldn't pay the fines and Marisol had rickets, I kept it in the loose brick and told myself I was keeping it *for* her. And then one day I realized I was just a man guarding a thing he was too frightened either to sell or to return, and that fear is not the same as faith, whatever the priests say." He turned his head and looked at Orsen directly, and there was something in the look that made Orsen's skin prickle, some appraisal, some weighing—cargo-weighted, hand-balanced, the family trade. "It's in the brick. Has been since the day your mother's shell came out of it and onto Marisol's neck. I'm telling you where it is not because I want you to sell it. I don't know what I want. I'm telling you because a man drowning should probably mention the boat he saw."

"Father—"

"And the other thing. The important thing." He gathered himself; Orsen watched him do it, watched the fever part around his will like water around a diver's blade. "Whatever happens with this plague, with the ships, with the company—you will be told, soon and often, that survival means becoming useful to the people who own the boats. Your mother's answer to that was to be useful to the trees instead. Trees can't pay, but they also don't fine you." He smiled, small and crooked. "I was too frightened to live by that. Don't inherit my fright. Inherit hers. It's worth more."

Orsen sat very still. In the pocket of their coat, against their heart, was the folded note from the rope loft—*forget the face*—which they carried now everywhere, the way sinners carry scripture they intend to break. They thought of Emrys asleep in the loft above a warehouse owned by men who weighed everything and valued nothing, and thought: *everything the water takes, it keeps safe.*

"I have to tell you something," Orsen started—

But the door banged open and Marisol came up two steps at a time with a face like weather of a different kind entirely. "The company criers are out," she said, breathless. "At the fountain, at the fish market, everywhere. They're reading names off a paper. Evacuation berths." She stopped in the doorway, chest heaving, eyes going from Father to Orsen and back. "They start with the clerks' families. Orsen—they posted the *list*."

Father sank back against his pillows, and the fever-light in his eyes banked itself down to embers, and he said, to the ceiling, in a voice almost too soft to hear:

"Well then. Let's find out what the trees left us."
