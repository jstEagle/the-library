## 10. Berths for the Faithful

The list went up on the warehouse door at half past seven in the morning, which was early even by the standards of men who enjoyed other people's suffering.

Orsen was there because Orsen was always there—first through the loading gate, hands already smelling of tar and hemp, ready to be useful and unseen. They had learned long ago that the first hour of the day belonged to whoever wanted it least, and so they took it, every morning, without complaint. It made them valuable in the way a good scale is valuable: reliable, quiet, and never suspected of wanting anything.

The clerk who nailed up the paper was a boy named Fenwick, seventeen at most, with soft hands and a nail apron he wore like a decoration for valor. He hammered four times, stepped back, admired his work as though he had composed it rather than copied it, and then walked off whistling toward the counting house.

A small crowd gathered. Dockhands, tally-men, two of the salt-women from the curing sheds who still smelled of brine at that hour. Nobody spoke. In Vellamare, reading was common enough among working people—the companies preferred it that way; it saved them paying clerks to explain things—but nobody read aloud.

Orsen read.

The heading was written in Mr. Quill's precise, upright hand: *Approved Passengers — Halloway Charter Evacuation, First Sailing.*

Below it, names. Not many names. Orsen counted twice, because the first count seemed like an insult and the second confirmed it: forty-one names for a ship rated to carry two hundred souls, and every one of those forty-one belonged either to a clerk with a ledger under his arm or to a family whose surname appeared somewhere in the company's founding papers like a watermark.

*Halloway. Halloway-Prentiss. Marchmont. Marchmont-Cole. Quill.* Even Quill had listed himself, though Quill would sooner drown than admit his own mother to a lifeboat unless she signed something first. *Vasquez* appeared nowhere.

That was expected. Expected did not mean painless. There is a particular kind of arithmetic the heart performs when it reads a list and finds its own name missing—a quick, involuntary sum, as if checking whether maybe, just maybe, one's existence had been entered somewhere after all, in some column too faint to see. The answer came back the same both times: nothing. Zero. A name absent is a name erased, and Orsen stood in the cold morning light feeling precisely as visible as fog.

They should have walked away. Instead they kept standing there, doing what they always did—memorizing faces. The salt-women's set jaws. The dockhand Pell, who had a striker's pamphlet folded in his coat pocket, looking at the list the way a man looks at a debt collector. And Marisol—

Marisol was not there yet. But she would come. She came every morning now, ever since the plague flags, hovering near the gate with her shawl pulled tight, asking in her careful company-clerk voice about *passenger manifests, third-party endorsements, family provisions*. Asking, always, in a tone that suggested she already knew the answers and found them disappointing.

* * *

She arrived while Orsen was pretending to inventory rope coils three feet from the door.

"Have you seen it?" she asked, low.

"I've seen it."

"And?"

"You're on it."

Marisol went very still. Then, in the voice of someone stepping onto ice and finding it held: "I'm on it."

"*Conditionally,*" Orsen said, because lying to her had never once worked and honesty between siblings is a knife you hand over handle-first. "Read the annotation."

She leaned past them. Orsen watched her lips move through the line beneath her name:

*Marisol Vasquez — approved, contingent upon continued satisfactory service of record. Sponsor: Q.*

"Sponsor: Q," Marisol repeated. "Quill sponsors me." She said it the way one says *the rat sponsors me*, but quieter, because the salt-women were listening with their whole bodies. "What does he want, Orsen? What does 'continued satisfactory service' mean when I don't serve him? When none of us serve him?"

"It means he wants something," Orsen said. "Quill doesn't spend ink without collecting interest."

Marisol's hand found the paper's edge, as if she could pull her own name closer by force. "And you're not here at all."

"No."

"Not even conditionally."

"Not even as a footnote."

For a moment her face did something complicated—guilt and relief wrestling in the same small space, each accusing the other of trespass. Orsen saw it and felt, absurdly, protective of it. Their sister had spent years resenting them for their caution, their bowed head, their company loyalty worn like a second indenture. Now the company had rewarded that loyalty with absence, and Marisol's resentment had nowhere left to stand, and she looked almost embarrassed by it.

"There has to be an appeal process," she said finally.

"There isn't. There's a petition box outside the counting house. Last quarantine, three hundred petitions went in and a man came out with a wheelbarrow and emptied them into the harbor. The fish have been unusually well-informed ever since."

That startled a laugh out of her, brief and guilty, the laugh she'd had as a girl before scarcity taught her to ration even that. Then it died. "Emrys," she said, very quietly, barely moving her lips.

"I know."

"She's not on any list. Babies aren't on lists unless they have bloodlines. She doesn't have a bloodline. She has—" Marisol stopped herself, glanced around, finished with: "—she has us."

"She has us," Orsen agreed, and picked up the rope coil as if inventory had suddenly become urgent, which, in a way, it had. "And we're going to need more than a sponsor's initial to move her."

* * *

The machinery of the Halloway Trading Company revealed itself most clearly not in its ledgers but in its doorways, and the doorway outside the counting house had become, overnight, a kind of altar. By noon there was a queue to read the list—people who had already read it, queuing to read it again, hoping the paper might have grown additional mercies in the interval. Paper rarely does.

Orsen carried a crate of brass fittings past the queue, slow enough to hear everything, fast enough to be accused of nothing.

"—Marchmont-Cole got *two* berths, and she married in, she's not even born blood—"

"—Quill's cousin's boy, the one who can't add, they gave him a berth and my Tomas has tallied for eleven years—"

"—my father drove pearls for this company thirty years and my name's worth less than a clerk's inkwell—"

Voices, all the voices, the great murmuring loom of grievance weaving itself thread by thread. Orsen catalogued it the way they catalogued everything: Pell, angry. Salt-woman Bren and her sister, angry. The tally-man Corvo, angriest of all, because Corvo had once believed himself favored—he dined with junior clerks, he owned a coat with actual buttons—and discovering one's favor was fictional hurt worse than never having any.

Inside the counting house, through the open transom, Orsen heard the true machinery running smooth and oiled. Quill's voice, unhurried, pleasant, terrible:

"Mrs. Marchmont-Cole, your second berth is of course honored, we simply require the amended signature page... no, the *third* page... Ah. Master Aldren, your apprenticeship bond includes evacuation consideration at the company's discretion, and I'm afraid the discretion has been exercised elsewhere. Do give your mother my regards."

Every sentence a gear. Every gear turning another. Forty-one names, and behind each name a signature, and behind each signature a debt, and behind each debt the quiet understanding that the ship was not leaving *because* of the plague so much as *despite* it—that the Halloway Company would happily sail through pestilence itself if the cargo manifest justified it, and human beings only made the manifest when their names were useful.

Orsen set down the brass fittings, straightened, and caught Quill's eye through the transom window.

It lasted half a heartbeat. Quill did not smile—Quill wasted nothing, not ink, not discretion, not expressions—but something moved behind his face, the small satisfied shift of a man confirming an entry.

He had known Orsen would be standing at that door. He had known Orsen would find their name missing. Perhaps—Orsen felt the thought arrive cold, like water finding a seam—he had arranged the posting time himself, half past seven, specifically so that Orsen, who was always first through the loading gate, would be first to read their own erasure.

*Sponsor: Q.* Under Marisol's name.

Not mercy. Bait on a hook, and the hook already in the water, and the water full of people desperate enough to bite.

* * *

The tenement that evening smelled of boiled cabbage and damp plaster, and their father sat propped by the window doing his breathing exercises, which these days resembled a man trying to persuade his lungs of something they had legally refused.

"The list," he said, when Orsen told him. He said it without surprise. Dying men have little use for surprise. "Forty-one names?"

"Forty-one."

"For a two-hundred-berth ship." He coughed, wet and long, and waved off the worry with two fingers. "Your mother would have said that's not arithmetic, that's alchemy. They turn people into ballast."

Marisol stirred the pot without looking up. "She's conditional," she said. "I'm conditional. Contingent upon continued satisfactory service of record. Which means Quill owns my berth like a pawn ticket, and I have to go ask him what the interest rate is."

"Don't," their father said.

"Papa—"

"Don't ask him what he wants. Men like that, when you ask what they want, they stop having to offer." He turned his head against the window frame and looked at Orsen, and his eyes, sunken as they were, still had the diver's trick of seeming to focus on something far below the surface. "You read the list first. Before anyone."

"I'm always first through the gate."

"Mm." A pause, filled with labored breath. "He counted on that."

"Yes."

"And your name wasn't on it."

"No."

"And instead of rage, you stood there memorizing who else was angry." The ghost of a smile. "My clever one. My invisible one." Then the smile faded into something older and sadder. "Invisibility is a fine cloak until you need someone to see you. Remember that. The sea taught me. You can be perfectly hidden under forty feet of water, holding your breath, being no trouble at all—and the pearl you're reaching for doesn't care how quietly you drown."

Marisol banged the pot down harder than necessary. "Then what do we do? Stand in the square and shout our names? They'll put us off the quay, Papa, they'll—"

"They'll do worse than that," he said mildly. "But better to be thrown off a quay for your name than to arrive safely ashore without one." He closed his eyes. The effort of the sermon had cost him; his next words came smaller. "Ask your questions carefully, Marisol. And you—" the unfocused gaze drifted back to Orsen, "—whatever he offers you for that berth, price it honestly. Include everything. Companies never do."

Later, after the cabbage and after their father's breathing had gone shallow and rhythmic with sleep, Marisol washed the single pot and spoke into the water.

"He'll want something bad," she said. "Whatever Quill wants for my berth. For *our* berth—you know he'll make room for you eventually, men like him collect leverage, and you're the most leveraged person in this city and you don't even know it yet." She scrubbed. "I've been thinking about it all day. And I've decided I don't care what it is."

Orsen said nothing.

"You do care," Marisol said, without turning. "That's the problem with you, Orsen. You care about all the wrong quantities. You count everyone's hunger and everyone's anger and every child on every quay, and you carry it all, and you think carrying it makes you good. It doesn't. It just makes you heavy." Now she turned, hands dripping, eyes bright and hard in the candlelight. "One ship. One sailing. I don't care what the manifest says or whose signature buys it. Emrys gets on that boat. I get on that boat. And if the price of that is Quill's little errand, or Quill's big errand, or Quill's whole rotten ledger set on fire—then the price is cheap, brother, and I'll pay it gladly, and you should start deciding whether you're coming with us or staying behind to be heavy."

She went to bed. Orsen sat a long while by their father's window, listening to the harbor breathe below—the slap of water, the far creak of ships riding at anchor with their yellow flags drooping like tired tongues—and turned the evening's sums over in the dark.

Forty-one names. One sister, conditional. One infant, unwritten. One father, unmovable. One self, absent.

Somewhere across the city, in a counting house with a good roof, Mr. Quill was no doubt awake too, drafting whatever it was he wanted. He was patient. He posted lists at half past seven because he knew exactly who read doors at half past seven. He had built his trap out of arithmetic, and arithmetic was patient, and the worst part—the part Orsen carried to bed like a stone—was the growing certainty that the offer would not be crude. It would be reasonable. It would even be kind, on its surface, the way everything at the Halloway Company was kind on its surface.

And it came sooner than expected. Before dawn, before even the gulls had organized their grievances, a runner tapped at the tenement door and handed Orsen a card of heavy cream stock, engraved, smelling faintly of the counting house's sand:

*Mr. Q. requests the pleasure of Orsen Vasquez's attendance. Warehouse office, eight o'clock. Regarding berths.*

*Regarding berths.*

Orsen stood in the grey light holding the card, and behind the door their father coughed once in his sleep, and below the window the harbor kept whispering its one endless secret, and Orsen thought: *include everything. Price it honestly.*

Eight o'clock was coming whether they priced it or not.
