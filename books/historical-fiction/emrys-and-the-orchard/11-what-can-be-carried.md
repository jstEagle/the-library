## 11. What Can Be Carried

Orsen had learned to weigh things by hand before they had learned to read. A sack of rice, a bolt of indigo, a crate of Halloway's finest porcelain—each one told its truth through the shoulders, through the small honest ache of the lower back. Some loads looked heavy and carried light. Some looked like nothing at all and left a person bent double by evening.

The plan they were building was the second kind.

They sat in the rope loft after midnight with Emrys asleep against their chest—she weighed almost nothing now and everything at once—and itemized what survival would cost. Orsen thought best in lists. It was the clerk's disease, caught honestly from ten years of Quill's ledgers, and for once they were grateful for it.

*Item the first: one berth chit.*

Berth chits were printed on cream stock, stamped in violet ink, signed by an assistant harbor-master whose signature Orsen had watched a hundred times from across the sorting floor. That was the trouble with being invisible. You saw everything. You saw the way Master Corvel's hand started each letter with a little downward stab, as if the paper had insulted him. You saw that his *R*s crossed back over themselves like a man retracing his steps in snow. You saw where he blotted, always twice, always left of center.

Copying it would take an hour and a stolen pen. The pen was easy; the company lost more pens than it sold nails. The hard part was the violet stamp, which lived in Quill's office, which meant entering the one room in Vellamare where Orsen Vasquez could be *seen*, because Quill noticed people the way a fisherman notices ripples.

Orsen added it to the list and did not let their hands shake, because Emrys was sleeping on them, and sleeping infants are barometers of fear.

*Item the second: one dockhand, corruptible.*

Every pier had its price list. Orsen knew it the way farmers know weather. Tam the younger drank his wages but could be trusted to drink them *away from work*. Old Besse took eggs. The man Orsen wanted—the man Orsen needed—was Ferrun, who worked the night crane on Pier Six, who had once loaded a crate marked FRAGILE with such contempt that it had contained, upon arrival, a very thorough lesson in impermanence. Ferrun owed money to men who collected with crowbars. Ferrun would take coin, and Ferrun would not ask questions, because questions were a luxury he had sold years ago.

Ferrun's silence would cost forty silver. Orsen had eleven.

This was the part of the list where the arithmetic got ugly. Orsen looked down at Emrys's small fist curled against their collarbone and performed the calculation every poor person in Vellamare knew by heart: what can be sold, and what does selling it cost you.

There was the mother's pearl.

It sat in its hiding place under the third floorboard of the tenement, wrapped in waxed cloth no bigger than a plum. Father had refused to sell it when the cough first came. He had refused when the rent doubled. He had refused with the particular stubbornness of a man who has already given the sea a wife and will not give it one thing more. Selling it behind his back felt like picking his pocket while he slept—which, technically, it was.

Orsen filed it under *last*, and knew, in the honest place below the ribs where all true accounting happens, that *last* was coming whether they listed it or not.

*Item the third: one father, relocated.*

This was the entry that made Orsen close their eyes. Moving Marisol was nothing; Marisol moved like smoke and argued like weather, but she could walk out any door unassisted. Moving Father—moving a man whose lungs had begun to fill like the hold of a leaking skiff, up the warehouse stairs, past the night watch, into a loft that smelled of hemp and tar—that was not logistics. That was confession wearing a stretcher.

But the tenement would not survive what was coming. Orsen had seen the quarantine maps pinned in the harbor office, seen the old quarter outlined in red grease pencil like a wound someone intended to lance. When the evacuation came—or the fires—Salt Row would go first. Salt Row always went first. It was the company's oldest arithmetic: the cheapest losses first.

So. The loft. Three nights of watching, one moonless, a borrowed handcart, laudanum thinned with cheap wine to keep Father quiet. It was a terrible plan. Orsen turned it over and over, looking for the seam that would make it merely bad, and found only this consolation: every plan is terrible. The good ones are just terrible more quietly.

Emrys stirred, sighed the enormous sigh of the very young, and slept on. Orsen pressed their lips to the downy crown of her head, tasting salt—tenement air, rope dust, the whole briny city—and began, in the dark, to practice Corvel's angry little letters against the floorboards with one finger.

* * *

The forgery took two attempts and nearly a decade off Orsen's life.

Quill's office smelled of lamp oil and vinegar; the man himself was at the harbor office arguing about tonnage, and would be for the length of the harbor master's famous monologue, which had once put an inspector to sleep standing up. Orsen had perhaps six minutes. They had rehearsed the walk so many times their feet knew it without consulting them: past the manifest shelves, past the model ship with its faded rigging, around the desk with its drawer that stuck unless lifted at the left corner—

The stamp was exactly where the audit papers said it should be. Companies are honest about only one thing: the location of their instruments.

Orsen pressed it to the blank chit—blanks kept in the same drawer, naturally; Halloway's trusted no one and therefore hid nothing, assuming theft required imagination—lifted it, and beheld a perfect violet wreath surrounding the words *BERTH GRANTED*.

Then the pen. Downward stab. Retraced *R*. Blot twice, left of center. Orsen's hand mimicked Corvel's frustration so faithfully that they felt genuinely irritated by the time they finished, which seemed fitting.

The first attempt failed on the date. Orsen wrote the ninth instead of the tenth, caught it, and stood frozen for a full thirty seconds listening to their own heartbeat like a traitor in the walls. The second attempt went into their shirt, flat against their chest, where it lay all afternoon like a second conscience—heavier, and less inclined to forgive.

They returned the stamp to its exact angle. They closed the drawer with the corner-lift. On the way out they straightened the model ship's rigging, because invisibility, done properly, leaves a room better than it found it.

That night, feeding Emrys watered milk from a stolen spoon, Orsen examined the chit by candlelight and felt something unfamiliar stir in their chest. Not pride. Pride would come later, or wouldn't. This was closer to vertigo—the dizziness of a person who has been invisible for sixteen years and has just discovered that invisible hands can still steal things, forge things, *change things on paper*, and paper, as Quill had taught them with every fine and manufactured fee, was the strongest material in the world. Stone crumbles. Paper decides.

"You see this?" Orsen whispered to Emrys, who regarded the chit with the profound indifference of the toothless. "This is nothing. Cream stock and violet ink. And it's worth more than my whole life on the company books." They tucked it away. "We're going to fix the exchange rate."

Emrys grabbed their finger and attempted to eat it, which Orsen chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

Ferrun could be found most evenings at the Broken Keel, a tavern so close to the water that the patrons had developed a superstition about ordering anything the sea itself might want. He sat at the end of the bar with the practiced stillness of a man conserving energy between debts.

Orsen bought two cups of the house wine—which tasted chiefly of the barrel—and sat down beside him without ceremony. Invisibility had its protocols. You did not approach a man like Ferrun with whispers and shifty glances; you approached him like a colleague discussing cargo, because shame attracted eyes the way blood attracted gulls.

"Crane on Six," Orsen said. "You run nights."

Ferrun looked at them with the flat appraisal of a man counting exits. "Who's asking."

"Nobody." Orsen set the cup in front of him. "Somebody who needs a handcart moved onto the pier before dawn, no log entry, and a blind eye for maybe twenty minutes. Two weeks from now, give or take."

"Twenty minutes of blind eye costs." He named a figure. Forty silver, exactly as the intelligence had reported. There was a comfort in corruption's consistency; it was the only free market in Vellamare.

"Thirty now," Orsen said, "and ten on the far side."

Ferrun's eyes narrowed—not at the split, Orsen realized, but at the total. "You're not company."

"I'm nobody," Orsen said, and drank. "The cart carries a sick man and some boxes. If anyone asks, you never saw it, because you won't have been looking, because you'll have been drunk somewhere better than this."

"No place better than this," Ferrun said, with genuine feeling, and then, because curiosity was cheaper than he'd expected: "Why the far side?"

"Insurance. In case your blind eye develops cataracts."

Something that was almost respect moved across Ferrun's face, brief as a cloud shadow. "Thirty now." He held out a hand like a mooring cleat. "Done."

Orsen shook it and felt eleven silver coins' worth of future collapse to nothing, and felt strangely light anyway. That was the thing nobody warned you about poverty: every transaction was a funeral, but you got so used to burying things that grief started to feel like travel.

* * *

Father knew immediately. Fathers on their deathbeds develop a genius for it; they have nothing left to spend attention on except noticing.

"You've changed the floorboards," he said, the next morning, propped on his pillows in the tenement room where the tide came in through the walls in winter. His voice was a wet whisper, but his eyes were still the gray of deep water. "Third one's loose. I heard you put it back wrong."

Orsen, who had spent half the night re-hiding the pearl and the other half deciding whether to tell him, sat down heavily on the edge of the bed. "I'm going to sell it."

The pause lasted long enough for the foghorn to sound twice out on the point.

"Sell what," said Father, in the tone of a man buying time to construct a lie, which was richly unfair, since he'd invented the technique.

"Papa. I'm selling the pearl. For the passage."

"The pearl stays." He tried to sit up, managed a rise of perhaps two inches, and subsided, coughing—a long, terrible, tidal cough that Marisol, mercifully, was out fetching water through. When it passed he lay gray and small among the blankets. "Your mother dove for that. Under the orchard. She held her breath past reason for it."

"She held her breath past reason for us, too. She'd trade it in a heartbeat and you know it."

"That's different—" he wheezed. "A pearl's a promise. You sell a promise, you don't get it back. Money comes and goes. Promises—"

"Papa." Orsen took his hand. It was light as dried kelp. "Listen to me. I forged a berth chit last night. I bribed a crane-man this morning. In two weeks I'm going to carry you out of here on a handcart dressed as cargo, and if the company finds out, they will take everything—Marisol, the baby, me. I need the pearl because the plan has holes in it, and holes cost silver, and silver costs promises, and I have decided—" their voice cracked, and they hated it, and pushed through— "I have decided that the only promise I am keeping is the one I made when she was left on the warehouse step. Forget the face. Remember? That was the order. Well. I forgot the order instead."

Father looked at them for a long moment. Somewhere below, Salt Row woke up: gulls, arguments, a baby crying in counterpoint to the foghorn, the whole unsinkable racket of the poor.

"You sound like her," he said finally, and there was ruin in it, and pride too. "Stubborn as ballast." His grip tightened with surprising strength. "Then sell it. But you tell her—" another cough seized him; he rode it out—"you tell the child about the orchard. Trees growing out of green water. Your mother used to say the apples floated up sometimes, after storms. Red ones. She said the sea keeps what it loves, but it gives back fruit." His eyes drifted toward the window, toward the harbor he had spent forty years diving into and never once forgiven. "Promise me the story survives. The rest is just freight."

"I promise."

"Good." He closed his eyes, exhausted by feeling. "Now. This handcart. Am I cargo or am I freight?"

"Cargo. Freight gets thrown."

"Hm." A ghost of the old grin surfaced. "Insist on fragile."

* * *

The plan assembled itself over the following days the way driftwood builds a raft—nothing joined properly, everything lashed together with hope. The forged chit, folded small. Thirty silver to Ferrun, delivered wrapped in oiled paper inside a fish, because Ferrun insisted discretion preferred fish. The remaining ten promised against the sale of the pearl, which Orsen took at last to a dealer on Rope Lane who bit it, weighed it, squinted at it, and offered a sum so insulting that Orsen negotiated him up purely out of maternal loyalty, and walked out with enough for the bribe, the laudanum, and a handcart with one squeaking wheel, because the silent wheels cost extra and everything now cost extra.

What remained, itemized: one sister to persuade. One father to move. One baby already packed, in every sense, in a market basket lined with stolen linen. Ten days until the ship. One moonless night among them.

It was, Orsen reflected, hauling the handcart home through the drizzle, an insane architecture. Every beam of it load-bearing and every beam of it rotten. But rafts float. They had seen them. Ugly, improvised, lashed-together things, riding swells that sank prettier boats.

They were halfway down Salt Row, planning the route to the pier step by step, when they saw the lantern outside the warehouse burning yellow—not the harbor's yellow plague flag, but a smaller light, a private one—and found Quill standing beneath it, checking his pocket watch, waiting for them with the expression of a man who has been doing arithmetic and likes his answer.

"Ah, Vasquez," he said pleasantly. "Walk with me. I have a proposition that I think you'll find... reasonable."

Orsen's hand, quite without instruction, drifted to the place where the forged chit lay hidden against their chest.
