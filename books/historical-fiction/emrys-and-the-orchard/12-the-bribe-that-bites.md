## 12. The Bribe That Bites

The dockhand's name was Teodor Brask, and he had hands like mooring rope and a laugh that could be heard from the fish market to the customs house. Orsen had known him for eleven of their twenty-three years—the whole span of the indenture—back to the days when both of them were small enough to sleep in net hammocks strung between warehouse bollards, back when a stolen orange split two ways could taste like a feast.

Which was precisely why Orsen chose him.

You did not give your savings to a stranger. A stranger would take the money and forget your face. Teo would take the money and remember it fondly, and fondness was its own kind of collateral. That was the theory, at any rate, constructed carefully over three sleepless nights and inspected for flaws the way one inspected cargo: lift it, turn it, listen for rattles.

The flaw, as it turned out, was in assuming that eleven years of shared oranges outweighed four months of quarantine wages—or rather, the absence of them. The strike at Pier Nine had entered its second week. The company had locked the gates on the strikers, and men like Teo, who had never struck anything in his life except his own knee after too much rum, were caught between loyalty and hunger. Hunger, Orsen learned, has better arithmetic than friendship. It counts faster.

They met at dusk behind the tar sheds, where the smell covered everything and no lantern reached. Orsen brought the money sewn into the lining of a cap—four years of small skimmings, unclaimed overtime that Quill's ledgers had somehow never recorded, coins won at cards by pretending to be worse at cards than they were. It came to thirty-one marks and change. It was, or had been until very recently, the entire material evidence of Orsen Vasquez's existence outside the Halloway Trading Company.

"You want a berth chit," Teo said, turning the cap over in his enormous hands. "Forged."

"Good as real. Better. Real ones have watermarks; I've studied them." Orsen kept their voice light, easy, the voice of someone asking for an orange to be split, not committing a hanging offense. "Three names on it. You know a man who knows a man."

"I know a man who *is* the man," Teo said, which was encouraging, and should not have been. He weighed the cap in his palm the way Orsen weighed cargo, and something flickered across his face—not greed exactly. Something sadder. Calculation. "This is everything you have, little ghost."

"Don't call me that."

"Everything you have," Teo repeated, gently, "for paper."

"For passage," Orsen said. "Paper's just what it looks like."

Teo looked out at the harbor, where the yellow plague flags hung limp on the quarantine ships like things drowned mid-flight. He looked a long time. Then he put the cap inside his coat, and he smiled his big honest smile, and he clapped Orsen on the shoulder hard enough to stagger a smaller person, and he said, "Three days."

Later—much later—Orsen would take that moment apart and examine every piece of it, looking for the seam where they'd gone wrong, and would find, infuriatingly, that there wasn't one. Teo had not lied well. He had not even lied cleverly. His eyes had simply gone somewhere else while his mouth made promises, the way eyes do when a man is already composing the report he intends to file. Orsen, who could read faces through canvas, who had built a life on reading faces, missed it because they wanted to miss it—because hope, in the end, is only a kind of squinting.

The lesson cost thirty-one marks and change.

* * *

Quill sent for Orsen at the ninth hour of the next morning, which was itself a message. Mr. Quill conducted all business at the seventh hour, sharp and early, because early hours said: I am diligent, I am owed, the world keeps my schedule. The ninth hour was leisurely. The ninth hour said: I have been thinking about you at breakfast, unhurried, the way one thinks about an interesting problem.

Orsen climbed the stairs to the audit office with their heart conducting quiet mutiny against their ribs. Below, through the window, Pier Nine was visible—the picket line, the strikers with their placards gone soft in the drizzle, a woman with a red shawl passing out bread from a sack that looked lighter than yesterday.

Quill's office smelled of ink and camphor and, faintly, of the candied ginger he sucked when his stomach complained, which was always. He sat behind a desk so orderly it might have been printed, and he did not look up for a full minute, because making people wait was the first instrument in his orchestra.

"Sit," he said finally, without looking.

Orsen sat.

"You've been to see Teodor Brask."

Not a question. Orsen's face did what it had trained eleven years to do, which was nothing at all. "I know a lot of people, sir."

"Mm." Quill opened a drawer—slowly, ceremonially—and set the cap on the desk. Orsen's cap. The lining hung open like a wound, empty. Beside it Quill placed a small stack of marks, counted already, squared at the edges. Thirty-one. And then a single additional coin, placed apart from the rest like a guest who hadn't been invited.

"Thirty-two," Quill said. "Brask asked a finder's fee. I paid it." He laced his fingers and regarded Orsen over them with pale, patient eyes. "Do you understand what I find interesting about this, Vasquez?"

That Orsen had savings at all. That Orsen had skimmed, schemed, forged intentions. That the invisible clerk had, in fact, been seen the entire time. Any of these. All of these. Orsen chose silence, which was usually the safest answer to rhetorical arithmetic.

"What interests me," Quill continued, "is not the crime. Forgery attempts cross my desk monthly. What interests me is the *quality*. Your chit design—" he produced a paper from another drawer, unfolded it, smoothed it flat with both palms, "—is flawless. Watermark, seal pressure, the clerk's cipher in the corner. I showed it to Harbor Master Dunn without explanation and he initialled it. *Initialled it,* Vasquez. He thought it was real. I had to tell him twice."

Somewhere below, the striker's bell rang its dull hour. Orsen watched Quill's hands on the counterfeit chit and understood, with the slow cold settling of ballast shifting, that this was not going to be an arrest.

"I don't understand, sir," Orsen said, which was a lie, the first fluent one, delivered without a single tremor. It felt, alarmingly, easy. Like discovering you could swim only after jumping in.

"No," Quill agreed pleasantly. "You understand perfectly. You simply know better than to say so." He leaned back. The chair creaked its small complaint. "Here is what happens now, in the version of events I have already written down. A warehouse clerk of no particular consequence attempted to forge evacuation documents during a quarantine crisis—an act of desperation, pitiable, motivated, we may assume, by fear for family. The penalty is forfeiture of indenture credits, extension of service by"—he consulted nothing; he knew the number—"six years, and remand to the company workhouse until such time as the matter resolves."

Six years. The number landed in Orsen's chest and sat there like swallowed stone. Six years past the indenture's end. Six years past freedom, dissolved into fines the way salt dissolves in seawater—invisibly, completely, everywhere at once.

"And the other version?" Orsen asked quietly.

Quill smiled. It was, by his standards, a warm expression—the warmth of a lamp in a room where something is being prepared. He drew a third document from the drawer and turned it so Orsen could read it upside down, then rotated it, unhurried, a man savoring the reveal.

It was a berth list. Three berths, provisionally assigned, the names left blank. First-class accommodations aboard the *Merrow's Grace*, chartered vessel, sailing upon quarantine lifting.

"The company rewards loyalty," Quill said. "It is our founding principle, after profit. The strike at Pier Nine is, from certain perspectives, a loyalty problem. Men refusing to work. Men encouraging others to refuse. Men—" he tapped the list of strikers' demands pinned beneath the blotter, "—making complaints about conditions, as though conditions were weather, as though anyone had promised them fair weather."

"You want names."

"I want *names*, dates, meeting places, and the location of the strike fund, which I am given to understand exists." Quill folded his hands again. "In exchange: three berths. Provisional, of course—contingent on information being accurate and useful. But provisional berths have a way of becoming permanent, Vasquez. I have seen it happen. Repeatedly. To people who understood which side of history the company butters."

Orsen looked at the blank lines where names would go. Three berths. Father, whose lungs were failing by increments the physicians priced beyond reach. Marisol, who dreamed of passage abroad with the fierce specific hunger of someone who has memorized departure schedules she cannot afford. Emrys—

Emrys, who slept in a rope loft smelling of hemp and Orsen's guilt, who had recently discovered her feet and treated them as a revelation, who would die in this city if Orsen did not do something.

Temptation, it turned out, did not arrive wearing a devil's face. It arrived wearing Quill's mild, reasonable expression, offering exactly what you needed at exactly the price you could almost convince yourself was someone else's.

"I'll need time to consider," Orsen said.

"Of course." Quill slid the blank berth list across the desk and left it there, within reach, a thing to be taken. "Take the document. Show yourself what you're considering. Consideration goes faster with props." He returned to his ledger, dipped his pen, and added, without looking up, in the tone of a man discussing freight rates: "The woman in the red shawl at Pier Nine. Her name is Anselma Corvo. She has two children. The bread she distributes comes from the parish, but the fund that buys grain for it comes from the strike chest. Begin there, if you begin at all. You have until Friday."

* * *

Orsen walked out of the audit office carrying the berth list folded against their chest like contraband, which it was, in every direction at once.

The rain had thickened. Down on the quay, the strikers' fire smoked under an oilcloth awning, and the woman in the red shawl—Anselma Corvo, now named, which changed her horribly from a cause into a person—was ladling thin soup into tin cups. A boy of perhaps five stood beside her holding his own cup with both hands, drinking with the total concentration of a child who has learned not to trust there will be more.

Orsen stopped at the edge of the awning. The woman looked up.

"You're company," she said. Not hostile. Just cataloguing, the way everyone in Vellamare now sorted faces into company and not-company, ship and not-ship, saved and left-behind.

"I'm nobody," Orsen said, and meant it several ways, and heard how much it sounded like a plea.

"There's soup if you're hungry-nobody," Anselma said, and held out a cup before Orsen answered, because that was apparently what she did—she fed first and interrogated never. The boy looked up at Orsen with frank, unguarded curiosity, and Orsen looked back and thought, with a violence that startled them: *Quill wants me to sell this child's bread to buy a cabin with a window.*

Orsen drank the soup. It was mostly water with opinions. It was the best thing they'd tasted in weeks.

* * *

The rope loft that evening smelled of hemp and warmed milk and the particular animal sweetness of a baby who had lately discovered solid mush and applied it with enthusiasm. Emrys sat propped against a coil of rigging, pounding one fat heel against the boards, delighted by the sound, by the lamplight, by the entire outrageous gift of existing.

Orsen sat down beside her and let the day fall off their shoulders in pieces.

"We're being offered a miracle," they told her softly. "A real one. Berths with windows. Three of them. You, me, your aunt Marisol. Your grandfather too, maybe—there's wiggle room in miracles, apparently, if you inform properly."

Emrys grabbed a fistful of Orsen's sleeve and pulled it toward her mouth with the gravity of a scholar consulting a text.

"And all it costs," Orsen continued, "is the names of everyone in this city who's kinder than the people paying us." They laughed—a short, cracked sound with nothing warm in it. "You're too young to understand the joke. I'm starting to wish I were too."

The baby released the sleeve and instead seized Orsen's finger, and gripped it with that astonishing infant strength, that absolute faith that fingers exist to be held. She looked up at Orser with her mother's unknown eyes and her own entirely known ferocity, and somewhere below, muffled by floorboards and rain, the striker's bell rang nine o'clock.

Friday. Two days.

Marisol's footsteps on the ladder—light, fast, her you-better-be-sitting-down rhythm. She swung up into the loft with rain in her hair and something bright and terrible in her face, and she saw the folded paper in Orsen's hand, and she went still.

"That's a berth list," she said.

"It's a berth list."

"Blank?"

"Blank."

Marisol sat down slowly across from them, eyes shining, and Orsen watched her sister read the shape of the offer in the space of three heartbeats—because Marisol was quick, always quick, quick enough to get to the arithmetic before the ethics arrived—and when she looked up, her expression was already the beginning of an argument.

"How much does it cost," Marisol said, "and where do we sign?"
