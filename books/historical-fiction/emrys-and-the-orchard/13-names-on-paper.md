## 13. Names on Paper

The paper sat between them on the crate like a third person who had come to dinner and refused to leave.

Orsen had carried it home folded into eight squares, tucked against their ribs the way they once carried stolen milk. Quill's handwriting was elegant in the way of men who had never been struck by anything heavier than a pen: *In consideration of services rendered and to be rendered…* Three names. Three berths. A blank space at the bottom where a name would go — not Emrys's, not Marisol's, but the name of someone else, some striker, some father with hungry children, whose only crime would be trusting Orsen Vasquez enough to stand near them.

*To be rendered.* As if betrayal were a trade good. As if conscience had a weight per hundredweight, a going rate, a dock fee.

"You've been staring at it for an hour," Marisol said.

"I'm weighing it."

"You weigh everything. You weighed a pear for four minutes last week before you bought it."

"The pear wasn't going to cost me my soul."

Marisol snorted. She was mending a shirt by candlelight, the needle flashing in and out like something small and impatient, and she did not look up when she said, "You don't have a soul to sell, Orsen. You have a ledger. And right now your ledger is very short on futures."

"Mari—"

"No. Listen to me." The needle stopped. She looked up, and in the candlelight her face was all angles and fury, seventeen years old and certain of everything, which is a luxury Orsen had never once been able to afford. "The plague flags are up. Father can barely walk from the bed to the door without sitting down twice. There is *one ship*. Do you understand? One. And the company has already decided who goes on it, and we are not on the list, and you are holding the only piece of paper in Vellamare that could change that."

"It costs other people their places."

"Other people!" Marisol laughed, and it was not a kind laugh; it had teeth in it. "Other people have been costing *us* our places since before either of us could write our own names. The Halloway Company added six years to your indenture with fines for broken rope and a wet winter and — what was the other one?"

"Insolence," Orsen said quietly. "I asked where the wages were."

"Insolence." She said the word like she was spitting out a bone. "And now one clerk offers you back three lives for the price of three names, and you sit there weighing pears."

From the corner of the room came a sound — wet, rattling, half cough and half laugh. Their father had been listening. He listened to everything now; his lungs had made idle conversation impossible, so he had become a collector of other people's words, hoarding them the way he once hoarded pearls he was never allowed to keep.

"Marisol," he wheezed. "Come here, girl. Bring the light."

She brought the candle. In its glow, Tomas Vasquez looked like a man the sea had already begun reclaiming — hollowed cheeks, lips bluish, hands restless on the blanket as if they still remembered rope and pearl-shell. His eyes, though. His eyes were still the clearest thing in the tenement.

"Papa," Marisol said gently, "you should be sleeping."

"I should be many things." He smiled, and the smile cost him breath, and he spent it anyway, because that was the kind of man he was — a man who paid for smiles the way other men paid for bread, cheerfully, without accounting. "I want to talk about the orchard."

"Papa—"

"The drowned one. Where your mother—" The cough took him then, a long terrible business, and Orsen moved without thinking, one hand bracing his back, the other holding the cup of thinned honey-water to his lips. This was the choreography of the last year: cough, brace, cup, wait. They knew it the way they knew cargo weights, by hand, by heart, without looking.

When he could breathe again, he gripped Orsen's wrist. His grip was nothing. It used to be able to crack a shell off a pearl without scratching the nacre. Now it was a bird landing.

"I heard you two arguing about a paper," he said.

Orsen's stomach dropped. "It's nothing, Papa. Company business."

"Company business." He rolled the phrase around like a bad oyster. "There is no such thing. There is only business done *to* the company, or done *for* it, and I have never known anyone to die of the second kind except slowly." His eyes found Orsen's and held them, fever-bright and terribly lucid. "What does it want you to do, this paper?"

The silence stretched. Marisol, remarkably, said nothing. Perhaps she wanted to hear him say it too. Perhaps she wanted a witness.

"Give names," Orsen said finally. "Workers who are striking. In exchange — berths. Three berths. On the last ship."

Their father closed his eyes. For a moment Orsen thought he'd fallen asleep, which happened mid-sentence now more often than not, the body simply refusing to be consulted on matters of schedule. But then he shook his head, slow, like a tide turning.

"Names," he whispered. "Your mother dove under the old orchard. Did I ever tell you why?"

"You told us. The trees were still down there. She said you could see them on clear days, the branches reaching up."

"She dove for pearls because pearls don't have names." His grip tightened, or tried to. "A pearl is nobody's daughter. You take it, and no one comes asking after it. No one drowns looking for it. But a *name*, Orsen—" He opened his eyes again. "A name is somebody. A name is a whole life folded up small enough to carry. Your mother wouldn't sell her last pearl even when we were starving, and I never understood until I was older that it wasn't the pearl she was keeping. It was the *not selling*. Some things you keep by refusing. Do you understand me?"

"Papa—"

"Do you understand me, Orsen?"

"Yes," Orsen said, because lying fluently was a skill they'd learned this week, and it worked on everyone except apparently dying men, whose eyes had been trained by thirty years of water to see through murk.

He didn't believe them. She saw that. But he let go of their wrist, and lay back, and said, to the ceiling, to the salt-stained beams, to a woman twenty years drowned: "Don't become the company, my child. The company takes names. Don't you ever become the thing that takes names."

* * *

Marisol couldn't sleep, so she went out to the stairwell, where the wind came through the broken pane and made thinking easier by making comfort impossible.

Her brother — her sister, her whatever-Orsen-was-today, the family joke being that Orsen was whichever sibling was most useful at the moment, which Marisol privately thought was the truest description of Orsen ever coined — was still awake. Of course. Orsen slept like a cat in a strange house, one ear open, four hours a night, and had done so for as long as Marisol could remember. Sometimes she hated that. Sometimes she loved it so much it frightened her.

She hadn't meant to find Emrys. That was the truth she kept returning to, turning over like a stone. She had come to the warehouse to bring Orsen supper and found instead a basket behind the flour sacks, and in the basket a baby, and the baby had looked up at her with eyes the color of harbor water and grabbed her finger with the absolute confidence of someone who had never yet been failed by the world, and Marisol's entire architecture of resentment had developed a crack straight down the middle.

She was still angry. She intended to remain angry. But it was angry the way the sea is salty — ambient, total, easy to forget while swimming.

"You're brooding," Orsen said from the doorway. "You get it from me."

"I get everything from you. It's disgusting."

"Take the blanket then. You're shivering."

She took it. She sat. For a while neither spoke, which was how the two of them talked best.

"If you sign," Marisol said at last, "we live. All four of us. Five counting the baby, who doesn't eat much and mostly sleeps, so economically speaking she's practically free."

"That's not how Quill will price her."

"Don't. Don't make jokes about her like she's freight. That's what they do." Marisol pulled her knees up. "Here's what I know, Orsen. Here is everything I know, all of it at once, because I'm tired of knowing it in installments. The strikers are right. The conditions at Pier Nine are a disgrace, the quarantine tents flood when it rains, and half the workers haven't been paid since the yellow flag went up. If I were braver I'd be standing with them instead of arguing arithmetic in a stairwell. But bravery is a food you have to already be full to eat, and I am *hungry*, Orsen. I have been hungry since I was six. Papa is drowning on dry land. You are owned. I am a conditional line item on an evacuation list that hasn't been posted yet but will be, and when it posts, my name will have a little asterisk next to it meaning *pending review*, because everything about us is always pending review."

Orsen said nothing. Orsen, Marisol knew, was very good at saying nothing; it was their native language, learned before speech, perfected over years of moving through the warehouse unseen.

"And here is the other thing I know," Marisol went on, quieter now. "If you don't sign, some of us might still make it anyway. The fishing boat. The forged chit. Whatever mad plan you're building in that head of yours. Maybe it works. But maybe it works the way Mama's last dive worked — technically perfect, and everyone remembers it, and it kills her anyway." Her voice broke, slightly, and she hated it, and continued. "Three names on a paper, Orsen. Three names, and Papa gets a doctor on a ship instead of a floor. Three names, and I stop being pending. Tell me exactly what the moral mathematics are, because you're the one who weighs things. Weigh *this*. Tell me what a stranger's freedom weighs against Papa's lungs."

"A stranger's freedom," Orsen said softly, "weighs the same as ours. That's the whole problem with weights, Mari. They don't care who's holding the scale."

"Oh, that's beautiful. That's a lovely sentence. You should embroider it on a pillow and starve beautifully."

"Marisol—"

"No. You listen to *me* for once." She stood, and the blanket fell, and she was shaking, though not from cold anymore. "Survival justifies things. Not everything — I know that, I'm not a monster, I cried for a week over a gull I hit with a rock when I was nine — but *things*. Necessary things. Ugly necessary things. Everyone who has ever lived through anything has learned this, and the ones who didn't learn it are dead and virtuous and no use to anybody, including themselves." She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again it came out smaller than she wanted. "Papa taught us to dive, you know. Before his lungs went. He put me under the water at the shallows and told me: the pearl is worth the drowning, but only if you come up. Coming up is part of the value. Otherwise you're just a corpse holding a rock." She looked at her sibling, this person she would burn cities for, this person who was currently breaking her heart by doing nothing at all. "Come up, Orsen. Sign the paper. Come up."

She went inside. She did not slam the door, because Emrys was sleeping, and even furious, even starving, even seventeen and certain, Marisol Vasquez was careful with small things. She got that from Orsen too. Everything, from Orsen. It was disgusting.

* * *

Orsen stayed on the stairwell until the candle inside guttered out, and then a while longer, because the dark was honest in ways the light refused to be.

They turned the paper over in their hands. Blank side. Quill had not bothered to write anything on the back; Quill's papers, like Quill's promises, were single-surfaced.

The temptation was not what Orsen had expected. It was not hunger, exactly — Orsen had been hungry so long it had stopped being an argument and become furniture. It was not even fear, though fear was present, sitting politely, the way fear always did around Quill. No. The temptation was simpler and far worse: it was the sight of Marisol's face when she said *three names and we live*. It was the arithmetic working. For once, for the first time in a life built entirely out of ledgers designed by other people, Orsen held a column of numbers that summed to something good. Papa breathing sea air instead of tenement rot. Mari on a deck instead of a list. Emrys —

Emrys, who had hiccupped tonight when Marisol fed her, and Marisol had laughed, actually laughed, first time since the yellow flag went up.

That was the seduction. Not mercy wearing a mask. Mercy *unmasked*, offered openly, priced fairly, by a company that had finally found the currency Orsen could not refuse. Quill understood something important: you don't corrupt the invisible by offering them visibility. You corrupt them by offering them a way to make the people they love visible instead.

Orsen pressed the paper flat against their knee. Thought about names. The strikers' names — Berta who shared her lunch; old Ferro with the ruined shoulder who still showed up; the boy, Tam, twelve years old, who had eaten his bread in the doorway yesterday and watched Orsen pass with the particular blankness of a child practicing not wanting things. Orsen knew that blankness. Had worn it for twenty years. Could teach a masterclass.

*Forget the face,* the note had said, a lifetime of seasons ago, and Orsen had disobeyed, and the disobedience had a name now, and gummy smiles, and a grip like a harbor buoy.

*Don't become the company,* their father had said, and Orsen believed him absolutely, and belief, as everyone in Vellamare knew, floats poorly weighted against cargo.

But there was a third voice, and it was Orsen's own, and it was the quietest, and it said: *you have lied once, fluently, and it worked. How long before fluent lies stop feeling like weather and start feeling like skin?*

Somewhere below, the harbor slapped at the pilings. Somewhere beyond that, the strike at Pier Nine would be waking into its fourth day, hungry and stubborn. Somewhere beyond that, a ship rode at anchor with a list of names already written, and none of them were theirs.

Orsen folded the paper back into eight squares. Then, deliberately, carefully, they folded it again, and again, smaller and smaller, until it was a hard little knot no bigger than a pearl — the size of a thing you could hold underwater, the size of the refusal that had killed their mother and freed her in the same motion.

They didn't sign it. Not tonight. Tonight they only carried it, which was, after all, the thing Orsen did best: carry, unseen.

But in the morning, Quill would expect an answer. And across the harbor, at Pier Nine, the strikers had announced they would march at dawn — and Mr. Quill, Orsen knew, was already sharpening his pen for names.

One way or another, by tomorrow night, someone's name was going on paper.
