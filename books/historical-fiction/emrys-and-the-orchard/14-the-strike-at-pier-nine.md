## 14. The Strike at Pier Nine

The strike began, as most things in Vellamare did, with a bell.

Not the harbor bell, which rang for ships and fires and the odd ceremonial visit of some company dignitary with too many buttons on his coat. This was the lunch bell at Pier Nine, rung by old Teodoro Vane, who had worked the Halloway docks for forty years and whose hands were so mapled with scars that Orsen had once privately thought of them as the warehouse's true ledger. Teodoro climbed onto an overturned crate, took the lunch bell from its nail — the same bell he had rung every midday for four decades to call his fellows to bread and beans — and struck it instead at dawn.

He rang it until half the waterfront came running. Then he set it down gently, as if laying an infant in a cradle, and said:

"No work today."

* * *

Orsen heard about it secondhand, which was how they preferred to hear everything. They were in the counting room, tallying bales of salted hides that smelled like a crime scene, when the foreman Pell came through with his cap twisted in his hands.

"Quill wants the manifests held," Pell said. "Nothing loads today. Nothing unloads either."

"Is it about the water?" Orsen asked, because everyone knew it was about the water.

It was about the water. The quarantine had been clamped over the harbor for eleven days now, and the Halloway Company had solved the problem of its stranded workforce the way it solved every problem: by charging them for it. Drinking water, once drawn free from the public cisterns, now came only from company barrels — three coppers a pail, deducted from wages before wages were paid. Rents in the company tenements had risen "temporarily," the word doing a great deal of limber work. And the sick were turned away from the company infirmary unless they could produce a chit proving current employment, which was difficult to produce when you'd been laid off for being too sick to work.

The workers of Pier Nine had done the arithmetic. It was the same arithmetic Quill had taught Orsen years ago: numbers arranged so that they always arrived at zero, and zero always arrived at the company.

"They're asking for names," Pell said quietly, not looking at Orsen. He was looking at the wall, which was safer. "Ringleaders. Quill's offering a bonus for information. Three silver."

"Three silver," Orsen repeated, keeping their voice as flat as a scale reading.

"For names." Pell finally glanced over, and there was something raw in his face — not accusation, exactly. Appeal. "Nobody's saying you have to. Only that some will."

He left. Orsen stood among the stinking hides and thought about the rope loft above the third bay, where a small girl was sleeping off her breakfast of stolen milk, and about how a name on paper could become a noose around more than one neck.

* * *

By noon the pier had become a strange kind of festival.

Strikers lined the quay in a loose picket that was mostly standing around and occasionally shouting at scabs, of whom there were few; nobody in Vellamare wanted to be seen hauling Halloway cargo while the yellow flag flew. Women had come down from the tenement rows with pots of fish stew and were ladling it out on a plank across two barrels. Somebody's uncle was playing a fiddle badly enough that people cheered anyway. Children darted between legs, picking up dropped coppers the way gulls picked up fish guts.

Orsen went down at the change of shift, ostensibly to deliver a message about withheld manifests, actually because the loft had felt too quiet and Emrys had been asleep and safe and Marisol was watching her, and because — though they would never have put this into words — they needed to see what a crowd of people looked like when they stopped being invisible to each other.

Teodoro Vane found them near the stew line.

"Vasquez." He said it without warmth but without coldness either, the way you'd address weather. "Company send you down to count us?"

"Company doesn't know I'm here."

"Then have some stew." He steered them toward the plank by the elbow, an oddly gentle gesture from a man built like a capstan. "Marta made it. She puts in too much pepper. Eat it anyway."

The stew was ferociously peppery and very good. Orsen ate standing up, the way they'd eaten everything since the age of nine, and watched the pier.

That was when they saw the child.

She couldn't have been more than five, sitting beneath the barrels that held the stew pot, hugging her knees. Her mother — a big-shouldered woman Orsen vaguely recognized from the coiling crews, one of the loudest voices on the picket line — was at the front of the crowd now, arguing with a company man who'd come down with a paper in his fist. The girl sat where she'd been told to sit, out of the way, and she was doing something Orsen knew intimately: she was being small and still and useful by taking up no space at all.

And she was watching the pot. Not staring — watching, the way Orsen watched faces. Sideways, patiently, without wanting.

Orsen looked down into their own bowl, still half full, still steaming.

They had stolen milk for eighteen months. They had memorized the patrol schedules of night watchmen, the blind spots in the counting room, the exact weight of silence in a loft full of rope. They had learned to want less so they could carry less, to need nothing so nothing could be taken. That philosophy had kept them alive through plague and ledgers and the slow theft of their own years. It was a good philosophy. It had never once asked them to hand their bowl to somebody else's child.

But there was another bowl on the plank already, and Marta was ladling it full, and she walked it over and crouched down by the barrels and said something to the girl that made her smile the way children smile when they've been caught being hungry and forgiven for it.

Orsen stood there holding their untouched second helping and understood, with a clarity that felt like fever breaking, exactly what Quill was buying with his three silver.

Not names. Names were just ink. What Quill wanted to purchase was this — the stew line, the fiddle, the woman crouching by the barrels. He wanted to buy the moment when a crowd of hungry people stopped trusting each other, because a crowd that trusts each other can hold a pier, and a crowd that suspects its neighbors is just a warehouse of future informants. Three silver per betrayal, and the whole quarantined city sold back to the company piece by piece.

The child ate her stew with her elbows tucked in, careful, economical, wasting nothing. Somewhere far away, in a rope loft, Emrys was probably waking from her nap, and Marisol was making faces at her, and none of it — not one copper of it — would ever be worth three silver.

"Good stew," Teodoro said beside them, meaning something else entirely.

"Yes," Orsen said. "It is."

* * *

The trouble came at dusk, as trouble prefers to.

A cart arrived at the pier gate carrying company guards — six of them, then eight, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once needed permission. Behind them, hired porters began unloading company water barrels, each stenciled with the Halloway crest, each destined for the favored tenement rows uptown while the strikers' cisterns ran low.

The picket line tightened. The fiddle stopped. Marta's voice rose from the front of the crowd, clear and furious, listing grievances the way other women listed market prices — rent, water, the infirmary, the fines — and the guard captain smiled the thin smile of a man holding paper against flesh.

"You're in breach of your contracts," he announced to the pier at large. "Every hour of this is docked from wages. Go home. There's soup waiting."

"There's no water for the soup," someone shouted back, and the crowd laughed, ugly and united, and the captain's smile thinned further.

Orsen stood at the edge of it all, in their gray company coat, marked by the very uniform the strikers were cursing. A coil of dread sat in their stomach. Because this was the scene Quill would read tomorrow, and Quill would turn to Orsen — reliable Orsen, invisible Orsen, Orsen who weighed cargo by hand and noticed everything — and he would ask for names.

He had, in fact, already asked. The offer was sitting in Orsen's chest like swallowed ballast: three berths. Three berths for names on paper. Marisol aboard, Father aboard, even Emrys smuggled onto a manifest under some invented cousinage. Everything Orsen had bled for since the night a clerk left a bundle on the warehouse steps and ordered them to forget a face.

All they had to do was point at Marta. At Teodoro. At the fiddle player, the stew pot, the child eating with her elbows tucked in.

* * *

Night found Orsen on the warehouse steps, unable to go up to the loft, unable to leave.

Pell sat down beside them without asking. For a long while neither spoke. Down at the water, the strikers had lit lanterns along the picket line — dozens of little flames strung along the quay like the orchard lights of some drowned festival — and the harbor lapped at the pilings with its patient, indifferent sound.

"My boy's got a cough," Pell said eventually. "Nothing bad. Yet. But the infirmary wants a current employment chit, and if I'm striking, I'm not employed, and if I'm not employed—" He rubbed his face. "You see the shape of it. It's a knot with no loose end."

"I see it."

"Quill'll break this inside a week. Hunger breaks everything." Pell turned, and in the dark his face was just a suggestion. "Somebody's going to give him names, Vasquez. Better it comes from nobody than somebody. You understand me?"

Orsen understood him perfectly. It was the closest thing to kindness anyone at the company had offered in years, and it was also a warning, and both of those things could be true at once, which was the trick of Vellamare, the trick of the whole world: everything worth having came tangled in barbed wire.

"I understand," Orsen said.

Pell nodded and left. His footsteps faded up the stairs, and then Orsen was alone with the lanterns and the water and the arithmetic.

Three berths. One child. One sister who dreamed of passage abroad. One father whose lungs were filling like bilges. All of it purchasable for a handful of names — and the names belonged to people who had, tonight, without knowing it, fed and sheltered and sung for strangers while the company counted its barrels.

Orsen thought of their father's story, told in fever: a woman diving beneath a submerged orchard, refusing to the last to sell the pearl that was hers. *The sea takes,* she'd believed, *but it doesn't get to take what you hand it.*

The company didn't get to take this. Not the stew line. Not the lanterns. Not the small, stubborn decency of people who had nothing sharing it anyway.

Conscience, Orsen discovered, does not catch fire all at once, the way warehouses burn. It catches the way lanterns catch along a quay — one flame, then another, passed hand to hand until the whole dark waterfront is strung with light. Tonight the last lantern lit.

They stood, brushed off their coat, and started up the stairs toward the loft, toward Marisol's sharp eyes and Emrys's sleep-heavy weight, toward the hardest conversation of their life — because tomorrow Quill would expect names, and Orsen had finally decided whose name they would give him.

Their own.

But when they reached the third bay, the loft door stood open, the lamp inside burned low, and Marisol was waiting on the top step with a folded paper in her hand and a look on her face that Orsen had never seen before — fear, yes, but underneath it something harder and colder, like iron left out overnight.

"Orsen," she said, "we need to talk about Quill."
