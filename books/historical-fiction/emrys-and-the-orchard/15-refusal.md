## 15. Refusal

The list lay on Quill's desk between them like a dead fish.

Orsen had seen it before—had seen it every night for three days now, folded into the same drawer, weighted under the same brass inkwell, waiting for them the way a trap waits for a fox that thinks it has found a shortcut home. Eleven names on it. Dockhands, rope-makers, two women from the cooperage who had the audacity to laugh too loudly in the yard. Strikers, all of them. Or people Quill *said* were strikers, which in Vellamare that autumn amounted to nearly the same thing.

"Take your time," Quill said, and smiled his paper smile.

He was a small man made smaller by his own furniture. He sat behind a desk built for someone twice his size, and he had arranged his office so that visitors always stood while he sat, always looked down at him and somehow ended up feeling beneath him anyway. It was a kind of arithmetic, Orsen thought. Everything about Mr. Quill was arithmetic. Even his cruelty came with columns and carried figures.

"I've told you," Orsen said. "I don't know these people."

"You know everyone." Quill tapped the list once, gently, as if it might bruise. "That's your entire value, Vasquez. You are the most thoroughly invisible man in this warehouse, and invisibility is a form of knowing. You see what the foremen can't see because they're too busy being looked at. So." He slid the list an inch closer. "Three berths. One for you, one for the sister, one—" and here he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over the interesting part, "—for whoever else you might consider family."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the harbor foghorn moaned its long stupid note, and Orsen thought: *even the ships are lying tonight.*

They had rehearsed this moment. Not consciously—Orsen did not rehearse anything consciously anymore; rehearsal was for people who believed they would be given a second take. But some part of them had been standing in this room for days, listening to Quill's voice, learning its grain the way their father had learned the grain of water before a dive. And that part of them knew something important: Quill wanted a signature. He wanted ink. He wanted the small civilized violence of a name given freely, because a name coerced could be retracted later, but a name *chosen*—a name chosen by a man trying to save his own skin—that name would hold forever.

So Orsen did not reach for the pen.

Instead they said, "May I ask you something, sir?"

Quill's eyebrows performed a small bureaucratic dance. "You may."

"The fines on my indenture. The broken crate in my fourth year. The 'unauthorized candle' in my sixth—I didn't have a candle, sir, I had a *reflection* off a nail head. Who writes those entries?"

For a moment the paper smile flickered, and underneath it Orsen saw something almost human: surprise. Perhaps even a flicker of respect. Then it smoothed away again, ironed flat by thirty years of ledgers.

"The company writes them," Quill said. "The company is not a person, Vasquez. It cannot be angry or merciful. It can only balance." He leaned forward. "Which is why I am offering you the only mercy this building contains: the chance to balance your own account. Sign, and your debts vanish. All of them. Every manufactured fine, every added year—you walk out of here owing nothing but gratitude."

"And if I don't sign?"

"Then the account stands." Quill opened a ledger—*his* ledger, the private one, bound in green leather gone soft with handling—and turned it toward Orsen with terrible gentleness. "And I'm afraid there have been... additional entries."

Orsen looked down.

There, in Quill's neat hand, itemized like everything else:

*Milk, stolen, forty-one instances.*
*Candle wax, unaccounted.*
*Bread, half-loaf, recurring.*
*One (1) infant, concealed.*

The room went very quiet. The foghorn moaned again, farther away now, as if even it wished to be elsewhere.

"You knew," Orsen said. Their voice came out strange—flat, small, like a coin dropped on stone.

"Vasquez." Quill closed the ledger and laced his fingers atop it. "I have known since the second week. Do you imagine a man who audits ten thousand entries a year cannot notice forty-one missing bottles of milk? I let it continue because I wanted to see something." His eyes, pale and patient as a fish's, held Orsen's. "I wanted to see what you would pay. Everyone pays, eventually. The divers pay with their lungs. Your sister pays with her pride. The strikers will pay with their backs. I only ever wondered what *you* would put on the scale. And now I know." He pushed the list forward one final inch. "You'll pay other people's names. That's the market rate. It always has been."

* * *

Later—or perhaps much later; time in that office moved like syrup—Orsen walked out through the warehouse with the list inside their shirt, against their chest, where it felt less like paper than like a coal.

They passed the rope loft without looking up. They had gotten good at that, at the art of the unturned eye. For years it had kept Emrys alive: never look directly at what you love when you are in a place that eats what it sees. But tonight the discipline cost something. Tonight the loft seemed to hum above them, full of sleeping weight and small breaths, and Orsen walked beneath it carrying eleven names and felt like a man smuggling plague.

Marisol was waiting in the shadow of the salt barrels, arms crossed, jaw set in the particular angle that meant she had already decided something and was merely awaiting the world's agreement.

"Well?"

"He offered three berths."

Marisol went still. In the lamplight from the high windows her face did something complicated—hope arriving and being strangled in the same instant, because she was smart enough to hear the rest of the sentence before it was spoken.

"For what," she said. Not a question. She'd heard the rest of the sentence in her bones.

"Names. Strikers. Eleven of them." Orsen pulled the list out and held it between them. "I'm supposed to sign by morning bell."

Marisol stared at the paper. Her lips moved slightly, reading. Orsen watched her read and understood, with a sorrow that had stopped surprising them long ago, exactly what she was seeing: not eleven strangers. Eleven tickets. A berth for her, a berth for Emrys, a berth for Father, and all it would cost was the signatures of people whose faces Orsen knew and whose names Marisol had never needed to learn.

"You could do it," Marisol said quietly. "You could sign it and no one would ever have to know it was you. That's the whole point of you, isn't it? Nobody sees you." She caught herself, winced. "I didn't mean—"

"No," Orsen said. "You did. And you're right. That's the whole point of me." They folded the list along its crease. "But there's a girl up in that loft who was left at a door with instructions to forget her face. And I've been thinking, Mari, that maybe the company doesn't get to teach me any more lessons about forgetting."

Marisol's eyes flashed—the old resentment, the old fire, the argument they'd been having since they were children fighting over a single egg. "So we all stay? We all burn with the orchard district because your conscience needs the company? Papa can't breathe, Orsen. *Papa.*"

"Papa dove for pearls under a drowned orchard because the company priced the air," Orsen said. "Everything the company takes, it first teaches us to call fair. I'm done calling it fair."

They stood facing each other across the folded paper, and the distance between them was four feet wide and twenty years deep, and then—because she was Marisol, because under all the flint there had always been the girl who shared her egg—her shoulders dropped half an inch.

"What will you do?" she asked.

What Orsen did was this.

* * *

They waited until the third watch, when the warehouse slept and even the rats conducted their business with a certain hush. They took the list to the counting room's great iron stove—the one that heated the auditor's seal-wax, the one Quill polished like a relic—and they fed it in, corner first.

Paper burns quickly. Orsen had expected that. What they hadn't expected was how *loudly* it seemed to burn, though of course it made no sound at all beyond a soft crumpling whisper, a sound like a small animal settling into straw. The corners curled black. The eleven names went amber, then ember, then nothing. The stove door swung shut on its own weight with a click that sounded, in the dark, like a lock turning.

Orsen stood there with their palms out to the grate's fading warmth and felt—not triumph. Nothing so clean. They felt terrified. They felt lighter than they had since the yellow flag went up over the harbor, and both feelings arrived together, tangled, inseparable, the way grief and certainty had been tangled in them for weeks now. Somewhere in the dark of their own chest a voice that sounded suspiciously like their father said: *good. Fear means you finally picked something worth being afraid for.*

They were still standing there, warming hands on a fire that had already eaten their future, when the voice came from behind them.

"Ashes by morning," Quill said. "How tidy."

Orsen did not jump. Years of practice held their body still even as their heart tried to climb out through their throat. They turned slowly.

Quill stood in the doorway in his nightclothes and coat, holding a lamp, and the light threw his shadow enormous up the shelving—a giant with a small, tired face. He looked past Orsen at the stove. He looked for a long time.

"Do you know," he said at last, conversationally, "in nineteen years at this desk, no one has ever burned a list of mine. Clerks have lost lists. Rats have eaten lists. Once a clerk's wife used one to light the supper stove, and he swore it was an accident until the day he died, poor fool, because the alternative was worse." He stepped closer. The lamp swayed; shadows rocked. "But no one has ever looked me in the face and chosen the fire. I confess I don't entirely know the procedure."

"There isn't one," Orsen said. "That's rather the point."

Something moved across Quill's face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. He set the lamp on the counting table with exaggerated care, the way a man does when his hands want to do something else.

"You understand what you've forfeited. Three berths. Gone. The chartered boats leave within the fortnight, and after that the quarantine holds until the fever burns itself out, and the fever does not care about your conscience." He paused. "It also does not care about infants."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Orsen listened to it fall all the way down.

"You knew," they said again, but differently now. Before it had been a question. Now it was a wall they were putting up between themselves and this man, brick by brick, because the alternative was to come apart in front of him.

"I knew," Quill agreed. "I've known for months. I told you—I was curious what you'd pay." He straightened his cuffs, a small precise gesture, armor for a small precise man. "I assumed money. Then favors. Then, when the strike began, information. I did not assume—" he gestured at the stove, at the ash, at the whole impossible evening, "—*this*. There is no column for this, Vasquez. There is no entry. You have presented me with an unbalanced account, and I find I dislike it intensely."

"Then write it down," Orsen said. "You're good at that."

For a heartbeat—one long, ringing heartbeat—Orsen genuinely believed Quill might laugh. The man's mouth actually twitched at one corner, a seismic event, tectonic, unprecedented in the geological record of Mr. Quill's face. Then it stilled.

"Here is what happens now," Quill said, and his voice had gone quiet in the way that quiet things are often more frightening than loud ones. "Tomorrow I report a theft. Not milk. Something larger, something that requires the constables and the company magistrates and, eventually, a cell in the quarantine brig—which, I should mention, has been repurposed for overflow from the fever wards." He picked up his lamp. "Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless the child is gone by morning bell. Out of my warehouse, off my books, out of Vellamare if you can manage it. I am not a sentimental man, Vasquez, whatever you're currently imagining. But I am a *tidy* one, and an infant hidden in a rope loft during a plague evacuation is a loose thread, and loose threads, in my experience, unravel into scandals." He lifted the lamp and turned toward the door, then paused, his back to Orsen. "Consider it a fine. Consider it the last entry in your account. Paid in full, one child, delivered."

"And after that?" Orsen asked. "Do I get my freedom? My years back?"

Quill stood in the doorway a moment longer, silhouetted against the corridor's dark, and when he answered, his voice had something in it Orsen had never heard there before—something that might, in a better man, have been called regret.

"After that," he said, "you get the morning. Which is more than the strikers will get, and more than you deserved, and less than you think." The lamp bobbed away down the corridor. His voice drifted back, thin and dry as dust: "Morning bell comes early, Vasquez. It always has. That's the one piece of arithmetic nobody argues with."

* * *

Orsen climbed to the rope loft in the dark, by feel, by memory, the way they had climbed it a hundred nights with warm milk in a stolen bottle and terror riding their shoulders like a second child.

Emrys was awake. Of course she was—infants keep their own watches, and theirs ran on trouble. She was lying in the nest of sacking, waving one fist at the rafters as though negotiating terms with the roof, and when Orsen's face appeared over the loft edge she produced her entire vocabulary: a squeak, a gurgle, and a sound like a tiny hinge that wanted oiling.

"Hush," Orsen whispered, gathering her up. She smelled of milk and sacking and the particular sweetness that was hers alone, the smell Orsen would have recognized in a crowd of thousands, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea. "Hush, little orchard. We're going."

Going where, they did not yet know. The boats were counted, the berths were posted, and every legal road out of Vellamare had just been nailed shut by a small tidy man in his nightclothes. But Orsen had spent their whole life learning the weight of small things, and they knew a truth the ledgers had never managed to record: that the heaviest thing in any warehouse is a locked door, and the lightest thing in the world is a plan that hasn't been written down yet.

Below, across the black water, the quarantine fires had begun to show their first orange teeth along the ridge of the orchard district. And somewhere down at Pier Nine, where the strikers camped among their banners and their hunger, there were dockhands who owed Orsen favors, and a chartered fishing boat with a hold that smelled of mackerel, and a dawn that was coming whether anyone signed anything or not.

Morning bell came early.

Orsen was already gone.
