## 16. Flight Through the Rope Loft

The rope loft smelled of hemp and old salt and, if one pressed one's nose into the darkest corner behind the coiled hawsers, of baby. Orsen had learned in fifteen months exactly which smells meant trouble—sour milk gone off, the sharp ammonia of a wet cloth left too long—and which ones were merely the smell of a child growing in a hiding place. This morning it was the good kind: warm skin, faint sweetness of the stolen honey rubbed into her gums, the particular dusty-sugar scent of Emrys herself.

She was awake. Of course she was awake. Children knew, somehow, when the world had tilted.

"Hush now," Orsen murmured, gathering her up with the blanket and the small bundle that held everything else—a change of clouts, the carved wooden pear their mother had once held, three hard biscuits, and the forged berth chit folded into an oilcloth square and sewn, by Marisol's steady hands, into the hem of Orsen's coat. "Hush. Today we go."

Emrys regarded them with her mother's—no, *some* mother's—dark eyes and did not hush. She made the low, conversational gurgle that was her way of filing complaints, and Orsen pressed her close against their chest beneath the loose coat they'd worn open all winter for exactly this purpose, and bounced her on instinct alone.

Down below, through the floorboards, the warehouse had already begun its morning argument. The quarantine had tightened two days past; no ships in, no ships out but the company's own, and the company's own were loading only what favored families could carry. Everything else—the rice, the salt fish, the barrels of oil the lower quarter needed to cook and to see by at night—sat locked in Halloway warehouses while prices climbed like ivy. Orsen had heard the dockhands talking before dawn. They were not talking the way men talk about weather anymore.

They were talking the way men talk just before something breaks.

* * *

Marisol found them at the loft ladder, as arranged, carrying a basket of mended sails she had no intention of mending.

"She'll not stay quiet," Orsen whispered.

"She never stays quiet." Marisol set down the basket and took the child with the practiced motion of a woman who had raised half the tenement street whether she wanted to or not. For a moment—just a moment—she looked at Emrys the way she looked at nothing else, all the sharp edges of her going soft as wet sand. Then she wrapped the blanket higher over the small face and became again the Marisol who counted everything and forgave little. "Riot starts at the oil delivery. Tam said nine bells. The guards will all be pulled to the doors."

"And you?"

"I'll be at Father's bedside, weeping piteously, asking any guard who'll listen whether the company intends to let a dying pearl diver suffocate in his own tenement while clerks load silver candlesticks onto their boats." She shrugged. "Grief is excellent cover. It's the one thing this city has never rationed."

Orsen almost smiled. Almost. "If it goes wrong—"

"Then it goes wrong, and I will stand there holding your child"—she said *your*, though they both knew whose child Emrys had become, in every way that mattered—"and looking too stupid to question. That has been my whole strategy since birth, brother. It works better than yours."

"Which is?"

"Being invisible."

"You're the one who taught me," Orsen said, and Marisol's mouth twisted—not quite a smile, not quite grief—and she kissed Emrys's blanket-wrapped head once, hard, and pushed the bundle back into Orsen's arms.

"Nine bells," she said. "Don't be early. Early men get remembered."

* * *

Quill came at eight.

Orsen knew it the way one knows weather changing—by the way the warehouse boys straightened, the way the tally clerks suddenly found urgency in their ledgers. Mr. Quill entered through the water door with his coat brushed and his boots dry despite the rain, because rain, like hunger, happened only to other people.

"Vasquez," he said, pleasantly, which was how Quill said dangerous things. "A word."

Orsen set down the coil they'd been pretending to inspect and followed him to the counting room, hands loose, face empty, heart conducting itself in triple time against their ribs where Emrys was not—Emrys was forty feet above in the rope loft, wrapped in silence, fed and gummed with honey precisely so she would sleep. *Please sleep. Please, little orchard, sleep.*

"The last manifest," Quill said, tapping a page without looking at it, "is short four tins of milk."

"Milk goes sour," Orsen said. "I threw it out."

"In January you threw out milk. In February you threw out milk. You are either the most wasteful man in Vellamare or the most generous." Quill looked up then, and his eyes were pale and patient, like something that lived under stones and had never needed to hurry. "You know what I find remarkable about you, Vasquez? Fifteen years, and I have never once seen you want anything. Every man wants something. Money, drink, a woman, revenge. You want nothing. A man who wants nothing cannot be bought." He folded his hands. "But he can be *broken* more cheaply than any of them."

Orsen kept their face still. It was the oldest skill. Carry little, want less, be unseen.

"There is a fishing boat leaving Pier Nine at dawn tomorrow," Quill continued. "Chartered irregularly. Two berths spoken for by persons whose names I am choosing not to look up until this conversation concludes. And somewhere in my warehouse—or perhaps not in it anymore—is a child who does not exist on any roll, born to no mother, fed from my stores." He said it all gently, the way a man describes a chess position he has already won. "I don't want the child, Vasquez. Children are noise and expense. What I want is *you*. On my side of a certain ledger. And I have found, in a long career, that nothing moves a quiet man faster than the sound of a small thing crying where he cannot reach it."

He let that sit in the room like damp.

"So," he said. "Shall I send two men up to the rope loft, or shall we discuss the strikers' names instead?"

* * *

Later, Orsen would never remember deciding. There was no decision, only the ancient animal arithmetic of the body, and the body answered before thought could interfere.

"The loft's empty," they heard themselves say, flat as ballast stone. "I moved her out three days ago."

Quill's eyes narrowed a fraction. It was the most expression he'd ever spent.

"Where."

"With a wet nurse in Salt Row. A woman paid to forget faces—you know the type, sir, you employ them." And here Orsen did something new, something Marisol would have applauded: they leaned forward slightly, and let a hunger show. Not for the child—for escape. "But if those berths are spoken for, and I'm to have none, then I've got nothing to sell you, have I? Names for a berth. That was the price. Three berths, you said. I'll give you names when I see my father's name on a list."

It was a lie braided out of truth, and it was fluent, and it worked—not because Quill believed it, but because Quill was a creature of paper, and paper required verification, and verification required time. He studied Orsen for a long, airless moment.

"Nine bells," he said at last, mildly, "there will be a disturbance at the oil doors. The guards will attend it. You will remain in the warehouse, Vasquez, where you can be found." He rose, buttoned his coat. "We will speak again after I've sent a boy to Salt Row."

* * *

Nine bells came like a struck match.

It began, as Tam had promised, with the oil wagon—but it did not end there, because hunger is patient all morning and impatient by noon. By the time the first barrel went over, it wasn't one crowd outside the oil doors, it was three crowds, and then it was simply *the quarter*, pouring through the warehouse gates in a brown river of elbows and fury, and the six guards assigned to hold the yard discovered that six is not a number.

Orsen was supposed to be in the warehouse. Orsen was, technically, in the warehouse—moving through it, along the wall, in the shadow of the shelving, with a sleeping child strapped beneath their coat and their heart beating so loudly they feared it would wake her. Around them the great room erupted: doors splintering, men swarming the salt bins, someone singing a drinking song badly at the top of the rice pile, a clerk standing on his own desk shouting numbers at people who had stopped believing in numbers years ago.

A barrel of lamp oil rolled past, borne aloft like a relic.

*Rope loft,* said the fear. *Get her out through the loft, down the cargo chain at the seaward window, along the foundation shelf to the drain grate—*

They climbed the ladder into smoke-colored light and found Marisol already there, breathless, sail basket abandoned, holding out her arms.

"They took the north gate," she gasped. "Whole street's coming through. Orsen—it's beautiful, actually, I hate to say it, all these people finally wanting things—"

"Window. Now. Talk later."

The cargo chain was cold and green with damp. Orsen had gone down it a hundred times with sacks on their shoulder; they went down it now with a child against their chest, slowly, hand over hand, feeling every link, hearing Emrys stir and begin—God, no—to fuss. Halfway down, a voice rose from the chaos below, thin and furious:

*"Milk! There's milk in the loft, I saw the tins—"*

And the ladder shook with climbing hands.

* * *

What saved them was the riot itself. The first man over the loft edge came down the chain like a starving cat, saw Orsen below him, and shouted *"He's got the milk!"*—which was true enough, in a way, and turned forty heads toward the wrong prize. The crowd surged toward Orsen not as hunters but as beggars, hands outstretched, and it was Marisol—who had come down after, quick as a rat along the foundation shelf—who turned and screamed at them with magnificent contempt:

"That's my brother, you fools, and that's his *sick child*, and if one of you touches him I'll tell the whole quarter where the company keeps the *salted pork!*"

Pork. The word detonated. The river of humanity reversed itself and thundered back toward the far stores, and in the eddy it left behind, Orsen ran—along the foundation shelf, bent double, one arm cradling the bundle, the other slapping flat against cold stone—with Marisol behind them and Emrys, miracle of miracles, waking fully now and announcing her displeasure to the sky.

"It's all right," Orsen gasped, to her, to themselves, to nobody. "It's all right, little orchard, we're going, we're going—"

The drain grate was where it had always been, rusted at one corner, wide enough for a thin man with nothing to carry. Orsen had carried nothing their whole life. They slid through sideways with the child pressed between chest and stone, tearing their coat, tearing the hem where the chit was sewn—*the chit*—feeling the oilcloth catch and tear free and vanish into the dark behind them—

No time. No time for anything but out.

They came up through the culvert into the gray drizzle of the tideline, soaked, bleeding from a scraped ear, with Emrys screaming like a tiny kettle and Marisol wriggling out behind them swearing in three languages. Behind them the warehouse roared and smoked. Ahead, across the mud flats, the tented camp of Pier Nine flickered with cookfires, and beyond it, dim in the rain, lay a mackerel-smelling boat and the arithmetic of dawn.

One berth left. Room for two. Father drowning on his lungs at home. Sister beside them, drenched and defiant. Child wailing in their arms.

"We made it," Marisol panted, laughing or sobbing, impossible to tell. "You mad, invisible ghost of a brother—we actually—"

"Orsen Vasquez."

The voice came from the top of the culvert embankment, and it belonged to neither Quill nor any guard. It belonged to a boy—one of Quill's runners, eleven years old, panting, holding out a folded paper with the company seal dripping in the rain.

"For the man with the child," the boy recited, bored and breathless at once. "Mr. Quill says: Salt Row was empty, the wet nurse was never real, and he's already moved your father's name onto tonight's list—which list, Mr. Quill says, can just as easily come *off*. He says come to the counting room by midnight, or he sends men to the tenement, and next time he won't need a note."

Marisol read it twice, though she didn't need to. Then she looked at Orsen—at the torn coat, the empty hem, the screaming child, the burning glow rising now over the orchard district where the fires had jumped the quarantine line—and said, very quietly:

"He doesn't know the chit's gone. He doesn't know we can't prove anything anymore." Her jaw set. "Midnight gives us twelve hours. The boat leaves at dawn."

Below them, at Pier Nine, a lantern swung at the end of the fishing boat's mast, swinging like a pendulum, counting down.
