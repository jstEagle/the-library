## 17. The Last Berth

The chit was real. That was the first miracle of the night, and Orsen turned it over three times under the guttering lamp of the rope loft, as if a third look might reveal it to be paper and wishful thinking after all.

*One berth. The *Maren Lee*, chartered, dawn tide, Pier Four. Two souls permitted.*

Two souls. Orsen read it again, slower, letting each word land like a stone dropped into still water. Two. Not four. Not five. Two.

Below, in the warehouse's shadowed aisles, Emrys slept in her crate-bed among the coiled hawsers, one fist curled against her cheek, breathing with the small, furious regularity of a child who had never once considered that breathing might be taken from her. Upstairs in the counting room, on a pallet Orsen had made from sacking and stolen kindness, their father coughed the long, wet cough that had become the only clock Orsen trusted anymore. And somewhere across the quarantined city, Marisol was walking back through streets that smelled of vinegar and fear, having gone to trade their mother's ring for blankets.

Orsen sat down on a coil of rope and did the arithmetic they had been avoiding for two days.

* * *

The arithmetic went like this.

Father could not walk to Pier Four unaided. He could barely sit up without the world tilting around him like a ship in weather. Dr. Ferrow — who came now only when paid in pearls, and there were no more pearls, because Mother had spent the last of them refusing to sell hers and Father had spent the rest of his life being proud about it — Dr. Ferrow had said the word *water* twice, and both times he had meant Father's lungs. *They are filling,* he'd said, in the gentle voice doctors use when they mean *drowning*. *It is like drowning, from the inside.*

So: Father needed carrying, or at least supporting, which meant one strong pair of arms committed before anyone else boarded.

Emrys could not travel alone. She was not yet two. She could say *Or* and *no* and *more*, and she said them with such authority that strangers on the dock sometimes obeyed her, but no captain alive would accept a toddler as a passenger without an adult attached. One berth for her, then, and one for whoever held her.

Which left Marisol. Which left Orsen. Which left one berth between them, and four people, and a boat leaving at dawn whether the arithmetic was finished or not.

Orsen set the chit down on their knee and pressed the heels of their hands into their eyes until the darkness bloomed orange.

There was a version of this where the answer was simple. There was a version where you counted up the useful lives and sent the strongest, the way the Halloway Company counted cargo — but Orsen had spent twenty years learning exactly how the company counted, and they would sooner have thrown the chit into the harbor than think like Mr. Quill for even one hour.

No. The arithmetic had to be done another way. The way Mother would have done it, standing at the edge of the drowned orchard with salt water in her hair and her last pearl in her fist.

* * *

Marisol came back past midnight, smelling of rain and other people's smoke, and found Orsen sitting where the lamp couldn't reach, watching Emrys sleep.

"You look," Marisol said, setting down the blanket bundle, "like a man doing sums he already knows will come out wrong."

"I'm not a man."

"No," Marisol agreed, dropping onto the rope bale beside them. "You're something worse. You're the responsible one." She said it the way she always did — half insult, half confession — and then, quieter: "How many?"

"One berth. Two souls."

Marisol was silent for a long moment. In the loft below, Emrys sighed in her sleep, a small theatrical sound, as if even dreaming required negotiation.

"Then it's me and the baby," Marisol said. "Obviously."

"It is not obviously anything—"

"Don't." Marisol's voice cracked like a whip, and then softened just as fast, because Marisol's anger had never been able to hold its shape for long; it always melted into something rawer underneath. "Don't do the thing where you decide for everyone and call it protection. I've watched you do it my whole life. You gave me your bread ration for six years and called it not liking bread. You took the night shifts and called it preferring the dark. You're going to stand on that pier tomorrow and put me on that boat and call it love, and I am telling you right now, Orsen Vasquez, I will jump off the blessed thing."

"You can't raise her alone on nothing—"

"I raised *you*," Marisol said. "Half-raised. Someone had to, after Mama. I was seven and I braided your hair and told you the sea wouldn't take us because I decided it wasn't allowed to. I can do it again. I'm better at it now. I have teeth."

Orsen almost laughed. It came out as something broken instead. "You hate the sea."

"I hate what the sea did. Different animal." Marisol pulled her knees up, making herself small in the way she only ever did when she was frightened and refused to show it any larger way. "You know what I dreamed? All those years in the tenement, listening to Papa cough through the wall? I dreamed of a place with trees. Real ones. Fruit ones. Where nobody weighed me." She glanced down toward the crate where Emrys slept. "She should have trees. Whatever happens to the rest of us, she should have trees."

"She'll have you," Orsen said. "That's better than trees."

"It isn't," Marisol said. "But it's what there is." She was quiet a moment, and then, in a smaller voice: "And you? What happens to you, if I take your berth?"

Orsen looked at the lamp, at the ropes hanging in their patient coils, at all the small machinery of a life built entirely out of borrowed time. "I've survived worse than staying."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have."

* * *

Near dawn — though dawn had become a rumor lately, the sky over Vellamare so thick with quarantine smoke that morning arrived gray and apologetic — Orsen climbed to the counting room pallet.

Father was awake. He was often awake now; sleep had grown shallow water over him too, and he surfaced from it coughing, gasping, sometimes calling a name that wasn't anyone's anymore. But tonight his eyes were clear. Lucid. The fever had burned off whatever fog usually lived behind them, and what remained was sharp and tired and terribly present.

"There you are," he said. His voice was a ruin, gravel dragged through brine. "Sit. My legs ache and you fidget when you stand."

Orsen sat. Father studied them for a while with those pearl-gray eyes — Mother's eyes, everyone had always said, the sea's own color lent to a diver — and then he said, "Your sister came by. Loudly."

"She does everything loudly."

"She says you're planning to stay." A pause. A breath that whistled faintly, like wind through a cracked window. "She says you're planning to give them the berth and stay."

Orsen said nothing, which was itself an answer, and Father closed his eyes as if the silence hurt worse than the coughing.

"I'm dying, little fish."

"Papa—"

"We'll skip the part where you argue. We haven't time and I haven't breath." He opened his eyes again, and there was something in them Orsen had rarely seen: not the fierce pride, not the stubbornness, but something gentler and far more frightening. Tenderness without armor. "Listen to me. If I go on that boat, I die in three days anyway. Maybe four. The doctor's lungs are honest even if his fees aren't. I will spend those days coughing into your sister's shoulder on open water, taking food from the baby's mouth, taking *room* from the baby's mouth, and I will die with salt spray in my face thinking: *my child traded their life for a berth I didn't need.*" He lifted a hand — it trembled, but he lifted it — and pressed one finger against Orsen's sternum, right over the heart. "I refuse. Do you hear me? I dove forty feet down for twenty years. I know how to hold my breath. This is me holding it. This is me choosing when."

"The berth is yours by rights—"

"Rights." Father smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of love. "Your mother had rights too. Right to her pearl. Right to her orchard story. The world asked her to sell and she said no, and it cost her everything, and I have never once — not once, in all these years — wished she'd said yes." His finger stayed pressed against Orsen's chest. "Defiance is the only inheritance I have to give you. Take it. Don't waste it being noble in the way the company taught you — the quiet way, the invisible way, the way where you disappear so others don't have to grieve. Be noble out loud for once in your life."

Orsen's throat had closed entirely. They managed, barely: "And if I can't let you go?"

"Then you'll have to dive deep," Father whispered, "and find me at the bottom of the orchard, and tell me yourself why you sold the pearl." His hand fell away. His eyes were drifting now, the lucidity ebbing like a tide going out. "Emrys," he murmured. "Good name. Good name. Trees under the water... still bearing fruit... your mama saw them..." And then he slept, truly slept, his breath rattling in his chest like pebbles in a jar, and Orsen knelt beside him in the gray light and wept the way they had learned to as a child: silently, completely, without moving a single muscle that anyone could see.

But there was no one to see. So after a while, they stopped hiding it.

* * *

Emrys woke at first light demanding *more* — more of what was never specified, more being, in her philosophy, a complete and sufficient request — and Orsen fed her the last of the goat's milk stretched with water, and she grabbed Orsen's nose with one sticky hand and held on with the grip of someone who has decided, permanently, that this nose belongs to her.

"Or," she said.

"Yes," Orsen agreed. "Or."

"Or *or*."

"Or or," Orsen confirmed solemnly, and Emrys nodded once, satisfied, as though a difficult treaty had been ratified.

Then Marisol appeared at the top of the loft ladder, hair wild, eyes red-rimmed but dry, carrying the bundle of blankets and a look on her face that Orsen knew from childhood — the look she wore when she had lost an argument to herself overnight and arrived at a conclusion she hated.

"I've been thinking," she announced.

"Dangerous."

"Shut up." She sat down hard on the rope bale. "Here's what happens. We go to the pier. All four of us. And we put *Papa* on that boat with the baby."

Orsen stared at her. "He can't travel. His lungs—"

"He can't travel *and live*, no. But he can travel and *choose*." Marisol's jaw was set. "He told me this morning — before you, don't look like that, he wakes early now — he told me he refuses to be carried anywhere. Fine. Then we don't carry him. We put him on that boat with Emrys, because a man who spent his life underwater deserves to leave it looking at the horizon instead of a ceiling, and because a baby cannot go alone, and because—" her voice wobbled, and she steadied it with visible effort, "—because then it's you and me for the second soul, and *that* fight I win, Orsen, I swear it, I will bite the harbormaster if I have to—"

"And you?" Orsen said quietly. "Who puts *you* on the boat?"

Marisol stopped. For a moment the whole plan stood there in the lamplight, beautiful and hollow, and both of them saw it at once: every arrangement ended the same way. Someone stayed. The arithmetic never balanced. Two souls, four hearts, and the remainder was always a person.

"That's the thing about sums," Marisol said finally, very softly. "Papa used to say it. However you move the numbers around, the total doesn't change. You can only choose *which* pain."

Down in the crate, Emrys sneezed — a tiny, furious sound — and then laughed at her own sneeze, delighted, as though she had invented it. Both of them looked at her. Neither spoke.

Outside, the harbor bell began to ring. Not the quarantine bell's slow toll — something quicker, brighter, urgent. Voices rose along the waterfront. Boots on cobblestones. A boy's voice, hoarse from shouting, running past the warehouse door:

"*The Maren Lee sails at dawn! Last berth! Last berth! They're counting souls at Pier Four!*"

Orsen stood. The chit felt suddenly heavy in their pocket, heavier than any cargo they had ever weighed by hand — two souls' worth of weight, in a piece of paper no bigger than their palm.

Marisol stood too. She picked up Emrys, who reached her arms out toward Orsen with absolute, uncomplicated trust, and said, "Or!"

"Yes," Orsen said, and their voice did not shake, because twenty years of practice held it steady. "We're going. All of us. To the pier."

And they went — out into the smoke-colored dawn, toward the water, toward the boat, toward the unbearable sum waiting at the end of Pier Four.
