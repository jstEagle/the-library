## 18. Which Family Member

The fishing boat came in on the last of the ebb tide, riding low and filthy, her hull scarred with barnacles the color of old teeth. Her captain was a woman named Bree Hallowell—no relation to the Halloways, and quick to say so with a knife's edge in her voice—and she had agreed to carry two souls out of Vellamare for a price Orsen had already paid twice over: once in coin, once in the burning of a list.

Two souls. That was the arithmetic. It had been the arithmetic for three days now, and Orsen had carried it the way they carried everything—close to the body, saying nothing, letting the weight settle into the shoulders where no one could see it shift.

Dawn was still an hour off. The quay smelled of tar and smoke and the sour-sweet rot of fruit left too long in the heat, because the orchard district was burning and had been burning since midnight, and the wind off the water carried the smell of it everywhere, into every corner of the city, as if the harbor itself had begun to grieve.

Orsen stood at the foot of the pier with a handcart, and in the handcart sat everything that remained of their family.

Father lay against the flour sacks, propped as upright as his lungs would allow. He was very light now. That was the thing no one warned you about the dying—how the body economized, how it gave up its weight piece by piece like a debtor settling accounts. His hands were folded on his chest with a dignity he had not possessed in weeks, and his eyes were open, and they were fixed on the sky above the burning quarter where the smoke made a second, darker dawn.

Marisol sat on the cart's edge with Emrys in her lap. The baby was awake—of course she was awake, she was always awake at the hours when sleeping would have been merciful—and she was gnawing with tremendous seriousness on the knot of Marisol's shawl, making small satisfied sounds, as though the end of the world were a thing one could teethe on.

"You're staring at the boat," Marisol said. "You've been staring at the boat for a quarter hour. It hasn't gotten any bigger."

"I'm checking her lines."

"You're counting her berths."

Orsen said nothing, which was, as always, an answer.

Marisol shifted Emrys to her shoulder and stood, and in the gray light she looked so much like their mother that Orsen's chest ached with it—the same sharp chin, the same way of planting her feet as though the ground owed her rent. "Orsen. Look at me. We've been over this."

"We've been over it your way."

"Then say it your way. Out loud. For once in your life, out loud."

The tide sucked at the pilings beneath them. Somewhere behind, in the burning quarter, a roof beam went down with a sound like a great sigh, and Emrys startled and began to cry, and Marisol rocked her, and Orsen watched the two of them—the sister and the child, the past and the future, both of them leaning into each other like masts in a wind—and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only when every other thought has been burned away, that the arithmetic had solved itself days ago. They had simply been refusing to read the answer.

* * *

It had solved itself, in fact, in Father's room, two nights before, when his fever broke just long enough for him to be lucid, and lucid Father was a rare and dangerous thing, because lucid Father noticed everything.

He had noticed Orsen counting coins on the table. He had noticed Marisol packing in the fierce, silent way she packed, as though each garment were an argument she was winning. He had noticed Emrys asleep in the drawer they'd lined with his old diving shirt, and he had looked at that drawer for a long time before he said anything at all.

"Your mother dove under an orchard," he said, to no one, to the ceiling, to the sea that had taken her. "Trees still standing down there. Fruit gone to stone. She said the fish swam through the branches like they were birds come home to roost." His breath caught, held, released. "She never sold the last pearl. I never asked her why. I think I was afraid of the answer."

"You're rambling, Papa," Marisol said gently.

"I am dying," he corrected, with the mild precision of a man stating the weather. "It makes a man honest. Orsen. Come here."

Orsen came. Father took their hand, and his grip was still strong, still the grip of a man who had pulled pearls from forty feet of black water, and he held it the way he had held it when Orsen was nine and the Halloway clerk had come with papers, and Father had squeezed and squeezed and let them go anyway, because there had been no other choice, because there is never any other choice until suddenly there is one and it is unbearable.

"You have a berth," Father said. "Marisol has a berth. The baby—" he paused on the word *baby*, tasting it, the way he must have tasted the word *daughter* once and found it too sweet to say aloud—"the baby has half of one. And I have lungs full of the sea, come home to roost." He smiled at his own joke, a small, cracked thing. "Do the sums, child. You were always better at sums than they ever knew."

"Papa—"

"I have done my diving," he said. "Forty feet down, in the dark, holding my breath. I know how to let go of a rope. Let me teach you the last thing I know."

* * *

Now, at the foot of the pier, Orsen finally said it out loud.

"Marisol goes. The baby goes. I stay."

"You *stay*—" Marisol's voice cracked like a whip, and Emrys wailed in sympathy, and Bree Hallowell looked up from her deck and pretended, with great professionalism, to be deaf.

"Someone has to be here when the quarantine lifts. Someone has to find out where the boat lands. Someone has to—" Orsen stopped, because the reasons had run out, and they both knew it, and what remained underneath the reasons was the truth, and the truth was this: a berth held two, and there were three of them who could live, and Father had already spent himself down to nothing, and the only question left was which of the living would pay.

"You swore," Marisol said, and her voice went low and terrible. "In the rope loft, the night I found her, you swore we'd all go together. You swore it on Mama's pearl."

"I lied," Orsen said. "I've gotten good at it. Ask anyone."

Marisol stared at them. And then—this was the thing Orsen would carry for the rest of their life, the thing that would keep them warm in cold years—Marisol began to laugh. It was a broken, furious, weeping laugh, the laugh of a person who has been out-argued by someone they love, and she laughed until Emrys stopped crying to stare up at her, and then she pressed her forehead to the baby's and said, "You hear that? Your family is impossible. Both sides of it. All of it."

"Marisol—"

"Take her." She held Emrys out. "Take her to the boat. I can't walk and hold her and argue with you all at once, and I intend to argue the whole way down the pier."

* * *

The pier was chaos. It was always chaos now, but the dawn chaos had a particular quality—lanterns swinging, crates sliding, a customs officer shouting about papers to no one who had any, and above it all the smoke rolling off the orchard district like a black tide coming in over the land. Orsen walked into it with Emrys against their chest, wrapped in the diving shirt, and the baby turned her face into Orsen's neck and made her small grunting sound, her *I-am-where-I-belong* sound, and Orsen had to stop, once, halfway down the planks, and stand very still and breathe.

"Almost there," they told her. "Almost."

She patted their collarbone with one imperious hand, as if to say *well, get on with it, then*, and Orsen laughed—a wet, cracked sound—and got on with it.

At the gangplank, Bree Hallowell counted heads with her eyes and her eyes alone. "Two," she said. "You said two."

"Two." Orsen turned back. Marisol was coming down the pier with the handcart, and Father lay in it, and he had insisted on coming, had insisted with the last of his stubbornness, because he would not let his children walk to the water without him, not the last time.

They lifted him out at the plank's foot. He weighed almost nothing. He weighed everything.

"Captain," Orsen said. "One more fare. Paid in full." They pressed the last of the coins into Bree's palm—Quill's own silver, though Bree didn't know it and never would—and Bree looked at Father's gray face, and at Marisol's set jaw, and at the bundle in Orsen's arms, and something in her weathered face went soft and hard at the same time.

"Get him aboard," she said. "Quickly, before the harbor watch remembers they exist."

* * *

It happened on the gangplank, the way the worst and best things happen: quickly, and without ceremony.

Father's breath simply stopped holding. He had been climbing, one plank at a time, Marisol before him and Orsen behind with a hand at his back, and halfway up he stopped, and leaned back into Orsen's chest, and said—clearly, in the voice he'd used for telling the story of the drowned orchard, the story-voice, the voice that made the dark water sound like a place you might want to go—"There she is. The trees are still standing."

And then he was heavy. Heavier than he had been in months, heavier than he had ever been, all his weight coming home at once like a debt called in, and Orsen caught him, and Marisol caught him, and between them they held him on the swaying plank above the black water while Emrys, sensing nothing, slept against Orsen's collarbone.

They did not carry him up. They carried him down, back to the quay, and laid him on the stones where the tide could hear him, and Marisol knelt and pressed her forehead to his hand and did not make a sound, and Orsen knelt beside her and held his other hand, and his hand was already cooling, already going over to the sea's side of things, and his face was peaceful in a way it had not been in two years, in a way it had not been since before the papers, since before the company, since the days when he came up out of the water with pearls in his fist and an orchard growing in his stories.

"He chose," Marisol whispered at last. "Did you see? He chose. He got to choose."

Yes. That was the mercy of it, the only mercy, and Orsen held onto it the way a drowning man holds a rope: Father had not been subtracted. Father had *let go*—had done it himself, at his own depth, on his own breath, the last dive, the free one. He had looked up through the black water at his daughters and his granddaughter and he had opened his hand, and the pearl he had refused to sell his whole life he gave away at last, freely, to the tide, to the story, to them.

Bree Hallowell came halfway down the gangplank, and for once she did not pretend to be deaf. She stood over them in silence for a moment, hat in her hands like a churchgoer, and then she said, gruffly, "Tide won't wait. It never does. It's the one honest thing about it." And she waited anyway, a full minute, two, her eyes on the water, until Marisol rose.

* * *

Marisol stood at the gangplank with Emrys in her arms, and the sky behind her was going from gray to the color of a pearl, and the smoke over the orchard district was lit from beneath, gold at its edges, as though the burning trees had sent their light ahead to see them off.

"If you die in that fire," Marisol said, "I will never forgive you."

"Noted."

"If you live and never write, I will forgive you, and that will be worse."

"Noted."

She came forward then and pressed her forehead against Orsen's, the way she used to when they were small and the tenement floor was cold, two children making one warm place between them. "Emrys," she said softly, to the baby, "this is the one I told you about. The one who carried everything. Remember the face."

And Orsen—Orsen, who had been ordered once to forget a face and had built a whole life on the refusal—felt something turn over in their chest, vast and slow, like a ship coming about.

"Go," they said. "Before I do the sums again."

Marisol went. Bree's deckhands hauled the plank. The *Merry Adela*—filthy, scarred, magnificent—leaned out on the turning tide, and Marisol stood at her rail with Emrys held high, high, higher, the baby's fist reaching back toward the quay, toward Orsen, toward everything, until the distance took them both, and the harbor swallowed the boat, and the water kept its own counsel.

Orsen stood on the quay beside their father and watched until there was nothing to watch.

Then the smoke rolled down off the hill, thick and gold-edged and smelling of apples, of all things—apples, from the old orchard stores going up in the quarries—and it came over the pier like a tide of its own, and Orsen did not run from it.

They lifted what remained of their father, and turned, and walked into it.
