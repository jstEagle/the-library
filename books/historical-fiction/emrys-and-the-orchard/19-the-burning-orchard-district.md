## 19. The Burning Orchard District

The smoke did not burn, at first. It simply took things.

It took the color from the sky. It took the harbor—the ships, the masts, the yellow quarantine flags that had ruled the city for a season—all of it gone behind a curtain of gold-gray haze, as though Vellamare had decided, after everything, to close its eyes. It took the sound of the company bells, which Orsen had heard every morning of their life since the age of nine, and replaced them with a low, wet roaring, the sound of a fire eating an old quarter that had been waiting all its life to be eaten.

And in exchange, strangely, it gave Orsen something back.

It gave them anonymity. In smoke, everyone is unrecognizable. In smoke, a branded thief is only another silhouette stumbling uphill with a body in their arms.

"Almost there," Orsen said, though they had no idea where *there* was. Their father's head lolled against their shoulder. He weighed almost nothing now—a man who had once hauled pearl cages up from forty feet of water reduced to a bundle of coat and bone. "You always said the hill roads were crooked on purpose. So tax collectors would get lost. Well. They're crooked."

No answer. There would be no more answers. Orsen kept talking anyway, because the alternative was to listen to the fire.

* * *

The old quarter had been dying for thirty years. Everyone knew it and no one said it, the way you don't remind a sick relation that they are sick. The orchard district—so named for the orchards that had once climbed the terraces above the harbor before the sea wall failed and the salt took the roots—had become a place of rope-walks and cooperages and tenements leaning against each other like tired drunks holding each other up. The Halloway Company owned most of it on paper and maintained almost none of it in fact, and when the plague flags went up the harbor chain, someone in a clean office had drawn a line around the whole quarter on a map and written, in tidy clerk's hand: *burn.*

They called it sanitation. Fires set at the terraces to purify the air, drive out the miasma, protect the healthy parts of the city. The healthy parts being, as ever, the parts with money in them.

Orsen climbed through streets they half-remembered from childhood—Mother's streets, once, before the sea took her—and found them full of people who had also read the map's handwriting and understood what it meant for them.

The strikers had not left. That was the first thing Orsen understood, standing in the mouth of Cooper's Lane with their father in their arms while ash fell like gray snow. Pier Nine had struck over the quarantine conditions—the locked warehouses, the rotten grain, the wages docked while men coughed blood into their rags—and Quill had expected them to break, because Quill believed everyone had a price, and mostly he was right, and Orsen was living proof of the exceptions that proved him wrong. But here they were. Dockers and coopers and rope-makers and their wives and children, hauling buckets up stairs, tearing shingles off roofs before the sparks could find them, beating the fire back from one building at a time with the stubborn fury of people who had already lost everything except each other.

"You can't save it," Orsen said hoarsely, to no one, to the street. "It's going."

"Course it's going," said a voice nearby.

A woman sat on a doorstep not three yards away, soot to the elbows, a child asleep across her lap with a wet cloth over its face against the smoke. She was older than Orsen by twenty years, maybe more, with rope-scarred hands Orsen recognized without recognizing—dock hands, warehouse hands, hands like their own. She looked at the body in Orsen's arms with no alarm at all. In this quarter tonight, bodies were being carried in both directions.

"Been going since I was a girl," she said. "Fire's just finishing the company's argument for them." She shifted the child, nodded uphill. "There's a cellar under the old press house on Terraced Street. Stone walls, tile floor, my husband's idea of a joke—he built it to store cider, and now it's the only thing on the street that won't light. We've got maybe forty souls down there. You want in, you carry your weight. Everybody carries."

"I—" Orsen's throat closed. They looked down at their father. "He doesn't need carrying anymore. He needs burying."

The woman considered this the way she might consider a tide table: factually, without flinching.

"There's ground behind the press house," she said. "Old garden, gone to weeds. Ground's soft enough to dig, if your arms are willing. We'll lend you a spade." She looked at Orsen's face then, really looked, and something in her expression softened by a degree. "Was he your da?"

"Yes."

"Diver?"

Orsen blinked. "How did you—"

"Arms," she said. "Divers' children grow up carrying divers. You hold him like he's still heavier than you, even now." She patted the step beside her. "Sit a minute first. The dead don't mind a delay. It's the living that need their breath."

* * *

Orsen sat. It was true; their lungs were full of knives.

Below, the harbor was invisible, but Orsen could hear it—the last ships leaving, the winches, the shouting, the whole machinery of evacuation grinding toward the horizon with its favored cargo. Marisol and Emrys were somewhere out past the chain by now, or would be by dawn. A chartered fishing boat, a berth that held two, a sister who had gripped Orsen's wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *you come after us, do you hear me, you come after*, and Orsen had lied to her with a smile, fluently, the way they'd learned to lie to Quill—one lie earning another, each one smoother than the last.

*I'll follow in spring,* Orsen had said. *When the quarantine lifts.*

Marisol had known it was a lie. Orsen had watched her know it. And she had boarded anyway, because Emrys was asleep against her shoulder, and there are lies you accept because the truth would cost the person telling them the last thing they have left, which is the belief that they chose well.

"She has your scowl," Orsen told their father. "I never noticed until today. She got angry at me and it was like looking in a cracked mirror." A pause. Ash settled on the coat over his face, and Orsen brushed it away, gently, the way you'd brush hair from a sleeping forehead. "She's going to raise Emrys to argue with authority. I hope you're proud. I certainly couldn't stop it, and I'm the one who taught her to read ledgers."

The fire roared somewhere below the lane, taking a cooperage, by the smell—hot pitch and split oak.

"You'd have liked the strikers," Orsen went on. Their voice had gone strange and calm, the way it went when the ledger finally balanced. "You spent forty years hauling up other men's pearls and they spent forty years hauling other men's cargo, and I think you'd have found a lot to agree on. Mostly that the company is a machine for turning backs into money." They laughed, and it came out wrong, half a sob wearing a laugh's clothes. "Mr. Quill would say that's just arithmetic. He loves arithmetic. He added six years to my indenture with fines I never saw itemized, and when I wouldn't inform for him he burned my name off the lists and wrote *thief* where it used to be." Orsen looked up at the burning sky. "But here's the thing, Da. Here's the thing I figured out too late and exactly on time."

The woman on the step pretended not to listen. Her child snored softly under the wet cloth.

"Invisible men can't be arrested," Orsen said. "A thief needs a name. I haven't got one anymore. He took it himself. Free of charge."

* * *

They buried Serafín Vasquez at dusk in the weed-choked garden behind the press house, with a borrowed spade and four witnesses.

That was how it worked here, Orsen learned. Nothing was asked; everything was understood. The rope-scarred woman—Bett, though she gave no name until hours later, and then only because Orsen was carrying water for her—stood at the head of the grave. Two strikers who had come up from the cellars for air stood at the foot. A boy of perhaps twelve held the lantern, and held it very seriously, as though the quality of his lantern-holding were the whole of his contribution and he intended it to be excellent.

Nobody knew any prayers for pearl divers, so Bett made one up on the spot, and it was better than anything in a book.

"He went down deep and he came up breathing," she said. "As many times as the sea allowed. More than it deserved. Put him down easy, friends—this one's earned the lying-down."

Orsen shoveled the earth themselves. Nobody offered to take the spade, which was its own kind of courtesy. When the last of it was tamped and the boy had laid a sprig of something green on the mound—rosemary, from somebody's windowsill pot, donated without comment—Orsen stood there with empty hands and discovered that empty hands, after so long a time carrying, had a weight all their own.

"He wanted to be buried facing the water," Orsen said. "So he could keep an eye on it."

"Then he's facing the wrong way," Bett said, "because the water's downhill." But she looked at the slope, and the smoke rolling over it, and corrected herself: "Still. Smoke's going seaward. Maybe he'll take the scenic route."

And Orsen, to their own astonishment, laughed. Really laughed. A short, cracked, entirely inappropriate sound that came up from somewhere below grief, and Bett laughed too, and the boy grinned, and somewhere overhead the fire ate another roof, and none of it mattered, and all of it did.

* * *

The cellar beneath the press house was low-ceilinged and smelled of old apple must, and it held, as promised, some forty souls.

Orsen descended the stone steps into lamplight and heat and the murmur of a small underground nation. Families had claimed corners with hung blankets. A copper stove glowed in the middle—vented through some ingenious arrangement of pipe and bucket that Orsen, who had spent nine years learning the geography of warehouses, examined with professional respect. Children slept in rows like stacked crates. An old man played a quiet game of cards with a woman who appeared to be winning systematically. Somebody had chalked a tally on the wall: names, and numbers beside them. Water carried. Bread baked. Hours stood watch.

"You're the diver's kid," Bett said, appearing at Orsen's elbow with a tin cup of something hot. It was cider, somehow, actual cider, from the press house's ancient stores. "Drink. You look like a rope that's been asked to be a knot."

"Thank you. For the grave. For—" Orsen gestured at everything: the cellar, the stove, the sleeping children, the improbable cider.

"Don't thank me. Work." She handed Orsen a second cup, steaming. "Carry that to Widow Corrag at the far end, she's got a cough. Then there's water wanting hauling from the cistern on Terraced Street, and after that old Pem needs relief on watch, he's been staring at the dark for six hours and starting to see things in it." She looked Orsen over, top to bottom, cataloguing. "Can you keep accounts?"

Orsen nearly dropped the cup.

"Why?"

"Because our tally-keeper was a cooper and he took a beam to the shoulder yesterday, and the chalkboard's turned into a war between three people who each think the others can't count." Bett's eyes were shrewd and kind at once. "You've got clerk's shoulders. Don't argue, I raised four sons, I know a clerk's shoulders when I see them. Question is whether you've got clerk's honesty."

"I stole a child from the Halloway Trading Company," Orsen said quietly, "and hid her in a rope loft for a season, and forged a berth chit, and bribed a dockhand, and burned a list of names rather than sell them, and I would do every bit of it again."

The cellar noise went on around them—cards, coughing, a baby fussing—but Bett heard every word. She took a slow drink of her own cider.

"Good," she said. "That's the right sort of dishonesty. Steal from them that steal first." She jerked her chin toward the chalkboard. "Tally's yours, then. Fair shares, honest weights. You'll fit right in—we're all thieves down here, by the company's reckoning. We strike, we hold goods back, we breathe air they say belongs to quarantine regulations." She smiled with about half her mouth. "Funny thing about fire, clerk. Burns away the fine print."

* * *

Orsen worked through the night.

They carried water up the crooked hill road and back down, trip after trip, until their arms remembered they were divers' arms and complained in a language of knots and burn. They relieved old Pem on watch at the smoke-hole and stared into the dark themselves and saw, in fairness, some things: the glow of the quarter burning street by street, and once, impossibly far below, a lantern moving along the quay in a pattern that looked like a man pacing. They kept the tally. They argued—gently, then firmly—with a cooper's cousin who believed his household deserved double water rations because his grandfather had built the press house, and won, because the argument was arithmetic and Orsen had been raised by ledgers, and fair shares are the oldest ledger there is.

Somewhere near dawn, sitting against the stone wall with chalk dust on their fingers and cider in their cup, Orsen realized they had not thought about Mr. Quill for four hours straight.

It was the longest such interval since the age of nine.

For nine years, Orsen Vasquez had lived inside the company's gaze like a fish lives in water—weighed, counted, fined, promoted, threatened, audited. Even defiance had been conducted in its shadow; even refusal had been refusal *to Quill*, a conversation, a relationship. But the company's maps had a line drawn around this quarter, and the line meant *gone*, and so for once in their life Orsen stood outside the line, among people the ledger had rounded down to nothing, and the astonishing discovery of the night was this:

Nothing was missing. The gaze lifted, and nothing was missing. There was water to carry and a board to tally and a widow to sit with and a grave on the hillside keeping an eye on the sea, and Orsen's name was nowhere in anyone's book, and the freedom of it was so large it was frightening—like stepping off a ship onto land after years at sea and finding the ground still sways.

"You're crying," observed the boy with the lantern, settling nearby with the frankness of the young.

"I'm not."

"You are."

"It's the smoke," Orsen said.

"It's a little bit the smoke," the boy allowed, generously. "Ma says the ones who cry are the ones staying. The ones leaving already went." He leaned his head against the wall. "Are you staying?"

Orsen looked at the chalkboard. Forty-one names now—they had added Orsen's that evening, at Bett's instruction, under no surname at all, just *the clerk*. No Orsen. No Vasquez. No indenture number, no fines outstanding, no brand of *thief*. Just a function, freely chosen, honestly weighted.

"I'm staying," Orsen said, and found it was true.

* * *

On the third day the fires reached the terrace gardens and burned themselves out against old stone, having eaten everything the company had abandoned and nothing it hadn't, which was, Orsen reflected, the most honest thing the company had done all year.

The quarter above the cellars was a ruin of black walls standing open to the sky like the ribs of a whale. But it stood. Cellars held. Press house held. And on the morning after the winds turned, the survivors came up blinking into clean gray light and began, without any meeting or vote, to clear rubble.

It was on the fifth day, hauling stone from the collapsed cooperage, that a runner came up the hill road—a girl of fourteen, fast and breathing hard, with news for Bett that she relayed to the whole work line in a voice pitched to carry:

"The company's posting reward notices in the lower town. Thieves, they're calling them. Warehouse theft, forgery, arson in aid thereof." She pulled a crumpled paper from her shirt. "There's a name on it."

Bett took the notice and read it, and her face did something complicated, and then she handed it to Orsen without a word.

The paper was fine stock, company watermark, Quill's immaculate hand. *Wanted for theft, forgery, and the abduction of a company ward: a person of indeterminate description, last seen in the orchard district.* No name. No face. Just a phrase, repeated twice for emphasis, underlined in Quill's careful strokes:

*A person of indeterminate description.*

And beneath it, the reward. Fifty crowns—more money than Orsen had seen in nine years of indenture, more than the manufactured fines, more than the berth chit, a fortune designed to buy exactly one thing.

Bett watched Orsen read. "Indeterminate," she said. "That's you, is it? Indeterminate?"

"That's me," Orsen said.

The work line had gone quiet. Forty faces turned toward the hilltop, toward the stranger the company wanted, the stranger whose name wasn't anywhere, whose face couldn't be described because no one down here had ever needed to describe it.

Then the cooper's cousin, the one who'd wanted double rations, picked his shovel back up.

"Fifty crowns and I still say we keep the clerk," he said. "Company money spends worse than bad cider." And the whole line laughed and bent again to the rubble, and Orsen stood amid the smoking ruins of the drowned orchard district, branded and nameless and—for the first time in nine years—entirely, magnificently unworthy of pursuit.

Except that Quill, Orsen thought, folding the notice small and putting it away like a man pocketing a debt, had never yet pursued anything he didn't intend to catch.

And *indeterminate* was not the same as *invisible*. Not forever. Sooner or later the refugee rolls would open. Sooner or later there would be lists again—and Quill loved lists.

Orsen picked up a shovel, and began, quietly, to plan how to disappear completely.
