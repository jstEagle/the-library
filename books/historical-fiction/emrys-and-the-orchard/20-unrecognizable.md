## 20. Unrecognizable

The name Orsen went by now was Fen.

It had been chosen in the smoke, more or less at random—a half-remembered boy from the rope loft days who used to whistle through the gap in his teeth and could tie a bowline blindfolded. *Fen*, short, salt-crusted, belonging to nobody's ledger. It fit the way an old coat fits: loose in the shoulders, smelling faintly of someone else's life.

Three weeks after the fires, Fen worked the morning tide at Pier Eleven, hauling crates of dried cod that smelled like a grudge left out in the sun. The work was honest and it hurt in all the ordinary places—lower back, palms, the long muscle of the shoulder—which was a kind of mercy. Pain you could name was pain you could carry. Orsen had always believed that. Carry little, want less, be invisible. The old philosophy still held. It was just that the invisibility had changed hands. Once it had been a cage built around them by other people's ledgers. Now it was armor they'd forged themselves, in fire, at terrible cost, and they wore it the way Father had worn his diving scars: not proudly, exactly, but without apology.

"You're stacking those crooked, Fen," said Doss, the foreman, a woman built like a capstan with a voice to match. "Crooked stacks fall on crooked workers. I've seen it happen. It's practically arithmetic."

"Everything's arithmetic," Orsen said, and straightened the stack.

"Don't get philosophical with me before breakfast. Philosophical dockhands drop crates."

Orsen dropped no crates. This was noted, as everything about Fen was noted by everyone, in the small gossip-ledger of the pier: *Fen works quiet. Fen doesn't drink much. Fen has a way with knots. Fen turned up after the fires with nothing but a canvas bag and a cough, took the cot above the net-shed, never asked anybody's business.* In Vellamare's burned quarter, never asking anybody's business was the highest currency there was. Half the workers on Pier Eleven were people the company had misplaced—the forgotten, the refused, two of the strikers from Pier Nine who'd been blacklisted so thoroughly their names had been struck from the hiring rolls like sins from a confession. They recognized each other instantly and agreed, wordlessly, never to say so.

That was the strange shelter of destruction, Orsen had learned. When everything burns, the ash levels everyone. A man with no past and a woman with no past can drink tea together like equals.

* * *

The refugee rolls were posted every Tuesday outside the harbormaster's office, pinned to a board scarred with knife-marks and older proclamations, and every Tuesday Orsen walked past them on the way to the chandlery—not slowly, not quickly, at the pace of a person with no interest whatsoever in lists.

They read them anyway. Of course they did. You can stop being loyal to a company; you cannot stop being able to count.

The lists were Quill's work; everyone said so. He had survived the evacuation with his ledgers intact and his position somehow improved, because disasters promote the men who document them. Now he administered relief, which meant he administered conditions. The rolls held names of the quarantined, the compensated, the relocated, the *pending*, each category a different flavor of waiting. Orsen scanned for Marisol's name the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth—every Tuesday, same ache, same absence. No word yet. No letter, no rumor, no scrap. The ships had gone east ahead of the quarantine winds, and east was a direction the mail treated as hypothetical.

But this particular Tuesday, scanning without stopping, Orsen felt the hair rise on the back of their neck before they understood why. Some animal part of them had done the counting first.

Quill stood at the board.

He was thinner than Orsen remembered, or perhaps the memory had inflated him—he had always seemed larger inside the warehouse, among his shelves and fines, where he was a small king. Out here in the flat daylight he was just a man in a good coat that didn't fit him anymore, running one finger down the refugee roll with the concentration of someone looking for a specific error. Two clerks waited behind him with satchels. Ordinary Tuesday business.

Orsen did not freeze. Freezing is what prey does, and besides—they had rehearsed this. Not formally, but in the way you rehearse everything you fear: in the shower of the net-shed, in the dark before sleep, walking the route in your mind until the panic wears a groove of habit. *Head down. Pace even. If he looks at you, let him look at a tired dockhand named Fen who wants his breakfast.*

They walked past him at the distance of a crate's width. Close enough to smell the ink on him—or imagined they did; Quill had always smelled of iron-gall ink and lamp oil, the perfume of a man married to his ledger.

Quill's eyes slid across the crowd of stevedores, snagging nowhere, and passed over Orsen without a flicker. Without a *flicker*. Not even the courtesy of suspicion. He was hunting names on paper, and Orsen no longer had one that mattered.

It should have felt like grief. For one strange second it did—a vertiginous little mourning, as if they had attended their own funeral and been disappointed by the turnout. *I carried your crates for nine years. I memorized your face because I was told to forget others. You weighed my life in fines and you cannot weigh me now at all.*

Then the second passed, and what rose up underneath the grief was something warmer and considerably less dignified: delight. Pure, fizzy, schoolyard delight. The cage had become armor, and the armor worked, and Mr. Quill, auditor of souls, clerk of debts, keeper of the arithmetic designed against them, had looked directly through the only person who had ever beaten him and seen nothing worth fining.

Orsen bought breakfast—fried dough, extra honey, a celebration no one else could read—and ate it on the seawall, swinging their heels like a child, feeling wicked and free.

*You knew about Emrys all along,* they thought at him fondly, across the water. *You waited to see what I would pay. Well, sir. I paid everything. And look—you can't even see me spending it.*

* * *

The cot above the net-shed had one window, a candle stub, and a resident mouse whom Orsen had named Auditor, on the grounds that he came at night, took a portion of everything, and could not be reasoned with. Auditor was tolerated. There were rules: the cheese rinds were negotiable, the pressed-flower tin was not.

The tin had come with Orsen out of the burning quarter—absurd, really, when they'd left behind everything else, and they knew exactly what it made them: a person who kept flowers. But inside the lid, wrapped in oilcloth against the salt air, were three things that constituted Orsen's entire remaining estate of hope. A lock of Emrys's fine dark hair, cut the night before the quay, because Orsen had read somewhere once that hair keeps its color longer than memory keeps anything. A scrap of blanket Marisol had woven her initials into, so the child would carry her even across water. And the first pearl—the mother's pearl, the one she had died refusing to sell, which Father had pressed into Orsen's hand on the quay with lungs already drowning and a grip that was still, impossibly, a diver's grip.

*"She wouldn't sell it,"* he'd whispered. *"Not for passage, not for medicine, not for me. It was hers. The one thing the sea couldn't have back."*

Orsen kept it now the way their mother had: not to spend. Just to own one thing outright.

On evenings when the wind came wrong off the water and brought back the sound of that last dawn—Marisol's face at the rail of the fishing boat, furious and weeping, mouthing *you come too, you come too*; the small weight of Emrys handed over like handing over the horizon; Father going quiet between one breath and the next with his hand still holding Orsen's, freed, finally, from choosing—on those evenings Orsen sat with the tin and talked to it, quietly, because talking to a pearl and a curl of baby hair was eccentric, and talking to nobody at all was worse.

"Your mother sends nothing yet," they told Emrys's hair. "Which means either she's safe and busy, or the letters aren't moving, or—" and here they always stopped, because finishing that sentence aloud gave it weight, and Orsen Vasquez—sorry, *Fen*—had sworn off carrying unnecessary weight.

Instead they finished: "When the letters come, I'll tell you everything twice. Once for the years I owe you and once for the years I don't."

Auditor squeaked from the wall, possibly in agreement, possibly demanding rent.

* * *

Doss found them at the end of the week, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a foreman about to make a problem everyone's problem.

"Fen. You know rope."

"I've met it socially."

"You know what I mean. The chandler's boy quit—went inland, says the plague followed the ports and he'd rather risk wolves. I need someone on splice-work and rigging repair, steady pay, and I thought of you, because you handle line like it apologizes to you. Only." She paused, and Doss pausing was an event with witnesses. "There's a form. Hiring roll wants a name for the book. Full name, birth quarter, prior employment."

The harbor sounds filled the silence for a moment—gulls arguing theology, a winch groaning somewhere, the slap of gray water.

"And if a fellow," Orsen said carefully, "preferred not to fill out prior employment?"

Doss studied them. She had spent thirty years reading weather and dockworkers, and could tell you which of the two lied more often. "Then the hiring roll writes 'unknown' and pays unknown-wages, which are lower, and the tax man grumbles, and life goes on." She uncrossed her arms. "Half this pier is 'unknown,' Fen. The fires made more of us than the books can hold. Nobody here asks. That's the whole point of the place." She turned to go, then added, not unkindly: "Though whoever you were—I've watched enough folk come out of that smoke to know a shape when I see one—you did somebody a good turn once. It's on you like flour. Can't say how I know. Foreman's intuition."

She stomped off. Orsen stood very still among the coiled lines, throat unexpectedly tight.

*Did somebody a good turn.* They thought of the striker's child at Pier Nine, the one who'd gone hungry while Quill dangled berths. They thought of Marisol covering their tracks through the supply riot, shouting directions at soldiers with the fluency of a born liar, which she denied to this day. They thought of Father saying, half-delirious, *don't become the company, little fish, whatever else you become.*

Perhaps kindness did leave residue. Perhaps it got into your hands the way tar did, and stayed after the scrubbing, and strangers could smell it.

"Well," Orsen told the rigging, "that's embarrassing."

They signed *Fen, unknown quarter, unknown prior* on the hiring roll, and felt the pen move like a small wedding—some final vow to the self they'd chosen over the self that had been assigned. Unknown wages were indeed lower. Want less, they reminded themselves. Carry little. It had taken twenty-some years and a city's worth of fire, but the philosophy finally belonged to them instead of to survival.

* * *

Sundays were the hard days.

The rest of the week, work ate the hours honestly. But Sunday mornings the pier went quiet, the fishermen rested or drank, and Orsen was left alone with the sea, which is a companion that never lets a conversation drop. The sea had taken their mother. The sea had, in its roundabout manner, delivered Emrys to a warehouse door. The sea was currently sitting on Marisol and Emrys somewhere to the east, keeping them the way it kept everything: temporarily.

So Orsen developed rituals, because ritual is what you do with longing when you can't afford to drown in it.

Sunday mornings: walk the high road above the burned quarter. The fires had left the orchard district a field of blackened trees standing like burnt matches, but—and this was the miracle nobody had ordered—the roots lived. Green shoots were coming up from the bases of the old apple stock, reckless and absurd, the way life does when nobody asks its permission. The displaced families who camped in the ruins had begun, without any official blessing, to clear and replant. Orsen joined them some Sundays, digging in ash-rich soil alongside a one-eyed stonemason and a grandmother who claimed to have been a smuggler, though her hands suggested accounting.

"Trees take years," the grandmother said the first time, watching Orsen pat down soil around a sapling with far more tenderness than the task required.

"I know," Orsen said. "I'm counting on it."

Because here was the thought they hadn't said aloud to anyone, the thought that lived under the pressed-flower tin like a keel under a boat: *Emrys will grow. Trees will grow. If I put both in the ground now, someday they'll be the same age.* Someday a young woman might walk up this hill—an orchard above the harbor—and ask why anyone had ever wanted her face forgotten, and the answer would be a hillside full of apples, which is a better answer than most ledgers ever produced.

Sunday afternoons: write letters that could not yet be sent. Orsen kept them in a second tin (Auditor had opinions) addressed to a ship, a sister, a daughter, wherever the tide eventually pushed its post. The letters were mostly news of no consequence, which is the truest kind. *The mason's cat has adopted the net-shed and audits our fish portions, so I am surrounded by colleagues of a type. The saplings are six inches of pure stubbornness. I have become the sort of person who argues with a mouse. You would laugh. I hope someone is making you laugh.*

And Sunday evenings, one hour, strictly rationed, for the dangerous luxury: remembering Emrys's weight in their arms. Sixteen pounds, give or take, the last time they'd lifted her. Orsen was tracking it forward in their head—she'd be walking soon, maybe walking already, tottering along a deck or a foreign street with Marisol swooping behind—and the image hurt so precisely because it was also the entire point. *She carries the future. I paid for it. Fair trade.* Though Orsen had learned, at the quay, at the ledger, at the strike, that fair trade and bearable trade were different animals entirely, and only one of them slept through the night.

* * *

On a Tuesday in the sixth week, the harbormaster's board grew a new paper.

Orsen spotted it from forty feet—the particular white of an official notice amid the faded congregation of old ones—and felt the old animal counting start up again, quick and involuntary, the way it had counted berths and fines and pounds of a child. They made themselves walk past at Fen-pace, bought their bread, walked back. Then, since the board was momentarily deserted, they stopped, casually, the way any curious dockhand might.

The notice concerned postal service. Resumption of eastbound shipping lanes, partial, weather permitting. A mail packet would run within the fortnight. Letters to and from the eastern settlements could be lodged at the harbormaster's office, fees according to weight.

Weight according to fees. Fees according to weight. Even the mail had a ledger. Orsen laughed out loud, alone in front of the notice board, startling a gull into profanity.

A fortnight. Fourteen days, give or take, minus however long the packet took to cross, plus however long the answer took to come back. An arithmetic, in other words—but for the first time in their life, one whose sum they *wanted*. Every number in it pointed home to somebody.

They walked down to the pier with the bread tucked under their arm and their heart doing something undignified against their ribs. Doss saw their face and narrowed her eyes.

"Either you've come into money or you're about to ask me for some."

"Neither. I'm expecting a letter."

"A letter." Doss said it the way other people said *plague flag*. "From who?"

"A sister," Orsen said. It was the truest thing they'd said in weeks, and it covered everyone it needed to cover. "And—" they hesitated, then let it out; the words had been waiting six weeks for daylight— "her daughter. My niece. She'll be about one and a half now. Probably walking. Definitely opinionated."

Something moved behind Doss's weather-read eyes—recalculation, perhaps, of the quiet dockhand with flour on him. "Well then," she said. "Best learn to whistle, Uncle Fen. Children come in answering whistles whether you teach them to or not."

Orsen smiled, and it was the smile of a person practicing, because the muscles had gone rusty from disuse and would need rebuilding, like hands for rope-work, like everything.

That night they opened the flower tin, set the pearl where the lamplight could find it, and began a letter they actually intended to finish:

*Dear Marisol—the mails are moving again. Tell Emrys the trees are coming up. Tell her there is an orchard above the harbor, and it is not drowned, and neither, despite rumors to the contrary, is her family—*

Above the cot, Auditor scratched at the wall, and outside, the tide came in carrying the whole eastern world closer by one day, and Orsen wrote until the candle guttered, counting the fortnight down like a diver counting breath.
