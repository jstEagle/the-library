## 21. Letters Carried by Tide

The letters came the way most good things came to Orsen Vasquez: sideways, unofficially, and smelling faintly of fish.

The first arrived in the pocket of a striker named Bette Corragan, who handed it over at the end of a long shift on the north quay with the studied casualness of a woman passing contraband—which, technically, she was. Quarantine law still forbade correspondence out of Vellamare's port, and the Halloway Company had paid good coin to keep it that way. A letter from a fugitive's sister was exactly the sort of paper Quill would have framed and hung above his desk, if he framed things instead of filing them into weapons.

Orsen waited until they were home—home being a room above a net-mender's shop, rented under the name "Sennet," which Marisol had once joked sounded like a fish and a sneeze had made a baby—and only then broke the wax seal with trembling thumbs.

*Orsen,* the letter began, in Marisol's sharp, slanted hand, the one she'd practiced copying off shipping manifests until it looked official enough to fool a harbormaster. *If you are alive, which I will assume until proven otherwise because you are too stubborn to drown and too cheap to starve quickly, then sit down before you read further.*

Orsen sat down on the edge of the cot.

*I am sitting in a chair. A real chair, with arms, on a real porch, attached to a real house that does not lean. The air here does not taste of salt and despair. Emrys has taken her first steps, directly toward a chicken, whom she attempted to embrace. The chicken objected. Emrys laughed so hard she fell down, then got up and did it again, because she is your blood through and through and does not understand the concept of consequences.*

*She walks everywhere now. She walks to the gate. She walks to the well. She walked into the sea up to her knees yesterday and when I pulled her back she looked at me with YOUR face, Orsen, the exact face you used to make at the warehouse foreman, the one that says I hear you and I intend to ignore you completely. I did not know whether to laugh or weep so I did both and she patted my leg as though I were the chicken.*

Orsen read that paragraph four times. Then a fifth. Walking. Laughing. A chicken, assaulted twice with love.

There was more, pages more, Marisol's handwriting loosening as she went, as if the act of writing to someone alive had unstoppered something.

*Father is buried on the hill above the town, facing the water, per his instructions, which he delivered in full while delirious and which we honored anyway because half of what he said delirious was truer than anything he said sober. He wanted "a view worth the ticket price." He has one. I put flowers there on Sundays and tell him you're alive, which is a lie I have decided to keep telling until it becomes true.*

*Emrys asks about the orchard. I don't know where she learned the word—perhaps from me, perhaps from Father's stories soaking into her before he left us—but she says it constantly. Orchard, orchard. She thinks it is a person. She waves at it. "Where orchard?" she asks strangers in the market. An old woman told her it lives across the sea and sleeps in winter, and now Emrys says goodnight to it every evening out the window. I have not corrected her. Some things should not be corrected.*

*Write back. I know you cannot. Write back anyway.*

*Your sister who saved your life and expects gratitude in the form of news,*
*M.*

Orsen sat for a long while after finishing, the letter resting on their knee like something warm and breathing. Outside, the harbor lights of Vellamare came on one by one, the same lights, the same harbor, but everything else had changed shape.

They laughed first—a startled, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering its purpose. Then, quietly, they wept. Then they laughed again, because Emrys had tried to hug a chicken. Twice. Because she waved at the orchard. Because somewhere across the water, a child carrying Orsen's stubborn chin was falling down and getting up and falling down again, entirely without consequence.

The room above the net-mender's shop had never felt less like hiding.

* * *

The problem with writing back was threefold: it was illegal, it was dangerous, and it required paper.

The illegality Orsen dismissed within a day. They had spent two years being dead to the Halloway Company; one more crime against Quill's tidy ledgers hardly registered. The danger was manageable—the tide-runners who moved letters out of the quarantine quarter charged triple and asked nothing, because asking questions was bad for business and worse for breathing. It was the paper that nearly defeated them.

Vellamare's stationers had all been bought by the company years ago. Every sheet sold in the city could be traced, numbered, accounted for. So Orsen did what Orsen had always done best: they became useful and unseen, this time in the service of themselves.

The net-mender downstairs, a widow named Odile with forearms like dock rope and the gentleness of a landslide, kept her accounts on scraps of sail canvas cut into squares. When Orsen offered to mend her nets in exchange for a dozen squares and a stub of graphite, she squinted at them for a long moment.

"Letters," she said. Not a question.

"Flowers," Orsen said.

Odile looked out the window at the hillside gardens—the ones the city had planted that first spring after the fires, on the scorched terraces above the harbor, partly in mourning and partly in defiance, because Vellamare had decided that if it was going to burn, it would grow back prettier. Poppy and sea-lavender, thrift and wild fennel, all of it rioting up the black slopes in colors no one had asked permission for.

"Huh," said Odile, and gave Orsen fifteen squares instead of twelve.

Writing the letter took Orsen three evenings. Not for lack of things to say—for drowning in them. Every sentence about Emrys demanded ten more. What did she eat? Did she sleep through the night? Had the chicken forgiven her? Was she saying words yet, and if so, whose words, and did she say *orchard* the way Mother used to?

But the letter also had to survive a journey through hands that would read it, and some things could not be written to a stranger's eyes. That Father died holding Orsen's hand on the quay, and that the memory lived in Orsen's chest like a coal that hurt to touch and refused to go cold. That Orsen had watched the fishing boat shrink for an hour afterward, standing among crates, invisible, wanting to shout and having no name left to shout.

So Orsen wrote the safe things, and hid the true things between the lines the way their mother had hidden pearls in her cheek, and trusted Marisol to find them.

*Dear Marisol,* the letter began. *I am well. The work is steady. The city grows back green, which surprises everyone including itself.*

*I walk past the gardens every Sunday. You would laugh at me. I have become a person who knows the names of flowers.*

*Tell the chicken I said no hard feelings. Tell Emrys the orchard is not sleeping. Tell her it is growing, and that it remembers her, even if she doesn't remember it yet. Some things wait.*

*I am well. I mean it. I am more than well. I am a person who owns a coat with no holes in it, and last week I ate a peach, a whole one, alone, without sharing it with anyone, and thought of you the entire time.*

*Your sibling who owes you a life and intends to pay in news,*
*Sennet.*

*P.S. — She has my chin. I am sorry. There was no way to prevent it. Tell her chins like ours get us through things.*

*P.P.S. — I think about the quay every day. I want you to know that I do not regret it. I have regretted many things. I have stolen, lied, carried things I shouldn't have, and once set fire to a list of names, which remains the single finest act of my life. But I do not regret the pier. Put that in your pipe and be furious about it.*

Orsen pressed the flowers on the fourth Sunday. Sea-lavender, because it grew on salt ground and refused to apologize for it. A poppy, red as a struck match. One sprig of wild pear blossom, stolen—borrowed—from the young trees the gardeners had started on the highest terrace, the ones everyone in the quarter called *the new orchard* without irony anymore, because hope, like weeds, establishes itself wherever nobody sprays.

Between sheets of Odile's sail-canvas, weighted with a flat stone, the flowers dried slowly into versions of themselves: thinner, quieter, but unmistakably still blooming.

Orsen knew the feeling.

* * *

The second letter came in November, folded inside a mended fishing net that Bette delivered with a straight face and a bag of oranges she claimed fell off a cart, which no cart in history had ever done.

*You sent FLOWERS,* Marisol wrote, and the capital letters were audible. *Dried flowers, in an illegal letter, smuggled past a quarantine cordon, addressed to a woman living under a false name. Do you understand that we are criminals now? Both of us? Our whole family is either dead or outlawed. Father would be SO PROUD.*

*Emrys ate the packing straw around the poppy before anyone could stop her. She is fine. The flower was fine too, eventually, once dried of toddler spit. She now refers to all mail as "flower." This includes bills, which I wish worked on the landlord.*

*Words update: she has forty of them. Her favorites are "orchard," "no," "chicken" (the feud continues), and "Oshen."*

Orsen stopped reading. Read the line again.

*Yes. Oshen. I told her about you—not everything, not yet, just that she has an Oshen far away who loves her, and she has decided this is the correct name for the concept of you. When your first letter came she carried it around the house for TWO DAYS. Slept with it. Fed it bread. The letter is ruined, covered in tooth marks and jam, and I have preserved it anyway, because apparently in this house we revere garbage if the right person slobbered on it.*

*Oshen. Honestly, Orsen, you survived plague and fire and Quill, and a two-year-old defeated your name in a single afternoon.*

Orsen laughed alone in the rented room until Odile knocked on the floorboards below to ask if they'd gone mad, and shouted back, "A little!" and heard the old woman snort.

*Now the serious part, so pay attention,* Marisol continued, her handwriting going careful and small. *I know what you gave up. I have known since the pier, since I saw your face while the boat pulled away, and I have spent two years being angry about it, because anger was easier than grief, and grief was easier than the truth.*

*The truth is this: you put me on that boat because you believed I could raise her better than anyone alive, and you were right, and I hate that you were right, and I love that you were right. She is kind, Orsen. Where does kindness come from, in our family? We hoarded it like pearls, you and Father and Mother, and I suppose someone finally cracked one open.*

*She asks about you. Not just "where Oshen"—she asks what you look like, what you like to eat, whether you are tall. I tell her you are very tall and afraid of chickens. She finds this hilarious. You are now, permanently, in family legend, a giant cowering from poultry.*

*Come see us when it is safe. I know it may never be safe. Come anyway.*

*All my fury and all my love, which have always been the same thing,*
*M.*

Underneath, in a wobbly, enormous scrawl that had clearly been achieved by guiding a toddler's fist:

*OSHEEN. FLOWER. CHIKIN.*

Orsen read the signature until the graphite smeared, then carefully, reverently, filed the letter in the tin box under the floorboard—the box that held everything Orsen owned that could not be carried: Mother's pearl-string knot, the burned corner of the informant list, and now two letters and a pressed poppy returned by return post, jam-scented, tooth-marked, holy.

* * *

Winter came gray and close to Vellamare, and with it came the letters' rhythm—one out with each tide-runner's run, one back whenever the currents and bribes allowed. Orsen learned to live by them the way sailors live by wind.

Marisol wrote of small things, and Orsen learned that small things were the entire cargo hold of a life. Emrys learning to count to five, skipping four with contempt. Emrys's ongoing war with the neighbor's rooster, now entering its second campaign. Marisol taking in sewing, then dressmaking, then employing two other refugee women, because Vasquez stubbornness expressed itself differently in each of them but never once considered surrendering.

Orsen wrote back of equally small things, and discovered a self that had never had room to exist before. A self who noticed that the pear trees on the high terrace had fruited for the first time that autumn. Who had opinions about compost. Who had joined, without meaning to, a loose fellowship of gardeners who met Sundays on the terraces and argued about drainage with the passion of admirals arguing about fleet formation.

*You have become a farmer,* Marisol wrote, sounding scandalized and delighted.

*A gardener,* Orsen defended, aloud, to an empty room. *There is a difference.* There wasn't, really, and they knew it, and they wrote it down anyway, and enclosed a pressed pear blossom with the confession.

In spring, a letter came heavier than usual. Inside, wrapped in waxed cloth, was a drawing—or rather, an artifact of drawing, executed in charcoal by an artist aged approximately three. It depicted, according to Marisol's accompanying legend: *the orchard. Brown scribble = trees. Blue scribble = sky, obviously. Small figure waving = Emrys. Large figure cowering in the corner = you (afraid of chicken).*

And at the bottom, in Marisol's hand beneath the child's scrawl: *"This is for Oshen. So Oshen not lonely. Orchard watches Oshen too."*

Orsen pinned it to the wall above the cot, where the harbor light caught it each morning. Brown scribble. Blue sky. A small figure waving.

Some mornings, before the docks, Orsen stood in front of it with tea going cold and let the whole impossible arithmetic settle: a bundle left at a warehouse door with orders to forget the face. A rope loft. A lie told fluently for the first time. A list burning. A quay. All of it folding down, year by year, into a charcoal scribble on a wall that said, in its entire illiterate splendor: *you are remembered. You are loved. You are not lonely.*

* * *

It was Odile, in the end, who brought the change, clumping up the stairs one bright morning in early summer with a letter in one hand and an expression on her face that Orsen had learned to fear slightly, because Odile's expressions tended to precede either generosity or catastrophe.

"Tide-runner brought this special," she said. "Wouldn't leave it with the others. Said it was marked urgent by the sender." She held it out. "Also you've got dirt on your face."

"That's camouflage," Orsen said, taking the letter.

"Mm," said Odile, and went back down the stairs unconvinced.

The letter was thick. Marisol's opening lines were normal enough—news of the sewing shop, the rooster war concluded in stalemate, Emrys now counting to six *including* four, a development celebrated nationwide. But halfway down, the handwriting changed. It slowed. It became the careful script of someone choosing each word like a foot placement on wet rock.

*Orsen. I need to tell you something, and I need you to promise you'll read to the end before you do anything rash, which I know is like asking the tide not to come in.*

*There was a man at the market last week. Foreign accent, company boots, eyes that counted everything. He bought thread from my stall and watched Emrys the whole time—not rudely, not cruelly, just... counting. The way certain men count. He asked no questions. He bought his thread and left.*

*He came back yesterday.*

*He asked me, very politely, if my daughter's name was Emrys. And then he asked—still polite, still counting—if I had any family remaining in Vellamare.*

*I told him my husband was a fisherman and my daughter's father was dead. Which is true, in the way that matters. He smiled at that. A smile like a ledger balancing.*

*Orsen—he had a scar through one eyebrow, white and old, the kind a rope leaves. I have seen that scar before. Think, Orsen. Think where you've seen it. I've been racking my brain for days and I can almost place it, and it frightens me more than if I could.*

*I am not frightened for us—we are far, and safe, and he is one man in a market. I am frightened for YOU. If a man with company boots is asking after Emrys in my town, then someone, somewhere, is asking after Emrys in yours.*

*Read to the end, as promised. Here is the end: whatever you have built there—the gardens, the name, the quiet life—hold it gently these next weeks. And if a man with a scarred eyebrow comes to your terraces buying pears and counting faces, you send word to me at once, and then, little brother, you RUN.*

*All my fury and all my love,*
*M.*

Orsen sat very still.

Outside, the harbor glittered, innocent as ever. Downstairs, Odile's needles clicked. On the hillside, the new orchard stood in full leaf, the pear trees heavy with their second summer's fruit, and somewhere in the green rows a bee bumbled drunkenly through blossom-fall, and the whole beautiful fragile architecture of a rebuilt life held its breath.

Because Orsen knew the scar. Of course they knew it. They had seen it a thousand times across an auditor's desk, catching the light while its owner added fines to an indenture and called it arithmetic.

*Quill.*

The name rose in Orsen's chest like water finding its level—old terror wearing a new suit, patient as tide, and already, somehow, on its way.
