## 22. Emrys and the Orchard

The trees did not ask Orsen Vasquez for anything except water in August and patience in April, and so Orsen loved them the way one loves anything that makes no demands on the ledger.

It had taken eleven years to learn this. Eleven years since the fires, since the quay, since the fishing boat that carried two hearts out of Vellamare harbor while a third stood on the stones and let them go. Eleven years of other names — Orsen had worn four by now, like coats changed at season's turn: Vasquez the dockhand, Vaz the tally-man, plain Sen who mended nets, and finally no name at all but *the orchard keeper*, which was the only one anyone above the harbor used, because it was the only one that fit.

The orchard sat on the hillside where the old Terrazzo estate had once kept its gardens, before the quarantine fires jumped the wall and ate everything down to the olive roots. Nobody had wanted the land afterward. The company had written it off in a ledger column labeled *LOSS* — the same column, Orsen suspected, where they had once written an infant girl's face. Land nobody claimed was land nobody watched. Orsen had squatted it first with a hoe and then with a deed bought honestly from a magistrate who never asked where the money came from, because asking questions cost more than silence.

Now there were forty apple trees where the drowned orchard of story had been, and plum trees along the south wall, and quince, and one pear tree grown from a cutting Marisol had sent in a smuggled parcel seven years ago, wrapped in wax cloth with a note that said only: *Plant this. It's from home.*

Home. Orsen still wasn't sure which direction that pointed.

The mornings began the same way they always had — with weighing. Old habits died hard in hands trained by the Halloway Trading Company to know the weight of things without a scale. Orsen would lift a green apple in each palm, feel the honest heft of them, and think: *this is what weight is for.* Not cargo. Not fines calculated against an indenture. Just fruit, heavy with rain, worth exactly what it was.

"You talk to them," said a voice from the gate. "You know that, don't you? You talk to the trees."

Orsen didn't startle. After eleven years, only two people came through that gate without knocking, and one of them was dead.

"I'm not talking," Orsen said, not turning around. "I'm negotiating. This row wants to be picked Thursday. They're holding out for more sun."

Old Tam, the fisherman whose boat had once carried Marisol and Emrys out of the burning harbor, leaned on the fence with the easy slouch of a man who had retired from urgency sometime in his fifties and never looked back. He was sixty if he was a day now, hands like knotted rope, and he came up the hill twice a week with salt fish and gossip, both of which he delivered whether wanted or not.

"Thursday's no good," Tam said. "There's a boat coming in Thursday."

"There's always a boat coming in."

"Not like this one." He set something on the fence post — a folded paper, soft with handling, the way letters got when they passed through many reluctant hands. "Out of Corvassa. Passenger mail. Been chasing you across three ports by the look of it."

Orsen's hands went very still around the apple.

Letters came rarely. Once or twice a year, Marisol's careful handwriting arriving inside double envelopes addressed to names that weren't quite lies anymore — *Sen, keeper of the hill orchard, above the harbor at Vellamare.* Each letter was a small miracle of persistence: smuggled past port clerks, bribed past mail rooms, carried by fishermen who owed Tam favors. Each one reported the ordinary glories of a life Orsen had purchased at the price of never witnessing it. *Emrys lost her first tooth. Emrys can multiply in her head faster than the schoolmaster. Emrys asks about the orchard again. I told her it waits for her, and she said, tell them orchards don't wait, they grow. She's right, isn't she? She's usually right.*

Orsen read those letters standing in the rows until the light failed, then read them again by candle, then tucked them into the tin box under the floorboard where they lived alongside a single pearl the size of a thumbnail — the last pearl, the one a drowned woman had refused to sell, and which her children had refused to sell after her, though there had been winters when selling it would have meant bread.

But this letter was different. This letter was thick. Two pages, maybe three. And Tam was looking at Orsen with an expression that meant he already knew what was in it, which meant Marisol had told him, which meant—

"Read it," Tam said gently. "Then come sit down before you fall down. You've gone the color of the quinces."

Orsen opened the letter with fingers that remembered how to be steady because steadiness had once been survival.

---

*Orsen.*

*I have started this letter nine times and thrown eight of them in the sea, which the sea does not need but got anyway. So here is the ninth, plain and simple, because you taught me that plain and simple is hardest to argue with.*

*We are coming home.*

*Emrys is seventeen. She has finished her schooling and outgrown her teachers and Corvassa, which she says is beautiful the way a painting is beautiful — lovely to look at, impossible to live in. She wants to see the harbor. She wants to see YOUR harbor, which she has drawn from my descriptions about forty times, and she has gotten the hills wrong every time, too steep, too green, and I keep telling her and she keeps redrawing them until I gave up and said go see for yourself.*

*She doesn't know everything. She knows she was born in Vellamare. She knows a keeper tends an orchard above the harbor who has loved her all her life from far away. She knows the flowers that come every spring are pressed from that orchard. She asked me once, when she was small, if the keeper was an angel, and I laughed until I cried and said no, angels are much easier to live with.*

*Here is what she does not know, and what I will leave to you to tell her or not tell her, because it is yours as much as mine: who the keeper is. What was given. What was paid. She carries your mother's pearl, you know. I gave it to her on her sixteenth birthday and told her it was from someone who stayed behind so she could go forward, and she held it in her hand for a long time and then said, "Then we should go back, so they won't be behind anymore."*

*Seventeen years old and she solves in one sentence what took me twenty years of resentment and ten of forgiveness to understand.*

*We sail on the *Meridian*, out of Corvassa, calling at Vellamare within the month. I am frightened to write that sentence and more frightened not to. Come to the pier, Orsen. Or don't come, and I will bring her up the hill myself, and she will stand in your rows and finally understand why her name is a place.*

*All my love, always, against every year I wasted not saying so —*

*Marisol*

*P.S. — She is taller than you. I am sorry. There was nothing I could do.*

---

Orsen read it twice. Then sat down in the dirt between the apple rows, because Tam had been right about falling down, and laughed — or cried, it was difficult at that altitude to tell the difference — while the blossoms drifted down like small white confessions.

"Well?" Tam said.

"They're coming." Orsen's voice came out strange, rusted from disuse in certain registers. "My sister. And—" A breath. "*And Emrys.* Grown. Seventeen."

Tam nodded slowly, as if confirming arithmetic he'd done long ago. "And what are you going to do?"

Orsen looked down the long rows. Forty apple trees in bloom, petals catching in the wool of Orsen's sleeves. Eleven years of fruit sold cheap to families who couldn't afford the market, of branches pruned and grafted, of a hillside brought back from ash because some stubborn part of Orsen had needed to prove that things given up for lost could grow again.

"I'm going to pick the early row," Orsen said. "And then I'm going to do what I should have done on the pier eighteen years ago."

"Which is?"

"Be there when the boat comes in."

* * *

The month that followed was the longest of Orsen's life, and Orsen had once spent nine days hiding an infant in a rope loft while an auditor walked the floors below.

Every morning Orsen inspected the orchard like a fleet. Every evening Orsen rehearsed sentences to the trees, and every sentence was wrong. *You've grown so tall* — no, absurd; Orsen had last seen her at three months old, everyone was tall. *You have your grandmother's eyes* — true, probably, but heavy, too heavy for a pier. *Welcome home* — presumptuous; what did Orsen know of being welcomed anywhere?

In the end Orsen settled on nothing at all, on the old doctrine: carry little, want less, be present. It turned out the philosophy survived translation. Be invisible, it had once meant. Now it meant simply: don't fill the moment with yourself. Let her have the space to arrive.

Tam came Thursday with the tide report. The *Meridian* would make harbor Saturday morning.

Saturday morning arrived gray and silvered, the harbor wearing its best shawl of light. Orsen went down the hill road on foot, taking the long way past the rebuilt quarter — the new houses with their bright shutters, the market square where strikers' grandchildren now sold fish beside clerks' grandchildren and nobody remembered which grandfather had refused to name which — down to the piers, where the flags hung limp and the water slapped the stone pilings in its patient, indifferent way.

Vellamare harbor. Orsen had left it in smoke and returned to it in secret and worked it under three names, and it had never once looked like this — like a place someone might cross an ocean to find.

The *Meridian* came in on the morning tide, a clean merchant brig with her rails crowded with passengers craning at the hills. Orsen stood at the end of Pier Nine — Pier Nine, of all piers, though Orsen chose to believe the harbor had a sense of humor rather than a sense of ceremony — and watched the gangway go down.

Travelers descended in twos and threes: merchants, students, an elderly couple arguing cheerfully about luggage. Orsen's heart performed each of these departures as a false alarm.

Then two figures came down together, and the whole pier seemed to tilt.

Marisol first — forty-four years old now, gray threading the black hair she'd always worn pinned too tightly, walking like a woman who had argued with the sea for weeks and won. And beside her —

Taller than you. *I am sorry. There was nothing I could do.*

Emrys was tall. She was tall and straight and moved like the pier belonged to her, carrying her own trunk without help because clearly no one had ever successfully offered. Dark hair, loose down her back. And when she reached the bottom of the gangway and stopped, scanning the crowd of hackmen and porters and fishwives with the fierce systematic attention of someone who had redrawn these hills forty times until she got them right, Orsen saw — unmistakably, impossibly, after eighteen years — the face.

The face Orsen had been ordered to forget. Sealed note. *Forget the face.* As if paper could command memory. As if a face could be disposed of like spoiled cargo, weighed and written off and entered in the LOSS column.

It had grown, of course. Faces do. But it was the same face. It was the face from the rope loft, from the stolen milk, from the terrible tender midnight feedings while Quill's boots crossed the warehouse floor below. It was the face Orsen had carried up a dark gangway at dawn and set aboard a fishing boat and released into the future like a hand opening.

Marisol saw Orsen first. Her chin came up, her jaw set — preparing, visibly, for the old war between them, the leverage-and-trust war they had fought to a draw across decades of letters. Then Orsen smiled, and whatever Marisol saw in it disarmed her entirely; her eyes flooded and she pressed a fist to her mouth and said, in a voice pitched for one person only:

"Oh, you *fool*. You absolute fool. You got *old*."

"You got *tall*," Orsen managed. "Both of you. There was nothing anyone could do."

And then Marisol crossed the stones and embraced her brother — her sibling, whatever the ledgers would have insisted on recording; family had never fit well in columns — and held on the way people hold on when they have spent twenty years practicing from a distance.

Which left Emrys standing three feet away, holding her trunk, watching with narrowed, intelligent eyes.

"This is the keeper," Marisol said, stepping back, wiping her face with her sleeve in an entirely ungracious manner. "Emrys. This is—"

"The keeper of the orchard," Emrys said. Her voice was steady; her eyes were not. She looked at Orsen the way one looks at a drawing that has stepped off the page — checking the hills, correcting the slope, getting it right at last. "You send the flowers."

"Every spring," Orsen said.

"For eighteen years." Emrys set down the trunk. Slowly. Like setting down something heavy that she had decided, upon inspection, she no longer needed to carry alone. "Mama says you loved me before I could remember anything."

"She says the truth," Orsen said. "Mostly. She leaves out the parts where I was terrified."

"Were you?"

"Constantly. You were very small and very loud and I had no idea what I was doing. I fed you milk I stole from the company stores and hid you in a rope loft and once you nearly sneezed us both into the gallows." Orsen paused. "You've grown out of the loudness, I hope."

"No," said Emrys.

"No," said Marisol. "Definitely not."

"Good," said Orsen, and found that their eyes had filled without permission, and decided, after a lifetime of invisibility, to permit it.

They stood there on Pier Nine — the three of them, plus one trunk, plus the gulls making their opinions known — while the harbor went on around them, boats landing, bells ringing, the world refusing to pause even for full-circle grace, which is the nature of worlds and half the point of grace.

"Show me," Emrys said finally. "The orchard. The real one. Mama drew it wrong on purpose, I think, so I'd have to come see."

"She draws everything wrong on purpose," Orsen said. "It's how she loves people."

"It's *how I plan*," Marisol protested. "Come on, both of you. The hill's steeper than I remember."

"It's exactly as steep as you remember," said Orsen. "You've just gotten comfortable in flat places."

* * *

They climbed the hill road in the late gold light, Marisos stopping twice to catch her breath and blaming the altitude of a town built at sea level, Emrys turning around and around to take in the harbor below, the harbor finally matching her drawings or perhaps the drawings finally confessing to the harbor.

At the top of the road, the gate.

Orsen had thought about this moment for a month and prepared nothing, which proved to be correct, because there was nothing to prepare. Emrys stepped through the gate and stopped among the trees — forty apple trees in late leaf, the plum row, the quince, the pear tree grown from a smuggled cutting — and stood very still.

"This is it," she said quietly. "This is my name."

"That's it," Orsen said. "Though I should tell you the original had the disadvantage of being underwater. This version grows better."

Emrys walked the rows slowly, touching the bark here and there, the way pilgrims touch the pillars of shrines. She stopped at the oldest tree — the first one Orsen had planted, crooked and stubborn, the one that had nearly died in its second summer and been talked out of it — and put her palm flat against it.

"It's warm," she said.

"Sun," said Orsen. "Stone under the soil holds the day's heat. Your mother could have told you — she grew up in a diver's tenement; she knows all about holding warmth in cold places."

Behind them, Marisol had sat down heavily on the gate bench and was pretending, badly, not to watch. Old Tam, who had followed them up the hill with a basket of salt fish and no shame whatsoever, elbowed her gently and said something that made her swat him.

Emrys turned. Seventeen years old, holding the future in her two hands the way she had once held it in a rope loft, and asked the question Orsen had been waiting eighteen years to hear:

"Tell me everything. From the beginning. All of it — the parts Mama softened and the parts she left out. I want the whole ledger." A small, sudden smile — and Orsen recognized it, with a jolt, as the smile that had once ended negotiations over bedtime in the loft. "Every entry."

So Orsen sat down in the grass between the rows, in the orchard that had been a story and then an ash field and then a promise, and began at the beginning — the warehouse, the weights, the clerk's sealed note, the bundle at the door.

And as the light went long and gold over Vellamare harbor, and the bees finished their rounds, and her daughter's laughter rose from the gate where her aunt sat, Emrys listened — and the face Orsen had been ordered to forget stayed, the whole evening through, impossible to forget and impossible to look away from, exactly as it had always been meant to be.

Somewhere below, the tide came in, carrying the smell of salt and apples down to the water, where it would drift out on the ebb toward ships and shores unknown — a small sweetness, going out into the world, asking nothing, wanting less, seen at last.
