## 1. The Ledger Beneath the Linens

The cold at Harrowgate came in through the stones before it came in through the windows, and by the first week of December 1813 the whole hospital smelled of it—wet masonry, carbolic, the sweet-rot undertow of gangrene that no amount of vinegar could drown. Mara Dravenmoor had learned to read the building the way other women read weather. When the east corridor went damp, the fevers would follow within a fortnight. When the chapel bell rope froze, the supply wagons were late. And when Colonel Aldous Vane's aide-de-camp appeared at the ward door with his leather folio and his pleasant, forgettable face, it was time to make certain her apron hung straight.

She was stitching a man's forearm closed when she heard the folio clasp click open.

"Hold still, Private Hobbes," she murmured, though the boy had already fainted twice and would not have moved if the roof fell in. "Nearly done."

Across the ward, forty beds in two long ranks, the aide moved from chart to chart with the unhurried air of a man counting spoons. Lieutenant Pell was his name. He never wrote anything down that Mara could see; he only looked, and looked away, and smiled at whoever caught him looking, as if to say *nothing here concerns you either*.

Mara tied off her suture, snipped the thread, and drew the blanket up over Hobbes's arm. His lips were grey. Nineteen years old, perhaps twenty, and he had told her yesterday, in the loose grammar of laudanum, that his mother kept bees.

"I'll write to her," she had said, because she always said it.

"She won't get it," he'd answered, with the terrible clarity the wounded sometimes found. "They struck me off at Salamanca. I heard the sergeant say so. Said I was dead." He had laughed then, a small astonished sound. "I don't feel dead."

She had not laughed. She had gone very still, the way one goes still near a bird on a branch, and she had said, *Go back to sleep,* and he had, and she had stood in the lantern light with her heart going like a drum while around her men moaned and coughed and the wind worried at the shutters, and no one—not one soul in that great room of dying—had noticed that anything was wrong.

That was the cost of noticing. It made the world a lonely country.

* * *

Her rules had come slowly, the way scar tissue comes, each one paid for.

Rule the first: never write a name where a name can be found. Not in a pocketbook, not in a Bible, not in the margin of a prayer book, however cleverly folded. She had seen what happened to papers. Papers were searched for the way dogs were set after hares.

Rule the second: never write more than the name itself, and the date, and the place where the man had last been counted alive. No opinions. No accusations. Ink could be read aloud in a courtroom someday, or read aloud over a hanging body, and ink did not know the difference between evidence and epitaph.

Rule the third—and this one she had learned hardest—never let your face change. Not when a roll was called and a breathing man was passed over. Not when a paymaster's clerk skipped a bed as though the occupant were furniture. Not when Lieutenant Pell smiled his spoon-counting smile. A face that changed was a face that confessed. She had practiced in the polished lid of Brandt's instrument case until she could hear a man erased from existence and look merely tired, merely a nurse at the end of a double shift, which was true besides.

And rule the fourth, unspoken, held somewhere below the others like a stone at the bottom of a well: *remember them anyway.* Remember whether they had a mother who kept bees, a wife in York, a limp from an old mill accident, a habit of whistling when the fever broke. Because a name alone was a fact, but a remembered man was a witness, and witnesses were what Vane—or whatever hand moved behind Vane—could least afford.

She did not yet say his name, even inside her own head, without care. Suspicion was not proof. Five years at Harrowgate had taught her the difference between a snake and a shadow shaped like one, and she had promised herself she would not hang a man in her mind on shadow alone. But the pattern—the omissions, the neat clerical gaps, the way the dead multiplied on paper exactly where money changed hands—that pattern had a shape now, and shapes did not lie the way men did.

* * *

At half past nine that night, after the lamps were trimmed and the worst of the day's work done, Mara sat on her cot in the nurses' attic with a candle shielded behind a tin plate and did the thing she had done every night for five years.

She unpicked three stitches at the left seam of her spare apron—the plain grey one, the one nobody remarked on—and drew out the ledger.

It was not a ledger in any sense a quartermaster would recognize. It was a quarto of thin paper, thirty-two leaves, sewn herself with waxed linen thread, small enough to vanish inside a rolled bandage. On its pages, in a hand smaller than a psalter's, were names. Two hundred and eleven of them now. She turned past the early entries—her earliest writing had been large, urgent, the hand of a woman who thought she might be caught any hour—and came to the blank space waiting at the bottom of the current leaf.

*Private J. Hobbes, 33rd Foot. Struck off rolls, Salamanca, July 1812. Alive, Harrowgate, Dec. 1813. Mother keeps bees.*

She hesitated over the last part. Rule the second said no opinions. But a mother's occupation was not an opinion; it was a thread, and threads were how a name became a man again if ever the world wanted him back. She wrote it. Her pen scratched like a mouse in the wainscot, and she paused at each scratch, listening for boots on the stair.

Two hundred and twelve.

She thought of Hobbes's face in the morning, the way he would ask again, hopefully, whether she'd written to his mother, and the way hope in this place was a wound that never got to close. She thought of the muster roll she had glimpsed last month in the records room—she was permitted to carry linens through it, nothing more—and how Hobbes's company list had ended in July 1812 with a tidy annotation in a clerk's copperplate: *killed in action, body not recovered.* Body not recovered. Of course not. The body was lying in bed nineteen of the east ward, asking about bees.

Someone was being paid Private Hobbes's pay. Someone was holding back a pension owed to a beekeeping woman in some village whose name Mara did not yet know but intended, God help her, to find.

She sewed the ledger back into its lining, three stitches, small and even, the same as all the others, and lay down in the dark with her hands folded on her chest like a woman already dead, and listened to the hospital breathe.

* * *

Morning brought snow and a convoy.

Twenty-two stretchers off the wagons, mud frozen to the blankets, men crying out when the bearers jolted them over the threshold. Mara worked triage at the ward door with Surgeon Brandt beside her, the old man's breath smoking, his hands steady as a carpenter's despite his seventy years.

"Gas gangrene, that one—take him to the far end and don't put him near the abdominal cases. This one sleeps, let him. This one—" Brandt peeled back a dressing and was quiet a moment. "This one has been dead a week and marching anyway. Fever ward. And you, soldier, what's your company?"

"Forty-fourth, sir," said the man on the stretcher, through cracked lips. "But they've got me down as—"

"Later," Brandt said gently. "Names keep. Blood doesn't."

But Mara had heard it. *They've got me down as.* The sentence unfinished, the sentence she had heard in a dozen variations across five years, and each time it landed in her chest like a coin dropped down a dry well. She marked the man's forehead with the charcoal cross for fever and added, silently, to the growing list behind her eyes: *find his regiment, find his entry, find the gap where he used to be.*

By noon the wards were full and running over, and Tilly Ashcombe, the youngest of the nurses, seventeen and freckled and fearless in the way of girls who had not yet learned what things cost, was moving among the cots with a basin and a courage that made Mara both proud and afraid.

"Nurse Dravenmoor," Tilly called softly, "this one's asking for paper. He wants to write home."

"Give him mine," Mara said. "I'll take the letter into town myself."

It was a small lie of generosity; there was no post from Harrowgate that did not pass through the regimental office first, and everyone knew it, and everyone sent letters anyway, hoping. Mara collected them because collecting them gave her a reason to be in the records room, and being in the records room with an armful of soldiers' letters was the most innocent thing a nurse could be.

She was stacking the letters—eleven of them, eleven threads leading out into England—when Lieutenant Pell came in.

He did it often enough that it should have been unremarkable. He stood at the muster shelves with his folio under his arm and pulled down the 44th, and the 33rd, and the 51st, and turned pages with one finger, and his lips did not move but his eyes went along the columns like a man reading music. Then he returned the volumes precisely to their places, squared their edges, thanked her with a nod, and was gone, leaving behind the faint smell of good tobacco and nothing else.

Mara stood among the shelved regiments with the letters against her breastbone and felt the old cold move through her that had nothing to do with December.

He was checking. That was the word for it, the only honest word. Checking the rolls against something—against lists carried in that folio, against orders given in a voice she had never been permitted to hear. And the men who did not match, the men alive in her wards and dead on those pages, were not errors. Errors scattered randomly. These gathered, season upon season, wherever pay and pension and prize money flowed together into the same quiet channel.

She thought of the colonel. Colonel Aldous Vane, who visited monthly, who brought wine for the surgeons and shawls for the matron and kind words for the dying, who had once knelt at the bedside of a boy from his own county and held his hand until the end, and wept, and been seen weeping, and spoken of it afterward with a modest shake of the head. *Any father would have done the same.* The whole hospital loved him. The whole hospital would swear to his goodness in any court in the land.

Mara had watched him kneel at that bedside and had watched, a moment later, his glance travel—not to the boy, not to God, but to the chart at the foot of the bed, to the name written there, with an expression she had spent five years refusing to name because naming it would mean acting on it.

She had named it now. In the dark, in the attic, with the ledger warm against her ribs, she named it: *inventory.* He had looked at a dying boy the way a merchant looks at stock.

But suspicion was not proof, and proof was a thing that lived in documents, and documents lived in the records room, and the records room had begun, lately, to have a lock.

* * *

Three days before Christmas, the fever took the man from the 44th.

He died at dawn, quietly, between one rattle of breath and the next, and Mara closed his eyes and straightened his blanket and stood a moment longer than she needed to, because someone ought to. On his effects list she wrote his name herself, in her own hand, so that at least one record of him would exist outside the regiment's arithmetic. Then she went to the records room, letters in arm, and found the shelf where the 44th lived, and confirmed what she already knew: struck off, eighteen months past, killed in action. No pension due. No widow notified. Somewhere in England a household was waiting for a letter that would never come, or worse—a household that had already received the official grief, the printed sympathy, the folded flag of bureaucratic closure, while the man himself had marched and starved and frozen and died in a fever ward with a stranger holding his hand.

She added him to the ledger that night. Two hundred and thirteen.

Then she sat with the candle low and did the arithmetic she permitted herself only rarely, because it was a corrosive sum. Two hundred and thirteen men. At a private's pay of a shilling a day, struck off for an average of—she stopped herself. She would not count the money. Counting the money made it real, and if it became fully real, fully sized, she did not know whether her nerve would hold. Better to know the names and not the sums. Better to carry the weight of men than the weight of figures, because figures could be argued with, and men could only be mourned or avenged.

A sound on the stair made her pinch the candle dead.

Boots—slow ones, deliberate, climbing toward the attic. Not Pell; Pell moved quick and soft. Not Brandt, who climbed sideways with a hand on the rail and a curse at every step. These boots came on like a man who owned the stairs and everything upon them.

Mara slid the ledger's hiding place flat with her palm, felt the three stitches sit true, and rose from the cot just as the lamp at the stairhead swung its light across the rafters and showed her the face beneath it.

Colonel Aldous Vane stood on the attic landing in his greatcoat, snow melting on his shoulders, smiling his gentle smile.

"Nurse Dravenmoor," he said warmly. "Forgive the hour. I'm told you're the one who writes the letters home. I wonder—might I have a word about the men under your care? There are a few names I find myself... curious about."

Behind the calm of her face, beneath the linen and the seams and the three small stitches, two hundred and thirteen hearts began to beat.
