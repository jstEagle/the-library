## 2. A Stranger with Cold Hands

The snow came down in the flat, indifferent way it did all winter at Harrowgate—falling as though it had somewhere better to be and no interest in arriving. Mara Dravenmoor stood at the ward window with a basin of grey water cooling in her hands and watched it bury the cart ruts on the road from the south.

Behind her, forty men breathed. Some of them well; some of them in the shallow, hitching rhythm she had learned to count without listening, the way a woman counts stitches. Private Ellery had begun that rhythm an hour ago. He would not see morning. She had already written his name on the slate by his bed, and beneath it, in smaller letters that only she would read, the name of his mother in Leeds, and the words *he spoke of you at the end* — which was a lie, because he had not spoken since Tuesday, but it was a lie she could live inside.

That was the arithmetic of Harrowgate. Every comfort purchased with a small forgery. She had stopped keeping accounts of those years ago.

"Mara." Tilly's voice, low, from the doorway. "There's a man at the gate."

"Then let him in. We've beds for the dying and none for the living, but we manage."

"He won't come in." Tilly hesitated, and something in the hesitation made Mara set down the basin. "He can't. He's on his knees in the snow, miss. Cook says he's been there a quarter hour, and he won't take help except—" she frowned, working to remember exactly, "except he keeps saying a word. *Wren.* Over and over. Like it's the only one he owns."

Mara felt the cold of the water through the basin's rim, sudden and specific, as if the winter outside had reached through the glass and found her wrist.

"Wren," she said.

"Yes, miss."

She untied her apron, thought better of it, tied it again. The apron went everywhere with her; it would go to the gate too. "Watch Ellery. If the rhythm changes, send for Brandt and don't wait for me."

Outside, the cold took her breath the way it always did, a hand closing briefly over her lungs. The snow had stopped falling and started simply hanging, filling the air like ash over a slow fire. Lamplight from the gatehouse made a yellow cone in it, and at the edge of the cone, kneeling in the drifts where the road met the hospital wall, was a man.

He was dressed for travel and dressed wrong for winter—a greatcoat meant for someone broader, buttoned crooked, boots gone soft with wet. Snow had built itself along his shoulders and in his hair, so that at first he seemed less a man than a shape the weather had decided to keep. His hands were pressed together in front of him, cupped, sheltering something against his chest the way a man shelters a coal he means to carry across a dark field.

Two orderlies stood a careful distance off, the way men stand from things they have decided are not their trouble.

"He won't come in," one said, echoing Tilly. "Won't take a hand."

"Then he'll die politely at our gate," the other muttered, "and be somebody else's paperwork."

Mara crossed the trampled snow and knelt. The cold came up through her skirts at once, insistent, and she ignored it. Up close the stranger resolved into details: a face gaunt past thirty or short of it, hard to say; lips cracked and bluish; eyes that were open but aimed at nothing, fixed on some middle distance where perhaps his brother was still walking ahead of him.

"You asked for Wren," she said.

His eyes moved. It took them a moment to find her, longer to focus, and when they did she saw the effort cost him something—the way a man spends his last coin to look at what matters.

"You know him," he said. His voice was ruined, scraped down to gravel. Not a question. A man who has carried a question three hundred miles does not ask it anymore; he presents it.

"I know the name," Mara said carefully. "Tell me yours."

"Emrys." He swallowed. "Emrys Wren. Thomas is my brother. I have a letter. I have—" His arms tightened around the thing he sheltered, and she understood then that the letter was not in his pocket. It had been against his skin, under his shirt, for days perhaps, warmed by the only heat he had left, guarded like scripture. "I have to give it to someone who will read it. Not burn it. They burn things here. I've heard they burn things."

"We don't burn letters," Mara said, and heard, underneath her own voice, the ledger sewn into her apron lining, forty names deep, and thought: *we burn plenty.*

She held out both hands. "Give me the letter, Emrys. Then you come inside, and you drink something hot, and you live long enough to hear whatever answer it carries. That is the order of events. Agreed?"

For a long moment he looked at her hands—chapped, red-knuckled, steady—and then at her face, doing whatever arithmetic half-frozen men do about strangers. Whatever sum he reached, he reached it slowly, and then his arms unlocked.

The letter was folded twice and wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string gone grey from handling. Even through the wrapping she could feel how soft the paper had become, worn at the creases like cloth. He had read it many times. Or he had been unable to stop reading it.

She took it. He exhaled—one long, shuddering release, as though the letter had been laced to his ribs—and pitched forward into the snow.

* * *

They got him into the fever ward's coldest corner, which was the warmest thing available, stripped the wet coat off him, and worked heat back into his limbs with blankets and bricks from the oven and the stubborn friction of four pairs of hands. He surfaced once, mumbling, and Mara bent close and caught only two words: *kept writing.* Then he sank again, breathing in long slow pulls, alive in the way of men whose bodies have decided to continue without consulting them.

It was midnight before the ward settled enough for her to sit by the lamp with the oilcloth in her lap.

She told herself she would wait until morning. She told herself a great many reasonable things: that the patient might wake and want privacy; that letters belonged to their addressees; that a nurse who opened other people's post was a nurse on a road that ended badly. All true. All beside the point. Because the name on the outer fold, written in a careful soldier's hand, was *E. Wren*, and the hand that wrote it—

She knew that hand.

She had spent five years learning hands. It was the occupational hazard nobody warned you about: copy enough muster rolls, enough death notices, enough pension claims, and handwriting stops being decoration and becomes identity. She knew Surgeon Brandt's cramped leftward slant. She knew Colonel Vane's florid, expensive capitals, each one drawn like a man signing a portrait of himself. And she knew the round, deliberate, schoolmaster-neat script of Corporal Thomas Wren, 33rd Foot, because she had copied it out herself, twice, into a ledger hidden in her apron—once from the regimental casualty return dated March 1811, and once from the letter of condolence sent to a mother in Shropshire who, as far as the army was concerned, had a dead son.

Thomas Wren, killed in action, body not recovered. Two years dead on paper. Two years of his mother's grief, official and sealed.

And now his handwriting sat in Mara's lap, dated—she turned it toward the lamp—four months ago.

Her fingers were not shaking. She noted that with a kind of distant professional approval. Five years of numbness held, the way a dam holds, invisibly and under enormous pressure. She untied the string.

*Dear Emrys,*

*If this reaches you I am alive, which will surprise you more than it surprises me. Do not write back. Do not tell Mother. Do not go to anyone wearing a uniform, and least of all do not go to anyone kind, because kindness is what they use.*

*I am kept at a place where men write. You will understand that sentence wrongly, and then rightly. We write all day. We write ourselves dead, brother. I have signed my own name to my own death notice eleven times now, and each time the ink dries I am a little less sure which of us it describes.*

*If you come—and you will come, because you are you—look for the fen. Look for lights that burn when honest places sleep. And find the woman at Harrowgate who writes down what the rolls leave out. There is a nurse there. The others speak of her in whispers, the good kind. She keeps names. Tell her the workshop remembers hers.*

*Forgive me for everything I cannot explain on paper. Paper is the problem.*

*Your brother,*
*T. Wren*

Mara read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way one reads a document one expects to testify about.

The ward breathed around her. Somewhere a man moaned in his sleep and was quiet again. The lamp hissed. Outside, the snow had resumed its indifferent falling, erasing the road, erasing the tracks, erasing everything except what a person wrote down and hid.

*A place where men write. We write ourselves dead.*

She had imagined many explanations for the gaps in the rolls. Corruption, certainly—she had assumed corruption the way one assumes weather, general and impersonal. Greed, yes: pay diverted, pensions swallowed, honors sold. She had built her ledger around those assumptions, name by name, a private museum of theft. But she had never once imagined this—that the dead themselves might be conscripted into their own deaths. That a clerk somewhere sat with a list of erased men and made them sign the erasure. That Thomas Wren's neat schoolmaster hand, the same hand that had written home every fortnight until March 1811, had spent two years forging its own extinction.

*Find the woman at Harrowgate who writes down what the rolls leave out.*

The lamp flame leaned in a draft and righted itself. Mara became aware, gradually, of her own heartbeat—not the numbness cracking, exactly, but something adjacent: the sound of ice shifting on a river, deep underfoot, the first groan before the break.

*The workshop remembers hers.* What did that mean? That the prisoners knew of her? That her ledger, which she had believed secret as a grave, was known on the other side of the wall too—that the erased men whispered about the one person who bothered to write them down?

She looked up at the stranger asleep in the cot. In lamplight, with the frost melted out of him, he looked less like a ghost and more like what he was: a man who had walked a very long way on very little, carrying a letter against his heart because it was the only warm thing he owned. His brother's handwriting. His brother alive. And his brother begging him—in the same breath—to trust no one kind.

She was, presumably, the kindness he'd been warned about.

Mara folded the letter along its worn creases, rewrapped the oilcloth, retied the string, and set it on the table by his cot where he would see it first thing. Then she drew her apron forward, and with the practiced motion of five years—fingers finding the seam, the needle-nicked flap, the oilcloth sleeve within—she slid the ledger partway out.

Forty-one names. She added nothing tonight. But she ran her thumb down the column until it stopped at the twelfth entry, written in March 1812 in ink she remembered buying:

*Wren, Thomas, Cpl., 33rd Foot. Killed in action (no body). Mother: Mrs. E. Wren, Shropshire.*

Alive. Signing his own death notice eleven times. Kept writing.

She pushed the ledger home, pressed her palm flat against the apron as if steadying a wound, and sat very still while the ice under her five years of numbness groaned again, louder, and began, unmistakably, to move.

At the door, Tilly appeared with fresh candles and a whisper: "Miss—he's stirring. Asking for you, I think. Or asking for the letter. He keeps saying he has to know if it was read."

Mara rose. Her knees ached from the snow at the gate, and her hands, she noticed, had finally begun to shake—now, when it no longer mattered, when the reading was done and could not be unread.

"Tell him," she said, "that it was read. And tell him I need to know one thing before he sleeps again: whether his brother ever mentioned a colonel by name."

Tilly blinked. "A colonel, miss?"

"A colonel," Mara said, and picked up the lamp, and carried it down the ward toward the stranger with cold hands, feeling—for the first time in five years—not weary, and not safe, but awake.
