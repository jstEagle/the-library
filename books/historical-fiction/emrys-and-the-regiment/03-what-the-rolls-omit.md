## 3. What the Rolls Omit

He woke to the smell of carbolic and wet wool, and for a moment he did not know where the war had put him.

The ceiling above was canvas, sagging with damp, stained brown in places he chose not to study. Somewhere beyond the partition a man was moaning in a rhythm too steady to be pain—more like prayer, or counting. Emrys lay still and took inventory of himself the way his brother had taught him, back when Thomas was twelve and full of soldier dreams: fingers, ten. Toes, ten. Ribs that ached like a kicked door but held. A fever banked low somewhere behind his eyes.

Alive, then. Barely, but honestly.

"You'll want broth before you want talk." The voice came from his left, dry as chalk dust. An old surgeon sat on a stool nearby, mending a strap with waxed thread, not looking up. "You came through my gate half-dead of cold, and you've slept two days. Whatever you're carrying in that coat pocket, it's kept warmer than you have."

Emrys's hand went to his breast before his mind caught up. The letter was there. Damp at the edges, softened at the fold, but whole. He had carried it from the coast road through sleet and three nights of ditches, and he had carried it in his sleep, he suspected, the way a drowning man carries the last thing he grabbed.

"Where am I?"

"Harrowgate." The surgeon bit off his thread. "Field hospital, third division's shadow. You're lucky the gate sentry thought you were a deserter worth saving rather than one worth shooting. Luckier still that Sister Dravenmoor argued your case. She doesn't argue often."

*Dravenmoor.* He turned the name over. He remembered almost nothing of the gate—a lantern, hands, a voice telling him *not yet, you don't get to die here, I haven't read your letter*—and then nothing. But the voice had been a woman's, level and unimpressed by death, and he thought it might have been hers.

"The sister," he said. "Is she—"

"She's always here." The surgeon stood, joints cracking like green kindling. "Eat when she brings it. And lad—if you've got business with the army, take it elsewhere. This house has enough ghosts."

He shuffled away down the ward, and Emrys lay listening to the moaning man count his way toward evening, and did not sleep again so much as sink beneath it.

* * *

She came at dusk with the broth, and he knew her at once—the same level grey eyes he'd seen through the fog of his collapse, set in a face that looked carved for endurance rather than beauty, though there was something in it that might have been both once, before the carving went on too long. Her apron was scrubbed thin. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow and her forearms bore the small white scars of someone who worked near fire and blades daily.

"You're awake properly now." She set the bowl on a crate beside him. "Can you sit?"

"I can stand if the occasion demands."

"It doesn't. Sit." She watched him struggle upright against the pillows with the patience of someone who had watched a thousand men do worse. Then, when he'd settled: "Your name."

"Emrys Wren."

Something moved behind her eyes—quick, controlled, gone. If he hadn't spent five years learning to read silences, he'd have missed it entirely.

"Wren," she repeated. Not *Wren*, the way people repeat a name they've just heard. She said it the way one sets down a weight to look at it.

"You know it," he said.

"I know many names." She straightened the bowl, though it needed no straightening. "Eat your broth."

"My brother is Thomas Wren. Corporal, Fourth Foot. Listed dead after—" He stopped, because her face had changed. Not dramatically. A single degree of stillness more than before, the stillness of a woman standing very carefully on ice she knows is thin. "You do know it."

"Broth first," Mara Dravenmoor said. "Then we'll see what kind of conversation this is."

* * *

He ate because refusing would have cost him her time, and he understood already that her time was the most rationed commodity in Harrowgate. When the bowl was empty she took it, glanced down the ward—nurses moving between cots, the moaning man finally silent, orderlies hauling a pail—and then, instead of leaving, she drew the stool closer and sat, folding her hands in her lap like a woman settling in for a confession. Hers or his, he couldn't tell.

"Now," she said quietly. "Tell me about your brother. All of it. Leave nothing out, even the parts that seem mad. Especially those."

So he told her.

Thomas had enlisted at seventeen, lying about his age with their mother's blessing because the alternative was the mill, and the mill had already taken their father's lungs and was working patiently on everyone else's. Thomas had written home every fortnight—cheerful, misspelled, alive in every line. Then Salamanca, or rather the muddy weeks after it, the skirmishing along the retreat where names went into the ground without ceremony. The regimental return had come to the parish: *Corporal T. Wren, killed in action.* No body returned. No effects beyond a Bible and a broken watch. Their mother had died within the year—not of grief, Emrys said, though grief had helped, but of the fever, and he sometimes thought she'd simply decided the world had taken enough and stopped arguing.

"And you didn't believe it," Mara said.

"I believed it for a while. That's the shameful part." His hands had closed around each other without his permission. "I grieved him properly. Two years. Then, in October, this came."

He drew the letter from his coat. It had traveled soft now, its creases going to fur, but the handwriting was unchanged—the forward-leaning scrawl, the crossed t's like fence rails, the little hook on the W that had made Thomas's schoolmaster throw his slate. Emrys had known it before he broke the seal. He'd known it the way you know a voice calling from another room.

Mara took it with both hands, the way one takes a wounded bird.

*Dear Emry,* it began. *I hope this finds you and finds you well, which is more than it finds me, though I am not complaining, only explaining.*

*You will have been told I am dead. Do not trouble to disbelieve it; it is official, and official things are true the way painted windows are saints. What I cannot tell you plainly, I will tell you crooked, and you were always better at crooked than me.*

*I am kept at a workshop where men are kept writing. The ink never dries here, and neither do we. The quills are fed twice a day and the men are fed less. When the wagon comes with the fen water, count the wheels. Four wheels carry away what eight wheels bring.*

*Tell no one who reads plain. Tell only someone who reads what is left out. There is a woman at the hospital with cold hands who keeps accounts the army does not keep. If you find her, give her my regards and my name, and ask her what happened to the other nineteen.*

*Burn this. I mean it, Emry. Some paper is safer as smoke.*

*Your loving brother, T.W.*

Silence filled the cot-space between them like water filling a boot.

"Nineteen," Emrys said at last, because someone had to say it. "Nineteen what? Nineteen men? She—who is 'she'? Did he mean you?"

"He meant me." Mara's voice was very quiet and very steady, which he would learn was how she sounded when things were worst. She read the letter again, slower, her lips moving slightly over certain phrases. "A workshop where men are kept writing. Ink that never dries. Quills fed twice a day." She looked up. "Do you understand what he's describing?"

"Forgive me, Sister, I've had a fever. Describe it to me."

"He's describing clerks." She said the word as if it tasted of iron. "Men made into clerks. Alive, imprisoned, and writing—writing documents. Discharges, returns, death notices, God knows what else. Your brother isn't dead, Mr. Wren. He's *stationery*."

The word landed in Emrys's chest like a dropped musket. He saw it all in a flash that felt less like understanding than like vertigo: the neat rolls he'd begged to see at the Horse Guards, the clerk who'd refused him with genuine regret, the sealed record, the tidy finality of *killed in action*. Two years of mourning built on paper. Paper made by prisoners. Paper made by his brother's hand.

"They made him write his own death notice." His voice came out strange, flattened, as if someone else were using it. "That's what you're saying. Somewhere in that workshop, Thomas sat down with a pen and wrote *Corporal T. Wren, killed in action*, and signed it, or copied it, or—"

"Or wrote the master copy that others copied." Mara folded the letter along its old creases with great care. "I can't prove any of it yet. But I can tell you what I hold."

And then she did something he would think about for years afterward: she glanced down the ward again, checked the partition shadows, and lifted the hem of her apron perhaps four inches, angling it so only he could see. Sewn into the lining, in a long flat channel of stitching, was something stiff and rectangular. She let the cloth fall.

"There are names in this hospital that appear on no roll," she said. "Men brought in wounded whose regiments have no record of them. Men who die here and are buried under *unknown*. Men who recover and are discharged into a paperwork fog so thick they can't prove they ever served—and so can't claim a pension, or a parcel, or in one case a widow's allowance that their family has starved without for three years. For five years I have written those names down. Where the army omits them, I keep them." She met his eyes. "Your brother's name is in my ledger, Mr. Wren. Entered the week after Salamanca's aftermath, marked *dead per regimental return, no body received*. I remember writing it. I remember being angry that there was no body, because even then I noticed that the ones without bodies were the ones whose pay kept flowing somewhere."

"Somewhere," Emrys repeated. "Where?"

"That's the question I've been asking a wall for five years." Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, worn smooth by use. "And tonight a wall walked through my gate and handed me a letter."

He wanted to laugh. It came out closer to a sob, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes until the ward swam. Grief, he discovered, did not leave when it was proven wrong. It simply changed weapons. Two years of mourning rearranged itself inside him into something harder and hungrier, and he could feel it sharpening even as he sat there, could feel it taking an edge.

"If he's alive," Emrys said, "then others are. The nineteen. However many. Men officially dead, kept hidden, kept *working*—because a dead man's pay goes somewhere, and a dead man's pension is never claimed, and a dead man can't complain when his name signs things he never touched."

"Yes."

"Some officer is running a forge. Not a forge for horseshoes—for *paper*. And the fuel is living men."

"Yes." She was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse—something between alarm and recognition, as if she were seeing her own thoughts wearing a stranger's face. "Mr. Wren. Emrys. Listen to me. What you're proposing to feel right now—the thing burning in you—I've seen it in men before. It gets them killed, or worse, it gets *other* people killed. Your brother's letter says it plainly: *tell no one who reads plain.* That means Vane's people are everywhere, including, I promise you, in this ward."

"Vane?"

"Colonel Aldous Vane. Commanding. He visits Thursdays, brings oranges for the convalescents, and is beloved by everyone who hasn't read his paperwork closely." Her mouth thinned. "Which is nearly everyone, because reading his paperwork closely is apparently fatal."

"What do you mean, fatal?"

She stood, smoothing her apron, and for a moment the mask of the efficient nurse settled back over whatever had opened between them. But her eyes stayed on his, and they were not kind eyes now; they were eyes doing arithmetic.

"I mean that Surgeon Brandt asked questions once, years before my time, and lost his post, and was wise enough to stop asking. I mean that last winter a patient who talked too loudly about missing muster books was transferred to a depot and never wrote to his mother again, though I sent three letters after him. I mean that your brother is alive because he is *useful*, and the moment he stops being useful—or the moment someone dangerous starts asking after him—he will stop being alive. You understand? Your existence is a threat to his. Every question you ask puts a pen nearer his throat."

Emrys sat with that. Outside, the wind worried at the tent ropes, and somewhere far off a picket challenged someone and was answered. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of an army sleeping on top of its secrets.

"Then teach me how to ask questions that don't sound like questions," he said finally. "You've done it for five years. You said it yourself—you notice what others are paid not to see. I'm nobody. A laborer with a dead brother who isn't dead. I can walk where an officer can't, ask what a nurse can't, wait outside walls a surgeon wouldn't approach. You hold the names. I hold the letter. Neither of us holds anything worth a damn alone."

Mara Dravenmoor looked at him for a long moment. He had the sense of a scale balancing behind her eyes, weights he couldn't see—five years of careful silence on one pan, and on the other, a letter in a dead man's living hand.

"*Marlow Fen,*" she said at last, quietly. "There's a shuttered quartermaster depot out past the fen road. Closed since spring, officially. Yet supply wagons go out to it quarterly, heavy going and light returning, and twice I've seen lamplight in its upper windows at hours when honest buildings sleep." She picked up the empty broth bowl. "*Four wheels carry away what eight wheels bring.* Wagons, Mr. Wren. Your brother is telling you where he is, in the only language they haven't taught him to fear."

She turned to go, then paused.

"Rest tonight. Heal. Because tomorrow I'm on duty until dusk, and the night after that, I intend to walk past the fen road on my way to absolutely nowhere—and if a recovered patient happened to stroll the same direction, well. Hospitals are full of coincidences."

She left him with the letter warm against his chest and the fever breaking like a tide going out, and Emrys lay in the dark listening to the ward breathe, and understood that his grief had found its work at last.

Two days later, wrapped in a borrowed greatcoat against the fen wind, he watched the shuttered depot from the treeline and saw the lamps burn, and saw the guards, and saw—through a window's dirty glass, for one impossible second—a familiar profile bent over a desk, pen moving, alive.

Alive, and writing.
