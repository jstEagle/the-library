## 4. Colonel Vane's Kindness

The snow came in the night and laid a white hush over Harrowgate, so that when Mara Dravenmoor crossed the yard at dawn with her apron stiff against her ribs—the ledger's weight sewn into the lining like a second heartbeat—her boots made the only prints between the ward door and the well. She liked these minutes before the hospital woke. The world unobserved was honest. Smoke rose straight from the chimney. Frost furred the gate hinges. Nothing had yet been asked of anyone.

She was breaking ice from the water barrel when she heard the wheels.

Not the creak of the supply cart—that sound she knew in her sleep—but the lighter, quicker rattle of a carriage on frozen ruts, and beneath it the jingle of harness that meant cavalry escort, which meant rank, which meant trouble dressed in good wool. She set down her pail and turned, and saw the Vane crest on the carriage door: a falcon volant, wings up, painted in gold that caught the morning light like something freshly sharpened.

"God's teeth," she said softly, to no one.

Surgeon Brandt appeared at her shoulder, wiping his hands on a rag gone grey with use. His eyes, milky at the edges after forty years of lamp-smoke, were not too dim to read a carriage. "He comes twice a winter," he said. "Never when there's fever. Always when there's paperwork."

"He came in November."

"And we survived November." Brandt tucked the rag into his belt like a man sheathing a weapon he did not quite trust himself to draw. "Smile, girl. It costs nothing you'll miss."

It costs more than you know, she thought, but she arranged her face into the expression she kept for inspectors and bishops—attentive, grateful, faintly simple—and went inside to wake the ward.

* * *

Colonel Aldous Vane filled a doorway the way good news fills a letter: entirely, and not to be trusted. He was a tall man built broad by tailoring rather than nature, fifty perhaps, with silver at his temples placed as precisely as embroidery and a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as it ought. He came through the ward door already apologizing for the hour, already removing his hat, already beloved of every nurse who had not been at Harrowgate five years.

"Ladies," he said, and the word landed like coin on a table. "Do not rise on my account—I see who is rising and who cannot, and I am ashamed to interrupt either."

He moved down the aisle of cots the way a man walks a property line, and Mara walked three steps behind him with the basin and cloths she carried for verisimilitude, watching him work. And it was work; she granted him that. He stopped at Private Hollis, whose leg had come off above the knee on Tuesday, and bent close, and asked his name, and repeated it back as though committing it to some ledger of his own. He touched the blanket at Corporal Finch's shoulder without disturbing the dressing. He produced from his coat, as if by sleight, an orange—*an orange*, in January, at the edge of the fens—and set it in the hands of a boy from Shropshire who stared at it as though the Virgin had sent it.

"There is a wife in Ludlow," Vane told the boy, "who will want to know you kept your colour. Write to her. Paper is free at the adjutant's tent." A smile that reached his eyes, or seemed to. "That is an order."

The ward warmed to him. That was the thing about Vane that Mara had never managed to explain to herself in words she could bear: he was not a performance of kindness that failed under scrutiny. The kindness was real. The orange was real. The hand on the blanket was warm, and the questions about mothers and home parishes were gentle, and the men believed him because there was nothing in him *not* to believe. The horror of him, if he was what she suspected—and she had learned to hold suspicion the way other women held faith, daily and in silence—was not that he pretended to care. It was that he could care, genuinely, about the boy in the cot and feel nothing at all about the boy erased from the roll, because the one was a man and the other was arithmetic.

"Miss—" He had turned, and she realized he had said her name once already. "Miss Dravenmoor. Sister Dravenmoor." The correction, delivered warmly, told her he knew exactly what she was and how long she had been it.

"Colonel."

"I hear the ward speaks of you as though you were part of the building." His eyes were pale grey, unhurried, and they rested on her face with perfect courtesy. "Five winters at Harrowgate. You must dream in laudanum doses by now."

"We keep our dreams small here, sir. They fit better."

He laughed—a real laugh, brief and genuine—and something in her chest tightened around the shape of the ledger beneath her apron. *Do not be charmed*, she told herself. *You have watched charming men sign things.* But the warning arrived late and half-hearted, and she hated herself a little for it, and that hatred was useful, so she kept it.

"You had a bad night two days past," he said. "A man brought in half-dead at the gate. The sentry logged it." He said it lightly, an anecdote, a detail gathered on the way. "Recovered?"

"Sleeping. Frozen feet, exhaustion, a fever just turning. He'll keep his toes if God and brandy are willing."

"No regiment? No papers?" The question still light. Still anecdote.

"Not on him. He'll tell us when he can tell us."

"Of course." Vane smiled, and the smile included her, the ward, the snow outside the high windows, the whole grey morning. "The army misplaces a great many things, Miss Dravenmoor. Men most of all. It is one of the reasons I make these visits—so that the men see the army has not misplaced its affection, whatever else it loses." He clapped his gloved hands together softly, a man delighted with his own day. "Now. Show me your worst, and I will show you what the Crown can spare."

* * *

What the Crown could spare, it emerged, was considerable: a barrel of good salt pork, two crates of bandage linen, a keg of brandy that made Brandt's eyebrows climb toward his hairline, and a purse of coin pressed into Mara's own hands for "small mercies"—fruit, thread, tobacco, the thousand tiny comforts no requisition covered. The nurses clustered near it like sparrows. Even Brandt allowed a grunt that, from Brandt, was applause.

Mara stood with the purse burning in her palm and performed gratitude, and behind the performance her mind ran its old cold arithmetic. Salt pork bought loyalty. Brandy bought silence sweeter than any threat. A colonel who visited twice a winter with oranges and coin was a colonel no board of inquiry would ever believe ill of, because boards of inquiry, like everyone else, believed their stomachs and their warm hands. If Vane was what the ledger implied—if he had signed away the pay of dead men who were not dead—then this generosity was not a contradiction of the crime. It was the crime's camouflage. You did not hide a wolf among sheep by making it look like a sheep. You made it beloved of the dogs.

"Miss Dravenmoor."

His aide had come to her elbow so quietly she started. He was a narrow young man, Captain Pell, with ink-stained cuffs visible beneath his gloves and the fixed pleasantness of a man employed to notice things. "The Colonel asks that the hospital's current returns be made ready. Patient lists, admissions since quarter-day, dispositions of the deceased." A thin smile. "He likes everything of a piece when he reports to the Duke. Tidiness is next to godliness out here, wouldn't you say?"

"The lists are in the records room," Mara said. "Sister Ansel keeps them. I can fetch her."

"No need to trouble her. I've been shown the room." He said it the way one mentions having been shown a garden. "I'll copy what's wanted myself. The Colonel prefers a fair hand."

* * *

She should have looked away. That was the rule she had lived by for five years—*see what must be seen, then be seen seeing nothing*—and for a full minute she obeyed it, changing dressings at the far end of the ward, wringing cloth, murmuring comfort, being furniture. But the records room stood open across the passage, its door propped with the brick that always propped it, and through the gap she could see Captain Pell at the writing desk with the admission book open before him, and he was not skimming. He was copying. Whole pages, in his fair hand, names and regiments and dates of admission and—her breath went shallow—dates of death, where the book recorded them, and dispositions of effects, where it recorded those. He worked quickly, without hesitation, the way a man copies a document he has copied many times in many rooms.

And once—only once—he paused at the bottom of a page, ran his finger down a column of names, and checked them against a folded paper he drew from his breast pocket. His lips moved. Three names, perhaps four. Then the paper went back into the pocket and the pen resumed, and Mara was looking at her basin as though it held the mysteries of faith.

*Thomas Wren*, she thought, and the name sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. *Died of wounds, August 1811. No effects returned. No grave recorded.* One of the first names she had ever written into the apron's lining, in a fair hand of her own, because the roll had lied and she had known it lied and had nowhere to put the knowledge except against her own body. Two years dead on paper. Alive, if Emrys's letter meant anything—and it meant something, it meant everything—in some shuttered place, kept writing.

And now a colonel's aide stood in her records room comparing patient names against a private list, and the stranger who carried that list's answer lay twenty feet away pretending to sleep, and the ledger that would hang them all was stitched into her apron four feet from a captain paid to find such things.

She finished her dressing. She emptied her basin. She did not run, because running was a confession, and she had spent five years teaching her body that truth could wait in the body's silence until truth could do some good.

* * *

Vane took his leave at noon, when the light came flat and white through the high windows and the ward smelled of pork and pine soap. He shook Brandt's hand—Brandt received the shake like a man accepting a bribe he intended to report later—and paused at the door for his final effect, which Mara understood now was an effect, rehearsed, the closing movement of the visit's little symphony.

"Ladies. Gentlemen. Men of the —th." He used the number, and the men who could sit up sat straighter, because a colonel had named them and naming was a kind of knighthood. "You are not forgotten here. Whatever the war takes from a man, let it never take the knowledge that he is *counted*." A beat, perfectly judged. "Every man of you is counted. I have made certain of it personally."

Mara felt the words go through the ward like warmth through a cold room, and felt them land on her like frost. Every man counted. Yes. Oh, yes—counted, entered, balanced, and collected upon. She lowered her eyes at the proper moment and raised them at the proper moment, and saw, as the Colonel turned, that his gaze crossed the cot by the window where Emrys lay, and stopped there for the length of one breath. Not alarm. Not recognition. Something quieter and worse: inventory. A man noting an entry that did not yet appear in any book he controlled.

Then the pale eyes found hers, and Vane smiled his true smile, and said, "See that your mystery guest eats, Miss Dravenmoor. The army regrets losing men. It hates losing mysteries."

The door closed behind him. The carriage wheels crunched away over the snow, taking the falcon crest back down the road toward whatever clean, warm, orderly place held the master book and the payroll of the dead.

* * *

The ward exhaled. Nurses descended on the brandy keg with the reverence of communicants. The Shropshire boy showed everyone the orange and did not eat it.

Mara stood a moment in the emptying passage, listening to the silence settle back into its usual shapes, and then she went to the window cot and sat on the stool beside it, and Emrys opened his eyes. He had heard all of it—she could see that in his face, the taut attention of a man who had lain very still through a very long conversation.

"Well," he said quietly. "That's the wolf."

"That's the wolf," Mara agreed. Her hands, she noticed, were steady on her apron, and the steadiness frightened her more than shaking would have. "And he brought oranges, and he shook every hand offered, and his captain copied our admission book page by page while we watched him do it, because we were busy being *grateful*." She heard the bitterness arrive in her own voice and did not trouble to remove it. "He knows something's at this hospital that shouldn't be. He doesn't know what. Yet."

Emrys pushed himself up on one elbow, and though the fever had taken the flesh from his face, something in his eyes had come back these two days that had not been there at the gate—something hard and lit, banked coal rather than dying fire.

"Then we'd best decide," he said, "what he finds if he comes back."

Outside, the snow had begun again, soft and thick and patient, filling in the wheel ruts, filling in her footprints from the morning, erasing, gently and without malice, every mark that had been made.

Mara watched it fall and thought: *he does it the same way. Kindly. Thoroughly. So that by spring no one can prove the road was ever used.*

She put her hand flat against her apron, over the stitching, over the ninety-one names sleeping in the linen, and began—to Emrys, to herself, to the falling snow—to count.
