## 5. The Widow's Testimony

The address on the scrap of paper read *Mrs. Hollis, tanner's lane, third cottage past the smoke-house, Kettleby*—and it had cost Emrys two days of listening and one night of drinking with a discharged wagoner who talked too much when the gin went round. The wagoner had never heard of Colonel Vane's workshop; he had heard of Mrs. Hollis all the same, because everyone in Kettleby had heard of Mrs. Hollis, the woman whose dead husband sent money home.

Emrys walked the last three miles from the coaching inn through a landscape gone grey and wet, hedgerows dripping, the road churned to porridge by supply carts. He had learned in the army that weather was a kind of language, and this sullen drizzle said what it always said: *keep your head down, keep walking, ask nothing.*

Kettleby was a village of perhaps forty roofs gathered around a church with a leaning spire. He found Tanner's Lane easily enough—the smell found him first, sharp and organic, rising from the smoke-house like a challenge. The third cottage past it had a garden gone to winter stubble, a goat tethered to a post, and washing on the line despite the damp: small clothes, a man's shirt mended at both elbows, sheets faded to the colour of old bone.

A woman knelt in the garden pulling carrots from the black earth. She was younger than he expected—not yet thirty, with a broad, capable face and hands red to the wrist. She heard his boots on the path and looked up before he could arrange his own expression into something harmless, and he saw her do the same arithmetic he had seen a hundred soldiers do at first sight of him: *friend, quartermaster, bailiff, or worse.*

"Looking for work?" she asked. Her voice was level. Her eyes were not.

"No, ma'am." He stopped at the gate out of courtesy and because he had learned that gates were honest things—they told you where you stood. "I'm looking for a man called Private Daniel Hollis. Fourth Regiment of Foot."

The carrot in her hand stopped moving.

"He's dead," she said. "Killed at Salamanca. Two years gone. There's a stone in the churchyard if you've come to gawp at it."

"I don't doubt the stone, ma'am. But I'd take it kindly if you'd tell me who paid for it."

That question landed harder than any he might have asked outright. She rose slowly from her knees, wiping her hands on her apron, and looked past him down the lane as though checking whether anyone stood within earshot—a habit, Emrys thought, of someone who had been taught the price of being overheard.

"You're not army," she said. "Army men don't ask who pays for stones."

"No, ma'am. I'm not army. Not anymore." It was close enough to true. "My name is Emrys Wren. My brother served under Colonel Aldous Vane. Thomas Wren."

Something crossed her face then—quick, swallowed, but unmistakable. Recognition. Not of the name; of the *category*. He was one of them, then. A family member. One of the people who had received a letter beginning *It is my painful duty to inform you*, and had spent the years since turning the words over like a coin that would not spend.

She unlatched the gate.

"You'd best come in," she said quietly. "And walk soft. My sister-in-law lives at the end house and she's got a tongue like a church bell."

* * *

The cottage interior was clean and poor and warm, a fire banked low in the hearth, herbs hanging from the beams, a single candle lit though the day was barely darkened. On the mantel, beside a chipped saint whose face had been rubbed smooth by handling, sat a framed miniature of a soldier—a round-faced young man with painted pink cheeks, holding a helmet far too large for him.

"Your husband?" Emrys asked, though he knew.

"Yes." Mrs. Hollis set water on to heat without asking whether he wanted any; it seemed to be reflex. "Daniel. Painted before he marched, when he still thought war would be an adventure." She did not look at the picture. People who lived with grief learned not to look at its furniture too often. "Now. Say what you came to say, Mr. Wren, and then say why I shouldn't be frightened of it."

"May I sit?"

"You may stand until you've earned the chair."

He respected that. He took off his hat, which was all the respect he had to offer in material form, and laid the truth out as plainly as he could: his brother listed dead two years past, no body returned, no effects forwarded; a letter arriving last month in a hand he knew better than his own; a hospital nurse named Mara Dravenmoor who kept names the rolls omitted; and now a trail leading from a shuttered depot at Marlow Fen to villages exactly like this one.

"I don't know what your husband's stone says, Mrs. Hollis. But I know what mine's letter said. And I know that last week a man I watched through a fence at Marlow Fen turned around, and it was Thomas."

The water began to mutter in the pot. She did not move toward it.

"There are women in four counties," she said at last, "who could tell you the same thing about their men. If they'd tell you anything at all."

"Then tell me yours."

She turned away from the hearth and studied him, and he let himself be studied. He had been interrogated once, briefly, by French partisans in '10 who wanted to know whether he was spy or fool; he had survived by standing still inside himself and letting them find nothing to grip. He used the same stillness now.

"Daniel sends money," she said finally. "Twelve shillings, every quarter, regular as tithe day. Comes wrapped in brown paper, no note, postmarked sometimes from York, sometimes from Leeds, once—I remember it plain, because it made me laugh and then made me cry—from Bath, of all places. Twelve shillings, and Daniel dead two years, and me a widow with a grave to tend and a purse that fills itself."

"Who delivers it?"

"The post. That's the devil of it, Mr. Wren. It comes through the post. Through the King's own mail, official as a summons. Dead men don't draw pay. So either the King is paying a ghost—which I doubt, whatever the parson says—or somebody is paying me out of some pocket that isn't the King's, and keeping the rest."

"And you never asked."

Her chin came up. For a moment he saw the young woman who had married a round-faced boy in an oversized helmet, and the steel underneath the apron.

"Asked *whom?*" she said. "The colonel? Shall I describe Colonel Vane to you, Mr. Wren? Because I expect you've met him. He came here himself, the spring after Daniel died. Came to the door with his hat in his hand and condolences in his mouth like sweetmeats. Told me Daniel fell honourably, told me the regiment remembered him, told me—" her voice caught on something between laughter and fury— "told me that if ever I found myself in hardship, I need only write to him *personally*. Do you understand? Not the pay office. Not the regimental agents. Him. Aldous Vane, personally, like a lord dispensing charity. And he stood there in the doorway smiling while his aide—that pale fellow with the ink-stained cuffs, always writing—wrote down everything in our house. What I had. Who visited. Whether I'd remarried. Whether there was family to speak for me."

"A list," Emrys said softly.

"A ledger," she corrected. "I know a ledger when I see one. My father kept sheep and I know what a tally-stick is for. We were being counted, Mr. Wren. Counted and filed, like stock."

The kettle began to sing. She poured it over the leaves in the pot with steady hands, set the pot to steep, and only then sat down herself, waving him into the chair across from her as though granting a pardon.

* * *

Over tea—weak, scalding, offered with a pride that forbade refusal—she told him the rest in pieces, the way people tell things they have never dared assemble whole.

There were others. Not in Kettleby itself; in Ketterby everyone minded everyone's business, and Mrs. Hollis's quarterly parcels were the village's most carefully unmentioned subject. But at market in Ashford she had fallen into conversation with a woman named Pike whose husband had been a corporal in the same company, dead these three years—and Mrs. Pike received money too. And a cousin in Whitstable whose brother was struck off after Talavera, presumed dead, and whose mother got a letter twice a year in a hand she swore was her son's own, unsigned, saying only *I am well, pray do not enquire.*

"They know," Mrs. Hollis said flatly. "Not all of it, maybe. Not the how or the why. But they know their dead are breathing somewhere. You cannot hide a thing like that from mothers and wives, Mr. Wren. We count footsteps in the dark. We know our men's handwriting better than we know our own faces. What we don't know is what we're allowed to say."

"And what happens," Emrys asked, "to those who say it?"

She looked into her cup for a long moment.

"You know the Pell family, out beyond the mill? No—you wouldn't, you're not local. There was a Mrs. Pell. Husband dead at Badajoz, or so the letter said. And Mrs. Pell was not a careful woman, Mr. Wren. She liked her gin and she liked her grievances, and one Sunday after church she stood up in the yard and told half the village that her Michael wasn't dead at all, that she'd had word from him, that the army was a den of thieves and she'd say so to whoever cared to listen. Loudly. Twice."

"What happened?"

"She wrote to Colonel Vane. As invited. Asking after her widow's pension, which had somehow gone astray." Mrs. Hollis's voice had gone very quiet, very even, the voice of a woman walking carefully across ice she already knew was thin. "Within the month she was taken up for debt—a bill for flour she swore she'd paid, and maybe she had, and maybe the baker's book said otherwise. Committed to the house of correction at Maidstone. Six weeks. When she came out she was a changed woman—quiet, obedient, wouldn't say a word against anyone. Moved away within the year. Last I heard she was in service in Dover and writes nobody."

"That could have been coincidence."

"It could," she agreed. "So could the fire at the Pike farm, and the way the militia suddenly found three years of back-taxes owed by the Whitstable mother. Coincidence is very loyal to Colonel Vane, Mr. Wren. It follows him about like a dog."

He believed her. That was the terrible thing—he believed her completely, and each confirmation settled into him not as surprise but as recognition, the way a lock recognizes its key. Mara's ledger, Thomas's coded letter, the lights at Marlow Fen, and now a countryside dotted with women who kept their mouths shut and their parcels wrapped in brown paper: it was not one crime but a system, an architecture, load-bearing walls of fear holding up a roof of respectable ink.

"Why tell me, then?" he asked. "You've more to lose than I do. I've got nothing left for them to burn."

She set down her cup. Her hands, he noticed, were not quite steady, and she noticed him noticing, and folded them deliberately in her lap.

"My Daniel is dead," she said. "Whatever else is true—whatever game the Colonel is playing, whatever pocket his twelve shillings comes from—my Daniel is under that stone in the churchyard, and no power in England changes that. But your brother is alive, Mr. Wren. And Mrs. Pike's husband may be alive, and the Whitstable boy certainly is, or his mother's letters are a miracle. Somebody has to be the one who isn't afraid." She lifted her chin again, and this time he saw that the steadiness was not calm at all—it was terror held upright by will, like a mast stepped in a storm. "I've been afraid five years. It hasn't bought me anything worth having."

"There'll be reprisals," he said. "If this goes forward, they'll come for anyone who spoke."

"I know it." She said it simply, the way one says *it will rain.* "But you listen to me, Emrys Wren. Those men at Marlow Fen—your Thomas among them—are writing their names in other men's ink, signing other men's deaths, spending their lives making dead men of themselves so that a colonel can wear a finer coat. Their families get twelve shillings a quarter and a closed mouth. That's not life and it's not death either; it's neither, it's the space between, and it eats a person alive. I've lived in it. Every woman you're going to find has lived in it. So when you go back to your nurse and your plans, you tell her this: the powerless aren't silent because we're stupid or content. We're silent because we're alone. Give us two of us in a room, give us ten, give us proof that speaking won't be shouting into a wind that only carries our voices to the wrong ears—and you'll find out how long we've been waiting to talk."

Emrys sat very still. Outside, the drizzle had thickened towards proper rain, drumming on the thatch, and the goat complained somewhere in the garden, and the little painted soldier smiled his painted smile above the hearth at nothing.

"Mrs. Hollis," he said at last, "the woman I told you of—Nurse Dravenmoor—she keeps a ledger. Names. Five years of names of men the rolls erased. Yours may be among them."

"Daniel's on no roll."

"That's rather the point, ma'am. He'd be on hers."

For the first time since he had entered the cottage, she wept—one tear, quickly mastered, dashed away with the heel of her hand as though swatting a fly—but her voice held.

"Then you tell that nurse from me," she said, "that she'd better keep that ledger safe. Because there are more of us waiting on it than she knows. And tell her—" the tear's twin escaped, and she let this one fall unchecked— "tell her that if my Daniel's name is written anywhere in this world outside of God's book, I want to read it aloud myself."

He stayed another hour. She fed him bread and cheese he had not asked for and could not politely refuse, drew him a rough map of the market towns where the other wives and mothers could be found—Ashford, Whitstable, a place called Netherfield where a sister-in-law kept silence like a miser keeps coin—and at the gate, in the rain, she gripped his hand hard enough to hurt.

"Walk soft through the village," she warned. "And Mr. Wren—if anything you do brings the Colonel's pale man down this lane, you come and warn me first. Don't let me learn it from a knock on the door. Promise me that much."

"I promise, ma'am."

He walked back through the rain with the map folded against his chest, and it was not until he reached the crossroads out of Kettleby that he allowed himself to understand what he was carrying. He had come looking for evidence and found a congregation. Mara had her ledger; Thomas had his workshop; and now, spread across four counties of mud and silence, there was this—women counting quarters, reading unsigned letters, keeping watch over graves that lied.

The powerless, keeping faith with one another in the dark.

He was two miles along the Ashford road, wet through and not minding, when he saw the carriage.

It stood at the mouth of a farm track ahead of him, glossy black, four horses steaming in the rain, a crest on the door he recognized now the way one recognizes a scar. A pale man with ink-stained cuffs stood beneath an umbrella beside the carriage window, taking instructions, nodding, writing.

The window was lowered. From within, unhurried, comfortable, a gloved hand extended two fingers—the smallest possible acknowledgment—and a voice carried through the rain, mild and pleasant as Sunday bells:

"Mr. Wren. How fortunate. I was just thinking of paying my respects to Widow Hollis."
