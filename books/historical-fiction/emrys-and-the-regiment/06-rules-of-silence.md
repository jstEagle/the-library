## 6. Rules of Silence

Surgeon Brandt found me at the washbasins an hour after Emrys was carried through the gate, his hands still dripping, his sleeves rolled to the elbow and stained to the shoulder in the way that had stopped troubling me years ago.

"You've taken a new patient," he said. Not a question. Brandt never asked questions; he made observations and waited to see whether you were honest enough to confirm them. It was, he liked to say, the only diagnostic method he trusted in a hospital built on lies.

"A stranger. Collapsed at the gate. Half-frozen." I wrung out the linen I'd been scrubbing and did not look at him. "Fever coming on. I put him in the far cot by the boiler."

"The one you keep empty."

"The one that's coldest. Fever likes company."

He grunted. Around us the ward breathed its night sounds—moaning, snoring, the wet rattle of a man at the far end whose lungs were filling like a boat taking water. Somewhere a candle guttered. Brandt stood beside me at the basin for a long moment, watching my hands rather than my face, which was his habit and, I suspected, his kindness.

"Mara," he said at last, quietly enough that the ward could not hear. "The man you pulled from the snow. Wren is not a common name hereabouts, but it is not so uncommon that a woman with your memory would fail to place it."

My hands went still in the water. The cold of it climbed my wrists.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Five years," he said. "Five years you have worked this ward, and in five years I have watched you write letters at midnight by a candle you trimmed yourself because the quartermaster's candles burn too fast. I have watched you remember men's names after they themselves forgot them under laudanum. I know what kind of woman keeps an empty cot by the boiler, Dravenmoor. It is not the kind who fails to place a name."

I dried my hands. Slowly. Giving my face time to settle into the smoothness I had practiced until it came as naturally as breathing.

"He asked after his brother," I said. "Thomas Wren. Killed in action, autumn of '11. It's in the rolls."

"It is in the rolls," Brandt agreed. "And that is precisely why you should let it lie where it is buried."

I turned to look at him then. He was older than the war—older, sometimes, than the century, with his white stubble and his bent spine and his spectacles mended twice with wire—but his eyes were quick as a boy's, and they held mine now without flinching.

"You know something," I said.

"I know what happens to people who ask questions in this garrison." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his apron, unhurried, though I saw his hand tremble very slightly—a palsy he would deny if accused. "In 1809 there was a clerk at the paymaster's office. Decent fellow. He noticed that a draft of forty men marched out of Harrowgate and only thirty-six names ever drew pay again. He wrote to the adjutant asking after the discrepancy. Polite letter. Very proper. He signed it."

* * *

Brandt did not sit down, and I understood that he did not intend to tell this story sitting, as though standing kept it honest.

"They posted him to Gibraltar within the month. Not punished—promoted, on paper. A fine warm posting, they said, good climate for a man of his constitution. His wife was given three days' notice to leave quarters. His effects traveled ahead of him and half of them never arrived. And when he wrote back from Gibraltar—two letters, then four, then nine—asking again, politely, properly, about those four missing names, the answers stopped coming at all."

"What happened to him?"

"No one knows. Gibraltar records show him transferred onward to a depot in Cork. Cork has no record of him at all. His widow petitioned the regiment for eleven years before anyone told her he had never officially existed past March 1810. She died last winter in a parish poorhouse." He set his spectacles back on his nose. "That is what a question costs, Mara. That is the price list. I watched it paid once and I have never forgotten the sum."

"You're telling me to stop."

"I'm telling you what I wish someone had told that clerk." He looked down the ward, at the rows of breathing men, at the candlelight moving over faces slack with morphine and pain. "You have done something these five years—I don't know what, and I have not asked, and do not ask me why, because the not-asking is the only protection I can offer you. Whatever it is, it has stayed small and quiet and beneath notice, and that is why you are still alive. But the man in the boiler cot has changed the arithmetic. A brother comes looking. Questions get loud. Loud questions are how widows end up in poorhouses."

"Or how frauds end up in courtrooms," I said.

Brandt's mouth folded into something too bitter to be called a smile. "In thirty years of soldiering I have seen two courts-martial worth the name, and both of them hanged sergeants for stealing rum. The men who steal armies do not stand trial. They stand on platforms and give speeches about sacrifice." He gathered his instruments—he had been cleaning a wound somewhere, then; there was blood drying in the creases of his knuckles I hadn't noticed—and paused at the edge of the lamplight.

"For whatever it is worth," he said, not turning around, "the boy in the boiler cot. Keep him fed and keep him ignorant and send him home as soon as he can walk. Men who come looking for dead brothers have a way of joining them."

Then he was gone, his footsteps receding down the stone corridor, and I stood alone at the basins with my hands still damp and my heart beating in a rhythm I had not felt since the winter of 1808.

* * *

Because that was when it started—the ledger, the rules, all of it. That was when I broke my first promise.

His name was Private Joseph Callow. Seventeen years old, lied on his papers to say nineteen, with a cowlick at the crown of his head that no amount of regulation cropping could subdue and a habit of whistling under his breath while he worked, tunelessly, endlessly, the way boys whistle when they are frightened and pretending not to be. He came to Harrowgate in November of '08 with a musket ball through his thigh, and he was the sort of patient nurses fight over and surgeons pretend not to favor: grateful, cheerful, obedient, forever apologizing for the smell of his own wound.

His mother lived in a village in Dorset whose name I have carried in my head for five years like a coin in a pocket: Winterbourne Steepleton. He talked about her constantly. How she salted her own pork. How she could read but pretended she couldn't so the squire's wife would bring her things to read aloud. How she had a letter from his father—who died at sea before Joseph could walk—that she kept in the Bible and took out on Sundays, and how she read it even though she claimed not to be able to read, sounding out the words with her finger, and Joseph knew perfectly well she could read because who else had taught him?

"She'll want to know exactly where it hit me," he told me, the second week, while I changed his dressing. "Not the gore of it. Just—where. She'll want to picture it proper. Tell her the left thigh, low, near the knee, and that I was shot walking toward the firing and not running from it. She'll want that part most. Tell her that first."

I promised. I promised him twice, and the second time he made me say it back word for word, like scripture, and laughed at my solemnity with his whole crooked grin.

He died on the ninth of December. The wound turned; the fever took him in thirty-six hours, and at the end he was past knowing me, calling instead for his mother in a voice worn thin as onion skin. I sat with him because no one else would, and when it was over I closed his eyes and straightened his blanket and went out into the corridor and was sick into a drain, and then I washed my face and went back to work, because that is what we did, that winter, every winter.

It was three weeks before I learned what happened to him afterward—or rather, what did not happen.

I had taken up the habit, by then, of reading the casualty returns. Not because anyone asked me to; a nurse had no business with the returns. But the returns passed through the records room, and the records room was cold, and I carried coals through it twice a day, and a paper left open on a desk is a paper a woman can read while her hands stay busy. I looked for Callow the way I looked for all my patients who died: to see him entered, recorded, witnessed somewhere beyond my own memory. It was a private ritual. The army forgot men in days; I checked.

Joseph Callow was not on the return. Not among the dead, not among the discharged, not among the transferred. I searched every column of every page for an hour with the coal scuttle growing cold in my hand.

The next week I looked again, thinking I had missed him. Then I asked—carefully, obliquely, the way one asks about weather—a clerk whether the November casualties from the 4th had been fully reported. He shrugged and said returns were returns, and what did I want, a nurse counting soldiers? And I understood two things at once, standing there with my cooling coals: first, that Joseph Callow had been erased—struck from existence between the ward and the ledger, so that his pay would continue flowing into some pocket not his own, so that some number somewhere would balance; and second, that if I said one word about it aloud, to anyone, I would go the way of the clerk Brandt spoke of, or simply the way of Joseph Callow, and no one would check the returns for me either.

So I did the only thing left. That night, in the lantern-lit cubby behind the linens where the hospital kept its stores, I took a spare apron from the shelf—the plain grey linen ones, replaced quarterly whether worn or not—and I unpicked six inches of its hem. Into the fold I slipped a single sheet of paper, cut small to fit, folded twice, on which I had written:

*Callow, Joseph, Pvt., b. Dorset (Winterbourne Steepleton). Died 9 December 1808, Harrowgate. Wound: left thigh, low, near knee. Shot walking toward the firing, not running. Mother living.*

And below it, because a promise is a debt regardless of the creditor's ability to collect: *Letter owed.*

I sewed the hem shut with thread matched to the linen, by candlelight, taking care that the stitches ran straight, and I hung the apron on my peg, and I wore it the next morning, and the morning after that, and I have worn it every working day since. There are more pages now. There are a hundred and forty-three pages. There are names I copied from returns and names I caught in conversation, names murmured by dying men, names I read upside-down on clerks' desks, each one folded into the hem of a garment that looks, to any searching eye, like exactly what it pretends to be: a nurse's apron, unremarkable, grey.

Over five years the ledger taught me rules, the way everything teaches rules to those paying attention. Never write a name the same day you learn it; grief makes handwriting careless. Never let two entries share a page; if one page is lost, let it take only one man's memory with it. Never record where the paper might be found on your person during a search—hence the apron's rotation, three of them now, hemmed identically, hanging in rotation so that no single garment grew suspiciously stiff along its lower edge. And the greatest rule, the one Brandt was circling tonight without knowing it: silence is not cowardice. Silence is triage. You cannot shout every truth in a garrison built on lies; shouting is how truths die. You write them small, you hide them deep, and you keep them alive the way one keeps a flame in a wind—cupped, sheltered, patient.

For five years the rules held. For five years I believed that noticing quietly was the same as surviving honestly, that my stitched hem was conscience enough.

Then a half-frozen man collapsed at our gate carrying a letter from a dead man, and every rule I owned began, quietly, to come apart.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in the linen room with the lantern hooded to a slit and the ledger open across my knees, running my finger down page after page until I found it, halfway down the eighty-first entry, in ink gone brown with age:

*Wren, Thomas, Cpl., 4th Regt. Reported killed in action 14 October 1811. No body returned. No effects listed. Next of kin: brother, E. Wren.*

Next of kin: brother. I had written it two years ago and thought nothing of it—a formality, a line to be filled. Now the line looked back at me like an open door.

E. Wren. Emrys. The man asleep in the boiler cot with frostbite whitening his fingers and a letter against his chest written in the hand of a corporal the army had declared dead—officially, tidily, profitably dead, dead in exactly the manner that stopped a corporal's pay and pension and honors from going anywhere but into the pocket of a colonel whose aide, I remembered with a fresh chill, had been copying patient lists only days ago.

No body returned. I saw it plainly now, the pattern beneath my own entries: of my hundred and forty-three names, twenty-nine shared that same terrible detail. Twenty-nine men reported killed with nothing to bury. Twenty-nine ghosts whose deaths balanced somebody's books. I had assumed they were lies told about corpses. It had never once occurred to me—they had never once been permitted to occur to me—that the corpses might object.

The lantern guttered. Down the corridor, the ward breathed on, and somewhere in it a living brother slept under a dead man's handwriting, and old Brandt's warning walked circles in my head: *keep him ignorant and send him home.* Send home a man whose brother was alive? Watch him walk out of the gate and out of reach, carrying half a truth I had spent five years stitching into a hem?

I folded the ledger closed. My hands were steady. That surprised me—that after everything, the fear arrived not as trembling but as a strange, cold clarity, the same clarity I felt over open wounds, where the only mercy is precision.

Tomorrow I would show him the eighty-first entry. Tomorrow the rules of silence would bend, or break, or prove finally what they were made of.

And tomorrow, I decided, watching the lantern-flame steady itself in the draught from under the door—tomorrow I would ask Emrys Wren exactly where, in the flat dark fen country east of Harrowgate, a dead man goes to learn the trade of writing.
