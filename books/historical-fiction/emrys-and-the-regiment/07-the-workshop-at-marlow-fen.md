## 7. The Workshop at Marlow Fen

The letter had said *where the reeds grow taller than a man*, and so Emrys Voss went looking for reeds.

He left Harrowgate before dawn on the third day, wrapped in a drover's coat two sizes too large and carrying a fisherman's creel he had no use for. Disguise was half the work of survival; his father had taught him that much before the war took everything else. A man walking with purpose drew eyes. A man walking with a creel and a slouch drew none.

The road out of the town thinned to a cart track, and the cart track thinned to nothing at all, and then there was only the fen — a grey immensity of sedge and standing water under a sky the color of old pewter. The cold came up through the ground itself, through the soles of his boots, as though the marsh were trying to pull him down by the feet. Mist hung in the hollows like something breathed there and forgotten.

*Where the reeds grow taller than a man.* Thomas's hand had written it, in the coded margins of a letter that should never have existed, from a man who should never have been alive to write it. Emrys carried the pages against his chest now, inside his shirt, where the paper had warmed to the temperature of his own skin until he could almost believe it was part of him.

Two years. His brother had been dead two years — officially, irrevocably dead, with a printed name on a casualty list and a sympathy note signed by an officer who had never seen Thomas Wren's face. Emrys had grieved him. He had done it properly, thoroughly, the way one does: the sleepless months, the drinking, the long argument with God. And then a gaunt stranger had stumbled through the hospital gates at Harrowgate with frozen hands and a letter, and every hour of that grief had turned out to be built on a lie.

He did not know yet whether to be furious or grateful. Perhaps, he thought as he walked, the two were the same thing wearing different coats.

* * *

The clues in the letter had been few, and maddeningly oblique — written that way, Emrys understood now, so that if the letter were intercepted it would read as a farmer's idle chatter about land and water.

*Old Marlow's place stands empty but the lamps are lit.*

*They keep writing where nobody reads.*

*If you come, come when the moon is a nail-paring, and walk the causeway, not the road.*

Tonight was the night. The moon above the fen was exactly that — a thin white crescent, a paring of nail thrown into the dark. Emrys reached the causeway as the last light failed.

It was a raised spine of packed earth and stone running straight out across the marsh toward a shape on the horizon that might have been a hill or might have been a building; in the failing light there was no telling which. On either side the water lay black and still, pocked with reed-beds, and the reeds there did indeed grow taller than a man — great dry banks of them, whispering whenever the wind moved, hissing when it stopped, as if the fen itself were discussing him.

He walked. The mist thickened. Twice he heard things in the water — the plop of some animal, the cry of a bird he could not name — and twice he stopped and let the silence close over him again before moving on. His breath hung about his head in clouds. His hands, deep in the drover's coat, had gone past cold into a kind of burning numbness, and he found himself thinking absurdly of Mara Dravenmoor's hands, quick and sure among the bandages, warming his fingers with a tin cup of broth the morning after he'd collapsed at her gate.

*You hold halves of one truth,* she had said to him, three nights ago, in the narrow storeroom where they'd spread Thomas's letter between them like a map to buried treasure. She had a way of saying enormous things flatly, without drama, as if she were reading a supply inventory. It steadied him more than any comfort would have.

She was working tonight. She had pressed a folded slip of paper into his palm before he left — names, dates, entries copied from her impossible hidden ledger — and told him: *If you find men in there who appear on this page, I need to know which. Not how many. Which.* Because a number proved nothing. A name proved a man had lived.

Emrys intended to bring her back more than names. He intended to bring her back his brother.

* * *

The building resolved out of the dusk gradually, the way ships do.

First it was only a darker mass against the darkening sky. Then the geometry emerged — long and low, roofed in slate, its ridge line broken by three squat chimneys. A wall ran around it, chest-high, topped with broken glass that caught the last grey light like a row of tiny teeth. A gate of iron bars stood closed in the wall's face, and beside the gate a lamp burned on a post.

And beyond the wall, behind shuttered windows, other lamps were burning too.

Emrys left the causeway fifty yards short of the gate and dropped into the reeds, moving low and slow, the way he'd learned to move along hedgerows in another country, another war, when moving badly meant dying well. The reeds closed over him. They were dry and razor-edged and they whispered against his coat with every yard, and he hated them and blessed them in equal measure.

He came up at last against the outer corner of the wall where it met a drainage sluice, and wedged himself into the angle, and looked through the bars of the gate.

A yard. Gravel, swept clean. A wagon under a tarpaulin. Two soldiers leaning against the wall by a side door, rifles slung, stamping against the cold — guards, then, real ones, not decorative. And set into the long face of the building, a row of tall windows whose shutters had been pulled to but not latched, so that thin blades of lamplight fell through the gaps and lay striped across the gravel like the ribs of some enormous pale fish.

Emrys settled in to watch, and made himself comfortable with discomfort, because watching was a trade and he intended to practice it properly.

For a long while nothing happened. The guards talked in low voices he couldn't catch. Smoke rose from the chimneys. Somewhere inside, faintly, came a sound that puzzled him at first — a rhythmic rustling, steady as a loom. Paper. Dozens of sheets being turned, sorted, stacked, the sound bleeding out through the shutters like the breathing of the building itself.

*They keep writing where nobody reads.*

Then a door opened somewhere within, spilling a wedge of yellow light across the yard, and a man came out carrying a bucket, and Emrys's heart stopped and started again in the space of a single breath.

The man wore a private's uniform. It fit him badly — too loose at the shoulders, too short at the ankle, as though it had been issued to someone else. He crossed the yard to a drain grating, emptied the bucket, straightened, and in turning back toward the door his face passed directly through one of the blades of lamplight falling from the shuttered windows.

Gaunt. Bearded. Cheeks fallen in, hair grown long and grey-streaked at thirty years old. Two years thinner and harder than the brother Emrys had waved off to war from a lane in Dorset with a knapsack on his shoulder and a joke on his lips.

But it was Thomas. It was Thomas, alive, walking, complaining quietly to himself the way he always had, kicking the drain grate in mild irritation as though the world's small inconveniences were still worth noticing — and Emrys felt hope go through him like a bayonet, so sharp and so total that it hurt worse than grief ever had. Grief, at least, he had been armored against. Hope had come in through the seams.

He bit down on the sound that rose in his throat. He pressed his fist against his own mouth. The reeds whispered. The guards went on talking. Thomas shuffled back inside with his empty bucket, and the door closed, and the wedge of light died, and Emrys crouched in the freezing mud of the fen with tears running freely down his face and freezing in his stubble, laughing without sound at the sheer unbearable size of what he had just seen.

His brother was alive. His brother was here. His brother was a prisoner doing clerical work in a shuttered depot in the middle of a marsh, guarded by soldiers, dressed in a uniform that belonged to no living man — because Thomas Wren was dead. Officially. In print. With a pension paid out to no one and honors recorded nowhere and a grave that held no body, because Colonel Aldous Vane did not bury the men he erased. He used them.

Emrys stayed in the reeds until his legs went numb, and then numbness became a thing he simply worked around, and he kept watching.

* * *

The work of the night revealed itself slowly, in fragments, the way a picture emerges from crosshatched ink.

An hour after Thomas went in, the side door opened again and four men came out into the yard under guard, marching in nothing so orderly as a formation — shuffling, blinking in the lamplight, holding their hands tucked into their armpits against the cold. Soldiers' bearing worn down to its skeleton. One of them coughed, a deep wet rattle that Emrys felt in his own chest.

They were taken to the wagon. The tarpaulin came off. Crates came down, and the prisoners — Emrys could no longer think of them as anything else — began loading it: boxes, bales, oilcloth-wrapped bundles loaded with a care that told him the contents mattered. Quarterly supply run, Mara had guessed, working it out from the rhythm of the requisition papers she'd seen pass through Harrowgate. The workshop fed the regiment forged documents the way a hearth fed smoke, and once a quarter the ash was hauled away.

One of the loading men straightened to shift a crate higher, and the lamp on the gatepost caught his face full on.

Emrys knew the face. Not from life — from a list. From a slip of paper in Mara Dravenmoor's apron lining, transcribed in her small fierce hand: *Private Samuel Oduya, 4th Foot, entered in the ledger 12 March 1811, reported dead of wounds, no burial record, pay continued eight months to a signature that was not his wife's.*

He had memorized the page she'd given him. All nineteen names. He recited them to himself each night like a rosary, and here, hauling crates in a fen at midnight, was one of them — breathing, bending, alive.

*Which,* Mara had said. *Not how many. Which.*

He counted as the loading went on. Three faces he could see clearly enough to be certain, and two more that might have been others on her list or might have been strangers. Nineteen names, and at least three of them standing in a lamplit yard doing a dead man's labor for the man who had killed them on paper.

The last crate went up. The tarpaulin came down. And then one of the guards did a thing that removed the last of Emrys's doubt and replaced it with something colder: he produced a slate from beneath his coat, checked it against the load, and chalked a mark. Counting. Always counting. A warehouse counts barrels. Only a crime counts witnesses.

The wagon rolled out through the gate as the guards stepped aside, and Emrys pressed deeper into the reeds and let it pass, noting the direction — south and east, toward the coast road — and noting too the driver: a civilian in a good coat, sitting beside the guard with the easy posture of a man paid to look elsewhere.

The gate clanged shut. The yard emptied. The striped lamplight went on falling through the shutters, and inside, faintly, the rustling of paper resumed, steady as a heartbeat.

* * *

He should have left then. Every instinct honed in the war said so: you have seen what you came to see, withdraw, report, return with a plan. Mara would say the same. Mara, who had survived five years at Harrowgate by knowing exactly how far to lean over the edge of a thing.

But Thomas was forty feet away behind a shutter, and Emrys had spent two years mourning a man who was close enough now to hear breathing, and there are instincts and then there is blood.

He circled the wall on his elbows and knees, giving the sluice-gate a wide berth where the ice crackled, and found what he was looking for on the building's blind northern face: a window, shuttered like the rest, but set low near the ground — a cellar light. The shutter had warped in the fen damp and stood a half-inch proud of its frame, and through the gap seeped a thread of warmth and lamplight and the smell of ink and candle tallow and, underneath it all, unmistakably, the smell of the room Emrys and Thomas had shared as boys — because no matter what was cooked or burned or spilled in it, Thomas's presence always smelled faintly of the cedar oil he rubbed into his boots.

Emrys put his eye to the gap and his lips to the shutter.

Below him, in a lamplit cellar lined with papered shelves, sat six men at trestle tables, each bent over work that needed no explanation: nibs, inkwells, blotters, stacks of blank forms bearing printed headings, and seals — brass seals, rows of them, hanging from hooks on the wall like kitchen utensils. Each man wrote steadily, ceaselessly, in different hands, and beside each man stood a guard, and Emrys understood with a lurch that the guards were not there to stop escape. They were there to make sure no writer saw another writer's work. Even in here, the silences had rules.

Thomas sat at the table nearest the window.

Up close, through a half-inch of gap, the change in him was worse than it had been in the yard. There was a tremor in his writing hand that he kept pressing down. There was a rawness at his wrists where cuffs had rubbed. But his posture was unchanged — the slight forward hunch, the left elbow propped, the habit of a boy who'd learned his letters on a kitchen table with his tongue between his teeth — and when he reached the bottom of a form he blew on the ink exactly as he had blown on his copybook lines twenty years ago while Emrys, older by four years and insufferable by four years, had flicked dried peas at his ear.

*Thomas,* Emrys breathed against the shutter. The word barely carried. He didn't dare make it carry further.

The nib kept moving. *Thomas.*

Nothing. The guard at the far end of the cellar shifted his weight. Somewhere above, floorboards creaked.

*Tom. It's Emrys. It's your brother.*

The nib stopped.

Not dramatically. It simply lifted from the paper, hovered, and did not come down, and Emrys watched the stillness move up through his brother's body like frost climbing a pane — the shoulders locking, the head bowing an inch, the whole man becoming a held breath.

Slowly, without turning his head, without looking toward the window, without giving the guard anything at all to notice, Thomas laid down his pen. And then, in a motion so small it might have been a stretch, so small that Emrys would have missed it if he had loved his brother any less, Thomas raised his left hand to the table's edge and tapped twice upon the wood.

Twice. The signal from childhood. Through the wall, through the floor, through the dark between their beds when something was wrong in the house: *two taps — yes. It's me. I hear you.*

Emrys's vision blurred. He pressed his forehead against the cold shutter and felt the warmth of the lamplit cellar on his skin and thought, absurdly, *he blows on the ink, he still blows on the ink* — and hope cut through him again, sharper than the first time, because the first time he had been watching a stranger turn. This time he had been speaking to his brother, and his brother had answered.

Thomas picked up his pen. He bent to his work. But as he drew the next stroke he let his hand drift, casually, to the edge of the form he was forging, and tilted it — just for a second, just enough — toward the window's gap.

Emrys read it upside down, in a flash, in his brother's neat and careful hand.

A death notice. A regimental death notice, on official stock, with the seal-box open beside it. And the name in the body of the text, the name of the man thereby declared dead, rendered in fresh wet ink:

*Wren, T., Corporal, 4th Foot.*

They were making Thomas forge his own death — again, or perhaps perpetually, a death renewed on some schedule like the supply runs, so that the paperwork would stay forever ahead of the truth. So that no auditor, no widow, no meddling nurse with a ledger could ever quite prove the man alive.

Thomas righted the paper. The nib resumed its steady scratch. And then, without pausing, without lifting his eyes, he tapped the table edge once more — twice — and then, deliberately, a third time.

Three taps. A signal they hadn't used since they were boys, and had sworn never to use again because of what it meant.

*Go away. Don't come back. You'll be the death of us both.*

The cellar lamp guttered. Thomas wrote on, his shoulders rigid, his head bowed, his face turned carefully away from the window and from his brother and from everything he wanted and could not afford to want. Above them both, out across the black fen, the wind got up in the reeds, and the whole marsh hissed like a held breath released.

Emrys drew back from the shutter into the dark.

His brother was alive, and his brother was afraid — not of dying in that place, Emrys realized, sinking back into the reeds with his heart hammering, but of being *found*. Of exactly what had just happened. Of a letter, a ledger, a face at a window — of hope arriving, armed and dangerous, at the worst possible hour.

He began the long retreat along the causeway with the moon-parings fading behind cloud, rehearsing already what he would say to Mara Dravenmoor, and how he would say it, and how he would answer her when she asked the question he could already see forming behind those steady grey eyes:

*Does he want to be rescued?*

Behind him, far out in the fen, the lamps of the workshop at Marlow Fen burned on until dawn — writing, writing, writing, where nobody read.
