## 10. Brothers in the Dark

The fen did not want him there. That was the first thing Emrys understood about Marlow Fen—that the land itself had been conscripted into Vane's service, every black pool and whispering reed arranged to turn back the curious. The road ended a mile out from the depot, as though the builders had lost their nerve, and after that there was only tussock ground that grabbed at his boots like hands, and water lying in the low places with a skin of ice so thin it broke at a glance, leaving dark wounds in the white.

He had dressed for invisibility: no coat with regimental buttons, nothing that would mark him as army or civilian. Just grey wool, grey scarf, and his brother's letter folded against his chest where his heart could worry at it. Three nights he had watched the depot from the ridge of a drainage embankment, learning its rhythms the way he'd once learned the rhythms of a printing house—the change of sentries at the second bell of the night watch, the kitchen smoke that rose before dawn, and the light.

That was the thing that had kept him on the cold ridge when sense told him to go. Lights burned past midnight in the long brick building, row after row of small yellow squares, and behind those windows men were working. He had counted shadows crossing the glass—eleven, twelve, once fourteen—and each shadow was a man the rolls said was dead. Each shadow had a mother, perhaps, or a wife, someone still setting an extra place at table out of a grief that had never been given a body to bury.

Tonight there was fog. It came off the water in slow banks, swallowing the moon, and Emrys thought: now or never, because courage was a currency that spent down, and his purse was nearly empty.

He went along the drainage ditch until the depot's palisade rose out of the murk on his left—a fence of driven stakes eight feet high, topped with barbed wire that glinted dully where the fog beaded it. Beyond it he could hear the sounds of the place settling into night: a door barred, boots on gravel, the flat call of a sentry answering another. Then, fainter, the sound that stopped his breath—a voice reading aloud numbers, unhurried, patient, the way a schoolmaster reads a register.

*Thomas.* Not since the glimpse through the window three nights ago—one face turning in lamplight, a face two years older than memory but carved from the same stone—had he heard his brother's voice. It was thinner than he remembered. But it was his.

Emrys moved along the ditch until the fence ran down to meet the water at the depot's western edge, where the drainage cut passed beneath in a culvert choked with reeds. He could not get through. He hadn't come to get through—not tonight. He had come to get close enough to speak, if speaking was possible, and he had reasoned it out on the ridge like a problem of typesetting: a man who works all day at a desk must sometimes be sent outside it. A man under guard is still a man, and men need air, need ash buckets carried out, need to stand somewhere and feel the sky.

He waited two hours in the reeds with the fog condensing on his eyelashes, until his feet had gone from painful to absent, and then—footsteps.

Lantern light swayed along the inside of the palisade, and a sentry's voice said, "Two minutes, Wren. Same as last time. You try my patience, you know."

"I know," said the voice Emrys had not heard in two years and would have known in ten thousand. "Thank you, Harker."

A gate creaked. A figure stepped out onto the narrow strip of frozen mud between the fence and the black water, carrying a slop bucket, of all the humble instruments of freedom. The sentry stayed visible by the gate, arms crossed, looking away with the practiced blindness of a man paid to see nothing.

Emrys rose out of the reeds slowly, hands open, the way one approaches a startled horse.

Thomas turned. The bucket hit the mud. For a moment neither of them moved, and the fog drifted between them like something alive, and Emrys saw his brother's face do everything at once—disbelief, joy, terror—and settle, terribly, on terror.

"Emrys." Barely a breath. "Christ in heaven. *Emrys.*"

"It's me." His own voice cracked on it. "It's me, Tom."

"You can't be here." Thomas crossed the distance in three strides and seized his coat with both fists, and this close Emrys could see what the lamp-glimpse had hidden: his brother had gone gaunt, cheekbones sharp as slate edges, hair greying at thirty-one, eyes rimmed red from lamplight and worse things. "You cannot *be here*. Do you understand? If anyone sees—"

"Then don't let anyone see." Emrys covered his brother's hands with his own. They were freezing; they were shaking. "Tom. Two years. I read the roll. I wrote to the regiment four times. I sat in the vestry while Father Ellery read your name from the list of the fallen and Mother—" He stopped. Swallowed. "We buried an empty box, Tom. I carried it myself. And then a letter comes in your hand saying you're alive, saying *ask at Harrowgate*, and I find a woman who keeps a book of names the army has thrown away, and every name in it leads here. To lights burning at midnight. To men doing clerical work behind a fence with barbed wire on it. What is this place?"

Thomas let go of him and stepped back, and Emrys watched his brother arrange his face into something guarded, and hated whoever had taught him to do that.

"It's a workshop," Thomas said. "Quartermaster stores, officially. Disused."

"And unofficially?"

"Forms." A bitter half-laugh. "Discharge papers. Death notices. Pension assignments. Requisitions for stores that never reach any store. Do you know how many documents an army needs to lose a man properly, Emrys? Neither did I. Now I could teach a class."

The words landed one by one, each heavier than the last. Emrys thought of the letter's coded phrases—*the work goes well, we are kept writing*—and understood at last what writing had meant. "They put you at a desk."

"They put all of us at desks." Thomas's voice flattened to something procedural, recited. "Men the rolls have lost. Men whose deaths were convenient. We write ourselves dead every week, in triplicate. There's a certain economy to it—I sign the death notice, another man files it, a third collects the pay and pension that stops flowing to any family because officially there is no family, only a deceased soldier with no dependents. It's very tidy. Colonel Vane prizes tidiness."

"Vane." The name came out of Emrys like a blade drawn. Mara had never said it outright in so many words, but she hadn't needed to; her silences around the man had shapes, and the shape was always the same. "He's here? He runs this?"

"He owns this. The regiment, the depot, the audit trail—it's all one machine, and he sits at the top of it polishing the crank." Thomas glanced toward the sentry at the gate, and lowered his voice further. "You have to go. Tonight. Now. And you must not come back."

"I'm not leaving without you."

"Yes," Thomas said softly, "you are."

* * *

For a heartbeat Emrys simply stared, because in twenty-nine years of life, first as the elder brother charged with the younger's keeping, then as brothers grown and equal, Thomas had never once told him to go away and meant it. Even at their parents' funeral, even at the worst quarrels, there had always been underneath it the current Emrys trusted more than anything else on earth: *we two against whatever comes.*

"We're getting you out," he said now. "There are people—Mara, the woman at Harrowgate, she has names, she has proof, she's been collecting it for five years. There's a way to bring the whole thing down, Tom, courts, testimony—"

"Stop." Thomas's hand came up as though to physically block the words. "Stop talking. Do you know what happens if that ledger surfaces? Do you have any idea?"

"The truth comes out. Vane answers for—"

"Vane answers with paper, Emrys. Paper and fire." Thomas grabbed his arm hard enough to bruise. "Listen to me, because I have had two years in this place to learn exactly how it works, and you've had a fortnight of listening to a kind woman read names. There are eleven men in that building tonight. Eleven. Every one of us died officially years ago—which means every one of us has no rights, no regiment, no existence. If the fraud is exposed, what do you suppose Vane does with eleven inconvenient witnesses who legally aren't alive? Does he hand us over to a court-martial and say, *here are the men I erased, kindly take their statements*?" He shook his head slowly. "There's a fen on three sides of this place and a marsh beyond it deeper than a horse. There are men in this camp whose job is not paperwork. I have seen what happens to a man who copies one page too carefully, asks one question too loudly. They found him in the outflow channel in spring, when the ice went. Officially he drowned. Unofficially—" He didn't finish it.

"So we get you out first. All of you, if we can—you, then the rest—"

"And how long do you imagine that takes? Weeks? Months? You think Vane doesn't count his ghosts every night?" Thomas's voice dropped to almost nothing, and it was worse for being quiet; shouting would have been easier to bear. "If your nurse's ledger reaches London—if anyone official so much as coughs in the direction of Marlow Fen—we are dead before the post rider clears the next parish. All of us. And you'd be next, and her too, this Dravenmoor woman. Vane is generous with the living who help him and thorough with the living who don't."

The fog moved. Somewhere across the water a bird cried out, harsh and lonely, and Emrys became aware of how far the silence carried, how thin the fence was, how many ways this meeting could end with both brothers in the ground beside their father.

"Then we go quietly," he insisted. "One man at a time. You, first, tomorrow night, same hour—there's a culvert on the west side, the reeds are thick enough—"

"*No.*"

It was not loud. It was absolute. Thomas stood in the lantern-spill with his shoulders squared, and Emrys saw with a strange, distant clarity that his brother had become a man made entirely of doors closed and locked, and that fear had mortared every one of them.

"Why?" Emrys asked. "Because they'll kill you. You've already said it. Tom—they've already killed you. It's signed, filed, in triplicate. What more can they take?"

Thomas looked at him then with an expression Emrys had never seen on him before—pity, and shame, and beneath them both something so tired it seemed older than either of them.

"You'd think that, wouldn't you," Thomas said. "But they can take more. They can take it twice." He pulled his collar aside, and in the uncertain light Emrys saw the bruising around his throat, old yellow-green fading into new purple. "I tried the culvert. Eight months ago. Before I understood the rules. They brought me back, and Captain Loxley—he keeps the discipline here—sat me down at my own desk, set fresh paper in front of me, and told me to write my death notice again. My own death notice, Emrys, in my own hand, with a date left blank. 'Whenever you need it dated,' he said. 'Whenever anyone asks about you.' That's the insurance. Every man in there has signed his own ending, and the ink is just waiting for a reason."

Emrys felt the ground tilt under him, or wanted to believe it had. "God, Tom."

"God isn't posted here." Thomas straightened his collar with hands that would not quite steady. "So hear me, and hear me as your elder in this, whatever the family order says. Drop it. Take the letter, take whatever that woman knows, and walk away. Go back to whatever you were before—a printer, a nobody, a man the ledgers have no interest in. Let the dead stay dead, Emrys. Here, the dead are the lucky ones. Nobody audits the dead. Nobody burns them."

"No."

The word was out before Emrys had decided to say it, and having said it, he found there was nothing else worth adding, so he stood in the freezing mud and let it stand there between them like a third man.

Thomas closed his eyes. When he opened them, all the doors were shut, all the locks thrown, and behind his eyes Emrys glimpsed something that frightened him more than the sentry, more than the whole armed architecture of Marlow Fen: his brother had already grieved for himself, two years deep, and grief that old does not argue. It only waits.

"If you bring that ledger anywhere near this place," Thomas said, "and any harm comes to me, I want you to carry one thing for the rest of your life. I begged you. Here, tonight, with the fog for a witness—I begged you, and you chose your conscience over your brother. Remember that when they read the sentence over whatever's left of me."

"Don't say that."

"Go home, Emrys."

"I don't have one," Emrys said, and heard his own voice break open on it. "Don't you understand? Since your name went on that list I haven't had one. Everything I've done these two years has been walking around inside a house with your chair empty. You're not asking me to drop a cause, Tom. You're asking me to bury you a second time, and I won't. I carried the box the first time. I won't do it again."

For one instant—one treacherous, human instant—Thomas's face came apart entirely, and Emrys saw the boy who had followed him through orchards and print-shops and every trouble of their shared childhood, reaching for him across the years. Emrys stepped forward—

And the sentry's boot scraped by the gate.

Thomas became a statue. Then, in a motion smooth with practice, he retrieved the slop bucket, straightened, and turned his back, and the transformation was so complete, so horribly fluent, that Emrys understood his brother had rehearsed this—not the turning away, but the dying; he had rehearsed his own dying daily for two years until it fit him like a uniform.

"Two minutes, Wren," the sentry called, bored. "In you come."

Thomas walked to the gate. At it he paused, half-turned, and spoke without looking back, so low the fog nearly took the words:

"There's a supply wagon leaves for the coast the first week of the quarter. The master book goes with it under the driver's seat. Vane's whole ledger of ghosts, in one satchel. Why I'm telling you this, God forgive me, I don't know—except that if it must happen, if you will not stop, then for the love of everything we ever were, do it *before* the wagon leaves. Because after it reaches London, there'll be nothing left to save. Not me. Not any of us."

The gate groaned, swallowed him, and clanged shut. The bolt drove home with a sound like a nail in a coffin lid, and the lantern swung away down the palisade, and Emrys stood alone in the reeds with the fog closing over the place his brother had been.

The master book. One satchel. The first week of the quarter—which gave him days, perhaps less.

He turned toward Harrowgate, toward Mara and her dangerous pages, and began the long walk out of the fen, and every step of the way Thomas's last words walked beside him, refusing to be silenced: *I begged you, and you chose your conscience over your brother.*

Behind him, row upon row, the yellow windows of the workshop still burned.
