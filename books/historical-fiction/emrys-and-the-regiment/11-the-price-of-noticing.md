## 11. The Price of Noticing

The fever came for Surgeon Brandt on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday he was dead.

Mara would learn later the precise hour—between the third bell of the morning watch and the fourth, in the narrow cot behind the dispensary where he had slept for eleven years—but she did not need a witness to tell her what had happened. She knew it the way one knows weather before it arrives, the way she had known, standing in his doorway the night before with his promise still warm in her ears, that something had already begun to go wrong in the world.

He had promised. That was the unbearable arithmetic of it. He had stood at his little window with his back to her, looking out at the frost-silvered burial ground, and he had said, *I'll speak for you, girl. I'm too old to be frightened properly anymore.* He had even smiled when he said it—a dry, crooked smile, the smile of a man handing over a weight he'd carried too long. *You'll need testimony from someone who was here before you were. Someone they can't call a liar or a sentimentalist. I watched Vane's first muster come in short four men and long four purses. I counted. Counting is what I do.*

She had left his room at midnight. At six, Tilly knocked at the ward door with her apron twisted in her fists and her face the color of tallow, and said, *Miss Dravenmoor—the surgeon—he won't wake.*

* * *

By noon the ward had settled into its version of grief, which was efficiency: sheets changed, instruments boiled, the body laid out with its hands folded and a shilling on each eye because Brandt had been specific about wanting coins over his eyes—"I've been robbed enough in life; I'll not start in death." The other surgeons from the annex came through in ones and twos, said their measured words, and left. An army hospital could not afford to stop for anyone, not even for the man who had kept it from becoming a charnel house. Death was the currency here; one more withdrawal caused no stir.

But Mara stirred. Mara could not stop stirring.

She stood at the foot of his cot while the attendants worked around her, and she made herself look at him properly, because she owed him that. His face in death was composed, almost amused, as though the fever were a poor joke he'd declined to laugh at. His hands were folded wrong—Brandt had never folded his hands in his life; they were always moving, always counting or cutting or gesturing at some incompetence—and she reached down without thinking and rearranged them, and the coldness of his fingers went through her like a lance.

Fever, they said. The putrid fever, the one that moved through camps like smoke through a keyhole. It had taken three orderlies in October. It took the strong and the weak alike, without preference, without pattern.

Without pattern. She turned the words over as she turned everything now, examining them the way she examined regimental rolls, looking for what was omitted. Three orderlies in October, all of them young, all of them stricken in the wards, in the ordinary course of their work. And Brandt, sixty-one years old, careful as a man defusing ordnance, who had not entered a fever ward unmasked since spring, who had lectured her a dozen times on miasma and contagion until she could recite it in her sleep—that man, dying in twelve hours, in his own cot, behind his own locked door, the very night after he promised to testify against Colonel Aldous Vane.

She heard herself asking the head attendant whether the surgeon had eaten anything unusual. She heard the man's answer—nothing out of the way, same broth as always—and she heard something else underneath the answer, something flat and rehearsed in it, and she understood that she was not the only person in Harrowgate who knew how to notice, and also how to stop noticing when noticing became dangerous.

That was the shape of it, then. That was the lesson written in the dead man's folded hands.

Vane had not sent soldiers in the night. There had been no violence anyone could name, no blood, no broken door. There had only been a fever, arriving with convenient timing, and afterward there would be a report, and the report would say what fevers always say, which is nothing, which is everything, which is *no one is to blame, these things happen, go back to your work.*

He killed with paperwork made flesh. He erased a living man the same way he erased names—with a document, a signature, an official hand smoothing official ink. And no one would ever call it murder because murder required a body with wounds, and this body was whole, and whole bodies tell the cleanest lies.

Mara looked down at Brandt and felt something in her chest crack—not break, she noted distantly, but crack, the way ice cracks on a pond: a single line of failure running through what had held for five years. She had built that ice carefully. She had learned, name by stolen name, boy by boy, to feel things at a distance, to fold her grief small and sew it into linings where it could not interfere with function. Weeping accomplished nothing. Weeping got people followed home.

But this—this was not grief alone. Grief was what she had brought to Harrowgate at nineteen, after her brother's name went into the dispatches and her mother's mind went into silence. What she felt now standing over Brandt was older and colder than grief.

It was accounting.

*You'll need testimony,* he'd said. Now she would have to be her own witness. Now every note in her ledger had lost its second voice, and the silence where his corroboration should have been was the loudest thing in the room.

Tilly appeared at her elbow, silent as a mouse in snow, and pressed a hand briefly against Mara's arm—the gesture of someone steadying another on a swaying deck.

"He left something," Tilly whispered. "For you. Under his mattress. I saw him put it there last week and I thought—well, I didn't think, miss. Old men hide things."

* * *

It was his spare spectacles, a stub of pencil, and a single sheet of paper folded eight times, covered on both sides in his cramped physician's hand. Not a testimony. A list.

Fourteen entries. Dates, muster numbers, sums. Four men short here, forty pounds drawn there, a payment authorized to a *Mr. Quill, agent* on dates that matched, entry for entry, the pattern she had spent five years mapping alone in candlelight. He had known more than he ever said. He had been counting, all this time, exactly as he promised—counting in advance, leaving the sum where it would survive him.

And at the bottom of the page, in letters pressed so hard into the paper they showed through to the reverse, one line:

*M. — If anything happens to me, it happened BECAUSE of this paper. Do not let them make his death mean nothing. Do not let them make YOURS.*

She read it four times. Then she folded it along his creases, and she stood in the dispensary surrounded by his jars and his labels and his careful, numbered world, and she allowed herself thirty seconds of weeping—thirty seconds, timed by the case clock on the shelf, because method was all she had left—and then she washed her face in the basin and went back to work.

There was so much work.

* * *

She copied the ledger that night, and the two nights after, working by the light of a shielded candle in the linen room between the hours when the night nurses completed their rounds. It was slow, brutal labor. The original ledger—sewn into the lining of her grey apron these five years, transferred apron to apron as fabric wore through—was written in her tightest hand, columns of names and dates and last-known details packed edge to edge like sardines. Copying meant transcribing two hundred and eleven names without error while listening for footsteps, while her eyes burned, while Brandt's folded hands floated at the edge of her vision every time she blinked.

Two hundred and eleven men. Two hundred and eleven mothers, wives, sons. She wrote them out onto loose folios purchased piecemeal from different stationers so no single purchase would draw attention, in three different inks so no single hand would be recognized, disguised slightly in each—a loop altered here, a slant changed there—so that if any one page were seized, it could not be traced wholly to Mara Dravenmoor, nurse, Harrowgate.

The copy was for sending. She had understood this since chapter one of this terrible book, but Brandt's death converted understanding into urgency. A ledger in one place could be burned. A ledger in one body could be buried. But truth distributed—truth scattered like seed across counties and ships and strangers' hands—truth like that could not be gathered up again by any colonel's tidy hand.

The problem was the original.

She could not send it; it was sewn into her clothing and she needed the copy work done first. She could not burn it; the thought did not even present itself for consideration. She could not keep it on her person forever, because she now understood, with the clarity of a woman standing beside a fresh grave, that Vane's reach extended into cots and kettles and the private hours of the night. If they searched her—and after Brandt, they would search her eventually—they would find the apron. They would strip the lining. They would find two hundred and eleven names and the woman who kept them, and then there would be a fever, and a report, and a smooth stone rolled smoothly into place.

The original needed to go somewhere no searcher would ever think to look. Somewhere beyond Vane's jurisdiction. Somewhere already sealed, already mourned, already *finished*—because no one, not even Aldous Vane, disturbed the dead.

It came to her in the burial ground, of all places, on the afternoon she walked out to help lay the wreath on Brandt's mound. Frost had hardened the earth into iron. The graves ran in rows, military in their own right, each marked with a wooden board and a painted number because proper stones cost money the army preferred to spend elsewhere. Numbers, not names. Even here, even in death, the erasure continued. She had noticed it years ago and stopped noticing; now she stood among the boards and felt her whole body reject the not-noticing, felt it like a splinter working toward the heart.

Chaplain's assistant Wilcox was nailing shut a coffin near the fence. A sergeant major, dead of his wounds, bound for England—home burial, family of some consequence, the family having paid for passage and lead lining. Such shipments went out twice a month in winter, when the ground permitted transport: coffins crated, manifested, sealed with lead customs stamps, hauled under bill of lading to the coast and loaded last upon the supply ships. Nobody opened them. Nobody *dared* open them. A coffin crossing the sea under seal was the most sacrosanct object in the entire apparatus of the army's logistics. Contraband was hidden in flour barrels and boot heels. No one hid contraband in the dead.

Mara watched Wilcox drive the last nail and thought: *the most respected thing in the world is a box no one will open.*

* * *

The next coffin bound for England belonged to Private Owen Fitch, twenty-three, died of gangrene on a Thursday, no family found. The army would ship him anyway—regulation required remains of the ranks be returned when transport allowed—and no one at the docks would count a coffin with no mourner attached. Mara volunteered for the preparation. It was the kind of offer that raised no suspicion; nurses prepared bodies constantly, and Mara had a reputation for doing the grim work without complaint, which was itself a kind of camouflage. People assumed a woman comfortable with corpses must be simple, or hardened, or both. They never assumed she was paying attention.

She waited until the ward was asleep. Then, by the light of a single candle, she unpicked the lining of her apron with Brandt's own dissecting scissors, stitch by stitch, and drew out the ledger.

It was smaller than five years of her life. That was her first absurd thought—a hand-span of oiled cloth, wrapped around a packet of papers thin as a hymnal, holding two hundred and eleven souls. Her fingers shook. Not with fear. With the vertigo of relinquishment: for five years the ledger had been part of her body's daily geography, its corners pressing her ribs when she bent over beds, its weight swinging against her hip as she walked the wards. To set it on the table between herself and the candle was to set down a limb.

She wrapped it in oilcloth. She wrapped the oilcloth in waxed canvas. She tied it with tarred cord, sealing the knots, and then—on impulse, on ceremony—she tucked inside the final wrapping one of Brandt's coins, the shilling from his eye that Tilly had given her because "he'd have wanted it doing some good." Let him ride with it, she thought. Let the old counter keep counting.

Private Fitch lay in his coffin dressed in a borrowed shirt, his hands arranged, his face composed. Mara worked steadily and gently around him, and spoke to him as she worked, because she had always spoken to the dead—it was a habit from her first year, from the boy whose name started everything—and because it seemed suddenly, urgently important that Fitch understand what he was carrying, and carry it well.

"You're going home, Owen," she told him. "Both of you are. There's a lady named Mrs. Hester Cobb in Devon who's been waiting five years to hear why her son Elias never writes, and everything she needs is in this box, and you're going to deliver it for me, and I will see you both remembered if it's the last thing I do on God's earth."

She fitted the ledger-wrapped bundle into the false bottom of the coffin—in the space between the inner box and the outer shell that undertakers left for packing straw, that no customs man in the history of armies had ever troubled to search. She packed straw around it. She closed the inner lid. She drove the nails herself, taking care with each one, and when the last was set she rested her palm flat on the wood and kept it there for a long moment, the way she had rested her hand on Brandt's folded fingers.

Then she painted the lead seal herself, pressing the customs stamp into soft metal, watching the official insignia bloom across the seam—official ink, official seals, the same flawless machinery Vane used to forge discharges and death notices. It occurred to her, with a cold clarity that felt almost like laughter, that she was using his own weapon against him: the authority of the untouched document, the power of the thing no one checks because no one believes it could be false. Let the system carry its own destruction. Let the bureaucracy of erasure haul two hundred and eleven resurrections across the sea.

Wilcox and an orderly carried the coffin out at dawn and crated it, and the manifest went into the quartermaster's file, and the crate went onto the wagon, and the wagon went toward the coast, and Mara stood in the yard with the frost smoking off the burial ground behind her and watched it go until the road bent and took it.

She had done the mathematics of risk a dozen times in the night. If the coffin were somehow opened—if the bundle rotted or drowned or was stolen—the original was gone, and the copy was all, and the copy was vulnerable in ways she had not yet solved. If the coffin arrived, the truth was beyond Vane's reach within the month, sitting in England like a loaded pistol waiting for a hand.

But there was the flaw in her plan, and she saw it plainly as the wagon disappeared: a pistol in England still needed a hand. Pages sent blindly might sit in a solicitor's office for years. They might reach the War Office and die in a drawer, filed by some clerk whose imagination couldn't stretch past the margins. Paper alone had never once, in five years, saved a single man. Paper needed a voice. Paper needed someone standing in a room where powerful men sat, saying the names aloud.

Brandt had been going to be that voice. Brandt was in the ground.

Which meant the pages could not simply be *sent*. They had to be delivered—carried by someone, placed in a specific hand, connected to a specific moment when the world was already watching. And she knew, with a certainty that settled into her bones like the frost settling into the yard, that the moment was coming. Vane was burning evidence now—records rooms, inconvenient witnesses, whole regiments' histories. A man burning that fast was a man who feared something arriving soon. An inspection. An audit. Some crack in the wall through which light might come.

When that crack opened, her copy had to be ready. Not in a drawer. In the room.

Behind her, boots on frozen gravel. She turned, and her heart performed a single hard knock against her ribs.

Emrys came across the yard fast, coat collar up, breath steaming, and there was something in his stride she hadn't seen before—not the gaunt wandering of the man who'd collapsed at her gate, but direction, velocity, a man with a destination. He stopped close, glanced once at the empty road, once at the burial ground, and lowered his voice.

"Mara. I found the workshop. I found the fen." His eyes were bright and terrible. "And I found my brother. Thomas is alive."

The yard seemed to tilt. Two hundred and eleven names shifted in her chest like ballast in a storm—one of them, *Wren, T., Corp.*, rising suddenly from ink to flesh.

"Alive," she repeated. "Then everything changes. Emrys—if he can testify, if the men at that workshop can testify—"

"They can," he said. "And Vane doesn't know yet that we know. Which means we have days, maybe. Mara—I need your ledger. All of it. Before he burns that workshop and every man inside it."

Her hand went, without permission, to the flat place at her hip where five years of weight had lived and no longer did.

"Emrys," she said slowly, "how do you feel about a journey to the coast?"
