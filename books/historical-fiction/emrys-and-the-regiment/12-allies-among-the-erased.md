## 12. Allies Among the Erased

The name stitched into the collar of my borrowed coat read *Hollis*. It was not mine. It had belonged, I suspected, to a man who no longer needed it, and the coat still carried the ghost of his shoulders—narrower than mine, a stoop I could not imitate. Three days out of Harrowgate, following the second of Thomas's coded phrases—*ask at the mill with two wheels for the man who grinds nothing*—I had learned that a false name is a garment you wear until it fits, or until it kills you.

The mill stood on the River Larken, its second wheel dry-rotted in its frame, turning nothing. I asked for the man who ground nothing and watched the miller's boy go white, watch him vanish inside, watch the door stay shut for so long that I nearly turned back into the snow.

When it opened again, it opened on a woman.

She was perhaps fifty, with flour worked so deeply into the lines of her hands that she seemed dusted with frost year-round. She looked at me the way one looks at weather—assessing what damage might come.

"You're not regimental," she said.

"No."

"You're not Vane's."

"I don't know who that is," I said, which was a lie, and she heard it, because her eyes narrowed and something behind them shifted—a door closing, then, slowly, opening again by a crack.

"Everyone knows who that is," she said. "Come in before someone sees you standing there looking like what you are."

*And what am I?* I wanted to ask. A brother of a dead man who wasn't dead. A keeper of half a truth. But I followed her in, and the mill swallowed me the way the fen had swallowed Thomas's workshop—into a world where everything ran on things unsaid.

* * *

There were six of them in the mill's back room, and they did not look like conspirators. They looked like men tired to the bone: a carter, a lame fiddler, two brothers with the same crooked nose, an old sergeant with a cough that rattled like gravel in a tin cup, and a young one—no older than twenty—who never stopped watching the window. The woman was Mrs. Sorrel; her husband had been a quartermaster-sergeant in Vane's column, officially killed at Salzburg Cross, unofficially alive and sending money home through channels so convoluted that even he seemed only partly sure how it arrived.

They sat me at the table and did not offer food, which was its own kind of answer.

"Thomas Wren," I said. "Corporal. Listed dead after—" 

"We know the rolls better than the rolls know themselves." The old sergeant said it without heat, a fact laid down like a stone. "Wren. Tall lad, ink-stained fingers, wrote a fair copperplate. He's at the Fen."

My heart struck my ribs. "You've seen him."

"Seen him carted in twice a year, when they move the paper stock." He coughed, long and wet, and recovered. "That doesn't mean we'll help you."

"Why not?"

"Because you walked here asking questions." The carter spoke for the first time, flat as a road. "Men who walk in asking questions get walked out asking them too, later, in a different room, with different people listening. We've buried friends twice—once on paper, once in the ground. You want us to dig a third grave?"

"Then why let me in at all?"

Mrs. Sorrel answered, wiping her hands on her apron though there was nothing on it. "Because of the nurse."

The room changed. Not dramatically—the young one's head came up, the fiddler's hand stilled on his knee—but I felt the whole room lean toward her words like grass toward wind.

"Mara Dravenmoor," she said. "She keeps a book. Names. Every man the rolls have eaten, she writes down. Rank, company, last seen, mother's name if she could get it." Mrs. Sorrel's voice caught slightly on that last part. "My husband is in her book. Number thirty-one. She got his mother's name from a letter he'd sent through me." She looked at me, and for a moment the miller's hardness fell away entirely, and underneath was just a wife who had spent four years not knowing whether she was a widow. "You came from her. Nobody walks out of Harrowgate carrying her trust unless they've earned it twice over."

"She didn't send me," I said carefully. "We found each other."

"That's how she works," said the old sergeant, almost smiling. "Found each other. Half the ghosts in this county have *found* Mara Dravenmoor without either party admitting it beforehand."

The two brothers exchanged a look over the table. The elder—Harker, I would learn, once a lance-corporal, now legally nobody—spoke slowly, weighing every word. "If the book surfaces, Vane burns everything. Workshop first, witnesses second. My brother and I are witnesses."

"If the book never surfaces," I said, "Vane wins forever, and your brother stays a ghost, and Mrs. Sorrel stays a widow, and every pension those men earned goes into a colonel's pocket until he dies comfortable in some garrison town." I leaned forward. "I've come to take the workshop's schedule and the location of Vane's master book. That's why I'm here. I won't pretend otherwise. But I'm not asking you to fight. I'm asking you to stop pretending to be dead when you aren't."

Silence, except the wheel groaning against the river and the young one's boot tapping, tapping against the table leg.

It was the young one who broke. He couldn't have been twenty; his beard grew in patches and his hands hadn't settled yet. "My mum gets letters," he said, to no one, to the window. "Signed with my own name. Written by another man's hand. She thinks—" His voice cracked and he hated it, I could see him hating it. "She thinks I write them."

Nobody moved. Then Mrs. Sorrel put her flour-white hand over his fist, and the gesture decided whatever remained to be decided.

* * *

They talked through the night, in fragments, each man giving up his piece the way a miser gives up coins—one at a time, wincing.

The workshop at Marlow Fen ran on the quarter-day. Every three months, a supply wagon came up from the depot at Ashcombe: new paper, ink, seals, blank discharge forms, and rations for the workers. It arrived at dusk and left before dawn, driven by two men who were paid too well to be curious. On that single night, the inner gate—the one facing the water, away from the road—was unbarred for the loading, and the guard rotated thin, because Vane's men believed, with the confidence of men who have never been robbed, that no one knew what the fen building held.

"The master book," Harker said, "is Vane's private ledger. Not the regimental one—the real one. What he's taken, from whom, what he's paid, what he's promised. It lives in a locked drawer in the clerk's annex, a small brick room attached to the workshop's north wall. Iron-banded. Two keys: one Vane carries, one the head clerk keeps on a cord round his neck. The clerk's name is Prosser. He drinks. He sleeps at the annex on quarter-night because he's afraid of the workers."

"Afraid of them?" I thought of Thomas's face turning toward the fence line, of the hollowed look of men doing clerical labor under guard. "They outnumber the guards ten to one."

"Do they?" The old sergeant's laugh had no floor under it. "Son, they outnumber the guards ten to one every night of their lives, and they're still there. Because their names are ash and their families are hostages to silence. Vane doesn't need many guards. He has the roll itself. A man who's officially dead can't testify, can't claim wages, can't marry, can't be tried—can't exist. Step out of that fence and you step off the face of the earth. That's his prison wall. Not timber. Paper."

The fiddler, who had said nothing all evening, lifted his head. "You want to break men out of a paper prison. Who takes them in? Where does a dead man go after he stops being useful to you?" His bow hand was ruined—two fingers gone at the second knuckle—and he flexed it as he spoke. "You'll carry them out into the snow and they'll freeze to death being free. Ask me how I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because I did it to myself, badly, in February, and lost these to the cold instead of the frost of fear." He held up the hand. "But I lived. Because Mrs. Sorrel fed me in this room for six weeks and never once asked me to be grateful loudly enough for the neighbors to hear." He looked around the table, and something passed across the faces there—not warmth exactly, but recognition, the grim comfort of survivors taking attendance. "That's what we are, friend. A society for the prevention of gratitude in public. If your nurse's book ever sees print, half of England will learn what we are. And the other half will learn what they allowed."

* * *

Near dawn, they gave me the last thing, and it was harder won than the schedule or the drawer.

"There are more of us," Harker said quietly, when the others had drifted to pallets along the walls and only we two remained by the banked fire. "Not here—scattered. Kent. A cooper outside Norwich. Two on the coast who work the smuggling runs and hear everything that moves between Vane and London. We call nothing a network. Networks have heads, and heads get cut off. We're just... men who know other men." He studied me across the embers. "You'll need them, when this goes however it goes. If it fails, they'll need warning. If it succeeds—" He paused, and I saw him wrestle with something. "If it succeeds, they'll need to be *named*. Restored. And restoration requires proof, and proof requires testimony, and testimony requires a court willing to hear dead men speak."

"It will hear," I said. "One way or another."

"You sound very certain for a man wearing a dead stranger's coat."

I looked down at the collar, at the stitching that read *Hollis*, and felt the full strangeness of my situation settle on me like the coat itself—heavier than it looked, warm only because someone else had suffered first. "My brother is in that workshop writing his own death notice," I said. "Certainty is all I've brought with me. Everything else I'm borrowing."

Harker nodded slowly, as if I'd passed some examination whose questions had never been spoken aloud. Then he reached into his shirt and drew out a small square of oilcloth, unwrapped it, and set on the table a stub of pencil worn to the length of a thumbnail.

"Quarter-night falls in nine days," he said. "The wagon comes at dusk. The water gate stands open two hours, no more. Prosser sleeps drunk by midnight." He pushed the pencil toward me. "Write down what you need to remember. Then burn the paper and keep it here." He touched his own temple. "That's how we've survived. Everything important lives where Vane can't search."

I wrote the times, the gate, the annex, the two keys. I burned the paper in the stove and watched the schedule curl and brighten and go to nothing, and understood, with a strange lurch, that I had just done what the erased do daily—converted truth into memory, made the mind the only archive left.

* * *

I slept three hours on a pallet between the fiddler and the young one, wedged like cargo among strangers, and woke once in the dark to find the young one awake, staring at the ceiling beams.

"Do you think she'll really read the names?" he whispered. "Out loud? Somewhere? All of them?"

"I think she'll die before she lets them stay silent."

He was quiet a while. "Mine's near the end of her book, my mum told me to say, if I ever—" He stopped, embarrassed. "Number sixty-two. If you see her again. Tell her number sixty-two has kept his head down like he was told."

"I'll tell her myself," I said. "When this is over. Both of us."

In the morning they fed me properly at last—bread and dripping, tea that was mostly hot water and hope—and Mrs. Sorrel packed the rest into a cloth and pushed it into my arms without ceremony, as if feeding a man were simply what happened to coats that walked in off the river road.

At the door, Harker shook my hand once, hard. "Nine days. Water gate. Don't be clever, don't be brave, don't be early." He hesitated, then added, low: "And Wren—if you get your brother out—tell him Salzburg Cross sends its regards. He'll understand. He forged enough of our discharges to know the signature."

The snow had stopped in the night. The road east lay white and unmarked in both directions, waiting for whichever of us would write on it first.

I turned toward Marlow Fen with a schedule in my skull, a master book's hiding place in my keeping, and—for the first time since Thomas's letter had cracked my life open on the Harrowgate cobbles—something that was not quite hope and far more durable: the knowledge that the dead of Vane's invention were not scattered and afraid, as I had imagined, but gathered, patient, and counting the same nine days I was.

Behind me, the mill's dead wheel groaned once against the current and went on turning nothing, faithfully, like a sentry the enemy had forgotten to post.

Ahead, past the white fields, the fen waited—and somewhere in it, a lamp already burned over a desk where my brother bent, unknowing, over paper that would soon be worth more than any of us could yet imagine.
