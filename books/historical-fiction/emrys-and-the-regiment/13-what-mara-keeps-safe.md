## 13. What Mara Keeps Safe

The snow came down all day on Harrowgate like ash from some distant burning, and Mara Dravenmoor spent it the way she spent every day—moving between cots with her hands steady and her mind counting.

Not counting the wounded, though there were forty-one of them now, and two she did not expect to see dawn. She was counting days. Eleven since Brandt's death. Nine since she had sewn the copied pages into the lining of a coffin bound for Dover. Eight since she had begun sleeping in her boots.

She had learned in five years at Harrowgate that danger announced itself in small ways: a clerk who lingered too long over a supply requisition; an orderly who asked where she kept her private correspondence; the particular quality of silence that fell when she entered a room where men had been talking about her. The hospital had gone quiet around her twice this week. Both times, Colonel Vane's aide, Lieutenant Pell, had been within earshot.

So when Tilly came to her at the end of the afternoon shift, breathless, cheeks bright with cold and something else—something that looked like fear wearing urgency as a disguise—Mara was already reaching for the apron.

"He's coming," Tilly said. "Lieutenant Pell. He asked Sister Agnes for the key to the nurses' quarters. Said he'd been sent to check for—" She swallowed. "For vermin. In the bedding."

"Vermin." Mara almost smiled. It was such a tidy word for what Vane was hunting. "How long?"

"Agnes is making him wait while she finds the key. She's looking very slowly." Tilly's chin came up, defiant. "She doesn't like him either."

Mara did not have time to be moved by that. She had perhaps four minutes, and she had rehearsed this moment so many times—in the dark, on night shifts, walking the frozen path to the latrine—that her body knew the sequence before her mind gave the order.

* * *

Her quarters were a cell eight feet by ten: a cot, a trunk, a washstand, a shelf of medical texts, and a window that overlooked the laundry yard. To any searcher, it would take three minutes to turn inside out. The trick was not hiding things. The trick was deciding, in advance, what you were willing to lose.

Mara knelt at the trunk and lifted out the false bottom she had built herself, thin cedar over the true floor, fitted so precisely that even she sometimes forgot it was there. From beneath it she took the packet she had prepared a month ago, when Brandt died and she understood that the questions would come to her door eventually.

It was a bundle of papers tied with plain string. Letters from her sister in York, real ones, worn soft at the folds. A page of arithmetic—hospital accounts she kept honestly, because honesty made the best camouflage. And beneath those, folded small, three sheets of foolscap covered in her own cramped hand: notes on patients, dosages, observations. Innocuous. Boring. The kind of papers a careful woman might keep and a suspicious man might waste an hour reading.

But she could not simply leave them in the trunk. A searcher who found nothing searched harder. A searcher who found something hidden felt clever, and a clever-feeling man stopped digging.

She thought of Brandt telling her once, years ago, over bad sherry in his cluttered office: *A lie is a load-bearing wall, Miss Dravenmoor. Build it well or don't build it at all.*

She tucked the decoy bundle under the loose brick behind the washstand—the one hiding place obvious enough that a diligent lieutenant would find it and feel triumphant—and then she took up the ledger.

The real one. The true one. Five years of names in her smallest hand, sewn into the lining of her spare apron, the apron that hung among six identical aprons on the peg behind her door. She had made them all alike on purpose, bought the same bolt of linen, stained each one at the hem with the same iodine marks. Let him search. Let him hold every apron she owned to the light. He would find nothing but a nurse's uniform, seven times over.

Except—

Her hands stopped. There was a flaw, and she saw it now with the terrible clarity that comes in the last minutes before disaster. The seventh apron was heavier. Not much. An ounce, perhaps two. But a man who handled linen for a living—a quartermaster's man, a soldier who had carried packs across Spain—might feel it. Might notice how it hung.

She could not re-sew it now. No time. She could not move it; there was nowhere better, and moving it risked everything.

Footsteps in the corridor. Keys jangling. Sister Agnes's voice, pitched high with performed reluctance: "I'm sure there's nothing in there worth a gentleman's time, Lieutenant—"

Mara pulled the false bottom back into place, stacked the linens, straightened the trunk, and stood just as boots sounded outside her door.

* * *

Lieutenant Pell was younger than she remembered—young enough that his mustache looked borrowed—and he had the particular stiffness of a man performing authority he did not yet own. He stood in her doorway holding Sister Agnes's key ring like a scepter, and behind him, uninvited, hovered Tilly.

"I'll need you to step out, Miss Dravenmoor," Pell said. "Routine inspection. There's been... concern about the condition of the quarters. Vermin."

"Of course." She stepped past him into the corridor, folding her hands. Beside her, Tilly did the same, and Mara felt the girl's shoulder press against hers—not accidentally. Tilly had planted herself there like a second witness, and Mara understood suddenly what the girl intended: to stay. To watch. To make sure nothing happened to her that no one could later describe.

"You needn't hover, Nurse—" Pell began.

"Matron's orders," Tilly said sweetly. "No gentleman enters a nurse's room without a female attendant present. For propriety." She smiled the smile she used on dying men, gentle and immovable. "I'm sure you understand, Lieutenant. We're very strict here about propriety."

Pell's jaw worked. He wanted to object and could not find grounds that would survive being repeated aloud. He went in.

What followed was twenty minutes that Mara experienced entirely through sound, standing in the corridor with her heart beating slow and enormous, like a drum in deep water. The trunk lid. The scrape of it across boards. Drawers opened and closed. The washstand shifted—there, the brick, the little gasp of triumph she imagined she could hear even through the wall. Pages turning. Turning. Turning.

Then a long silence.

Then: "Miss Dravenmoor. What are these?"

She entered the room with the expression she wore for difficult families—sympathetic, unhurried, faintly weary. Pell stood holding her decoy letters, her sister's handwriting, her honest arithmetic, her three dull pages of clinical notes. He held them the way a man holds a fish he has caught and cannot name.

"My personal papers," she said. "Correspondence, accounts, case notes. I keep records of my patients' treatments. It's how I learn." She let a note of gentle puzzlement enter her voice. "Is there something improper in them? If so, I'd genuinely like to know. I should hate to think I'd written anything careless."

Pell flipped through the pages again, visibly deflating. Dosages. Poultice recipes. A letter about her sister's new baby. Nothing. Nothing dressed as something, and he could not tell the difference, because he had never learned—as she had learned over five years of watching men die and names vanish—that the most convincing secret is the one that looks exactly like an ordinary life.

"These are fine," he said stiffly, repacking them. "I'm required to inspect all personal effects. There have been irregularities in the regimental stores. Theft." His eyes came up and held hers, and for one moment the performance dropped and she saw the actual man underneath: young, ambitious, frightened of his colonel, doing a job he half-understood for reasons he had never been told. "You'll forgive the intrusion."

"Thieves should worry us all," Mara agreed pleasantly. "Though I confess I can't imagine what anyone would steal from me. Linen and laudanum."

"No," Pell said. "I don't suppose you keep anything worth stealing."

He returned the papers to the brick—she watched him do it, watched him replace it exactly as he'd found it, proud of his discovery, certain he had seen the whole of her—and then he made his final circuit. Cot. Shelf. Window. And then, God help her, the pegs behind the door.

He lifted the first apron. Turned it over. Dropped it on the cot.

The second. The third.

Tilly's hand found Mara's sleeve. Mara did not look at her. She watched Pell's hands—big, blunt, impatient hands—working through the aprons like a farmer sorting grain, and she calculated with a strange floating calm: *four, five, six.* He was getting bored. She could see boredom arriving in his shoulders, in the way he was already glancing toward the door. Six aprons of identical linen, identical stains, and a cold room, and a colonel waiting, and a young man's evening calling.

He picked up the seventh.

He weighed it. Mara saw him weigh it—saw the fractional pause, the tiny frown, the moment where the extra ounce of paper-and-thread met a soldier's instinct for weight. His thumb pressed the pocket seam.

"Miss Dravenmoor," he said slowly, "why does this one—"

"It's mended," Tilly said.

Her voice came out clear and easy, and Mara nearly turned to stare at her. The girl stepped forward, plucked the apron from Pell's hands with the brisk entitlement of seniority she absolutely did not possess, and held it up to the lamp. "See? Torn at the pocket last week, during the amputation on Ward Two. Blood everywhere. I stitched it myself." She traced the seam with one finger—over the exact spot where, beneath the linen, five years of stolen names lay flat against the fabric. "My stitching's ugly, I know. Matron says I sew like a farrier shoes a horse. But we're short of aprons, Lieutenant, and Miss Dravenmoor works double shifts, so she wears the mended one and I wear the old ones. You're welcome to count our aprons against the stores inventory if you like. I'll fetch the book."

The offer was perfect. It was generous, verifiable, tedious. Pell looked at the apron, at Tilly's open, guileless face, at the corridor beyond where his colonel waited and his supper grew cold, and Mara watched him choose comfort over diligence, the way men had chosen it in front of her for five years, the way entire armies chose it, the way the world was mostly built.

"No need," he said. He set the apron down. "That'll be all, ladies."

He locked the door behind himself with ceremony, returned the keys to Sister Agnes with a word of thanks, and his boots receded down the corridor toward the officers' wing, unhurried, satisfied, carrying nothing.

* * *

They did not speak until full dark. Mara found Tilly in the linen store, folding bandages with more force than the task required, and closed the door between them and the ward.

"You lied to an officer of the Crown," Mara said quietly.

"I covered a torn pocket." Tilly didn't look up. Her hands kept folding, creasing each bandage into a knife-edge. "There's a difference, and if anyone ever asks, that's the difference, and I'll say it till they tire of hearing it."

"Tilly." Mara reached out and stilled the girl's hands. They were trembling now that there was nothing left to fold. "Do you understand what you did? If he had looked under that stitching—if he had cut the seam—"

"Then he'd have found names." Tilly raised her chin, and in the lamplight her eyes were wet and furious at once. "My brother's name is in that apron. Private Daniel Trench, Seventh Foot, listed dead at Salamanca. My mother got a letter saying he'd deserted. Deserted! Danny couldn't desert his dinner." Her voice cracked and she hated it, Mara could see her hating it, and she pressed on anyway. "I've known what you carry for a year, miss. Since the night you wrote out his name and I saw you do it. I've been waiting for you to ask me to help. You never ask anyone for anything, and it's the most infuriating thing about you."

Mara stood very still. Five years of solitary method, of rules, of *silences that keep people safe*—and all that time, one of her own nurses had known, had watched, had waited, had chosen her side without being asked, without being told the cost.

"The cost," Mara said at last, "is what happened to Surgeon Brandt."

"I know." Tilly wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, briskly, the way she did everything. "And I know you hid copies somewhere clever, and I know you sleep in your boots, and I know you've decided you'll hang alone rather than spend anyone else. Well, I won't have it." She took a breath. "Danny is alive somewhere—I know he is, the same way you know it, the money comes, someone's keeping the men alive to keep writing—and if there's a chance your book brings him home, then I'm in it. Not beside it. *In* it. So the next time you need a lie told, miss, you'll tell me first. That's the price. That's what I want."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere down the ward a man moaned in his fever, and the wind worried at the shutters, and Mara Dravenmoor—who had built her whole survival on trusting no one, on carrying everything alone, on the doctrine that every shared secret was a blade pointed at two hearts instead of one—felt the doctrine crack clean through.

Silence could bury, she had always believed. She had built her ledger in silence, guarded it in silence, prepared to die for it in silence.

But silence had also just saved her. Tilly's smooth lie, Sister Agnes's slow search for the key, the whole quiet conspiracy of women who saw what was done to their men and had learned to answer it without raising their voices. Silence was not only the weapon used against the erased. It could be armor, worn together.

"All right," Mara said. "From tonight, you know everything."

Tilly nodded once, soldierly, and then ruined her own composure by throwing her arms around Mara and holding on hard, and Mara—after a moment's astonishment—held on back.

* * *

Later, alone, Mara sat on her cot in the dark and did not sleep. Through the window, far off across the white fields, she could see a smudge of light where no light should be—at Marlow Fen, she was nearly certain, though the fen was miles off and the light only a rumor against the clouds. Emrys was out there tonight. She had watched him go at dusk, wrapped in a laborer's coat, carrying Thomas Wren's letter and a plan sketched on slate, and she had not said *be careful*, because the words were too small for what she meant.

Pell would report to Vane by morning: *nothing found, some letters, a mendéd apron, a tiresome nurse.* Vane would read the report and be reassured, or pretend to be. But Vane had offered her passage home nine days ago, which meant he already suspected, which meant the next search would not be conducted by a bored young lieutenant with a key ring. It would come differently. Quietly. Perhaps at night. Perhaps with men who were not required to explain themselves.

She put her hand against the apron hanging on its peg, feeling the flat shape of five years pressing back against her palm.

*Hold on,* she told the names in the dark. *A little longer. Help is coming—from the fen, from London, from wherever Emrys is walking tonight. Hold on.*

Far away across the snow, the light at Marlow Fen burned on.
