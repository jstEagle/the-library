## 14. The Inspection

The snow had turned to sleet by the time the inspector's carriage came through Harrowgate, and Emrys watched it from the rise above the north road, hating how much hope he could feel building in his own chest. Hope was a thing he had learned to distrust. It arrived the way fever did—warm at first, then burning everything it touched.

Four horses, army grey, mud to their hocks. A crested door. An escort of two dragoons riding stiff against the weather. Somewhere behind that glass window sat a man with a commission from the War Office, a man whose whole trade was ledgers and lists—the very weapons Emrys needed.

*They audit regiments,* Thomas's letter had said, in that coded language of kept men. *Paper comes to check paper. That is when the fen holds its breath.*

Emrys pulled his coat tighter and started down toward the road.

He had been three days in Harrowgate, sleeping in the loft above the hospital's stable because Mara had declared him too thin and too stubborn to send away, and because proximity to her ledger—and to the muster rolls she'd begun quietly copying—felt like standing near a fire in January. He had not told her he meant to speak to the inspector. She would have forbidden it. She would have been right. But three years of searching for a brother the army insisted was dead had cured him of waiting for permission.

The carriage slowed where the road met the hospital lane, wheels grinding through slush. Emrys counted his advantages as he walked: he was nobody, a civilian in a patched coat; no roll contained his name; Vane's people had no reason yet to watch him. He would simply be a man with a grievance, presenting a petition—as any citizen might. He had rehearsed it on the walk from the fen. *Sir, my brother is listed among the fallen at Salteau Cross. I have lately received a letter in his hand. I ask only—*

Only what? Only that the machinery of the state turn its great blind eye toward him for one moment and *see*.

The carriage halted before the hospital's gate. A footman unfolded the steps, and the man who descended was not what Emrys expected. He was young—younger than Emrys by several years—with a smooth pink face and the air of a man who had dressed carefully for weather he did not respect. His coat was immaculate beneath a fur-collared greatcoat. He surveyed the hospital, the ward-blocks, the burying ground beyond the wall with its crooked wooden markers, and his expression suggested he found all of it faintly distasteful, like a smell.

Major Sir Harold Pemberton-Grice. Emrys had heard the name whispered at the fen: an auditor, a creature of the Paymaster General's office, famous for completing his inspections faster than any man in the department. *Famous for finding nothing,* one of the ghosts had said, and spat.

Still. He was here. Paper had come to check paper, and Emrys intended to put himself between those papers.

He crossed the yard at a respectful pace, breath steaming. "Sir—if I might beg a moment—"

Pemberton-Grice glanced at him the way a man glances at a puddle. "Hospital matters are handled through the commanding officer."

"It is precisely the commanding officer I've come about, sir." Emrys kept his voice low, steady, humble. He had learned humility the way soldiers learned drill, until it lived in his body. "My name is Emrys Wren. My brother, Corporal Thomas Wren, fell at Salteau Cross. So the rolls say. Yet eleven days ago a letter reached me—in his hand." He drew the letter from inside his coat, holding it out like evidence at a trial that had not yet convened. "I ask nothing but inquiry, sir. One honest inquiry."

For a heartbeat—half a heartbeat—the young major's eyes flicked to the letter. Emrys saw something move behind the smooth face, some reflex of interest, and he thought: *there, there is a man in there somewhere, under the polish.*

Then Colonel Aldous Vane stepped out of the ward-house doorway, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

"Major!" Vane's voice carried across the yard, warm as a hearth. He came forward without hurry, gloved hands open, a smile on his face that Emrys had watched him bestow on dying men. "You find us buried in winter, sir, but our hearts are warm. Come in, come in—you'll take brandy before you take reports, or I'll answer to your father, and I'm too old for duels."

"You know my father, Colonel?" The major's posture changed subtly, weight shifting, a boy greeting a headmaster's friend.

"Knew him in Flanders, before you were breeched. A finer officer never lived." Vane's arm came around the younger man's shoulders with practiced ease, steering him toward the ward-house, and his eyes passed over Emrys once—a glance so brief and so complete that Emrys felt it like a hand turning out his pockets. "This fellow bothering you? Hospital's full of anxious souls. Grief makes beggars of us all. Sergeant!"

A sergeant appeared as if grown from the frozen ground. "Sir."

"See this man receives whatever charity the hospital offers and then sees him off the grounds." Still smiling. Still warm. To Emrys, without turning his head more than an inch: "Mr. Wren, isn't it? We grieve for your brother. A good soldier. The army remembers its own." A pause, thin as a razor. "*All* of them."

The sergeant's grip settled on Emrys's arm—not rough, but absolute. He let himself be turned away, the letter still extended uselessly toward a carriage that no longer concerned him, and understood with terrible clarity that Vane had known his name before he ever spoke it. Had perhaps known which bed he slept in, and how many steps it was from that bed to the inspector's window.

*He knows about the ledger,* Mara had said after Vane's visit to the ward. *And now he knows about you.*

The sergeant walked him to the gate and released him there, professionally incurious. Beyond the wall, the sleet had thickened. Emrys stood in it, letting it strike his face, doing arithmetic with his fear.

Vane would give Pemberton-Grice brandy and a warm room and a version of the regiment stitched from whole cloth. The audit would take two days, perhaps three. And then the major would ride back to London having found nothing, because finding nothing was his profession, and the workshop at Marlow Fen would burn a week later, and Thomas—

No. Not Thomas. He pressed the thought flat. There were two days in which the law, blind as it was, would be standing inside the walls of Harrowgate. If it would not look at the truth by itself, then the truth would have to arrange itself where even a blind man could not avoid it.

He looked back once at the ward-house windows, at the firelight and the moving shadows of important men taking comfort, and turned toward the town.

* * *

By evening, Harrowgate village wore its Sunday face. Word had gone round that the inspection was a great honour, and the inn—the King's Arms—had been given over entirely to the major and his dragoons. Lanterns hung at the door. Someone had swept the snow from the step into neat grey banks. Emrys stood across the lane in the shadow of the smithy's awning, watching officers arrive in ones and twos to pay their respects, and built his plan from the materials at hand.

It was a poor plan. He knew it was a poor plan. It amounted to getting close enough to a man of consequence to press a letter into his hands before anyone could stop him—one page, written fair in his neatest hand, laying out the facts as he and Mara knew them: names on rolls, names off rolls, wages drawn for dead men, letters in dead men's hands. Mara had helped him write it. She had argued for three hours first—*a piece of paper handed in a doorway proves nothing and tells him everything; if it fails, we have spent our only surprise*—and she was right, and he had done it anyway, because patience had never once returned anything he had lost.

At half past eight the dining-room windows blazed yellow, and through them Emrys could see the company assembled at long tables: Vane at the major's right hand, silver flashing, laughter moving down the table in waves wherever Vane leaned. The man dined corruption into existence. It was almost admirable.

Emrys went in through the kitchen.

He had sixpence for the pot-girl, a shilling for the cook, and a story about delivering a message from the surgeon—all of it bought passage to the corridor outside the dining-room, where he stood in the steam and clatter with his heart going like a drum and waited for the servants to withdraw with the second course. In England, he told himself, a man could always petition. It was in Magna Carta, wasn't it, or near enough. The law loved a petitioner. The law existed *for* petitioners.

The footmen filed in bearing roasted birds. Through the open door Emrys saw the table entire: Pemberton-Grice flushed with wine and flattery, Vane rising to propose a toast, glasses lifting in ranks like a manoeuvre executed flawlessly. *To the regiment—to the fallen, whom we honour—to the living, whom we keep.*

*Whom we keep.* Emrys's hand tightened on the letter until the paper cracked.

He slipped along the corridor, through the butler's door, into the warmth and noise of the dining-room before anyone understood what had entered it. Three strides brought him level with the major's chair. He dropped to one knee on the polished boards, as petitioners did in old engravings, and held up the single sheet in both hands.

"Major Pemberton-Grice. In the matter of Corporal Thomas Wren, reported fallen at Salteau Cross, and nineteen others besides. I submit it to your office, sir. Read it before you leave Harrowgate. I ask nothing else."

The room went silent in rings, silence spreading outward from the dropped stone of him. A footman froze mid-pour, wine arcing red into a glass. Somewhere a woman gasped. Emrys kept his eyes on the major alone, on that smooth pink face now mottling with astonishment and affront.

"Read it, sir. That is all. It concerns men your office pays wages to, who are also buried under wooden crosses. You cannot lose by reading it."

Pemberton-Grice looked at the paper. Looked at Vane. And Emrys watched the whole tragedy of the next four days compress itself into a single exchange of glances: the young official seeking instruction from the older officer, the way a schoolboy seeks it, the way a subordinate does, the way a client does from the man who has dined him and praised his father and filled his glass.

"I say," the major began, and cleared his throat, "this is most irregular. Who admitted this fellow?"

Vane's voice was sorrow itself. "A troubled man, Major. Brother lost at Salteau Cross—no body recovered, you understand, and grief without a grave turns the mind. I have tried to help him. Heaven knows I have." He turned to Emrys then, and his eyes were kind, and that kindness was the most frightening thing Emrys had seen in five years of war's leavings. "Wren. Take your paper home, lad. Grieve for your brother as he deserves—among the honoured dead. Do not make a spectacle of yourself before gentlemen who have real work to do."

"He is not dead." The words tore out of Emrys louder than he intended. "He wrote me a letter. His hand, sir, his very hand—I have it, I can show you—"

"Grief," Vane repeated softly, to the table, to the room, to history, "is a hard taskmaster."

Two dragoons took Emrys's arms. He did not fight—it would have finished everything, made him madman entire—but he did not fold the letter either. He held it out as they pulled him backward, held it toward the major with both wrists gripped, paper trembling, and Pemberton-Grice looked at it for the space of one breath.

One breath. Then the major turned to Vane and said, warmly, "You were telling me about the drainage works at the fen, Colonel," and the moment passed over Emrys's head like weather, and he was being marched out through the butler's door into the cold, past the terrified pot-girl, out into the lane where the sleet had stopped and the stars had come out, indifferent and bright.

They did not beat him. They were too clever for that. They set him down beyond the last house, straightened his coat with two efficient hands, and the elder dragoon said, "Go home, friend. Whatever it is, it isn't worth the rope," and walked back whistling.

Emrys stood in the lane. The letter was crushed in his fist. Far off, through the black shapes of houses, the King's Arms blazed and rumbled with laughter, and he understood—finally, completely, in the marrow—that there was no petition, no magistrate, no office in London that would save Thomas or the others while men like Pemberton-Grice held the scales. The law was not blind. Blindness could be pitied. This was bought and paid for, this blindness had a price list, and he had just seen the transaction conducted over roast pheasant and good claret.

Something settled in him then that had been loose and rattling since Salteau Cross. It felt almost like calm.

If the law would not see, then seeing would have to be done by other means. If the rolls were instruments of theft, then someone would have to take the master book out of the thief's hands—not plead for it, not petition for it. Take it. He thought of the ghost-network at the fen, of the schedule Mara had copied, of the quarterly supply run four days distant, and of Thomas behind the fence in the dark saying *drop it, Emrys, drop it or they'll kill me*, and he thought: *I am coming anyway, Tom. Forgive me. Or don't.*

He began to walk, out of the village, along the white road toward Harrowgate hospital, and his footsteps made the only sound in a world gone very quiet.

Mara opened the ward-house door before he knocked. She had seen the whole dinner through the window from the upper ward, she said—she'd been changing dressings with one eye on the lane—and she read what had happened in his face before he spoke.

"They wouldn't even take the letter," he said. His voice sounded strange to himself, scraped clean. "He looked at it, Mara. One breath. Then he asked about drainage works."

She stood a long moment in the doorway, lamplight behind her, and then she stepped aside to let him in. "Then we stop asking," she said quietly, and closed the door against the cold.

From the fen, four days before the supply run, the light at Marlow Fen burned steady through the dark—and tonight, Emrys noticed for the first time, there were two lights.
