## 15. Ashes in the Ward

The order came on a Tuesday, delivered with the casual cruelty of a man signing off on ration quantities.

"By directive of Colonel Vane," said Lieutenant Harrow, standing at the threshold of Ward Three with a paper he did not quite meet Mara's eyes to read, "the hospital's records room is to be purged. Typhus precautions. The old linens, the ledgers, the correspondence—all of it to be burned in the yard at noon tomorrow."

Mara was changing a dressing on a boy whose leg had been opened from calf to knee by French shot. Her hands did not stop moving. She had learned, five years ago, that her hands could be steady while everything behind them screamed.

"Typhus," she repeated.

"Fever cases in the east wing." Harrow's gaze drifted over the ward—the rows of cots, the smell of carbolic and rot, the men who watched him with the flat patience of the dying. "The medical board recommends it. Paper harbours vermin. Vermin harbour fever. The colonel is only thinking of the men."

"The muster rolls," Mara said. "The discharge papers. The letters home that haven't been posted yet. Are those vermin too?"

"Mrs. Dravenmoor—"

"Sister Dravenmoor." She tied off the bandage and rose, wiping her hands. "I've kept this ward for four years, Lieutenant. I know what typhus looks like, and I know what paper looks like, and I have never once seen the one breed in the other. But you go ahead and tell the colonel his orders were carried out. I'm sure they will be."

Harrow flushed. He was young enough that some part of him still flinched at wrongness; Vane would grind that out of him within the year, as he had ground it out of every decent man who served under him. He left the directive on the table beside the inkwell, like a thing he didn't want to touch again.

Mara picked it up. The hand was Harrow's—Vane never wrote his own orders anymore—but the language was all Vane: precise, unhurried, wrapped in concern for the men like a blade wrapped in linen.

She thought of Surgeon Brandt. Eleven days dead now, buried under a marker with someone else's name because the sexton's book had been full and no one had cared to correct it. *Fever*, they had said. He had been hale the night before, promising her testimony against the colonel, laughing at her caution over a shared cup of small beer. Then fever. Then a coffin nailed shut before dawn.

Caution, Mara understood, had not saved him. Caution had only decided who died first.

* * *

That evening she stood in the records room doorway and let herself look at it properly, perhaps for the last time.

It was a narrow stone chamber off the east corridor, windowless, lit always by a single tallow lamp. Shelves ran floor to ceiling along two walls: muster rolls going back to 1808, regimental returns, hospital admission books, bundles of soldiers' letters held pending post, pay abstracts sealed with wax gone brown as old blood. Dust lay over everything in a soft grey felt. No one came here but Mara and the clerk who had died the previous spring, and since then no one but Mara.

Five years of names lived in this room. Not just hers—hers lived in the lining of her apron and, since Brandt's death, in a second copy hidden inside the false bottom of her workbox. But these rolls were different. These were the army's own memory. If Vane burned the ledger of the world, there would be nothing left to contradict him. Every erased man would become permanently unmade—not missing, not misplaced, but *never having existed* in any record anyone could produce at trial or tribunal.

She had told herself, since Brandt, that survival was the objective. Keep the ledger. Keep breathing. Wait for a safer season.

Standing in that cold room with the lamp guttering, Mara found the arithmetic had changed. There was no safer season coming. Vane had moved from suspicion to action—he was burning records while an audit's ashes were still warm, while London still turned over whatever pages Mara had smuggled out. He was not cleaning house. He was destroying the crime scene.

And if the true muster rolls went into the fire, then her ledger became a widow's word against a colonel's seals. Paper against parchment. A nurse against the Crown's own machinery.

She began pulling shelves.

* * *

She worked until well past midnight, by feel more than light, because light through the door crack invited questions. She knew the collection the way she knew her ward—by year, by company, by the particular way each clerk had folded his bundles. She wanted one box. Only one. The 1811–12 returns, the period covering the campaign after which Thomas Wren and eleven other men had vanished from the rolls between one quarterly accounting and the next—the heart of the fraud, the span her ledger named most completely.

The box was oak, iron-strapped, heavier than it looked. She got it into the corridor, then stopped, because carrying an oak box through a hospital at one in the morning required somewhere to put it, and every hiding place she considered seemed designed by fate to be the first place searched.

"Tilly."

The girl materialized the way she always did, silent in stockinged feet, shawl over her nightdress, eyes enormous. She was nineteen and had lied for Mara once already, during the search of her quarters, telling Vane's aide with a straight face that Sister Dravenmoor kept no personal papers beyond recipes. She had trembled for an hour afterward. She had never asked what the papers were.

"You heard the order," Mara whispered.

"Everyone heard it. Cook's already crying about her recipe book." Tilly looked at the box, and Mara watched her understand it in real time—the way the girl's face went from question to answer to a kind of terrified clarity. "That's the true ones. That's the real rolls."

"It's nothing. It's linen inventories." Mara took her wrist. "Tilly. Listen to me. Tomorrow at noon those shelves burn. Everything on them. And I am not going to stand in the yard and watch the last proof that these men existed go up in smoke because I was afraid. I've been afraid for five years and it has bought me nothing except the ability to watch clearly while good men are murdered on paper."

"But if they see you take it—"

"Then they see me take it." The words came out of her with a steadiness that surprised her own ears. "But they won't see you. You'll be in the yard when it happens, with the other nurses, weeping appropriately. Do you understand? Whatever happens in that corridor, you were never here."

Tilly's jaw set in a way that made her look suddenly much older, and suddenly very like Mara herself. "You told me once that silence keeps people safe. You never said which people it keeps safe." She lifted the far end of the box before Mara could argue. "And I'd rather be caught helping than spend the rest of my life knowing I stood in a yard and watched."

They carried it together down the service stair to the laundry, where the great copper boilers stood cold, and tipped the box into the largest one, and laid wash-blue sacks and mangle cloths over it, and then Mara scattered wet linen across the top so the whole looked like nothing more than a boiler awaiting the morning batch.

"Tomorrow," she said, "when they come for the shelves, you will be elsewhere. Swear it."

Tilly swore it, and slipped away up the dark stair, and Mara sat alone in the laundry with her back against the cold copper, listening to the building settle, feeling—for the first time in five years—not afraid.

That frightened her more than anything Vane could do. She filed the feeling away to examine later, if she lived long enough to have later.

* * *

Noon came grey and windless, the yard packed with snow trampled to grey ice. Two wagons stood by the records room door, and orderlies ferried out armloads—shelves stripped bare in an hour, the work done with military efficiency because it was, in its way, a military operation.

Vane attended in person.

He stood near the pyre in a greatcoat lined with wolf fur, gloved hands clasped behind him, conversing pleasantly with the surgeon-major about the weather as though the weather were the actual business of the day. He had a fire lit before the first bundle arrived—a big, cheerful, wasteful fire, the kind officers ordered when fuel was somebody else's problem—and Mara understood that too. A fire already roaring needed no explanation for how fast it consumed things.

She stood with the nurses at the edge of the yard, hands folded, face arranged into its usual expression of weary competence. Beside her, where Tilly should have been, stood nobody. Good girl.

The bundles went in. Admission books. Letters—soldiers' letters, unposted, some of them the last words a mother would ever have received, burning now because paper harboured vermin. Mara watched a corner of one flare, a line of blue ink visible for a moment like a fish surfacing, then gone.

Something in her chest closed like a fist.

*Burn it all,* she found herself thinking, with a heat she hadn't permitted herself in years. *Burn your tidy fire. I have your names in my apron. I have your signatures copied in my workbox. And I have one box of truth sitting in a wash boiler twenty feet from where you're standing, Colonel, and you will never find it, because you cannot imagine that a woman who scrubs other people's blood off sheets could hide anything worth finding.*

"Mrs.—Sister Dravenmoor." Vane had crossed the yard without her noticing, which itself was a failure of vigilance that appalled her. Up close he smelled of bay rum and cold air, and his smile was the one from Chapter Four of her private catalogue: the generous one, the one he wore when dispensing charity he intended to invoice later. "A grim business, hygiene. But necessary. You of all people appreciate necessity."

"I appreciate fires, Colonel. I've seen enough of them." She nodded toward the pyre. "Though usually we wait for men to die before we burn their effects."

"These aren't effects. They're clerical surplus." He examined her with those pale, patient eyes, and Mara felt the old assessment in them—the weighing, the pricing. "Still, sentiment has its uses. Keeps us human. Tell me, have you completed the ward inventory? The medical board will want current figures after the purge."

"The inventory is accurate, Colonel. It always is."

"I know," he said softly. "Accuracy is your defining quality. One might almost think you kept accounts of your own."

The sentence hung between them in the frozen air, and neither moved to break it. Down the yard a bundle of pay abstracts caught with a whump that sent sparks spinning skyward, and a cheer went up from an orderly who was quickly silenced.

"I keep accounts of lint and laudanum," Mara said. "If you find others, Colonel, I trust you'll tell me. I'd hate to be accused of industry."

For a moment—one heartbeat, no more—something moved behind Vane's pleasantness, something quick and cold, the look of a man deciding whether a problem was worth solving now or later. Then he smiled wider, wished her health, and walked away across the ice with his wolf fur shining, leaving Mara to realize her nails had carved four crescents into her own palms through her gloves.

Blood, when she checked, in tiny half-moons. She did not mind it. Pain was proof of pulse. Pulse was proof of intent.

* * *

The fire burned until dusk. Mara stayed for all of it, because someone should witness, and because leaving early would look like grief and grief would look like guilt.

When the last shelf stood empty and the orderlies had gone to supper, Vane departed with them, and the yard belonged to the snow and the settling smoke and one exhausted woman in a nurse's grey dress.

She waited a full hour. Then she went down to the laundry, sent the single duty maid away with a fabricated message about scalded skin in Ward One, and uncovered the boiler.

The box sat exactly where they had left it, dry, untouched, smelling faintly of oak and old wax. Mara lifted the lid. The 1811–12 returns lay in their bundles, every page intact, every name present—including, on the September roll of the 33rd Foot, in a clerk's careful copperplate: *Wren, T., Corporal.* Present, alive, paid, accounted for. Two pages later, in a different hand entirely, in ink that had never passed through any legitimate registry: *struck off. deceased.*

Two hands. Two truths. One lie.

She closed the lid and stood holding it, and understood that she had crossed some line in herself sometime during the past day—quietly, without ceremony, the way all her important crossings happened. The woman who had survived five years by noticing and recording and *waiting* was gone. In her place stood someone who would carry this box through fire if she had to, who had watched Vane's face across a burning yard and felt not fear but a terrible, clarifying anger, the kind that does not shake the hands.

Brandt had waited for a safe moment and died of fever. Mara was done waiting for safe moments. The copies already on their way to London would speak; this box would testify. And if Vane wanted it, Vane could come and try to take it from her personally, and explain to a court-martial why he had wrestled a nurse.

There remained only the problem of where a box could live that no search would reach.

She was still turning the question over when she climbed to the ward that evening and found Emrys waiting for her by the high window at the corridor's end, hood up, having entered—by whatever ghost-roads he traveled—past sentries, past curfew, past sense. Snowmelt dripped from his coat. His face, in the lamplight, had the look of a man carrying news that could be either salvation or catastrophe.

"You shouldn't be here," Mara said.

"We found the schedule," he said. "The workshop's supply run goes out in nine days. And Mara—" he glanced down the empty corridor, lowered his voice to almost nothing, "—Thomas has agreed to leave the door unbarred. But there's a condition, and it's mine to ask, and you need to hear it tonight."

Behind them, faintly, the smell of smoke still hung in the walls of the hospital, and would for weeks.
