## 16. The Plan at Marlow Fen

The safe house was a drover's cottage half-swallowed by the fen, its thatch rotted green and its windows papered over with old broadsheets. Emrys felt the building before he understood it—a low hum of held breath, the particular quiet of men who had practiced not being heard. Six of them sat around a peat fire that gave more smoke than light, and every one of them wore a name that belonged to no roll in the Army's keeping.

Hollis, who had been Sergeant Hollis once and was now "Mr. Greave," a carter. Pell, dead at Aldeburgh on paper, alive and lame in fact. The brothers Finch, listed as lost with their transport ship, who had never seen the sea that day at all but a locked depot door instead. And old Cathcart, oldest of them, who had been erased so long ago he sometimes struggled to recall which lie answered to his face.

They had not wanted to trust him. They still did not, entirely. But Mara had come with her ledger sewn into her coat lining, and she had read to them—not all of it, not yet, she said; there was a night coming for that—and she had read *their* names, their true names, aloud in her flat nurse's voice as though reciting scripture. Something in the room had changed when she did it. Emrys had watched grown men flinch at the sound of themselves.

Now they bent over a plan drawn in charcoal on a scrubbed tabletop, and the fen wind worried at the papered windows like something begging to be let in.

"The quarterly run comes at month's end," Hollis said, tapping the map with a scarred finger. "Wagon from Marlow Fen down the towpath to the coast road, escort of four, driver who doesn't ask questions because he values his questions. It carries the quarter's finished work—discharges, transfers, death notices, whatever the Colonel's trade requires—down to be posted through legitimate channels. That's your window. While the wagon's gone, the workshop runs thin."

"How thin?" Emrys asked.

"Guards stay. Vane's not a fool; he keeps six inside regardless, and the gate stays shut." Hollis looked up. "But there's a coal delivery same morning, through the yard door on the north side, and the yard door is barred from within by one man only."

Every head turned, though no one said the name. They all knew whose hand barred that door.

"He'll do it," Emrys said.

"You don't know that." Pell's voice was bitter. "Two years in that place does something to a man. We've sent word in before. We've had word back." He spat toward the fire. "The word back is always: leave it."

Emrys thought of the fence at night, the fog between the palings, his brother's whisper coming through the gap like something dragged over gravel: *Drop it, Emrys. Please. If it surfaces they'll kill me and burn what's left.* He had gone away from that fence with his hands shaking and his purpose intact, somehow—because love that asks you to abandon your brother to his captors is not love obeyed, it is love hijacked, and Emrys knew the difference even if Thomas could not yet see it from inside the wire.

"He'll do it," Emrys said again, quieter now. "Not because I've persuaded him. Because he's been waiting two years for someone to give him permission."

Mara spoke from her corner, where she sat with a candle stub and the ledger open on her knees, making her careful copies. "Permission isn't enough. He needs to believe the thing he fears most will happen anyway unless he acts." She did not look up from her writing. Her nib scratched softly, steadily, like an insect working at winter. "We must make the truth heavier than his fear. That's all any of this is—scales."

"And how do we do that?" Cathcart asked.

She set down her pen. In the firelight her face looked carved, all planes and shadow, five years of Harrowgate pressed into the lines around her mouth. "By showing him the book. Not stealing it—showing it. If he sees the master ledger with his own eyes, sees his own name in it, sees the sums, then he knows the world already knows whether or not we succeed. Fear of discovery is worse than discovery itself. Any soldier knows that. It's why men confess at the scaffold stairs."

A silence settled, thick as the peat smoke. Then Hollis laughed once, without humor. "God help us. A nurse with a general's head."

"I'm not a general," Mara said. "Generals spend men. I've spent five years trying not to."

* * *

They planned for three days, and the planning had the quality of all desperate arithmetic: everything counted twice, everything found short.

The ghost-network's contribution was knowledge bought over years at terrible prices. The wagon left at first light on the last Thursday of the quarter—always Thursday, because the coastal clerk took his market half-day Friday and Vane's schedule bent around another man's idleness like water around stone. The escort was drawn from the garrison rotation, never from the workshop's own guard, so the six inside would not be thinned by sentiment. The coal cart arrived mid-morning, and for perhaps the length of twenty minutes the north yard stood crowded with barrels and bustle and eyes pointed everywhere but down.

"Twenty minutes," Emrys said. "That's what we're wagering everything on. Twenty minutes of coal dust and confusion."

"Less," said Finch the younger. "Say fifteen. The guards will count the barrels fast because they want their tea."

Fifteen minutes. Emrys turned the number over the way he'd once turned coins in lean winters, checking both faces. Fifteen minutes to get four men through a yard door that might or might not be unbarred, subdue or evade six guards, find the master book—which Cathcart believed lived in the strong room behind the clerical floor, under double lock, the second key worn on a chain around the neck of Vane's senior clerk—and get out before the yard emptied again. And if the door stayed barred? If Thomas's nerve failed, or his fear won, or his handlers had already smelled the change in him?

Then they would have burned their one advantage—the ghost-network's anonymity, months of careful approach—for nothing, and every man in this cottage would need to run farther than the fen to escape what came after.

Emrys laid out the rest anyway. Mara would wait with the cart on the towpath, playing the nurse she was, ready with plausible business if anyone stopped them—a sick carter, a fevered horse, anything. Hollis and Pell would take the coal yard. The Finch brothers would hold the lane south in case the wagon returned early. And Emrys would go in first, alone, because there was one person in that workshop more likely to open a door for his own face than for any stranger's.

"You shouldn't speak to him," Mara said afterward, when the others had drifted out to see to the cart and the fog had come up off the water to press against the windows. "Not during. You saw what his letters asked. A man who begs you to stop is a man halfway to betraying you just to make the asking stop."

"Then I'll say nothing during," Emrys said. "I'll stand where he can see me, and I'll let him choose. It has to be his choice, Mara. Anything else is just another man being moved about by someone stronger, and God knows he's had enough of that."

She studied him for a long moment. Candlelight moved in her eyes. "You're wagering him," she said at last, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. "Your brother. On the table with everything else."

"Yes."

"And if the wager loses?"

Emrys looked at the fire and found he had no answer that would survive being spoken aloud.

* * *

He went to the fence alone that night, though Mara had argued against it and the network had forbidden it outright. Forbidden things, Emrys had learned, were simply things other people were afraid to do; courage was mostly a disagreement about odds.

The fog lay on the fen like a second darkness. He came up on the palings from the marsh side, boots silent in the wet sphagnum, and put his mouth near the gap where the boards had warped apart, and waited. Somewhere beyond, a lantern swung along the clerical floor's high windows—men in uniform, kept writing, kept alive and kept dead at once, scratching out the paperwork of their own erasure.

After a long while, footsteps in wet gravel. A shape resolved against the palings, gaunt as famine, and Emrys's heart did what it always did at the sight of his brother—that lurching drop, grief and love arriving together like twin riders.

"You shouldn't be here," Thomas whispered.

"No. But I am."

"They've changed the watch. There's a new man on nights, a Vane creature, smells trouble like a dog smells fear. If you're planning—" The words strangled themselves. *If you're planning something*, he had been about to say, and could not finish it, because finishing it made it real.

"We are," Emrys said. "Month's end. The supply run. There's a coal cart that comes through the north yard, and a door you bar yourself, every morning, and I am going to stand on the other side of it and ask you for the only thing left in this world worth asking you for."

Silence. The fen dripped. Somewhere far off a marsh bird cried once and thought better of it.

"They'll kill me," Thomas said at last. The words came out flattened, rehearsed—he had said them to himself so many times they'd worn smooth. "You don't understand what he's made me do, Emrys. My own death notice. My own hand. He had me write it twice because the first time my letters shook, and he stood behind me saying, *again, Corporal, again, you're dead, do try to look dead on paper.*" A sound escaped him that was either laughter or the opposite. "And the others. Names every week. Men whose mothers I write to in a fair hand, telling them their sons died honorably, knowing they're mending roads somewhere or rotting in this fen. I'm not a prisoner, Emrys. I'm an instrument. Instruments don't get to open doors."

"Instruments don't get asked," Emrys said. "I'm asking."

Through the gap he could see only a slice of his brother: one eye, the ridge of a cheekbone, the stubble he'd last touched when they were boys wrestling on their mother's kitchen floor. The eye closed.

"If it fails," Thomas whispered, "he won't just kill me. He'll erase me properly this time. No false record to hide behind. An actual grave, actual dirt, and no one—no one—will ever know I was here at all. Do you understand? That's what he takes. Not lives. *Having-been.*"

"I understand." Emrys pressed his forehead to the cold wood. "And I'm asking anyway. Because there's a woman three miles from here with a book full of names, and yours is in it, Tom. Written in her own hand, dated, witnessed. Whatever Vane burns, that page survives. You have already been remembered. All that's left is to decide whether you'll be remembered free."

The eye opened. In the fog-light it was wet.

"Swear to me," Thomas said.

"Anything."

"Not that I live tonight. Not that the plan works. Swear me the other thing." His voice cracked and he mastered it the way he'd mastered it on parade grounds and in places Emrys did not let himself imagine. "Whatever happens—whether the door opens or doesn't, whether I walk out or I'm buried in that yard—if you get the book, if it costs me everything, you use it. Every name restored. Every pension claimed. Every mother told the truth, even the ugly parts. Especially mine." A breath. "Swear that I live afterward means more than breathing, Emrys. Swear that I live in the rolls, in the record, in the mouths of men who say my name. Swear that whatever's left of me ends up *somewhere*, written down, and I'll bar no doors."

Emrys found his throat had closed. He worked it loose.

"I swear it," he said. "Your name first, out loud, in front of everyone. And every name after it, until the list is done. On our mother's soul. On yours."

For a long moment there was no sound but the fen breathing. Then, through the gap, a hand emerged—thin as a bundle of laths, filthy to the wrist—and gripped his forearm with a strength that surprised him, that reminded him, absurdly, of their boyhood, of arm-wrestles lost and avenged, of a brother who had once been whole.

"North yard," Thomas whispered. "Third barrel from the wall, morning of the run—I'll chalk a mark on the ones that matter. When the coal cart comes, I go out to check the door's barred, same as every month. Same as every month, Emrys. Nothing new. Nothing for the new man to smell." The grip tightened. "And if it goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"Everything goes wrong eventually. Ask any dead man." The ghost of a smile crossed the visible slice of face. "If it goes wrong, you swear the other part anyway. Promise me the record matters more than the rescue. Promise me you'll be the kind of man who chooses the book."

Emrys could not answer that one. He nodded instead, and hoped the dark hid the failure in his face, and felt his brother's hand slip back through the gap into the world that owned him.

The footsteps retreated across the gravel. The lantern climbed the stairs inside. Emrys stood a long time with his forehead against the palings, breathing fog, feeling the splinter-rough wood print itself into his skin like a signature.

* * *

Back at the cottage, the fire had been fed and the network waited, and Mara rose from her corner the moment he came through the door, reading his face the way she read wounds—one glance, triage complete.

"He agreed?"

"He agreed." Emrys sat heavily and held his palms to the weak heat. "On one condition. That we value the book above the man. That if it comes to choosing, we choose the record." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "My brother has spent two years as a pen in another man's fist, and his price for freedom is a guarantee that the writing survives."

Mara was quiet a moment. Then she went to the table, opened the ledger, and turned pages until she found what she sought. She set the book before him and put her finger on a line.

Thomas Wren. Corporal. Date of last muster. Date of official death. *Date of entry: in my hand, this date, witnessed by no one because there was no one left to witness.*

"He was right," she said softly. "It does matter more. Not because men are worth less than paper—they aren't, God knows, I've buried too many to think so—but because paper is how the strong keep winning. Vane understands ledgers better than any of us. He built a kingdom out of blank spaces." She looked up, and the candlelight made her eyes fierce. "So we take the tool out of his hand. We don't just steal the master book. We finish it. Every man in this room, every man in that workshop, entered, dated, witnessed. This time by people who mean it."

Around the fire, the ghosts had gone very still. Old Cathcart spoke first, his voice rusty. "Witnessed," he repeated, as though tasting it. "Nobody's witnessed me since Balaclava."

"Then it's overdue," Mara said.

One by one they came to the table—Hollis, Pell, the Finches, Cathcart—to look at their own names written in a steady hand, and something passed among them that Emrys had no better word for than regiment: the old word, doing its old work, in a cottage full of men the army insisted had never existed.

They ran the plan once more from end to end. Wagon leaves Thursday at first light. Coal cart at mid-morning. Fifteen minutes, maybe twenty. Third barrel from the wall, a chalk mark, a door that would be checked and found—perhaps—unbarred. Mara on the towpath with her cart and her calm. The book, if God was good, in Emrys's hands by dusk.

It was past midnight when the others finally slept. Mara remained at the table, copying, her nib scratching its small patient insect-song. Emrys watched her from across the room and thought about wagers—his brother's life on one side of the scale, a book on the other, and a woman who had spent five years learning exactly what each weighed.

"Mara," he said quietly. "If it goes wrong—"

"Then we do the other thing," she said, without looking up. "The thing we should have done years ago. We publish. Every name, every page, every copy, scattered like seed. You can burn a workshop, Emrys. You cannot burn a story once it's walking around with legs on." She lifted her pen, and in the guttering light her mouth curved into something that was almost a smile and entirely a threat. "Sleep. Thursday is coming."

Outside, the fen lay black and still, and thirty miles of cold water away, a lamp burned late over a desk in a shuttered depot where a colonel slept badly beside a satchel of stolen papers, and a corporal lay awake memorizing which barrels to chalk.

Three days to the wagon. Three days to the door.

And on the table between the sleeping ghosts and the burning peat, the ledger lay open to the next blank page—waiting, as it had waited five years, for someone brave enough to fill it.
