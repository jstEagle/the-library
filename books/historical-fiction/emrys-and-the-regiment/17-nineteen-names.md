## 17. Nineteen Names

The barn smelled of hay dust and tallow and the cold sweat of frightened men.

Mara had chosen it for its silence as much as its seclusion—a half-collapsed tithe barn two miles from the fen, where the wind through the broken slates made a sound like the sea, deep enough to drown a voice raised in ordinary conversation. Emrys had spread straw in the chancel-end where the roof still held, and the ghost-network had done the rest: lanterns hooded with tin, sentries posted at the gaps in the wall, nineteen men gathered in the dark like a congregation that had forgotten how to pray.

She stood among them now with her apron in her hands.

Five years she had carried this weight against her body—linen over linen, the ledger sewn flat into the apron's lining so it rode beneath her ribs like a second heartbeat. She had unpicked the stitches an hour ago in Tilly's borrowed room, by candlelight, with the same bone-handled scissors her mother had used on christening gowns. The act had felt like surgery. Like opening a wound she had kept closed not because it was healed but because bleeding would have killed her.

Now she held the book out in both hands, and nineteen men looked at it the way starving men look at bread.

"You should understand what you're about to hear," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "These are not my words. I copied them. I copied them in the linen closet while the wounded were sleeping, and I copied them in the deadhouse while the dead were waiting, and I copied them on my knees behind the dispensary counter because there was nowhere else a woman could stand for ten unwatched minutes. I want no one to mistake me for brave. I wrote these names down because I could not bear to let them be unwritten."

A man near the front—Hollis, the one with the burned jaw who had not spoken since Emrys brought him in from the coast road—shifted his weight. The straw crackled beneath him like small gunfire.

"Colonel Vane's clerks will tell you tomorrow, if they speak to you at all, that none of you exist," Mara went on. "That you deserted, or died honorably, or were never enrolled—the story changes depending on which lie is cheapest. What follows is the other record. Nineteen names. Nineteen men who stood where duty put them and were struck from the rolls for the price of a horse or a commission or a winter's brandy. I know your mothers' names. I know what was owed to you. And tonight, before we go into that fen, every word of it gets said out loud. Because words spoken alone can be dismissed." She lifted the ledger higher, and the hooded lantern light moved across its pages like water. "Words spoken together become testimony."

Nobody breathed. Somewhere outside, the wind worried at the slates.

Mara opened the book to the first page and began.

* * *

"Private Daniel Ashcombe, Fourth Company. Enrolled September the eighth, 1808. Struck from the rolls March 1811, recorded dead of fever at the age of twenty-three. He did not die of fever. I nursed him through fever in the spring of 1810 and he walked out of Harrowgate on his own legs. His mother is Bridget Ashcombe of Netherfold, a widow who has received none of the pension owed to her son—fourteen shillings a quarter, three years' arrears, forty-two shillings stolen before the ink on his death notice dried."

In the third row a man made a sound like a hinge giving way.

He was young—or had been young once; it was hard to tell anymore—and he pressed both fists against his mouth as though he could push the sound back in. Emrys moved toward him, but Mara caught his eye and shook her head. Let him make the sound. The sound was the point.

"Ashcombe," she repeated. "Daniel Ashcombe. Son of Bridget. Alive as of this night."

The young man lowered his fists. "She'll be seventy in the spring," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle. "I sent money when I could. Through a chapman. You never know if it—" He stopped. Started again. "You knew her name."

"I know all their names," Mara said gently. "That is the whole of what I have done. It is very little. But it is yours."

* * *

She read for two hours.

The candles guttered and were replaced; the wind rose and fell; men arrived late through the wall-gap and were waved to the straw by the sentries, and still her voice went on, level and unhurried, each entry given its full measure because she had decided long ago that if she ever read them aloud, she would not skim, would not summarize, would not do to these names in public what Vane had done to them in private—which was to treat them as arithmetic.

"Private Samuel Oduya, Second Company. Enrolled June 1810. Struck January 1812, recorded killed at the crossing, though no action occurred at the crossing that month. Owed: eleven months' pay, a boot allowance never issued, and the good-conduct badge his sergeant recommended in writing—a recommendation that also vanished. His sister Grace Oduya keeps a lodger's room in Deptford and tells neighbors her brother is at sea."

"He is at sea," someone murmured, and a few men laughed—not bitter laughter, or not only bitter, and Mara felt something shift in the room, some hard knot beginning to loosen.

"Corporal Edmund Pryce. Enrolled—" She stopped. Turned the page. Her thumb found the entry by feel; she had traced it a hundred times in the dark of the linen closet. "Enrolled April 1807. Struck December 1811 after the retreat, recorded dead of wounds. I dressed those wounds myself. He was walking within the month. Owed: back pay of eight pounds, two shillings; a pension of ninepence a day for the loss of two fingers; and the letter he asked me to write to his wife, which I wrote, and which I was told by Captain Lorne not to send, because Mrs. Pryce was already receiving the widow's settlement and questions might arise."

A man in the back stood up. He was heavyset, grey at the temples, and he held up his left hand so the lantern light caught it—two fingers gone at the second knuckle, the stump healed crooked.

"You wrote it anyway?" he said.

"Yes."

"It never came?"

"No. I gave it to Captain Lorne for the post bag. I learned later what the post bag at Harrowgate was." She had learned it in the worst way possible, from a boy named Willet whose letter to his mother had gone into that bag and into a stove, and she did not say this aloud, but the grief moved through her face and Pryce saw it, whatever it was, and nodded slowly, and sat back down.

"She thinks I'm dead," Pryce said, to no one. "Four years dead. She'll have stopped wearing black by now. She'd have had to, with the rent." He rubbed the stumps of his fingers together, an old habit, a gesture like trying to snap something invisible. "I used to dream she'd get another letter. In the dreams I could never think what to say in it."

"Say it tomorrow," Emrys said quietly, from beside the door. "When we have the master book, the letters can go anywhere. Every post bag in England, if you like. Nothing Vane sealed stays sealed after tomorrow."

* * *

Somewhere past the middle of the ledger—it was page thirty-one, though Mara would not remember that later; she would remember only the moment—the reading stopped being hers.

It happened without anyone deciding it. She reached the entry for Private Thomas Merrick, enrolled 1809, struck 1813, owed a coat allowance and sixpence a day to a father in York who had spent his savings hiring a man to search the battlefield for a grave that did not exist—and before she could continue, a voice from the right side of the barn picked up the next line.

"Private Ansel Coote," said Hollis, the burned man, his ruined jaw making the name come out sanded and strange. "Enrolled August 1809. Struck February 1812." He swallowed. "That's me. That's my entry. I never thought—I didn't know anyone had—" He couldn't finish. He didn't need to. Mara simply turned the ledger toward him, and by the dim light he read his own name in her careful hand, his mother's name in Exeter, the sum of eleven pounds owed against four years of silence, and then he closed his eyes and read it again from memory, as if stamping it somewhere permanent.

After that the barn would not be silent again, but neither was it disorderly. Each time she reached a name, its owner spoke it with her—sometimes stumbling, sometimes in whispers, sometimes with a ferocity that startled the straw. *Private Josiah Fenn. Private Lucas Wray. Corporal Thomas Wren.* At Wren's name the whole room seemed to lean, because everyone knew whose brother stood by the door, and Emrys said the second half of the entry along with her—*"owed three years' pay; recorded dead October 1811; alive as of this night"*—and his voice broke on *alive* the way voices break on things too large to carry any other way.

Thomas Wren sat apart from the others, as he had since arriving under Emrys's escort, hands folded, face shuttered—the posture of a man still performing obedience to masters who were not present. But when his own name was read, Mara saw his lips move. Saw him give himself back to himself, silently, like a man returning a borrowed coat.

* * *

Near the end, Mara's voice began to fray. Two hours of reading by tallow light, five years of carrying, one night of saying it all aloud—it cost something, and the men heard it cost something, and perhaps that mattered more than the words themselves. When she paused to wet her throat, Tilly appeared at her elbow with watered wine, having slipped out from Harrowgate under cover of her night rounds, sworn to secrecy twice and swearing it again now with the fierce joy of a girl finally allowed inside a secret worth keeping.

"There's more wine in the flask," Tilly whispered. "And Hollis's lot found a cheese. Somebody's got a cheese, Miss Dravenmoor, after all this. Men are strange."

"They are faithful," Mara said. "Which looks strange from the outside."

She read the last entries standing. Private Benjamin Aycliffe. Private Ross Hume. Private Peter Sorrell, owed a pension and a wooden leg the army had charged to his own pay and then erased along with the leg and the man. And then the final page—the most recent, the rawest—Private Thomas Wren's fellow clerk, Private Nathaniel Quill, struck from the rolls only seven weeks prior, whose entry was written in fresher ink than all the rest, because Mara had copied it from Emrys's account rather than from her own eyes.

"Alive as of this night," she finished, and closed the book.

Nineteen men sat in straw and lamplight and did not move.

Then Hollis stood, and stuck out his hand—whole, unburned, offered across the dark—and took the hand of the man next to him, who took the hand of the man beyond, and so on down the line, a chain of grips traveling through the barn like current through wire until it reached Thomas Wren at the far end and pulled him, gently, into the circle. No one spoke. The chain tightened once, all together, and released.

"We should sleep," Emrys said at last, hoarse. "Wagons roll at first fog. Thomas knows the door. The rest you know."

Men settled into the straw in twos and threes, closer together than strangers sleep, and Mara wrapped her apron—unlined now, lighter by one book—about her shoulders and found that her hands were trembling at last, now that the work was done, the way a house shakes after the storm has passed rather than during.

Tilly crouched beside her. "Nineteen," the girl whispered. "You wrote nineteen names and every one of them was breathing tonight to hear it. Do you know what that is, Miss Dravenmoor?"

Mara looked down the barn—at Ashcombe asleep with his fists still curled; at Pryce showing his maimed hand to a man from the Second Company as if introducing it; at Thomas and Emrys sitting shoulder to shoulder in the doorway light, not speaking, not needing to.

"It's a muster," she said. "The first honest one in five years."

But sleep did not come for her, and near the coldest hour it was Emrys who woke her, crouching at her side with the lantern shuttered to a slit, his face gone pale as paper.

"Mara." His voice was barely breath. "Sentry's whistled from the fen road. There's a rider coming—Vane's grey, Tilly swears it, she'd know the horse. And he's not riding past."
