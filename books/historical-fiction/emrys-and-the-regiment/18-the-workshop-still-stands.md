## 18. The Workshop Still Stands

The fog came off the fen at dusk, the way Thomas had promised it would in the note scratched into the doorframe—a message meant for no one, left by a man who had learned to speak only in things that could not be read aloud. *Fog thick by quarter-moon. Wagon comes after dark. Door on the water side is oldest. Bar rotted.*

Emrys stood in the reeds and watched the fog do its work. It swallowed the fen foot by foot, erasing the causeway, the black water, the distant smudge of the depot's roofline, until the world was reduced to the width of a lantern's throw. He had spent two years learning to move through country where being seen meant being killed—scouting forage lines outside besieged towns, crossing no-man's-land with nothing but dark and nerve—and he had never once been grateful for weather like this. Tonight it felt like a gift from a God who had otherwise been conspicuously absent.

Behind him, crouched among the reeds, waited eleven men who had every reason to let the whole rotten enterprise burn.

They called themselves ghosts, and Emrys had come to understand that the name was not self-pity. It was armor. There was Corporal Hesse, listed dead at Talavera, his jaw rebuilt with a silver plate that caught even this lightless air and glinted faintly when he turned his head. There were the Merrick brothers, entered on the rolls as drowned in the Tagus, though both of them could not swim. There was quiet Sam Oduya, whose name had never been on any roll at all—he had been pressed, unpaid, unrecorded, a man the army had found convenient to forget before Vane ever touched him. Eleven men whom Colonel Aldous Vane had written out of existence, and who had agreed, after three nights of argument in a smuggler's loft, to wager their fragile new lives on one desperate hour.

*If we fail,* Hesse had said, *we are dead men twice over. But I have been dead once already. It did not hurt as much as you would think. What hurt was watching my wife collect nothing.*

And Mara. She stood apart at the edge of the reeds, wrapped in a borrowed greatcoat too big for her, her ledger strapped against her ribs beneath the wool. She had insisted on coming, and Emrys had understood within a minute of her insistence why arguing was useless. This was not a woman who stayed behind while names were at stake. Her face in the dark was pale and utterly still, and her eyes never stopped moving—the habit of five years of noticing what others were paid to overlook.

"The schedule holds?" she asked softly, when he came back to her.

"The wagon comes at moonrise to collect the quarter's papers. Two outriders, a driver, four guards rotating on the doors. Thomas leaves the water-side bar loose." He paused. "He agreed because I promised."

"I know what you promised."

"Then you know what I'm about to break if this goes wrong."

She looked at him then, directly, and something in her expression reminded him of the first night at Harrowgate, when she had held his frozen hands between hers and read his grief like a page. "You promised your brother would live," she said. "That is not a promise you break tonight. That is the promise everything else exists to keep. Remember which thing is the mission, Emrys. Not the book. Him."

He wanted to believe her ordering of it. He was not sure he could. The master book—Vane's own ledger of erasures, the twin shadow-record that matched Mara's stolen fragments and proved them, sealed and signed and initialed—was the whole case. Without it there would be another inquiry, another year of polite correspondence while witnesses died of convenient fevers. With it, Vane would hang or rot in a cell, and twenty-six men would be returned to the rolls with their pay and their names intact. Twenty-six men. And one of them was Thomas, who had spent three years forging documents in a shuttered depot on the fen because the alternative was a shallow grave, and who had begged Emrys through a fence in the dark to let it all go rather than get himself killed.

*They will burn me, Emrys, not you. They burn what they can reach.* His brother's voice still lived under his skin, low and terrified and terribly reasonable.

Across the water, a light appeared. Then two. The wagon, coming along the causeway with shuttered lamps, moving slow through the fog like a ship.

"Positions," Emrys breathed.

* * *

They moved in ones and twos, spread along the causeway's verge, each man carrying his part: Hesse and the Merricks toward the stable gate to take the outriders quietly; Sam Oduya flat in the reeds beside the water-side wall; Mara at the treeline with the second wagon—one they had borrowed, ostensibly a farmer's cart, actually the ghost-network's escape—ready to drive it up the moment the door opened. Emrys went last and alone, hugging the old stone wall where the fen lapped at the depot's foundation, feeling his way by touch toward the oldest door.

The plan was simple, and simple plans were the only kind that survived contact with soldiers. Let the supply wagon in. Let the gates close behind it. Take the drivers before they could raise alarm. Enter through the rotted-barred door. Find the counting room on the north side, where Thomas had told them the master book lived in an iron-strapped chest that the guards were forbidden to open—only Vane's clerk knew the key, and the clerk slept above the stables. Be gone with the book before the quarter-hour bell. Leave no bodies if they could manage it, because bodies brought investigations, and investigations brought Vane's kind of justice, which was the same thing as none.

Emrys reached the door and put his fingers to the wood. It was older than the rest, swollen with fen-damp, and the iron bar inside—when he pressed his eye to the gap between planks—hung crooked in its brackets, one end lifted free of the slot. Thomas had done it. His brother had stood on the other side of this plank today and loosened the bar that kept him captive, with guards ten paces away, knowing what it would cost him if anyone noticed. Emrys rested his forehead against the cold wood for one breath, two, letting the nearness of him settle like an anchor.

Then the wagon rattled up to the main gate, lamps swinging, and voices carried thin across the fog—"Quarter run, open up, Vane's seal"—and the gates groaned wide, and there was no more time for anything but motion.

Inside the wall, boots on gravel. The drivers dismounting. A guard complaining about the damp getting into his matches. Emrys slid the bar aside with both hands, easing it clear so it made no sound, and pushed. The door swung inward six inches and stopped on something—a wedge, a boot. A hand closed on his collar and pulled him through into darkness that smelled of ink, tallow, and wet stone.

For one blind instant they grappled, and then the grip changed—not fighting, holding—and a voice he had known since childhood shook against his ear.

"You came. God help us, you actually came."

Thomas was thinner than the fence-darkness had shown him. Thinner than the letters had lied. His hands, when Emrys gripped them, were stained to the knuckle with ink, the fingers of a man who wrote fourteen hours a day, and they were shaking. Up close, in the faint gray light through a high window, Emrys saw the bruise along his brother's jaw, fresh, yellow-green at the edges.

"They moved the schedule," Thomas whispered. "That's not the quarterly wagon—that's early. They've been loading chests since noon. Emrys, he's coming himself. Vane. Tonight. The inspector's report went wrong, someone talked in London, and he's clearing everything that can be cleared. Papers first. Then—"

"Then the men."

Thomas didn't answer. His silence answered.

* * *

They crossed the yard in the wagon's shadow while the drivers' attention was fixed on the gate. Emrys signaled from the wall—two raised fingers—and heard, rather than saw, the drivers taken: a soft scuffle, a grunt cut short, Hesse's low voice saying *down, stay down, you'll thank us later*. One of the outriders got a shout half out of him before the night closed over it. Then the yard belonged to the ghosts.

Mara drove their borrowed cart through the gate with more courage than any woman had shown in Emrys's sight, straight up to the counting-room steps as if she had delivered goods to army depots all her life—which, he supposed, in a manner of speaking, she had. She was down off the bench before the wheels stopped, moving with terrible purpose.

"The chest is in there," Thomas said, pointing to the counting room's shuttered window. "Iron-strapped. The clerk has the key on his neck. He's above the stables, asleep, or pretending to be."

"Get the key," Emrys told Hesse. "No blood unless it chooses you."

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters and miracles both do. Hesse and the Merrick brothers went up the stable stairs. Mara and Emrys forced the counting room. Inside, by the light of a single shaded candle Emrys dared to lift, the room revealed itself: ranks of shelving heavy with paper, bundles tied in red tape, stacks of blank discharge forms waiting to become lies. And against the far wall, exactly as promised, the chest—black iron straps, brass lock, government broad-arrow stamped deep in the lid.

Mara knelt before it the way other people knelt at altars.

Hesse came back in ninety seconds with a key corded around his fist and a clerk walking docilely ahead of him, white-faced and cooperative in the way of men doing rapid arithmetic about which side of history to stand on. The key turned. The lid came up.

The book inside was not beautiful. It was a quartermaster's ledger, calf-bound, fat, its corners bumped from years of carts and campaigns. But when Mara lifted it onto the table and opened it, her breath went out of her in a sound Emrys had never heard from her—not grief, not relief, something older and harder than either. Page after page of names in Vane's precise, elegant hand. Dates of alleged death. Amounts drawn against dead men's pay. Initials approving pensions never paid. Margins annotated in a different ink, a colder one: *sold*, *resold*, *hold for transport*, *no family — safe*.

"There," Mara said, and her finger stopped on a line. "Wren, T., corporal, 3rd company. Died of wounds, October 1811." Her voice did not shake. That somehow made it worse. "He signed this. He sat at a desk and wrote his own death and drew his own pay into Vane's pocket, for three years."

"Turn the pages gently," the clerk said suddenly, hoarse. "There's carbon paper beneath. If you're going to copy anything, mind the set-off—it betrays itself."

Nobody laughed, but something passed across the ghosts' faces that in better days might have become laughter.

They worked in shifts—Mara checking names against the list she had carried against her ribs for five years, Sam Oduya wrapping the book in oilcloth, the Merricks hauling document bundles into the cart, everything done in a rhythm of controlled noise. Emrys kept his post at the window, watching the fog-bound yard, and let himself feel, for perhaps forty seconds, the impossible sensation that they were going to win.

That was when the bells started.

Not the quarter-bell. All the bells of the depot at once, hammered frantically, and lights blooming in windows that should have been dark, and through the fog from the causeway road the sound Emrys had dreaded every night of the journey here: hooves. Many. Coming fast.

"He's early," Thomas said from the doorway, gray-faced. "God forgive me—he wasn't supposed to be here till the second watch. He changed it. He changes everything now, he trusts no one—Emrys, go, take the book and go, there's still the water side—"

Through the window Emrys saw the riders resolve out of the fog at the main gate, and at their head a figure sitting straight in the saddle with the easy, unhurried posture of a man arriving at his own estate. Even at this distance Emrys knew him. You would have known him anywhere, that man, by the way the world seemed arranged around his convenience.

Colonel Aldous Vane did not ride into his yard shouting. He sat his horse and looked slowly around at the lit windows, the scattered lamplight, the cart standing at the counting-room steps, and Emrys watched comprehension move across him like cloud-shadow across a field. Watched him understand, in one long moment, exactly what had been taken from that room.

Vane dismounted. He handed his reins to his aide. And then he gave a single order, quietly, to the sergeant beside him—a gesture, almost casual, toward the long low range of buildings with their stores of lamp-oil, tallow, spirits, paper, straw.

"No," Emrys said. He said it to the glass, uselessly, and then he was moving.

* * *

Afterward he could never reconstruct those minutes in order. Memory kept them the way fire keeps things—as heat without sequence.

They ran for the water-side door with the book between them, Mara's arm hooked through the oilcloth strap, ghosts scattering to their escape routes and finding the routes closing. Soldiers pouring through the main gate in files. Torches being lit—not to search by. To throw.

The first flame arced out of the fog and burst against the stable wall, and the fen-damp timber fought it for perhaps ten seconds, and then lost. The second followed the first. Vane's voice carried across the yard, calm, pitched for posterity: *"Records contaminated by fever contagion. Burn them all. No man enters the buildings—fever kills faster than deserters."*

A story, already finished, already polished. By morning it would be in the dispatches: records destroyed to prevent pestilence; regrettable; thorough. And whatever bodies the fire found in the buildings would be found as ash, unnamed, and the rolls would say what the rolls had always said. *Dead. Deserted. Never existed.*

"The counting room," Mara gasped as they ran. "It's stone-floored—the chest—even if it burns, the iron protects—" But her voice cracked on it, because they both knew the truth: a book does not survive being inside a burning building regardless of what shields it, not really, not when the roof falls in.

They burst through the water-side door into the long clerical hall, and the world in there was already orange. Flame had found the eastern end where the lamp store stood, and it moved along the ceiling beams with a sound like applause. Smoke layered down from the rafters in shelves of its own.

"The back way," Thomas shouted. "There's a coal chute to the fen—"

And then, in the smoke, Emrys realized his brother was not running with them. He had stopped. He had turned back toward the interior rooms.

"Marta—" Emrys caught the name wrong and didn't care. "Who's inside?"

"The Portuguese boy. Joaquim. They locked the sick ward when the fever scare started—they lock it every night—" Thomas's face was wrecked. "I forged his death certificate myself. Last spring. He's sixteen."

Emrys had promised his brother would live. His brother had just run into a burning building to keep a similar promise, and there was no arithmetic in the world that balanced those two facts, so Emrys stopped trying to balance it.

"Take the book!" he roared at Mara, over the roar of the beams going. "Out! Get it to London! That's the order—that's the only order I get to give!"

Her hands were on the oilcloth, and her face was a battlefield, five years of discipline warring with something newer. For one heartbeat she looked as though she would refuse him—as though she would follow them both into the fire because a name unread was a name unfinished.

"Both of you," she said. "Do you hear me? Both of you come out, or everything in that book dies twice." And then she was gone into the smoke toward the water door, the master book clutched to her chest, and the ceiling beam over the corridor let go in a gout of sparks, and Emrys turned and ran after his brother into the heart of the light.

The heat hit him like a wall, like a living animal leaning on him. Somewhere ahead, through the smoke, he could hear Thomas coughing, hammering at a barred infirmary door, and behind it—faintly, terribly—a boy's voice answering.

The stairs to the gallery sagged overhead. The far wall glowed through its plaster like a grate. Emrys put his shoulder to the door beside his brother, once, twice, wood splitting, lungs filling with hot ash, and thought with strange clarity of Mara's voice on the first night at Harrowgate: *Some people are only real to those who write their names down.*

"Not this one," he said aloud, to nobody, to the fire, to every colonel who had ever picked up a pen. "This one comes out."

On the third blow, the door gave—and through the smoke, small and coughing, a shape moved toward their hands.

Behind them, the roof began to come down.
