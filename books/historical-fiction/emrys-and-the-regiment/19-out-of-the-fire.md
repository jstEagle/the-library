## 19. Out of the Fire

The fog at Marlow Fen had thinned to rags by the time the first shout went up, and through those ragged gaps Mara saw the sky over the workshop begin to change—not dawn, not yet, but something redder and more terrible, a glow rising behind the shuttered windows like a fever breaking through skin.

*Fire,* she thought, absurdly, calmly. *He has set it early. He is burning it while his own men are still inside.*

She had been crouched behind the supply wagon with Tilly's brother Wat and two of the ghost-network men—Hollis and a scarred giant who called himself only Petersen, because Petersen was the name of a dead man whose papers fit him well enough—when the lantern signal they'd planned on never came. Instead came Vane's carriage, lamps blazing, arriving a full hour before any schedule said it should, and then came the smell of pitch, and then the orange bloom of flame in the eastern window, and then everything that had been a plan became simply people running.

"Emrys!" she called into the dark, and could not find him.

She had told herself, all these months, that she was done with silence—that her ledger had been five years of shouting written very small. But standing in the fen's black mud with a burning building ahead of her and no soldier's orders anywhere in reach, Mara Dravenmoor discovered how much of the nurse still governed her: the part that moved toward screaming rather than away from it, the hands that knew what to do before the mind consented.

"Stay with the wagon," she told Wat, already moving. "If anyone comes out burning, you put them down in the mud and roll them. Nothing else. Do you hear me?"

He heard her. She was already gone.

* * *

The workshop was worse up close than any fire she had known at Harrowgate, and Harrowgate had burned twice in her years there—once by accident, once by order. This fire had been *fed*. Pitch and lamp oil had been laid along the walls; she could see the neat, deliberate spill-lines catching light one after another, like sentences being written in a language only destruction could read. Vane had not merely wanted evidence destroyed. He had wanted it *erased*, the way he erased men—thoroughly, officially, with no ash left that could be named.

The main doors were barred from outside now. Someone had dropped the bar into its brackets as the flames took hold. Mara put her shoulder against the door and pushed, and the heat came through the wood and pressed against her face like a living thing objecting to her presence.

"Not this way," rasped a voice, and Hollis was beside her, pulling her back by the sleeve. "Side wall—the old loading door. It faces the water. The fire's eating from the east end westward—if your man went in for his brother, he'll have gone to the far side, where the clerks' benches are."

"How do you know?"

"Because that's where I sat for eleven months," Hollis said, "before I wrote myself out of my own name." He looked at the flames, and his scarred face did something complicated. "Go round the north. I'll draw the sentries. They'll be running anyway—the whole guard detail bolted the moment the first barrel caught."

She ran. Mud sucked at her boots; smoke lay along the ground in a low grey tide, and she ducked her head beneath it where the air was still cold and clean. The fen water glimmered on her right, flat and indifferent, reflecting the fire so faithfully that for a moment she seemed to be running between two burning buildings—one real, one only its ghost.

The loading door stood open a hand's width, breathing smoke. She hauled at it, felt the rusted runners give with a shriek, and slipped sideways through the gap into heat like a wall.

Inside, the workshop was an inferno arranged with terrible order. The long clerical benches where men had copied out death notices for their own lives were already alight; paper curled and blackened and lifted on the convection like a migration of burnt butterflies—pages, hundreds of pages, decades of careful forgery ascending toward the hole in the roof. Somewhere beyond the smoke, something heavy fell, and a voice cried out—a human voice, cracked and desperate—and another voice answered it, hoarse and furious:

"Thomas! Thomas, take my hands—"

Emrys.

She followed the voices, low, one arm across her mouth, eyes streaming. The smoke stratified: black above, grey in the middle, and near the floor, in the last clean stratum of air, a narrow lane of survival. She crawled into it, skirts dragging through oil and ash, and saw them at the far end of the aisle between benches: Emrys on his knees beneath a fallen beam he was holding up with his back and both arms, and beneath the beam, pinned by the leg, a gaunt man with ink-stained fingers, coughing in the red light.

Thomas Wren. Alive. Real. His face was thinner than his brother's, paler, but the resemblance struck Mara even now, even here—the same bones under the ruin, the same stubborn jaw.

"I can't—" Thomas gasped. "Emrys, the leg—go, *go*, if they come back and find you—"

"If you say that once more," Emrys snarled, straining against the beam, "I will write it in your own hand and have it read aloud at your funeral, which will not happen today."

Mara reached them. She had carried men twice this size out of surgical tents on her own; fear made them lighter, or her arms stronger, or both. She knelt in the glass and muck, took stock the way she would in any ward: leg pinned mid-shin, beam's weight partly resting on a bench-end, bleeding but not spurting, airway clear, consciousness good.

"You," she said to Thomas, briskly, in the voice that had moved five years of wounded soldiers. "Look at me. Not the fire. At me. We are going to lift this, and when we do, you will pull yourself backward along the floor with your arms, and you will not stop until you taste fen water. Nod if you understand."

He nodded, wide-eyed, as though the sheer authority of her were a rope thrown into drowning water.

"Together," she said to Emrys. "On three. And when I say move *back*, you drop it and go—you do not lift with your spine, you lift like I showed the stretcher-bearers at Harrowgate, legs and fury. Ready. One—two—"

On three they heaved. The beam groaned, shifted, rose perhaps four inches—enough. Thomas dragged himself backward with a sound Mara would remember in her sleep for years afterward, a sound between a scream and a hymn. "Back," she snapped, and they let it fall, and the place where he had lain was a black scorched hollow in the boards.

Then there was only the arithmetic of escape: one man who could not walk, one doorway too narrow for carrying, fire advancing along the oil-lines with the patience of a clerk ruling lines on a page.

"Shoulders," Mara ordered. "I take the broken leg, splinted with—this." She tore the apron lining where the ledger had lived for five years, ripping out the last of its hidden pocket, and bound his boot to his shin with the cloth. There was a strange rightness in it, using the ledger's hiding place to carry a man out of erasure. "Emrys, when I say the count, you take him through the loading door sideways. I will walk backward ahead of you and keep the cloth off the sparks."

They moved through the burning workshop the way she imagined souls must cross somewhere worse—slowly, bent double, through a cathedral of falling embers. Halfway to the door, a shelf collapsed behind them and the heat shoved at their backs like a hand, and Thomas, half-conscious against his brother's neck, began reciting names. Mara realized, with a jolt, what he was doing: the muster list, the master book's contents, poured out by memory in case none of them survived to read it. *"Aldridge, private, Kent. Barrow, corporal, no county recorded. Callum, Daniel..."*

"Hold the names," she told him fiercely. "We will hold the rest."

* * *

They came out into air that tasted like snowmelt and salvation. Wat and Petersen took Thomas from them at the waterline, laid him in the mud, doused a smoldering sleeve, splinted the leg properly with a wagon slat. Emrys stood swaying, hands blistered, hair singed at the temple, looking at his brother as though he needed to watch him breathe for a full minute before believing it.

Only then did Mara see the satchel.

Down the fen track, moving fast in the pre-dawn grey, went Colonel Aldous Vane—unhurried enough to look dignified, fast enough to make the lie of it plain—with a leather satchel slung across his body and his aide and two dragoones around him, heading for the coast road. He did not look back at his burning industry. Men like Vane did not look back at things they considered already finished.

"He has the coin ledger," Hollis said grimly, coming up at a run. "The pay-book. Whatever he couldn't prove without, he's taken."

"The master book?" Mara asked.

Hollis turned out the canvas sack he'd been carrying since before the raid and drew it out: large, swollen with damp, one corner charred to a black curl—but closed, clasped, whole. The master book of Colonel Aldous Vane's quiet empire: every erased name, every diverted pension, every forged discharge, entered in a fair hand with dates and sums.

"We had it off the strong-room shelf when the first barrel went," Hollis said. "Petersen's doing. He said—he said he'd waited three years to steal something *worth* stealing."

Mara took it in both hands. It was heavier than her ledger had ever been, warm at one edge, and it smelled of smoke and ink and five years of other people's grief. She opened the clasp. The pages inside were scorched brown along the outer margins, some edges eaten entirely, but the entries ran deep and dark in the gutters of every leaf, and where the fire had lapped, the ink remained legible beneath the scorch like bone beneath burned skin.

*Legible.* Her eyes found a line at random and her throat closed: *T. Wren, Cpl.—deceased 14 Oct. 1811—notice drafted, sealed—pension redirected per instruction.* Thomas Wren, alive in the mud ten feet from her, spitting soot and cursing his leg, reduced in this book to a transaction.

"It's damaged," Emrys said quietly, beside her. "Will it serve? In London, before a tribunal—pages half-burned, sums illegible—his counsel will call it fabrication. He'll say we burned it ourselves for theater."

"It will serve," Mara said. She touched the charred margin almost tenderly. "Do you know what a defense counsel will see when he looks at this? Damage. I see proof of method. A man does not burn his own accounts and leave them to be found—he burns them to be *gone*. This book survived because men risked their lives entering a fire Vane lit to destroy it. Every scorch mark is testimony. Let the court ask why the margins are blackened. Let him answer that under oath."

Emrys looked at her—at the soot in her hair, the blood on her split knuckles, the ruined apron with its empty hidden seam—and something in his exhausted face eased, the way a man eases when he sets down a weight he has forgotten he was carrying.

"They will also ask," he said, "about the copies. The pages you smuggled to London weeks ago. Whether they arrived. Whether anyone read them."

Mara looked down the coast road, where the lamps of Vane's party had shrunk to sparks swallowed by the grey.

"They arrived," she said. "Whether they've been *read*—that, I don't know. Paper travels slowly when powerful men would rather it traveled nowhere. But it travels." She tightened her grip on the master book. "And he is racing a ship, and we are racing nothing but distance and daylight. Thomas needs a surgeon, the book needs dry land and a locked chest, and I need—" she paused, surprised by her own answer, "—I need to start copying. Tonight. Before anything else happens to these pages, there will be three copies in three hands, carried by three different people, because I have watched too many truths die of being singular."

Behind them the workshop groaned, settled, and sent up a column of sparks that rose and rose and scattered over the black fen water like a reversed snowfall, each ember dying with a small hiss as it touched the surface. The place where men had been kept writing was becoming only light and heat and a smell that would linger in the reeds for a year.

Thomas, propped against the wagon wheel with Wat binding his leg, had stopped reciting names. Instead he was watching the fire with an expression Mara could not immediately parse—until she did. It was the look of a man watching his own gravestone burn. Two years of forged death, of existing only as a redirected sum in a colonel's column, and now the instrument of that erasure was ash, and he was *cold*, and his leg hurt, and his brother was kneeling beside him saying nothing, just gripping his shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"It's gone," Thomas said hoarsely. "All of it. The benches, the seals, the presses—God, the presses. He'll say the evidence burned."

"He'll say a great many things," Mara replied. She held up the master book so the firelight caught its scorched edge. "But this says otherwise. And so do nineteen names in my ledger, and so do you."

Thomas stared at her, and then at the book, and something broke open in his face—not grief exactly, and not relief, but the strange compound of both that Mara had seen only on the faces of men pulled back from death: the understanding that survival is not free, that someone paid for it, and that the debt must now be honored in the only currency that matters, which is *testimony*.

"Then we go to London," Thomas said. His voice steadied as he said it, finding its footing word by word. "I'll stand up in whatever court will have me. I'll identify him with this leg in irons if it comes to that. He made me draft my own death notice, Mrs. Dravenmoor. My own name, in my own hand, dated and sealed. I signed it because they told me my brother would hang for a deserter's kin if I refused." He looked down at his ink-stained fingers, trembling now that the adrenaline was leaving them. "I have wanted, for two years, to unwrite one line. Just one. Let it be that one."

Emrys made a low sound, half laugh, half sob, and pressed his forehead briefly to his brother's uninjured shoulder.

Mara turned once more to the fen. The fire was settling into itself now, the great orange roar diminishing to red veins along the collapsing roofline, and in its dying light she thought of Surgeon Brandt, who had asked questions once and died of a sudden fever for it; of the widow who received money from a husband on no roll; of the boy whose name she had promised to send to his mother and had failed, five years ago, and built a life's method out of failing. All of them were in the book against her chest, scorched but legible. None of them had lived to see this. That was the loss braided through the triumph, ineradicable: victory arrived late, and its lateness had a body count.

But it had arrived.

"Wat," she said, turning to the wagon. "Bring the tarpaulin. We wrap the book in oilcloth, then wool, then tarpaulin. Hollis—how many of your network are within a day's walk?"

"Six," Hollis said. "Seven counting Aldridge, and he's slow but he's loyal."

"Send for them. Each takes a copy. Different roads, different days." She was speaking rapidly now, the ward-voice again, organizing chaos into method. "Vane has a satchel of coin-ledgers and a bribed ship, and he believes he has outrun us. Let him believe it. But a man fleeing to the coast passes through towns, and towns have post riders, and post riders can be faster than carriages if you know which ones to pay." She looked at Emrys. "How soon can you ride?"

Emrys glanced at his bandaged hands, at his brother's grey face, and Mara watched him weigh it—the brother he had crossed a country to find, finally, bodily, *there*, ten feet away, needing him—against the pursuit his every instinct was howling for.

"Give me an hour," he said. "And give me one page of the master book to carry in my coat, over my heart. If I'm stopped, they can take everything else. Not that."

She tore out a single leaf—Barrow, Corporal, no county recorded, pension redirected per instruction—and pressed it into his blistered hands.

"Ride for the coast road," she said. "He will make for a ship, and ships wait for tides, and tides keep honest schedules, which is more than I can say for bribed captains. You will not catch him alone." She allowed herself the smallest smile, and it cost her cracked lips and she paid it gladly. "But he does not know about the others. He does not know that the erased have learned to count themselves."

Behind her, the last wall of the workshop at Marlow Fen fell inward with a sigh, sending up one final galaxy of sparks. Mara did not watch it fall. She was already unwrapping the oilcloth, already checking the pages for damp, already, in her mind, sharpening the first of three pens.

The workshop burned. The truth did not.

And forty miles away, at a grey dawn harbor, a colonel with a stolen satchel stood on a cold quay waiting for a ship that was, at that very hour, being watched by six pairs of eyes belonging to men whose names appeared in no roll anywhere in England—men who had nothing left to lose, and one thing left to give: *witness*.
