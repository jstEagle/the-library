## 20. Vane's Last Ledger

The sea at Duncastle Quay was the color of tarnished pewter, and it had been chewing at the pilings all night with a sound like teeth.

I crouched behind the fish-curing shed with the master book wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, and I watched Colonel Aldous Vane stand at the end of the quay like a man waiting for a coach that might never come. He had traded his regimentals for a plain grey greatcoat, but he could not trade away his posture. He stood the way he had stood on every parade ground and in every hospital ward: spine straight, chin lifted, as if the world were an assembly of soldiers awaiting his inspection. Even hunted, he inspected.

The sloop *Meridian* rode at anchor beyond the harbour mouth, riding lights doused. She would come in on the pre-dawn tide—so Vane believed—for she had been promised gold. What she had actually been promised, by men whose names appeared in no roll of His Majesty's Army, was a different cargo entirely: one colonel, delivered to whatever justice could be arranged before he set foot on French or American soil.

Thomas stood at my shoulder in the shadow of the shed. He had not spoken in an hour. His hands, which for two years had forged discharges and death notices in another man's ink, were flexing and unflexing at his sides, as though they could not decide whether they still belonged to a clerk or a soldier.

"He'll try to talk first," Thomas said at last. "It's what he does. He talked me into that workshop with nothing but kindness."

"Then we let him talk," I said. "And we let the others hear him do it."

Below us, along the quay's edge, the ghosts were taking their positions. They moved the way they had learned to move in two years of erasure—unhurried, unremarkable, men dressed as fishermen and draymen and idlers, each of them carrying some ordinary thing: a coil of rope, a crate of herring, a lantern they had no need to light. Hollis, who had died at Alberghe on paper and gutted fish here in the flesh for eighteen months, sat mending a net not twenty feet from where Vane paced. Two brothers named Cade, both listed dead by the same flawless hand on the same day, leaned against a bollard sharing a bottle that had never once been opened. And at the landward end of the quay, where the road came down between salt-bleached warehouses, Mara Dravenmoor stood beside a cart as if waiting for the morning market, her ledger's twin—the copied pages, the true ones—bound shut beneath her cloak.

She had sent them to London ten days ago. Three copies, three roads, addressed to three men at the Horse Guards whose names Surgeon Brandt had whispered to her years ago and sworn were honest. We had heard nothing since. The silence had been its own kind of weather, pressing down on everything.

Now the tide turned, and the *Meridian*'s boat put off from the ship, four oars dipping quietly, and Vane stepped forward to meet it, and everything began.

* * *

He heard us before he saw us—or rather, he heard that the quay had stopped being empty. A man like Vane knew the difference between a working quay and a watching one. He turned slowly, the way a man turns when he already knows, and found himself surrounded by the dead of five regiments.

I walked out from the shed with Thomas at my side and the oilcloth bundle under my arm, and I set my feet on the wet stones and made myself stop three paces from him. Close enough to see that he had not slept. Close enough to see that his eyes went first—not to me, not to Thomas—but to the book.

"Mr. Emrys." He said my name the way he must once have read a muster roll: accurately, without warmth. "You have something of mine."

"It was never yours," I said. "That is rather the whole point."

"The point," Vane said, "is a matter of arithmetic." He spoke reasonably, almost gently, and I understood why men had followed him into the workshop and stayed there, why widows took his money and told no one, why even Brandt's warnings had taken five years to become testimony. His voice was the voice of every reasonable thing ever done to unreasonable people. "Nineteen names in your nurse's little book. Nineteen pensions, nineteen bounties, nineteen widows' allowances over six years. Do you know what that sums to? More than you will earn in your life, Mr. Emrys. And it is spent. Spent on powder, on surgeons, on the men who lived because the money existed. You would take that engine apart for the sake of—" he gestured faintly at the fog-bound water— "paperwork."

"Nineteen names," I said. "My brother among them. You had him write out his own death notice, didn't you. You had him make the ink match."

Something crossed Vane's face then. Not guilt—I don't believe the word applied to him in any form I'd recognize. It was the look of a craftsman hearing his work praised by someone he'd hoped wouldn't understand it.

"The notices had to be consistent," he said. "A clerk's hand varies with fatigue. Wren has an unusually steady hand. It seemed a waste not to use it."

Thomas made a sound beside me, low in his chest, and I put my hand on his arm and held him where he stood.

"You can't hang us all," Vane went on, and now his voice dropped, became intimate, the voice he must have used across desks in shuttered rooms. "The book names men above me. Men whose signatures you have never seen because they never needed to sign. Burn that page and keep the rest, and I will see you compensated. Every man on this quay restored to the rolls, back pay, pensions—yes, real ones—and passage home for you and your brother tonight. No trial. No scandal. No mothers learning how their sons actually lived. Think of them, Emrys. Some of these men are better off dead on paper. Their families have made peace. You would reopen graves for nothing."

"And that," said a woman's voice from the landward end of the quay, "is exactly what he did to all of you."

Mara came forward out of the fog with the lantern raised, and I saw what she was doing: she was making herself visible, making all of us visible, so that any watcher from the road or the headland would see not an ambush but a reckoning. Her voice carried flat and clear over the water, the voice of a woman who had spent five years reading aloud to dying men.

"You offered Thomas Wren silence for two years," she said. "You offered Mrs. Calloway silence for a pension she never received. You are offering it again now, at the end, because it is the only currency you have ever held. But we've brought our own ledger, Colonel. It balances."

Vane looked at her for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was the smile from the hospital ward, warm and paternal, and I finally understood why it had always chilled me: there was nothing behind it. It was a door painted on a wall.

"Miss Dravenmoor," he said. "They told me you were clever. I regret now that I only offered you a promotion."

* * *

He drew while he was still smiling.

I want to be precise about this, because for years afterward people asked me how it happened, and the truth is that it happened exactly the way everything else in his career had happened: smoothly, in the space of a reasonable sentence, with no warning anyone was paid to notice. His hand went into the greatcoat, and the pistol came out, and it was aimed—not at me, not at Thomas, but at Mara, at the ledger and the woman holding it, because Vane's arithmetic had never once failed him and he knew precisely which single item on the quay was worth more than his own life.

He never fired.

Hollis threw the net. I will not pretend I saw it coming; none of us had planned it. Eighteen months of mending nets on that quay, and his arms knew the motion better than any drill. The weighted mesh opened against the fog and came down over Vane's pistol arm, and the shot went into the sky and startled gulls off the warehouse roof, and then the quay was moving. The Cade brothers took his shoulders. Hollis took his wrist until something cracked and the pistol spun away onto the stones. Vane fought—he was stronger than his age suggested, and desperate as a drowned man—but he was one living soldier against nineteen dead ones, and the dead had waited too long to lose.

We put him on his knees on the wet planking. Hollis knelt on the pistol. And Thomas stepped forward, out of the shadow of my shoulder, and stood over the man who had erased him.

"Corporal Thomas Wren," Thomas said, and his voice shook only at the start. "Seventh Company. Reported killed in action, October the ninth, 1811. Alive." He paused, and steadied. "I identify Colonel Aldous Vane as the author of that report. I wrote the fair copy myself. I know the hand. It is mine."

The words went out over the water and did not come back. Somewhere in the fog the *Meridian*'s boat crew, understanding at last what cargo awaited them, had begun backing water, pulling for open sea. Vane knelt on the quay with blood at his lip and his broken wrist cradled against his coat, and he looked up at Thomas with an expression I will carry to my own grave: not fear. Contempt, thinning into disbelief, as if the entire mechanism of the world had failed to perform a function he considered basic.

"You'll be hung alongside me," he said. "Deserters. Forgers. Half of you aren't even men in law. You're clerical errors."

"Then correct the error," Mara said.

* * *

The officers came at first light.

We heard the horses on the coast road before we saw them—six riders, then the glint of more behind, and a carriage with the King's arms on the door. The ghosts melted toward the warehouse walls out of old habit, and I felt my own body do the same, five years of hiding pulling at my heels. But Mara walked out into the middle of the quay to meet them, lantern still lit against the greying dawn, and after a moment Thomas and I went and stood with her, and then, one by one, so did all the others.

Major-General Sir Edmund Harlance descended from the carriage looking like a man who had not slept since certain pages reached his desk. He had read them, that much was clear from his face. He had read them the way one reads a wound report.

His eyes went to Vane, kneeling between the Cade brothers, and stayed there a long moment.

"Colonel Vane," he said. "You are relieved of all command and placed under arrest by order of the Judge Advocate General. The charges—" he unfolded a paper, though I do not think he needed to; I believe he simply wanted Vane to hear them read aloud, formally, in the open air, where for six years nothing about this man had ever been read aloud truthfully— "are fraud upon the Crown, falsification of the rolls, theft of pay and pensions appertaining, and the unlawful detention and coercion of serving soldiers. You will be tried by court-martial at Harrowgate. These men—" and here his gaze moved across the nineteen, across the fog-thinned faces of the erased— "will be restored to the rolls pending investigation. Rations and quarters at government expense."

One of the Cade brothers laughed. It was not a happy sound. It was the laugh of a man being handed back his own name after two years, and finding it fit strangely.

Vane was lifted to his feet. As they turned him toward the carriage he twisted once and found me across the quay, and called out—loud enough for the officers, loud enough for the fog, because a man like him never stopped calculating audiences:

"The pages above me, Emrys! Ask her whose names are on the last leaf! Ask why the book survived the fire!"

The carriage door shut on the rest of it.

Mara had gone very still. I crossed to her. Beneath her cloak she held the copied pages, and her thumb was pressed flat against the binding, hard, as though holding something shut.

"The last leaf," I said quietly. "What did he burn? What did he mean?"

"I don't know yet," Mara said, and her eyes, which had faced him without blinking, would not quite settle on mine. "But the original master book is in your arms, Emrys. And I haven't dared read its final page since we pulled it out of the smoke."

The fog was lifting off the water. Behind us the *Meridian*, cargo-less, weighed anchor and slipped away seaward, and the sun came up the colour of new brass on nineteen faces that no roll had ever counted—and on one small, scorched book, whose last page neither of us had yet found the courage to turn.
