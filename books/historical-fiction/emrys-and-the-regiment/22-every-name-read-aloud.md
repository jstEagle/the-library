## 22. Every Name Read Aloud

**Mara**

The printer's shop on Stonegate smelled of ink and hot metal, and I stood before the galley proof with my hands behind my back like a woman afraid to touch her own life.

*The Harrowgate Ledger: Being a True Accounting of One Hundred and Forty-Two Soldiers Erased from the Rolls of the Line, with Testimony of the Living.* Below the title, no publisher's flourish, no general's endorsement—only my name, Mara Dravenmoor, set in type for all of England to read. The same name Colonel Vane's counsel had tried to drag through the mud of a court-martial three months past. They had called me a meddler, a sentimentalist, a woman whose grief had curdled into obsession. The tribunal had not agreed. The tribunal had read page after page in a silence so complete I could hear the rain against the tent canvas, and when they finished, the president of the board had asked only one question of me: *Why did you keep it?*

Because someone had to remember them while they were still alive to be remembered. That was the answer I gave. It was also the answer this book would give, ten thousand copies of it, if the printer's estimate held.

"You'll want to check the names again," Mr. Halloway said, wiping his inked fingers on an apron gone gray with years of the same gesture. "We can still correct anything."

"They're correct." I had copied them four times over—the original sewn into my apron lining, the duplicate hidden in a coffin bound for England, the copy surrendered as evidence at trial, and now this last transcription, checked against the recovered muster rolls Tilly and I had pulled from the burning records room at Harrowgate. "Every one of them."

Halloway nodded and turned back to his compositor's frame, and I stood a moment longer reading down the first column. *Wren, Thomas, Corporal, 3rd Battalion. Private Aldous Reed. Private Samuel Ochs. Drummer boy William Fenn, aged fifteen.* Names I had written by candlelight beneath linens while men died six feet away, names that had been lies on official paper and truths nowhere at all until I made them true somewhere.

The Army had offered to take the ledger into its archives once the verdict came down. A quiet absorption, a locked room, a clerk's stamp. Restored to the records, they called it—which meant buried in them. I had thought a great deal about archives in those weeks, about the difference between a record and a memory. A record sits where no one goes unless they are already looking. A memory walks out of doors. It gets read aloud.

So I had refused them, politely, in writing, with Surgeon Brandt's old stubbornness sitting in my spine like a second skeleton. He had lost his post for asking questions. I intended to spend the rest of mine answering them.

"Mrs. Dravenmoor?" Halloway again, hesitant. "There's a lady asking after you at the door."

Tilly stood on the cobbles in the pale spring light, bonnet crooked, a letter in her fist and her whole face lit like a window at dusk. She was nineteen now and had refused every offer to leave Harrowgate; she kept the ward's linen accounts and had begun, quietly and without asking anyone's permission, to write letters for soldiers too broken-handed to hold a pen.

"It's from the pension society," she said, breathless. "From Emrys. The first payments went out Tuesday. Eleven widows, Mara. Eleven, and the twelfth found alive in York."

I took the letter and read the figures twice. Money raised from subscribers to the book before the book even existed—from readers who had heard the trial reported in the papers and wanted, for once, to be on the side their consciences already occupied. Behind me, in the shop, the press began to turn with a sound like steady breathing.

**Emrys**

My brother could not drive a nail straight, which we discovered on the first morning and confirmed every morning after.

"Hold it plumb," I told him, watching the fence post lean eastward like a drunkard considering the horizon.

"It *is* plumb. The ground is crooked." Thomas Wren, who had survived two battles, three years of coerced forgery, and one burning building, squinted along the post with one eye shut. His left hand still bore the burn scars, and the fingers did not entirely close, but he had learned to grip a mallet well enough, and he swore the work was rebuilding the hand faster than any surgeon's advice. "Besides, you're one to talk. Your walls are vertical. Nothing else about you is."

It was the closest thing to a joke he had made since the fen, and I let it pass without answering, because some mornings the answer was a lump in the throat.

The cottage sat on a south-facing slope within sight of Harrowgate's chimneys—not so near the hospital that the wind carried the smell of the wards, not so far that a man couldn't walk there in twenty minutes to sit with the wounded or carry coal or, as Thomas did more often than he admitted, transcribe letters for men whose hands shook. He had wanted to be near people being put back together. I understood it better than I said aloud. For three years he had been used as an instrument of erasure, his own handwriting turned against his own name. Setting a fence post that stayed set was a kind of testimony too.

The pension society had grown out of the ghost-network in a way none of us had planned. Reed kept the books—he had been a quartermaster's clerk before Vane's men took him, and arithmetic was the one language the workshop had never managed to poison for him. Ochs handled the collections up north. There were forty-one of us now, men living under names given and taken back, and the society met quarterly in the back room of a York inn, and at the first meeting there had been an argument about what to call ourselves.

*The Erased Men's Society,* Reed had proposed, and half the room laughed and half flinched.

Thomas had ended it, standing up slow in the way he still did sometimes, as if expecting the floor to be barred. "Call us the remembered," he said. "That's the only thing we were never allowed to be."

The name stuck. We had stationery printed. I confess I found the stationery absurd and loved it completely.

By afternoon the fence line ran straight for thirty yards, and Thomas sat on the wall drinking small beer and looking out over the slope toward Harrowgate with an expression I had learned not to interrupt. But today he spoke first.

"She's publishing it," he said. "The ledger. I heard from Reed."

"I know. I subscribed."

"You understand what it means." He turned the mug in his scarred hand. "Every name in that book is a name I wrote a false death notice for. When people read it, they'll be reading my handwriting in the margins of every page whether it's there or not." He looked at me then, and his eyes were clear in a way the fen had never let them be. "I want it published. I want you to know that. Some days it's the only thing that makes the last three years weigh anything at all."

Below us, on the road from town, a figure in a gray traveling dress stopped at our gate, and Thomas stood up so quickly he spilled the beer.

**Mara**

They had built the cottage themselves, which explained much about the cottage. The gate hung at an angle that suggested enthusiasm rather than carpentry, and the garden plot beyond it was freshly turned and optimistically fenced. Emrys came down the path to meet me, and Thomas followed, and for a moment none of us spoke—the three of us standing in spring mud with the whole winter of the trial between us and nothing left to hide behind.

Then Thomas bowed, formal as a court proceeding, and said, "Mrs. Dravenmoor. Will you stay for supper? We have eggs, bread, and a mutual inability to make either palatable."

I stayed two nights.

On the second evening we sat by the fire while rain worked the thatch, and Thomas showed me something he had begun keeping in a schoolboy's copybook: letters he was drafting to the families of the erased, telling each mother, each wife, each father that their man lived, where he might be found, how to reach him through the society. He had written nine. There were a hundred and forty-two names in my ledger, and not all of the men behind them had surfaced—but the ones who had were finding each other, the way scattered soldiers find each other, by instinct and rumor and the smell of shared survival.

"Reed wants the society to hire a reader," Emrys said, poking the fire. "Someone to go through the returned letters, the parish records, the prison rolls—looking for the ones still missing. Someone who knows how names get lost."

"That sounds like work for a person who spent five years noticing what others were paid not to see," I said.

Both brothers looked at me. I had known the offer was coming; I had felt it forming in Emrys's silence on the walk back from the gate. And I had already answered it in myself, though I made them wait while I considered the fire, because a decision should be seen to have weight.

"I'll do it," I said. "But not only that. There's something else. Next month—May, when the war news has settled and the roads are dry—I'm going to stand somewhere public and read the ledger aloud. All of it. Every name. In the market square at Harrowgate, if the constable permits, and if he doesn't, just outside it."

"Why aloud?" Thomas asked quietly. He knew why. I could see he knew. But he wanted to hear it said, perhaps because for three years his voice had been used to unmake things, and he needed evidence that voices could make them.

"Because paper can be archived," I said. "Paper can be locked away and stamped *resolved*. A voice has to be heard by somebody, and everybody who hears it carries it. I spent five years writing in secret. I intend to spend the rest of my life speaking in public." I smiled, and it surprised me how easily it came. "Besides, the men deserve to hear their own names spoken as if they matter. Not entered. Spoken."

Emrys laughed softly. "You've become dangerous, Mrs. Dravenmoor."

"I was always dangerous. I was just quiet about it."

* *

**Mara**

The third of May dawned clear over Harrowgate, and by nine o'clock the market square held more people than market day ever drew.

They came from everywhere. Widows in black who had received the first pension payments and worn their best shawls to say so without saying so. Soldiers on crutches from the hospital wards, Tilly marching them down like a sergeant-major of the wounded. Men I recognized from the ghost-network, arrived from York and Leeds under their restored names, standing together in a loose formation no officer had ordered. Surgeons. Farmers. Children perched on cart wheels because their mothers wanted them to see it. And at the edge of the crowd, apart from everyone, a handful of Army officers sent to observe, whose faces I watched only long enough to confirm that they had come to listen rather than to interfere. The trial had taught even Whitehall that this ledger could not be unheard.

Emrys had built me a platform of planks—level, as it happened; he was improving—and Thomas stood beside it holding the copybook against his chest like a breastplate.

I opened the ledger, the original, its pages soft as cloth now from four years of handling, and I read the preface I had composed a year ago in the dark outside the firelight:

*I was a nurse at Harrowgate, and these are the men the Army said were dead.*

Then I began with the first name, and gave it slowly, clearly, the way one speaks to a person and not a record.

"Wren, Thomas. Corporal, Third Battalion. Alive. Restored."

A cheer went up from the loose formation, and Thomas, to his eternal credit, did not look away.

"Ochs, Samuel. Private. Alive. Restored."

"Reed, Aldous. Private. Alive. Restored."

"Fenn, William. Drummer boy, aged fifteen." My voice caught, as it always would, and I let it catch, because the crowd should hear that too. "Alive. Found apprentice to a wheelwright in Derby. Restored."

Name after name. Some drew cheers. Some drew weeping from women in the crowd who had lit funeral fires for sons now standing awkwardly among the living. Some drew nothing but my own voice carrying across the square, steady, unhurried, and that was enough, because a name spoken into silence is not wasted—it settles somewhere and waits.

It took two hours. My throat gave out at the hundredth name and Tilly came up onto the platform and read the next ten herself, her young voice ringing off the shopfronts, and I stood beside her and understood that this was the whole of it, this passing of the book from hand to hand, from throat to throat, until no colonel's pen could ever gather the names up again.

When the last name was read, I closed the ledger and looked out at the square, at the faces of the remembered and the people who had come to remember with them.

Five years ago I had learned which silences keep people safe. Now I knew the other thing, the thing I would tell anyone who asked for the rest of my life: some silences are graves, and the only way to empty a grave is to speak over it until the ground gives the dead back.

The fiddlers struck up—someone had thought to hire fiddlers—and the square turned into something between a fair and a homecoming. Thomas was surrounded by men clasping his scarred hand. Emrys stood at the platform's edge waiting for me, and offered his arm as if I were a general reviewing troops.

"Same time next year?" he said.

"Every year," I said. "Until there's no one left to read back."

And there wasn't a day afterward—not one, not to the end of my life—when someone, somewhere in England, didn't say one of those names aloud, and hear it answered.
