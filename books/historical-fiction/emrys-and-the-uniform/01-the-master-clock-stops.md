## 1. The Master Clock Stops

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Coral Ashgrove would remember later, because Tuesdays were the day Tobias Wrenn inspected the shop's escapements and found fault with all of them, and so the knock at the door came in the middle of a lecture about the moral failings of cheap brass.

"Apprentice," Wrenn said, not looking up from the movement he held to the light, "there's someone at your door. Unless you've taken to knocking on it yourself, which I wouldn't put past you."

Coral wiped his hands on his apron — a habit, since his hands were never truly clean in that shop, only less greasy — and opened the door to a boy of perhaps twelve, breathless from the hill road, holding out an envelope sealed with grey wax. The wax bore the impress of an armillary sphere. The observatory seal. Coral had seen it exactly once before, five years ago, when a summons had come for Wrenn himself, and Wrenn had climbed the hill alone and returned saying nothing except that the great clock was "temperamental as a bishop."

The envelope was addressed to *Mr. C. Ashgrove, Journeyman Horologist* — journeyman, though he'd been apprenticed ten years and never formally advanced, because Wrenn believed titles were like mainsprings: dangerous if overwound.

He broke the seal with his thumbnail.

*Mr. Ashgrove — The master clock at Vellmarsh Observatory has ceased regulation. Previous interventions unsuccessful. You are requested to attend at your earliest convenience. Bring your own tools. — R. Quill, Keeper.*

"Requested," Wrenn said, when Coral read it aloud. He set down the movement at last and looked at his apprentice over the rims of his spectacles. "Not summoned. Requested. That's either courtesy or a trap."

"It's a clock that won't run."

"All clocks run," Wrenn said. "It's people who stop." He turned back to his bench, and Coral understood the conversation was over, which was how most of their conversations ended — not concluded but simply allowed to run down, like a movement left unwound.

But that night, packing his tools by lamplight, Coral caught Wrenn watching him from the doorway of the back room, and there was something in the old man's face he couldn't name. Not worry exactly. Something more like a man listening to a sound he can't yet identify.

"Mind the third wheel," Wrenn said finally. "In anything. Clocks or otherwise. It's always the third wheel where they hide the trouble."

"The third wheel?"

"Everyone looks at the escapement and the mainspring. Nobody looks at what connects them." He tapped his own chest, twice, and went to bed.

---

Vellmarsh sat in the crook of two hills like an egg in a spoon, garrison town, market town, garrison again — the army had been there so long the townsfolk had stopped noticing the uniforms the way one stops noticing weather. The observatory crowned the higher hill, white dome catching the morning sun, and below it the town spilled down toward the river in grey slate roofs and chimney smoke. From the summit road Coral could see the whole arrangement: church, barracks, market square, the bridge over the Velle with its stone arches, and beyond, the flat country running east toward the capital, three days' travel by coach.

A soldier in observatory livery — odd combination, that, army cloth cut to civilian pattern — met him at the gate.

"Ashgrove? Keeper's expecting you." The man looked at Coral's tool case the way soldiers look at everything: assessing weight, assessing threat. "You'll sign the visitors' book."

"I'll sign whatever needs signing."

Inside, the observatory smelled of cold stone and lamp oil and, beneath both, the particular scent of a large mechanism under strain — hot metal, worn brass, the faint sweetness of oil burning off somewhere it shouldn't. Coral had spent ten years learning to read buildings the way other men read faces, and this building was telling him something already. It was telling him it had been neglected carefully. There was a difference between neglect and careful neglect, and he'd learned that difference at Wrenn's bench: dust that fell naturally lay in drifts; dust that had been disturbed and allowed to resettle lay in fingerprints.

The keeper, Roderick Quill, was a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and the harried look of someone who had been awake for several days in succession without admitting it to anyone. His office was crammed with star charts and correspondence, and every surface had been recently straightened, papers squared, nothing left loose.

"Ashgrove. Good. Good." He shook Coral's hand without warmth, as though shaking hands were a formality invented by people with time to spare. "You come recommended by Wrenn. That's recommendation enough. The clock's stopped keeping true — losing four minutes a day, then six, then yesterday it simply stood. I've had two men look at it. Neither could say why."

"Where are they now?"

Quill's eyes flicked up, just once. "Gone. Reassigned." He said it too quickly, the way a man says a word he's rehearsed. "The clock matters, Ashgrove. More than you'd think. The garrison sets its watches by us. The railway sets its signals by us. When we lose time, everyone downstream loses it after us, and no one upstairs tolerates confusion about time. Time is order. Order is—" He stopped himself, smiled thinly. "You'll want to see it."

They descended a spiral stair into the clock room, and Coral felt the change in air pressure the way divers feel it — a settling, a hush. The room was circular, stone-floored, dominated by the master clock itself: nine feet of case in black walnut, glass panels revealing a movement of gleaming brass and steel, weights hanging silent on their cables, pendulum still.

Silent. That was the first wrongness. A stopped clock is not quiet; a stopped clock is a held breath. This room had been quiet so long the silence had gone stale.

"When did it stop?"

"Three weeks ago tomorrow."

"Not last night. Not gradually over months. Three weeks ago tomorrow, precisely."

Quill shifted. "The men noted the date."

"And the men are gone." Coral set down his case and knelt before the lower glass panel. "Keeper, I'm going to ask you questions that will seem impertinent. Answer them anyway. Has anyone worked on this clock besides the two men who've been reassigned?"

"No."

"Has anyone asked to work on it?"

Another flicker. Faster this time. "No."

"You're lying, sir, but I'll let it lie, because I don't need you to tell me. The clock will." He took out his loupe, screwed it into his eye, and opened the panel.

The movement was beautiful — Wrenn-quality work, older, from the hand of some master dead thirty years, maintained with devotion until recently. And then Coral saw the trouble, and his professional pride, which had been warming in him like a banked fire, went suddenly cold.

The third wheel was jammed.

Not broken. Not worn. Jammed — a sliver of steel shimmed between its teeth and the pinion above it, wedged with a precision that made his stomach turn, because only a clockmaker could have done it, and only a clockmaker would have done it *that way*. A saboteur breaks things. A craftsman stops them gently, so gently the mechanism isn't damaged, so gently that any competent repairer can restore function in an hour — which meant whoever did this wanted the clock stopped temporarily, reversibly, and wanted it to look like wear.

And around the jam, arranged with the same loving precision, the gears had been repositioned. Coral traced the train upward with his eyes, counting teeth, checking depths, and found that the fourth wheel had been lifted off its pivot and turned a quarter-inch, and beneath the fourth wheel, in the space that quarter-inch exposed, there was a gap in the back plate of the movement that should not exist.

A compartment. Hidden behind the works, accessible only when the train was disarranged in exactly this fashion — which was why the clock had been stopped this way, and why the two repairmen before him had been unable to find the fault. They'd looked for damage. There was none. There was only intention.

Coral sat back on his heels. His heart was doing something unprofessional.

"Mister Ashgrove?" Quill, behind him. "Do you see the problem?"

"I see a problem." He kept his voice level, bored even, the voice Wrenn used when a customer tried to haggle. "Wear in the train. I'll need the afternoon. Possibly longer. I work best alone — hands in the way, you understand."

"Of course. Of course." But Quill lingered, and Coral, still bent to the movement, watched the man's reflection in the polished brass of the great wheel. The keeper's face was doing arithmetic it didn't like the answer to. Then he left, and his footsteps on the spiral stair were faster going up than coming down.

Coral waited until the sound faded entirely. Then he waited another five minutes, because ten years of apprenticeship had taught him that haste was the enemy of everything, including survival.

He thought about Wrenn's voice: *it's always the third wheel where they hide the trouble.* The old man had never been inside this observatory in five years. And yet.

He thought about the two reassigned repairmen. Men didn't get reassigned for failing to fix a clock. They got reassigned for finding something while fixing one.

He thought about the fingerprints in the dust.

Then he took up his pliers, lifted the fourth wheel from its pivot with the tenderness of a man handling a sleeping child, and turned it the quarter-inch that opened the hidden space in the back plate.

Inside was a book.

Not a mechanism part, not a tool, not the love letters or smuggled coin he might have imagined an hour ago. A ledger — real leather, real binding, the kind used for accounts, for registers, for records that matter. It fit the compartment exactly, the way a part fits a housing it was made for. Someone had measured this space for this book.

Coral drew it out and set it on his knee, and the weight of it was wrong, heavier than paper should be, and he understood that the weight was history, was consequence, was the thing Wrenn meant when he talked about silences that protect people and silences that bury them.

His thumb rested on the cover. Through the spiral stair above, faintly, came the sound of boots — more than one pair, descending slowly, pausing, descending.

Someone had wanted this clock stopped. Someone had hidden this book where only a clockmaker would ever find it, and had trusted the finding to a craftsman's eye rather than chance. And now, three weeks late, the finder had arrived — with the sound of military boots on the stair, and the whole afternoon ahead of him, and no idea at all whether opening the cover would save anyone or damn everyone, himself included.

Coral Ashgrove, who had learned ten years of silences at another man's bench, closed his hand around the ledger and slid it, in one smooth motion, inside his coat.
