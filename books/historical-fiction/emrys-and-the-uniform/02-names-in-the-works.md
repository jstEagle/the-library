## 2. Names in the Works

The boots on the stair passed by.

Coral stood motionless in the clock room with the ledger pressed against his ribs, listening as the footsteps climbed past the landing, up toward the dome where the assistant keeper was coiling rope around the telescope mount. A voice called something about tarps. Another answered. The army had come to Vellmarsh's observatory the way weather comes — announced for days, arriving without malice, taking up space.

He waited until the sounds settled into the ordinary noise of men at work, then climbed the narrow stair to the old head horologist's workshop, a room at the tower's shoulder that no one had entered, if the dust on the sill meant anything, since Aldric Vane had died in it fourteen months ago.

Coral had never met Vane. He knew him the way one craftsman knows another across distance — by reputation, by the tools he'd left behind, by the famous regulators in the transit room that still held their rate to within a half-second a week. Wrenn had spoken of him sometimes, in the careful tone he reserved for men he respected but hadn't liked. *Vane was a man who counted everything*, Wrenn would say. *Counting is a virtue in our trade until it becomes a vice in every other.*

The workshop was small and fiercely ordered. Files hung in graded rows. Brass shavings had been swept into a pan and never emptied, frozen mid-chore by death or whatever had come just before it. On the bench sat a movement clamped in the holder, its train half-disassembled, the pallet fork set down precisely where a hand had lifted from it.

Coral did not touch any of it. He sat down on Vane's stool instead, drew the ledger from inside his coat, and set it on his knees like something that might still be returned.

It was an ordinary ledger. That was the first wrongness about it — not a fine bound book with a lock, nothing that said *secret*, only a stationer's account book of the kind every workshop in the country bought by the dozen, its canvas cover gray with handling. The spine had been rubbed soft. Someone had opened this thing hundreds of times.

He turned the cover.

The first page was dated four years earlier, and it was not prose at all. It was columns. Coral came from a family of people who read columns the way priests read scripture — his eye went down the ruled lines automatically, sorting, summing — and it took him three lines to understand what he was looking at, and when he understood it, his body reacted before his mind did, a cold slide in the stomach, his hand flattening against the page as if to hold it shut.

Names.

A name on each line, in Vane's small, exact, horologist's hand — the hand of a man who lettered dial numerals for a living, so that every name looked engraved. Beside each name, a date of birth. Beside that, an address. And beside that, in a code of three letters Coral did not yet know but would learn by heart before the night was out, a classification.

*REQ.* Requisition.
*CON.* Conscription.
And the third mark, which appeared less often and always alone, with no figure after it and no annotation, which Vane had written — Coral was sure of it, though he could not have said how — more slowly than all the others:

*ERS.*

Erasure.

* * *

He read for two hours. He did not mean to; there was no intention left in it after the first ten minutes. Reading became a kind of falling, each page a step down into colder air.

The ledger ran alphabetically, but not quite. It began alphabetical and then broke its own order, names inserted out of sequence in different inks — brown-black, then blue, then a grayish iron-gall that had bitten through the paper — and Coral understood, with the part of his mind that still worked like a craftsman's, that Vane had been copying from another source, updating, revising. This was not the original record. This was a copy. Which meant somewhere there was a master list, and somewhere behind that, an author.

Four hundred and six names. He counted them because counting was the only thing keeping his hands steady. Two hundred and sixty-one marked *CON* — boys and men of fighting age, mill workers, farm hands, a schoolteacher, two of the three sons of the Ferris family who ran the ferry. Ninety-eight marked *REQ* with figures after them — figures in pounds or in head of livestock or, once, chillingly, in *beds*, thirty-two beds requisitioned from the Almshouse of Saint Brinna, which had precisely thirty-two beds.

Forty-seven names were marked *ERS*. No figure followed those. Nothing to take. Nothing to count. Just the name, the birth date, the address, and the slow-inked mark, and then — Coral checked, flipping back and forth with growing horror — blankness. For the *ERS* entries there were no further annotations anywhere in the book. The others accumulated notes over the years, marginal dates, corrections, small tallying marks like the chalk strokes a clockmaker makes on a case while regulating. The erased names got nothing. As if once the word was written, the man or woman under it stopped being a matter of record.

He found people he knew.

That was the moment the ledger stopped being paper and became Vellmarsh. Page nineteen, halfway down: *Halloway, Marta — b. 1867 — Candle Street — ERS.* Mrs. Halloway, who sold candles and soap and lent money quietly against pawned watches, who had once let twelve-year-old Coral warm himself in her shop for an hour when his mother was late collecting him, and had never mentioned it afterward. Erased. The word sat beside her name with the terrible neatness of a stamped receipt.

Page twenty-three: *Quill, Edmund — b. 1891 — Tanner's Row — CON.* Eddie Quill, who had delivered flour to Wrenn's shop every Thursday for years and whistled badly and was, as far as Coral knew, the gentlest man in the eastern parish.

Page thirty-one, near the bottom, in the oldest ink, added in a break in the alphabetical flow as if it had been among the first things ever entered: *Ashgrove, Elias — b. 1849 — Clockmaker's Row — ERS.*

Coral read his father's name four times.

His father had been dead nine years. Dead of a bad heart and a lifetime of lamp-oil fumes, dead in his chair with a watch in his hand, dead so ordinarily that the vicar had spoken warmly at the funeral. There was nothing left to erase. Unless — and here Coral's mind turned a corner he did not want it to turn — erasure was not something that happened to you all at once. Unless it had already been done, quietly, years ago, to a man too sick to notice, and the ledger was simply Vane's copy of a sentence already carried out. He thought suddenly, absurdly, of the parish register, of trying to recall whether he had ever actually seen his parents' marriage line with his own eyes, or whether he knew it existed only because everyone had always said it did.

The clock above him ticked through all of this with its repaired, obedient heart, and Coral sat in a dead man's workshop holding a dead man's book and understood that he was reading a list of the dead-to-be, arranged in advance with a craftsman's precision, and that his own family name had been in the works for four years while he sat at a bench downstairs learning silences.

* * *

There was a letter folded into the back cover.

He almost missed it — the fold was thin, the paper matched the ledger's endpaper — and when he found the edge and pulled it free, he saw it had been opened and refolded many times, worn along the crease like a prayer book's page.

It was addressed to no one. It began mid-thought, in the same engraved hand:

*— have confirmed it a third time, which I take to be final. The quartermaster's office at Eastreach holds the master rolls. What I keep here is copied from what passes through the garrison paymaster, whose clerk sells sight of it for two crowns a month and does not know what he sells.*

*I have considered destroying my copy and have decided against it, for reasons I will set down plainly, so that whoever finds this understands they were good reasons and not merely an old man's vanity.*

*A list that exists nowhere cannot be argued with. When they come for a man whose papers say he was never born, what will he produce to contradict them? His neighbors' memories are not evidence; memories fade with the men who hold them. But ink endures, and ink in sufficient quantity frightens the sort of people who run armies, because armies run on the fiction that everything is counted, and a count that contradicts theirs is a weapon.*

*So I keep the count. I copy every name that passes through that clerk's greasy hands, and I add what I can learn from the town itself, and I wait for the day the count becomes useful. If that day comes after my death, then whoever reads this should know the rules I worked under, so that my errors may be forgiven as honest ones:*

*I never wrote a name I could not verify from two sources. I never showed the book to anyone in Vellmarsh. I never told even the ones I loved most, because a secret shared by two is a secret halved, and halved again at each telling after. Whoever you are, you found this by craft, which means the craft chose you as it once chose me. Take it somewhere it can be printed. Ink is patient. People are not.*

*A.V.*

Below the signature, in fresher ink, one more line had been added — shakier, the hand of a man writing against time:

*If I am taken, the book stays in the works. Only a clockmaker will look there.*

Coral sat with the letter in both hands for a long time.

So Vane had known he might be taken. Not *might die* — everyone knew that — but *taken*, the way one speaks of mechanisms seized by the crown, impounded, decommissioned. And the heart failure recorded on his death certificate, the peaceful death in the workshop that the whole town had accepted without question — Coral turned the facts over carefully, like a suspicious movement under magnification, and made himself look at what he saw. A man who keeps a secret for four years, who refuses to show it to anyone, who dies alone at his bench fourteen months ago, weeks before the army arrives to seize every record in the district — and no one asks a single question, because no one knows there is a question to ask.

Wrenn had gone to the funeral. Wrenn had come back quiet and had not spoken Vane's name since. Coral thought of that silence now — one of the ten years of silences he had learned at the old man's bench — and wondered how many of them had this shape. Wondered whether Wrenn had suspected. Whether that was why Wrenn, three weeks ago, hearing the master clock had failed, had sent his apprentice instead of going himself. Whether sending Coral had been protection or sacrifice, and whether there was any difference anymore.

No. He stopped that thought deliberately, the way you ease a mainspring off its click before it strips. Wrenn had sent him because Wrenn's knees were bad and the hill was steep. Some silences were just tired legs. He would not build a conspiracy out of an old man's arthritis.

But the cold fact remained at the center of the room, turning slowly like a governor coming up to speed: forty-seven people in Vellmarsh had been sentenced to nonexistence, and Coral Ashgrove was now the only living soul outside the army who knew their names.

* * *

The light in the workshop had gone amber and long when voices rose below — the army officers concluding their survey of the dome. Coral folded the letter back into the cover, wrapped the ledger in the clean rag he used for polishing, and stood a moment in Vane's doorway, looking back at the ordered bench, the swept-and-unemptied pan of brass, the half-finished movement in its clamp waiting fourteen months for a hand that would never return.

*Thank you,* he said silently, because there was no one else to say it to, and because a craftsman honors the work of the dead by acknowledging it. Then he went down through the clock room, where the great mechanism he had spent three days restoring ticked on in perfect, innocent order, and out onto the stair, where a young lieutenant with a clipboard was coming up.

They passed within arm's length. The lieutenant glanced at him — at the tool roll under one arm, the rag-wrapped bundle under the other, the apprentice's plain coat — and looked away with the mild incuriosity of a man cataloguing furniture.

"Repair work's finished," Coral said, and was proud of how level it came out. "The clock will keep better time than it has in twenty years."

"See that it does." The lieutenant was already climbing. The army had no interest in clocks that worked.

The road down from the observatory runs steep and white through gorse toward the town, and Coral took it at a pace just short of running, because his body needed to move and his mind needed desperately not to think, and the two impulses canceled into a kind of numb forward motion. Below him Vellmarsh lay in evening light — lamps being lit along Candle Street, smoke rising straight from bakery chimneys, the ferry lanterns doubling themselves in the river. Four hundred and six names. He found he could not look at any single window without wondering which page of the book lived behind it.

Mrs. Halloway was sweeping her step as he passed. She raised her broom in her usual wry salute. *ERS*, said the ink in his memory, in that slow deliberate hand, and Coral nodded to her and kept walking and felt like a man carrying water past a housefire.

At home — the two rooms above Wrenn's shop that had been his since apprenticeship — he ate bread and cheese standing up without tasting either, and then did the thing he had been circling all the way down the hill. He cleared his bench. He laid the ledger open upon it under the lamp, took out his finest graver and a sheet of the thin Japanese tissue paper Wrenn hoarded for dial transfers, and began to think about copying.

Because the letter had said it plainly: *take it somewhere it can be printed.* But the ledger weighed as much as a brick and told as much, and the roads out of Vellmarsh, he had seen with his own eyes that afternoon, now had soldiers on them. A book could be searched. A book could be seized. A book could burn.

He worked until well past midnight, tracing names, inventing as he went a cramped cipher of his own devising, and slept two hours with the ledger beneath his mattress and his father's name pressed against the small of his back.

He woke to bells.

Not the clock — the clock was right, he had set it himself — but the church bells of Vellmarsh ringing at six in the morning when they had no business ringing at all, and under them, faint but unmistakable, the measured percussion of a marching column coming up the capital road.

Coral was at the window with the ledger already in his coat before his mind finished forming the thought that finished the chapter of his old life: they were not coming for the town gradually, with notices and procedures, the way law arrived.

They had come overnight. They were here now. And somewhere down on the market square, a drummer boy was beating the assembly while a sergeant with a voice like a slate scraper read out, to a town still fastening its buttons, the first of the requisition orders.

Coral buttoned his coat over the book, took up his tool roll, and went down to see what the new world looked like.
