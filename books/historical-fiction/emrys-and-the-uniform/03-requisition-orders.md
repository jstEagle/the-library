## 3. Requisition Orders

Coral Ashgrove woke to the sound of boots.

Not one pair, not the shuffling tread of the milkman or the measured pace of the night watchman making his rounds along Cordwainer's Row, but many pairs — hundreds of them — striking cobbles in unison somewhere beyond the shuttered window of his room above Mrs. Peverell's house. The rhythm was wrong for men. Men walked in fits and starts; they paused, they coughed, they shifted a heavy load from one shoulder to another. This was a single sound, a machine-sound, and Coral had spent ten years learning to hear machines.

He lay still in the grey hour before dawn and counted. Four beats to the bar, maybe five hundred bodies moving down the market road toward the square. He had heard troop movements described by veterans who drank at the Turk's Head — old Sergeant Maddox with his ruined knee, holding forth about column formations as though describing weather — and what Coral heard now matched nothing in those stories. This was not a passing column. Columns passed. This was an arrival.

The boots faded toward the river end of town, where the garrison lay behind its low brick walls like a fist kept loosely closed. Then came another sound: hammers. Iron on wood, methodical, patient. Nails going into boards.

Coral dressed in the dark, hands finding each button without instruction, and went downstairs to the kitchen he had shared with Mrs. Peverell's household for three years. She was already there. That alone frightened him more than the boots had. Mrs. Peverell rose at six, never earlier; her clockwork was as reliable as anything in his toolbox. But she stood now at the cold hearth in her dressing gown, arms folded tight, watching the window as if the glass might tell her something the dark withheld.

"You heard it too," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I heard nails."

"Boards." She nodded once, slow. "They're boarding up Harker's print shop. I could see the lanterns from the landing. Two soldiers and a corporal, working their way down the shutters like carpenters." Her voice held very steady, the way a clock case holds steady around machinery that has begun to run fast. "At this hour. Before light."

Coral put the kettle on because his hands wanted occupation. The small ritual — fill, set, strike the match — bought him thirty seconds of ordinary in a morning that had none. Through the kitchen window Vellmarsh lay under a sky the colour of unpolished pewter, and against that sky, on the market road, he could see them now: ranks of soldiers in field grey, supply wagons with canvas tilts dark with dew, and two mounted officers sitting their horses at the corner of the square like chess pieces someone had placed and then forgotten to move.

"They came over the ridge after midnight," Mrs. Peverell said. "The Hendersons' boy ran down from the farm at first light to say there were more on the north road. Wagons, guns, the whole apparatus." She turned from the window and looked at Coral directly, and something in her face made him think of the ledger hidden upstairs inside the lining of his tool satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, weighing nothing and everything. "Whole apparatus," she repeated. "That's not a patrol, Coral. That's an occupation."

The kettle began its low climb toward a whistle, and Coral took it off before it could sing. Silence, suddenly, seemed valuable. He did not know yet how valuable. He would learn.

* * *

By nine o'clock the notices were going up.

Coral watched the first of them pasted to the noticeboard outside the corn exchange, brushed on with flour-paste by a soldier who worked with the brisk unhappiness of a man assigned a task beneath his rank. The sheet was large, printed in a heavy serif hand, headed with the army's stamp and a preamble of legal language that managed to be both wordy and vague. Coral read it twice while the market crowd gathered around him in a widening half-circle that nobody spoke within.

BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL MILITARY ADMINISTRATION OF THE VELLMARSH DISTRICT:

All civic records, ledgers, registers, rolls, accounts, and lists of every description — including but not limited to parish registers, guild books, trade apprenticeship rolls, property assessments, tax schedules, employment records, and correspondence of municipal bodies — are to be surrendered to designated requisition officers no later than six o'clock on the evening of Friday next.

Failure to comply will be treated as obstruction of military administration.

Citizens are reminded that accurate records protect lawful persons.

That last line did more work than all the rest combined. Coral heard it land in the crowd the way a cracked bell lands — a wrongness felt in the chest rather than understood. *Accurate records protect lawful persons.* As though the records were being taken for the town's own good. As though names were a kind of armour the army intended to fit everyone with, whether it suited them or not.

Old Mrs. Tallow, who sold eggs and had sold eggs since before Coral was born, squinted at the notice and asked no one in particular, "What counts as a ledger? I keep my egg money in a exercise book."

"Then you'd best surrender your exercise book," said Mr. Dantry the ironmonger, without humour.

"They can't want *egg accounts*," Mrs. Tallow said, but she said it uncertainly, and Coral saw her begin, in some private chamber of herself, to worry.

He left the crowd before his face could tell anyone anything. He walked home the long way, past the print shop with its fresh-boarded windows, past the parish church where a lieutenant stood on the steps conferring with a curate whose shoulders had the defeated slope of a man being shown a document he could not refuse, past the guildhall, where two soldiers were carrying filing cabinets out through the doors with the careful indifference of furniture movers. The cabinets were locked. It didn't seem to matter.

Every register in Vellmarsh, thought Coral, walking faster. Every roll, every list, every book with names in it. They were sweeping the town's memory into wagons, and they wanted it done by Friday, politely, by the townsfolk's own hands.

Which meant, said a colder voice in him, the voice of the works themselves, of escapements and mainsprings and the honest arithmetic of gears — which meant they knew something was written down somewhere that they had not seen yet. You do not seize every record in a district unless you fear a record exists that you do not control.

The ledger in his satchel pressed against his hip like a hand.

* * *

Mrs. Peverell surrendered her household book on Wednesday.

She did it with the particular dignity she brought to everything — hat pinned straight, gloves on, the book tied in brown paper and string like a parcel for posting — and she came back from the requisition desk at the guildhall pale around the mouth but composed. Coral had the kettle going again when she returned, and tea ready, and for a while they sat either side of the kitchen table saying nothing, which by then had become a language between them.

"There was a list," she said at last. "Pinned alongside the surrender notice. Names." She unwound her string, folded her brown paper into a neat square though there was nothing left to wrap. "Posted names. People of interest to the administration, it said. My name is on it, Coral."

The words went through him like a punch pin through a watch plate — small, precise, devastating.

"Yours—" He stopped. Started again. "Why would your name—"

"I don't know." She looked at her folded paper. "I've kept lodgers for twenty years. Students, clerks, a travelling dentist, and now you. I've never troubled anyone. I pay my rates. I vote the way my late husband voted, which is to say rarely and forgettably." A breath. "But there it is. Half a column of names and mine among them, third from the bottom. And do you know what struck me, reading it?"

Coral shook his head.

"Not fear. Recognition. I knew four of the other names. The schoolmaster. The woman who runs the refuge on Chandler's Lane. The man from the railway office who helped the Hendersons when their boy went missing from the census two years back — you remember, we spoke of it." She had spoken of it once, obliquely, in the way Vellmarsh people spoke of such things, in lowered voices with the window shut. "Four names out of forty-odd, and every one of them a person who had *helped* somebody. That's the pattern. That's what the list is. It isn't a list of criminals." She met his eyes. "It's a list of people who were paying attention."

Coral held his teacup very still. Ten years in Tobias Wrenn's workshop had taught him that a mechanism tells you everything if you watch long enough — that a gear wearing unevenly, a pallet stone gone glossy, a mainspring set crooked, all confess themselves eventually in the way the hands move. He was watching the mechanism of Vellmarsh now, three days into its occupation, and the confession was everywhere. Shops closing early. Neighbours nodding to one another and then, carefully, not nodding. The Hendersons' shutters staying closed past noon. The town running, but with a new drag in the train, a friction that hadn't been there before, as though something coarse had gotten into the oil.

And underneath all of it, the ledger sat in his room, in his satchel, behind a false panel of his own devising that he had installed Tuesday night with shaking hands and Wrenn's old trick of the waxed dowel.

"Mrs. Peverell," he said quietly, "if they come asking about me—"

"They won't ask," she said. "They'll just come. That's how it works now, I suspect. Asking implies you have a right to refuse." She rose, collected her gloves, smoothed them across her knee. "I want to say something to you, Coral Ashgrove, and I want you to hear it as landlady's advice and nothing else. I'm an old woman and my name is already on their list, so I can afford to say it." She paused at the kitchen door. "Whatever you're carrying — and I don't know that you're carrying anything, and I haven't looked, and I won't — whatever it is, ask yourself whether keeping quiet about it protects anybody anymore. Because silence used to be a kindness in this town. We kept our voices down so the neighbours wouldn't be embarrassed. That was the whole of it. Embarrassment, and nothing worse." Her hand rested on the doorframe a moment. "I don't think embarrassment is what they're collecting."

She went up to bed, and Coral sat alone in the kitchen until the coals settled, turning the question over the way he would turn a doubtful movement in his hands under the loupe, looking for the flaw.

* * *

He went to the observatory on Thursday, ostensibly because the master clock needed regulating again after his repairs — a true enough excuse; the new pallet stones were still bedding in, and the escapement ran a hair fast in damp weather. In truth he went because the observatory was the only place in Vellmarsh where he could think at altitude, and because Tobias Wrenn was there, and because there are conversations a man cannot have in a kitchen.

Wrenn had heard the notices read aloud in the square and had, characteristically, responded by winding every clock in the building and polishing the case glass until it blazed. He listened to Coral's account of the posted list — Mrs. Peverell's name, the schoolmaster, the woman from Chandler's Lane — without interrupting, standing at the master clock's open case with one hand resting flat against the wooden frame as though taking its pulse.

"Forty names," he said at last. "Or forty-odd."

"So she said."

"And every one a helper." Wrenn nodded slowly, eyes on the pendulum's slow arc. "You see what that means, lad? It means they've been counting longer than they've been marching. Nobody draws up a list like that overnight. Somebody's been keeping accounts on this town for years — quietly, in some office, in some book we've never seen." His thumb moved along the clock case, an absent, affectionate gesture. "And now they've come to collect all the other books, so theirs will be the only memory left standing."

"The ledger," Coral said. The word came out lower than he intended. Up here, under the great clock's tick, it felt almost safe to say. Almost. "If they take every register in town and the ledger isn't among them, they'll know it exists. They'll tear Vellmarsh apart looking for it."

"If they take every register in town," said Wrenn, "and the ledger *is* among them, it'll be ash by Saturday, and everyone in it will be exactly where the army wants them — nowhere, nameless, never-was." He turned then and looked at Coral, and his eyes were as clear and unhurried as they had been across a workbench for ten years. "You've been carrying it a week now. How does it sit?"

"Like a fuse."

"Good. A fuse should feel like a fuse." Wrenn took out his pocket watch, opened it, checked it against the master clock out of pure habit — both were correct; they were always correct — and snapped it shut. "Here's your trouble, Coral, and mind, I had the same trouble at your age. You were trained to fix things quietly and tell no one. That's a workshop virtue and a town virtue and mostly it's served you well. But there comes a time when quiet is just another word for complicit, and the only way to find out which kind of quiet you're standing in is to ask who benefits from it." He put the watch away. "Ask yourself that tonight. Not me. Not the ledger. Just you and the question."

Coral stayed while the clock struck noon, twelve notes rolling down the hill and over the rooftops of the occupied town below, and then he walked home through streets where every board, every list, every locked cabinet seemed to lean inward toward him.

Friday's deadline hung over Vellmarsh like weather. Tomorrow at six o'clock, the last of the town's books would go into the wagons, and the administration's memory would stand alone. After that, every hour the ledger remained hidden was an accusation waiting for a reader.

He let himself into Mrs. Peverell's house as the light failed. On the hall table, folded neatly beside the fruit bowl, he found a note in her upright, unhurried hand:

*Coral — two men called while you were out. Civil clothes, but boots that march. They asked how long you'd lodged here, where you'd trained, whether you received visitors. I answered plainly and they wrote things down. Your supper is in the oven. Whatever you decide, decide soon. They were writing down my answers as though your name belonged on a list too.*

He stood in the hallway a long moment, the note in one hand, the other resting flat against the stair rail, and above him, at the top of the house, behind a waxed dowel and a panel of his own making, the ledger waited with its hundreds of names — requisitioned, conscripted, erased — for someone to stop being silent about them.

Coral took off his coat, went upstairs, and reached for the satchel.
