## 4. An Officer at the Door

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, which was the first wrong thing about it.

Coral knew the observatory's rhythms the way he knew the ticking of his own heart. In the three days since the columns had come down from the north — boots on cobbles, banners stiff with frost, the whole town holding its breath behind shuttered windows — the hilltop had learned a new schedule. Soldiers came at dawn to post their notices on the observatory door. They came at noon to smoke on the terrace and spit over the balustrade at the town below. They did not, as a rule, knock. The army of Vellmarsh garrison had not yet learned that a door was anything other than an obstacle awaiting orders.

But this knock was measured. Three raps, evenly spaced, with a pause between each one long enough for a man inside to answer. It was a civilian's knock. A gentleman's knock.

Coral set down his tweezers. On the bench before him lay the escapement of the master clock, disassembled into forty-one pieces arranged in strict rows on a square of grey velvet, each piece cleaned and waiting. He had been working on it for two days with a concentration that was only partly honest. The truth was that his hands needed occupation or they would shake, and the other truth sat six feet away in his leather satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, weighing more than any ledger had a right to weigh.

Three hundred and eleven names. He had counted them twice, then a third time because he could not believe the second count. Requisitioned. Conscripted. Erased. The last word was the late head horologist Aldous Vane's own, written in a small, precise hand beside names that had no explanation at all — no seizure of property, no draft notice, nothing but a date and a single word, *erased*, as though the men and women themselves had been removed from the town like scratches polished out of brass.

Coral crossed the workroom, paused with his hand on the latch, and made himself breathe.

*You are a clockmaker. A clockmaker has been summoned. There is nothing else in this room.*

He opened the door.

The officer on the step was young — younger than Coral had expected, though he could not have said what he'd expected. Twenty-five, perhaps twenty-six. Tall without being lanky, with the kind of posture that looked bred rather than drilled, and dark hair cut close beneath a peaked cap that sat slightly too straight, as if its owner wore it out of obligation rather than belief. His uniform was immaculate: lieutenant's pips, garrison colours, boots that reflected the grey morning light. Everything about him said *the army, precisely rendered.*

Everything except his eyes, which were doing something Coral had never seen a soldier's eyes do in Vellmarsh. They were reading the room before they read the man. Moving past Coral's shoulder to the benches, the tools, the tall case of the master clock standing open like a patient on a table.

"Lieutenant Hale," the officer said. "Emrys Hale. I'm instructed to conduct a full inventory of the observatory's contents." He produced a folded paper, official seal, the ink still sharp. "By order of Colonel Vasse."

"Inventory," Coral repeated. His voice came out level, which surprised him. Ten years under Tobias Wrenn had taught him that level was a thing you built, plank by plank, under load. "Of what, Lieutenant? The clocks?"

"Of everything." Hale stepped inside without waiting for invitation — not rudely, Coral noted, but with the automatic authority of a man who had never once been told no by a doorway. "Records, instruments, correspondence, personal effects. The colonel wants a complete accounting before the end of the week."

"The end of the week." That was when the notices said all ledgers and registers were to be surrendered. Coral had stood in the market square two mornings ago and watched Mrs. Halloway the fishwife read the posted list with her lips moving, and had seen her find her own name among the requisitioned, and had seen her go very quiet and fold her hands and walk home the long way, past the church, as if hoping God might amend the document. "That's a great deal of inventory, Lieutenant. The observatory holds sixty years of the Vane workshop's papers."

"I'm aware." Hale had stopped at the main bench. He was looking at the escapement pieces on their velvet, and something in his face shifted — a flicker, quickly suppressed, that Coral would spend the rest of the day trying to name. Recognition, maybe. Or longing. "You're the apprentice who was summoned to repair the clock. Ashgrove."

"Coral Ashgrove."

"You fixed it?"

"It runs."

"How long did it take you?"

"A day and a half." Coral kept his hands visible and idle, one resting on the bench edge. "The mainspring hadn't failed, whatever your engineers reported. Someone had packed the gear train with wax and grit. Deliberately."

Hale turned his head slowly. For the first time he looked directly at Coral, and Coral felt the look like a hand laid flat against his sternum — not pushing, just pressing, testing what gave. "Deliberately," Hale repeated.

"There are ways a mechanism fails and ways it's made to fail. I know the difference. Any journeyman does."

"And did you report this?"

"To whom?" The question escaped before Coral could dress it in politeness, and he saw the corner of Hale's mouth move — not quite a smile, more the ghost of where a smile might once have lived. "I reported it to the observatory's records. Which I understand are now the army's records."

"They are." Hale unbuttoned his cuff and began, methodically, to roll both sleeves to the elbow. It was such an unexpected gesture that Coral simply stared. "Then we'll begin. You'll assist me. You know what these things are; I only know what my orders say they should be." He drew a notebook from his breast pocket and clicked a pencil. "Start with the far wall. Name every instrument. Don't omit anything, however small. Especially the small things."

*Especially the small things.* The words went through Coral like a probe through a keyhole.

They worked. That was the strange, terrible ordinary thing about the next two hours: they worked. Hale moved through the observatory with a thoroughness that was almost respectful, listing barometers and orreries and the great transit telescope with its brass fittings gone green at the joints, and Coral named each item and watched the pencil move and understood that every entry was a nail going into a coffin lid. Once the inventory existed, the observatory would be sealed. Whatever was hidden here would be hidden under army lock, and the man who had hidden it was dead, and the only living soul who knew what slept in the works of the master clock was standing in this room answering questions in a steady voice while his heart performed the fastest escapement of its career.

Hale asked few questions beyond the inventory itself. But each one landed precisely.

"Vane kept the workshop registers himself?"

"Every entry in his own hand."

"No assistants recording?"

"He trusted no one else's figures." This was true, and Coral delivered it steadily, and hated how easily the truth served the lie.

"Hm." Hale wrote. Then, without looking up: "Where are those registers now?"

Coral's hands, folding a cleaning cloth, did not stop moving. He had prepared for this question for three days. He had prepared four answers. What came out was the fifth, the one he invented in the space of a heartbeat: "In the archive room, presumably. I haven't touched them. They're not clockwork."

"Presumably." Hale's pencil paused. "We'll check there next, then."

It was while Hale was measuring the archive shelves — Coral carrying the lantern for him, playing the docile assistant, hating himself for how naturally the obedience came — that the disaster happened, and it happened not through error but through habit, which is how all true disasters happen to craftsmen.

A soldier's whistle sounded from the terrace outside, two notes, and Hale stepped to the window to answer some shouted question about transport schedules, and Coral, left alone beside the open satchel he had carried everywhere for three days like a second spine, did the thing his hands had done a hundred times in Wrenn's shop when an unexpected customer walked in: he swept the loose item off the bench and into his coat.

The ledger was not loose. The ledger was wrapped in oilcloth and buried in the satchel's false bottom, and Coral knew this, and his hands knew it too — but the body is a stupid animal in crisis, and the body decided, in the quarter-second of Hale's turned back, that the weight of the thing against his ribs was safer than the weight of it in plain sight, and so Coral Ashgrove stood in the archive room of the Vellmarsh observatory with three hundred and eleven stolen names pressed against his chest, and heard boots turn on stone behind him.

He did not turn around. Turning around would have been an admission. He stood very still, facing the shelf, listening to the footsteps cross the room with the unhurried precision of a pendulum, and stop.

"Lieutenant," Coral said, to the shelf. "The shelving is elm. Sixty years old. It tends to—"

"Open your coat."

The voice was quiet. That was the worst of it. No bark, no threat, none of the parade-ground theatre the garrison soldiers performed for the fishwives. Just a flat, controlled request, the way a man asks for a tool.

Coral closed his eyes. Behind them he saw the ledger's pages: the dates, the initials, the word *erased* repeated like a refrain. He thought of Mrs. Halloway walking home the long way. He thought of Tobias Wrenn, who had told him on his first day, *a clockmaker's first duty is to the truth of the mechanism, lad, and his second is to know when not to say so aloud.*

He opened his coat.

The oilcloth bundle sat in the inner pocket where his traitor hands had put it, absurdly visible, absurdly obvious, the wrapping already slipping so that the edge of a page showed — a page dense with that small precise handwriting, columns of figures, a name.

The silence lasted four seconds. Coral counted them, because counting was what he did when the world came apart; he counted the beats of things.

"Turn around," Hale said.

Coral turned.

The lieutenant stood an arm's length away, and his face was doing something complicated and mostly invisible, the way a movement does inside a sealed gear train — you can't see the wheel turn, only feel the drag change. His eyes were on the ledger. Not on Coral's face. On the page, on the handwriting, and Coral watched something move through him that looked, impossibly, like grief.

"You've read this," Hale said. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Twice."

Another silence. Outside, the soldiers on the terrace were laughing at something, the sound thin and distant through the glass, belonging to another world. Somewhere below in the town a church bell rang the quarter hour, and Coral's mind, helplessly, automatically, registered that the bell was two seconds slow and would need regulating, and he wanted to laugh at himself and could not.

Lieutenant Emrys Hale reached out and took the ledger from Coral's coat.

His fingers brushed Coral's chest through the fabric — briefly, impersonally, the touch of a man handling evidence — and then the ledger was in his hands, and Coral's life was in his hands, and Coral stood in the cold archive room and waited to be arrested. He found, distantly, that he was composing the sentence already: *apprentice clockmaker detained in the theft of military records*, and wondering whether Wrenn would be implicated, and whether the old man would lie for him or tell the truth, and knowing Wrenn would do neither, knowing Wrenn would say something cryptic and infuriating that the interrogators would puzzle over for a month—

Hale put the ledger inside his own coat.

Coral's thoughts stopped like a mainspring snapped at full tension.

The lieutenant buttoned his tunic over the bundle with two efficient movements, picked up his notebook and pencil from the shelf where he'd set them, and looked at Coral with an expression that had been rebuilt, brick by brick, into perfect blankness.

"The archive registers," he said, "are accounted for. I'll note them as surrendered and catalogued." He clicked the pencil once, an oddly domestic sound. "Your repair work on the master clock is satisfactory. You're dismissed from further duties at the observatory effective today. Don't return."

"Lieutenant—" Coral's voice cracked on the word and he steadied it. "I don't understand."

"No," Hale agreed. "You don't." He moved toward the door, and Coral caught one glimpse of his profile in the grey light — the jaw tight, the eyes fixed on the middle distance, a man carrying something heavy and pretending it weighed nothing. At the threshold he stopped. He did not turn fully; he spoke half over his shoulder, quietly enough that the words barely survived the distance between them.

"The clock was stopped deliberately, you said. Packed with wax and grit." A pause. "Whoever did it wanted time to run out on something. Wanted someone to come looking." His hand rose briefly, touching his own chest where the ledger lay hidden, an unconscious gesture, gone as soon as it appeared. "Someone did."

Then he was through the door, and his boots rang on the terrace stones, and the soldiers' laughter swallowed him, and he was gone down the hill toward the garrison with three hundred and eleven names against his heart.

Coral stood alone in the archive room for a long time.

He became aware, eventually, that he was shaking — not his hands, which he had trained into stillness across ten thousand hours of fine work, but something deeper, a tremor in the floor of himself. He sat down on Wrenn's stool, the old man's stool, worn smooth by forty years of the same shape of sitting, and he tried to think.

*Spared,* said one voice in him. *He saw what you carry and he let you keep your life. Why? Because he read the grief in your face? Because he's a decent man in a bad uniform?*

*Trapped,* said the other voice, colder. *He knows. He has your name, your face, your confession written in the way you stood when he told you to open your coat. He didn't arrest you because an arrest makes noise, and noise brings questions, and questions bring attention to a ledger the army doesn't yet know exists. He's keeping you in his pocket alongside it. A spare part, held in reserve.*

And beneath both voices, a third, quieter, that Coral did not want to hear and could not stop hearing: *he touched his chest when he spoke of the man who stopped the clock. He knew. He knew Vane, or he knew of him, and he took that book the way a man takes a weight off a wound.*

Outside, the afternoon was turning. Light crawled across the workroom floor and climbed the tall case of the master clock, and the clock ticked on above him, steady, indifferent, repaired by his own hands, running the honest seconds of a dishonest world. Coral looked at it for a long while.

Then he noticed the thing that made the blood leave his face.

On the bench, where his traitor hands had swept the ledger free, lay the oilcloth — unfolded, empty, smoothed flat. And tucked beneath its corner, folded once, was a slip of paper in a hand he did not know, small and precise, five words only:

*MIDNIGHT. COM ALONE. BRING NOTHING.*

Coral read it three times. Then he looked at the clock, and the clock looked back with its blind brass face, and somewhere down in the garrison town a lieutenant was walking through the gates with a stolen ledger under his coat, and Coral Ashgrove understood that he had not been spared, and had not been trapped, but summoned — and that midnight was seven hours away, and would be the longest seven hours of his life.
