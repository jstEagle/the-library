## 5. What the Uniform Owes

The observatory door opened at seven minutes past midnight — Emrys counted the strokes of the tower bell against it, a habit drilled into him at the academy: *a man who is late has already told you one lie about himself* — and Coral Ashgrove slipped inside with a shuttered lantern held so carefully it might have been a sleeping bird.

Emrys stood in the dark among the shadows of instruments and said nothing. Let the boy find him. Let the boy be afraid for a moment longer; fear kept men honest.

"You came," Coral said.

"You read the note."

"I read the note." The lantern lifted a fraction, spilling a blade of light across the floorboards, up the legs of a transit telescope, and finally to Emrys's face. Coral's breath caught — not dramatically, just a small mechanical hitch, like a tooth in a wheel catching on its neighbor. "You're in uniform."

"I'm always in uniform. It's bolted to me by now." He stepped out from behind the great pillar that held the dome's ribs. The moon through the slit of the open shutter fell across the floor in a long silver lane, and he deliberately did not cross it. Some instincts you didn't trample on the first night. "The sentries logged me as conducting a nocturnal inventory of the timekeeping apparatus. Colonel Vasse signed the order himself, which means if anyone asks, I was here on his authority doing his work. It also means I have until dawn before someone wonders why an inventory takes all night."

"An inventory." Coral's voice was flat. "Of which the principal item is missing."

"Which is precisely why it takes all night. Paperwork is the army's true religion, Ashgrove, and its god is slow. A falsified inventory done quickly looks like what it is. Done slowly, with the right number of crossed-out lines and corrections in a clerk's hand, it looks like an honest muddle. Nobody is ever hanged over an honest muddle."

Coral set the lantern on a workbench and turned the shutter almost closed, until only a coin of light remained. In its glow his face had the stillness of a man performing arithmetic he doesn't want the answer to. Twenty-two, perhaps twenty-three, Emrys judged. Younger than the hands suggested. Clockmakers aged in their fingers first.

"Say what you came to say," Coral said. "You have my ledger under your coat. You've had it since yesterday noon. If you wanted me hanged you'd have done it already, so either you want something worse or you want something stranger, and I'd rather know which before my nerve goes."

*Straight at it.* Good. Emrys had rehearsed a dozen openings on the walk up the hill — smooth ones, officerly ones, lies arranged in presentable rows — and heard himself discard every one of them somewhere around the second switchback. This boy repaired things for a living. He would smell a polished lie the way a jeweler smells paste.

So he gave him the unpolished one.

"My father's name was Elias Hale," Emrys said. "He was a surveyor. Roads, canals, boundary lines — the dull cartography that holds a country together without anyone noticing. In 1906 he was seconded to a military survey of the northern districts. He wrote to my mother every week. Then the letters stopped, and when she wrote to the corps they answered that no such officer existed on their rolls. Not dead, mind. *Nonexistent*. As though the ground had opened and then politely closed again and everyone agreed not to mention it."

The coin of light trembled very slightly. Coral had gone motionless in the way of a man listening with his whole body.

"My mother petitioned for eleven years," Emrys went on. "She got four different answers from four different ministries, each one contradicting the others, each one signed by a clerk whose name had also, by the time we checked, ceased to exist. She died in the winter believing she had been widowed by a ghost." He found he was standing very straight, parade-ground straight, and made himself stop. "I enlisted because the army was the only institution that had ever touched my father, and I thought — I was nineteen, I thought like a nineteen-year-old — that somewhere in its ledgers there would be a line with his name on it, and that if I could just get deep enough into the machinery I would find the line and pull it."

"And did you?"

"Yes." Emrys let the word sit. "Three years ago. A requisition annex, buried in a northern district file. Elias Hale, surveyor, seconded, reassigned — and then struck through, with a stamp I've since learned to recognize. The erasure stamp. The same stamp that's in your ledger, Ashgrove, if you looked closely at the margins. It isn't used for the dead. Dead men keep their names. It's used for men who saw something, or measured something, or counted something they weren't supposed to count."

He watched Coral absorb this — watched the boy's eyes go, involuntarily, toward the clock case looming in the dark above them, the master clock with its stopped heart and its hidden compartment, and Emrys understood that Coral had already looked at the margins. Had already seen the stamp.

"My father surveyed roads," Emrys said quietly. "And somewhere between one milestone and the next, he became inconvenient. They didn't shoot him. Shooting makes paperwork. They *unmade* him. And the machine that unmade him is the same machine whose lieutenant is standing in front of you wearing its buttons and carrying its paybook." He paused. "That's what the uniform owes me. That's why you weren't arrested yesterday."

* * *

For a long moment there was only the sound of the hill wind working at the shutters, and beneath it — so faint Emrys might have imagined it — the tick of some small auxiliary clock keeping faith somewhere in the dark.

"You knew about the compartment," Coral said at last. "Yesterday. When you walked in and I was standing there with it half out of my coat. You knew what it was."

"I suspected. The jamming pattern in the escapement was wrong for wear and right for intention. Someone built that hiding place into the works, and someone else wanted it sealed badly enough to sacrifice the whole clock to do it." He shrugged, and felt the ledger shift against his ribs where he had carried it up the hill tonight, wrapped in oilcloth, returned to its owner. Because that was the other thing he had come to do. He reached inside his coat and laid the bundle on the workbench between them. "But knowing a thing exists and knowing what's in it are different animals. I read enough of yours last night to wish I hadn't."

"Then you know about the lists." Coral didn't touch it. His hands stayed flat on the bench on either side of the parcel, framing it, the way a man frames a wound. "Requisitions. Conscriptions. And the third list — the erasures. There are people in this town walking around today who are already struck through in that book, and they don't know it yet. Mrs. Halloway who keeps the bakery. The Ferrymen brothers. Half the choir of St. Brannock's." His voice cracked once and he mastered it, and Emrys noted the mastery and disliked how much it reminded him of himself. "They're not being erased for anything they did. They're being erased because their shops stand on ground the quartermaster corps wants, and dead-or-nonexistent men leave better-titled property than living ones."

"Yes."

"So why am I still holding it?" Coral's eyes came up, and there was steel under the fear now, and Emrys thought: *there it is. Wrenn's training, or his mother's, or the trade's own stubborn doctrine — whatever it is, it will hold.* "You could hand this to Vasse tomorrow and be decorated. A lieutenant who uncovers seditious documents hidden in government property, in wartime conditions — they'd make major by autumn. Instead you bring it back to me at midnight and tell me ghost stories about your father. What do you want from me, Lieutenant Hale?"

"What I want," Emrys said, "is to burn the machine down. What I'm asking for is much smaller, and much more survivable for both of us."

He moved to the workbench, cleared a space among the scattered tools — files, pinion gauges, a jeweler's loupe on a ribbon — and spread the inventory forms he'd brought, weighting their corners with brass weights off the rack. The moon-lane from the shutter slit reached almost to the bench's edge. He worked just outside it, out of old habit.

"The occupation orders require a complete inventory of the observatory within the week. Every instrument, every record, every register of appointments and repairs — everything with writing on it gets catalogued and impounded. Which means that within the week, a corporal with a checklist is going to climb a ladder, open that clock case, and find the compartment your jammed gears were guarding. Even if the ledger were still inside — even if it were a blank book — the compartment itself is evidence. Compartments don't hide themselves. Somebody built it, somebody used it, and the army's first question after *what is it* is always *who*."

"So it has to leave town."

"It has to leave town, and the fact that it ever existed has to become a clerical fiction. Here's how." He tapped the top form. "My inventory will record the master clock as *dismantled for transport*, movement crated separately from case, crates logged to the garrison store. There'll be a genuine crate — I'll see to that — and it will contain genuine clock parts, because I intend to actually dismantle part of the movement myself tomorrow, in view of witnesses, so the story has bones. But the compartment—" he drew a small precise circle on the margin of the form, and erased it immediately with his thumb— "will be recorded as never having been there. A repairman's false panel, noted and dismissed. Nothing. Clerks dismiss nothing all day long; it's the bulk of their trade."

"And the ledger?"

"Goes south. To the capital." He looked at Coral directly now. "There's a journalist there named Aldous Merrin. He publishes a paper called *The Evening Standard* that the War Office has tried to suppress twice, which in my experience is the surest mark of a man worth trusting. He's printed requisition scandals before — smaller ones, provincial ones, and survived them, because he verifies everything three times before ink touches page. Your ledger is bigger than anything he's had. Hundreds of names. Original documents, in an official hand, with official stamps. If it reaches him, it prints. And if it prints, Vasse falls, the occupation collapses, and every struck-through name in that book gets written back into the world."

"If it reaches him." Coral picked up the oilcloth parcel now, weighing it in both hands as though recalibrating it. "Vellmarsh has three ways out, Lieutenant, and as of Tuesday morning all three have checkpoints on them. The bridge, the garrison gate, and the mountain road. I've watched soldiers search a grandmother's market basket for contraband cheese. You're proposing to carry six hundred pages of the army's worst sins through one of those gates in a coat pocket."

"I'm proposing *you* carry them. Not me." He let that land, and watched it land. "Think it through, Ashgrove. I'm the one man in this garrison who cannot move that ledger an inch. My person is subject to inspection at any hour, by any superior, without cause shown — that's what the uniform buys, and what it costs. If I'm searched and it's found on me, the story ends in ten minutes: officer apprehended with stolen documents, interrogated tonight, shot by Friday, and the ledger back in Vasse's safe with my name added to it in red ink. But you—" he gestured at the bench, the tools, the whole moonlit museum of the trade— "you're the observatory's own apprentice. You have a legitimate reason to be moving clock parts through a checkpoint that no soldier on earth will understand well enough to question deeply. A disassembled regulator case is a box full of incomprehensible brass to a conscript from the coal country. To you it's a language. Use your language as luggage."

"And you? When the ledger turns up published in the capital with the observatory's stamps all over it, and Vasse starts pulling threads — where does the thread end?"

"At a lieutenant who filed an honest-looking, dishonest inventory, and who will be standing in plain sight when the pulling starts, looking exactly as guilty as a man can look without proof." Emrys smiled, and was aware the smile had no warmth in it, only structure. "I told you what the uniform owes me. I intend to pay it. But understand what I'm asking, because I won't dress it up twice. If you take this, you stop being a clockmaker who noticed something and start being a conspirator. There's no apprenticeship for it. No master to correct your mistakes gently. The mistakes are fatal, and mostly they're fatal to other people — the printer whose press hides you, the landlady whose attic shelters you, whoever the army decides was your accomplice once they've decided you existed. Do you understand the weight of it?"

Coral was quiet for a while. He turned the parcel over once, slowly, and Emrys realized he wasn't weighing the paper at all. He was thinking about names. Emrys had seen men weigh ammunition with less care.

"There's a man in this town," Coral said finally, "named Tobias Wrenn. He taught me the trade — took me off the workhouse list when I was twelve, which is its own kind of erasure run backward. Ten years he's taught me that a mechanism keeps honest time only if every wheel trusts every other wheel, and that most of what people call loyalty is just noise, but real trust—" his thumb moved along the oilcloth seam— "real trust is load-bearing. You don't put it in unless the whole structure can carry it."

"I'm not asking you to trust me."

"No," Coral agreed. "You're asking me to bet my life that you hate the same machine you serve. And I've known you one day." He set the parcel down and looked up, and his eyes in the coin of lamplight were steady and very clear. "But I've read that ledger, Lieutenant, every page of it, and I know what the machine does to men who measure too much. Your father counted miles and vanished. You're counting crimes, and you're still standing inside the counting house. Either you're the bravest liar the army ever issued, or you're exactly what you say you are." A pause. "I'm going to choose the second, because the first possibility keeps me awake and the second gives me something to do at dawn."

Emrys exhaled — a breath he hadn't known he was holding, released before he could stop it, and hated himself for the release. Trust, offered and accepted, was just another exposure. Another surface for a knife. And yet the boy's hand on the table, six inches from his own, seemed suddenly the most solid thing in the dark room.

"Then listen, because we haven't much night left." He pulled a folded map from beneath the inventory forms — not the garrison issue map, but an older one, pre-war, with the roads drawn generously and the checkpoints absent, and he began, in low voice, to annotate the gaps. "The bridge is weakest at dawn changeover... the gate sergeant counts tool rolls by weight, not contents... and the mountain road—" he stopped.

He had heard it. Faint, far below, threaded through the wind: boots on the hill path. More than one pair. Not hurrying — patrols never hurried — but coming up the observatory road with the unhurried certainty of men following instructions written by someone else.

Coral heard it a half-second later. Their eyes met across the workbench, over the ledger, over the map with half its secrets spoken.

"That's not for you," Coral whispered. "Inventories don't send squads at two in the morning."

"No," Emrys said, and snuffed the lantern with two fingers, and reached for his pistol. "No, they don't."
