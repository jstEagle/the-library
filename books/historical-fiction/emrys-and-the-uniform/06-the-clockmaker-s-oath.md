## 6. The Clockmaker's Oath

Tobias Wrenn's shop smelled the way it had always smelled: oil of clove, brass filings, and the faint sweetness of beeswax rubbed into old walnut cases. Ten years ago that smell had been the whole world to a hungry apprentice with ink-stained fingers and no family name worth speaking. Tonight it smelled like the last ordinary room in Vellmarsh.

Coral came in through the back, as Wrenn had taught him to come in everywhere — quietly, by the way nobody watched.

"You're limping," Wrenn said without turning from his bench. He was bent over a carriage clock no bigger than a fist, his loupe screwed into one eye, his white hair catching the lamplight like frost on wire.

"I'm not limping."

"You've been limping since you were nineteen and fell off my roof chasing a kite." Wrenn set down his tweezers and looked up. His eyes were pale and quick and missed nothing, which was precisely why Coral had come. "Sit down, boy. You've got the face of a man carrying something heavier than a coat pocket should hold."

Coral sat on the customer's stool, then stood again, then sat. He laid the ledger on the bench between them.

Wrenn did not reach for it at once. He finished adjusting the escapement he'd been working, seated the last wheel, closed the carriage clock's back plate with a click as gentle as a door being shut on a sleeping child. Only then did he wipe his hands on his apron and open the ledger to its first page.

The shop went very quiet. The kind of quiet Coral had learned, in ten years of standing beside this man at this bench, meant that Wrenn was reading not with his eyes but with his whole attention, the way he read a movement that ran half a second fast.

Page after page turned. Names in the late head horologist Aldery's cramped hand. Requisitioned. Conscripted. Erased. The columns of dates marching beside them like soldiers themselves.

At last Wrenn closed it and folded his hands on top of the cover, and Coral saw that the old man's knuckles were white.

"How many?" Wrenn asked.

"Four hundred and eleven names. Maybe more — some pages are water-damaged."

"And the army has taken every registry in town."

"Every one. The parish rolls, the trade guilds, the school records. Notices on every post by dawn. Everything surrendered by week's end." Coral swallowed. "Mrs. Calloway's name is on the posted list. She took me in when I had nowhere, Tobias. She doesn't even know what 'requisition of premises' means yet. She thinks they're going to paint her front room."

Wrenn grunted. It was not quite a laugh. "They'll take her house for officers' billets and put her out on the street with two hours' notice, and the notice will be perfectly legal." He rose, joints cracking like a case opening, and went to the shelf above his bench where he kept the good tea, the tin he pretended was only for special customers. He spooned leaves into two cups without asking whether Coral wanted any. He never asked. That was one of the silences between them that had always meant care.

"Tell me about the officer," Wrenn said, his back turned.

Coral started. "How did you—"

"Because you came here instead of running, which means someone gave you reason to hope, and hope has a uniform these days." Wrenn set the cups down. "Lieutenant Hale. Emrys Hale. I've seen him at the observatory twice. Stands like a man who learned posture from a book rather than a parade ground. What did he do?"

"He found me with the ledger half down my coat. He could have arrested me on the spot. Instead—" Coral heard himself say it and still couldn't make sense of it. "He put it in his own pocket and told me to go home through the fish market, where there were fewer patrols."

Wrenn sat, wrapped both hands around his cup, and regarded Coral over the rim of it for a long moment.

"Then either he's setting a trap with more patience than most sergeants possess," Wrenn said, "or he's a man at war with his own buttons."

* * *

They spread the map across Wrenn's workbench at midnight, weighting the corners with four brass clock weights and a horseshoe Wrenn kept for reasons he'd never explained. A single candle, shielded behind the hood of a broken regulator case so its light fell in a blade across the paper.

Three routes led out of Vellmarsh toward the capital. Three checkpoints stood across them like locks on a safe.

"The bridge," Coral said, tapping the first. "Militia, mostly. Young men with rifles and something to prove."

"Mm. And a sergeant who inspects cart beds with a mirror on a pole, which your militia boys forget to mention because they're busy proving things." Wrenn's finger moved west along the river road. "The garrison gate. Full company, proper search protocol, and Sergeant Odran, who once returned a watch I'd repaired because the serial number didn't match his records. The man reads serial numbers the way I read escapements."

"And the third?"

Wrenn's finger stopped on the mountain road, where the contour lines crowded together like startled sheep. For a moment he said nothing, and Coral understood that the silence itself was information — one of those silences Wrenn had spent a decade teaching him to hear.

"The mountain pass checkpoint is new," Wrenn said finally. "Erected three days ago. Before the occupation was even announced. Do you understand what that means?"

Coral looked at the little inked square on the map, sitting on the only road that didn't pass through the town center, the road a smuggler would choose, the road a man planning an occupation would want covered before anyone knew there was anything to smuggle. Cold walked up his spine.

"They knew about the ledger," he said. "Or something like it. They built the trap before they knew what would walk into it."

"Or Aldery's death wasn't the heart failure the register recorded." Wrenn leaned back. "Old Josiah Aldery was many things, but careless with his heart wasn't one of them. He wound the master clock every Sunday for thirty years."

The candle guttered, steadied. Somewhere outside, a patrol's boots rang on cobbles, unhurried, the rhythm of men who owned the night now.

"So we don't use the mountain road," Coral said. "We use the bridge or the gate."

"We use whichever one they're *not* watching harder tomorrow, which we won't know until we know." Wrenn pulled a stub of pencil from behind his ear. "Which means you need to look like nothing worth stopping. Not a courier — couriers get searched *because* couriers are worth stopping. Something duller. A tradesman making a routine run. A man with papers so boring that reading them feels like punishment."

"A clock parts delivery," Coral said slowly. The idea assembling itself the way a train of wheels gathers under drive. "A disassembled regulator going to a repair client in the capital. Heavy case, awkward to carry, full of fiddly bits no infantryman wants to understand. I can build a false floor into the case—"

"Stop." Wrenn held up one finger. "You've just described exactly what a clever man would do, and clever men build false floors, and sergeants like Odran have met clever men before. If you hide it, you must hide it so well that hiding becomes the story. Or—" his pale eyes glittered, "—you don't hide it at all, and we think very hard about what that costs."

"That's mad."

"Most good mechanisms look mad until you see the escapement." Wrenn began sketching on the map's margin — quick, precise strokes, the dimensions of a standard regulator case, the weight distribution, the way a soldier's eye travels across a pile of metal parts. Watching him work, Coral felt the old ache of apprenticeship rise in his chest: the certainty that whatever this man knew, he would hand over if you were patient enough to deserve it.

"Bridge first," Wrenn said. "It's the weakest, which means it'll tighten fastest. You cross tomorrow or the day after, while they're still bored."

"And if I'm stopped? If they find it?"

Wrenn set down the pencil. In the candlelight his face looked older than it had an hour ago, older than it had seemed any day in ten years, and suddenly Coral saw the arithmetic the old man had been doing silently all evening.

"Tobias. Come with me."

"No."

The word came without hesitation, without heat, final as a ratchet clicking into the next tooth. Coral opened his mouth and Wrenn raised the same finger.

"Think before you argue, because you'll waste your breath. I'm sixty-eight years old, I have a limp of my own that has nothing to do with kites, and I am the most respectable man in Vellmarsh." He counted off on those scarred fingers. "Forty years at this bench. Warden of the Guild chapel. I repaired the mayor's grandfather's watches and taught half the tradesmen in this town to tell time properly. When the army seizes the guild registers next week — and they will — somebody respectable has to be standing there handing them over. Smiling. Cooperating. Being exactly the harmless old clockmaker they expect."

"So they'll leave you alone."

"They'll leave me alone because I am *useful* left alone." Wrenn's voice was level, but Coral had learned long ago that the level voice was the one that carried the weight. "And while they're leaving me alone, someone has to be here. Someone has to keep paying Mrs. Calloway's rent when they evict her. Someone has to know which families have gone and which have been taken, and keep the count, because your ledger stops the day you carry it over that bridge, and the counting mustn't stop. Someone has to sit in the guild hall when Vasse's clerks come, and remember every name they cross out."

"You'd be alone."

"I've been alone in this shop for forty years, boy. It's called having a trade." But something moved behind his eyes, quickly shuttered. "Besides. If I run, they burn the shop within the week — that's how it works, you've read your own ledger. An empty workshop is a barracks by Friday. And this shop—" he touched the bench, an odd, tender gesture, like touching the case of a dead friend's clock, "—this shop has hidden worse things than ledgers. There's a cavity behind the great wheel cabinet you helped me build when you were sixteen that I've never told you the purpose of. Now you know."

Coral stared at him. "You built it *then*? Ten years ago?"

"I built it because in 1903 a man came to my door at midnight with documents inside his coat, and I turned him away because I was afraid, and I have read his name in newspapers since." Wrenn's hands were flat on the bench now, steady, but Coral could see the tendons standing out like cables. "I promised myself I would not turn away the next one. Then the next one turned out to be a starving apprentice with no references and ink on his fingers, and keeping *that* promise got complicated."

The candle flame bent. Coral found he could not speak.

"Ten years," Wrenn went on, quieter. "You swept my floor, you slept in the loft, you ate at my table, and I never asked what happened to the people who should have been raising you, because asking questions is sometimes the cruelest thing a man can do. You learned that from me too — which silences protect people. Well." He straightened his shoulders, and it was like watching a mainspring being set. "Now it's my turn to be silent, and your turn to carry something. That's not sacrifice, Coral. That's just the debt moving down the bench to the next pair of hands."

* * *

They worked until nearly three.

Wrenn stripped down a travel regulator with the economy of a surgeon, laying each component on felt in its exact order of removal, narrating in a low voice: "Escapement out first, always, or the whole train drops and any fool can tell the case has been opened in haste. Slow is invisible. Fast is a confession." The ledger, rolled tight as a wick and wrapped in oilcloth, slid into the hollow pillar casing of the movement frame — a space that existed, genuinely, in every regulator of that pattern, because the maker had designed the pillar hollow to save brass, and no soldier alive would know it, and no soldier alive would weigh a case against a memorized specification because no such specification existed outside Wrenn's head.

Then Wrenn made him do it himself. Twice. Three times. In the dark, by feel alone, timed against the shop's regulator ticking on the wall.

"Again. Your hands are shaking."

"My hands don't shake."

"They're shaking now, and I'd rather they shake here than at a checkpoint. Again."

On the fifth attempt Coral assembled the case blind in four minutes and eleven seconds, and Wrenn checked his pocket watch and nodded once, which from Tobias Wrenn was a parade and a medal ceremony.

They sat afterward with the last of the tea gone cold, the map folded away, the false story of Coral's journey rehearsed down to the name of the fictitious client in the capital — a widow named Hartswell, Gilder's Lane, who required her striking mechanism serviced quarterly and had, conveniently, died two years ago in a way no one in Vellmarsh would ever learn.

"You'll need money," Wrenn said, and pushed an envelope across the bench before Coral could refuse. "Guild benevolent fund. Entirely legitimate. Entirely yours."

"Tobias—"

"Take it. You can't refuse a man's dying wish and you can't refuse his living one either, so save us both the argument." Wrenn looked at him across the bench, and for a moment the master horologist fell away and Coral saw simply an old man in a lamplit room, looking at the closest thing to a son the world had given him. "One thing more, and then you go home before the patrol changes."

"Anything."

"When you get stopped — and you will get stopped, someday, somewhere, if not at that bridge — remember this." Wrenn held up the envelope, then set it down again. "Fear travels. A searcher catches fear off a man like a cold off a child, and frightened men search harder, because hurting something makes their own fear feel smaller. So you will give that sergeant your papers, and you will stand there thinking about gear trains. Nothing else. Gear trains, the third wheel's depth, the drop of the pallet stones. Bore yourself, Coral. A bored man has never once been arrested."

Coral laughed despite everything — a short, cracked sound — and Wrenn allowed himself the ghost of a smile.

"And the ledger," Coral said. "If it comes to it. If I'm taken. You'll—" 

"If you're taken, I'll know nothing about any ledger, because you will never have shown me one, and this conversation never happened, and you are simply my former apprentice traveling on guild business." Wrenn's eyes were flint. "That is the oath, boy. Not the dramatic kind. The boring kind. The kind that consists of lying competently for as long as it takes. Can you swear it?"

Coral thought of Mrs. Calloway's name on the posted list. Of four hundred and eleven names in Aldery's cramped hand. Of a lieutenant with a book-learned posture who had pocketed a man's death warrant instead of serving it.

"I swear it," he said.

"Good." Wrenn blew out the candle, and in the sudden dark his voice came dry and steady as ever. "Then go home through the fish market. Fewer patrols. And Coral —"

He paused, and in the pause Coral heard the wall regulator tick on, indifferent, faithful, marking time for a town that no longer knew what time it was keeping.

"Come back and tell me what the bridge looked like. An old man likes to know his mechanisms are holding."

Coral slipped out the back way into the freezing dark, the ledger's weight riding solid against his ribs inside his coat, and had almost reached the corner when the sound stopped him.

Bells. Not the church bells — the garrison bell, ragged and urgent, ringing in a pattern no bell in Vellmarsh had ever rung before. Doors were opening along the lane. Lamplight bloomed in windows.

Down the hill toward the market square, voices rose above the rooftops — criers, army criers, and beneath the wind Coral caught the words being thrown door to door:

*Curfew extended. All guild members to report for registration. By order of Colonel Vasse.*

The lists were growing. And Wrenn was a guild member of forty years' standing, the most respectable man in Vellmarsh, and Coral was already turning back toward the shop when he saw the torchlight coming up the lane.
