## 8. Lessons After Curfew

The observatory at night was a different country.

Emrys had learned its geography by daylight — the long gallery of orreries and orphansed instruments, the dome's ribs overhead like the skeleton of some patient whale, the master clock standing in its alcove with the solemnity of a cathedral. But after curfew, when the lamps of Vellmarsh guttered out one by one below and the garrison's patrols became the only moving things in the town, the observatory belonged to the dark and to whatever two men chose to do inside it.

He came up through the coal path, the back way Wrenn had shown Coral, carrying a satchel that weighed nothing and everything: three forged passes in various stages of honesty, a captain's authorization blank lifted from the adjutant's office, and his own conscience folded small enough to fit beside them.

Coral was waiting by the master clock. He always waited by the master clock, as though the mechanism might need him, as though it were a living thing that grew anxious without an attendant. Perhaps, Emrys thought, it did. He was beginning to suspect that Coral Ashgrove extended his sympathies to anything made of interlocking parts — clocks, towns, men.

"You're late," Coral said. No lamp; he'd learned to work by starlight through the dome's shuttered oculus, and his hands moved in the dark with the certainty of ten years' apprenticeship.

"A sergeant wanted to discuss my inventory figures." Emrys set the satchel on the workbench and lit the single shaded lamp they permitted themselves, angled so its glow never reached the windows. "I discussed them. Badly, on purpose. He'll spend tomorrow trying to find my arithmetic errors, which is a night he isn't spending wondering where I go."

"That's dishonest."

"That's survival."

"Those can be the same thing," Coral said quietly, "if you're careful about it."

Emrys looked at him. The lamplight caught only half of Coral's face — the sharp line of cheekbone, the eye that held light like a watch crystal holds it. There was no accusation in him. That was the strangest thing about the clockmaker, Emrys had decided over four nights of these stolen hours. Coral didn't forgive easily, exactly; he *understood*, the way he understood a mechanism, tracing every spring of a man's behavior back to its mainspring, and finding rarely that anyone was truly broken, only wrongly wound.

"Sit," Emrys said. "Lesson one."

* * *

They worked at the far bench, away from the clock, because Coral had insisted the master clock should not have to witness forgery. Emrys laid out the passes under the lamp.

"Checkpoint procedure at the bridge goes thus: halt at twenty paces. Do not approach until summoned. When summoned, you approach at a walk, hands visible, and you speak only when spoken to. The sentry asks your name, business, destination. You answer in that order, no more. Volunteers add things. Volunteers get searched."

"And if he asks something I can't answer?"

"He won't. That's the point of a good pass." Emrys tapped the document — Corporal Hale, escort duty, one case regulator parts, Halloway & Sons, capital. It bore a signature that belonged to no one and looked like everyone. "A pass does the talking. Your job is to be boring. Boring men pass checkpoints. Interesting men pass through court-martials."

Coral studied the paper the way he studied movements — tilting it toward the lamp, reading the fibers. "This ink is wrong."

"It's the army's ink."

"No." Coral touched the signature, barely. "Army ink dries with a slight sheen where the pen pressed hard. This has been blotted evenly. Whoever taught you to forge learned from someone who signed slowly."

Emrys felt something turn over in his chest, slow and heavy, like a great wheel taking up slack chain. Ten days ago this man had been a stranger caught hiding contraband in his coat, and now he was reading ink under lamplight and finding the story of Emrys's teacher written in the drying pattern.

"Sergeant Maddox," Emrys said. "Before his transfer. He could copy any hand in the battalion. He used to say a signature is just a man's way of signing his own warrant."

"He sounds like a horologist."

"He sounds like a man who got transferred to a place with no forwarding address, when he started asking what happened to requisitioned records." Emrys heard his own voice go flat and stopped it. "Lesson two. If you're searched, you don't resist and you don't explain. Resistance looks like guilt. Explanations sound like guilt. You stand like a man who has been searched fifty times and found empty fifty times, because the search itself bores you."

"How do I stand bored?"

"You think about gears," Emrys said, and Coral laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly muffled, as if even laughter needed a permit after curfew.

"I don't have to pretend for that one."

* * *

They drilled. Emrys played sentry while Coral approached across the dark gallery, again and again, correcting his pace — too quick, again, slower, you're carrying nothing worth hurrying about — until Coral's walk achieved a kind of magnificent tedium. Then they swapped, and Emrys crossed the floor as a courier, and Coral challenged him, hesitant and precise, and Emrys showed him how a real sentry's questions looped back on themselves, how the third question was usually a trap dressed as carelessness.

"What's in the case?"

"Regulator parts."

"Regulator for what make?"

"Lippman & Voss, 1907 pattern." Emrys tapped the bench. "Know your lie down to the serial number. A liar who knows details is just a man telling the truth about unimportant things."

Coral wrote everything in a little notebook in a hand so fine the lines might have been engraved. Emrys watched him write and thought, not for the first time, that there were worse fates than being known by a man like this — a man who recorded carefully, who understood that precision was a form of devotion.

The clock above them ticked through the hours, and somewhere past midnight Emrys realized they had stopped drilling.

* * *

It happened the way these things happen — not as an event but as a slow settling, like dust finding a surface. They were sitting on the bench with the passes put away, sharing cold tea Coral had brewed on a spirit lamp, and the silence between them had changed quality. It had stopped being a gap in the conversation and become a thing they were both holding.

"My father's name was Elias Hale," Emrys said, to the tea, to the middle distance, to whatever part of Coral was willing to receive it. "You won't find it in any register now. I've looked. The parish books show a line struck through and the word *amended* in the margin, and no amendment following."

Coral said nothing. He had the gift of not saying nothing — of making silence into a room another person could move around in safely.

"He was a surveyor. Roads, bridges. Good at it. In '04 the army requisitioned his maps of the northern valleys, and then requisitioned his field notes, and then requisitioned *him* — a consultancy, they called it, six months in the capital. He came home eleven months later and never spoke of it, and two years after that he was gone. Not dead. There's a grave, but I've stood at it and I know in my bones it's empty. *Erased.* The word in the ledger — I knew it before I read your ledger. I'd seen the shape of it my whole childhood. A chair no one sat in. A coat no one mentioned. My mother going still whenever a uniform came to the door, which was often, because we lived near the depot." He turned the cup in his hands. "I enlisted to find the machine that ate him. And I found it. I'm inside it. I wear it."

"And?" Coral asked softly. "What did you find?"

"That it isn't run by monsters." Emrys laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's the horror of it, Coral. It's run by clerks. Decent men, most of them, men who love their children, who are kind to horses, who follow procedure because procedure is comfortable and questioning it is expensive. Vasse is the exception, and Vasse is worse than a monster, because a monster you can flee and a true believer you must argue with. Every day I salute men who would weep at a lame dog and sign a requisition order that turns a family into a rumor."

The master clock ticked. Below, Vellmarsh slept the sleep of the occupied.

"When I was fourteen," Coral said at last, "Wrenn caught me filing an escape wheel badly. Sloppy work, done fast, done to be finished. He didn't shout. He took the wheel, and he held it up, and he said: *every part you make, someone will have to trust with their time.* Their *time*, Emrys. Not their money, not their pride. Time. The thing a life is measured in." Coral looked at him across the lamplight. "I think the army makes parts badly. I think whole towns have been assembled with sloppy filing, and people are trusting them with their time anyway, because there's nothing else to wind."

Emrys set down his cup. His hands, trained to stillness on parade grounds, were not entirely still.

"Why are you helping me?" Coral asked. It wasn't an accusation. It was the question of a craftsman examining a joint that bore weight and wanting to know its provenance. "Not the ledger — the ledger is principle, anyone with a spine and a grudge. But this. Forged passes. Night lessons. You could lose everything, and you've met me four times."

Five times, Emrys almost said. Five, counting the market square before any of it, counting the moment he'd seen a young man with ink-stained fingers arguing with a fishmonger about the price of eels and thought, absurdly, *there is someone I would like to know*. But the market square was a different country too, and he kept it.

"Because I know what the ledger costs to carry," he said instead. "Because my father carried something, once, that got him erased, and I have spent nine years wishing someone had helped him carry it." He paused. The next truth came up his throat like a swallowed gear, catching on everything. "And because I find that I mind, specifically, what happens to you. Not to the courier. To you."

Coral went very still, the way mechanisms go still — not dead, but gathered.

"The uniform," Coral said carefully, "between us."

"What of it?"

"It could become a wall at any moment. Tomorrow morning you could arrest me and be commended for it. I could hand you to Vasse and buy safety for the whole town. We're being honest tonight, so let's be honest about that too."

"Yes," Emrys said. "We could. Either of us. And I could have, ten days ago, on the first night, when I took the ledger from your coat and felt your heart going like an escapement through the wool." He met Coral's eyes. "Every hour since, I've known the wall is there. I've decided to build doors in it instead. That's all any of us can do with walls."

The lamp flame bent slightly, some draft from the dome finding them. Neither of them moved. The space between them on the bench was perhaps a hand's width, and it contained rank and regulation and the Articles of War and nine years of saluting and ten years of silences, and it seemed to Emrys the most crowded hand's width in the empire.

Coral closed it. Not all the way. His hand came to rest beside Emrys's on the bench, little finger touching the edge of Emrys's palm, the smallest possible contact, a bearing seated in its race — precise, load-bearing, deliberate.

"This is unwise," Coral murmured.

"Extremely."

"We should stop."

"We should," Emrys agreed, and turned his hand over so their palms met, and neither of them meant a word of it, and both of them knew it.

Above them the master clock, keeping faith with its maker, counted the hour with a soft deep strike that Emrys felt in his sternum. Four o'clock. The sky beyond the dome's oculus had gone from black to the color of gun metal.

"Curfew lifts at five," Emrys said, without moving. "I have inspection at seven."

"Then you'll want to go."

"I will."

Neither of them moved for another minute, and that minute was the truest either had kept in years.

* * *

He was at the coal path door, satchel lighter by three passes and heavier by other cargo entirely, when Coral spoke from behind him.

"Emrys. The list posted today. Did you see it?"

"I saw it." Fourteen new names. Requisitions, transfers, one *obstruction — pending review*. He had stood in front of it in full uniform, reading it with the correct blank face, while something in him screamed against the inside of his own skin.

"Wrenn's name is on it," Coral said. His voice was level, which is how Emrys knew it was costing him. "Obstruction. Pending review. What does *pending review* mean, in your army?"

Emrys stood in the doorway with the gray light coming up behind him, and knew that the next lesson could not wait for nightfall, and hated himself for the answer he was about to give.

"It means," he said, "that they haven't decided yet whether to erase him or merely ruin him. And it means, Coral, that we stop practicing with drills. As of tonight, we practice with the road."

He heard, behind him in the dark of the observatory, a clockmaker's breath catch — and then steady, like a movement being wound.
