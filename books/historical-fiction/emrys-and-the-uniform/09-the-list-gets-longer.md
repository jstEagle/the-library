## 9. The List Gets Longer

The notices went up on a Tuesday, nailed to the market cross before dawn by soldiers who worked in pairs and said nothing to anyone. By seven o'clock the square had filled with people pretending not to read, which in Vellmarsh meant standing close enough to see every word while examining loaves of bread they did not intend to buy.

Coral arrived with a repair slip for the baker's wall clock — a genuine job, blessedly, because his hands needed something honest to do — and he stood at the edge of the crowd with the slip crushed in his fist and read the new postings twice.

The first list was familiar now. Requisitions: carts, coal, copper, canvas, grain. Names of households marked for billeting. The army's appetite written in a tidy clerk's hand.

The second list was new.

*Persons of Interest — Registry Pending*, the header said, and beneath it ran eleven names. Coral scanned them once, twice, feeling the way he had felt at eleven years old when a mainspring he'd been compressing had slipped its grip and bitten through the skin of his palm: not pain at first, only a terrible hot pressure, and then the blood.

Tobias Wrenn's name was fourth from the top.

Not *requisitioned*. Not *conscripted*. The word beside it was **obstruction**, and there was no explanation given, because obstruction required none. It was the sort of word that meant someone in a grey office had looked at an old man's signature on a petition, or heard about an old man's questions asked too loudly at the garrison gate, and had decided that respectability itself was a kind of interference.

The ledger in Coral's coat seemed to grow heavier as he read. He had taken to carrying it everywhere — folded into a oilcloth wrap inside a false bottom of his tool satchel, which he now never let out of arm's reach, even to wash or sleep. Ten days since Emrys Hale had pocketed it off him like a pickpocket and walked away; nine days since the same officer had come back through the window after midnight and turned Coral's life into something with two halves, one lived in daylight and one conducted in whispers.

He read the list a third time because his eyes refused to accept it, and then he bought a loaf of bread he did not want from the baker, whose own name appeared further down under requisitions, and walked home without running, because running would have been a confession and Vellmarsh had learned in ten days to watch everything.

* * *

Wrenn was at his bench when Coral came to the shop that afternoon, working on a carriage clock with a loupe screwed into his eye socket, his white hair standing up in tufts where he'd pushed his hands through it. The bell over the door chimed. Wrenn didn't look up.

"You've seen it, then," the old man said.

"The whole square has seen it."

"Good." Wrenn lifted the loupe away and set down his tweezers with the exaggerated care of a man controlling his hands. "Then we needn't waste time discussing my feelings about it, which are nobody's business and of no mechanical use."

"Wrenn—"

"Sit down, boy." There was nothing unkind in it. There rarely was. But Coral sat, because ten years of apprenticeship had built obedience into him deeper than any joint in his body. "Obstruction," Wrenn continued, tasting the word like a coin he suspected was clipped. "You know what I did? I wrote to the garrison asking by what authority they'd seized the parish registers. That's all. One letter, properly composed, correctly addressed, with my full name at the bottom, because if you're going to object to something, you ought to be findable when they come to ask why."

"That's why they've listed you. Being findable."

"That's precisely why." Wrenn took up his tweezers again and bent over the carriage clock, though Coral could see his eyes weren't tracking the work. "I taught you clocks for ten years, Coral. What did I teach you about them, first thing?"

Coral knew the answer the way he knew his own heartbeat. "*A clock is a promise.* You said it the first day. You said most things people make are hopes, but a clock is a promise — that noon will come when it says noon, that time won't lie to people who can't afford lies."

"Mm." The old man's mouth twitched. "And what is the army doing in this town?"

"Lying about time. About records. About who exists."

"So." Wrenn fitted a pallet stone with a click so small Coral felt it rather than heard it. "One of us has to keep making promises, and I'm too old to run anywhere. Which brings me to you. Show me your satchel."

Every instinct Coral had developed in the past ten days screamed against it. He did not move.

"Tobias. If they search this shop—"

"They searched it eight days ago. Sloppily — a sergeant with cold hands who opened three drawers and read nothing." Wrenn finally turned on his stool to face him fully, and Coral saw how tired he looked, how thin the skin had gone over his knuckles. "They'll search again, and next time they'll be looking for paper. So the ledger cannot stay in your satchel, and it cannot stay in your rooms, and it cannot leave town yet, because you haven't found your third road out — I know you haven't, because you'd have told me, or you'd have gone already. So where is it going to live, Coral?"

"In plain sight," Coral said slowly, and even as he said it, the idea was assembling itself in his hands before it finished forming in his head. "Or... pieces of it."

He drew a pocket watch from his vest — not his good one, but a customer's repair, a rusted thing awaiting cleaning — and popped the case. The white enamel dial sat in its circle like a small pale moon.

Wrenn watched him, waiting, in the manner of a teacher who knows when to stop talking.

"The army seizes ledgers," Coral said. "Registers, lists, anything with columns of names. They know what those look like. But a watchmaker's bench is covered in dials, and dials carry numbers, and nobody counts a watch face as a document." His finger traced the enamel. "These numerals are painted. Enamel paint over firing. If I grind my own pigment fine enough, I can paint *other* numerals — tiny ones, between the hour markers, in code only I can read. Twelve watches. A dozen names per dial, maybe more if I use the case backs. The ledger stays here, hidden where they've already looked. And I carry the names out on my wrist, past their checkpoints, wearing them like—" he almost said *like a uniform*, and stopped.

Silence held the shop for a long moment. Outside, boots passed on the cobbles in the rhythm of a patrol, four men, unhurried.

"It will take weeks," Wrenn said at last.

"I know."

"And if they take you at a checkpoint with a dozen coded dials on you, they will not need to read them to hang you. Suspicion will do."

"I know that too."

Wrenn nodded slowly, and then he did something Coral had never seen him do: he reached across the bench and gripped Coral's forearm, hard, his old fingers surprisingly strong. "Then we will divide the work," he said. "You'll paint. I'll grind pigment and cut stencils — my hands are steadier than yours at fine work, don't argue, they simply are. And I'll teach you the old trade-secret cypher, the one the guild used for shipping prices, because you only half-learned it and half-learned cyphers get people killed." He released Coral's arm and turned back to his clock. "Now hand me that pestle and mortar, and fetch every dead watch in the drawer. We've promises to keep."

* * *

They worked for six nights.

By day the shop remained a shop: Coral at the bench repairing soldiers' watches — the occupation had created a boom in horology, an irony Wrenn pronounced "the funniest thing to happen in Vellmarsh since the fire of '61" — while patrols passed the windows and officers' wives brought in bracelets to be sized and left gossip behind them like spilled sugar. By night the shutters closed, the lamp was hooded, and the two of them bent over the benches like conspirators in some miniature religion, grinding white enamel pigment finer than flour, cutting stencils from tin sheet with needles, painting letters smaller than grains of rice onto the faces of broken time.

The cypher was older than either of them. Guild masters had used it for centuries to hide shipping costs and supplier names from thieving journeymen: a rotation of numeral-substitutions keyed to the month, layered again with a second key chosen by the individual master. Wrenn gave Coral his key — a date, 1847, the year of his first apprenticeship — and made him write it out forty times until the substitutions came without thought.

"A cypher isn't secrecy," Wrenn lectured, tapping the bench with his knuckle. "Secrecy is hoping. A cypher is *engineering*. Anyone can hope. Only the right key opens the mechanism, same as a lock. Understand the difference and you'll live longer."

On the fourth night, Coral copied the first page of the ledger onto watch faces by lamplight, transcribing names into rotated figures, painting each tiny glyph with a brush of three hairs, firing each dial gently over the spirit lamp, and holding the finished result up to the light to see twelve erased townsfolk gleaming faintly between the hour marks of a dead woman's wristwatch. Something in his chest turned over — grief and purpose locking together like wheels in a train, no longer distinguishable, perhaps never again to be distinguished.

On the fifth night, Wrenn caught him shaking.

It was small at first. Coral's brush hovered over a dial and would not descend, and he realized he was looking at the name he was encoding — Aldous Pettibone, ferryman, sixty-one, *erased, March 1911* — and remembering Mr. Pettibone's dog, which had followed the ferryman everywhere and had howled on the dock for a week afterward before disappearing itself, and Coral could not remember whether he had ever actually known what had happened to either of them, and suddenly ten years of not-asking pressed on him at once.

Wrenn pried the brush from his fingers without a word, set it down, and poured two fingers of brandy from the bottle he kept for cleaning purposes and never for drinking.

"Drink," he said. "Then listen. When I was your age, in the last war, I repaired chronometers for the navy. Every instrument that came through my hands might steer a ship full of men onto rocks or home to harbor, and I never knew which, and it ate me alive for a year. Then an old master told me something I've repeated to every apprentice since, and I'm going to say it to you now, differently." He leaned forward, lamplight pooling in the seams of his face. "You cannot carry every name in your head, boy. You'll break. That's what the ledger *is* — a head bigger than yours, that doesn't break. Your job isn't to grieve all of them tonight. Your job is to move them. Grief is for afterward. Afterward is a place we have to build first. Do you understand me?"

Coral drank the brandy, which tasted of nothing and everything, and picked up the brush.

"Yes," he said. And found, strangely, that the shaking had stopped — not because the fear was gone, but because it had changed state, the way water changes at a boil. Fear that had been fluid and formless had become something with direction and force. He recognized it, distantly, as the thing the army probably mistook for weakness in civilians and was wrong about.

On the sixth night, a knock came at the shutters — three slow, deliberate raps that were neither patrol nor customer.

Wrenn blew out the lamp in one motion. In the sudden dark, Coral slid the ledger into the hollow beneath the treadle lathe that they'd prepared on the second night, a cavity accessible only by removing a plate held by screws filed with fake slots so they'd strip before turning. His heart hammered. Then the knock came again, and with it, low through the wood:

"It's me."

Emrys Hale's voice, pitched for the street, worn smooth of rank. Coral let him in through the rear door, glancing both ways down the alley — empty, blue with snow-light — before bolting it behind the young officer.

Emrys stood dripping in the doorway, greatcoat collar up, cap under his arm, and for a moment the three of them regarded each other in the relit lamp's glow: the apprentice, the master, and the enemy uniform that kept arriving at inconvenient hours to behave honorably.

"You should not be here," Wrenn said flatly. "Lieutenant, you should *never* be here."

"Probably not." Emrys's eyes moved across the bench — the stencils, the pigment, the row of dials drying on blotting paper, each one crowded with microscopically fine figures — and Coral watched understanding arrive in his face, followed by something that looked almost like awe. "That's the ledger?"

"Pieces of it," Coral said.

Emrys crossed the room slowly and picked up one of the watches, tilting it to the lamp. He studied it for a long moment, jaw tight. "How many of these?"

"Four finished. Eight more dials to paint. Then the case backs."

"And the original stays here." It wasn't a question. Emrys set the watch down with more care than he'd picked it up. "In this shop. Where Wrenn's name just appeared on a public list for obstruction."

The silence stretched. Coral looked at his mentor, and Wrenn looked back at him, and neither of them said anything, because they had already had this argument in every silence of the past week and had settled it.

"That's the arrangement," Coral said finally. "Someone respectable stays with it."

"Respectable," Emrys repeated, and there was something raw underneath the word. He turned to Wrenn. "Sir. I inventory this observatory on Friday. Colonel Vasse has ordered a secondary audit of all registered tradesmen — shops, workshops, premises. Yours will be visited within days. If they find the ledger here—"

"Then they'll find it in the custody of the town's licensed horologist, wrapped among forty other papers in a locked cabinet, with no reason whatsoever to open it, provided the searching officer is a fool," Wrenn said mildly. "If he is not a fool, then an old man's word that he's merely keeping his late colleague's accounts will buy him a hearing, which is more than a fleeing courier would get. My presence protects the ledger, Lieutenant. That is arithmetic, not bravery. Kindly do not romanticize me."

But Coral saw what Emrys saw: that the arithmetic had a remainder, and everyone in the room could compute it.

"I can get you out," Emrys said quietly. "Both of you. There are roads west that—"

"No," said Wrenn, with the finality of a weight driven home onto a arbor. And then, gentler, in the tone he used at the end of lessons: "Young man, you wore that uniform into my shop tonight knowing what it costs you every time. I'll wear my own uniform out of it — forty years at this bench, this town, these names. Different cloth, same debt." He gathered his tools, signaling the night's lesson was done. "Take Coral home the long way. And Coral—" Coral paused at the door. Wrenn didn't look up from the carriage clock he was reassembling, but his voice followed, level and clear as a struck bell.

"When the last dial is painted, you leave. No goodbyes worth remembering. A mechanism only fails when someone stops trusting it — and I trust you to go."

* * *

Emrys walked him home through streets gone silver and silent under a thin new frost. They took the long route as instructed, along the canal towpath where the ice was building its first glassy skin and the garrison's lanterns were distant smudges of orange. Their footsteps fell in rhythm without either of them willing it.

"He won't leave," Emrys said at last. It wasn't accusation. It was a soldier reporting terrain.

"No."

"Vasse doesn't put names on lists for decoration. If they take him—"

"I know what happens." Coral's breath hung white in the air. "He taught me which silences protect people. He's choosing one now. The least I owe him is to let it mean something."

Emrys was quiet for several paces. Then, in a lower voice, almost lost to the water lapping the stones: "When this is over — whatever 'over' means — I want you to know that I've started lying better than I pray. At chapel, I stand in the ranks and recite the words and feel nothing, and then I walk into Vasse's office and lie about inventories and my pulse doesn't rise two beats. I don't know which man that makes me."

"The one who came to the shop tonight," Coral said.

Emrys stopped walking. Under the frost-light his face was unreadable for a moment — and then he laughed, short and disbelieving, breath steaming, and Coral realized he had never heard the lieutenant laugh before and that the sound did something to the cold air that felt permanent.

At Coral's door, they parted the way they always parted: a formal nod, two feet of air between them, everything unsaid stacked up like crates in a warehouse. But at the corner, Emrys paused and called back softly:

"Friday. The audit reaches Wrenn's shop Monday at the latest. Whatever you haven't painted by Friday — paint faster."

Coral climbed the dark stairs to his rooms, lit no lamp, and sat at his window watching the garrison's fires across the valley. On his table lay five finished watches and a half-painted sixth, twelve names sleeping in each dial, seventy names carried already in his coat pocket while he slept.

Three days. He would need to work through tomorrow's repairs, through the curfew bell, through whatever fear remained in him after fear ran out.

Across the square, dim in the frost, he could just make out the notice board where the lists hung — and even at this distance, even in this light, his eye found the fourth line of the second posting and rested there, on a name in tidy clerk's handwriting that had taught him everything.

He wound the five finished watches, all together, five small hearts ticking in the dark.

Monday was coming. He intended to be gone before it.
