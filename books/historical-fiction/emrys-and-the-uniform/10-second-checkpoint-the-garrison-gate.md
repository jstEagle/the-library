## 10. Second Checkpoint: The Garrison Gate

The garrison gate did not look like a place where men died. It looked like a place where paperwork was born.

Coral had passed it a hundred times in the ordinary course of his apprenticeship — the wide arch of grey stone between the old customs house and the cooper's yard, hung since spring with the army's banners, through which grain carts and milk carts and funeral processions had all rolled without slowing. Now there were sandbags stacked in a horseshoe at its mouth, a machine gun on a tripod that no one pretended was decorative, and a sawhorse barrier with a painted board swinging from it: **ALL PERSONS AND GOODS SUBJECT TO INSPECTION.**

He came toward it in mid-morning light, pulling the little handcart Tobias Wrenn had used for forty years to deliver repaired clocks about town. The wheels squeaked on the cobbles in a rhythm Coral knew as well as his own pulse. On top of the cart, visible to any eye that cared to look, lay the regulator case — the long wooden coffin-shaped box he had carried across the bridge checkpoint three days earlier, its disassembled movement nested in straw, the ledger sewn flat into the false bottom beneath the chime barrel. He had rebuilt the disguise for this crossing with more care than he had ever given a customer's clock. The screws were seated at authentic depths. The dust of the workshop floor had been sprinkled over everything like seasoning.

The queue ahead of him was short and slow. A woman with a crate of live chickens. Two farmers with identity cards gone soft at the corners from being handled too often. A boy of perhaps fifteen leading a horse and trying not to look like a boy who might, in six months' time, be worth conscripting.

Coral counted the soldiers. Six at the barrier. One at the machine gun, bored. One leaning in the archway's shade with his rifle slung and his attention on nothing, which was worse than boredom — that kind of soldier was thinking.

And then there was the sergeant.

Coral saw him before the sergeant saw Coral, which gave him perhaps ten seconds to study the enemy, and he used them. The man was past forty, built like something carved out of the same grey stone as the arch, with a face that had stopped expressing things decades ago and a small red ribbon above his left breast pocket that meant he had been somewhere unpleasant and come back. He did not wave people through. He looked at each one — not their papers first, but their faces, their hands, their loads — and only then asked for documents, as though papers were an afterthought to whatever arithmetic he was doing behind those still eyes.

*He's the kind they send when they've lost something,* Coral thought, and his mouth went dry. Word had come down after the bridge — Emrys had warned him of it two nights ago, his voice low in the dark of the observatory: *Vasse knows a courier got through. Every checkpoint has new orders. They're not looking for faces anymore. They're looking for anything that doesn't fit.*

The chicken woman passed. The farmers passed, after a long minute of the sergeant turning their cards over and over. The boy with the horse passed, though the sergeant made him open his saddlebags and held up a folded newspaper and read the front page before letting it go.

Then it was Coral's turn.

"Name and business."

"Coral Ashgrove. Clockmaker's apprentice." He kept his voice level by an effort that felt physical, like holding a heavy weight at arm's length. "Delivering a repaired regulator to Mrs. Halloway's house on Chandler Street. Repair order here, signed and stamped." He produced the paper Emrys had forged — one of Emrys's forgeries, good enough that Coral himself had almost believed it, bearing the signature of a garrison quartermaster who had, Coral prayed, no reason ever to check his own hand.

The sergeant did not take the paper. He looked past it, down at the cart.

"That's an observatory regulator case."

The words landed in Coral's chest like a dropped weight.

"I— yes, sir. Observatory pattern. My master built the case himself, years back, when—"

"I know what it is." The sergeant's voice never rose. That was the terrible thing about it; it simply proceeded, like water finding its level. "I stood post at that observatory for eight months before the war college. Carried a case just like it down that hill twice a year when Wrenn sent his work out. He never let anyone else touch his cases. Never let anyone else carry them." The still eyes came up from the cart and settled on Coral's face. "Wrenn doesn't send work out anymore. I've read the notices. Wrenn's under citation for obstruction."

The world narrowed to the width of the sergeant's gaze. Behind Coral, the queue shuffled. Ahead, beyond the barrier, Chandler Street ran off into the ordinary morning, impossibly close, impossibly far.

"My master is unwell," Coral said. "His hands shake now. He trains me to do the carrying. That's what apprentices are for." It was true, or nearly true, or true enough — Wrenn's *hands* were fine, but the rest of the sentence was the shape of truth, and Coral clung to that shape the way a drowning man clings to driftwood. "The repair order has the observatory's seal."

"Seals get printed." The sergeant stepped around the cart, unhurried, and Coral understood with cold clarity that this man was not going to be fooled by straw and screw-depths and workshop dust. This man had carried these cases himself. This man knew their weight.

"Open it."

Coral's heart was a runaway escapement, spinning free. He knelt — slowly, deliberately slowly, the way a man kneels when he has nothing to hide — and unlatched the case. The smell of straw and machine oil rose up. He lifted the movement tray with both hands, presenting the dismantled regulator: the great brass wheel train wrapped in cloth, the pendulum rod lying bare along its velvet channel, the chime barrel gleaming dully.

The sergeant leaned over it. And Coral watched the man's eyes move across the works with the unhurried thoroughness of someone who actually understood what he was seeing — and Coral realized, with a fresh drop of dread, that this was worse than ignorance. At the bridge, three days ago, the soldier had seen brass shapes and been satisfied by their plausibility. This man would see if a wheel was missing. This man would know that a regulator of this size came apart into precisely so many pieces, and would count them.

His eyes drifted down. Toward the base of the case. Toward the chime barrel's mounting plate — toward the seam Coral had cut and re-fitted so carefully, invisible to any eye that didn't know to measure it.

"Lieutenant Hale."

The name came from behind Coral, from the shade of the archway — the idle soldier, the one who had been watching nothing. Coral's blood stopped and started again in the same half-second, because the voice had said the name the way one salutes a rank, and when Coral turned his head, Emrys Hale was pushing off the wall and walking toward them in the full grey of his officer's uniform, cap squared, stride even, every inch the machine the army had built him to be.

*He was here the whole time.* Coral's mind reeled. *He followed me. No — he posted himself here. He knew this gate was the danger and he stood in the shade and waited, and I never saw him.*

"Sergeant Ossler," Emrys said, stopping at the barrier. His voice was mild. His eyes did not meet Coral's at all, and Coral understood that too — the deliberate blankness, the refusal to acknowledge anything worth a sergeant's suspicion.

"Sir." Ossler straightened, but not much. Old soldiers straighten exactly as far as they must.

"You've got Ashgrove there held up. Is there a problem with his order?"

"There's a problem with the whole observatory, sir, if you'll pardon me. Colonel Vasse's directive of last week — all observatory property subject to verification against the master inventory. This apprentice is hauling a regulator case that should be sitting on the hill, and his master is under citation. With respect, sir, I'd want to know how observatory property came to be walking down the hill in a handcart."

"It walked down the hill," Emrys said, "because I signed the requisition."

A silence. Somewhere behind them a chicken complained; somewhere further off, boots on stone, a patrol passing.

"The regulator at the observatory has a cracked escapement cradle," Emrys went on, in the tone of a man reciting something tedious he had explained many times already. "The master clock depends on it for the sidereal correction — you'd know that better than most, Sergeant, having stood post there. Without the regulator, the observatory can't issue time signals to the garrison, and the Colonel's own directive requires the garrison to keep accurate signals. So I signed a work order Monday last authorizing the apprentice to remove the instrument, effect repairs at the shop on Tanner's Row, and return it within the week. The order will be in the garrison logbook. You're welcome to verify it."

Ossler's jaw worked. Coral watched the man think, and watched the thought move through him like weather: the instinct to dig, warring with the fact that a lieutenant had just handed him, in front of six soldiers and a queue of civilians, a perfectly documented reason why digging would be insubordinate.

"With respect, sir," Ossler said at last, slowly, "the Colonel's been very particular about the observatory lately. If property's leaving the hill, I'd expect the Colonel himself to have countersigned."

"Then write to the Colonel," Emrys said. "Ask him whether Lieutenant Hale's standing repair authority covers horological instruments. Ask him whether he wants the garrison's chronometers drifting because his checkpoint held a clock part on the cobbles for an hour." He paused, and something in his voice shifted — not warmer, but heavier, the way a scale tips. "Or ask me, Sergeant, and I'll tell you plainly: I have inventory responsibility for that building. If that instrument isn't repaired and back on the hill by Friday, the irregularity lands on my record, not yours. Do you want to explain to the Colonel why you delayed an authorized repair?"

It was beautifully done. Coral could see the shape of it even through his fear: every sentence true in isolation, each one a small verifiable fact arranged like gears in a train, so that the whole mechanism turned smoothly toward exactly one outcome. But Coral also saw what the sergeant saw, or something near it — the lieutenant spending himself. Because Emrys had just claimed, on the record, in front of witnesses, ownership of the observatory's comings and goings. He had tied his name to Coral's cart with wire that would not easily be cut. If Vasse ever pulled this thread — if Ossler mentioned it to the wrong clerk, if the logbook entry drew a curious eye — the trail would run straight to Emrys Hale, and there would be no gear-train of truths to hide inside.

Ossler looked at the regulator works a moment longer. Then he reached in, lifted the chime barrel with one blunt finger, weighed it, set it back in its channel. His eyes crossed the mounting plate. Crossed it again.

Coral did not breathe.

"Reassemble your instrument," Ossler said, stepping back. "And tell Wrenn his cases always were the best-built things in this town." He turned away, waving forward the next person in the queue, and the barrier swung aside.

* *

Coral got the case closed and the cart moving before his hands remembered how badly they wanted to shake. He took the cart up Chandler Street, around the cooper's yard, through two turns he had rehearsed until they were reflex, and into the yard behind the Halloway house where the delivery was supposed to end — except there was no delivery, there never had been; the address was a fiction, and the real cargo would travel onward tomorrow by another route. For now he only stood in the yard's shade with his palms flat on the cart's handlebar, breathing.

Emrys found him there eleven minutes later, having walked out the gate at a leisurely officer's pace and taken a route that touched no other soldier's sightline. He came through the yard gate, and the moment it latched behind him, all the mildness fell off his face like a dropped mask.

"Never," he said quietly, "do that to me again."

"Do *what?*" Coral's voice cracked on the word. "Walk through your checkpoint with your contraband in a clock case, at your request?"

"Take the main road at mid-morning with Ossler on duty. I told you the rotation. I told you Tuesday mornings were his. I told you—" Emrys stopped himself. Pressed two fingers to the bridge of his nose. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to something rawer. "He recognized the case in four seconds. Four. I counted them from that archway while I decided whether I was going to have to shoot a sergeant of the garrison in front of his own men."

"You'd have—"

"No." Emrys lowered his hand. "I wouldn't have. That's the hell of it. I'd have let him arrest us both, and we'd be in a cell right now, and it would be cleaner than what I actually did." He laughed once, without any humor in it at all, and sat down heavily on the mounting block beside the stable door. In the plain daylight, out of uniform-bearing, he looked younger than his rank and older than his years, both at once.

Coral came and sat beside him. The stones were cold through his trousers. For a while neither spoke, and the sounds of the occupied town filtered over the wall — a handcart, a door, a drill sergeant's distant counting.

"What you spent back there," Coral said finally. "I watched you spend it. Your authority. Your name in front of his men. You put yourself in the logbook next to me, Emrys. If anyone ever pulls that thread—"

"They won't pull it today."

"But someday. And when they do, it won't be a cell for you. Sergeants go to cells. Lieutenants who forge orders and falsify inventories go against walls." He heard his own voice shaking and could not stop it. "I've been doing the arithmetic since the bridge. Every time you step between me and them, you pay something out of yourself that you don't get back. Your credibility isn't endless. It's a mainspring, Emrys. It winds down. And I can't sit here and watch you burn it coil by coil and pretend that's a fair trade."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. Then he said, looking at the middle distance, "My father kept a shop in the capital. Stationer's. Paper, ink, ledgers." A thin smile. "Ledgers. I only understand the joke of that now. When they erased him — struck his name from the register, dissolved the lease, reassigned the building — I was fourteen. There was no trial. No paper I could find, and my father was a man who believed in paper. The system that ate him left no record of the eating." He turned and looked at Coral directly, and his eyes were very clear. "I enlisted to walk around inside the machine and learn where it keeps its teeth. Everything I have in it — the rank, the credibility, the access — exists for one purpose. To be spent. I've just spent some of it the way I always intended. So don't stand there telling me the price is unfair. The price is the point. It's the only purchase I've ever wanted to make."

Coral wanted to argue. He opened his mouth and found nothing in it that could stand against that, and closed it again, and instead did something he had not planned: he put his hand over Emrys's where it rested on the cold stone between them. Neither of them looked at it. Neither moved it. The gesture sat there in the daylight, small and enormous, in flagrant violation of every regulation either of them had ever lived under.

"You should know," Coral said softly, "that at the gate, when you said my name — when you stepped out of that archway — for one second I forgot to be afraid. That's the most dangerous thing that's happened to me yet. Not the sergeant. That second."

"Mine was worse," Emrys said. "When I saw Ossler lean over the chime barrel, my first thought wasn't *the mission's blown.* It was *he's going to arrest Coral.*" He exhaled. "That's how it goes, then. That's what this is."

"Yes," Coral said. "I suppose it is."

They sat with it, the admission, letting it be true in the cold yard, because there was no safer place and no later time, and because men walking toward a third checkpoint could not afford to leave important things unsaid.

Emrys stood first, and the officer returned to his posture like a coat going back on. But his hand lingered on Coral's shoulder a moment longer than regulation allowed.

"One more thing," he said, and his voice changed — flattened, urgent. "I came out here with more than a warning about Ossler. There was a posting on the garrison board this morning. New citations." He hesitated, and Coral felt the ground tilt before Emrys even said it. "Tobias Wrenn. Obstruction, second count. The citation converts to a tribunal hearing Thursday."

"Thursday," Coral repeated. The word felt like a stripped gear, spinning uselessly. "That's two days."

"Two days," Emrys said quietly. "If you're not on the mountain road by Thursday night with that ledger, Coral, then everything he does next — everything he's already done — buys nothing at all. Go home. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow I'll draw you the mountain road passes, and we finish this."

He straightened his cap, became entirely a lieutenant again, and walked out through the yard gate toward the garrison, leaving Coral alone in the cold with a clock case full of stolen names and, suddenly, a countdown ticking underneath everything — two days, two days, two days — like a mechanism running down toward some hour he could not bear to imagine.
