## 11. Found Family in a Wine Cellar

The smuggler's tavern was called the Salt-Cured Herring, which Coral thought an honest name for a dishonest place, and he found it by the method Wrenn had taught him years ago: follow the street that pretends it doesn't lead anywhere. It ran down behind the tanneries, where the smell kept respectable people away and the cobbles gave way to mud, and at its end stood a low building with a sagging lintel and a sign so faded it might once have said anything at all.

He went in at half past nine, as instructed, carrying his toolbag like a man who wanted nothing but a drink and a corner to drink it in. The taproom held six people, none of whom looked at him, which was itself a kind of welcome. A woman with forearms like a blacksmith's drew him a cup of something dark without being asked.

"Clockmaker," she said. Not a question.

"Repair of domestic timepieces," Coral answered, giving the second half of the phrase Wrenn had drilled into him that afternoon. "Domestic only."

"Down and left," she said. "Mind the third stair. It lies."

The cellar stairs were narrow and smelled of brine and lamp oil, and the third stair did indeed lie — it shifted under his boot with a hollow clack, and Coral froze, listening. Nothing above changed. He stepped over it the rest of the way down.

The wine cellar was long and low, arched in old brick furred with salt bloom, and it was fuller than he expected. Casks along one wall, most of them genuine if the smell meant anything. But against the far wall, half-hidden behind a rack, someone had built a partition of crates and canvas, and from behind it came light, and voices, and — this undid him for a moment — laughter.

A man looked up when Coral came around the partition: forty-ish, ink-stained to the elbows, spectacles pushed up into thinning hair. He was seated before a small hand press, the kind that fit on a table, surrounded by paper.

"You'd be Ashgrove," he said. "Wrenn's boy."

"Coral. Yes." He hadn't been anyone's boy in ten years, and hearing it made something in his chest go strange.

"Fen Arledge." The printer did not offer a hand — his were black to the wrists — but he nodded toward the room beyond, where the partition evidently continued. "We've company tonight. Come and meet them, then we'll talk routes."

Coral followed him past a second crate-wall into what must have been an older cellar, a root cellar once, dug into the hill itself. It was warm here, warm enough that a single candle made it feel lit rather than dark, and there was a stove, a proper little iron stove with a pipe going up through the stone, and around the stove sat four people.

One was a nun in a grey habit gone soft with age, mending a coat that could not possibly have belonged to her. Two children — a girl of perhaps nine and a boy of five or six — lay asleep on pallets, the girl's arm flung across the boy protectively even in sleep. And sitting upright on a barrel, watching Coral arrive with the flat, unblinking assessment of someone who had learned not to trust faces, was the girl herself — no, no, she was asleep; the watcher was a second woman, young, plain-dressed, with the callused hands of a laundress and eyes that had stopped being young some time ago.

"Sister Perpetua," Fen said, indicating the nun. "Marta. And those two—" he tipped his head at the sleeping children "—are the whole reason this hole exists."

"They're not mine," Marta said, as though Coral had asked, or accused. "Their father worked the garrison stables. They erased him in March. There wasn't a record of him after that, so there couldn't be a record of them, so the school struck them off, and the landlord—" she stopped, and started again, flatter. "Nobody has to house people who don't exist. It's very tidy."

Coral stood in the doorway with his bag in his hand and understood, all at once and bodily, what the ledger in its disassembled hiding place actually weighed. Names. This name, their father's name, was in it somewhere — he had read it, he realized, entry after entry in that cramped dead man's hand, *stablehand, garrison, requisitioned then erased,* and he had felt it as dread, as abstraction, as history happening to strangers. Now the abstraction had an arm flung over its brother.

"I'm sorry," he said, and heard how useless it was, and hated it.

"It's not your doing," Sister Perpetua said mildly, not looking up from the coat. Her voice was the voice of someone who had said similar things many times and meant them every time. "Sit down, clockmaker. You look like a man who hasn't eaten since the bridge."

"How—"

"Fen talks in his sleep, and everyone in Vellmarsh knows about the bridge now." She bit her thread, held the coat up, considered a sleeve. "You got through. That's the part that matters. Marta, there's bread."

There was bread, and hard cheese, and a jug of watered wine that Fen pressed on him before Coral could refuse. He ate sitting on an overturned crate, and the food was ordinary, and the warmth of the stove was ordinary, and yet he had to keep pausing because his throat kept closing. He tried to remember the last time he had sat in a room where nobody wanted anything audited from him. His lodgings didn't count; Mrs. Pell counted his comings and goings with anxious eyes since her name had appeared on the posted list, and her worry was a weight he carried whether he visited or not. Before that — the shop, and Wrenn, and even Wrenn loved through instruction, through correction, through the endless patient teaching of which silences protect. Coral had spent ten years being useful and alone, and he had told himself it was the shape of his life, the way a mainspring's shape is set, coiled tight by design.

The children woke near midnight, as children do, without ceremony. The boy simply sat up, saw a stranger, and stared; the girl woke more carefully, checking exits first.

"This is Coral," Marta said. "He fixes clocks."

"He fixes the big one?" the boy demanded, pointing upward, toward the hill, toward the observatory Coral could not see but knew was there, keeping its broken time over all of them.

"The biggest one."

"My da said the observatory clock never lies," the girl said, from her pallet, in the tone of someone contributing to a serious discussion. "He said whatever else they change, the clock keeps true time."

*Kept,* Coral almost said. *It stopped.* But he looked at her face, at the absolute faith in it, and instead he said, "That's right. And I'm going to make sure it goes on telling the truth."

Sister Perpetua's needle paused for just a moment. Fen glanced up from his type tray. Neither commented. But Coral felt the sentence land among them like a coin dropped in a collection plate, and understood that he had just promised these strangers something, and that they had banked it.

The boy, whose name was Tam, fell asleep again within minutes against Marta's shoulder. The girl, Sela, did not. She relocated to a spot nearer the candle and produced from somewhere a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper, and began drawing the observatory from memory — a round tower, a dome, a flagpole rendered with great care.

"You know it well," Coral said.

"We lived on Chandler's Row before." She didn't look up. "You can see it from our window. You could. Our window's somebody else's now."

She drew the dome twice, dissatisfied both times, and Coral, who had never in his life spoken to a child except to be examined by one, heard himself say, "The curve's wrong because you're drawing it flat. It's shallower than you think. Like this—" and he took the pencil and showed her, a single arc, and she studied it with fierce concentration and copied it perfectly, and something in Coral's chest that had been rusted shut for a decade swung open with a creak he could nearly hear.

*This is what you never let yourself have,* he thought, sitting in the warmth with pencil shavings and sleeping children and a nun who hummed while she mended. *This is the thing you decided you didn't get to want.*

And underneath the warmth, cold and rising like water into a bilge, came the other thought, the one he had been outrunning since the bridge:

*I am bringing it here. All of it. Every soldier, every checkpoint, Colonel Vasse himself, if he learns what passes through this cellar.*

Because the ledger existed, and Vasse wanted it, and Coral was now a thread in a web with children at the center of it. He thought about the sergeant at the garrison gate, the sharp-eyed one, the way his gaze had snagged on the observatory stamps on Coral's tools before Emrys had swept in burning his own credibility like torch-paper. If that sergeant talked — and sergeants talked — then a description of Coral Ashgrove was already circulating, attached to suspicion, and here Coral sat drinking the network's wine in the network's cellar.

Fen spread a map of the capital road across two casks at nearly two in the morning, weighting the corners with type blocks. Marta took the children back to the pallets and got them down again with the practiced economy of someone who had done it in worse places, then joined them at the map, standing close, her shoulder near Coral's, reading upside down.

"There are three ways up the mountain road," Fen said. "Only one matters, because only one has the checkpoint, and the checkpoint is why you're here instead of on a mail coach like a sensible man."

"Wrenn says the mountain checkpoint is the weak one," Coral said. "Undermanned. The captain there is said to take bribes and look away."

Fen and Marta exchanged a glance. It was quick, but Coral caught it, and it dropped through him like cold water finding a crack.

"What," he said.

"Nothing," Fen said, too fast, and then, because printers are bad liars — they deal all day in exactness — he rubbed his eyes and admitted, "Wrenn's information is three weeks old. Things have changed on the mountain road. We don't send people that way anymore. We send them through the marshes, north, which takes four days and costs you your boots and probably your health, but it's *empty*, and empty is worth more than cheap."

"Why has it changed?"

"New men," Marta said. "Since the records seizure, they've doubled the mountain garrison. And there's a rumor — just a rumor — that they're watching for a courier. Someone leaked that papers would move. Somebody always leaks."

Coral stood very still. The candle flame leaned and steadied. *Watching for a courier.* Which meant either coincidence, which he didn't believe in — he was a horologist, he believed in cause and escapement, in every effect having a mechanism — or someone had talked, or someone had guessed, and if someone had guessed then Emrys was exposed, and if Emrys was exposed then everything they had built was already swinging toward its fall.

"You knew this," Coral said slowly, "before I came. Wrenn sent me anyway."

"Wrenn sent you because you carry something worth four days of marsh," Fen said. "And because he trusts you. And because—" Fen hesitated, and chose honesty, which Coral would remember later as the thing that made him trust Fen back. "Because Wrenn knows he's being watched. His name went up on the list yesterday, obstruction, and a man on the list stops being a courier and starts being evidence. He needed the ledger out of his orbit. Out of his shop. So he sent it to you, and you to us, and yes, he knew the mountain road was hot, and yes, I argued with him about it for an hour, and no, I didn't win."

"He won," Marta said grimly. "He usually does. Old men who've survived three governments always do."

"He stayed behind," Coral said. "He told me someone respectable had to stay."

"That's Wrenn," Fen said softly. "He thinks he's armor. He thinks if they arrest the respectable clockmaker, the town will say *see, even him*, and the saying of it will slow them down a week. Maybe two weeks. Maybe long enough." He straightened, and his ink-stained hands flattened on the map. "So. The marshes, or wait for the roads to cool, or—" he looked at Coral with a printer's directness "—or you tell me what's so valuable it's worth a man's life on a mountain road, and I'll decide if my cellar's worth holding it another week."

Coral looked at the map. At the northern line threading through the marshes, four days, empty, brutal. At the mountain road, direct, fast, and — if the rumor held — baited.

Then he looked back toward the inner room, where two children slept whose names appeared nowhere in any register in Vellmarsh, whose existence the army had declared a clerical impossibility, and who were nonetheless entirely real, breathing slow and even in the candlelight, guarded by a nun and a laundress and a printer with ink to his elbows.

"I'll show you," Coral said. "Part of it. Enough for you to judge."

He crossed to his toolbag, knelt, and with a watchmaker's screwdriver opened the false bottom of the regulator case he'd built at Wrenn's bench — brass plates, jeweled bearings, every component genuine except the compartment beneath the balance wheel, where a packet wrapped in oilcloth lay snug as a pulse. He unwrapped it halfway, let Fen and Marta see the first page only — the heading, the column of names, the late head horologist's cramped hand — and closed it again before their faces could finish changing.

Fen sat down on his stool as if his legs had been taken from under him.

"That's why they sealed the records," he said hoarsely. "That's not a list of requisitions. That's a confession. That's *hundreds*—"

"And it reaches the capital," Coral said, wrapping the oilcloth with steady fingers, steadier than he felt, "or every name in it stays erased forever. Including hers." He nodded toward the inner room. "Including her father's."

Silence, except the tick of Fen's wall-regulator — a smuggler's timer, Coral noted absently, good quality — and the stove's small settling breaths.

"The marshes, then," Marta said at last.

"No," Coral said, and heard Wrenn's voice in his own, the old lesson arriving at last in full: *a mechanism only fails when someone stops trusting it.* They expect the courier to hide. Every trick, every disguise, every marsh and false bottom — that's what they're watching for. "I haven't decided the route yet. Give me until tomorrow night. I need to think like a clock, not like a fugitive."

Fen studied him for a long moment, then nodded and began rolling up the map. "Beds are pallets and privacy is theoretical," he said. "But you're among your own now, clockmaker. Whatever else happens — you're among your own."

Coral lay down after four, fully dressed, the regulator case under the pallet frame with its heart against the stone. He slept maybe two hours, and dreamed of the observatory clock striking true while soldiers climbed the tower stairs, and woke at dawn to the sound of Sister Perpetua already up, quietly mending, humming.

It was Fen who brought the morning's news down the stairs, white-faced above his ink stains, the folded gazette shaking in his hand.

"Coral," he said. "They've arrested Wrenn."
