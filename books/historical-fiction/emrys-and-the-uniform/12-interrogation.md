## 12. Interrogation

The summons came at six in the morning, delivered by a corporal who would not meet Emrys's eyes.

*Colonel Vasse requests Lieutenant Hale's presence at the garrison. Immediately.*

*Requests.* In three months of occupation, Emrys had never once been *requested*. He was scheduled, rotated, dismissed. Requests were what the colonel extended to men he was measuring for something — a promotion or a grave, and Vasse rarely made the two difficult to distinguish until it was too late.

Emrys dressed with the particular care he gave to inspections. Uniform pressed, boots polished to a dull correctness rather than a gleam — a gleam invited comment, and comment invited scrutiny. He checked his pockets the way a man checks his conscience: thoroughly, and finding nothing, not quite believing it. The ledger was gone from his rooms, gone from the observatory, folded into whatever hiding place Coral Ashgrove had devised inside a clock case, and if Vasse's men had found it, this morning summons would be the least of his concerns.

He looked at himself in the cracked mirror of his quarters. A lieutenant of the Ninth Garrison Regiment, twenty-six years old, four years of service, one commendation for orderly administration. That was what the glass showed. What it could not show was the inventory document in his desk drawer with three falsified line items, or the memory of Coral's face when Emrys had pocketed the ledger like a common thief and walked out into the rain.

*You are either the bravest man I know or the biggest fool,* Ashgrove had said.

*There's no meaningful difference,* Emrys had answered, and been surprised at how much he'd meant it.

He straightened his collar and went to find out which one the colonel intended to make of him.

* * *

The garrison had been built as a courthouse under some earlier regime, and its interrogation room still carried the furniture of law: a long oak table, tall windows barred more for ornament than security, a hearth that had never in living memory held a fire. Someone had moved in a single iron chair and set it on the side of the table facing away from the windows. The geometry of the room told you everything before anyone spoke. Sit where we can watch your face. Sit in the light while you do it.

Vasse was already there, of course. The colonel conducted his life like a siege — he arrived first, ate last, slept less than anyone he commanded, and treated all of it as proof of his superiority rather than his joylessness. He was fifty-odd, grey as gunmetal, with small precise hands that he kept folded on the table like instruments laid out for surgery. Two chairs stood empty beside him. Emrys noted them and understood. This was to look like a panel. It was to feel like a crowd.

"Lieutenant Hale." Vasse did not rise. "Sit."

Emrys sat. Not in the iron chair — that was across the table, positioned for someone else, someone who hadn't been invited to sit at all. He took the plain wooden one at the table's end, angled enough toward the window that he would not be squinting into the light. Small resistances. They were all he had left, and he had learned to spend them carefully.

"You're wondering why you're here." Vasse opened a folder — not dramatically, not slowly, just with the efficiency of a man who resented ceremony even when it served him. "I'll tell you. Property has gone missing from the observatory inventory. You certified that inventory complete."

"The inventory was complete as of the fourteenth," Emrys said. His voice came out level. It always did. That was the trouble — it always did, no matter what was underneath.

"As of the fourteenth." Vasse turned the folder around and slid it across the oak. Photographs, actual photographs, which meant money had been spent, which meant the matter had climbed high enough to frighten someone in the capital. The observatory's master workshop, shot from three angles. An open tool cabinet. A regulator case lying disassembled on a bench. "Master clock stopped. Head horologist deceased — natural causes, we're told, though the timing is convenient. And an apprentice, one C. Ashgrove, who appears in the requisition ledgers of three separate towns under slightly different spellings of his surname. Do you know him?"

"I've met him." Keep it flat. Keep it bored. "He performed repairs under my supervision. Clockwork is outside my competence, Colonel, but the army requires the observatory functional, so someone had to sign off on the work."

"And the work required—" Vasse's finger touched the photograph of the disassembled regulator, "—this?"

"Ashgrove said the escapement had failed. He stripped it for transport back to his shop. I signed a requisition for the parts."

"He never collected the parts."

"Then he repaired it in place after all. I don't audit clockmakers, sir. I audit rifles and rations, and I do it well. If you'd like a report on the observatory's escapement, I can request one from a man qualified to write it."

The silence that followed had weight. Emrys let it sit without filling it. He had learned that trick young, at his father's table, in the years after his father stopped existing — the silence is a tool, boy; whoever reaches for it first has already lost it. He reached for nothing. He watched Vasse's small hands and waited.

"You have a reputation, Lieutenant Hale." Vasse leaned back. Outside, a supply wagon ground past the barred windows, and the light shifted across the table between them like something being dealt. "Efficient. Quiet. Punctual to the minute. Your superiors at the academy described you as 'a machine.'" A thin smile, the first. "I have always found that men described as machines are either very good or very hollow. Which are you?"

"Both, sir, on alternating days."

Something flickered behind the colonel's eyes — amusement, possibly, or the recognition of a fellow practitioner. Because that was the thing Emrys had understood within thirty seconds of sitting down, the thing that sat in his stomach like swallowed ice: Vasse knew how to lie fluently too. This whole interview was conducted in lies. The empty chairs were a lie about procedure. The photographs were a lie about certainty — a man with proof did not need an interrogation, he needed a cell and a firing detail. Even the folder was a lie; folders like that were assembled to be read across a table, their contents curated, their omissions deliberate.

Vasse suspected. Vasse had almost nothing. And both of them knew the other knew it.

"There was a ledger," Vasse said.

And there it was, laid down without ornament, the way you set a blade on a table between two men.

"The observatory kept maintenance records," Emrys said. "Logbooks, calibration notes. They were surrendered with the rest of the official papers."

"They were. I have read them. Fascinating reading, if you enjoy watching a man record humidity for thirty years." Vasse's voice remained conversational, almost gentle, which was worse than shouting. Gentle was for when he wanted you to relax. "But there's a story among the townsfolk — you hear these things, even through walls, even through loyalty oaths — that old Horvath kept a second book. A book of names. Every soul in this province marked for conscription, requisition, or removal, entered by hand over decades, because Horvath believed that a list is power and power should be kept close to the heart." He unfolded his hands for the first time and pressed them flat on the table. "If such a book existed, Lieutenant, and if it fell into the wrong hands, it would be a weapon. Names, ranks, addresses, dates. Everything the requisition apparatus depends upon, mirrored and exposed. It would take a year to rebuild what one thief could destroy in an afternoon." He paused. "So you understand why I am asking you directly, officer to officer, whether you have seen it."

"I have not."

Two words. Emrys heard them leave his mouth smooth as river stones, polished by four years of military correspondence, of casualty reports written in passive voice, of the phrase *regrettable losses* deployed over paperwork that had real faces behind it somewhere. He had lied his whole career in small administrative increments, and every increment had prepared him for this moment, and he hated it — hated the fluency itself, the way the lie required no effort, no cost, no tell. A man ought to pay something for a lie. Ought to feel the coin leave his hand. But his hand didn't even move.

Vasse studied him for a long moment.

"You know what I find instructive about clocks?" the colonel said. "A clock cannot lie. Every gear that turns leaves wear on the gear beside it. Every hand that moves testifies to the spring behind it. You open the case and the truth is all there is — a mechanism's history is written in brass, and brass does not perjure itself." He rose, crossed to the window, stood looking out at the town below, red roofs stacked down the hillside like spilled coins. "People are different. People will swear the mechanism is sound while the mainspring is snapped in their coat pocket. I have hanged men on less certainty than I currently possess regarding you, Lieutenant Hale, and I mention that not as a threat but as arithmetic. Certainty accumulates. Yours is not yet sufficient. But it is accumulating."

"Sir, if I've given cause for suspicion—"

"You've given cause for *attention*, which is different, and considerably more dangerous." Vasse turned back. "Your repair orders for the Ashgrove boy. Produce them today. Your duty roster for the nights of the eleventh through the fifteenth. Your signature on the observatory inventory will remain on file pending review. And Lieutenant—" the smile again, thinner, "—you will dine with me tomorrow evening. I find I prefer to keep my accumulating certainties close. Where they can be watched."

Emrys stood, saluted, and was dismissed with a flick of those small precise fingers, and walked out of the courthouse-garrison on legs that felt borrowed, down the steps into the cold morning, and only at the bottom realized his hands were shaking.

Not during. Never during. During, he had been a machine — the academy's word, Vasse's word, and God, how the words came home to roost. It was only after, in the ordinary air, that the cost presented itself for payment: the understanding that the lie had cost him nothing, that his conscience had stood aside like a sentry waving through an officer with proper papers. Duty and conscience had shared his body for four years the way two tenants share a thin-walled room, tolerating each other, keeping quiet hours.

One of them had just been evicted. He no longer knew which.

* * *

He spent the afternoon producing documents, because producing them promptly was the only defense against a man like Vasse — guilt hid best inside diligence. The repair orders were genuine; that much of his paper trail was honest, because Emrys had learned early that the strongest forgery is the document nobody needed to forge. The duty rosters placed him exactly where he had actually been, which was nowhere, doing nothing, on the nights that mattered. He wrote out each form in his careful machine-hand and thought, *brass does not perjure itself,* and wondered what Vasse would say if he knew that the colonel's own metaphor had become the creed of the man stealing his evidence — that Coral Ashgrove trusted mechanisms precisely because they couldn't lie, and had built his whole courage on it.

At dusk he walked the long way back to his quarters, along the lane beneath the observatory hill, because the long way passed the clockmaker's shop, and because he had decided — in the way one decides things that were decided long ago — that he needed to see a light in the window.

It was there. Faint, hooded, the lamp turned low. Behind the darkened glass he could make out the shape of Coral at his bench, head bent, hands moving with that terrible patient precision, doing — if Emrys knew anything anymore — exactly what he should not be doing: copying names onto watch faces, miniatures of the miniature, redundancy layered into redundancy, so that the ledger could die in a dozen places and survive in one.

Emrys did not stop. Stopping was attention; attention was accumulation. He walked past like any officer on any evening, boots striking the cobbles in regulation time, and felt the shop's warmth pass through the cold air and touch him anyway, briefly, like a hand between the shoulder blades.

But the walk had done its work. Halfway up his own street he understood what had been assembling itself in him since the courthouse, the thought arriving with the calm of a mechanism reaching the hour:

Vasse dined with men before he destroyed them. Tomorrow evening there would be wine and courtesy and questions shaped like compliments, and Emrys would perform fluency one final time across a table set for two. And whatever happened at that dinner, the margin was gone. Vasse's arithmetic did not subtract. It only added, night by night, document by document, until the sum was sufficient and the sentence followed like a shadow follows a body.

Which meant Coral could not stay in that shop with its hooded lamp and its patient hands. Which meant the plan — the checkpoints, the courier route, the slow careful smuggling that Tobias Wrenn had spent a week mapping — could no longer afford its own patience.

Which meant tonight.

Emrys reached his door, paused with his key in the lock, and looked back down toward the hill, where the observatory stood black against the last grey light, its stopped clock face blind above the town like a socket missing an eye.

Tomorrow he would lie to Vasse across a dinner table and buy whatever hours the lie could purchase. Tonight there was a back stair to the clockmaker's shop, and a forged pass in his desk with a name on it that was not his, and a man inside who trusted mechanisms more than people and had, without ever saying so, taught Emrys Hale the same faith.

He went in to get the pass. From down the valley, faint as breath, the town's smaller clocks began to strike the hour — and Emrys counted the strokes, and waited, as a man waits when he knows that everything he has left to give is time, and that the spending of it starts now.
