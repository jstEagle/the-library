## 13. Wrenn's Last Wind

The notice went up on the garrison wall on a Tuesday morning, and by noon the whole town knew Tobias Wrenn's name was on it.

Coral heard it first from the baker's boy, who delivered the news breathless and half-frightened, as though the words themselves might be contraband: *Old Wrenn, they say he's been at the posted lists. Scratching them out. In broad daylight, with a pencil.* The boy lingered on Coral's doorstep just long enough to see how the news landed, then fled before Coral could ask anything more.

Coral stood in his workshop with a half-repaired carriage clock in his hands, its tiny escapement balanced between two fingers, and felt the world tilt slightly on its axis. Not surprise — he had known Wrenn for eleven years, since the day the old man had caught a thirteen-year-old orphan picking the lock of the shop's back door not to steal anything but only to look at the regulator clocks, and had said, instead of calling the constable, *Well. If you're going to pick locks, boy, you'll learn to do it without scratching the plate.*

Not surprise. Something worse. The sick certainty that comes when you have watched weather gather for days and it finally breaks over your own roof.

He put down the clock, put on his coat, and walked up the hill to the observatory.

* * *

Wrenn was there, of course. He was always there; the observatory had been his post for thirty years before it had ever been anything to Coral, and even after his hands grew too unsteady for fine work he had kept his key, kept his chair in the corner of the clock room, kept his habit of winding the master clock's barrel every third day whether it needed it or not. *A clock that's never let to run down never learns to be trusted,* he liked to say, which Coral had long ago decided was either horological wisdom or an argument about people dressed up in gears.

The old man sat in that chair now, whittling a peg for the case of the sidereal dial, and when Coral came through the door he did not look up.

"So," Coral said. "The lists."

"So," Wrenn agreed. "The lists."

"You scratched out names." Coral crossed the room and stood over him, hands working at his sides. "With a pencil. In front of the sentries."

"I used ink for the first two." Wrenn blew a curl of wood from the peg and examined it against the light. "Pencil only came out when my ink froze."

"Wrenn."

"Three names, Coral. Three. The Halloway widow, who is sixty-eight years old and whose requisition notice would have taken her house and turned her into the street before the first frost. The Ferro boy, who is fifteen and has a lame leg and was marked for conscription anyway because some clerk three hundred miles away decided Vellmarsh hadn't met its quota. And—" the old man's voice caught, very slightly, on the third name, and he smoothed it over the way he smoothed a burr off a toothed wheel, "—and a woman whose name I won't say aloud in this room, because this room has ears in it now, you know that as well as I do."

"The room has ears," Coral said, "and you gave them your name anyway."

"They'd have found me eventually." Wrenn set down the peg and the knife, and looked up for the first time. His eyes were pale and clear and terribly calm, and Coral understood, with a lurch, that this calm had been prepared. That the old man had been getting ready for arrest the way he got ready for winter: early, thoroughly, and without complaint. "I'm seventy-one years old, Coral. My name is on every guild register in the county. My face is known to every sergeant between here and the coast. When they started posting lists on walls, I made my arithmetic, and the arithmetic said: sooner or later, one way or another, they come for Tobias Wrenn. The only question left to me was what I'd be doing when they did."

"And what you chose—"

"Was the only respectable thing available." For the first time something like humor moved across the seamed old face. "You've told me often enough, boy. Somebody respectable has to stay behind."

"That was different. That was about the ledger. About someone above suspicion keeping the shop open, keeping eyes on the—"

"Keeping eyes on the ledger," Wrenn finished gently. "Which is gone. Which left this town inside your coat three weeks ago, and which is, if you've done what I taught you to do, halfway to the capital by now, or sitting somewhere safe waiting for the road to open." He said it plainly, without looking toward the corners of the ceiling where ears might live, because he had spent a lifetime saying dangerous things in plain words precisely so that no one would guess they were dangerous. It was one of the thousand lessons Coral had absorbed without knowing it was a lesson. "So the work changed. Staying behind used to mean watching. Now staying behind means standing in front. Every list they post has names on it, and every name is a person, and a person who sees their own name scratched out — even once, even crudely, even hopelessly — knows for one moment that somebody saw them. That they were not erased quietly. That is worth a pencil, Coral. It may even be worth what it costs."

"It's going to cost you everything."

"Yes," said Wrenn, agreeably. "I know."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, because there was nothing else to do and because both of them would rather have died than sit facing each other across a table while the hours drained out of the day. Coral serviced the strike train of the master clock, cleaning and oiling with more care than the mechanism needed, buying minutes with unnecessary precision. Wrenn sat by the window with the sidereal dial's case across his knees, fitting the peg he'd whittled, sanding it, fitting it again.

Twice, Coral almost spoke of it — of the mountain road, of the plan Emrys and he had argued over past midnight, of whether Wrenn could be gotten out ahead of whatever was coming. Twice he opened his mouth. Twice Wrenn, without turning, said, "No," as though he could hear thoughts the way he could hear a mainspring about to snap.

At half past four the bells of the town churches rang the quarter hour, slightly out of true with each other the way they always were, and five minutes after that Coral saw the column come up the hill road.

Four soldiers and a corporal. Unhurried. They had done this enough times now that they no longer rushed; arrest in Vellmarsh had become routine administration, a formality with rifles attached. Coral watched them climb, small in the grey light, and his mouth went dry as unbaked clay.

"Ah," said Wrenn, without rising. "Punctual."

"Wrenn, there's time. The cellar under the Kestrel Mark is still empty, we could have you below ground within the hour, the nun could—"

"Coral." The old man laid a hand on his arm, and the hand was light and dry and steady. "Listen to me now, because I'll only say this once, and then I'm going to wind the clock, and then we will both conduct ourselves with dignity in front of the Emperor's men."

Coral listened.

"When you came to me, you were all sharp edges and stolen glances, a boy who trusted nobody because nobody had ever earned it. I didn't teach you wheels and springs. Any fool can teach wheels and springs. I taught you that silence has uses. The silence that protects a frightened person is a good silence. The silence that protects yourself at another's cost is a bad one. Everything else I ever told you was decoration." His grip tightened fractionally. "Now. You are carrying something that a great many frightened people are counting on, and you are going to carry it through places designed by men whose entire trade is making people afraid. And when your hands shake on that mountain road — and they will shake, boy, they shook on mine at your age and I was only carrying a letter — I want you to remember what I am about to tell you, and I want you to build your courage on it like a foundation stone."

From the yard below came boots on gravel, the jingle of equipment, a fist testing the observatory door.

"A mechanism only fails," Wrenn said, "when someone stops trusting it."

"That's not—" Coral's voice cracked. "That's not even true. Springs fail. Pivots wear. Metal fatigues—"

"Metal fatigues," Wrenn agreed, "and yet clocks run for two hundred years on tired metal, because someone keeps trusting them. Keeps winding them. Keeps believing the escape is worth the tick. A man who trusts his watch walks differently than a man who doesn't. A town that trusts its clock sets its life by it. Trust isn't a feeling, Coral — it's maintenance performed in advance of proof." He stood, slowly, joints cracking, and took up the winding key from its hook beside the master clock's case. "Now hush. They've knocked twice already, and it would be rude to keep the gentlemen waiting."

The knock came again, harder, and the corporal's voice followed it: "Open in the name of the garrison. Tobias Wrenn is to be taken for questioning."

"One moment," Wrenn called back, in the mild, unhurried tone he used with delivery boys. "I'm attending to the clock."

And he wound it.

Coral watched him do it and understood, finally, that he was watching the whole of the man distilled into a single act. Wrenn fitted the key to the going barrel and cranked it with both hands, counting under his breath as he had counted for thirty years, giving the great movement its full measure of tension, not a click short. Then the strike barrel. Then the subsidiary dials, each in turn, each counted full. The guards hammered the door. Wrenn wound. The old brass and steel drank the stored energy of his last strength, and the pendulum swung on, indifferent and eternal, keeping the time that no corporal could alter.

"There," Wrenn said at last, hanging the key back on its hook. He looked around the clock room — at the dials, the cases, the boy he had raised — and Coral saw his eyes shine once, briefly, before the old discipline closed over it. "Full to the last click. She'll run eight days without me. Someone should check her Thursday next."

"I'll check her Thursday next," Coral said. "And every Thursday after."

"I know you will." Wrenn straightened his coat. Took up his hat. At the door he paused and, absurdly, gravely, shook Coral's hand as though they were two colleagues concluding an appointment. His palm was warm. "Mind the escapements. Mind the silences. And Coral — don't you dare blame yourself for any of this. I did the arithmetic. The answer was always me."

Then the door opened, and the soldiers filled it, and Tobias Wrenn walked out between them with his head up and his hat straight, past the corporal's proffered papers, down the gravel path, down the hill road, into the dusk that was gathering early under a low lid of cloud. Coral stood in the doorway until the column vanished. Behind him the master clock ticked on, full-wound, faithful, keeping the seconds exactly as it had been taught.

* * *

Emrys found him there after dark.

Coral never learned how the lieutenant had heard, or how he had arranged to be absent from his own quarters on the night the garrison arrested a civilian; he had long ago stopped asking how Emrys managed the things Emrys managed. He only knew that the door eased shut and the officer's greatcoat smelled of cold rain and pipe smoke, and that Emrys looked at him for a long moment in the light of the single lamp, taking in the untouched tools, the unfinished work, the state of him.

"Coral—"

"Don't." Coral's voice came out flat and strange. "Don't say you're sorry. Don't say it was brave. Don't tell me the colonel's process, I don't want to know the process. He wound the clock." His hands, which had been steady all evening by an act of will that had emptied him, began to shake now that someone had come to witness them. "He wound it full, Emrys. Eight days. He counted every click."

Emrys crossed the room and did the thing that neither of them had ever quite dared in daylight: he put his arms around him. Coral held on to the rough wool of the uniform — the enemy's uniform, the uniform that had taken Wrenn away, the uniform that was spending itself coin by coin to keep Coral alive — and let himself shake, and Emrys bore it without a word, one hand flat and steady between Coral's shoulder blades, the way you'd steady a movement that had been dropped.

After a long time Coral drew back. His eyes were dry. Something behind them had finished hardening.

"He said not to blame myself," Coral said. "So I won't. I'm going to spend it instead. All of it. Every hour he put into me."

"Good," Emrys said quietly. "Because you should hear what I heard tonight, before I came. There's word moving through the garrison channels — I couldn't get its source, but it's being treated as credible, and it's being passed upward, which means Vasse wants it passed." He paused, and Coral watched the officer and the man fight briefly for custody of the next sentence. "There's a courier expected on the mountain road. Someone leaked it. Vasse has doubled the checkpoint and moved up a company that isn't local — men who don't know faces, only orders. They're setting a trap, Coral, and everyone in the network who's heard the rumor is now afraid to move, which means whoever carries the ledger will carry it alone, into that."

The clock ticked into the silence. Full-wound. Faithful.

"Then it has to be me," Coral said. "It was always going to be me. He built me for this — I only understand that now." He turned to the master clock and laid his palm flat against the warm brass of the case, feeling the beat travel up through his arm like a pulse. "Eight days, he gave us. We'll use every one."

Above them, the pendulum swung on through the dark, trusting them to keep faith with it, and outside, far off along the ridge, the mountain road waited under its new lanterns for whatever came next.
