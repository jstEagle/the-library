## 14. Third Checkpoint: The Mountain Road

The mountain road out of Vellmarsh climbed through pine in long switchbacks, and Coral had been told—by the nun, by the printer's boy who carried messages in flour sacks—that the third checkpoint sat at the pass where the road narrowed between two walls of granite. He had prepared for it the way he prepared everything: meticulously, obsessively, with the whole of the previous night spent inside the wine cellar fitting the ledger into its hiding place.

He had built the hiding place himself. That was the trouble, he would think later. He had built it too well.

It was a regulator case, a dummy one, weighted and finished so that it looked like what it pretended to be: an instrument of precision, the kind of thing a clockmaker's apprentice might be trusted to carry over the mountains to a buyer in the capital. The case was real; only the movement inside was false, a shell of brass wheels that turned and ticked convincingly when a handle was cranked. Between the plates of that false movement, sealed in wax paper and bound with watchmaker's tape, lay the ledger. Four hundred names. Every requisition, every conscription, every erasure that Aldous Wrenn had dared to write down in his last years, plus everything Coral had copied since.

Coral had rehearsed the lie for two days. *I am a courier of instruments. I carry a regulator to the house of Monsieur Delacourt, horologist, Rue Saint-Aubin. My papers are in order. My master is dead and I am finishing his contracts.*

He had rehearsed it so thoroughly that when he crested the final bend before the pass and saw the checkpoint, he felt almost calm.

Then he saw the rest of it, and the calm died all at once, like a mainspring snapping in a closed case.

There were too many soldiers. A checkpoint needed four men and a sergeant. This one held twelve, maybe fourteen, and they were not arranged like men checking papers. They were arranged like men conducting a search. There was a table set up on the gravel, and on the table lay things that made Coral's stomach drop through the road beneath him: a pry bar, a mallet, a screwdriver of the wrong size for anything delicate, a hatchet. Beside the table, a soldier was kneeling over a peddler's cart, ripping up floorboards with his hands because no one had given him a tool thin enough to do it properly. Another had a woman's carpetbag open in the dirt and was stabbing a bayonet through folded linens.

And at the edge of it all, hands clasped behind his back, watching the road down from Vellmarsh with the patience of a man who has already caught what he is fishing for, stood a captain Coral had never seen but knew instantly by description: Vasse's man. The one the nun had called *le renard*. The fox who worked the mountain roads.

They know, Coral thought. Not suspected. *Knew.* Word of a courier had leaked—the tavern raid must have shaken more loose than the printer—and Vasse had done what any careful man would do. He had not waited at the garrison gate, where Coral had already passed once and might not pass again. He had put his net around the neck of the bottle, the one narrow road between Vellmarsh and the capital, and told his soldiers to open every case, every seam, every hollow thing that came up the mountain.

Coral slowed the handcart. Its wheel squealed, a small stupid sound, and one of the soldiers turned toward it with interest.

*Turn back*, said a voice in him that was not fear exactly but something older, something that sounded like Tobias Wrenn on his first day at the bench: *A job you cannot finish calmly is a job you have already ruined. Put down the file. Breathe.*

But there was nowhere to turn back *to*. The road behind descended through open ground for a mile before the first cover of trees, and the fox was already looking down that road, and a man on foot pushing a cart could run perhaps as fast as a soldier could raise a whistle to his lips. Behind Coral, ahead of Coral, the trap had the beautiful terrible symmetry of a well-made mechanism. Every part doing its work. No way to stop it from the outside.

So he did what he had been trained to do since he was nine years old, standing on a stool at Tobias Wrenn's bench while the old man disassembled a repeater worth more than the whole shop.

When you cannot defeat the mechanism, he thought, you stop fighting it. You find the flaw it was built around. You go through the flaw.

He stopped the cart twenty yards from the table and simply stood there, letting them come to him. It cost him everything he had. Every nerve in his body screamed to run, or to turn the cart, or to do *something*, and he overruled all of it and made his hands hang loose at his sides and put an expression on his face that he hoped read as exhaustion, grief, boredom—the ordinary weather of a young man alone on a hard road.

The fox detached himself from the treeline and came down the slope unhurried. Up close he was younger than Coral expected, with a neat brown mustache and eyes that moved over Coral the way Coral's own eyes moved over a clock: case first, then dial, then, without hurry, the movement underneath.

"Name," the captain said.

"Coral Ashgrove."

"Trade."

"Apprentice horologist." He let the word sit a moment, then added, as if it were an afterthought that cost him something to say: "Late apprentice. My master is dead. Aldous Wrenn, of Vellmarsh. I'm carrying his effects and his unfinished contracts over to the capital. The family of a client is waiting on a regulator he'd promised them before—" He stopped. Swallowed. It was not entirely acting; saying Wrenn's name aloud still hurt, and the hurt did the work for him. "Before he was taken."

Something flickered in the captain's face at the name Wrenn. Recognition, yes—but more than recognition. Satisfaction, banked and patient, like a stove turned low.

"Wrenn," the captain repeated. "The old man who tampered with the posted lists."

"He wasn't—" Coral began, and caught himself, and let the anger flash and die just as a grieving apprentice's honestly would. "Forgive me, sir. He was seventy years old. He wound clocks. Whatever they say he did, he couldn't have."

"Mm," said the captain. He walked slowly around the cart. His gaze rested on the regulator case, and Coral watched him look at it and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was the moment everything would be decided. The case was exactly the kind of object a search like this existed to open. A fine instrument, heavy, hollow, built to conceal. If the fox had been told *a courier carrying documents*, then here was a courier carrying a document-shaped box, and no captain hoping for promotion would fail to pry it apart on a table in front of witnesses.

"Open it," the captain said.

* * *

Here is what Coral did not do. He did not argue. He did not step protectively between the soldiers and the case, though every muscle in his arms wanted to. He did not explain that the regulator was delicate, that rough handling would ruin it, that it represented three months of Wrenn's final work—because explanations are what guilty men offer, and he had learned that much in two checkpoints already.

What he did was this. He sighed. A long, worn-out sigh, the sigh of a man who has watched soldiers damage his master's property twice already this week and has stopped expecting better.

"Gently," he said. "Please. It's the last work he ever did."

And then he opened it himself.

This was the whole gambit, the one desperate idea he had assembled in the space of ten heartbeats on the squealing road, and it went against every instinct he owned. He lifted the case off the cart, set it on the search table beside the hatchet, and unlatched the front panel with the little key from his watch chain. Then he swung the movement out on its bracket—the whole false movement, ledger sealed inside it, ticking away softly where a soldier cranked the test handle—so that the works stood exposed under the grey mountain light, gears and escapement and pendulum cradle, intricate and gleaming and utterly unremarkable to anyone who did not know what was welded shut inside the plates.

"The movement comes out for transport," Coral said. His voice was steady. His hands were not; they shook faintly as he pointed out components, and he decided in that instant not to hide the shaking. Let them see it. A liar with steady hands looks like a liar. A boy whose hands tremble over his dead master's last clock looks like nothing but what he appears to be. "You can see there's nothing in the case itself. The case is empty but for the bracket. Everything of value is here." He touched the gear train lightly, reverently, the way he actually did touch such things. "If your men need to look, they can look. But the plates are hand-fitted. Prying them will destroy it, and I'll have carried my master's last piece over a mountain to be broken by a bayonet."

The soldiers looked at the movement. Coral looked at the soldiers looking at it, and understood in the same moment both how fragile and how strong his position was. He was telling the truth about everything except the one thing that mattered. The case *was* empty. The movement *was* a movement. Every fact he offered them was verifiable and true, and threaded invisibly through the true facts ran the single false one, thin as a hairspring: *there is nothing inside the movement.*

That was why it could work. That was why, God forgive him, it *had* to work. A hidden thing invites searching. An open thing invites only inspection, and inspection requires knowledge, and knowledge of clockwork is exactly what the army had not sent up the mountain.

The fox stepped closer. He bent over the movement, and Coral's heart beat so hard he was sure the man must hear it, must see the pulse jumping in his throat, must notice how Coral's forefinger stayed pressed against the rear plate of the movement—not steadying it, exactly. Covering the seam. The way a man naturally covers a wound.

"You'll understand," the captain said mildly, "that we're looking for a courier."

"Everyone on this road is, apparently," Coral said. "I'm looking for a client's house. If you find a ledger in a clock, Captain, you may arrest me twice."

A soldier behind the fox snorted. The fox did not.

For a moment that stretched like wire under load, the captain studied the movement. Then he straightened, took a pencil from his tunic, wrote briefly on a slip of paper, and held it out.

"Delacroix's house, Rue Saint-Aubin?" he asked.

"Delacourt," Coral said. "Rue Saint-Aubin."

The fox's eyes came up sharp at the correction—he had written Delacroix from memory, from a list, from some briefing—and for one black instant Coral thought he had won the battle and lost the war in the same breath, corrected a detail no honest courier should have needed to correct.

But the fox only looked at him for a long, cold moment, and something in that look said: *I don't believe you. I simply cannot prove otherwise yet.*

"Sign here," the captain said instead, tapping the slip. "Name, origin, destination. Report to the civil bureau in the capital within three days to confirm your arrival. Anyone who fails to report is presumed to be the courier we're looking for." He smiled without warmth. "You understand. It makes everything simpler for everyone."

Coral signed. His hand shook. He let it shake.

* * *

They let him through. A soldier even helped him lift the regulator case back onto the cart, handling it carefully—more carefully than Coral expected, because the boy was young and someone's mother had taught him that fine things break—and for a quarter mile Coral pushed the handcart up the switchbacks with the ledger riding openly through the checkpoint behind him, past the ripped cartons and the scattered luggage, past the woman reassembling her carpetbag with white lips, and he did not look back once because he had learned that too: a man who does not look back is a man with nothing behind him.

Only when the road dipped over the shoulder of the mountain and the checkpoint dropped out of sight did his legs inform him what they thought of all this. He steered the cart behind a fallen pine and sat down hard on a stone and shook for a full two minutes, breath coming in ragged pulls, hands clamped over his mouth so the sound wouldn't travel. The pines sighed overhead. Somewhere below, a hawk circled the pass where twelve soldiers were still tearing apart honest people's belongings, hunting a courier who had already walked between their fingers wearing the truth like armor.

*Master's effects*, he thought, and laughed—a cracked, awful laugh, quickly smothered. They had searched him and found nothing, because there was nothing to find. Only a grieving apprentice and a clock. The ledger had crossed the most dangerous ground between Vellmarsh and the capital not hidden at all but carried in plain sight, and the thought should have felt like triumph and instead felt like the loneliest thing he had ever done, because the man who had taught him that mechanisms only fail when someone stops trusting them was in a cell in Vellmarsh and would never walk this road.

He got up. He checked the movement—reflex, ritual, prayer—confirming the seal, the waxed paper, the binding tape. Unbroken. Four hundred names, still ticking inside a clock.

Three days, the fox had said. Report to the civil bureau within three days, or be presumed guilty.

Which meant, Coral understood, sinking cold and clear-eyed as the mountain shadow swallowed the road, that somewhere behind him a message was already moving faster than any handcart could: *apprentice, Ashgrove, regulator case, capital-bound*. And somewhere ahead, in the ministries and bureaus of the capital, that message would arrive before he did.

Unless he moved faster than the mail.

He tightened his grip on the cart handles, set his shoulders toward the pass, and began, for the second time that day, to climb.
