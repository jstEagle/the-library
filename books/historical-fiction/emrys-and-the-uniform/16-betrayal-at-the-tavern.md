## 16. Betrayal at the Tavern

The dumbwaiter had been the first thing Coral noticed about the cellar, three nights ago, because a clockmaker cannot help but notice any mechanism in a room. It was a simple thing — a rope wound round a drum, a counterweight of scrap iron, a box the size of a bread oven rising through the wall to the kitchen above — and Sister Anselm had laughed when she caught him studying it.

"You'll be rebuilding that before you leave," she'd said. "Everything you look at, you end up taking apart."

"I only take apart what's broken," Coral had answered.

"It was broken," she said. "Now it isn't."

He thought of that exchange now, crouched in the dark at the bottom of the shaft with the rope burning through his palms, and understood that he had been wrong to find comfort in it. Nothing down here was unbroken. He had simply not yet found where the crack ran.

The evening had begun the way the best evenings in the cellar began: with the printer, Josse Ferro, arguing politics over a jug of thin red wine while two children — Pell, who was nine and missing two front teeth, and his sister Wren, who was six and missed nothing — sat cross-legged on flour sacks trying to teach Coral a rhyming game from their village. The cellar beneath the Salt Cellar tavern was warm from the kitchens above, low-ceilinged, lit by a single lamp turned down to a thumb's flame. Crates of honest wine stood along one wall; along the other, hidden behind a false bulkhead Ferro had built himself, were crates of another kind entirely — forged passes, altered registers, printed handbills denouncing the requisition lists, and four people whose names had been struck from Vellmarsh's books so thoroughly that the army's own clerks could no longer have said what had become of them.

Coral had been among them five days. Five days of sleeping on a pallet between the children's, of Ferro teaching him the weight of ink and Sister Anselm teaching him the geography of the tunnels under the town, of Emrys arriving after curfew in plain clothes with news from the garrison and staying long past the hour he could justify. Five days of something he had never once permitted himself in ten years of apprenticeship and silence: belonging.

"We had a printer like you in Corlow," Ferro was saying, refilling Coral's cup though Coral hadn't asked. "Set type by candlelight for twenty years. When they came for his press he stood in front of it like it was his mother." Ferro shook his head. "Sentimental man. A press is just a machine. You can build another machine."

"Can you?" Coral asked. "Where?"

Ferro smiled, and there was wine and defiance in equal measure in it. "That," he said, "is what makes it worth doing."

Above them, faintly, the tavern noise changed.

It was nothing most people would have caught — a lull where there should have been laughter, a chair scraping, the particular quality of a door opening that is opened by someone who does not mean to pay for what he takes. But Coral had spent ten years learning to hear what mechanisms said when they were about to fail, and a tavern full of smugglers was a mechanism like any other. He raised a hand and the room went quiet, even the children, even mid-game.

Boots. Not the shuffling of drinkers. Boots in step, plural, descending the stairs to the tavern's ground floor, and then — worse — the sound of the kitchen door above being put to use, hinges complaining under a hand that didn't care about noise.

"Raid," Coral said. "Upstairs and the stairs both. How many ways out?"

Sister Anselm was already moving, snuffing the lamp without a flicker of wasted motion. "Two. The tunnel behind the false bulkhead runs to the tannery. And the dumbwaiter goes up to the kitchen yard — but the kitchen's compromised if they've come through it."

"They came through it," Coral said. He could hear them now, directly overhead, boots on kitchen flags. "How many can the dumbwaiter carry?"

"One at a time. Two if they're small." His mind was already running the mechanism the way he ran any mechanism: load, counterweight, friction points, failure modes. The drum took the rope at three wraps; more than three and it slipped. The counterweight would lift a grown man if the guides were true. The guides were true; he'd oiled them himself two nights ago out of pure professional compulsion while Sister Anselm held the candle. "Anselm — the tannery tunnel. Does it surface inside the walls or outside?"

"Outside. Past the east ditch."

"Then children first up the shaft, everyone else down the tunnel. If they've blocked one way, they haven't blocked both. Not yet."

Pell had gone very still on his flour sack. Wren had taken hold of her brother's sleeve. In the darkness Coral knelt and put his hands on their shoulders, and heard his own voice come out steady, which surprised him, because nothing inside him was steady at all.

"You're going to ride a little box up through the ceiling. I'll send you up safe and Anselm will pull you out at the top. You remember the reed bed we told you about? Where you'd go if there was ever trouble?"

"Yes," Pell said, in a small hard voice far too old for him.

"Go there. Don't wait for us. Count to a hundred before you move, then don't stop moving until you see the willows. Can you count to a hundred?"

"A hundred and forty," Pell said.

"Then show off later. Go."

The rope was cold and rough. Coral got both children into the box — Wren standing on Pell's feet so they'd fit, her arms around her brother's neck, her face pressed into his collar — and paid out the counterweight with a gentleness he did not feel. The drum turned. The shaft swallowed them with a soft scrape of wood on wood. Above, someone in the kitchen shouted, and then the shout became confusion as an empty bread-oven-sized door opened and produced two children instead of contraband.

"Ferro," Coral breathed. "Tunnel. Now."

But Ferro wasn't moving toward the tunnel. He had gone to the false bulkhead — not to open it, Coral realized with a horror that arrived whole and fully formed, but to stand in front of it, the way the printer in Corlow had stood in front of his press.

"The passes," Ferro said quietly. "Four hundred blank passes with the garrison seal. If they find those, they don't just break this cell. They trace every name we've moved. Every family. They'll pull the thread all the way back through Vellmarsh." He picked up the poker from the little stove they used for warming ink. "Someone has to buy time for the fire to do its work."

"There's no time to burn four hundred—"

"Then I'll spend what time there is." Ferro's smile in the dark was almost gentle. "A machine is just a machine, boy. You can build another. That's what makes it worth doing. Go."

The kitchen door burst inward at the same moment the cellar door gave, and lamplight poured down the stair in blades, and Coral learned in a single white instant exactly how a good man becomes a diversion: by deciding it before anyone can argue.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, and for years afterward Coral would remember it in pieces, the way a shattered movement keeps its wheels turning independently until someone stops them.

He went down the tunnel because going back was death and uselessness together, and Ferro's poker rang against a soldier's helmet somewhere behind him. Sister Anselm was ahead of him, skirts hauled up, moving through the black with the certainty of a woman who had walked this passage since before the army knew Vellmarsh's name. Behind them, muffled by earth and stone, the cellar filled with shouts and the crash of the bulkhead coming down — Ferro fighting, briefly, terribly, and then not fighting anymore.

And the dumbwaiter. The dumbwaiter was still cycling above them, counterweight and drum working through the last of the momentum Coral had given it, and in his mind's eye he saw it plainly: the box descending empty through the kitchen wall, the rope slackening, the whole elegant little machine delivering itself into the hands of the garrison with its mechanism intact and legible. A clockmaker leaves fingerprints in everything he touches. Oiled guides. Balanced load. A rerouted escape that worked perfectly the first time it was tried.

They would know, within the hour, that someone in that cellar had been a craftsman.

They would know, when they questioned the tavern keeper about who had come and gone these five days, that a young man with an apprentice's hands had been asking after the state of the ropes.

And they would know — this was the piece that stopped Coral's breath in the middle of a running stride, so that Anselm hauled him forward by the wrist through the dark — they would know it because of the ledger. Because a courier who moved through checkpoints with clock parts was exactly the kind of courier whose network a man like Colonel Vasse would now tear the province apart to map. The raid wasn't only a raid. It was a confession extracted from the cellar itself, read aloud to Vasse in evidence: there is a ledger, there is a route, there is a man who carries it.

"He'll confirm it exists," Coral gasped as they came up out of the tunnel mouth into the cold stink of the tannery yards. "Vasse. Ferro won't tell him anything, but he doesn't have to. Blank passes with the seal, a tunnel, a dumbwaiter — Vasse can prove the network moves documents. He doesn't need Ferro to say the word *ledger*. He only needs to believe it, and now he has every reason to."

Anselm pulled him behind the lime pits as lanterns bloomed along the east ditch. Her habit was torn at the hem and there was ash or soot across her cheek, and her eyes in the lantern light were terrible and calm.

"Then the ledger must reach Merrin before Vasse stops believing and starts knowing," she said. "How many days?"

"Two. Three if the mountain road holds."

"The mountain road doesn't hold," she said. "It never has. Every trap ever set in this province was set on a mountain road, Coral. Remember that."

* * *

Emrys found him an hour before dawn, in the reed beds where Pell and Wren had counted their hundred and been counted out the other side by a nun's cousin with a cart of turnips and a face like a closed door. The children were gone east by then, folded into some other link of the chain Anselm refused to name, and Coral sat in the frozen reeds with his hands around his knees and watched the sky over Vellmarsh go the color of old pewter.

Emrys came out of the dark the way he always did, quiet, coatless, having left the uniform wherever he left it these nights — and stopped dead when he saw Coral's face.

"Coral. What happened. Who—"

"Ferro's taken." The words came out flat, each one placed like a component on a bench. "The cellar's gone. Anselm's scattered. The children got out through the shaft — I sent them up the shaft, I'd oiled the guides, it worked beautifully, it worked like a *clock* — and Ferro stayed behind to burn the passes and they took him, and I ran, Emrys, I ran down a hole while he—" His voice cracked and he hated it and kept talking anyway, because stopping was unthinkable. "I brought this on them. Every one of them. I came into that cellar carrying the most dangerous paper in the province and I let myself feel at home there. Warm. Fed. Like family. And family is exactly what it cost them."

Emrys knelt in the reeds in front of him. This close, Coral could see the sleeplessness on him, the bruised skin under his eyes, the way his hands — officer's hands, steady hands — hovered and then committed, settling on Coral's shoulders.

"Listen to me. Vasse didn't find your ledger tonight."

"He doesn't need to find it. He knows it *moves*. That's enough to close every road—"

"Yes." Emrys didn't soften it, and Coral loved him for that even as it cut. "Yes, it is. By tomorrow there will be triple checks at the bridge, a full muster roll at the garrison gate, and Vasse will have half a company strung out along the mountain road on the strength of a guess and a burned storeroom. Which means," and here his grip tightened, "that whatever we were going to do in three days, we do now. Tonight-tomorrow. Before the new orders are written, while the gate sergeants are still working off yesterday's instructions and half of them slept through the alarm."

"In three days I might have been ready."

"You'll never be ready. Nobody's ready. Wrenn wasn't ready when he taught you which silences protect people; he just chose." Emrys's voice dropped, and the last of the officer's cadence went out of it, leaving only the man. "Coral. Look at me. Ferro made his choice standing in front of that bulkhead, and it wasn't your fault, and it wasn't yours to prevent, and if you make it yours, Vasse wins twice — once for the cellar and once for you. The ledger isn't a debt you owe those people. It's what they spent themselves to protect. Honor it. Don't bury yourself under it."

Coral wanted to believe the arithmetic of it. He tried it against the image that would not leave him — Ferro's smile over the wine jug, *a machine is just a machine* — and found it did not balance. Some entries, once made, could not be reconciled; a clockmaker knew that better than anyone. But he nodded, because dawn was coming, and dawn meant roads closing, and grief was a luxury that had to wait its turn behind survival.

"There's something else," Emrys said, and now he did hesitate, which frightened Coral more than anything the night had yet done. "When they searched the cellar, they catalogued everything. I saw the inventory sheet an hour ago. There's an item logged — recovered documents, partially burned, bearing references to observatory records." He met Coral's eyes steadily. "References to you, Coral. Your name is on a garrison document tonight. By morning it will be on Vasse's desk."

The pewter sky lightened another shade. Somewhere across the ditches, Vellmarsh's church bells began the hour, and Coral counted them out of pure reflex — six strokes, unhurried, true, the work of some unknown keeper who had wound them in the dark without knowing what the day would ask of anyone listening.

"Then there's nothing left to be but bold," Coral said. He stood. His legs held. "Wrenn always said the best lie is the truth worn like armor. So I'll wear it. Apprentice Ashgrove, transporting his late master's effects, grieving, harmless, *documented* — and if Vasse wants to arrest a clockmaker for carrying clocks, let him explain to the whole province why a clock is worth a firing squad."

Emrys rose beside him. For one moment, in the grey before sunrise, with the reeds silvering and the town waking unaware below them, he took Coral's hand — openly, deliberately, as if daring the light itself to witness it.

"Third checkpoint," he said. "Mountain road."

"Mountain road," Coral agreed, and did not say what Sister Anselm had already told him: that every trap ever set in this province was set there.
