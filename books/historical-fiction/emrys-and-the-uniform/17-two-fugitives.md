## 17. Two Fugitives

*Emrys*

The uniform came off in a pig trough.

That was how Emrys would remember it afterward — not the court-martial he'd escaped, not the bullet still singing in Coral's shoulder, but the absurd domesticity of kneeling in straw behind a farmer's barn at dusk, unbuttoning his own coat while Coral stood watch with a pistol he didn't know how to hold. He'd folded the jacket neatly, out of ten years of habit, and sunk it under the grey water with two stones from the fence line. The brass buttons caught the last light once, twice, and then it was gone.

"There," he said, and his voice did something strange on the word, as if a gear had slipped. "No more Lieutenant Hale."

Coral looked at him — this man in a borrowed labourer's shirt, sleeves wrong at the wrist, hair plastered down with ditch water — and Emrys braced for the thing he'd seen in other men's faces when the army let go of them: relief, or pity, or the quick arithmetic of *what is he worth now*.

What he got instead was Coral holding out his hand.

"Then help me over this wall," Coral said. "I can't feel my left side and you're apparently unemployed."

Emrys laughed — actually laughed, a raw sound that frightened crows out of the hedgerow — and took the hand, and hauled him over, and understood that he had just been answered before he'd managed to ask anything.

They walked after dark. The capital was four days away by road, less if they cut across the Harrow flats, but every road had ears now. Vasse's warrants would describe them by morning: an officer absent without leave, a clockmaker's apprentice with a wounded arm and no explanation for either. Emrys knew exactly what the reports would say, because for six years writing such reports had been his work. It was strange, knowing himself from the outside like that. *Hale, E., Lt. Morally unreliable. Associates with persons of interest.* His father's file had said nearly the same thing, thirty years earlier, in another colonel's hand.

They slept the first night in a hay barn whose owner they never saw, curled a careful foot apart in the dark, both awake, neither pretending otherwise.

"Coral," he said, somewhere past midnight.

"You should sleep."

"I killed a man at Kestrel Ford." He hadn't meant to say it. The words came out the way blood leaves a wound you've been pressing your palm against. "Before all this. Before Vasse, before any of it. A deserter. My own side. They made us draw lots so we'd all be guilty of it, and I drew short."

In the darkness Coral moved — not away. Closer. His good hand found Emrys's sleeve and stayed there.

"He was nineteen," Emrys said. "He cried for his mother, and I stood there because standing there was duty, and duty is the only thing I have ever been any good at, and now I've burned it, and I find I don't regret a single match." His voice failed on the last of it. "What does that make me?"

"A man," Coral said simply. "It makes you a man who stopped trusting a broken mechanism. Wrenn would tell you—" He stopped. Emrys heard him take the grief of it and set it aside, deliberately, the way he'd set down a tool. "Wrenn would tell you that's not betrayal. That's maintenance."

Emrys lay in the hay and felt something in his chest come unlatched, a door he'd been leaning against since he was twelve years old and learned to read his father's name only in the absence of it. He did not sleep. But for the first time in his life, lying awake felt like rest.

*Coral*

By the second day the shoulder had gone from white pain to a deep red throb, and Coral had privately catalogued everything wrong with it — the pull of torn muscle when he raised his arm, the heat under the bandage that meant infection was considering an offer — and had decided, with the same cold bookkeeping his mother had used through three bad winters, that none of it could be afforded yet.

What he could not put on a ledger was Emrys.

He watched him across the morning: Emrys buying bread from a farm cart with coins counted out in a labourer's accent he'd invented somewhere between the barn and the lane, Emrys carrying Coral's pack without being asked, Emrys scanning every horizon with that flat, total attention that was the last habit of the uniform left on his body. He had done this for Coral. Not for the ledger — the ledger could have gone with any courier, and Vasse's men would never have chased a lieutenant this hard over paper. He had burned his rank, his pension, his legal existence, for one specific stubborn clockmaker who had gotten himself shot.

"You're staring," Emrys said, not looking at him.

"I'm assessing," Coral said. "You've got a limp developing on the left. You've been favoring it since the fence."

"I've been favoring it since Kestrel Ford, it's an old friend. You're changing the subject."

"Yes," Coral admitted. "I am."

They argued that afternoon, properly, for the first time since the observatory — about the route, about whether to risk the coaching inn at Marlow Cross, about whether Coral's shoulder warranted a doctor or merely silence. It had the rhythm of an old marriage in it somewhere, and Coral heard the rhythm and went quiet in the middle of insisting he did not need a doctor, because the quiet was worse than any argument. Because underneath the argument was the thing they kept stepping around like a hole in the floor.

Emrys stopped walking. They stood in a beech wood with evening coming down through it, gold and slow, and Emrys said, "Say it. Whatever it is you keep not saying. I've been shot at twice this week; I'd rather face it."

"You'll ruin everything," Coral said. "That's what I keep not saying. There's nothing left of me that isn't this mission and this shoulder, and if I say the other thing out loud and it goes wrong, there won't even be wreckage. There'll just be nothing."

"The other thing," Emrys repeated. He came closer. In the dimming wood his eyes were very steady. "You mean that I have been in love with you since the night I pocketed your ledger and walked out of the observatory with my heart going like a runaway escapement. Is that the thing?"

The words landed in Coral's chest like a weight finding its pallet — that small mechanical click of a part seating exactly where it was always meant to go.

"That's the thing," he said. His voice was not entirely level. "Though I'd worked mine out earlier. The night you taught me the pass procedures. You held my hands to show me how a forgery feels different from an honest signature, and I thought, *oh no*, out loud, in my own head, *oh no, not him, anyone but him.*"

"Why anyone but me?"

"Because you were the uniform." Coral laughed, unsteady. "Because everything I was running from wore that coat, and there you were inside it, being— being *this*. Being the man who gave his father's ghost a reason. I didn't know what to do with that. I've known how to fix broken things since I was seven years old and you were the first broken thing that terrified me."

Emrys reached out and took his injured arm with a gentleness that had no business living in hands trained on rifles, and Coral let him, and the wood went on turning gold around them, indifferent and beautiful.

"Everything else is gone," Emrys said quietly. "Rank, name, future, home. Vasse will hang one of us if he catches us, possibly both, possibly in alphabetical order. So I'm going to say this plainly while saying things plainly costs nothing extra." He drew a breath like a man stepping off a ledge. "I love you, Coral Ashgrove. Not despite the mission. Not despite anything. Plainly."

"Plainly," Coral echoed, and kissed him.

It was not gentle, exactly — there was too much fear and relief in it for gentle — but it was true, and true was the only currency either of them had trusted in weeks. Coral's good hand fisted in Emrys's borrowed shirt. Somewhere above them a wood pigeon called, ordinary as bread, and the world declined to end.

When they broke apart, Coral rested his forehead against Emrys's collarbone and said, muffled, "We still have two days of road and a journalist to find and an entire army between here and the truth."

"Yes."

"And you're limping."

"*Moderately,*" said Emrys, and Coral felt him smiling against his hair.

*Emrys*

They crossed the Harrow flats by night and hid by day, and the hiding places got stranger as the country emptied toward the capital's outskirts: a chapel vestry where Coral repaired the church warden's stuck clock in payment for a night's shelter, working one-armed and swearing softly at the escapement while Emrys kept watch and fell a little further in love with each muttered curse; a culvert; a hayrick; the empty goods wagon of a freight train that carried them shrieking and swaying through a moonless stretch of country while Coral slept against his uninjured shoulder and Emrys stayed awake memorizing the weight of him.

He had expected — he could admit it now — that loving Coral would feel like desertion. Like one more post abandoned. Instead it felt like the opposite: like the first duty he had ever taken on freely, with his eyes open, understanding what it cost and paying gladly. His father had been erased for loving wrongly, or living wrongly, or simply for being in the ledger's way; Emrys had enlisted to learn which, and somewhere between Vellmarsh and the Harrow flats he had learned something better. That the ledger was right about everyone it named. That every name in it had been someone who loved something. That was the whole crime. That had always been the whole crime.

On the fourth evening the capital rose out of the plain ahead — a smoke-bruised sprawl under a sky going violet, domes and chimneys and the ministry district's cold stone glittering westward like a jaw. They stood on a low ridge above the turnpike, two men in stolen shirts with one pistol, one ledger sewn into a clock case, and one piece of paper with a journalist's name on it that Merrin's own network had pressed into Coral's hand back in Vellmarsh.

"Aldous Merrin," Coral read. "Off Chandler's Row. Behind a print shop with a green door."

"Ministries first," Emrys said. "If we go to Merrin straightaway and he's watched, it's over. We go in through the bureaucracy. Make them deny us to our faces. Paper trails are the one weapon Vasse can't confiscate."

Coral looked at him sideways. "You do realize you've just proposed that two fugitives file a complaint."

"I'm aware of how it sounds."

"It sounds like something Wrenn would have loved." Coral folded the slip of paper away, and his mouth curved — that dry, crooked curve Emrys had begun to live by. "Come on, then, Lieutenant."

"Not lieutenant. Not anymore."

"No," Coral agreed, and took his hand, right there on the open ridge with the whole capital laid out below them and the whole law of the country presumably arrayed against them, and did not let go. "Come on, then, Emrys."

Below them, in the gathering dark, the print shops of Chandler's Row were lighting their lamps one by one — and among them, though neither man could know it yet, a green door was already being watched.
