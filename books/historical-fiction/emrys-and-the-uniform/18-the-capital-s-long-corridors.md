## 18. The Capital's Long Corridors

The capital announced itself before we saw it: a brown haze of coal smoke hanging over the plain like a lid on a pot, and beneath it the sound — not the clatter of Vellmarsh's market square but a deep, continuous roar, the noise of two hundred thousand lives grinding against one another. Emrys stood at the rail of the third-class carriage with his coat collar turned up and his lieutenancy stripped from him in all but the cloth, watching the city grow out of the murk.

"You'll want to keep that hand out of sight," he said, nodding at my bandage. "Cities are kind to strangers until they notice them."

"It's my shoulder they'd notice."

"Then let them notice your shoulder and nothing else."

We had come eleven days across country since the print shop in Harrowgate — or whatever that town had been; I had stopped keeping track somewhere around the third river crossing. My shoulder had been cleaned and bound by a midwife who asked no questions and accepted no payment except a mended clock. Emrys had taught me to walk like a man with somewhere dull to be: shoulders loose, eyes level, never hurrying, never dawdling. *Be boring*, he said. *Boring men pass every checkpoint in the empire.*

The ledger rode against my chest in a canvas satchel I had taken to wearing under my shirt, where it lay against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Six hundred and forty-one names. Wrenn's careful hand. Three hundred pages of requisitions and conscriptions and erasures — men struck from registers so their farms could be folded into army contracts, widows deleted so their pensions could lapse, whole families unwritten because someone in a garrison office had wanted their house.

I had carried it over a bridge, through a garrison gate, down a mountain road, across four counties. The last hundred yards would be through the corridors of ministries, which I was beginning to understand were worse than any checkpoint yet devised.

* * *

The Ministry of Information occupied a building that seemed designed to make visitors feel they had died and gone somewhere tedious. Its corridors ran straight for distances no honest building required, lined with doors bearing brass plates whose lettering had been polished by generations of clerks. The air smelled of ink, floor wax, and despair.

"Petitions are received Thursdays," said the first clerk, without looking up.

"It isn't a petition," Emrys said. "It's evidence of crimes committed under military authority. I'm asking to see the Minister's office directly."

That got us looked at. The clerk raised his eyes with the slow reluctance of a man lifting a stone to see what lived beneath it, took in Emrys's height, his cropped hair, the soldier's way he stood even out of uniform, and grew very still.

"Military matters," the clerk said, "are handled by the Military Liaison Office. Room 214."

"We've come *from* the military. That's rather the point."

"Room 214," the clerk repeated, and rang a small bell on his desk as though concluding an argument.

Room 214 contained a captain named Pell who offered us tea, chairs, and sympathy in carefully equal measures. He listened to Emrys's account — we had agreed beforehand how much truth to tell, which was almost none — with an expression of profound professional concern that never once touched his eyes.

"Garrison irregularities," he said when Emrys finished. "Vellmarsh, you said. Northern command. Yes." He made a note. "These matters are investigated internally, of course."

"They haven't been investigated. They've been *committed*. By the internal channels you're describing."

Captain Pell smiled the smile of a man who has heard many accusations and buried most of them. "Mr. Hale, isn't it? Formerly Lieutenant." His pen moved. "I see your service record here. Discharged under circumstances the record describes as—" he consulted something below the desk, though I suspected he already knew it by heart— "*administrative*. You'll forgive me, but a dismissed officer arriving with allegations against his former garrison, accompanied by a civilian with no credentials whatsoever..." He spread his hands. "You must see how this looks."

"How does it look?"

"Like a grievance." Pell folded his hands. "Every month we receive a dozen men convinced the army wronged them. We have procedures. Forms. In eighteen months to two years, if your evidence is genuine—" and here he glanced at my satchel with the briefest flicker of interest, which frightened me more than any suspicion could have— "you may expect a finding. Meanwhile, I'd advise you not to circulate these allegations privately. False testimony about military operations carries penalties."

"The testimony is true."

"Then you have nothing to fear from waiting." He stood, and shook our hands, and walked us to the door with the courtesy of a man closing a lid.

In the corridor outside, Emrys walked thirty paces without speaking. Then he stopped beside a window overlooking a courtyard full of filing carts and said, quietly enough that only I could hear:

"He knew my name before I gave it."

"I noticed."

"That means someone forwarded word from Vellmarsh. Vasse's reach extends further than we thought." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Or it means nothing, and every dismissed lieutenant looks alike to them. Either way, we've shown our faces to the machinery. We won't get a second visit."

"Then we find Merrin."

Emrys looked at me, and there was something in his face I had learned to read over those weeks of flight — the look he wore when he was about to say a thing he'd been carrying for days. "Coral. Aldous Merrin isn't a ministry official. He's a newspaperman the ministries have spent ten years trying to ruin. He publishes from a shop off Cordwainer Street with two assistants and a press older than either of us. If Pell's people wanted to bury this ledger, do you know what they'd do? They wouldn't stop us reaching Merrin. They'd let us reach him, and then they'd buy the paper that prints him, and that would be the end of it." He exhaled. "Truth is cheap, Coral. What's expensive is truth with teeth. We need to give him something that makes silence more dangerous to them than printing."

"Then let's go make him afraid," I said. "Properly afraid. On our side."

* * *

We found Cordwainer Street on the third day, after two nights in a doss-house where Emrys slept sitting upright against the wall with his bad shoulder to the room and I lay awake listening to him breathe and cataloguing, as I did now each night, everything I would lose if we failed. It was a short list, because we had so little left, and somehow that made each item heavier.

Merrin's establishment was easy to find and hard to enter. The ground floor was a stationer's, legitimately, selling ink and foolscap to schoolboys, and the proprietor — a wide woman with forearms like a blacksmith — told us Mr. Merrin saw no one without an appointment, and made appointments for no one.

"We've come from Vellmarsh," I said.

Something moved behind her eyes. "Never heard of it."

"There's a garrison there seizing town records. There's a list of six hundred names of people the army has erased. And there's a man called Colonel Vasse who will hang everyone who helped carry that list out of his reach, beginning with the woman who runs a stationer's shop on Cordwainer Street, if he learns she passed messages for a journalist."

She regarded me for a long moment. Behind her, the shop's stock of paper sat in bales taller than I was — enough paper, I thought, to print a great many things.

"You're the clock boy," she said at last. "He wrote to us. Weeks ago. Said if a young man ever came with ink on his fingers and a northern accent and talked about ledgers, we should believe him." She stepped back from the counter and unlocked a door in the paneling behind her. "Mind the stairs. They creak, and he likes to know who's coming."

* * *

Aldous Merrin was fifty-odd, gray, and built like a question mark from decades bent over copy. His office above the stationer's held a desk drowning in paper, a wall of pinned clippings connected by red string in patterns I couldn't follow, and a smell of pipe smoke so thick it had opinions. He didn't rise when we came in. He looked at Emrys first, then at me, then at the satchel, and said:

"Well. Either you're exactly who Wrenn's letter promised, or you're the cleverest trap anyone has ever sent me. Sit down anyway. Traps sometimes say interesting things."

"You knew Tobias Wrenn?" I asked.

"Twenty years ago he repaired my mother's carriage clock and refused payment because she'd once sheltered a printer during the dock strikes. He wrote to me in March — before your troubles started, mind — saying that if ever a package left Vellmarsh heading south, it would be worth more than its weight in gold, and that I should trust the hands carrying it and no one else's." He drew on his pipe. "Then the letter-burners at the garrison post office apparently got efficient, because nothing else arrived. So. Show me."

I unwrapped the satchel on his desk.

I have seen men handle fine mechanisms and I have seen men handle dangerous ones, and Merrin did something I hadn't seen before: he handled the ledger like both at once. He turned the pages slowly, reading not just the entries but the hand that had written them, and I watched his face change as understanding settled into it — the particular horror of a man who realizes a thing is real that he has spent years suspecting and being called mad for suspecting.

"Six hundred and forty-one," I said. "Cross-referenced against parish registers, tithe rolls, and garrison requisition orders. Wrenn kept duplicates of everything the army destroyed. Signatures. Dates. Some entries in Vasse's own hand."

"I know this hand." Merrin touched one page with a fingertip, not quite touching the ink. "A land transfer in Marrowfield, 1909. A family called Ostrey. Father, mother, three children. Gone from the register; farm reassigned within a fortnight. I ran a piece on it. Twelve hundred words on page nine, buried between a cattle disease report and a shipping notice. They let me print it because it was nothing — one family, one farm, one sad story, easily explained away." He closed the ledger gently, as though it might wake. "This is six hundred of those stories in one binding. This isn't a scandal. This is a verdict."

"Then publish it," Emrys said.

Merrin looked up at him with something close to pity. "Lieutenant—"

"Lieutenant no longer."

"No longer. Which is precisely my problem." He rose, joints cracking, and went to the wall of string. "Do you know why I am still alive, Mr. Hale, after twenty years of making powerful men uncomfortable? Because everything I print is *verifiable*. I check, and re-check, and I can show my working, and that makes me expensive to destroy. Destroy me and you must destroy the verification, which means destroying witnesses, records, courts. It's a costly business killing a careful man." He turned back. "A ledger produced by a fugitive apprentice and a cashiered officer, alleging crimes by a serving colonel? I print that tomorrow and by Friday I'm charged with forgery and sedition, my press is seized under the same emergency statutes your Colonel Vasse has been hiding behind, and the ledger becomes *evidence against us* — a fabricated document, provenance unknown, carried by known associates of a saboteur." He tapped the cover. "They wouldn't even need to lie. You two *are* fugitives. You've burned half of northern command's paperwork getting here, God love you. From where I sit, in law, in print, in the eyes of any magistrate they drag before a compliant bench — you are not witnesses. You are the crime."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the stationer served a customer; the till rang like a small distant bell.

"So we're worthless," I said.

"You're not worthless. You're unverified." Merrin came around the desk. "There's a difference, and it's the difference that keeps men like me breathing. Give me corroboration I can stand on — a second source independent of you both. Parish records I can inspect myself. A requisition order with Vasse's signature that exists in some file your colonel doesn't control. An erased man, living, who can stand in a witness box and say *I am Ostrey, I exist.* Give me one thread like that and I can pull until the whole cloth comes apart, because I promise you, gentlemen, once this is printed properly, there are men in the Cabinet who will use it to gut the entire requisition system, and they will be *glad* of the leverage. Truth is worthless without someone powerful enough to be afraid of it. My job is to make them afraid. But I cannot build fear on sand."

Emrys was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, in the flat voice he used for the worst truths: "The Ostreys. You said the family was erased in Marrowfield. Are any of them alive?"

Merrin's eyebrows rose. "The daughter, perhaps. There was a report — unconfirmed, seven years old — of a girl matching her description placed in service in the capital under another name. Why?"

"Because Coral mended clocks in Marrowfield the summer he was sixteen, and the Ostrey farmstead was on his rounds." Emrys looked at me. "You knew them."

I did remember. A farmhouse with a crooked weathervane. A girl my own age who wound the kitchen clock too tight, always, because her mother had liked it loud. "Elsbeth," I said slowly. "Elsbeth Ostrey. She'd be — twenty-four now. Twenty-five."

"Find her," Merrin said, "and bring her to me, and I will put her name and Wrenn's ledger in the same edition, and I will spend every favor I have left making sure it lands where it cannot be unpicked." He began pulling clippings from the wall, hunting through them with sudden ferocity. "There was a placement agency — Harrow & Vane, near the river — that handled servants for families wanting discreet staff. Discreet meaning undocumented. If the girl went through them—"

The door on the stairs opened without a knock.

The blacksmith woman stood in it, and I knew before she spoke, from the set of her shoulders, from the fact that she had left her counter, that the day had turned.

"Two men downstairs," she said. "Plain coats, army boots. Asking after a northerner with a bandaged arm." Her eyes went to Merrin. "And a third one staying in the street. He's not even trying to look like anything."

Merrin moved fast for a question-marked man. The ledger vanished into a hollow beneath his desk drawer with the ease of twenty years' practice. "Back stairs," he said. "Through the paper store. Aldwych tunnel, then scatter." He thrust a card into my hand — an address, written in pencil, in the same hand that had answered Wrenn's letters. "Harbourmaster's arches, Bermondsey. Ask for Verity. Go now, and Mr. Hale—" he caught Emrys's sleeve— "the Ostrey girl. Find her. She's the thread."

Boots on the stairs now, unhurried, in the rhythm of men who owned the building's future and knew it.

We went down through bales of paper in the dark, Emrys ahead of me, his hand finding mine in the blackness without either of us deciding it should, and behind us, faint through the floorboards, I heard a door open above and Merrin's voice, dry and unhurried, saying:

"Gentlemen. You've saved me a walk to the ministry. Do sit down. Let's discuss how afraid you ought to be."
