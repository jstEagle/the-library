## 19. Vasse Closes In

The print shop smelled of ink and hot metal, and Emrys had learned in the army that the places which smelled of work were the places that kept working while the world ended outside. Aldous Merrin's shop was a long narrow room behind a false-front stationer's on Cordwainer Lane, two floors of iron presses and paper stacked like sandbags, lit by a single hissing gas lamp that Merrin refused to turn higher because, he said, light through a window at three in the morning made men curious.

Emrys stood at the foot of the back stairs with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, helping Coral sort the last of the type when he heard the boots.

Not one pair. Not the shuffling patrol-step of bored sentries. Boots in rhythm — six men at least, moving in step, moving with purpose — and beneath them the creak of a cart stopping in the lane.

Coral heard it too. His hands went still over the tray of lead letters, and for a moment the whole room seemed to hold its breath with him: Merrin at the press, the apprentice boy by the drying racks, the gas lamp's flame leaning as a draft came under the door.

"Front or back?" Coral asked quietly.

Emrys closed his eyes and listened the way Wrenn had taught him to listen to a clock — not to the sound itself but to what the sound implied about everything hidden inside it. The boots were at the front. The cart had stopped at the front. But there would be a second party; Vasse never spent six men where he could spend twelve.

"Both," he said.

* * *

It had been eleven days since they'd crawled out of the river valley with the ledger sewn into Coral's coat lining, eleven days of ditches and haylofts and one unbearable night in a shepherd's hut where Coral had finally said aloud the thing they'd both been carrying like contraband heavier than paper — *I don't want to survive this without you knowing* — and Emrys had answered by taking his hand and not letting go until dawn. Eleven days, and the capital had turned out to be not a refuge but a maze. Three ministries had turned them away. Two clerks had pretended not to speak English. And then Merrin — gray-bearded, ink-stained, unimpressed by guns — had read the first page of the ledger at his kitchen table, gone white, and said, "Do you understand what you've brought me? Do you understand I can't print this alone?"

Verification, he'd said. Corroboration. A second source, an affidavit, something more than the word of a deserter and a clockmaker. He needed four days. He'd had three.

Now it was the fourth day, three hours before dawn, and Colonel Aurel Vasse had found them anyway.

"The back," Emrys said. "There'll be a window over the yard. Take the ledger and go."

"We're not doing this again." Coral's voice was low and furious and shaking only slightly, which meant he was terrified and fighting it, which meant Emrys loved him so much it felt like injury. "You held the stairway at the garrison records office. You held the stairway at the ferry. You are not—"

"Coral." Emrys took his face in both hands, thumbs along his jaw, ink smearing across the stubble. "Listen to me once. Vasse wants me alive. Whatever else that man is, he follows procedure — a lieutenant taken resisting arrest is a trial, paperwork, witnesses, questions he can't afford. You, he can disappear. A clockmaker with no family and no rank doesn't need a trial. He needs a ditch." He pressed their foreheads together. "So the arithmetic is simple. I'm worth more to you standing at that stair than running beside you."

"That isn't arithmetic, that's cowardice wearing your face."

"It's the opposite." Emrys smiled, and was surprised to find the smile was real. "I've been afraid my whole life, love. Of my father's name being spoken wrong. Of wanting things I wasn't issued. Of you, most of all, from the moment you stood in that observatory with paper falling out of your coat like a man caught stealing bread." His voice cracked and he let it. "I'm not afraid now. Do you know why? Because for the first time there's nothing left I'm protecting except this."

He pulled back, picked up the ledger from the proof table, and held it out.

Above them, the front door came down with a sound like a felled tree.

* * *

They moved. That was the mercy of trained people — grief and terror could argue forever, but bodies that had been drilled did as they were told. Coral took the ledger and ran for the back stairs; Merrin killed the gas lamp and shepherded the apprentice boy toward the cellar hatch; and Emrys crossed to the foot of the main staircase — the long central stair that ran from the composing floor up to the paper store and the gallery above — and put himself at its base the way he'd been taught to hold a trench: low, angled, presenting the smallest possible silhouette, the iron rail at his hip.

Twelve men came through the ruined door with lanterns and carbines, and at their head, unhurried, buttoned to the throat despite the hour, walked Colonel Aurel Vasse.

"Lieutenant Hale." Vasse's voice carried in the press-room the way a verdict carries in a courtroom — flat, final, addressed to the record rather than the room. "Desertion. Theft of military documents. Conspiracy against the Crown's administration." He removed his glove finger by finger. "You may add breaking and entering, if it helps your conscience feel properly weighted."

"You have no warrant for this building."

"I have twelve soldiers and a printing press full of treason, Lieutenant. Warrants are for peacetime." Vasse's eyes went past him, up the stairs, calculating. Emrys watched him do the mathematics — the gallery, the paper store, the second exit — and saw the exact moment the colonel concluded that whatever mattered was already climbing toward the roof. "Sergeant Brann. Six men upstairs."

"No," Emrys said, and stepped onto the first stair.

Vasse sighed, the sigh of a man disappointed by weather. "Lieutenant. You are one unarmed man on a staircase."

"I'm not unarmed." It was a lie. He had a boot knife and a length of iron pipe he'd taken from a broken press frame, and he gripped the pipe now and let it be seen. "And I'm not alone in what I am, Colonel. You erased my father. You erased four hundred names in Vellmarsh. You've spent ten years teaching me exactly how little your rules are worth, and tonight I'm spending the lesson."

Something flickered in Vasse's face — not anger. Recognition. The look of a chess player seeing a gambit he'd read about in a book and never believed anyone desperate enough to play.

"Take him," Vasse said quietly. "Alive."

* * *

They came up three abreast, because a staircase is a funnel and every soldier knows it, and Emrys learned in the first thirty seconds that he was better than any of them — not stronger, not faster, but willing. They fought like men doing a job. He fought like a man paying a debt.

The first came in high with a carbine butt; Emrys dropped under it and drove his shoulder into the man's knee and felt the joint give with a sensation he knew he'd carry forever, and then the second was on him and the pipe rang off a rifle barrel and the third grabbed his collar, and after that it stopped being fencing and became wrestling, ugly and close, boots skidding on paper-dust, lanterns swinging wild shadows up the stairwell walls. He took an elbow across the mouth and tasted blood. He heard shouting below — Vasse's voice, cold, ordering someone down to the yard — and understood with a lurch that they'd found the back window, that Coral was out or nearly out, and the knowledge went through him like warmth through frozen hands.

*Hold the stair.* Hold it until the yard was empty. Hold it until Merrin's cellar route swallowed Coral whole. Hold it until—

A carbine stock came down on his shoulder — the left, the old wound — and the world went white at the edges. He went to one knee. Hands seized his arms. And through the ringing in his skull he heard, distantly, from somewhere beyond the back wall, the sound he'd been listening eleven days for and would listen for the rest of his life:

Whistles. Not army whistles. The short-two-three of the capital watch, the constables' pattern, and behind them voices, many voices, and Merrin's apprentice boy shrieking from a rooftop that here, here, come here.

Merrin hadn't waited for verification. Merrin had sent for the watch the moment the door came down — *a second source*, the old man had said, and apparently the second source was half the city's constabulary, who wanted Vasse's occupation scandalized as badly as any newspaper did.

The hands holding Emrys loosened. Someone shouted *constables, fall back, fall back* — and then, cutting through it all, one sound that stopped Emrys's heart like a hand around its mainspring:

A single shot from the yard.

Then another.

Then silence, and boots running away into the lanes, and Vasse's voice, farther off now, giving orders Emrys couldn't parse through the blood-noise, and Emrys wrenching free of the last grip and going down the stairs two at a time and out through the ruined door into the gray before dawn with no plan at all except the yard, the wall, the window.

* * *

The print shop's back yard was eight feet of cobblestones between the building and a high brick wall, and in the middle of it, against the wall where he'd boosted himself up and been hauled back down by two of Vasse's men, lay Coral.

He was sitting up. That was the first thing Emrys's eyes did before his mind allowed anything else — found him vertical, conscious, cursing. The second thing they did was find the blood, dark on the pale gray cobbles, spreading from Coral's shoulder with the patient indifference of spilled ink.

One of the soldiers who'd caught him lay groaning by the wall with a broken nose. The other had run with the rest. The shot — Emrys would learn later that it had gone wide, a panicked pull at a fleeing man on a wall — had torn through the meat of Coral's left shoulder just below the collarbone, missing the joint, missing the lung, missing, by the width of a horologist's fingertip, everything that couldn't be mended.

"No," Emrys said, though the man was clearly alive, clearly swearing, clearly furious — "no, no—" and then he was on his knees in the blood with his jacket off and folded under his hands, pressing hard, and Coral was looking up at him with an expression of absolute outrage.

"You *held the stair*," Coral said. "I told you—"

"You climbed a wall with a hole in you."

"Somebody had to be dramatic. You'd used it all up on your speech." Coral's teeth were chattering. His color was wrong. But his right hand came up and closed on Emrys's collar with surprising strength and dragged him down until their foreheads touched, blood and ink and gun oil between them. "Ledger's safe. Roof, then gutter, then the chandler's yard. Merrin has it — he went for the watch, the mad old fool went for the *watch* — Emrys. Emrys, look at me. It's printed by Friday. It's done. We did it."

Boots were coming back — constable boots, the two-three rhythm — and lantern-light bloomed in the gateway, and Emrys knelt in the yard holding the man he loved together with both hands and realized that for the first time since he'd put on the uniform at seventeen, he had no orders to follow, no mission, no next checkpoint. There was only this: pressure on a wound, dawn coming up gray over the chimneys, and the astonishing, unbearable fact of still being alive.

"I heard the shot," he said. His voice didn't sound like his own. "In the stairwell. Everything I am — everything — it just *stopped*. There was nothing after that sound. Do you understand? There was no world."

"I know," Coral said. "I heard yours too. At the garrison gate. When you stepped between me and that sergeant." His eyes were glassy but fixed on Emrys with terrible clarity. "That's what it is, then. That's the answer to the question we've been asking since the observatory. It isn't duty, it isn't the uniform, it isn't even courage. It's that there's no version of the world I accept where you aren't in it."

"Sentimental," Emrys managed, "for a man bleeding on a printer."

"Clockmakers keep time," Coral said. "You're my time. All of it."

The constables flooded the yard then — six of them, truncheons out, followed by Merrin with the ledger clutched to his chest like a rescued child and the apprentice boy behind him white as paper — and the world resumed its machinery: bandages fetched from the shop's first-aid tin, a surgeon sent for, Vasse's men cornered two streets over and arrested with satisfying completeness, the colonel himself already gone, vanished into the administrative arteries of the city, wounded worse than any bullet could manage but not yet dead.

They loaded Coral onto a shutter as gently as if he were made of the same glass as Wrenn's regulator clocks, and Emrys rode beside him holding his good hand, and Coral looked up at the ledges of the rooftops sliding past against the brightening sky and said, "Four days. He gave us four days and we used three and a half. Print it Thursday, Emrys. Don't let them make us wait for Friday."

"It'll be printed," Emrys said. "Whatever else happens. It will be printed."

But in the corridor of the surgeon's house, an hour later, while Coral slept under dressings and ether, a constable found Emrys and spoke to him quietly, hat in hand, apologetic as a man delivering bad weather: the raid on Vasse's quarters had been executed at dawn. Papers seized. Ledgers recovered.

Colonel Vasse himself — gone. Not fled, the constable said. That was the trouble.

He'd gone to the Ministry of War, walked into the adjutant's office at seven o'clock, and filed charges. Desertion, theft, conspiracy, treason. Against Lieutenant Emrys Hale. And against one Coral Ashgrove of Vellmarsh — whom, the filing stated, the Crown had never had any record of at all.

As far as the ministry was concerned, Emrys read from the copy the constable passed him with shaking official hands, the man in the surgeon's bed did not exist. And a man who does not exist, the filing concluded in Vasse's precise, unhurried hand, may be dealt with by whatever means present themselves.

Emrys stood in the corridor with the paper in his fist and listened to Coral breathe behind the door, slow and steady as a well-made escapement, and understood that the last checkpoint had not been the mountain road after all. It was here. It was now. And this time there was no uniform left to hide behind, no rank, no procedure — nothing between Coral and the machine but a newspaper that hadn't inked its first line yet, and whatever Emrys Hale could become in the four days before it did.
