## 22. Emrys and the Uniform

The uniform came back to Vellmarsh in a parcel wrapped in brown paper and string, and Emrys Hale carried it up the observatory hill as though it were something that might still bite.

It had followed him through the scandal like a dog he couldn't shake. The army had stripped him of rank with one hand — the court-martial's findings read aloud in a cold room in the capital while Coral sat in the gallery with his hands folded, refusing to look away — and then, with the other, had returned his effects: his mother's letter, a worn copy of a surveying manual, and the uniform itself, brushed and pressed and folded by some anonymous quartermaster's clerk who had never heard of Colonel Vasse or a ledger full of names. That was the thing about the machine, Emrys had learned. It did not know what it carried. It only knew how to fold.

He stopped at the observatory door, the parcel under his arm, and looked at the building for longer than he needed to.

Three months had passed since the *Vellmarsh Clarion* had run the ledger across eight columns, since the warrants, since the columns of soldiers had marched out of town the way they'd marched in — in step, in silence, certain of their purpose and wrong about everything. Three months since he had lain in a prison hospital bed with a bullet hole through his shoulder and woken to find Coral asleep in the chair beside him, still holding the ledger, as if even unconsciousness had not persuaded him to let it go.

The door was unlocked. Coral never locked it anymore. He said a clockmaker whose clock struck true had nothing worth stealing.

Inside, the tower smelled the way it always had — oil and brass and dust warmed by sunlight through the south windows — but underneath that, now, there was something else. Bread, maybe. Someone had been baking in the observatory's little back room, which was the kind of fact that would have been unthinkable a year ago, when the building had belonged to the army's schedules and nobody's comfort.

Emrys climbed the stairs slowly. His shoulder ached on landings, an honest pain, better than guilt.

Coral was at the top, sleeves rolled, half inside the master clock's case. He didn't turn around, but his voice came out warm from among the gears.

"I heard you on the stairs. You still climb like a man expecting to be arrested."

"Habit." Emrys set the parcel on the workbench. "You've flour on your collar."

"That's Wrenn's fault. I've been keeping his old recipes alive along with everything else." Coral backed out of the case, wiping his hands on a rag, and his eyes went to the parcel the moment Emrys stopped shielding it. He didn't ask what it was. He knew its shape the way he knew the shape of any mechanism — by what moved around it.

"The army sent it," Emrys said. "Everything I own fits in this paper."

"Most men's do."

"It's the uniform, Coral."

"I can see that it's the uniform."

Emrys looked at it. Through the brown paper he could see the fold of the collar, the dark line of braid where his lieutenant's insignia had been unpicked, leaving small pale ghosts in the cloth. "I keep thinking I should burn it. It seems like the thing a man does. A ceremony. But I can't make my hands want it."

Coral set down the rag. He crossed the platform, took the parcel out of Emrys's grip, and weighed it in both hands like a movement he was assessing for faults.

"Don't burn it," he said. "Burning's easy. Burning is what people do when they want history to forget them. Come here — there's a nail in the case wall that's been bothering me for weeks. I think it should hold something better than nothing."

* * *

They hung it together, though it did not need two people. Coral held the coat against the inside of the clock case, flat against the great wheel's housing, where it would hang in the dry dark among oil and brass; Emrys drove the nail, and Coral adjusted the shoulders twice until the collar sat straight, and neither of them spoke, because some installations are too precise for talk.

When it was done they stepped back.

In the dim interior of the case, lit whenever the case door swung, the uniform hung with its empty sleeves straight and its unpicked braid catching what light the gears allowed. It did not look like a relic of shame. Emrys had expected it to look like a skin he'd shed, something crumpled and defeated. Instead it looked like what it had always been: a costume that a young man had once put on because he believed it would take him somewhere, and which had taken him, in the end, exactly here.

"It should face the escapement," Coral said quietly. "So every second it releases, the uniform gets a piece of the light. Let it watch how time is supposed to be kept."

"You're a strange man, Coral Ashgrove."

"I'm a clockmaker. We're all strange. It's in the apprenticeship papers." Coral closed the case door most of the way, leaving it open a hand's width, and stood looking through the gap. "Tobias would have liked this. He always said you shouldn't hide the things that taught you. He said a workshop should be honest about its scars, same as its hands."

Emrys thought of Tobias Wrenn, whom he had never met, who had wound this clock one last time with guards' hands on his arms and then given Coral a sentence instead of a goodbye. There was a brass plate inside the case now, screwed in at eye level: *T. WRENN — WHO KEPT FAITH WITH THE MECHANISM.* Townsfolk came up the hill some Sundays just to open the case door and look at it, the way other towns visited graves.

"He passed me a debt I never agreed to," Emrys said. "I wasn't the one he sheltered."

"You wore the uniform he distrusted, and you turned it around. That's paying a debt the hard way." Coral glanced at him. "He'd have counted it."

Below them, through the floor, the clock gathered itself and struck eleven. They both stood still for it — they always did now; it was not something they had decided, it was simply what their bodies had learned — twelve strokes rolling down through the tower and out over the roofs of Vellmarsh, where people were hanging washing, arguing about fish prices, teaching children letters that included their own true names.

"There's work downstairs," Coral said, when the last stroke had settled. "If you're staying."

"I'm staying."

"You say that every morning."

"And every morning it's still true. One day you'll stop testing the mechanism."

"One day the clock will stop winding itself," Coral said, and picked up his toolbox, and went down the stairs ahead of him, whistling something tuneless that Emrys suspected was Wrenn's too.

* * *

The bench they shared was built for one.

That was the joke of it, and after six weeks neither of them found it funny anymore so much as true: Coral's father's old workbench, dragged from the shop below the lodgings on Cordwainer Row, made for a single apprentice and a single master, now crowded with two sets of tools, two lamps, two chairs that knocked at the legs whenever either man leaned back. They could have built another bench. Coral had the lumber. Neither of them raised it.

Their days had found a shape, the way mechanisms find a shape — not designed but discovered, each part wearing into its neighbor until the fit was smooth.

Mornings, Coral took the delicate work: the carriage clocks and the repeaters, the jeweled movements brought up from the capital by customers who had heard, somehow, that the clockmaker who broke a garrison's occupation also handled an escapement like an apology. Afternoons were the town's clocks — the tavern regulator with its pendulum gone sour, the chapel tower that struck the quarter-hours in the wrong order, the schoolhouse clock whose hands Coral replaced with new brass he polished himself, saying the children should learn to tell time from something beautiful.

And Emrys worked beside him on the jobs that wanted patience rather than fineness: cleaning cases, polishing dials, hauling the long-case bodies upright with his good arm while the shoulder complained and Coral pretended not to notice and Emrys pretended not to notice Coral pretending. He had learned to file. He was slow at it, methodical in the military way, and at first the slowness shamed him — a lieutenant reduced to learning the grain of brass — until the evening a farmer came in with a grandfather clock three generations deep in neglect, and Emrys spent four hours easing its swollen case doors, and the farmer watched him work and said, "You've got the hands for this, sir," and called him *sir* without a shred of irony, and Emrys had to go out to the yard and stand in the cold for a while.

"You're mending," Coral told him that night. "That's all it is. Mending feels like losing until you look back."

"I spent five years learning to break things in order."

"Now spend ten learning to fix them. You'll come out ahead."

They fell into an argument, as they did most evenings, about whether a clock owed its owner honesty or mercy — Coral maintaining that a clock that ran fast was lying, Emrys maintaining that a clock that stopped dead was crueler than one that drifted — and the argument was not really an argument, it was the shape their ease took, the sound of two men filling a workshop with noise because silence had been dangerous for too long and they were teaching themselves it wasn't anymore.

Some nights, though, the silence came anyway, and it was good silence.

There was one such night in late autumn, rain working at the windows, the stove throwing orange light across the bench. Coral was re-pivoting a balance staff under the loupe, his breath held the way he held it for such work, and Emrys was supposed to be sorting screws but was actually watching him — the small economy of his hands, the way ten years of apprenticeship lived in every motion without effort, the lamplight pooling in the hollows of his face.

"You're staring," Coral said, without looking up.

"Yes."

"Any particular reason?"

"You're the finest mechanism in the room. A man inspects the finest mechanism in the room."

Coral laughed — a short, real laugh that fogged the loupe — and set down his tools, and took off the loupe, and looked at Emrys across the cluttered bench with an expression Emrys had learned to read the way he'd once learned to read troop dispositions: closely, gratefully, knowing what it cost to be shown.

"Do you remember," Coral said, "the first night you came back here? What the Uniform Owes. Chapter and verse. You stood right about where you're standing and you told me your father's name had been erased and you proposed we commit treason together, and I thought — this man has come to get us both hanged, and God help me, I believe him anyway."

"I remember you didn't thank me."

"I didn't trust you."

"You hid the ledger in your coat badly enough that a child could have seen it. Trust wasn't the issue. Survival instinct was."

"My survival instinct has been wrong about you from the beginning," Coral said, "in both directions. First it said you were a trap. Then it said you were impossible. It has finally settled on *inevitable*, which I'm told is the correct reading."

Emrys came around the bench. There was barely room between the chairs; there had never been room; that was the point of the bench. He put his good hand on Coral's shoulder and felt, beneath the shirt, the tension of a man who had been carrying weight for two years finally setting a portion of it down.

"In the hospital," Emrys said, "when I woke up and you were asleep in the chair — I lay there for an hour watching you hold that ledger even in your sleep. And I thought: I have served under colonels and I have burned my country's orders in a print shop stairwell, and none of it ever felt like duty the way this does. Sitting in a chair. Holding a book. Refusing to put it down."

"Duty," Coral said. "You're using army vocabulary at me."

"I'm telling you that I've found the only posting I don't want to resign."

"And what posting is that?"

"Yours," Emrys said. "However long the commission runs."

Coral reached up and closed his hand over Emrys's where it rested on his shoulder, and for a while neither of them said anything, because the stove ticked and the rain came down and above them, faint through two floors of timber, the master clock kept its steady division of the dark into survivable pieces.

"We should wind the turret lights before bed," Coral said at last, softly, against Emrys's wrist. "The lamp oil won't last till morning otherwise."

"Tomorrow," Emrys said. "Tonight the clock can keep time without being watched. It's earned the privacy."

* * *

Winter came, and with it the anniversary.

Vellmarsh marked it without ceremony, because the town had learned its lesson about ceremonies — that they could be seized, repurposed, turned inside out. Instead people simply went about their business on the day the columns had marched in, and at noon, when the observatory clock struck, they paused in the streets and shops and let the twelve strokes finish before resuming, which was its own kind of observance, and no orders required it.

On the anniversary evening, Coral climbed the tower alone.

Emrys was below, finishing a mantel clock for the widow of the printer — Aldous Merrin had secured the man's release in the spring, thin and half-blind and asking immediately whether anyone had saved his type — and Coral wanted, just once, to stand with the master clock without talking to anyone.

He opened the case. The uniform hung where they'd hung it, dust-free because Coral kept it dust-free, the pale ghost-marks of the unpicked insignia catching the lamplight. Below it, the great wheel turned its patient turn, and the escapement ticked its permission, second by second by second.

Coral put his fingers on the case frame, beside the brass plate.

"I kept it wound," he said, to Wrenn, to the uniform, to the mechanism, to whoever was listening. "All of it."

Then he wound the barrel, checked the beat, listened until the tick was perfectly even, and went down the stairs toward the lamplight and the cluttered bench and the man waiting there with tea for two, as the clock above them struck the hour — unhurried, unafraid, and true.
