## 1. The Weight of Empty Paper

Three months, and Gideon Thackeray had learned to measure grief in candle stubs.

He went through them the way other men went through tobacco or prayer — one a night, sometimes two if the coasting winds kept Felix awake and the boy's restlessness crept down the stairs like a draft. Tonight's stub was already bowing at the waist, its flame leaning toward the window as though it too wanted a look at the road.

The Atlas Felix lay open before him on the kitchen table, which had been cleared of everything except the atlas, three pens, an inkhorn wrapped in flannel so it wouldn't clink, and a loaf of bread pushed discreetly to the far edge in case the work ran long and he ran hungry. The chart beneath his pen was the Cornish coast from Lizard Point to Penzance, and Master Aldous Felix — dead these three months, drowned or burned or broken, no one could say which, because no one had been permitted to say anything at all — had drawn the soundings with a delicacy that still made Gideon's throat ache. The old man had dotted the sea floor as if it were freckled. Every shoal a small kindness: *here, careful, here.*

Gideon filled in the last six miles and sat back.

"Anything?" he called softly, up the stairs.

The pause that followed was Felix-shaped. The boy had a genius for pauses; he'd inherited it, Gideon suspected, from all those years of listening at the master's knee without ever being told what the grown conversations meant.

"Cart," Felix said at last, through the floorboards. "Or wheels. Coming up from the ford."

"Cart or wheels?"

"I said what I said."

Gideon blew out the candle. Darkness dropped over the table like a lid, and he slid the finished chart into the space between two blank sheets — *empty paper*, they called it now, the whole stack of it, because paper with nothing drawn on it drew no eyes and burned no men — and carried it to the sea chest by feel. The false bottom lifted on oiled hinges that made no sound because Gideon had spent an entire evening making certain they would never again make a sound. He laid the chart flat among its brothers, thirty-one of them now, each one a small act of treason rendered in beautiful hand, and closed the chest, and put the mending basket back on top of it exactly where Mrs. Prynne's mending basket always sat, because nobody searched under stockings with holes in the heels.

Above him, the floor creaked once — Felix shifting at the attic window — then twice — Felix deciding the cart was not, after all, a cart worth dying over — then fell quiet.

*Empty paper.* That had been the master's phrase, back when there'd been a workshop to hide things in instead of a widow's cottage rented under a name that belonged to a woman four years buried. *If they come for you, Gideon, give them empty paper and an honest shrug. A shrug has saved more lives than a sword.* Aldous Felix had been full of such sayings, most of them practical, several of them funny, all of them useless to him in the end, because when the Admiralty men came they hadn't asked questions first. They had asked questions second. First they had burned everything, and the light of it had reached the harbor, and for one long terrible minute every boat in the bay had turned to look.

The master had not come out.

They said the fire took him. They said a great many things. What Gideon knew was this: three months of ash, one funeral held anyway because towns need funerals the way ships need keels, and one orphan boy who had stood at the graveside with no body to grieve over, grieving magnificently.

And one atlas, half-finished, hidden in a sea chest, growing by one candle-stub a night.

* * *

Felix came down near midnight, the way he did, ostensibly for bread and actually for company. He was eleven going on twelve and built entirely of elbows and watchfulness, with the master's dark hair falling into his eyes and none of the master's patience holding it back.

"The cart was Mr. Tregose," he reported, taking the heel of the loaf without asking, because in this house bread rights were earned by vigilance and he'd been vigilant all evening. "Taking turnips to the mill. I watched him unload. I counted the turnips."

"How many?"

"Fifty-one. Which seemed excessive, so I watched until he dropped one in the lane, so really fifty delivered and one lost." He tore the bread and chewed. "You're doing the Lizard tonight? You were on the Lizard."

"You could see that from the stairs?"

"I can see your shoulders. You do a thing with your shoulders on the coast charts. Different from the rivers." He said it lightly, around the bread, as if it were nothing, as if he had not just described, with a child's careless accuracy, something Gideon himself had never noticed about his own body.

"You should be asleep."

"So should you."

"That's different. I'm a grown man ruining my health through stubbornness. It's traditional."

Felix grinned — quick, crooked, the master's grin worn slightly too large, like a coat cut for broader shoulders — and for a while they simply sat in the dark kitchen, two keepers of an empty-paper secret, listening to the wind come off the water.

It was Felix who heard the horse first. Not a cart this time; a rider, hard-paced, coming up the cliff road from the quay, which no honest rider did at half past midnight unless the tide had brought him in. Gideon's hand found the boy's shoulder and pressed him flat against the wall, out of the window's line, and the two of them stood breathing like a single frightened animal while hooves rang closer, closer, and stopped.

Knocking. Three blows, unhurried.

"Gideon Thackeray." A stranger's voice, roughened by salt crossing. "I've ridden from Falmouth and crossed worse than your lane to find you. Open the door. I bring word of Aldous Felix."

The world went very narrow and very bright, as though the blown-out candle had relit itself somewhere behind Gideon's ribs.

"He's dead," Gideon said to the door. "Whatever game this is—"

"He is *alive*," said the voice, "and imprisoned, and writing letters in a hand you'd know anywhere, because you spent ten years grinding his ink. Open the door, man. It's raining, and I have papers that will drown if you don't."

Gideon's hands were shaking so badly it took him twice as long as it should have to draw the bolt. Felix had gone utterly still against the wall — that trained stillness, the boy's oldest inheritance — but his eyes in the dark were enormous, and fixed on the door as though he could burn through it with wanting alone.

The stranger came in with the storm behind him: a lean man of perhaps forty, soaked past saving, hat ruined, cloak streaming, with the deep-sea pallor of someone who had spent a fortnight being sick on a packet boat and had opinions about it. He set a satchel on the table — directly on top of where the loaf had been, Gideon noted distantly, some part of his mind still keeping house — and drew out oilcloth, and under the oilcloth letters, four of them, tied with cord.

He held the first one to the light of the lamp he'd brought, and Gideon saw the handwriting.

That was the moment. Not the knocking, not the word *alive*. The moment was the handwriting — that looping, confident, faintly smug script that Gideon had mixed ink for ten thousand times, that had corrected his earliest charts in margins gentle as a father's, that had signed the apprenticeship papers binding Gideon Thackeray to Aldous Felix until death or mastery parted them.

The hand was unsteady now. The loops trembled. But it lived. It *lived*.

"Well," Gideon said, and found his voice had gone somewhere and left a stranger's in its place, thin and high. "Well," he said again, and sat down rather harder than he intended on the bench.

Felix crept forward and looked at the letter over the stranger's arm, and Gideon watched the boy read the signature, and watched eleven years of graveside grief crack open like ice in spring — hope flooding in where nothing had been supposed to grow again.

"Read it aloud," Felix whispered.

The stranger broke the seal. Outside, the rain came down on the cottage roof like applause, or like warning.

"*To Gideon Thackeray,*" he read, "*and to my son, if he is with you —*"
