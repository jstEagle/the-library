## 2. Ink and Ransom

Gideon Thackeray had imagined this moment a hundred times in three months, and in none of his imaginings did he throw up.

But there it was. Corvane sat dripping by the hearth of the ruined malting loft, letters fanned out on the table like the winning hand in a game Gideon had been losing for half a year, and Gideon's stomach simply announced its opinion and made for the door. He was sick into the gutter rain, hands braced on his knees, while behind him a stranger who smelled of tar and wet wool said, kindly enough, "That's about how I took it too."

"You're lying," Gideon said when he came back in. "He burned. I saw the workshop burn. I carried what was left of him out of it — or what I told myself was left of him."

"You carried out his coat," Corvane said. "The coat survived. The coat, as it happens, was the only thing that did." He held up one of the letters. "Read the hand."

Gideon read the hand.

It was Aldous Felix's script, unmistakable even through prison blurring and water stains: the great sweeping ascenders, the little hooks on his *f*s that looked like fishing lines waiting for a bite. Three months Gideon had spent imitating that hand in secret, working by candle stub to finish the Atlas Felix, and now here it was, alive, complaining about prison porridge in a postscript that was pure Master Aldous: *The porridge is an insult to oats, which are blameless.*

"He's in the Marshalsea wing at Portsmouth," Corvane said. "Debtors' side, mostly. They can't prove anything against him anymore because they burned all their evidence along with everything else — Admiralty men are thorough the way a house fire is thorough. But he owes them. And they have decided what he owes them is the atlas."

"The atlas they burned him for."

"They regret the burning," Corvane said, with a shrug like a man describing weather in another county. "Charts are worth more than principles. They'll trade his freedom for the finished work. Every leaf."

Gideon sat down slowly. The false-bottomed sea chest sat against the wall where it had sat for three months, holding the masterwork of a dead man who was not dead. He had built his whole grieving self around that chest. Now grief was being asked to make change.

"There's a condition," Corvane said.

"There's always a condition."

"Delivery in person. By courier. To the Admiralty office at Portsmouth, before the month is out." Corvane looked at the chest, then at the boy asleep in the corner — when had Felix fallen asleep? The child could sleep standing in a gale; it was one of his many useless talents. "One courier travels light. One courier travels free."

*And one boy,* Gideon thought, *travels nowhere.* He kept his face very still, an old habit from the workshop, where Master Aldous could read an unfinished thought off a face like coordinates off a chart.

"I'll need paper," Gideon said. "A lot of paper."

Corvane raised an eyebrow.

"If they want the atlas," Gideon said, "they'll have the atlas. There is nothing in the arrangement that says I can't keep a copy. Nothing at all."

Corvane smiled, slow as sunrise over cold water. "Aldous said you'd say exactly that. Word for word." He tapped the last letter. "His exact words were: 'The boy will copy it, every leaf, and think himself clever. Tell him not to skimp on the coastlines.' "

* * *

Felix was not asleep. Felix was never as asleep as people believed, a fact he considered his single most valuable talent and shared with no one.

He lay in the corner with his breath going long and even, and he listened to the men talk, and he learned three things. First: Master Aldous was alive, which made his chest feel like a bell rung too hard. Second: the atlas could buy him back. Third — and he made himself say it to the inside of his own skull until it stopped stinging — couriers travel light. Couriers travel free. Boys do neither.

So that was settled, then. He would not be going anywhere. It was better to know a thing early than to spend weeks hoping it might be otherwise. Hope, Felix had learned at eight years old in the workhouse, was just pain paid in installments.

What he would not do was mope. Moping was for boys who had nothing better to do, and Felix had something much better to do.

The next morning, while Gideon slept off his night of copying, Felix went to work.

The master had taught him things without ever seeming to teach them — that was Aldous Felix's particular genius, smuggling education past a child's defenses the way smugglers moved brandy. Surveying had come to Felix disguised as games. Measuring the church tower by its shadow had been a game. Pacing out distances and counting his steps had been a game. Learning the stars had been songs before bed.

Now Felix dug the old brass instruments out of the sea chest's false bottom — Gideon thought he didn't know about the false bottom; Felix knew about the false bottom, the hinges squeaked, honestly — and set up at the loft's one unbroken window with a slate and chalk.

By noon he had measured the harbor. Badly. By evening he had measured it again, slightly less badly. By the third day he could take the angle to the lighthouse and calculate its distance within a hundred yards, which the master would have called "tolerable" in the tone that meant nearly good, and Felix had decided tolerable was the sweetest word in the English language.

He was doing it for two reasons, though he only admitted one of them. The admitted reason: so the atlas would be perfect when it was delivered, because he could check Gideon's coastlines against what was actually out the window, and the master's name would not be shamed by a rushed copy. The unadmitted reason he kept folded up small, like a letter he couldn't bear to reread: if he could prove he was useful — if he was not merely the boy but the surveyor — then perhaps when the choosing came, he would not be so easy to leave.

* * *

Gideon noticed on the fifth day, and it undid him worse than Corvane's news had.

He had come back from the chandler with paper hidden in a flour sack — copying an entire atlas required more paper than a man could buy openly without questions — and found Felix at the window with the master's cross-staff held wrong, squinting down it at the headland, lips moving through sums.

"What are you doing?"

Felix started so hard he nearly put his eye out. "Nothing."

"That's the surveyor's staff."

"It's a stick."

"It is a stick worth forty pounds."

"Then I'm being careful with it," Felix said, chin up, and Gideon saw it then — the whole architecture of it, the slate covered in figures copied from memory of lessons the boy had apparently absorbed whole while pretending to be bored, the harbor measurements, the fierce desperate industry of a child building a case for his own keeping.

Something in Gideon's chest turned over and would not turn back.

"Felix," he began, "you should know—"

"The tide tables are wrong in your second sheet," Felix interrupted, with the brutal confidence of the young. "You've got the spring tides an hour early. I checked against the harbor bell."

Gideon looked at his own careful copying, three weeks of work, and then at the boy. "You checked my tide tables."

"Somebody has to," said Felix, "if you're going to pass it off as the master's work."

And that was when Gideon understood, with the clarity of cold water, that he could not send this child anywhere. Not away from himself. Not into harm. Not into the arithmetic of berths and couriers where boys weighed less than atlases.

Which meant, some cold arithmetic of his own whispered back, that somebody else might pay for the boy instead.

* * *

The Admiralty scouts came to the harbor on a Tuesday, in grey coats, counting ships.

Felix spotted them first from the window — he knew the walk of them, the master had taught him that too, disguised as a game called Spot the Revenue Man. "Two of them," he said quietly. "At the quay. Looking at the packet ship."

Gideon joined him. Below, the *Larkspur* rode at her moorings, taking on cargo, and a mate with a manifest was arguing with a fishwife while the grey coats watched everything and wrote nothing and missed nothing.

"They're cataloguing," Gideon murmured. "Every hull, every berth. That means orders are coming. Something's about to happen to this coast."

"War things?"

"Worse. Administration things." Gideon watched the scouts confer, touch their hats to nobody, and move on up toward the harbormaster's office. His mouth had gone dry. "They count ships before they empty towns, Felix. And they count berths."

The word *berths* hung in the air between them like smoke.

That night Gideon laid out the finished leaves of the Atlas Felix across the loft floor, forty-two charts of coastline that three people had bled and burned and nearly died for, and he began to copy the forty-third by candlelight, faster now, racing something he couldn't yet name. Felix slept — or performed sleeping — in the corner, one hand curled around a slate.

Outside, far down the coast toward Portsmouth, Gideon imagined he could almost see the place where a living man sat behind a debtors' wall complaining about porridge, waiting to be bought.

In the morning, a drumbeat woke the town. Red-coated riders at the harbormaster's door, and a notice being nailed to the customs house board:

**BY ORDER OF THE ADMIRALTY: THE PORT IS TO BE EVACUATED. ONE SHIP WILL SAIL. PAPERS ONLY.**
