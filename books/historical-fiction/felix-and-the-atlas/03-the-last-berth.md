## 3. The Last Berth

The redcoats came on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Surely the end of a town deserved a more dramatic day.

Felix watched from the bakery window as the officer nailed the proclamation to the market cross, his boots splashing through puddles left by the night's storm. *By order of the Admiralty, all inhabitants lacking papers of residence shall depart aboard His Majesty's packet ship* Merrow *no later than the morning tide, Friday.* The words were read aloud twice for the crowd, once in English and once — for the fishermen who pretended not to understand either — in the flat shout of a man who had never had to make himself heard over gulls.

"Evacuated," said Mrs. Pellow beside him, sucking her teeth. "Eleven years I've lived here and they call it *evacuating*. It's emptying us out like a jug."

Felix did not answer. He was doing sums, and the sums were bad.

Papers of residence. Gideon had papers — forged, Felix suspected, but convincing ones, bought from a man in Rye who owed Master Aldous an old debt. Corvane had papers, being English-born and insufferably well-documented about it. And Felix had a baptismal record from a parish that had since burned down, a name that appeared in no ledger anywhere, and eleven years of being raised by a cartographer who believed that if a thing wasn't drawn, it didn't officially exist.

Which meant, when the arithmetic finished itself, there was one berth on the *Merrow*, and it was not going to be his.

He should have felt frightened. What he felt instead was a strange, cold clarity, the kind he imagined surveyors must feel when the fog lifts and they finally see how far off their map the coast truly lies.

Gideon's POV

The trouble with love, Gideon thought, was that it made a man count.

He counted at the harbor: the *Merrow*, riding low and impatient, taking families first, then goods, then — if there was room, which there would not be — sentiment. He counted the atlas: forty-three finished sheets in the sea chest, each one worth more than the house they sat above, each one the exact price of Master Aldous's freedom. He counted Corvane, who stood at the window turning his father's letters over in his hands as though he could read them a second time into meaning something new.

"One berth," Corvane said. "I've spoken with the master of the packet. There is one berth remaining after the papers are checked, and it is mine by right of passage purchased." He looked up. "But I can transfer it. To you. Or—"

"To no one," Gideon said. "Because you're taking the atlas to London, and I'm not letting it out of my sight until it's out of this country."

"And the boy?"

The question hung between them the way a dropped glass hangs before it breaks.

"The boy comes with me," Gideon said. "Or I stay with the boy. That's the whole of my arithmetic."

"Then your arithmetic ends with you both stranded on a quay watching a ship leave," Corvane said gently. "And my father rotting in the Marshalsea while you two are arrested for harboring unregistered persons. The Admiralty men coming for the evacuation will check papers, Thackeray. They will check *his*."

Gideon wanted to argue. Instead he heard, very faintly, the sound of the street door easing shut — that particular soft click of a latch lowered with care, which every person who has ever tried to hide something learns to recognize too late.

He was up the stairs two at a time. The loft was empty. Felix's coat was gone from its peg. On the table, weighted under the whetstone, lay a piece of paper covered in the boy's careful, upright hand:

*I heard everything. Don't be stupid. One berth and three of us was never going to come out right, and I'm the one with the least paper and the most practice at disappearing. Go to the workshop ruins at low tide if you want to find where I put the spare charts in '44. You never listened when I told you. You will now.*

Beneath the signature, in smaller writing, almost shy:

*Tell him I got the rhymes right. All of them.*

Gideon read it three times. Then he swore — fluently, at length, in the manner Master Aldous had always called "the true apprentice's prayer" — and reached for his own coat.

* * *

The docks were chaos wearing uniforms. Press gangs worked the crowd with cheerful brutality, sweeping up any boy too young to argue and too small to run, and the evacuation clerks sat behind trestle tables stamping papers with the serene indifference of men who had never once been wet or hungry. Felix moved through all of it the way water moves through a fence — sideways, patiently, finding every gap. He had learned it young. Before Master Aldous, there had been four years of learning nothing else.

He meant to disappear properly. He meant to be gone before Gideon could follow. But his feet, traitors, took him the long way — out along the shingle, past the fish sheds, to the blackened skeleton of the workshop that stood on the point like a burnt tooth.

It had been three months since the fire. The sea had taken its tithe of the wreckage: roof beams furred with salt, drawers of instruments half-buried in sand, the great drafting table split down the middle as though grief had axed it. But the north wall still stood, and on the north wall, soot-dark but intact, was the thing Master Aldous had loved best in the world — the great chalked rose of winds, sixteen points, drawn for Felix's ninth birthday so the boy could learn the compass points standing in bed.

Felix touched the chalk lines. N, NNE, NE. *North-nor'-east, the puffin goes east,* sang a voice in his memory, warm and patient and smelling of pipe smoke. Every point had its verse. Every verse hid its numbers — bearings, depths, soundings, the whole secret coastline of the master's life's work, poured into nursery songs because the Admiralty burned maps but nobody had ever yet burned a song out of a child.

"I can redraw all of it," Felix said aloud, to the wall, to the wind, to nobody. "Every chart. I just never knew that's what I was carrying."

"That," said Gideon's voice behind him, ragged with running, "is the single most infuriating thing you have ever said."

Gideon's POV

They stood in the ruin of the workshop while the tide turned, and Gideon let the boy talk — about the songs, about the verses he'd thought were nonsense, about the night he'd realized the counting rhymes matched the soundings in the atlas sheet by sheet. And with every word, Gideon felt the choice he'd been strangling on for three days quietly untie itself.

The atlas could buy Master Aldous's freedom. But Felix — dirty, undersized, paperless Felix — was worth more than the atlas. Was the atlas. Was everything Aldous Felix had actually spent his life making, disguised as a boy who hummed while he worked.

"You understand what you're asking me to do," Gideon said finally. "I hand Corvane the atlas. All of it. And I bet everything — his freedom, our lives, the whole work — that you're right. That it's all in your head."

"It's not in my head," Felix said. "It's better than that. It's in my *sleeping*. I dream in bearings now. It's very annoying."

Gideon laughed — a cracked, unexpected sound that startled gulls off the beams. Then he held out his hand, and Felix, after a suspicious pause, shook it, solemn as a signing witness.

They found Corvane at the quay, already arguing with the purser. Gideon set the sea chest down between them.

"Forty-three sheets. Complete. Unmarked. Worth whatever your father's freedom costs and then some." He looked the courier dead in the eye. "You take the atlas and the last berth. The boy sails with me on the *Merrow* without papers, because you are going to stand at that gangway and tell the clerk, loudly and boringly, that the ship cannot leave until a manifest error is corrected — and clerks fear nothing so much as a well-dressed man being loud and boring."

Corvane studied him for a long moment. Something moved behind his face — not gratitude; something older and sadder.

"My father told me about you," he said. "'The stubborn one who loves the work more than the reward.'" He picked up the chest. "He was wrong, incidentally. About which of us was sent for whom. But that conversation keeps." He paused at the gangway, glancing back once. "There's a letter in the chest lid, Thackeray. Under the false bottom. From my father. I suggest you read it before you decide what you're rescuing — and from whom."

Then he was gone up the plank, atlas and all, and the bell of the *Merrow* began to ring for departure.

Under the false bottom, folded twice, Gideon found a page in the unmistakable hand — living, wry, unhurried. He read it standing on the quay with Felix gripping his sleeve, and the color left his face slowly, like a tide going out.

"Felix," he said. "What exactly do you remember... about your mother?"
