## 4. What the Sea Keeps

*Gideon*

The packet ship *Merrow* cleared the harbor mouth at dawn, and Gideon Thackeray stood at her stern rail watching the town dissolve into grey — the crooked chimneys first, then the church tower, then the low green shoulder of the headland where he had once buried a copper plate of the northern coast and told no one. Three months of hiding had taught him the geography of fear. It was strange to feel it lift all at once, like a coat taken off his shoulders by a stranger.

Felix was not watching the land. The boy was watching Corvane.

The man stood apart at the bow, soaked cloak finally dried to something resembling a cloak, turning a letter over and over in his hands. He had not explained why he'd accepted the atlas with such ceremony only to hand it back into the Admiralty's own custody at the quay — or why, when Gideon had come running down the pier with the sea chest and the boy and no papers at all, Corvane had simply looked at Felix for a long moment and said, "Aboard. Quickly," as if he'd been expected all along.

"Ask him," Felix whispered, appearing at Gideon's elbow. He had salt in his hair and the particular brightness of a boy who has run away and been forgiven and intends to enjoy it.

"I've been standing beside him for an hour," Gideon muttered. "One doesn't simply *ask* a man why he's been turning a letter into origami since sunrise."

"You're a coward."

"I'm a careful man. There's a difference, though admittedly it's narrow."

Corvane solved it himself. He crossed the deck with the rolling gait of someone who had spent more time on water than land, and held out the letter. "You should read this," he said. "I've read it forty times and I still don't understand it, which suggests it wants another reader."

The hand was unmistakable. Aldous Felix's writing — the great looping capitals, the ink blots where the master's pen always dug in when he was excited, the little marginal sketches of gulls that appeared in every letter he had ever sent, even bills of lading. Gideon read it once at the rail, then again with his back against the mast because his legs had opinions.

*My son,* it began.

"My son," Gideon said aloud, stupidly.

"Yes," said Corvane. "He never mentioned me?"

"He mentioned a wife who died in the Indies. He mentioned a fever. He mentioned—" Gideon stopped. "He never mentioned you."

"He wouldn't." Corvane smiled without much joy in it. "I chose the sea over the charts when I was fifteen, and he chose the charts over everything, always. We were both stubborn about our choices for twenty years." He nodded at the letter. "Read what he asks."

Gideon read aloud, quietly, the wind taking some of it: *"You will find Thackeray hiding like a fox and working like ten men, because that is how he is made. Give him whatever story gets the atlas aboard — he would never surrender it for my sake, but he will surrender anything for the boy's, which is precisely the arithmetic I am counting on. Do not tell him until you are at sea, or he will ruin everything by being noble."*

Gideon lowered the letter. "He used me."

"He trusted you," Corvane said. "In my experience those look identical from the outside."

"And the prison? The ransom?"

"A fiction. He was freed within a fortnight of the burning — the Admiralty got their atlas, or enough of one; they lost interest in the cartographer the moment they had the map. He could have walked out through the front gate any day these three months." Corvane looked toward the shrinking coast. "He stayed in the debtor's wing because it was the one place they weren't looking, and because leaving meant deciding. Whether to send for the boy. Whether to start again at sixty-eight years old with nothing but his hands. So he wrote to me instead and asked me to do the deciding for him."

"To rescue the atlas," Gideon said slowly, understanding arriving like tide.

"No." Corvane shook his head. "To rescue *you*, Thackeray. Read the last line."

Gideon read it. He read it four times before he could trust his voice.

*"The atlas was never the treasure. The atlas was the bait I used to catch an apprentice who loved maps more than his own safety, and a boy who learned every chart I ever drew because he thought I didn't notice. Whatever you save of them, save that. The paper can be burned a hundred times. What they carry between them cannot."*

*Felix*

Felix found Gideon an hour later, still holding the letter, looking like a man who had been shown the inside of his own chest and found rooms he hadn't known were there.

"You knew," Felix said. "About the songs. You knew what they were."

Gideon blinked at him. "I knew you sang nonsense. Beautiful nonsense, but nonsense. 'When the Old Bear dips his ladle, drink your milk and mind the candle'—"

"'And north by north-west lies the honest harbor,'" Felix finished. "That's not nonsense, Gid. That's latitude. Forty-nine degrees and some minutes, the way the Master used to sing it to me when I was five and couldn't hold a pencil. Every rhyme I know is a coastline. Every song is a sky." He said it carefully, watching Gideon's face, because he had rehearsed this confession in the master's ruined workshop while smoke still hung in the rafters, expecting anger, or worse, indifference.

Gideon sat down very suddenly on a coil of rope and laughed — a cracked, wet laugh that turned halfway into something else entirely.

"Three months," he said. "Three months I worked myself half blind finishing the Atlas Felix, page by page, terrified the Admiralty would burn it again, terrified I'd get it wrong, terrified I was the last person alive who carried those coastlines. And the whole time—" he gestured helplessly at Felix "—the whole time the true atlas was singing rounds in my kitchen about minding the candle."

"The paper version was better drawn."

"The paper version," Gideon said solemnly, "was better drawn. But yours doesn't burn."

Felix felt something settle in him then, some knot he had been carrying since the night he'd overheard Corvane say *one berth* and understood exactly what he was worth measured against a wooden box of maps. He had offered to stay. He had meant it. And now here was Gideon, laughing on a rope coil because Felix's nursery rhymes were worth more than the masterwork of the age.

"Will you teach me?" Felix asked. "Properly. Surveying, drafting, the mathematics. You're the only apprentice he ever finished training."

"I'm the only apprentice he ever *had* who survived having me," Gideon said. "But yes. Though I warn you, my method involves a great deal of shouting and erasing."

"That sounds exactly like his method."

"It should. I stole it from him, along with his pens and, apparently, his entire philosophy of love." Gideon rubbed his eyes. "He let us go, Felix. He arranged the whole thing — the letters, the berth, the timing with the evacuation — and he never once asked us to stay. That's the part I keep snagging on. It would have cost him nothing to ask."

"It would have cost us everything to answer," Felix said quietly. He had thought about this a great deal on the workshop floor, staring at the burnt beams. "He knew that. That's why he didn't ask. It's the kindest thing anyone's ever done to me and it's terrible."

"Yes," said Gideon. "That's fathers for you."

They looked at each other, both hearing it, neither quite brave enough to examine it further. Above them the sails thundered and filled; below them the Atlantic drew its long grey breath.

"Tonight," Felix said, scrambling up. "Come up tonight after the light goes. I'll start teaching you the star songs properly. There are eleven, and the hardest one has a bit where you have to whistle."

"There will be no whistling."

"There will absolutely be whistling. It's the constellation Cassiopeia, Gid, she demands it."

That evening, when the coast had gone dark behind them and the first stars came out hard and cold over the water, Felix stood at the bow with Gideon beside him and began, softly, with the easiest song — the one about the Old Bear and the milk. His voice wobbled on the second verse, the way it always did, because the Master had sung it to him first and some songs keep the shape of the voice that made them.

Gideon joined in on the third verse, badly, and kept going anyway.

Behind them, Corvane leaned against the rail and listened, and did not sing, and did not need to. Ahead of them lay open water, a new country, and no chart at all — which, Felix thought, taking a breath for the whistling part, was only fitting.

Every good map, the Master always said, begins as somebody's memory of home.

And somewhere behind them, in a debtors' cell he could have walked out of any day, an old man was presumably sketching gulls in the margins of a farewell letter, keeping them — the apprentice and the boy, the atlas made of rhymes — safe in the only library that could never burn.
