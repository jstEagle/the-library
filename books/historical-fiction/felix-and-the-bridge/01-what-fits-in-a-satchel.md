## 1. What Fits in a Satchel

The whole of my life, I have decided, must fit in a satchel.

This is not a philosophy I chose so much as one that chose me, the way weather chooses a man standing in an open field. My father kept a shop in the river district — leather goods, belts, valises, the small honest commerce of hides — and when the war took the shop, it took the building and the stock and the ledger with the customers' names written in his careful upright hand. What it did not take, because I happened to be carrying it, was a satchel. So I learned early that a life divides neatly into two columns: what fits, and what doesn't. Everything I have loved that was lost belonged to the second column. Everything I have kept belongs to the first.

The satchel itself is brown calfskin, my father's own work, with a brass buckle gone green at the edges and a strap I have mended twice with waxed thread. Inside it, on any given morning: a spare fan belt (the car is a 1911 Berliet with opinions), a tin of engine oil, a pencil stub worn to the length of a thumb, a notebook, and whatever letters Mrs. Vance has entrusted to me that day. It weighs, fully loaded, about as much as a sleeping cat. I have weighed it. I weigh everything. A man who measures his life in portables becomes compulsive about the arithmetic.

You will want to know about the letters.

Mrs. Vance is a suffragist — a term she pronounces the way other people pronounce *general* or *doctor*, with the full weight of the office behind it. She is sixty-one years old, built like a church pew, and she has spent thirty years arguing that women should vote, a proposition that has earned her heckling in four provinces and a certain grim celebrity everywhere else. She cannot drive. She writes letters the way other people breathe — constantly, and with more force than strictly necessary — and since the railways went to the army, someone must carry her correspondence to the towns the trains no longer reach. That someone is me. I drive the Berliet; I carry the satchel; I deliver the letters.

And I read them.

I want to be honest about this from the start, because everything that follows depends on it, and because a man who lies in the first chapter will lie in all the others. I read the letters. All of them. Not out of malice, and not entirely out of curiosity — though I will not pretend curiosity had nothing to do with it, at the beginning. I read them the way a customs officer inspects cargo: because it is there, because it crosses my border, because a man alone in a car for six hours with a satchel full of sealed envelopes will eventually notice that sealed envelopes are not actually sealed against a man with a steaming kettle and a patient hand.

The first time, I told myself it was an accident. The kettle was on in a hotel room in Maraval, the letter lay open on the table where the flap had come unstuck in the damp, and my eyes, which have always been faster than my conscience, read four lines before I could stop them. I sealed it again with the kettle. I delivered it. And then I discovered the thing that I have never been able to explain properly to anyone, because there has never been anyone to explain it to: I remembered it. Word for word. All four lines. A week later I could still recite the sentence where a woman named Céline told her sister that the apple trees had bloomed late but heavy, and that she had put up eleven jars, and that she was not lonely, she wanted that understood, she was not lonely.

Eleven jars. Late bloom, heavy crop. Not lonely.

I have carried that sentence for two years now, and it has cost me nothing and given me nothing, and I cannot make myself put it down. That is the trouble with memory. It is the one cargo you cannot declare at the border and cannot leave behind at the checkpoint. It fits in the satchel whether you want it to or not.

After Maraval, I stopped pretending the reading was accidental. I developed a method, because I am a methodical man — my father used to say I alphabetized my socks, which was unkind and also true. The kettle trick works on gummed flaps; a thin blade works on the wax-sealed ones, slid along the seam while the wax is warmed over the engine manifold, which sounds delicate but is really just patience. I read in the car, in the grey hour before dawn, or in hotel rooms with the door bolted. I read standing up, which I find keeps me honest — a man will linger over a letter sitting down, the way he lingers over wine, but standing he reads the way he eats at a counter: quickly, and slightly ashamed.

And I began to keep a record.

Not of the letters themselves — I burned the first transcriptions when I understood what a hanging offense they'd constitute — but of the things in them that the letters' own recipients would never know. A widow in one town who did not yet know she was a widow, because the army letter had gone to the wrong address and been returned. A man writing to three sisters, telling each she was his favorite. A mother asking after a son who, per an earlier letter in the same satchel, had been listed missing at a place called Verzy. I wrote the names down in a notebook — not the notebook I carry now, an earlier one, a cheap thing with a marbled cover — and I told myself I was doing it for efficiency. So that if a letter came through for the missing son, I would know to deliver it with care. So that if the widow's corrected address surfaced, I could route it properly.

This was, I admit, a lie I told myself with a straight face, which is the hardest kind of lie to keep straight.

The truth is that the notebook was for me. A man alone — and I am alone, I have been alone since the shop went and my father with it and my sweetheart Odile with a lieutenant from the artillery — a man alone needs some evidence that he exists to someone. The letters were evidence. Every name in that notebook was a person who had, without knowing it, let me into the room where they grieved or hoped or lied to their sisters about being their favorite, and I had stood quietly in the corner and witnessed it. It is not friendship. I know that. But it is a shape that fits where friendship would go, the way a spare key fits a lock it wasn't cut for, and on the bad nights — and there were bad nights, in the winter mud, in the hotels where the only other guest was a rat with ambitions — I would take out the notebook and read a few names and feel something adjacent to company.

Paper, you see, does not leave you. Paper does not get conscripted, or transferred to the eastern front, or decide that a chauffeur is a chauffeur and not a person. Paper says what it said, every time you ask it. I trust paper the way other men trust their mothers, and I am aware this says something about me that is not flattering, and I have decided to be at peace with it.

* * *

The morning this story properly begins — though of course it began earlier, everything begins earlier — I was under the car.

It was the second week of November, in the last winter of the war, though no one had yet said *last* out loud because saying it out loud is how you invite the opposite. The Berliet had developed a rattle in the rear axle that sounded like a man shaking a tin of buttons, and Mrs. Vance had a circuit to run: three towns upriver in four days, meetings in each, letters in every direction. So there I was at half past six in the livery yard, flat on the creeper, in the dark under the chassis, with the axle in my hands and my breath making small clouds against the differential, when the first frost of the season came down on the yard like a decision.

I mention the frost because of what happened to the satchel. I had left it on the running board, and the damp got into the brass buckle, and for the rest of that day the buckle stuck, and I had to work it with my thumbnail every time I opened the bag, and I mention this because a satchel is not a symbol, whatever a novelist might tell you. It is a leather bag with a sticky buckle, and the axle is an axle, and the war is a war, and most of life is small cold maintenance performed by men nobody will remember. I find this comforting. Your mileage may vary.

Mrs. Vance came out at seven with her collar up and her breath steaming and stood over the car with the expression of a woman inspecting a subordinate regiment.

"Almeida. Will it go?"

"It will go, Mrs. Vance."

"It had better. The Saint-Aubin meeting is at two, and if I am late, Madame Perrin will open the floor to questions, and the last woman who did that let a dairy farmer talk for forty minutes about how voting would ruin the milk."

"Would it?"

"Don't be clever, Almeida, it's too cold." But I heard the smile in it, because Mrs. Vance's smiles live in her voice; her face is reserved for more serious deployments. She was, I should say, the closest thing I had to an employer and the farthest thing I had from a friend. She paid me eleven francs a day and my lodging, she had never once asked whether I had people, and when I had been ill with the fever in the spring she had sent the hotel doctor and a note reading *Recover. The schedule is unforgiving.* I have thought about that note a great deal. It was, I believe, the warmest sentence she had available to her, and I have decided to take it as what it was.

I rolled out from under the car, wiped my hands on the rag I keep for the purpose, and loaded the satchel. Fourteen letters that morning, by my count. I counted everything, then as now. Fourteen envelopes, and I knew before noon I would know all fourteen by heart, and there was a part of me — the part I like least, the part that keeps the notebook — that was already looking forward to it.

We made Saint-Aubin by half past one, which was a small miracle involving a ford, a detour through a beet field, and a farmer named Guesse who charged me two francs to pull us out of the beet field with his mule and then apologized for the price, which is the most honest transaction I was part of that whole winter.

* * *

Here is what I have learned about towns in wartime, having driven through perhaps forty of them: the war is visible in a town the way age is visible in a face, not in any one feature but in the whole set of it. Saint-Aubin had the set. The café was open but half its chairs were stacked and the other half held men too old or too young, and the men drank their coffee looking at the table, and the town's one flag hung from the mairie at an angle nobody had corrected, because correcting a flag's angle requires a belief that angles matter.

Mrs. Vance spoke for an hour in the mairie hall to perhaps sixty women and four men, one of whom was the dairy farmer, and I stood at the back with my cap in my hands and listened, because I always listened. She was good. I say that as a man who has heard a thousand speeches in hotel lobbies and station platforms and once, memorably, from a man standing on the roof of a tram. She did not raise her voice. She built her sentences like bridges — load-bearing, no ornament — and she walked the audience across them one plank at a time until they stood on the far side and were surprised to find they had gone anywhere at all.

"Half this country's work is done by women," she said, "and none of its decisions. You bake the bread and bury the dead, and when the men who made this war sit down to make the peace, there will not be one chair at that table for the women who held the country upright while they were busy breaking it. I am not asking you to be angry. I am asking you to be *counted*. Anger is free. A count is a number, and numbers are the only language governments have ever learned."

The dairy farmer asked his question about milk. Mrs. Vance dismantled him in four sentences, gently, the way you take apart a clock you intend to return to its owner in working order. The women laughed. It was a good afternoon.

The letters came afterward, as they always did. Women pressed them on her at the door — replies to her circulars, appeals, a petition with forty signatures — and she handed them to me without looking, the way you hand a coat to a man whose job is coats, and I stowed them in the satchel with the sticky buckle and told myself, as I always told myself, that I would deliver them unread.

I did not deliver them unread. You know this already. But that evening, in the Hôtel du Commerce in Saint-Aubin, with the kettle on and the frost coming down again outside the window like a second, colder army, I opened the satchel and worked the buckle with my thumbnail and took out the letters, and one of them was different.

Most of Mrs. Vance's mail was movement business — dues, dates, resolutions, the plumbing of any organization. This one was a woman's hand, slanted, hurried, on paper that had been written on and then written over, as though the writer had run short and could not bear to waste. The address was not the movement's post box. It was a private name, a widow's name, in a town two days' drive south of us. And the letter inside was not about votes.

It was from a woman whose husband had died at the front, and it was addressed to another woman whose son was posted to the same sector, and the whole of it was a single long sentence of warning, essentially: *I know you will be told he is well, because I was told mine was well, and mine was not well, and the telling is the cruelest part, so I am writing to say that whatever they tell you, you write to the chaplain directly, and you write twice, because the first letter goes into a drawer somewhere and a drawer is where hope goes to be misfiled.*

I read it twice. Then I sat with it on my knees in the hotel room while the kettle ticked as it cooled, and I did not copy it into the notebook, because there was nothing in it to record — no name for my ledger, no fact, nothing a customs officer could stamp. There was only a woman telling another woman the truth, at cost, with no way of knowing if it would arrive or help.

And I thought: this letter is doing more good than I have ever done, and it weighs less than my pencil stub, and it will fit in the satchel, and I will carry it, and that will be the entire extent of my participation in the human race this week.

I sealed it with the kettle. I put it back in the satchel. And I lay on the hotel bed with my boots off and my coat on, because the room's stove had opinions of its own, and I looked at the ceiling and made my usual inventory: one satchel, one notebook, one car, one employer, one hundred and forty-odd names in memory, zero people who knew any of this about me.

The arithmetic of a portable life. It balances, but only just, and only because I fudge the entries.

In the morning we would drive south, toward the river towns, toward the widow's address and the others. There was frost on the running board when I went down to check the axle, and the buckle was stuck again, and I stood in the yard working it with my thumbnail and thinking about drawers, and where hope goes to be misfiled, and whether a man could build a life out of other people's letters or whether that was just a way of going through the world unclaimed.

Mrs. Vance came out at seven with her collar up. She had a new sheaf of correspondence in her hand — the hotel had forwarded mail overnight — and she held it out to me across the frost, and there was something in the way she held it, a half-second of hesitation, that I noticed then and did not understand until much later.

"Two of these are official," she said. "Army post. They came addressed to me, which is unusual."

I took them. The top one had the field-grey border of military correspondence, and a stamp I did not recognize, and a weight in the hand heavier than paper should have — two pages at least, maybe three.

"Anything for Saint-Aubin?" she asked.

"No, Mrs. Vance," I said, which was true, and put them in the satchel, which was the beginning of everything else.
