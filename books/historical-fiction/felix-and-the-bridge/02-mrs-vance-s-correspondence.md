## 2. Mrs. Vance's Correspondence

The letters Mrs. Vance dictated were, as a rule, better armed than most of the soldiers I had seen that winter.

I say this with respect. I drove her through the river districts twice a week and sometimes more, and while the wheels sank and the mud did its patient work on the axles, she would unfold herself from the passenger seat — a woman of sixty-odd years built along the lines of a church spire — and begin to compose aloud, in the manner of artillery officers preparing a barrage.

"Dear Madame Lefevre," she would say, and I would watch the road and listen. "I have received your report, and I must tell you that your committee's cowardice is of such a high order that it can only be described as *architectural*. They have built their timidity to last. It will stand for generations. Future scholars will visit it."

"Shall I write 'architectural,' Mrs. Vance?" asked Colette, her secretary, when Colette was with us. When she was not, it was my job, which meant that the suffrage movement's correspondence passed through the hands of a chauffeur who could barely spell "committee."

"Italicized," said Mrs. Vance.

The car was a 1911 Delaunay, black, temperamental, named Berthe by Mrs. Vance's late husband against everyone's better judgment. She had a habit of stalling in wet weather and an opinion about it, which was that she would start when she felt appreciated. I talked to her. This is not something I would admit under oath. But there were mornings on the Mill Road where the choice was between talking to Berthe and walking, and I chose flattery.

That Tuesday we were bound for Maraic, twelve kilometers north, where a circuit meeting had been arranged in the back room of a draper's shop. The war had made everything both harder and easier for Mrs. Vance: harder, because petrol was rationed and men in uniform looked at a motorcar full of paper the way dogs look at a butcher's window; easier, because half the constables who might once have arrested her had been sent to the front, and the ones remaining were mostly too tired to arrest anybody for anything except stealing chickens.

We came to the first checkpoint outside Vessy. It was not much of a checkpoint — a sawhorse, two soldiers, a brazier that gave off more smoke than heat. The younger one waved us down with the weary authority of a man who had been standing there since dawn and intended to be annoyed about it until relieved.

"Papers."

I handed over the car documents, my driving permit, Mrs. Vance's travel authorization. He read them the way children read — moving his lips.

"Suffrage," he said, as though the word tasted like a coin he'd found on the ground.

"Circuit meeting," said Mrs. Vance from the seat beside me, without opening her eyes. She had a gift for making a closed pair of eyes more intimidating than an open stare. "You're welcome to attend, corporal. We'll be discussing whether your mother is a citizen."

The corporal blinked. His colleague, older, with a face like a boiled ham, snorted into his scarf and then pretended he hadn't.

"Go on, then," said the younger one, and we went.

"That boy," Mrs. Vance observed, after a decent interval, "will one day be somebody's clerk, and God help the filing system." Then, without any transition at all: "*Dear Madame Perrin* — and mark this well, Hazel, because I shall want it quoted back to me at the meeting — *we are told again that the hour is not ripe. I have been hearing about the ripeness of the hour for thirty years, and I observe that the hour has done nothing whatsoever to ripen itself, and so we must do our orchards' work for them.*"

"*Do our orchards' work for them,*" I repeated, taking the hill in second gear.

"Good. And then — *if they will not hand us the fruit, madame, we shall bring ladders.*"

"Ladders," said Colette's notebook, in my handwriting, misspelled. I spelled it *ladderes*, realized, and crossed it out. Mrs. Vance did not correct me. She had decided long ago, with the strategic patience she applied to everything, that correcting my spelling would take time she needed for correcting the government.

* * *

I should explain what I did with the post, because it matters, though I did not know yet how much.

Mrs. Vance received a great deal of mail. Some of it was ordinary — circulars, dues, angry letters from bishops. Some of it was circuit business, coded lightly, the codes being of the sort designed less to fool an intelligence service than to give the ladies involved a feeling of conspiratorial satisfaction. ("*Our mutual friend's garden requires seeds*" meant, as far as I ever determined, that a particular train station was being watched.)

And some of it was other people's.

This happened more often than anyone remarked upon. The postal service was stretched thin; the army's field post was worse; addresses got smudged or guessed at. Letters arrived at Mrs. Vance's house that had no earthly reason to be there, and because she was a known name in the district — because her door was the door the confused postman remembered — strays collected on her hall table like leaves against a fence.

It fell to me, on driving days, to sort them. Official, circuit, personal, stray. That was the order of the satchel's compartments, and mine, and eventually — though I did not see it then — of my whole life.

I read the strays. I want to be honest about this, since honesty is the only currency I have ever had much of. I read them because they were there, and because paper has never once lied to me, and because a letter tells you what a town will not. I told myself it was diligence. A driver who knows what he carries is a driver who can protect it. There was even truth in that. But the truth underneath was simpler and less flattering: I was lonely, and other people's words were company, and I took them the way a cold man takes someone else's fire.

The military envelopes I left sealed. That was policy — mine, unspoken, absolute. Field-grey border, official stamp: those went in the satchel untouched, delivered to Mrs. Vance's desk, dealt with by her. Whatever was in them, it was above my weight, and I had a firm conviction — then — that a man survives by knowing which doors are his.

* * *

The road to Maraic followed the river, which was in its winter mood, brown and muscular, shouldering past the alders. The old bridge stood at the halfway point — stone, three arches, older than anyone could remember being new. We crossed it as we always did. Two soldiers at the far end now, where before there had been none, but soldiers were becoming weather; you noted them and drove on.

"Steady," murmured Mrs. Vance, as the arch jolted under us, and whether she spoke to me or to Berthe I never learned.

At Maraic the meeting ran long. I sat in the draper's back room near the stove, in a chair that had been designed by someone who hated people, and listened to Mrs. Vance work a roomful of shopkeepers' wives and schoolmistresses the way a bell-ringer works bells. She began quiet and got louder only when they leaned in. She used the orchard line. It landed. A woman in a green shawl cried a little and pretended it was coughing.

Afterward there was tea I would not describe as tea to anyone I respected, and the replies to dictate, and by four o'clock the light was going the color of dishwater and Mrs. Vance said, "Home, Hazel, before the frost takes the road."

We were ten minutes out of Maraic, dusk gathering in the ditches, when Berthe began to misbehave — a stutter in the engine, a smell I didn't like. I pulled up under a poplar and put my head under the bonnet, which was cold work, and Mrs. Vance, who considered mechanical trouble an insult offered personally to her family, got out to stand over me and offer advice.

It was while I was elbow-deep in Berthe's opinions that I heard the sound.

A bicycle. Thin tires on frozen mud, coming fast behind us. A dispatch rider, field-grey, hunched over his handlebars. He drew level, slowed — why he slowed I understood later, and the understanding sat in me like swallowed ice — and held out a bundle of envelopes wrapped in a rubber band.

"For the post collection at Vessy's gone forward," he said. "These are district. You're the motorcar that runs the river route? Vance?"

"I am Mrs. Vance," said Mrs. Vance, in the voice she reserved for officials and bishops.

"Then these are yours to carry. Sign here." He produced a board, a pencil, a form. She signed. He pedaled off into the dark without another word, and that was all, and at the time it seemed like nothing — a bored soldier, a shortcut, the war delegating its errands wherever convenient.

There were six envelopes. Five had the field-grey border.

The sixth did not.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I have replayed it enough times to have opinions about it.

I put the five military envelopes in the official compartment, sealed. I put the sixth — a plain envelope, civilian, addressed in a careful looping hand to *Madame Odile Ferrand, Rue des Tilleuls, Saint-Aubin* — in the stray compartment, meaning to sort it properly at home.

Saint-Aubin was our own town. Rue des Tilleuls was four streets from Mrs. Vance's gate. And the letter had come into the army's hands somehow, been bundled with its correspondence, and been handed to us to deliver — which meant that somewhere between the sender and the soldier with the bicycle, the postal system had failed it, and it had fallen to me.

I tell myself I checked the seal only to confirm it was broken. It was. The flap had lifted in the damp; the gum had given up weeks ago, perhaps. Any reasonable person would have pressed it flat and thought no more of it.

I am not claiming to have been reasonable.

I read it in the light of the side lamp while Mrs. Vance consulted the sky about frost. It was short. Civilians' letters usually are, when they have waited a long time to arrive.

It was from a corporal named Étienne Ferrand, writing from a depot I will not name, and it said, in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*Mother — They have moved us again. I cannot say where. Do not believe everything you are sent, and do not send the grey socks, the grey socks arrive full of holes, send the brown. I am well. I am well, I promise you, I am well. Your son, Étienne.*

*I am well. Three times.*

I have carried a great many sentences since, and I have developed a theory about them: that a person repeats only what he needs to hear himself say. Nobody writes *I am well* once and means it easily. Once is information. Three times is prayer.

He was telling his mother he was alive because he was not certain it would still be true by the time the letter reached her, and he wanted the words already in the house, waiting, like a lamp left on.

I pressed the flap down. I put the envelope in the compartment. I closed the bonnet and told Mrs. Vance it was the carburetor, and that Berthe would behave if we were kind to her, and we drove home in the dark, and I did not say one word about any of it.

* * *

Why did I say nothing? Because it was her mail to sort and not mine — no. Because privacy — no. I have had two years to construct explanations and they all fall down.

I said nothing because delivering the letter was easy and speaking of it was not. Because to say *this letter passed through the army's hands* was to open a door onto a corridor whose length I did not want to measure. Because the widow — if she was a widow; the word *mother* proved nothing either way — because Madame Ferrand would read *I am well* and choose, as mothers do, to believe it, and my telling her about the three repetitions would not add one gram of truth to her house. It would only move the weight from my pocket to hers.

That is what I told myself, that night, winding my watch by candlelight: that knowledge kept is kindness.

I have since had occasion to test that principle rather severely, and I will spare you the suspense. It is not kindness. It is the first payment on a debt, and the debt compounds.

* * *

I delivered the letter the next morning. This was outside my duties and I did it anyway, walked the four streets with the envelope in my coat like contraband, and knocked at the blue door on the Rue des Tilleuls.

Madame Ferrand was small and quick, with flour on her wrists. She took the envelope, turned it over, saw the handwriting, and made a sound I will not attempt to reproduce — half breath, half something else — and then, because I was still standing on her step, she remembered to be embarrassed.

"You'll take a coffee?" she said. "It's chicory, but it's hot."

I said I had a car to tend, which was true, and she said, "Bless him, my Étienne, he never could write short," and closed the door, and I stood on her step for a moment longer than a man ought to stand anywhere, listening to her footsteps go quickly away and then stop, presumably at the kitchen table, presumably to read it again.

*I am well. I am well, I promise you, I am well.*

Three times.

I walked back to the house and got out the brush and bucket, and I washed Berthe from bonnet to running-board in weather cold enough to make the water complain, because it is difficult to think while your hands are freezing, and there were things I did not want to think and therefore required an occupation.

Mrs. Vance watched me from the study window for a while. I felt it the way you feel weather changing. She did not comment. She was assembling that week's correspondence into the satchel compartments, and I noticed — because noticing papers was becoming my trade without my consent — that the field-grey envelopes had multiplied. Eight now, where the month before there had been two. The army was writing to her more and more, and whatever it wanted, it wanted it in triplicate.

"Hazel," she called, as I wrung out the chamois. "Thursday, we run the northern circuit. Wear the collar that doesn't wilt. There's a new checkpoint at the quarry road and the sergeant there believes in collars."

"Thursday," I said.

"And Hazel." She paused, envelope in hand, and looked at me across the yard with those pale eyes that took inventories. "You've been quiet since Maraic."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quietly quiet, normally. This is a different vintage." She let it hang there a moment — she had a bellringer's instinct for when to leave a note ringing — then went back to her sorting. "Well. So long as it doesn't interfere with your spelling."

I finished the car. I ate supper. I wound the watch again, though it did not need it.

And then I knelt down in my little room at the back of the house, and put my fingernails to the floorboard under the cot, and lifted the board I had loosened months ago for no reason I could name at the time — a man prepares hiding places the way other men prepare soup, without examining why — and I looked at what was there.

A cheap notebook, quarter-bound, bought for laundry accounts and never used for them. Opened, it showed a single line of my handwriting, dated fourteen months back, the first thing I had ever thought to keep:

*Tues., 11th. Postman Daligre taken by patrol, Rue Basse, no charge given. Returned Friday. Will not speak of it.*

One line. One name. I had written it the way you write anything true — quickly, before you could talk yourself out of it — and hidden it the way you hide anything dangerous, and then for fourteen months I had added to it, sparingly, guiltily, a name here, a date there, the taken and the unsent and the unexplained, until the notebook had become a kind of second town, a town with no buildings in it, only its people, and only the things that had happened to them.

I sat back on my heels with the notebook on my knees, and I thought about a boy who wrote *I am well* three times, and a mother with flour on her wrists, and eight field-grey envelopes in a satchel downstairs, multiplying.

And I took out my pencil, and beneath the postman's line I wrote the next entry, the one I had been carrying for two days without knowing it was an entry at all:

*Wed., 12th. Letter of Cpl. Étienne Ferrand routed through army post, delivered by hand, Rue des Tilleuls. Writes "I am well" three times.*

I looked at that sentence for a long while. It was not evidence of anything. It proved nothing, accused no one. It was only a fact that had nowhere else in the world to live.

That, I understood — kneeling on the cold floor with the board in my hand — was the whole point. Somewhere upstairs in this war there were rooms full of ledgers deciding what had happened and what had not, whose son was well and whose was a rounding error. Against all of that I had one floorboard and a pencil.

I put the notebook back. I pressed the board down flat until it clicked, and lay awake a long time listening to the frost form on the windows, and thinking — though I did not yet know the shape of it — that Thursday's drive north would take us past the quarry road, and past the bridge, and that the army was writing to Miriam Vance more and more these days, in triplicate, and that sooner or later one of those field-grey envelopes would not stay sealed.
