## 3. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

My room at the back of Madame Fisk's boarding house was seven feet by nine, and I had measured it twice, because a man who measures his life by portability ought to know the dimensions of everything he owns.

The room contained: one bed, one chair, one washstand with a basin chipped at the rim, one window overlooking the yard where Madame Fisk's hens conducted their endless parliament, and one floorboard that did not match its neighbors. The mismatched board sat under the bed, third from the wall, and if you knelt — which I did every night and most mornings, the way other men kneel to pray — and fitted your fingernails into the seam, it came up with a sound like a sigh.

Underneath was my ledger.

I want to be precise about what this book was, because precision is the whole point of it. It was not a diary. Diaries are for feelings, and I had made a private arrangement with myself years ago to keep my feelings in a smaller container, somewhere near the liver, where nobody could requisition them. It was not an account book either, though I kept accounts in it. It was a register. Names, dates, descriptions, last known circumstances. A census of everyone the war had taken from our town without bothering to leave a receipt.

I bought it in the spring, from Osei the stationer, who asked no questions because Osei was a man who understood that certain paper purchases are like certain midnight walks — best conducted without commentary. He wrapped it in brown paper as though it were a hat, and said, "Good binding, Mr. Almeida. That'll hold ink for fifty years."

"Fifty years," I said, "is more than most of us need."

"Speak for yourself," said Osei. "I intend to be insufferable until ninety."

That was the entire conversation, and it remains one of the most honest ones I have ever had.

* * *

The first entry went in on a Tuesday in April, and I can recite it the way some men recite scripture, which is to say imperfectly but with conviction.

A patrol had come through on market day and taken three men off the square outside the bakery — no warrants, no paperwork, just a sergeant with a list and the list was shorter than the number of men he pointed at, so that the arithmetic itself was a confession. One of them was Tomas Brand, the wheelwright, forty-one years old, a widower with a limp he'd earned falling off his own roof fixing tiles for a neighbor who couldn't afford him. I watched from across the street with Mrs. Vance's satchel on my shoulder, letters inside it addressed to strangers in cities I would never see, and I did nothing. There was nothing to do. A driver who intervenes becomes a passenger very quickly, and passengers in those circumstances travel badly.

But that night I lay in my seven-by-nine room listening to the hens argue about nothing, and I thought: somebody wrote down that Tomas Brand existed. Somebody wrote down when he was born, what his hands looked like, how much he charged for a cartwheel, whether he whistled while he worked (he did; tunelessly; it was terrible). And none of that was written anywhere anymore except in the heads of people who might forget, or die, or be persuaded otherwise. The army kept records, yes — but the army's records were written by the army, and an institution that takes men off squares does not write itself up honestly.

So I got out of bed, found a pencil stub, and started with what I knew:

*Tomas Brand, wheelwright. B. 1879 approx. Widower. Lame left leg (roof, 1911). Whistles badly. Taken from bakery square, 14 April, by patrol under Sgt. with red mustache (name unknown). Three others taken same time — see below.*

The "see below" was optimistic, since I didn't know their names yet. But optimism, I have found, is simply a form of homework you haven't done yet. By Friday I had all three names. By Sunday I had the sergeant's — Danner — extracted from the barber, who extracted it from a corporal who came in for a trim and talked the way lonely men talk to barbers, which is to say completely.

I wrote Sergeant Danner's name in the ledger too. Not as a victim. As a fact. My ledger did not judge; it only remembered. Judgment could be arranged later, by better-qualified parties.

* * *

Over the following months the book grew the way a river grows, invisibly and then all at once.

There were the men taken by patrols — eleven now, twelve counting a traveling tinker who happened to be in the wrong doorway at the wrong hour and whom nobody in town knew well enough to miss, which is precisely why somebody had to note him down. *Tinker, name unknown, age ~50, gray beard, mended pots, spoke with a northern accent. Taken 3 June, Mill Road. No one reported him missing. I am reporting him missing.* That line took me three attempts to write, because my hand kept shaking, and I resented the hand for it. Feelings in the liver, Almeida. Keep them there.

There were the letters never delivered. This was my own particular sin, and I recorded it with the scrupulousness of a man confessing in installments. Letters entrusted to me or passing through my hands that were confiscated at checkpoints, seized in "routine inspections," or simply vanished into the great hungry drawer of military administration. Each one I logged: sender, addressee, date, contents as far as I could read them through the envelope in the half-second of shadow before a soldier snatched it away. Some of those entries were short. Some were long enough to break a heart, if I'd allowed mine to attend, which I generally did not.

And there was Peter.

Peter Okonkwo, fourteen when I first met him, fifteen when they took him, and I want it understood that I argued. I stood in the requisition office with my cap in my hands and said, in my best chauffeur's voice, which is a voice made entirely of apologies, "Sir, the boy's papers say sixteen, but I know his mother, sir, and the boy himself will tell anyone who listens that he's fifteen, he's proud of being fifteen, sir, he thinks it's nearly grown" — and the clerk stamped the form anyway, because the quota needed filling and quotas do not read birth certificates.

His mother, Adaeze Okonkwo, sells fish by the fountain. When I told her — because someone had to, and it wasn't going to be the clerk — she did not weep. She stood very straight behind her buckets and said, "You write things down, Mr. Almeida. People think I don't notice things. I notice things." And she looked at me in a way that suggested she had noticed me noticing, and I went home and added Peter's entry with the fullest detail I possessed:

*Peter Okonkwo, b. 1902 (mother confirms; conscription papers falsified to 1899). Fish-seller's son. Can whistle tunefully (contrast: T. Brand). Left-handed. Afraid of deep water, which makes the irony unbearable. Conscripted 9 September, depot at Harrow Cross. Mother: Adaeze Okonkwo, Fountain Square.*

I did not write the part about the irony in the ledger. Irony is not evidence. But I am writing it here, in the place where I keep everything else, and a man may be permitted one unledgered observation per boy.

* * *

Now, a confession about method, since method is the closest thing I have to a religion.

Every night I took the book out from under the floorboard, read it through, and memorized the newest entries. Then I put it back. This was not sentiment; it was redundancy engineering, though I didn't have the phrase for it yet. Paper burns. Paper is confiscated. Paper falls into rivers. But a memory walked around freely wearing my face and nobody had ever once demanded to inspect it. If the book were lost, I could rebuild it — slower, worse, but rebuilt. The information would survive its container. I had become, without quite intending it, a backup copy of my town's conscience.

The ritual had its comedy, I'll admit. Kneeling every night beside the bed like a man at vespers, whispering names into the dark — *Brand, Okonkwo, the tinker, Danner* — I sometimes caught myself and thought: if Madame Fisk heard this through the floor, she'd assume I was praying, and she'd be more right than wrong. Once her cat got under there — don't ask me how; the cat had talents — and I spent a hideous quarter hour coaxing it out with scraps of dried fish, sweating like a forger, while the animal regarded me with the serene contempt cats reserve for men with secrets. Cats, in my experience, always know. It's why I distrust them and keep feeding them anyway.

Mrs. Vance, I should say, knew nothing. Or — no. Let me be honest, since honesty is tonight's project: Mrs. Vance knew *something*. She had that talent common to great organizers and great aunts, of noticing the shape of a thing without demanding to see its contents. She had remarked once, mildly, watching me copy an address onto a delivery slip, "You have a clerk's soul, Hazel. Everything in its column." I'd said thank you, unsure whether I'd been complimented or diagnosed. She hadn't raised the subject again. But she had also begun, that autumn, to leave certain newspapers on the seat of the car folded to particular pages, and I had begun to read them, and neither of us commented on the arrangement. Trust, between people who cannot afford trust, looks exactly like this: folded newspapers and silence.

* * *

Which brings me to the night the ledger changed character — the night it stopped being a record of what had been done and became a plan for what I would do about it.

It was late October. Rain had been coming down for days with the persistence of a bill collector, and I had spent the evening at the Grand reading letters over the stove — my legitimate work, reading aloud to Mrs. Vance whatever she couldn't face holding herself, which that week meant nearly everything. One letter had been from a woman in the capital describing bread lines. Another was circulars about buttons. The third I still carry somewhere behind the sternum: a mother asking after a son at the front, wanting to know if anyone had seen him, because the official notification used the word "missing" and the word "missing," she wrote, is a door left open, and she could not close it herself.

I drove home through the rain with the wipers slapping their slow useless rhythm, and I thought about doors left open.

In my room, I knelt. The board sighed. The lamp came down to a thumb-flame. And instead of adding an entry, I turned to the front of the book — past Brand, past the tinker, past Peter and his fear of deep water — and on the flyleaf, in my smallest hand, I wrote:

*If this book survives me, let it reach someone who prints things. Every name herein was real. Verify, publish, repeat. — H. Almeida*

Then I sat back on my heels and felt something I hadn't felt since the war began. Not safety — I want to be clear, I felt no safety whatsoever, and any man who feels safe while keeping secret records of an occupying army is either a fool or already dead. What I felt was *use*. Complicity, up to that night, had been my condition; purpose was now my occupation. The difference sounds small. It is the difference between being carried by a current and swimming, however slowly, toward a shore.

I read the whole ledger through twice, committed the new flyleaf to memory along with the rest, closed the book, doused the lamp, and slid it home under the floorboard. Third from the wall. The seam settled with its sigh.

Through the window, the hens had gone quiet for once, and the rain was easing, and down in the town the lamps of the occupied square burned in their orderly rows, and somewhere among those buildings was a municipal records office containing every birth and marriage and death our town had ever bothered to write down, kept by clerks who believed, as clerks must, that paper is permanent.

I fell asleep thinking what a comfort it is that institutions protect their archives.

I was wrong, of course. But I didn't learn it until the notices went up in the square — printed, official, and polite as a dinner invitation — announcing that the army would be conducting an audit of all municipal records, effective immediately.

Dread, I discovered, arrives in triplicate.
