## 4. Orders Come Down

The notice went up on the market cross on a Tuesday, which struck Hazel Almeida as fitting. Tuesdays were the day the town had always posted its small announcements — the fair dates, the cholera advisories, the year the fountain was declared off-limits to trout. The town read Tuesdays the way other towns read Sunday. So when Corporal Weiss of the occupying army climbed the cross with a hammer and three sheets of paper, half of Veracourt was already in the square to receive him, and the other half arrived within minutes, drawn by that oldest of rural telegraphs: one person pointing.

Hazel was there because Mrs. Vance's motorcar needed fuel and the fuel depot stood at the corner of the square, behind the cooper's shop, and because he had learned long ago that it paid to be present when powerful men began writing things down. He leaned against the car's fender, cap tipped forward, and watched.

The corporal was young and polite and looked as though he'd rather be anywhere else, which Hazel noted with professional interest. Politeness from an occupying army was not comfort; it was packaging. He had carried enough letters these past two years to know how men announced terrible things in beautiful language. There was a whole genre of it. *It is with profound regret that I must inform you* — he'd read that opening forty times, at least, and each time it meant the same thing underneath, which was simply: someone is dead.

The first sheet went up. The crowd shuffled closer like sheep investigating a fence post.

**NOTICE OF REQUISITION**, it began, in a clerk's careful hand. **By order of the District Command, the following are hereby required for the provisioning and security of the civilian population:** — and then the list, itemized with the loving precision of a man who enjoyed lists: billeting quarters for two hundred men; all grain held in excess of eight weeks' household supply; horses, mules, and draft animals between the ages of four and twelve; motor vehicles and their registration papers; fuel stocks.

And then, at the bottom, almost shyly, as if it had been added last and by someone else:

**Further, all municipal records, registers, and archives shall be surrendered to District Command for audit. Compliance is expected within seventy-two hours. Cooperation will be remembered gratefully.**

Hazel read it twice. He was quick at reading — quicker than most people guessed, standing there in his chauffeur's cap looking like a man whose entire mental life consisted of gear ratios and petrol octanes. He read it twice, and then he did what he always did with information that mattered: he put it away somewhere flat and dry inside himself, the way he'd fold a letter into thirds.

*All municipal records.*

He turned the phrase over. It sounded so harmless. Records were the town's least glamorous possessions — birth registers gone soft at the corners, tax rolls nobody could reconcile, the marriage book with the wine stain shaped like Portugal on page thirty. Who would want them? What use was a record of who owned which field when the fields themselves might change hands by treaty?

But Hazel trusted paper more than people, and he knew what paper could do. Paper said you were born. Paper said you married, bought, inherited, existed. Paper was the difference between a man who had lived in Veracourt for fifty years and a man who had never been here at all. And a government that took your paper could make you into anything it pleased — a stranger, a vagrant, a name that had never troubled anyone.

He thought of the ledger under his floorboard, and felt the thought travel through him like cold water finding a crack.

*For audit.* Audit of what? You audited accounts. These were people.

* * *

Mayor Fouchard came out of the Hôtel de Ville at half past ten, moving with the particular gait of a man walking to his own funeral who has been assured it will be tasteful. He was a round, pink, fundamentally decent man who had been elected three times largely on the strength of remembering everyone's children's names, and he had spent the war perfecting the art of being agreeable to whoever held the guns. Hazel did not despise him for it. Courage was like flour — some households had plenty, some had none, and you rarely got to choose which house you were born into.

"Good citizens," the mayor began, and stopped, because the good citizens had already read the notice and were now watching him with the flat patience of a jury. He wet his lips. "You will have seen the proclamation. The Command has assured me — assured me personally, over their signatures — that this requisition is administrative in nature. A matter of accounting." He held up the letter he carried, triplicate, carbon copies blue and pink flanking the white original like a very dull tricolor flag. "They wish only to examine our records. To — to bring order."

"To take them," said Madame Brissot from the front row. She was seventy-eight and sold eggs and had never once in her life mistaken a euphemism for breakfast.

"Not to *take*, madame. To *audit*. The records will be returned once the audit concludes."

"And when does it conclude?" asked Henri Brissot, her son, who farmed the north fields and had the family gift for cutting directly to the bone. "When the war ends? Or when we've forgotten what it says?"

There was a murmur. The mayor raised both hands like a man stopping traffic. "Henri. Please. I understand your feeling. But I would ask you to consider what refusal buys us. Two hundred soldiers need beds. If they do not billet in our houses by schedule, they will billet in our houses by choice, and choice is a great deal worse than schedule. That is not gratitude. That is arithmetic." He paused, and his voice dropped into something quieter and more honest. "I signed the compliance order this morning. With trembling hand, yes. But I signed it. And I will tell you why — because every alternative I asked after was worse. I asked. Believe me, I asked."

Nobody answered him. In Veracourt, silence was the loudest available dissent, and they deployed it now with full force. The mayor bore it for a moment, then gathered his triplicate and went back inside, and the crowd broke apart into knots of low conversation, the way water breaks around a stone.

Hazel stayed by the car. He watched Corporal Weiss post the second and third notices — grain collection Thursday, animal inventory Friday — and he noticed something small and useful: the corporal checked a folded list before each posting, and on that list, beside the third item, there was a notation Hazel couldn't read at this distance but which had clearly been added in different ink. Something underlined. Something that mattered more than the rest.

He filed that away too. His head was getting crowded. Some nights he worried it would fill up entirely, like a granary, and there'd be no room left for ordinary things — his mother's face, the smell of the river in April. But it hadn't happened yet, and until it did, he intended to keep collecting. Information was the only currency that never debased.

* * *

By afternoon the square had organized itself into the stages of grief, Veracourt-style. Stage one was argument: a cluster of farmers outside the café conducting volume negotiations with fate. Stage two was strategy: Madame Brissot and three other grandmothers quietly discussing which grain cellars had false walls and which walls might acquire false walls by Thursday. Stage three — the largest group, and the quietest — stood near the Hôtel de Ville steps, looking up at the shuttered window of the records room, saying nothing at all.

Hazel walked past them slowly, taking the measure of each knot. Old Léon the ferryman was there, hands jammed in his pockets. The schoolteacher, Mademoiselle Aubry, thin as a candle. The priest's curate, who crossed himself and then, glancing about, uncrossed himself, which Hazel found either scandalous or deeply reassuring, depending on how you thought about God's position on audits.

"You look like a man doing sums," Léon said as Hazel passed.

"I'm always doing sums," Hazel said. "It's the driving. You spend six hours a day calculating whether you can make the hill before dark, it gets into everything."

"And? What do the sums say?"

Hazel considered lying, decided against it — Léon had ferried half the town across the river for forty years and could read a face better than Hazel could read a letter. "They say the grain we can live without. Barely. The animals we can't argue about. But the records—" He lowered his voice. "Léon, my grandmother died in '09. Where does it say she lived? Where does it say she was ever here?"

The old man was quiet. "In the register. Saint-Aubin's parish, and the civil copy besides."

"And if both copies go to headquarters, and headquarters decides Veracourt was never properly registered at all—"

"—then she was never here." Léon chewed on it. "That's a wicked kind of theft. Stealing the dead."

"Stealing the dead," Hazel agreed. "And the living too. It's all one ledger to them."

Which reminded him, unpleasantly, of another ledger entirely — his own, sleeping under the floorboard of his room above the coach-house, forty-one entries long as of Sunday. Forty-one names, dates, circumstances. Men taken by patrols. Letters that arrived addressed to people who no longer answered their doors. A boy named Tomas Ferro, fifteen years old by the parish count and fourteen by his mother's insistence, conscripted in September with his boots unlaced because they'd come for him at dawn.

Hazel had started the ledger out of guilt, which he supposed was as good a founding document as any. Now he understood, with the slow certainty of ice forming, that it might soon be something more than guilt's remedy. If the town's own memory was going to be packed into crates and hauled to District Command — *for audit*, in triplicate, *cooperation gratefully remembered* — then every private record in Veracourt was about to become contraband or scripture, depending on who won.

He didn't yet know which his ledger would be. But he knew the deadline had just moved.

* * *

Mrs. Vance took the news the way she took all news: as ammunition.

"They want the records," she said that evening, pacing the length of her sitting room while Hazel sat by the fire cleaning the car's lamps, or pretending to. "Of course they want the records. Grain feeds the body, horses feed the army, but records — records feed the *story*. Whoever holds the registers gets to say what happened here. Get to say whether anything happened here at all." She stopped at the window. Below, a patrol clumped past through the slush, four men breathing steam. "Mark me, Hazel. Before this winter is out, someone is going to do something in this town that the town will need to prove happened. And they intend to make proving it impossible."

"That's a grim way to read a notice about billets, ma'am."

"It's the accurate way." She turned. Miriam Vance was sixty-two, built like a church door, and had survived three decades of men telling her to calm down by the simple method of refusing entirely. "I've spent thirty years fighting clerks, Hazel. Suffrage is mostly a paperwork problem, whatever the newspapers think. Every right we've ever been denied lives in somebody's registry. And every time we've won one, it's because someone copied the wrong document at the right moment." Her eyes narrowed. "Speaking of which."

"Ma'am?"

"You read very well for a chauffeur. I've known it since March, when you read the petrol invoice upside down while filling the tank. I've said nothing, because a driver who reads is a driver who notices, and a driver who notices keeps us alive." She let that sit between them like a coal. "So I'll ask you plainly, and you may lie to me if you must, though I'll know: did anything in that notice today strike you as odd?"

Hazel set down the lamp and cloth. Here was a moment, and he knew it — the kind you didn't get back. He could be invisible a while longer. Invisible was warm. Invisible had a floorboard and a floorboard had a ledger and the ledger, so far, had kept him safe precisely because nobody knew it existed.

"The records clause," he said. "It's listed last, but the ink's fresher. Added late. Somebody at District Command wanted it in there badly enough to reopen the printing."

Mrs. Vance smiled, slow and satisfied, the smile of a woman confirming a suspicion she'd been nurturing like a houseplant. "Added late. Yes. I thought so too." She came and sat, folding her hands. "Seventy-two hours, the notice says. Then everything this town knows about itself goes into a crate." She looked into the fire for a long moment. "We should hope nothing happens in the next few days that anyone would want to deny."

Hazel would think about that sentence later — often, and with a pain in his chest like a cracked rib — because three nights later, shots rang out over the old bridge in the dark, and by morning the army had nailed up a notice about bandits, and Mrs. Vance's careless prophecy sat over all of it like weather.

But that evening there was only the fire, and the patrol passing below, and Hazel carrying his lamp oil back to the coach-house, where he knelt, pried up the board, and counted his names. Forty-one. He touched the page once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't want to wake.

Then he sharpened his pencil, and waited.
