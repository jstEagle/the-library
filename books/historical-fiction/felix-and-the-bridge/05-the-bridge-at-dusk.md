## 5. The Bridge at Dusk

There is a particular quality to a town the day after it has been requisitioned. I had never known this before Tuesday, and now I know it the way one knows a toothache — intimately, and against my will.

The square still stood. The fountain still burbled its idiot little song. The baker still opened his shutters at half past five, because bread does not care whose flag is on the customs house. But everything had gone quiet in the way a house goes quiet when the father comes home drunk — everyone moving softly, everyone listening, everyone pretending the furniture hasn't been rearranged.

Mrs. Vance noticed it too. She said nothing about it directly, which was itself an act of discipline for a woman who had once delivered a forty-minute speech to a county committee that had asked her to keep her remarks brief.

"Mr. Almeida," she said that morning, settling into the motorcar with her satchel of correspondence, "we are going to be very careful drivers for the next while."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Careful is not the same as cowardly."

"No, ma'am."

"It is merely careful." She smoothed her skirt over her knees. "I want you to understand the distinction, because I intend to go on being inconvenient to every man in uniform who has ever told me to mind my place, and I require my driver not to get us both shot in the process of being inconvenient."

"I'll inconvenience us moderately," I said.

She laughed — one short bark, quickly suppressed, as though laughter were also something the army might requisition. And we drove out through streets where the shopkeepers had all, overnight, learned a new way of looking through you.

* * *

I should explain what I do with my days, now that the war has come to sit down in our town like a relative with no announced departure date.

I drive Mrs. Vance. That is the occupation printed, if anyone asks, which no one does, because chauffeurs are furniture with hands. What I actually do is more complicated and less respectable: I read her mail.

Not by opening envelopes — I am not a burglar. The mail simply passes through my hands. She dictates letters in the car, letters to suffrage chapters in three counties, letters to newspapers, letters to a cousin in the capital who apparently owes her money and regrets it. And then there are the sealed ones, the ones she hands me at the post office window to carry back to the motorcar, and here is where I confess my sin: paper talks to me. It always has. An envelope held up to certain light, a letter left open on a seat for ninety seconds while its owner argues with a postmaster — these things speak. I have never once made them speak loudly. They simply talk, quietly, the way a river talks under ice, and I listen because listening costs me nothing and knowing has kept me alive through four years of a war that officially ended our town's involvement two years ago.

We are, officially, a town behind the lines. Unofficially, we are a town the lines pass through whenever the lines feel like it.

That afternoon she sent me to the post office alone with eleven letters. Eleven. I carried them in the leather satchel that has become, without my ever deciding it, the organizing principle of my existence. Everything I own fits in that satchel or could. My mother taught me this before she died: *Hazel, a man who can carry his whole life in one bag cannot be robbed of anything but the bag.* She meant it as advice about travel. I have made it into a philosophy. Some men build houses. I build inventories.

At the post office, Sergeant Prewitt — new since Tuesday, with the complexion of a man who has been fed exclusively on boiled things — took longer than usual with the outgoing mail. He weighed each letter twice. He looked at Mrs. Vance's name on the return corner the way a man looks at a dog he's been told might bite.

"Your employer," he said, "writes a great many letters."

"She's a correspondent," I agreed.

"That's one word for it." He stamped the last one harder than stamps require. "Tell her the postal regulations apply to everyone. Even ladies."

"I'll tell her," I said, "though I expect she knows. She reads the regulations recreationally."

He did not find this funny. Very few soldiers find anything funny anymore; humor, like grain and horses, has been requisitioned.

Walking back to the car, I passed the notice board outside the mayor's office, where the requisition orders hung in triplicate — one for the town records, one for billeting, one for grain — and beneath them, fresh that morning, a fourth notice I hadn't seen:

*BY ORDER OF THE MILITARY GOVERNOR: The old bridge across the Alden will be closed to civilian traffic effective immediately. Crossings permitted only with written authorization.*

The old bridge. Not the rail bridge upstream, which was already guarded — the old bridge, the stone one, the one with the saint's niche halfway across where children left pebbles and courting couples left initials and the whole town had crossed, twice daily, since before anyone's grandmother was born. The bridge where Tomas Okafor used to fish off the downstream parapet with a line his father tied for him, catching nothing, catching nothing, and once — the whole street talked about it for a week — catching an eel so large his mother made him give it to Father Ambrose out of what she called theological caution.

Why close a stone bridge? Armies close bridges for tactical reasons, and there was nothing tactical within thirty miles of ours except mud and sheep. I stood there reading the notice a third time, and a feeling came into me that I did not yet have a name for. It wasn't fear exactly. Fear has a temperature. This was colder than fear. It was the feeling of arithmetic beginning — of numbers arranging themselves in your head without your permission.

I got in the car and drove Mrs. Vance to her meeting and said nothing, because there was nothing yet to say. Only a closed bridge. Towns survive closed bridges.

* * *

But I couldn't stop thinking about it, and so that evening I did a thing I am not proud of and would do again: I went out.

I told myself it was for exercise. I told myself the evening air was good for the lungs. I told myself several other lies of increasing elaborateness while I put on my oldest coat and walked down through the tannery lane toward the river, because the truth — the plain, unglamorous truth — is that I wanted to look at the bridge, and wanting to look at a thing until you understand it is the closest thing I have to a vice.

Dusk in November comes early and brings friends. By six o'clock the light was the color of dishwater going grey, and the river ran dark under the arches, and the bridge stood black against the last of the sky like a sentence someone had started and stopped writing.

There were soldiers on it.

Not the usual pair of sentries with rifles slung loose and cigarettes cupped. These were different. There must have been twenty of them, moving with purpose, and they were doing something to the bridge — I could see lanterns bobbing along its length, and figures working at the parapets, and the flat metallic sounds of things being set down carefully. Barbed wire, I thought. Or charges. My knowledge of demolitions comes entirely from novels, so I will not pretend to expertise; I only know that soldiers who seal a bridge at dusk, in the dark, quietly, without bugle or proclamation, are not preparing a defense. Defenses are loud. Defenses want to be seen. Whatever this was, it wanted privacy.

I found what shelter I could — a stack of tanbark barrels by the tannery wall, which smelled powerfully of everything tanneries smell of, and which I mention now only because for the rest of my life the smell of oak bark will return me to that hour — and I watched.

A lorry came down the mill road without lights. Then another. Men unloaded from them in silence, which is the most frightening sound an army makes. I counted — counting is another of my vices, or perhaps my one talent — and got to somewhere past sixty before the darkness and the distance defeated me. Sixty men, two lorries, lanterns hooded, on a bridge that led nowhere of any military consequence whatsoever.

And then, at some hour I could not precisely fix — seven, half past, the time when honest towns are sitting down to soup — I heard the shots.

I want to be exact about this, because exactness is all I have to offer and I have decided it must be enough. The shots came in volleys. Not continuous firing, not a battle — battles have a texture, a ragged back-and-forth. This was measured. A volley. A pause long enough to count to ten in. Another volley. A pause. I lay against the tanbark with my heart going like a bird in a box and I counted the volleys because my body insisted on counting even while my mind begged it to stop.

Seven. Seven volleys, roughly eight shots to a volley by my ear, though ears are liars at night and across water.

Afterward — and afterward lasted a long time — there was the sound of work again. Lorry doors. Engines turning over. And then the bridge stood empty under the moon, sealed at both ends, and the river went on underneath it doing what rivers do, which is carry things away and tell no one.

I walked home the long way, through streets where every window was dark, and I understood — not fully, not with proof, proof would take days to arrive — but understood the way you understand weather coming, that whatever had happened on the old bridge, the word *bandits* would be involved. Armies have a small vocabulary for such nights. Bandits. Saboteurs. Regrettable incidents during security operations. The words arrive pre-printed, like funeral cards.

What I could not make fit — and I turned it over and over walking home, like a coin I suspected of being foreign — was the number. Fifty or sixty men to seal a bridge. Two lorries. Seven volleys. If they'd been shooting bandits, why bring sixty men to a bridge bandits would have to approach openly, in country where every farmer knew every other farmer's face? And if they hadn't been shooting bandits —

If they hadn't been shooting bandits, then who had been standing on the old bridge at dusk, in the dark, in November, in groups of eight?

I got home. I lit no lamp. I sat in the dark at my table for a long while, and then I did the thing I have done eleven times before, the thing that is either my penance or my purpose, I have never settled which.

I lifted the floorboard.

* * *

Under the floorboard beside my bed there is a space deep enough for a ledger and shallow enough that a casual searcher would find nothing, because searchers, like burglars, are looking for what fits their expectations, and no one expects a chauffeur to keep accounts.

The ledger is a school exercise book, bought for two coppers from Hessler's stationery shop the week the conscription notices first came. On the first page, in ink I have since learned to write smaller, is the entry that started everything:

*14 March. Pieter Voss, wheelwright's apprentice, taken from the north road by patrol, reason unstated. His mother asked at the garrison office and was told no record exists. Age 19. Has a scar on his left hand from a chisel. Whistles when he works. His mother's name is Marta.*

I wrote the whistling because it seemed important at the time and seems more important now. A record that says only *Pieter Voss, 19* could be anyone. A record that says *he whistles when he works* is a person. The army keeps numbers. Someone has to keep the whistling.

Eleven entries in four years. Not many — I am not proud of the arithmetic of my courage, which has always moved at the speed of a man walking carefully — but each one true, each one checked, each one carrying whatever detail I could gather: ages, trades, mothers' names, the color of a coat last seen in, the joke a man made at the ferry crossing the week before he vanished. Letters that were never delivered I note separately, in the back pages, because undelivered letters are their own kind of disappearance.

Tonight I opened the book to the first blank page, and my hand shook, and I waited for it to stop, and it did not entirely stop, and I wrote anyway.

*I do not know the names,* I began. *Seven volleys heard from the tannery bank, between seven and eight o'clock, 9 November. Approximately eight shots per volley. Soldiers observed sealing the bridge beforehand: no fewer than fifty, possibly sixty. Two lorries, unmarked, arrived without lights. No battle sounds preceded the volleys. No returning fire whatsoever.*

Then I sat with the page and hated it, because it was an account of the army and not of anyone, and my whole ledger exists because I refuse to let the army be the only narrator.

And then — I don't know how else to describe it; it was less a thought than a door opening — I thought of who had reason to be on a bridge at night, in ones and twos and eights, in a town where the curfew bell rang at nine and every honest soul was behind a door by half past.

Smugglers, whispered the part of me that wanted to be wrong. Deserters. Men moving quietly because they had reasons of their own.

Or: people the army had summoned. People told to report. People given written authorization — *crossings permitted only with written authorization* — and marched onto a stone bridge in the dark in groups small enough to manage.

I thought of Tomas Okafor, who fished off the downstream parapet, who was nineteen now and worked at the flour store, who had been seen — I had seen him myself, Tuesday, before the requisition — being questioned politely at the garrison office about a cousin in the hills. I thought of the Kessler brothers, who ran charcoal down from the ridge and were known to cross at odd hours and were, everyone agreed, probably smuggling something, though what a man could smuggle out of a town with nothing left in it was a question nobody pressed. I thought of young Ferenc Balo, the choirboy with the voice like struck glass, who had told Father Ambrose openly that he would not register for the labor levy, and who had been told, gently, by everyone who loved him, to be sensible, to be quiet, to wait it out.

I don't know. That is the honest entry, and honesty is the entire currency of the ledger, so let me spend it plainly: I do not know those names belong on the page. I know only that seven volleys happened where no battle was, and that a town of four thousand souls contains perhaps a hundred men with reasons to be abroad after curfew, and that the army brought sixty soldiers and two lorries to a bridge it had just closed, and that in the morning there would be a notice, and the notice would use a small vocabulary.

I wrote three names. Underneath the account of volleys and lorries, in the column where names go, I wrote:

*Tomas Okafor, 19, flour store. Questioned at garrison re: cousin, 7 Nov.*
*Aldo Kessler, 24, and Miklós Kessler, 21, charcoal burners. Crossed at night habitually.*
*Ferenc Balo, 16, choir. Refused labor levy registration, publicly, October.*

Three names. Possibly wrong. Possibly all wrong, possibly the boys are asleep in their beds this minute and the volleys were something else entirely, target practice, desert executions from some other jurisdiction, anything. I wrote above them the only honest heading available:

*Possibly among the dead on the old bridge, 9 November. UNCONFIRMED.*

Unconfirmed. The word looked weak on the page, and I hated it, and I kept it, because a ledger that lies to comfort itself is worth exactly what the army's records are worth.

I put the book back under the floorboard. I lay down. I did not sleep. Outside, around midnight, boots passed in the street — a patrol, unhurried, checking doors — and I listened to them the way you listen to a sound in your own house at night, cataloguing it, memorizing it, filing it away in the satchel inside my head where I keep everything that fits.

* * *

In the morning the notice was on the board outside the mayor's office, and I had called it exactly, which gave me no satisfaction whatsoever, only the gray cold pleasure of a man proven right about a funeral.

*NOTICE TO THE POPULATION. During the night of 9 November, armed bandits attempting to sabotage the old bridge were engaged by loyal forces. Several bandits were killed. Order has been restored. Citizens are reminded that harboring bandits or aiding them in any way is punishable by death. Signed, by order of the Military Governor, Capt. E. Brandt.*

Several bandits. Killed. Engaged — as though the volleys had been a conversation.

I stood in the little crowd that gathered to read it — a crowd that read in silence, that shuffled and looked at the ground and dispersed without a word spoken above a murmur, which is its own kind of testimony — and I watched faces. Old Hessler the stationer read it twice and went pale in a specific way. Frau Okafor did not come near the board at all; she stood across the square at the fountain, filling a jug she did not need filled, and she did not look at the notice, and she did not look away from the direction of the bridge, and I thought: she knows. Mothers know the way rivers know. Something had already reached her, by whisper or by instinct, and she was standing very still over her jug the way you stand over a wound.

Captain Brandt. I noted the signature. I noted the neat, upright hand of it, the confident loops — a man who signs death notices the way other men sign dinner invitations. I filed the name away with the others.

And then, because the world is obscene in its scheduling, Mrs. Vance's morning mail arrived, and I carried it to the motorcar, and the topmost envelope bore the garrison franking and was addressed to her in that same neat, upright, confident hand.

*From the office of Capt. E. Brandt, Military Governor.*

She was late getting out — dressing, gathering papers — and the envelope sat in my hands in the cold motorcar, talking to me the way paper talks, quietly, under the ice.

I am not proud of what I did next. I have decided to stop saying that. I am done being proud or ashamed; the ledger doesn't have columns for it.

I steamed it open over the kettle in the kitchen, with the door locked and the window fogged, and I read it twice, and I copied it word for word into my memory, which holds text the way a wax cylinder holds a voice, and then I sealed it again with the glue stick Hessler sells for a copper, and I carried it out to the car just as Mrs. Vance came down the steps calling for me to hurry, we haven't all day, Mr. Almeida.

The letter concerned the seized municipal records. It instructed Mrs. Vance — as chairwoman of the town's literary society, custodian of certain archives — to surrender any documents in her keeping relating to the events of the autumn, and it informed her, in a paragraph of bureaucratic silk, that all records collected under the requisition order were being forwarded to army headquarters *for consolidation and disposition.*

Disposition. I turned the word over all the way to the county road. Disposition, as in temperament. Disposition, as in the arrangement of troops. Disposition, as in what one does with a thing one wishes to be rid of.

Burn, said the word, quietly, under the ice. It means burn.
