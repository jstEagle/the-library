## 7. The Widow Okafor

The condolence letter sat in my satchel like a stone I'd agreed to carry up a hill.

I had known it was there since morning. Mrs. Vance had handed me the mail bundle on the porch steps, and even through the wrapping I could feel that one envelope was different from the others — heavier, or so I told myself later, though paper weighs nothing at all. It was addressed in a clerk's careful hand to *Mrs. Adaeze Okafor, Weaver's Lane, by the tannery*. The army seal on it was crisp and fresh, stamped with a date two days after the shots on the bridge.

The army was quick about its paperwork when it wanted to be. That was the thing I kept learning. Slow to pay for grain, slow to answer complaints about requisitioned horses — but swift as a weasel when it came to telling a mother her son was dead.

I took Weaver's Lane last, after three deliveries that meant nothing. A tailor's confirmation. An invoice for lamp oil. Letters that would be read once and forgotten by supper. I read them all anyway, standing in doorways, because that is what I do — but these went into my memory the way water goes into sand, leaving no mark.

The Okafor letter I did not open. I told myself this was respect. I told myself a great many things on the walk down past the tannery, where the wind carried the sharp vinegar smell of the curing vats and the cobbles were slick with the morning's frost-sweat.

* * *

Mrs. Okafor's house was narrow and upright, like a hymnal on a shelf. The paint was the color of dried marigolds, and someone — she, I supposed — had planted winter kale in the strip of garden by the gate, rows straight as soldiers. There was a mended window on the ground floor, the crack sealed with resin and a small wooden crosspiece, and I remember thinking: here is a woman who fixes things. Here is a woman who does not leave broken things broken.

She answered the door before I knocked twice, which meant she had seen me coming down the lane. Of course she had. In a town the size of ours, an army envelope arriving by hand is not a delivery. It is an event. Neighbors had probably watched me the whole way, each of them silently praying the letter was for someone else, each of them ashamed of the prayer.

"You're the Vance girl's driver," she said. Not a question.

"Hazel Almeida, ma'am."

"Almeida." She said it slowly, tasting it, filing it away somewhere. She was a tall woman gone slightly stooped, the way trees stoop that have stood through a lot of weather, with gray threading close-cropped black hair and hands that were rough at the knuckles from decades of work. Her eyes were dry and terribly steady. "You'd better come in. If you stand on my step holding that envelope much longer, the whole lane will know before you've gone, and I'd rather hear it once than eleven times."

I followed her into a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and cloves. There was a loom in the corner, half-dressed with warp threads, and a chair by the stove that was worn smooth on the arms. On the mantel stood a photograph of a young man in a borrowed suit, one shoulder higher than the other because the jacket didn't fit, grinning like the suit didn't matter at all.

I knew his face. Everyone in town knew his face. He'd been the one who could fix any engine ever made and several that hadn't been made yet.

"Sit," she said. "Tea?"

"I shouldn't—"

"You shouldn't what, deliver your letter? No. But nobody delivers news like that standing up. Sit down, Mr. Almeida. It's crueler if you rush."

So I sat, and she poured tea from a pot with a chipped spout, and I held the envelope in both hands like something that might go off.

* * *

Here is what I expected: that she would take the letter unopened, the way people do, saving the wound for later, alone. Or that she would ask me to read it aloud, which has happened twice, and which I hate in a way I can't fully describe — my voice turning official grief into spoken words, my mouth doing the army's work.

What happened instead was this. She set down her cup, looked at the envelope without touching it, and said:

"Tomas Ekwe was on the bridge."

It was not quite a question either. But it wanted an answer, and it deserved a true one, and I have spent enough of my life around lies to know one when it's waiting in my throat. The lie would have been easy. *"I don't know anything about the bridge, ma'am. I only carry letters."*

"He was," I said.

"And he lived."

I looked up. Her face gave nothing away, but her hands, wrapped around her cup, had gone white at the knuckles.

"A boy came over the wall behind the tannery last night," she went on, in the flat voice of someone reciting something learned by heart to keep from screaming. "Sixteen, maybe. Soaked to the bone, teeth chattering. Wouldn't give his name. Wouldn't come inside. He stood out there by the kale and he said he'd been on the bridge when they closed it after dark, and that when the shooting started he went into the water on purpose, because the water was better than what was happening on the planks. He said my Tomas pulled him over the railing first. Pushed him over, more like. Said Tomas was still on the bridge when he last saw him, but the bridge wasn't—" Her jaw worked once. "Wasn't all standing. And then he went along the riverbank toward Mill Road and climbed the wall, and asked God's pardon for what he was about to do, and ran."

She finally looked at me directly, and I understood that the steadiness had cost her everything she had.

"So you see, Mr. Almeida, I already know whatever that letter says. I have known since midnight. What I don't know is whether the army knows that I know, and what they do to mothers who know things." She slid the envelope back across the table toward me, an inch, just an inch, as though returning a move in a game. "So tell me. Do I open this and weep like a good citizen? Or do I open this and keep my face like a shut door?"

I thought about Mrs. Vance's letters, all those fiery pages I carried through checkpoints under the noses of men who never thought to search a chauffeur. I thought about the ledger under my floorboard, the names I'd been collecting in secret like a miser hoarding coins no bank would honor.

And I heard myself say, "Open it and keep your face like a shut door, ma'am. And let me tell you why."

* * *

I told her some of it. Not the ledger — not yet, not in words; I didn't have the words yet, and besides, a floorboard is a floorboard until you say otherwise. But I told her that the army had requisitioned the town records, all of them, every birth and marriage and death the municipality had ever bothered to write down, and that they'd done it quietly, politely, in triplicate, and that papers were being moved into the commandant's house on Cathedral Street. I told her that notices had gone up about bandit activity on the bridge, which was strange, given that the only bandits our town had ever produced were boys who poached rabbits from Colonel Hartley's orchard, dead or alive of old age now.

I watched her understand it the way I had understood it — in stages, each worse than the last.

"They're going to burn the books," she said quietly. "Aren't they. All of it. Every record that says who lived here."

"It looks that way from where I sit."

"Then there will be no proof my Tomas ever existed at all. Except—" she touched the envelope, still unopened "—this. Which will say he died of bandit-related wounds, or resisting lawful authority, or some phrase written by a man who wasn't there."

"Yes," I said. Because there was no point pretending otherwise.

Mrs. Okafor nodded, slowly, the way one nods at a diagnosis. Then she stood, went to the mantel, and brought back the photograph. She set it on the table between us, facing me.

"Then here is what I want from you, Mr. Almeida, and I'll thank you to listen carefully, because I've thought about this since the boy went over my wall and I've got it down to essentials." She drew a breath. "His name was Tomas Ekwe Okafor. Not Thomas. Tomas — T-o-m-a-s, the way his father spelled it off the ship's manifest, and I'll not have it anglicized by some clerk's habit. Ekwe, after my mother's father, who was a boatman and would have had opinions about bridges. Okafor. Born the ninth of March, 1899, in this house, delivered by old Mrs. Prynne who sneezed through the whole business. He stood five foot ten in his boots, though he told girls six foot. He had a scar on his left thumb from a flywheel, and he whistled through his teeth when he concentrated, and he once repaired Captain Brandt's own motorcar and complained about the carburetor for a week afterward to anyone who'd listen."

She stopped. Her voice hadn't broken once, and somehow that was worse than weeping.

"That is what I know," she said. "That is mine. Nobody can requisition it. But memory is a stingy thing, Mr. Almeida. It keeps what it likes and drops the rest, and in twenty years I might remember the whistling and lose the birthday, and in forty years some well-meaning person might write down 'Thomas' because it looks tidier." She turned the photograph around so his grin faced her. "So I'm asking you to hold it too. Two copies, as it were. You drive for Mrs. Vance. You go everywhere and nobody sees you. Well — I see you."

I am not going to pretend my eyes were perfectly dry. But I took out my notebook — the ordinary one, the one I used for deliveries and mileage — and I wrote it while she dictated, every word, and when I hesitated over the date she repeated it: "Ninth of March, 1899," with the patience of a schoolmistress and the iron of a general.

When I finished, she read it back over my shoulder, checked it the way she must have checked her warp threads, and nodded once.

"There," she said. "Now it exists twice."

* * *

We drank the tea. It was strong enough to trot a horse across, and I mean that as praise. She asked polite questions about Mrs. Vance and the car and whether the roads to the east were as bad as rumored, and I answered, and for a quarter of an hour we were two people having tea, which I suspect was a mercy she was granting us both.

At the door, she paused with her hand on the latch.

"The boy who came over the wall," she said. "He wouldn't take food. Took a blanket, though, off the line, which I let him think he stole." Almost, very almost, a smile. "He said there were others. Others who went into the water. He'd counted splashes, he said, and then made himself stop counting." She looked at the lane, empty now, the neighbors having given up their vigil. "If names surface, Mr. Almeida — if that boy finds his tongue, or if the river gives anything back — you'll know where the kale grows straight."

"Yes, ma'am," I said. "You'll be second to know. After the page."

She studied me a moment longer, this woman who fixed broken windows and grew things in frost, and whatever she saw in my face seemed to satisfy her, because she said, "Good," and shut the door with the soft click of a lock that would hold.

* * *

I walked back up through the town slower than I needed to, past the shuttered flour store and the livery stable where army horses now stood in stalls built by local hands, and I found my thoughts arranging themselves in a way they hadn't before.

I had always thought of the ledger as theft — taking names that weren't mine, keeping accounts I hadn't been authorized to keep. A private sin, a small treason committed nightly under a floorboard. But Mrs. Okafor hadn't asked me to steal anything. She had *hired* me. Person by person, name by name, she had put me on salary, and the wage was exactness: get the spelling right, get the birthday right, get the scar on the correct thumb.

Memory as duty. Owed individually. Collectable on demand.

By the time I reached the square I had my notebook out again, walking, adding beneath Tomas's entry a line about the boy over the wall — no name, but a description: *sixteen or near, light-haired, cut on right eyebrow, went into the water on purpose and lived.* Witnesses count too. Someone would have to explain, someday, how we knew what we knew, and it might as well start with me.

I was so absorbed in this new arithmetic — so pleased, in my quiet and lonely way, to have found a use for the only talent I had — that I nearly walked into the notice being pasted to the town hall door.

A corporal with a bucket of paste was smoothing it flat: **REQUISITION ORDER NO. 4**, it began, and below that, in the same tidy hand I'd seen on the Okafor envelope, a list. Billets. Grain. Motor vehicles, with papers to be presented for inspection within forty-eight hours.

*Motor vehicles.*

I looked across the square to where Mrs. Vance's car sat parked exactly where I'd left it, gleaming faintly in the cold, and I thought of Mrs. Okafor's parting gift — *you'll know where the kale grows straight* — and of Ruth Ellery's name, which I did not yet know I would learn, and of forty-eight hours, which suddenly felt less like a deadline and more like a countdown.

The corporal dipped his brush and reached for the next notice.

"Well," I said to no one, folding my notebook away, "that complicates things."
