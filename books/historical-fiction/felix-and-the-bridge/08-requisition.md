## 8. Requisition

The notice went up on the town hall door on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were market days. The square was full of people who had come to buy eggs and argue about the price of butter, and instead they got to read, in three languages and a font of bureaucratic serenity, that their town now belonged — provisionally, temporarily, in the friendliest possible way — to the army.

*Requisitioned for the duration of military necessity.* Billets. Grain. Horses. Fuel. And at the bottom, almost as an afterthought, the line that had made my stomach drop when Mrs. Vance read it aloud over breakfast: *all municipal records and registers, for audit.*

Audit. As if the war were an accountant.

"They'll take the flour store first," Mrs. Vance said, buttering her toast with the calm of a woman who had been arrested twice and found it merely inconvenient. "Flour is always first. Then horses. They'll leave the livery man his saddle, out of sentiment."

She was nearly right. They came for the flour store on Wednesday, and for the livery stable on Thursday, and they came politely, which was somehow worse than if they'd come with axes.

* * *

I watched the livery requisition from across the street, standing beside the motorcar with my hands folded, wearing the expression of a man who is furniture. It's a useful expression. I'd been practicing it for years.

Old Tomas Ferro stood in the doorway of his own stable while four soldiers walked his aisles, running their hands along his horses' necks like suitors. He'd had six animals. Good ones. The gray mare he'd raised from a foal, the pair of bays that had pulled his hearse for twenty years and knew the route to the cemetery better than any priest.

"Paperwork," said the sergeant, not unkindly, holding out a clipboard. "You'll be compensated. At government rates."

"Government rates," Tomas repeated. His voice was steady but his hands weren't. "Sergeant, I sold that mare's mother to your colonel's father. I shod every horse in this valley for thirty years. What are government rates?"

The sergeant consulted his paper as though it might contain an answer to anything except itself. "Four hundred crowns per animal."

Tomas laughed. It wasn't a happy laugh, but it wasn't entirely broken either, and I remember thinking — I remember noting, because noting is what I do — that a man's laugh can survive quite a lot before it stops being his own. "Four hundred. That mare cost me eight hundred to feed last winter alone."

"Then you've been feeding her too well." The sergeant actually smiled. There was no cruelty in it. That was the terrible thing. He was a decent man doing an indecent arithmetic, and both of them knew there was no appeal above the clipboard.

They took five of the six horses. They left Tomas the old gelding, Biscuit, on account of Biscuit being twenty-three years old and half-blind and, in the sergeant's words, "not worth the transport." Tomas put his arm around Biscuit's neck after the soldiers left and stood there a long while, and I looked away, because some things you shouldn't witness even when you're memorizing everything else.

Then he caught my eye across the street and called out, "Hazel! You still driving that lady around in her motorcar?"

"For now."

"Well, mind the roads. Half the drivers they're bringing in have never seen mud." He said it loudly enough for the soldiers to hear, cheerfully, a man performing normalcy at volume. Underneath it, quieter, just for me: "They took my books too, boy. My breeding records. Forty years. Why would an army want my breeding records?"

I didn't answer. We both knew why armies want records. Armies want records the way I wanted letters — to know everything, to hold everything, to decide what survives. The difference was that Tomas's ledger of mares and foals was innocent, and mine was not, and the army's intentions were neither.

* * *

The flour store fell on Friday without ceremony. Herr Weiss, the miller, handed over his keys himself, and I heard later — through Mrs. Vance's network, which heard everything within forty miles — that he'd asked only one question: whether his men would be paid through the month. The lieutenant in charge had said yes, and everyone present understood it to be a lie told for comfort, which is sometimes the only currency left in an occupied town.

By Saturday morning the pattern was legible. They were taking everything that could be weighed, counted, or written down. Which meant the motorcar papers were coming. I mentioned this to Mrs. Vance over coffee, and she set her cup down with a small decisive click.

"Yes. I've been expecting them since Tuesday. Hazel, listen to me." She fixed me with the look she used on cabinet ministers and slow-witted journalists. "That car is not a luxury anymore. It's our legs. Without it we're two pedestrians with opinions. Whatever they offer you, whatever they demand, keep the car in service. Do you understand? Grovel if you must."

"I don't grovel," I said. "I negotiate."

"You flatter yourself. You're going to grovel beautifully, and I'm going to be upstairs pretending not to hear it, and afterward neither of us will mention it." She picked her cup back up. "That's how dignity works in wartime, Hazel. It hides in the pantry."

* * *

They came Monday, mid-morning, while Mrs. Vance was conveniently upstairs drafting a letter about the moral bankruptcy of empires. Two soldiers and a corporal named Drexler, who had the round pink face of a choirboy and the manner of a man who had discovered paperwork could do what bayonets couldn't.

"Motor vehicle registration," he announced on the doorstep, as though delivering a telegram. "And keys."

"The car belongs to Mrs. Miriam Vance," I said. "A private civilian. The requisition order covers municipal property and—"

"It covers vehicles of utility to the occupation." Drexler smiled at me. Choirboy smile. "Is the vehicle operational?"

"It's operational."

"Then it has utility." He held out his hand for the papers as if asking me to pass the salt.

I gave him the registration. I had no choice, and we both knew I had no choice, and the knowing sat between us like a third soldier. But I did not give him the keys. Instead I stepped back into the hall and said, in my most reasonable voice, the voice I used for checkpoints and customs men:

"Corporal. May I make an observation?"

Drexler sighed the sigh of a man whose day contained many forms. "Briefly."

"Your requisition covers the vehicle. Fine. Take it. Park it in the square with the other confiscated cars. In three weeks the magneto will fail — it's temperamental, needs a particular touch — and none of your mechanics will know why, because none of them built these engines, and the parts come from a factory that is currently on the other side of the front." I let that settle. "Or. The car stays where it is, garaged here, maintained by the one driver in this town who knows its habits, and placed at the disposal of your officers whenever they need transport. Which, if I'm not mistaken, is often, since you took the livery's horses on Thursday."

I hadn't known they'd taken the horses until Thursday. But I'd seen the empty stalls, and I'd done the arithmetic every occupied town eventually does: an army with more officers than horses needs wheels.

Drexler's smile flickered — recalculated. "Officers' transport requires a licensed driver."

"I've driven this valley's roads for six years. Mud, ice, artillery convoys parked sideways on the Mill Road. Your drivers have been here nine days." I shrugged, hands open. "Take the car. Or take the car *with* me. Those are the options. I'm flexible on the paperwork, Corporal. I'm not flexible on who turns the crank."

He studied me for a long moment. I kept my face pleasant and empty, a man discussing weather. Inside, my heart was going like a telegraph key. This was the whole game, right here on the doormat: the car was Mrs. Vance's mobility, the network's circulation, and soon — though Drexler couldn't know it — possibly the only thing between my ledger and a bonfire. If they towed it to the motor pool, we were pedestrians with opinions.

"Who assigned you this cleverness?" Drexler asked. Not hostile. Almost curious. "The woman upstairs?"

"Nobody assigns me anything, Corporal. I'm a chauffeur. I solve problems so my employer doesn't have to think about them. Isn't that the entire profession?"

Something in that landed. Maybe he had a chauffeur. Maybe he *was* somebody's problem-solver, far from home, doing sums he didn't like. He wrote for a while on his clipboard — that sacred object, that portable altar — then tore off a pink slip and handed it to me.

"Vehicle retained for officers' transport service. Driver: Almeida, H. You report to the commandant's house tomorrow, seven o'clock. Captain Brandt's office will assign your schedule." He tucked the registration into his satchel. "You drive for us now, Almeida. Remember that. Every road, every passenger, logged."

"Of course," I said. "I'm very good at logs."

If he caught the joke, he didn't show it. He clicked his heels at the door — a small absurd courtesy, the war's idea of manners — and walked back down the street to confiscate something else.

Upstairs, Mrs. Vance emerged the moment his boots faded, exactly as promised, having heard nothing whatsoever.

"Well?" she said.

"We keep the car. I drive for the commandant starting tomorrow."

She went very still. "Brandt's house?"

"So I'm told."

"Hazel." She sat down slowly, all the fire in her banked down to coals. "Do you understand what you've just done? You've volunteered to be the army's errand boy. You'll be at their elbow. In their houses. Listening to—" She stopped herself, and I watched her arrive at the same place I'd already arrived, the way she always did, three seconds behind me and arriving harder. "Listening to everything."

"That occurred to me," I said mildly.

"And the humiliation doesn't trouble you? Wearing their cap badge, saluting their captains, driving the men who—" She gestured vaguely eastward, toward the bridge, toward everything neither of us said aloud.

I thought about Tomas Ferro's empty stalls. About Biscuit standing blind and useless in a paddock, the one horse not worth taking. About the pink slip in my pocket, which was either a leash or a passport depending entirely on who held the pen tomorrow.

"Mrs. Vance," I said, "a man who owns nothing can't be robbed. A man who drives their car goes everywhere they go." I tapped the pocket with the pink slip. "Besides. Someone has to know which clerk handles the paperwork."

She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her turn away and busy herself with the teapot, which was her way of conceding a point without admitting it.

"Seven o'clock," she said finally. "Wear the gray uniform jacket. And Hazel — polish the brass. Servility is a costume. Wear it well."

* * *

That night I took up the floorboard one last time before the new life began, and added a line beneath Tomas Ferro's name: *5 horses, 400 cr. each. Breeding records seized.* Beneath Herr Weiss: *flour store, keys surrendered, wages promised (false).* Small entries. The war's petty thefts, itemized. History would record the battles; someone had to record the horses.

Then I sat on the edge of my bed with the pink slip in my hand and admitted the thing I'd been not-admitting since the doorstep: tomorrow I would put on their cap badge, hold their doors open, call the men who sealed the bridge "sir," and smile while I did it. And the worst part — the part I set down here honestly, because a ledger should be honest even when its keeper isn't — the worst part was that some cold practical corner of me was already pleased.

Because Captain Brandt's house was where the town's records had gone.

And tomorrow, God help me, I'd be driving straight up to the door.
