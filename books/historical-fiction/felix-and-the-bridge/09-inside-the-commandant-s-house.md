## 9. Inside the Commandant's House

The first thing you learn driving for officers is that they never look at their driver. Not really. They look *through* him, the way you look through a window at weather. A chauffeur is furniture with an engine, a coat rack that happens to know how gears work. I had counted on being invisible my whole life, and it turned out I'd been practicing for exactly this: three days of holding doors for Captain Brandt while he talked past me as if I were a lamppost with opinions about carburetors.

The second thing you learn is where they keep things.

I want to be careful here, because there's a version of this story where I make myself out to be braver than I was. The truth is that for the first two days I was sick with fear — the ordinary, animal kind, the kind that lives in the stomach and makes your hands slip on the wheel. Every time Brandt spoke to me I heard my own voice come out too quickly, too agreeably, the voice of a man auditioning for the role of Nobody At All. I smiled so much my face ached. I agreed that the weather was fine and the roads were bad and the town was lucky to have such orderly administration. Yes sir. No sir. Very good, sir.

And all the while, underneath the smiling, that cold practical corner of me from the night before sat quietly in the back of my skull with its notebook open, taking attendance.

* * *

Brandt's house — the Commandant's house, though everyone in town still called it the Delacour house, because the Delacours had built it and the army had merely moved in like damp — stood on the rue des Tilleuls behind iron gates that had been painted gray sometime around the occupation, as if color itself were suspect. It had been a handsome house once. It still wanted to be. You could see it straining under the sandbags and the sentry box and the telephone wire running up the facade like a scar.

I drove the captain in at half past eight on the third morning, through gates that opened for his car before we'd finished slowing, which told me the guards had orders about timing, which told me the captain kept a schedule, which told me — and here my blood did something complicated, part dread and part something I refuse to call excitement — that schedules could be learned.

"Wait with the car," Brandt said, gathering his gloves. "I may need you at eleven."

"Yes, Captain."

He went inside without looking back. Furniture, engine, coat rack. I parked in the gravel court, in the shade of the linden trees the street took its name from, where I had a view of the front door, the kitchen entrance, and the windows of what had been — I knew this because everyone in town knew it; Mrs. Delacour had given tours of her parlor the way other women gave concerts — the grand salon.

It was the parlor window that did it.

There are moments when you understand something has changed forever, and usually those moments arrive dressed as something small. Mine arrived dressed as paper. Through the long window of the salon, in the morning light, I saw stacks. Stacks and stacks of them, on tables brought in for the purpose, on the floor, on the piano — I recognized the piano, walnut, Challen & Sons, Mrs. Delacour's pride — and every stack was bound in the faded colors I knew better than I knew my own handwriting. Blue for births. Green for marriages. That particular municipal brown-gray for deaths. The register of the commune of Saint-Aubin-sur-Risle, forty years of it or more, piled in an enemy's parlor like firewood.

My hands were shaking on the wheel. I let them shake. There was nobody to see, and I have always believed a man should be permitted thirty seconds of honest terror per day, taken wherever he can find it.

Then the thirty seconds ended, and the cold practical corner of me said: *count.*

* * *

What follows, I set down plainly, because it is the plainest thing I know.

I sat in that car from nine until eleven, and I made myself useful to my own purposes the way a carpenter makes himself useful to a house. I cleaned the windscreen twice. I checked the tires. I ate a heel of bread. And every ten minutes or so, casually, the way any bored driver might glance at a window, I looked at the parlor and I learned it.

The registers had come in over two days — I'd seen the requisition carts pass the garage, but hadn't understood until now what the tarpaulins hid. They were stacked roughly by size, which meant somebody had sorted them, which meant a clerk. Clerks, plural, probably: no one man sorts forty years of a town's memory in an afternoon. Around ten o'clock a soldier in spectacles came to the window with a clipboard and began working down the stacks, and I watched him do it, and I timed him, because timing is free and everything else costs.

He worked methodically, left to right, top to bottom. He wrote on his clipboard after each volume. He paused longest at the brown-gray ones — the death registers, though God knew the deaths that mattered weren't in them. He worked until a quarter to twelve, then stopped, squared the last stack with both hands like a man tucking in a child, covered the whole arrangement with a dust sheet, and drew the curtains.

Covered. Curtained. Like furniture in a house whose owners aren't coming back.

I thought about that dust sheet more than I thought about anything else all week. You cover things you intend to keep. You cover things you're saving. But you also cover things you can't quite bear to look at yet, and I supposed an army couldn't quite bear to look at forty years of names it was planning to unname.

At noon Brandt came out, said "The mill, then lunch at the Lion d'Or," and got in without a glance. I drove him to the mill and listened to him talk flour quotas with a man who trembled so badly his pencil chattered against his pad, and at the Lion d'Or I sat in the car eating cold potatoes while the captain drank wine inside, and I thought: *the clerk with the spectacles works mornings. Nine to quarter-to-twelve. Which means if the burning happens, it happens after lunch, or on a morning he isn't there, or on orders that come from above him — and whoever carries those orders will be the thread. Pull the thread.*

That was the shape of my thinking now. Threads. Hours. Windows and curtains. Fear hadn't gone away — fear sat beside me in the car all day like a passenger I couldn't evict — but it had changed jobs. It used to be a siren. Now it was an accountant.

I am not sure which frightened me more.

* * *

In the afternoon Brandt surprised me by not needing the car, and surprised me further by forgetting I existed entirely, which was the best thing that happened to me all week, because it meant I had two hours in the court of the rue des Tilleuls with nothing to do but be furniture near a kitchen door.

Kitchens, I have found, are the true intelligence services of any occupied town. Armies seal parlors and post sentries, but kitchens must be supplied, and supply requires conversation, and conversation requires someone to complain to. The Delacours' cook had been dismissed in the first week — replaced by an army cook, a heavy placid woman from the north who missed her sister and disliked the captain's adjutant — and by half past two I had learned, in exchange for carrying two crates of onions and agreeing at length that adjutants were the same everywhere, the following:

One. The registers were indeed to be burned. She said it the way you'd say the drains needed seeing to, but she said it, and she said it had been mentioned at supper.

Two. The burning awaited a final order — "paperwork first, everything's paperwork with these ones" — and the paperwork belonged to the clerk with the spectacles, whose name was Weiss, and who ate alone and left his vegetables.

Three. Captain Brandt would be hosting a dinner within the fortnight — "a celebration, they're saying, for the peace, though you'd think a body might wait until there was one" — and the wine order alone was enough to make a woman question the future of civilization.

I thanked her for the onions' sake and walked back around to the court feeling as if I'd swallowed a live coal. Burned. It was to be *burned.* I had known it — I'd read the routing order with my own eyes, copied it word for word into my head where no one could search it — but knowing a thing and hearing a tired cook say it over a crate of onions are different animals. One is a document. The other is a fact with gravy on it.

Weiss, then. Clerk Weiss, spectacles, methodical, eats alone, leaves his vegetables. I turned him over in my mind like a coin I'd found on the pavement. A man who squares off his stacks before covering them. A man who pauses longest at the death registers. I didn't know what that meant yet. It might have meant diligence. It might have meant disgust. In my experience the two lived close together in quiet men, closer than anyone liked to admit, and the trick was knowing which side of the line a man would fall on when the fire was lit.

I would have to find out. And finding out meant getting inside that house, past the sentries, into the orbit of a clerk, wearing the smile of a man with no opinions about anything at all.

* * *

At five o'clock Brandt came out early, coat over his arm, moving fast, and barked: "The bridge road. Quickly."

I drove. Of course I drove. My hands did it without consulting me, and some distant part of me noted, filed, and hated them for it. The bridge road ran along the water, and as we came around the bend I saw the barriers, the wire, the sentries, the planks — I saw the place I had not been able to stop thinking about since the night of the shots, and I watched the captain's face in the mirror, and his face was nothing. That was the worst of it. Not anger, not grief, not even satisfaction. Nothing. A man looking at a bridge.

He spoke to the sergeant at the barrier for four minutes. I caught fragments through the open window — *revised schedule*, *materials*, *before the thaw* — and none of it made sense except in the way it made terrible sense. They weren't done with the bridge. Whatever had happened there, whatever the notice called bandit activity, the army intended to change the bridge itself, to alter the very stones, until the scene and the story matched.

On the drive back Brandt sat with his gloved hands folded and said, without warning, "You've driven this town long?"

"Six years, Captain."

"You'll know the households, then. Which ones hold grudges." A pause. "Useful."

"Very useful, sir," I said, and heard my voice do the agreeable thing again, and wished I could have said instead: *Sir, I keep a ledger of what your men have taken from these households, and one day it will be read aloud in a room you don't control.* But wishing is cheap, and I had six years of practice at swallowing expensive truths, so I swallowed this one too and drove us home through the falling dark.

At the gates he got out and then, for the first time in three days, actually looked at me. Directly. The way you look at a machine you suspect of making a new noise.

"You were at the house all afternoon," he said.

"With the car, Captain. As instructed."

"The cook says you carried onions."

"I'm happy to carry onions, sir."

Something moved behind his eyes — amusement, possibly, or its military cousin, contempt — and he grunted and went in. The gates closed behind him with a sound like a full stop.

I sat in the car in the dark court and let my heart hammer itself out against my ribs, and I laughed once, a short ugly laugh with no humor in it, because I had just been *noticed,* and being noticed was the one thing I could not afford, and the onion business — my one good piece of tradecraft — had gone and put my name in a captain's mouth.

But underneath the fear, cold as ever, patient as ever, the accountant in me was already writing up the day's entries:

*Registers: salon, south window. Sorted, listed, sheeted, curtained.*
*Clerk: Weiss. Mornings only. Leaves vegetables.*
*Burning: awaits final order. Dinner party planned — wine ordered — peace rumored.*
*Bridge: alterations scheduled. Before the thaw.*

*Driver: possibly compromised. Proceed carefully.*

I closed my eyes and saw the dust sheet settling over the registers like snow, and I thought: sheets come off. Sheets always come off eventually. The question was whether anything would be left under them when they did — and whether the man pulling the sheet would be Weiss, or Brandt, or me.

I started the engine. Behind me the house's windows glowed, warm and gold and full of enemies having supper, and somewhere in that glow sat forty years of my neighbors' births and marriages and deaths, waiting politely, in triplicate, for the match.

Two days later, Mrs. Vance asked me why I smelled of onions — and then asked me, in a voice I'd never heard her use before, exactly whose kitchen I'd been carrying them in.
