## 10. Mrs. Vance Suspects

The trouble with secrets, Hazel Almeida had learned, was that they multiplied. You started with one—a ledger under a floorboard, three names written in pencil—and before you knew it you had a stolen army routing order folded inside your boot heel, a copy of a copy in the lining of your coat, and a habit of copying things at odd hours that any sensible person would call reckless.

It was nearly midnight when he sat at the kitchen table with the lamp turned down to a thumb's-width flame, copying the requisition schedule he'd memorized from Captain Brandt's parlor. Which clerk. Which ledgers. Which mornings. His pen moved in small, careful strokes, the way it always did, as if neatness were a form of protection.

He heard the stair creak on the fourth step from the top.

There was no time to hide anything, so he did the only thing left: he kept writing, and turned his shoulders slightly, uselessly, as though a hundred and forty pounds of trembling chauffeur could constitute a wall.

Mrs. Vance came into the kitchen in her dressing gown and her boots—she never wore slippers; she said a woman who couldn't run in her shoes shouldn't own them—and stood looking at him for a long moment.

"Well," she said.

Hazel put down the pen. "Ma'am."

"You're up late."

"You too, ma'am."

"I heard my typewriter keys being imitated," she said, "in a hand considerably more careful than mine." She crossed the kitchen, took the lamp, and raised the wick without asking, flooding the table and all its guilt with honest light. Then she pulled out a chair and sat down across from him like a judge who had decided in advance to be merciful but wanted him to sweat a little first.

Hazel had driven Mrs. Vance for two years. He had listened to her dictate letters that could peel paint off a governor's door. He had watched her stare down a police captain so thoroughly that the man had apologized to *her*. But he had never seen her look at anyone quite the way she was looking at him now—not angry, exactly. Worse. *Interested.*

"May I?" she said, and reached for the page.

He let her take it. Arguing would have been worse.

She read it through once, slowly, her lips moving slightly on the clerk designations. Then she set it down, folded her hands, and said, "How long?"

"Ma'am?"

"How long have you been keeping records, Mr. Almeida? Because this is not the work of an amateur, and I have known amateurs." A dry pause. "I have *sponsored* amateurs."

Hazel considered lying. It lasted about a second and a half, which was, he would think later, about how long his courage had ever allowed him to be stupid.

"Three years," he said. "Since they took Tomas Ferro off the mill road and nobody wrote it down anywhere except me."

* * *

Miriam Vance had suspected for some time.

Not the ledger specifically—she hadn't known about the ledger, and that omission stung her more than she intended to admit, even privately, even in her own head where she kept her most honest minutes. What she had suspected was quieter and had accumulated the way damp accumulates in a wall: the boy read everything he carried. She'd caught him at it a dozen times, or rather caught the evidence of it—the way he could answer questions about correspondence he was never meant to open, the way widows' names made his jaw tighten, the way he drove home from certain deliveries silent as a held breath.

She had said nothing because she trusted no one, and a chauffeur who read mail was, by her arithmetic, a risk she could manage. She had spent thirty years managing risks: police spies in her lecture halls, informants in her reading circles, one memorable bishop who had attended three consecutive suffrage meetings under the transparent alias of "a concerned citizen of faith." Compared to those, a quiet young man who read other people's grief and apparently *wrote it down somewhere* had seemed almost benign.

But tonight there was a page on her kitchen table with army clerk rotations on it, copied in a hand steadier than her own nerves had been since the requisition began, and benign was no longer the word.

She had come downstairs intending something entirely different—tea, mostly, and a private hour with her own correspondence, which these days required privacy the way other people's required stamps. Instead she had found her driver committing what she guessed was treason, and found herself, unexpectedly, having to decide what she was.

She looked at him now across the lamp. Twenty-six, maybe twenty-seven. Thin as a fence post and about as expressive, until you knew where to look. She knew where to look; she had made a career of knowing where to look in people who'd been taught to keep still.

"Three years," she repeated. "And in three years you never thought to tell me."

"Tell you what, ma'am?"

"That you were doing it." She tapped the page. "This. Whatever else is under whatever floorboard you've got it under—don't look shocked, Mr. Almeida, everyone in this house knows the third-floor board squeaks and you're the only one who ever goes up there with a reason."

Hazel's face did something she'd never seen it do: it went briefly, humanly, mortifiedly young.

"It wasn't yours to be burdened with," he said.

That, she thought, was the most insulting sentence anyone had offered her in a decade, and she included in that category the entire provincial government.

"I have chained myself to railings," she said, with the dangerous mildness she saved for real occasions. "I have been jailed in four counties. I have been fed bread and water by a magistrate who wept while he did it, because even he was embarrassed. I have carried a petition with eleven thousand signatures to a parliament that then adjourned for shooting season. Do not tell me, Mr. Almeida, what I may or may not be burdened with. My shoulders are the only part of me that has never failed."

"Yes, ma'am," he said, and then, because he was honest to the bone and it leaked out of him sideways: "But it's different. Your war has rules. Petitions. Papers. If they catch me with this—" he touched the page, "—there's no magistrate weeping anywhere. There's a ditch behind the garrison wall and a notice about bandits."

Miriam considered that. It deserved consideration. The boy had a point, and she disliked him mildly for it, the way one dislikes anyone who says true things at inconvenient volumes.

"And yet," she said, "here we both are, at midnight, in my kitchen, looking at army paper. So it seems we are already in the same ditch, only standing at opposite ends of it, pretending not to know each other." She rose, went to the cupboard, and took down the good tea—the tin she hid behind the flour, which was a small hypocrisy she permitted herself—and set two cups on the table without asking whether he wanted any. Asking would have made it a courtesy. Not asking made it a fact.

"Sit properly," she said. "We're going to talk business."

* * *

They talked until nearly three.

Hazel told her everything, or very near it—the ledger, the bridge at dusk, the shots he couldn't account for, the widow Okafor's name spoken carefully in the middle of it like something breakable. Mrs. Vance listened the way she did everything: completely, taking no notes, which he understood after a while was itself a kind of mercy. Some things shouldn't exist on paper until paper was safe.

Then she told him about the network, and Hazel's whole understanding of his employer rearranged itself.

"The sewing circle," he said slowly. "The one in Marlow. That isn't a sewing circle."

"It is absolutely a sewing circle," Mrs. Vance said, with dignity. "Mrs. Prewitt produces an extraordinary number of hemmed sheets. It also moves letters, medicines, occasionally people, and information, along routes that the army has never successfully mapped, because the army cannot conceive that a map might pass through a hatbox." She sipped her tea. "We have moved things past checkpoints for six years, Mr. Almeida. Soldiers search men. They search luggage. They do not search deeply enough into the domestic lives of women, because they believe our lives are shallow. This has always been their error and it will be their epitaph."

"You want to move the ledger."

"I want to move *testimony*," she corrected. "Your ledger is a list. A list can be dismissed—anecdotes, rumors, the fevered bookkeeping of a sentimental chauffeur. Testimony requires witnesses, corroboration, and a reader with a press card. We happen to know of a journalist stranded at the Grand Hotel waiting for the armistice, and journalists, unlike magistrates, print things."

"Why?" Hazel asked. It came out blunter than he meant. "Why help me? You didn't have to come downstairs tonight. You could have waited until morning and fired me. Or reported me."

Mrs. Vance looked at him over the rim of her cup for a long time, and Hazel had the sense of a gate opening somewhere behind her eyes just far enough to see weather.

"Because I have spent thirty years trusting no one," she said, "and it has kept me safe, and it has kept me useless. There is a difference between caution and cowardice, and I am old enough now that I must audit which one I've been practicing." She set the cup down with a small decisive click. "You read every letter I've ever given you, and you never once sold me, betrayed me, or lied to me about a widow. That is a better record than most of the men I've worked with. I am not helping you, Mr. Almeida. I am investing. And I expect a return."

"What return?"

"The truth," she said simply, "on the record, in print, signed by someone with the nerve to publish it—before the armistice signs the rest of it away." She stood, gathered his copied pages, squared their edges against the table, and handed them back to him with the formality of a woman returning a hymnal. "The routes will need testing. Ruth Ellery—that's our journalist—cannot be approached directly; the hotel lobby has more ears than a cornfield. We'll go through Marlow, through Mrs. Prewitt, whose hats are legendary and whose hatboxes are bottomless."

Hazel took the pages and put them back inside his coat, and found that his hands were almost steady.

"Ma'am," he said, "you understand each other means nothing now. If they catch this, we hang together."

"My dear boy," Miriam Vance said, blowing out the lamp herself, "I have been hanged in effigy four times. It loses its charm. I should like to try the genuine article in better company."

* * *

At the door of the kitchen she paused, one hand on the frame, and Hazel saw her hesitate in a way she never hesitated anywhere else.

"The floorboard," she said, not turning around. "Third floor, under where you sleep. You should know that I dust up there on Tuesdays, and I have dusted around that board for three years without once lifting it."

Hazel felt the floor tilt very gently beneath him.

"You knew."

"I suspected," she corrected. "Knowing is what tonight was for." And then, over her shoulder, in a tone that was almost—almost—warm: "Good night, Mr. Almeida. We leave for Marlow at nine. Wear your driving coat. Mrs. Prewitt will want to meet the man whose handwriting is going to make history, and I warn you now, she will feed you until you surrender."

He lay awake long after, listening to the house settle, the ledger under its board no longer feeling like a secret but like cargo—something loaded, something bound for somewhere. Three names had become thirty. Thirty had become testimony. And somewhere across town, in a parlor stacked to the picture rails with his neighbors' lives, an army clerk was already sorting paper for the fire.

Nine o'clock, then. Marlow. A hatbox.

He fell asleep making lists, which was, as always, how he prayed.
