## 11. The Neutral Correspondent

The message came to us folded inside a pattern for knitted socks.

Mrs. Vance held it up to the lamp in the back of the motorcar, squinting at the grid of numbers and letters as though it were a particularly stubborn crossword. Outside, the rain had turned to sleet, and the wipers — the left one, anyway — kept a valiant, arrhythmic effort against the glass. *Squeak. Scrape. Squeak. Scrape.* I had long ago stopped noticing it, the way a man stops noticing his own heartbeat.

"It's from Adèle," Mrs. Vance said. Adèle ran the milliner's shop in Saint-Rémy and, in her spare hours, ran rather more than hats. "She says the socks are 'a lovely pattern, though the heel is difficult, and the wool must arrive before Thursday or the whole garment unravels.'"

I kept my eyes on the road. "Does it translate?"

"Everything translates, Mr. Almeida, if you know the language." She smoothed the paper on her knee. "The heel is the difficulty. The wool is the testimony. Thursday is—" She stopped. I heard her do arithmetic in the pause, and I did not like the sound of it.

"Thursday," I said.

"Or thereabouts. The armistice delegations are meeting at the Grand Hotel. If the victors sign first, and the record is sealed first, then whatever happened on that bridge becomes a footnote in somebody else's history, written in somebody else's ink." She folded the pattern away into her glove with the neatness of a woman who had spent thirty years making men underestimate the size of things she could fit into small spaces. "There is a journalist staying at the Grand. An American. Neutral passport, neutral byline, and — this is the important part — a reputation for being impossible to frighten."

"What's her name?"

"Ruth Ellery." Mrs. Vance said the name the way other people said the names of racehorses. "She was in the capital when the war started and simply never left. Files dispatches through the consulate. The authorities have tried twice to have her expelled and failed twice, because the paperwork, you'll be amused to hear, was wrong."

I was, in fact, amused. There is a particular pleasure in watching a bureaucracy trip over its own shoelaces.

"And she'll take it?" I asked. "Testimony. From a chauffeur."

"She'll take it from anyone who can tell her something true. That's rarer than you'd think, Hazel. Most people who come to her come to tell her something useful." Mrs. Vance looked out the window at the sleet. "You're going to tell her something costly. Those are different commodities entirely."

* * *

The reply went back through the network the way everything did: slowly, in pieces, disguised as things nobody wants to steal. A hem measurement here. A recipe for plum preserves there. I dictated my part of it sitting in the motorcar outside the church while Mrs. Vance attended a "sewing circle" that had never, in my acquaintance, produced a single sock.

I wrote: *I have a ledger of names. Forty-one entries. I have a copy of an order routing the town's records to army headquarters for destruction. I have the name of the clerk who handles them and the hour. I was present on the river road the night the bridge was sealed. I can describe the trucks, the officers, the number of shots. I am nobody. That is the point of me.*

It took Adèle's network two days to answer, which was two days of me driving Captain Brandt's officers to their dinners and smiling with my teeth while my hands on the wheel did the only honest work available to them. Two days of Mrs. Vance dictating letters about votes for women in a voice like a bell while I carried, folded against my chest, the knowledge that the answer might be *no*.

The answer came on a Tuesday, in the form of a grocery list.

*Butter. Eggs. Flour. Tea — the good kind, not the army kind. And a note: I am told you keep accounts. I keep a different kind. Come tell me what's in yours. Room 214. The Grand Hotel keeps excellent tea and terrible security. Ask at the desk for the lady who complains about the plumbing.*

"She wants to meet," Mrs. Vance said, reading over my shoulder in the garage, where we had gone to be private among the smell of oil and hay. "Hazel, do you understand what she's offering? A neutral correspondent, on the record, with a wire out of the country."

"I understand what she's offering." I put the grocery list in my breast pocket, over the ledger, where it sat like a small warm coal. "What I don't understand is why."

Mrs. Vance gave me one of her looks — the kind that made cabinet ministers, in her telling, sit up straighter. "Because she's a journalist, and journalists are vultures, and thank God for it. Because a massacre without a witness is a rumor, and a rumor without a name is nothing. Or because—" and here her voice did something quieter, "—because someone has to. Hazel, the whole world is full of people who could have done something. It is chronically understaffed with people who did."

* * *

I went on Thursday, in the motorcar, because a chauffeur arriving at the Grand Hotel is furniture, and furniture is not searched.

I had dressed the part. Mrs. Vance had insisted on inspecting me before I left, tugging my collar flat, straightening my cap, stepping back with her head tilted like a painter considering whether a canvas was finished. "You look," she pronounced, "respectably invisible."

"Thank you."

"It was a compliment."

"I know," I said. "That's the sad part."

The Grand Hotel sat on the square like a fat cat that had decided the war was beneath its notice. Its windows were lit. Its doorman wore a uniform cleaner than any soldier's. Inside, the lobby smelled of coffee and floor wax and money, and officers of three different nations stood about pretending not to look at one another, which is the national sport of diplomats.

I asked at the desk for the lady who complains about the plumbing.

The clerk — a young man with the weary eyes of someone who had heard every possible request and found all of them tiresome — did not blink. "Third floor," he said. "She's been at it since Tuesday."

Room 214 was on the second floor. I noted this discrepancy and decided I liked her already.

She opened the door before I finished knocking, which meant she'd been watching the corridor, which meant she was careful, and I have always trusted careful people more than charming ones.

She was small and round and fifty-ish, with spectacles that magnified her eyes to the size of a curious owl's, and she wore a cardigan that had clearly lost a long war against its elbows. She looked me up and down once.

"You're the chauffeur."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Vance's chauffeur."

"Yes, ma'am."

"The one who reads her mail."

I felt the floor tilt slightly. "She told you that?"

"She told me you were trustworthy. In her language, that's the same thing." Ruth Ellery stepped back and opened the door wide. "Come in before someone sees you looking honest. It's conspicuous in here."

The room was a battlefield. Papers everywhere — on the bed, on the floor, weighted down with teacups and a shoe. A typewriter sat on the writing desk like a small iron altar, and beside it, an actual teapot, actual tea, actual cups. The good kind, not the army kind.

"Sit," she said, and I sat, on the one chair that appeared to have been cleared by intention rather than accident. She poured tea without asking, and I drank it without ceremony, and for a moment we were just two people having tea, which is how, I have since learned, most important things begin.

Then she pulled a notebook from the wreckage of her bed, opened it to a fresh page, and looked at me over her spectacles.

"Right," she said. "Tell me what's in your ledger."

* * *

I had rehearsed it, of course. In the garage, in the dark, driving Brandt's officers through the rain — I had rehearsed it the way you rehearse anything you're afraid will come out wrong. I had planned to begin with the order, the routing of the records, the clean bureaucratic sentence that proved intent.

Instead I heard myself say: "The first name in it is Tomas Ferreol."

She didn't write yet. She just waited, pen above the page, and I understood that this was the whole art of her: the waiting.

"He was a farrier's son. Fifteen years old. The patrol took him in March for conscription — they wrote down eighteen, because eighteen is legal and fifteen is a scandal. His mother came to the livery three times asking if anyone had seen him. The third time I wrote his name down, because I didn't know what else to do with the fact of her." I turned my teacup in its saucer. "He's still on the list. Nobody's come back to cross him off."

Ruth Ellery wrote. Her pen moved fast, and her face did nothing at all, which I recognized — it was my own face, the one I wore driving for Brandt. We were the same kind of animal, she and I. People who kept still on the outside while everything important happened underneath.

"Go on," she said.

I went on. I told her about the ledger — forty-one names, and now forty-four, because of the bridge. I told her about the requisition order, the records "for audit," the mayor's trembling gratitude. I told her about the notice on the post: *bandit activity*, and the shots I'd counted from the river road, and the arithmetic that wouldn't come out to anything a reasonable man could live with.

She stopped me once, near the beginning. "Why you?" she asked. Not unkindly. "You drive a car. You could drive it in the other direction."

I thought about the honest answer, and then I gave it to her, because she had earned it with her terrible plumbing and her good tea. "Because I read the letters," I said. "All of them. For two years. I know things about this town that the town doesn't know about itself. And when the army took the records, I understood that I wasn't holding gossip anymore. I was holding the only copy." I met her owl-eyes. "A man who is the only copy of something has a job, whether he applied for it or not."

She wrote that down too. Word for word, I suspect. Journalists are magpies for a good sentence.

Then she set down her pen, and the room changed temperature.

"Here is my difficulty," she said. "My dispatches go out through the consulate wire. Once the armistice is signed, this town will be inside the victors' administrative zone, and everything in it — every record, every witness, every grave — becomes subject to their review. If I file after the signing, my dispatch can be delayed, edited, or 'lost' for weeks. By the time it prints, the official history will already have its boots on." She leaned forward. "If I file *before* the signing, it goes out under the old rules, and once a thing is in print on three continents, even generals can't unpublish it. Print, Mr. Almeida, is the only afterlife I can offer anyone."

"How long?" I asked. "Before the signing?"

"Nobody knows. Days. Possibly tomorrow. Possibly a week — these negotiations move like glaciers with egos." She took off her spectacles and rubbed the bridge of her nose, and for a moment she looked every one of her fifty-odd years and every mile of this long stupid war. "So. I will take your testimony. All of it. I'll type it myself, tonight if you like, and I'll corroborate every name against your ledger page by page. But you have to get it here. All of it. The ledger, the order, the clerk's information — the whole account, in my hands, before the pens come out at that table downstairs."

"And if I can't?"

"Then I'll print what I have, and it will be a shadow of the truth, and shadows," she said, "are how massacres get to be footnotes."

The sleet had started again outside the window, ticking against the glass like a small impatient clock. Downstairs, faintly, I could hear an orchestra tuning. The Grand Hotel was hosting a concert for the delegates. The world was preparing to celebrate the end of the war, and I was sitting in room 214 learning that the end of the war was also a deadline.

"I can have it here," I said. "In three days. Maybe less."

Her eyebrows went up. "The roads are closed after dark. There are checkpoints on every route out of your town. Brandt's garrison holds the bridge and the mill road both."

"Yes," I said.

"You've thought about how."

"I've thought about nothing else for a week, ma'am."

Ruth Ellery looked at me for a long moment — the owl assessing the mouse, or possibly the mouse assessing the owl; I genuinely could not tell which of us was which — and then she picked up her pen.

"Three days," she said. "I'll keep the wire warm. And Mr. Almeida—" she tapped the notebook, where my sentences about the only copy sat in her quick shorthand, "—when this is done and printed, I'm going to want your name in the article. A testimony without a witness is just a rumor with better grammar."

"I'd rather not."

"I know," she said, not unsympathetically. "Think about it. People believe names. It's the whole reason we're in this mess."

* * *

Mrs. Vance was waiting in the motorcar two streets from the hotel, under the one gas lamp on the street that still worked, and when I slid in behind the wheel she didn't ask anything. She just looked at me in the rearview mirror, and I said, "She'll take it. But it has to be in her hands before the signing."

"Then we have our deadline," Mrs. Vance said. She settled back against the seat and pulled her gloves on, finger by finger, and I watched her in the mirror become, visibly, a general. "The mill road checkpoint is the problem. Adèle's courier tried it Tuesday."

"I know," I said. "The network went quiet after."

"Then it can't be the mill road."

"No," I agreed. "It has to be a road nobody checks. A road with a reason to be checked and a reason to be waved through." I started the engine, and it caught on the second try, coughing in the cold. "Mrs. Vance, how would you feel about your chauffeur driving Captain Brandt to the hotel himself?"

There was a silence from the back seat. It was the kind of silence that in a smaller woman would have been a shriek.

"You want to hide the ledger," she said slowly, "in the car of the man burning it."

"In the spare tire well. Under the jack. Where no officer in history has ever looked, because officers don't look at the parts of a car that do the work." I pulled out into the empty street, toward home, toward the garage, toward the floorboard and the ledger sleeping under it with its forty-four names. "They trust me, Mrs. Vance. It's the one advantage of being furniture."

"And if they search?"

I drove through the sleet and didn't answer, because there wasn't an answer, and we both knew it, and the lamp behind us shrank in the mirror until the road ahead was just the road.

That night I took up the floorboard one last time and read the ledger through, all of it, every name, out loud, in the dark, so that if the worst happened on the mill road or any road, the words would at least have been said once by a living voice. Tomas Ferreol. The widow Okafor's boy. All forty-four.

Then I wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, found the spare tire iron, and began, very carefully, to make a chauffeur's car into an ark.
