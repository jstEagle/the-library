## 13. Brandt's Dinner Party

The summons came at nine in the morning, in the voice of a corporal who knocked on Mrs. Vance's door as though the door had personally offended him.

"Captain Brandt requires the motorcar this evening. Seven o'clock. Officers' mess at the Delacroix house. Formal."

"Formal," Mrs. Vance repeated, when the corporal had clattered back down the steps. She was standing in the parlor with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she reserved for newspaper editorials about women's constitutionally delicate nerves. "They are going to celebrate an armistice that hasn't been signed yet. They're going to drink to peace out of glasses requisitioned from the Delacroixs, who are living in two rooms above their own bakery."

"The Delacroixs make very good bread," Hazel said. It was not a useful observation. It was simply the truest thing he could manage before his thoughts ran somewhere he couldn't afford to follow.

Mrs. Vance looked at him over her spectacles. She had a way of looking that made Hazel feel like one of her letters — opened, read, and filed. "You'll be driving them."

"I'll be driving them."

"And you'll keep your face the way you keep it now."

"This face?"

"That one exactly," she said. "The face of a man thinking about bread."

She turned back toward her study, then paused. "Hazel." She almost never used his first name; it landed like a coin dropped in a quiet church. "Whatever you see tonight — whatever you hear — you come home afterward. That is your only order. Everything else you do is your own affair, and I have never asked, and I am not asking now. But you come home."

"Yes, ma'am," said Hazel, who had already decided, some weeks ago, that coming home was the minimum of his ambitions and that the maximum was something no one had ordered him to do at all.

* * *

He spent the afternoon with the car, because the car was the one thing in his life that responded to effort with improvement. He polished the bonnet until it held a warped reflection of the chestnut trees in the square. He checked the tires twice, including the spare nested in its well beneath the boot floor — a hollow space, lined with rubber, roughly the dimensions of a hymnal and a folded sheaf of papers. He did not put anything in it. He simply looked at it the way a man looks at an empty drawer while deciding what his life will need next.

At half past six he drove to the commandant's house, which had belonged to the Perrin family before the war and belonged to Captain Brandt during it. The Perrins' rose garden had been trampled into a motor pool. Their drawing room curtains hung in the windows like bandages on a head wound.

Brandt emerged at seven precisely, gloves on, cap set at the angle of a man who had never once doubted the mirror. Behind him came Lieutenant Sorrel, young and pink-cheeked, carrying a leather document case, and Sergeant Vasseur, who was broader than the doorway and took it personally.

"Almeida," Brandt said, settling into the back seat without looking at him. "You know the Delacroix house?"

"The bakery, sir. By the mill lane."

"Drive as though the evening mattered."

Hazel drove as though the evening mattered. The streets were curfewed and empty, lit at intervals by lamps that had survived the requisition of their oil, and the car's headlamps carved long nervous cones through the dusk. In the back seat, Sorrel was whispering something to Vasseur, and Vasseur laughed — a short, satisfied sound, the laugh of a man being told a joke whose punchline is someone else's bad news.

Hazel adjusted the mirror a half-inch and kept his eyes on the road and thought about bread. Sourdough, specifically, from the Delacroix ovens, which used a starter older than the mayor and produced loaves with a crust that cracked like river ice in March. He thought about the smell of it at four in the morning. He thought about it so thoroughly that his hands stayed level on the wheel and his breathing stayed even, and if anyone had asked what the chauffeur was thinking, they would have seen only a man fond of bread.

That was the trick he'd learned, and he hated that he'd had to learn it: rage was a fuel like any other. You didn't burn it in the open. You banked it. You kept it covered, hot, patient, ready for the one morning it would be worth something.

* * *

The Delacroix house glowed. That was the word for it — every window amber, every curtain gone, the furniture rearranged into a salon, the Delacroixs' good silver marching down a tablecloth that had certainly never lived in that house. Two soldiers stood at the door taking officers' coats. Somewhere inside, a piano played something by a composer who would have objected to the company.

A staff sergeant pointed Hazel around back, where the other drivers waited among the delivery carts and the coal shed: a lieutenant's driver chewing a toothpick, and old Fenouil, who drove for the quartermaster and had driven for the Delacroixs before the war and now drove for the men who slept in the Delacroixs' beds, a progression he described to anyone who would listen as "a career."

"Evening, Almeida," Fenouil said. "You get fed?"

"I get fed."

"Because they don't feed us until the tenth toast, and by then the cook's given everything warm to the adjutant's dog." He jerked a thumb at the kitchen door. "Madame Delacroix is back there supervising her own kitchen at bayonet-point. She slipped me two rolls. I'm telling you as a friend: get in line near the kitchen door around eight."

"Is that allowed?"

"Nothing is allowed," Fenouil said placidly. "That's what makes it a party."

Hazel found a place against the coal shed with a view of the kitchen door and the lighted windows above it. From where he sat he could hear fragments of the dinner whenever the piano paused — the crash of some utensil, laughter with too many men in it, the rising cadence of toasts. He counted the cars in the yard. Six. Six drivers, six officers or sets of them, and among the coats he'd watched go through the door he was fairly sure he'd seen the collar insignia of Colonel Aumont's staff, which meant the men from the bridge night were inside. He had memorized those collars the way other people memorize constellations.

At eight, the kitchen door opened on a wedge of steam and light, and Madame Delacroix herself appeared — a small woman with flour to her elbows and the eyes of somebody doing arithmetic she didn't like. She saw Hazel, assessed him in one pass (chauffeur, young, alone, probably hungry), and put two rolls in his hand with the fierce discretion of a smuggler.

"You eat," she said. "And you drive carefully. My husband says the soldiers' drivers kill more chickens than the artillery."

"Thank you, madame."

"My ovens," she added, quietly, to no one in particular, and went back inside.

Hazel ate the rolls slowly, methodically, grateful in a way that hurt. Warmth helped. Bread helped. He had learned that too: you kept something human in your stomach or the cold got ideas.

* * *

It was nearly ten when the kitchen door failed to latch properly behind the last of the serving girls, and Hazel, walking the perimeter of the yard because a driver walks when he has nothing else to do and looks purposeful doing it, found himself close enough to the gap to hear the dining room clearly. He did not decide to listen. He decided, afterwards, that listening had happened to him, the way weather happens.

The piano had stopped. A man — Aumont, by the wet gravel timbre of the voice — was holding forth.

"—and I'll tell you the truth, gentlemen, the armistice can't come soon enough for my clerks. Do you know what we're sitting on? Fourteen months of requisitions, two hundred and eleven executions files—"

"Nineteen hundred pages of grain levies alone," another voice put in, younger, aggrieved. "My wrist has a permanent crease from writing *requisitioned in good order*."

Laughter. Glasses. Hazel stood very still beside the coal shed, in the dark, in the exact posture of a man checking a tire.

"And the town records," Aumont continued, and Hazel's heart performed a maneuver he felt in his teeth. "Don't get me started. The audit found discrepancies. Discrepancies! As though the town kept books like a monastery. Half those ledgers contradict each other and the other half contradict us."

"They'll be sorted," Brandt said. His voice carried differently than the others — less wine in it, more wire. "Everything gets sorted before the delegation arrives. Nothing leaves this sector that reflects poorly on the command."

"Sorted," someone repeated, delighted by the word. "Is that what we call burning things now?"

"It's what we've always called it," Brandt said. "We just used to call it promptly."

More laughter — but Hazel noted, filing it away with the terrible precision that had become his whole character, that the laughter had a seam in it. One voice had not joined. It coughed instead, an awkward, muffled cough, the sound of a man swallowing something. He couldn't place it. Someone junior, seated low, near the water pitcher rather than the bottles.

"And the bridge business?" That was Vasseur, casual as a man asking after a mutual friend. "All squared away? Because my corporal swears a fisherman found something at the weir last week, and the story's making rounds."

A silence, brief as a held breath.

"The bridge business," Aumont said, "is a paperwork problem, and paperwork problems resolve themselves." A glass touched wood. "To resolution."

"To resolution," the room echoed.

"To the armistice," someone added, "and to peace — real peace, gentlemen, with proper archives."

The toast went around again. Hazel stood in the dark with his back against the coal shed and let the rage arrive, all of it, the full tonnage of it, and held it perfectly still.

This was the thing he understood now, standing there — the thing the ledger had taught him entry by entry, name by name: they were not monsters. That would have been simpler. Monsters could be hated cleanly, the way children hate wolves. These were men who toasted peace with proper archives, who worried about their wrists, who laughed at their own jokes, who had sealed a bridge at dusk and shot people into a river and then, months later, over stolen wine, called the aftermath a paperwork problem. The horror wasn't that they didn't know what they'd done. The horror was that they knew exactly, and their consciences weighed it in ledgers, and the ledgers came out favorable, and they drank to themselves.

*Paperwork problems,* Hazel thought. *I am your paperwork problem.* He was carrying, at that moment, in a notebook against his ribs, forty-one names that contradicted them. He was, in the strictest sense, a discrepancy.

The thought steadied him the way nothing else had. Not comfort — direction. They feared paper. Paper was the one thing they respected enough to destroy. Which meant paper was the one thing that could hurt them, and he was made of it, and they had just told him the schedule.

*Before the delegation arrives.* Before the victors signed and sealed the record. There it was again — the deadline, ticking under the toasts like a second clock nobody else could hear.

* * *

The party broke up past midnight. Officers emerged pink-faced into the cold, embracing each other with the unearned tenderness of the very drunk, and Hazel brought the car to the door and held it steady while Brandt and Sorrel poured themselves in. Vasseur went off with another driver, still laughing.

"Almeida," Brandt said from the back seat, as they pulled onto the mill lane. "Did you eat?"

"There were rolls, sir."

"Good. Good drivers are hard to find." He said it generously, the way a man pets a dog. Then, after a moment, in the tone of someone continuing a conversation Hazel hadn't been invited to: "Sorrel. Tomorrow morning, the audit crates from the mairie go to the courtyard. All of them. Have the detail report at six."

"All of them, sir?" Sorrel's voice had a careful blankness in it. "Even the—"

"*All* of them, Lieutenant."

"Yes, sir."

Hazel drove through the sleeping town with his headlights off along the boulevard — regulations, apparently, were also a paperwork matter — and delivered his passengers to the commandant's house at twelve-forty. Brandt got out, paused with one hand on the door, and looked back. For a heartbeat Hazel thought the captain's eyes went to the boot of the car, to the spare tire well that held nothing tonight but its own clean emptiness.

"Young man," Brandt said. "You've been in this town your whole life?"

"Yes, sir."

"Do you love it?"

Hazel considered the question with a driver's honesty. "I know where everything is, sir."

Brandt smiled faintly at that, whatever it meant to him, and went inside. The door closed. The lamplight in the Perrin windows went out one by one.

Hazel sat in the cold car with his hands on the wheel and let the stillness drain out of him slowly, the way a boiler cools. Then he took out his notebook — right there, in front of the house, because the audacity of it was a kind of armor — and wrote, in his smallest hand:

*Tonight, Delacroix house. Aumont, Brandt, et al. "All records to the courtyard." Detail reports 0600. They are burning everything tomorrow.*

He stopped. Added, underneath:

*One officer at dinner did not laugh.*

He didn't have a name yet. Just a cough, a seat near the water pitcher, junior insignia, and the particular quality of a silence that has a conscience inside it. But Hazel had spent a winter learning to read people the way he read letters — for what they carried between the lines — and he knew that sound. Somebody in that room was already composing his own private discrepancy.

Tomorrow at six, the courtyard. Which left tonight.

Hazel started the engine and drove home through the empty streets, planning how a man might get himself assigned, at dawn, to drive a captain's clerk.
