## 14. The Clerk Who Kept Copies

The rain had been coming down for three days, the kind of patient, gray rain that made the whole town feel like it had been left out on a shelf and forgotten. I drove Captain Brandt to the telegraph office and back, to the quartermaster's and back, to a farmhouse outside Mill Road where he spent forty minutes behind a closed door and came out looking satisfied in the way that never meant anything good for anybody. I held the door for him. I said "sir" at the correct intervals. I kept my face arranged into the pleasant blankness that had kept me alive this long.

And all the while, in the pocket of my driving coat, against my hip, there was a folded piece of paper that weighed approximately nothing and approximately everything.

I should start further back.

* * *

The clerk's name — the name he gave me, anyway, though I have since doubted everything about that conversation except the paper itself — was Weiss. Junior Clerk Weiss, third desk from the window in the requisition office, a narrow young man with ink-stained fingers and the posture of someone who had been told to stand straighter so many times that he'd simply stopped hearing it.

I had noticed him weeks before he ever noticed me, or rather, before he ever decided to let me notice him noticing me. That is a distinction that matters in an occupied town. He had the habit, when officers weren't looking, of squinting at the ledgers in front of him as if they smelled bad. Not the bored squint of a tired man. The squint of a man reading something he wished he hadn't.

I know that squint. I invented it.

He appeared at the motorcar one gray afternoon while I waited outside the requisition office for Brandt, who was inside arguing about tonnage of flour with a man whose signature he needed and whose soul he did not intend to pay for. Weiss approached under the pretense of checking a delivery manifest, which we both knew was a fiction, because there was no delivery, and the manifest he carried was upside down.

"You're the driver," he said. "Vance's driver."

"I'm Mrs. Vance's driver," I agreed, since correcting him would have required explaining what I actually was, and I wasn't sure myself anymore.

"The American lady. The one who gives speeches." He glanced at the office windows. Rain ran down them in ropes. "She has opinions."

"She has several," I said. "She keeps them in good repair."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth that was almost a smile and then thought better of it. "My mother reads about her in the smuggled papers," he said. "She thinks Mrs. Vance is very brave." A pause, in which a great deal was transacted without either of us signing for it. "My mother is wrong, of course. Officially."

"Of course," I said. "Officially."

Then Brandt's voice barked something from inside, and Weiss snapped upright like a puppet whose string had been yanked, and walked away carrying his upside-down manifest, and I sat in the cold car and thought: *that was either nothing, or it was the most dangerous conversation I've had since the bridge.*

With Weiss, it was never nothing.

* * *

He came back four more times over two weeks, always under cover of some errand, always with thirty seconds of talk that sounded like weather to anyone watching. He learned that I liked coffee, which was true, and that I drove the same route every Tuesday, which was also true, and that I could be found alone between nine and ten in the morning outside the Hotel Metropole, which was truest of all.

On the fifth visit he didn't talk about coffee.

"There's a burn order," he said. His voice was so quiet the rain nearly took it. "For the municipal records. All of them. It comes down from headquarters — not this headquarters, the real one, up the river. They want it done before the armistice. Before anyone can—" He stopped himself. Looked at his shoes. "Before anyone can ask to see them."

I kept my hands on the wheel. Ten and two, like a man with no thoughts at all beyond the proper operation of a motorcar.

"That seems like a lot of paper to burn," I said.

"It's four hundred pages of grain allotments," he said. "And it's also—" and here his voice did something I will remember until I die, it dropped into a register below fear, below anger, into the flat place where people go when they have run out of ways to say a thing politely — "it's also the list. From the bridge. The one they typed up afterward. The one that says *bandits* where names should be."

The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the street a sentry stamped his feet against the cold, one-two, one-two, like the town's heart beating in a uniform.

"They made a list," I said carefully, "of the men they killed on the bridge."

"And then they wrote *bandits* next to every name," Weiss said. "Twenty-three entries. I typed the fair copy myself." He looked at me then, directly, and his eyes were wet and furious and about nineteen years old. "I typed the word *bandit* twenty-three times. Do you know what that does to a person? Typing a lie twenty-three times?"

"No," I said. "But I know what it does to read one twenty-three times."

He stared at me. And I understood that we had just exchanged the only currency that mattered in that town, which was the admission of what we each already knew, and that from this moment forward neither of us was invisible anymore, and that I should have felt afraid, and did, distantly, the way you feel cold through several layers of wool.

Brandt came out of the requisition office then, slamming the door hard enough to rattle its glass, and Weiss became a statue of bureaucratic diligence, and I opened the captain's door, and we drove away, and I did not look back at the third desk from the window, because looking back is how people get caught, and I intended to be caught never.

* * *

For six days after that, Weiss did not come to the car, and I told myself it was better this way, and did not believe myself for a moment. I went through my routines. I drove. I read the mail I carried — Mrs. Vance's correspondence had thinned now that half her correspondents were under surveillance, but enough still moved through my hands to keep my ledger fed. At night, by candle stub, I added entries. A miller's son taken for questioning on the fourth. Two brothers from the tannery whose shop had been "requisitioned" along with the brothers themselves. The boy from the choir, fifteen years old, who had been conscripted in September and whose mother still set a place for him at table, which she showed me once when I delivered a letter, and which I have never managed to stop seeing.

Twenty-three bandits. I had three of their names already, gathered from widows and whispers. Twenty blanks waiting in my ledger like pulled teeth.

On the seventh day it rained harder, and Weiss came back.

He came at nine-forty in the morning, outside the Metropole, exactly as advertised, and he did not loiter or pretend to check manifests. He walked past the car at normal speed, and as he passed, something dropped through the open window into my lap — a small, flat package wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string, the size of a hymnal — and he kept walking, and he said, without turning his head, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather:

"Burn order's signed. Tomorrow week. Maybe sooner."

And then he turned the corner by the pharmacy and I never saw him again.

Not that day, not the next, not ever. I asked, in my careful sideways fashion, among the people who move things quietly from hand to hand in an occupied town. Nobody had seen him. The requisition office said Junior Clerk Weiss had been transferred upriver. There was no record of the transfer. There was, by then, very little record of anything; that was rather the point of the season. Whether they caught him, whether he ran, whether he is today a grocer in some neutral country with a new name and a hatred of typewriters — I don't know. I have made my peace with not knowing, which is not the same as being at peace.

But I know what he left in my lap.

* * *

I waited until I was home, door barred, shutters drawn, candle lit, before I untied the string. My hands were steadier than I expected them to be. Fear, I have learned, is like weather: it arrives, it does its work, it moves on, and what remains is whatever you built before it came.

Inside the oilcloth were two things.

The first was a single sheet of thin army paper, copied in a neat clerk's hand — not typed, written, which meant he had done it himself, late, by hand, risking his neck with a pen instead of a machine because a machine leaves a ribbon and a ribbon leaves evidence. It was headed with nothing, dated with nothing, signed with nothing. It began:

---

*Copy of page list, Bridge Incident, 23 October. Fair copy filed HQ Records, ref. B-114. Original order directs destruction of all municipal records incl. above upon receipt of Armistice Confirmation or 1 December, whichever first.*

*List follows:*

1. Aldous Perrin, wheelwright, b. 1889
2. Tomas Okafor, apprentice printer, b. 1899
3. Henri Lescalle, fishmonger
4. Emil Brandt-Faur, laborer
5. Josef Kranz, student
6. Peter Voss, bargeman
7. Anton Riehl, shoemaker
8. Lucien Aubry, carpenter
9. Marek Dolezal, baker
10. Stefan Dolezal, baker (brother of above)
11. Gaspard Noyon, ferryman
12. Wilhelm Ott, tailor
13. Daniel Ferro, mason
14. Isaac Ferro, mason (brother of above)
15. Pavel Sorin, railway worker
16. Jean-Baptiste Coll, farmer
17. Otto Lindqvist, dockhand
18. Rafael Cruz, teacher
19. Miklos Horthy-Vas, blacksmith
20. Samuel Okonkwo, dockhand
21. Ernst Kellner, waiter
22. Baptiste Roux, bargeman
23. Name illegible in original; body unidentified; entered as "unknown male, approx. 17 yrs"

*Note appended to file by Capt. R. Brandt: "All subjects bandits. No civilian involvement. File complete."*

*I certify this copy true. — [unsigned]*

---

The second thing in the oilcloth was a note, on cheaper paper, in the same careful hand, and this one I reproduce exactly as it was written, because some documents deserve to survive verbatim:

---

*Friend —*

*I cannot type this one. Ribbons are counted. You will understand.*

*Twenty-two names and one unknown. The unknown was maybe seventeen. Somebody knows who he was. Maybe you do. Ask the river.*

*They will call them bandits in the official history. Paper burns. What you do with this is your business. I am only the man who typed the lie too many times.*

*If I am gone when you read this, do not look for me. Looking is how they find people. Burn this note. Keep the list. Tell the woman who gives speeches that my mother was right about her after all.*

*— W.*

---

I sat there for a long time with the candle burning down and the rain working at the shutters, and I confess that the first thing I felt was not nobility or resolve or any of the things a person ought to feel holding twenty-three names rescued from a fire. The first thing I felt was shame — hot, specific, humiliating shame — because for months I had believed myself the keeper of the town's true memory, the secret historian, the man with the ledger under the floorboard, and here was proof that I had been keeping a diary while the actual record sat in an army filing cabinet with a reference number.

Three names I had. He had given me twenty-three.

Then the shame did what shame does in people who are built for endurance: it turned into fuel. I got my ledger out from under the floorboard. I opened it to the bridge pages — three entries, three grieving families, three names spelled the way grief spells them, slightly wrong, remembered secondhand. And I corrected them. Tomas Okafor, apprentice printer, born 1899 — I had him down as a typesetter, born 1900. Henri Lescalle, fishmonger — I had no surname at all, only "the fish man from the lane." One by one, the blanks filled. The pulled teeth went back in.

Number twenty-three I could not fill. *Unknown male, approx. 17 yrs.* Somebody's son. Somebody had cooked supper for that boy the night before the bridge, and washed his shirt, and told him to be home early, and he had gone out and become a line item.

Weiss had known it too. *Ask the river,* he wrote. Which meant he knew something about the river, or suspected it, or — more likely — he had processed enough paperwork in that office to understand that when an army seals a bridge at dusk and reports "bandit activity" at dawn, the arithmetic of bodies versus paperwork rarely balances, and the difference goes into the water.

I copied the list twice. One copy went into the ledger. One went into the lining of my coat, sewn flat against the small of my back, where a man carries nothing worth finding. The original I did not burn, despite the instruction. I know, I know — he said burn it, and disobeying a dead-or-vanished man's safety instructions is the height of foolishness. But I put it inside the spine of Mrs. Vance's old suffrage pamphlet, *Votes and the Working Woman*, which no soldier in the history of armies has ever voluntarily opened, and I reasoned that a document certifying itself true deserved better than smoke. Sentiment, perhaps. Or perhaps I understood even then that someday someone would need to see the handwriting — the human, hurried, guilty handwriting — and not merely the words.

Then I blew out the candle and lay in the dark and listened to the rain, and did the arithmetic that had become my nightly prayer:

Twenty-three names. One journalist at the Grand Hotel. Nine days, maybe fewer, before the burn order was executed. One route across occupied lines that had already failed once, at Mill Road, at the cost of a courier whose name I carried too. One motorcar. One chauffeur whom nobody saw.

And somewhere in that arithmetic, unaccounted for, a junior clerk with ink-stained fingers who had typed a lie twenty-three times and then spent his truth to buy it back.

*Tell the woman who gives speeches that my mother was right about her after all.* In the morning, I decided, I would tell her. She would want to know that she had believers inside the machine. And she would need to know the rest of it too — that the clock had a date on it now, that the fire had a schedule, that our window was closing like a shop at dusk.

But first — before the telling, before the planning, before the risk — there was something else. Something the clerk had planted in me with five words, and it would not leave me alone.

*Ask the river.*

It was raining still. It would rain tomorrow. The river would be high and brown and indifferent, and the bank below the bridge would be mud and reeds and whatever the water had finished with and returned.

Tomorrow night, I told the dark ceiling. Tomorrow night I would walk down and see what the river keeps.

Because number twenty-three was somebody's son, and I had promised a widow once that I would get the names right, and I had learned since then that getting a name right sometimes means going where the names wash ashore.
