## 16. Betrayal in the Choir Loft

Corinne Badeau had learned to type in her father's notary office, where the rule was: accuracy above all, and never read what you type. The first rule had made her employable. The second, she was violating magnificently.

She sat in the choir loft of Saint-Aubin's with a typewriter on a folding table between the organ pipes and the rail, typing out testimony that would, if everything went well, end several careers and possibly begin a war-crimes trial. If everything went badly, it would simply end her. She found that thinking about the second possibility too long made her fingers fumble, so she thought instead about the ribbon. The ribbon was nearly spent. She'd have to ask Hazel for another, which meant another meeting, which meant another set of risks, which meant —

No. The ribbon. Think about the ribbon.

*...the boy was approximately fifteen years of age, wearing a butcher's apron, identified by the widow Okafor as Thomas Okafor, born the fourth of March...*

Below her, the church was empty except for Father Aubry, who swept the same three pews over and over with the diligence of a man who had decided that if he could not save the town he could at least keep its floor tidy. He was their cover. A church that was being swept was a church that was merely being swept. Soldiers had come twice to look around, and both times the captain in charge had grown visibly uncomfortable under the old priest's mild, unhurried gaze, the way schoolboys do before a headmaster who has forgotten nothing and forgiven most of it.

The organ pipes ticked as the evening cooled. Corinne typed.

She was on page nineteen when she heard the motorcars.

Three of them, by the sound — the low grind of army engines downshifting on the rue des Tilleuls, then idling. Her hands stopped of their own accord, hovering over the keys like small frightened animals.

Father Aubry did not look up from his sweeping. But he stopped sweeping, and stood very still, and that stillness told her everything: this was not a routine patrol. Routine patrols came to one car and a bored lieutenant. This was three motors and silence.

The church door boomed. Once. Twice. Then boots, many boots, spreading down the aisle in that particular cadence soldiers have, half march and half lope, and a voice — young, trying to sound older — announced that the building was to be searched for seditious materials, and that all persons present would remain where they were.

*All persons present.* Which meant they didn't know who was here. Which meant someone had talked but hadn't talked completely. Corinne's mind performed its cold arithmetic even as her body went hot: pages one through nineteen in the case beside her, carbon copies folded into the hymnal at seat nine, the ribbon still platen-mounted, every letter she'd typed that week pressed legibly into its fabric like a confession.

They would find the hymnal. They always checked the obvious places eventually, and hymnals were obvious once you knew you were looking for paper.

She looked at the ribbon. Then at the candle bracket on the wall, ten feet away, unlit since the army had requisitioned the town's wax.

Then at the organ.

"Father," she said clearly, pitching her voice down toward the empty nave, "shall I play the evening office?"

It was, by any honest measure, an absurd thing to say. There was no evening office. There had been no evening office since the shelling stopped the bells. Father Aubry looked up anyway, and after a heartbeat's confusion — she watched him assemble the fiction in real time, the way he assembled his sermons, from whatever scraps lay to hand — he said, gravely, "If you would, my child."

Boots paused below. A lieutenant's voice, tight with the annoyance of a man whose script has been deviated from: "Nobody plays anything. Nobody touches anything."

"Then I'll sit quietly," Corinne called back, "until you've finished."

What she actually did, in the dark of the loft while six lanterns bloomed below and men began pulling up floorboards that hadn't been loose since the previous century, was this: she fed the ribbon off its spools, wound it around her hand like a skein of wool, and lowered herself into the gap behind the largest organ pipe, where a girl of her build fit precisely because, at fourteen, hiding from her piano teacher, she had measured it. She took the pages with her. Nineteen pages of the dead of the bridge, folded flat against her stomach under her coat, and the inked ribbon coiled in her fist, and she arranged her breathing into the slow pattern her mother had taught her for bad nights, and she waited.

They found the typewriter almost immediately. Of course they did; it sat in plain view, because hiding it had seemed less important than finishing page nineteen. She heard the lieutenant turn the roller with two fingers, the way one examines a dead bird.

"Someone's been writing."

"The organist transcribes music," Father Aubry said. "Her manuscripts are all — "

"There's no manuscript here, priest."

"No," the old man agreed mildly. "There isn't, is there."

A pause in which Corinne could hear the lieutenant deciding how much trouble he wanted. Below her, a hymnal fell open — she heard it slap the pew — and she understood, with a lurch, that they had the carbons. Seat nine. Half the testimony, in her own careful hand.

"Bring the machine," the lieutenant said at last. "Bring the papers. Search the loft."

And that was when Father Aubry began to play.

He had not touched the organ in four years. His hands found the keys like old friends who'd quarreled and made up without discussion, and the sound that filled the church was not hymn-like at all but something older and stranger, a low rolling thunder off the big pipes that made the loft boards hum and the lanterns gutter, and the soldiers stopped, all of them, because no order they had covered this. In the commotion — the shouting up the stair, the corporal sent to investigate the loft, the lieutenant demanding the priest cease immediately and the priest, playing louder, declining through the medium of the instrument itself — a girl slipped down the north stair, out through the vestry, over the wall into the graveyard where the grass hid the drop, and ran.

She ran with nineteen pages against her belly and an inked ribbon biting into her palm, past the mill road, past the shuttered bakery, along the river path where the water moved black and patient, and she did not stop until she reached the door of the house on Chemin des Vignes where a woman named Miriam Vance was known, to perhaps seven people alive, to take tea at eight o'clock sharp.

Corinne pounded on the door with the heel of her hand. When it opened, she said, "Half of it is gone. They have the carbons. And they took Anselme yesterday — I heard it from the baker's boy — Anselme is the one who talked." Her voice cracked only slightly, and only on the last word. "I got the rest out. I need Hazel."

Mrs. Vance, who had already put down her teacup, said, "Hazel will be here within the hour. Come in before the whole street memorizes your face."

Behind them, faint across the rooftops, the organ was still playing.

* * *

I got the summons at the garage — a boy, one of Mrs. Vance's quiet children who were everywhere and nowhere, tugging my sleeve and saying only *tea, eight o'clock*, before melting into the dusk the way those children do. I closed up the livery, checked the spare tire well out of pure ritual — the ledger slept inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, patient as a seed — and drove through streets gone strange with curfew to the house on Chemin des Vignes.

You learn things driving for people. You learn that fear has a smell, and that houses smell different after they've been searched. This house smelled of tea and lavender and, beneath it, fresh lamp-oil where a lamp had been knocked against a wall. Mrs. Vance received me in the parlor with perfect composure, and beside her sat a girl I'd seen twice at network meetings and never once heard speak — Corinne Badeau, the notary's daughter, gray-faced, her right hand bandaged where the ribbon wire had cut it, a stack of typed pages on her knees like something rescued from a fire.

"They raided Saint-Aubin tonight," Mrs. Vance said, without preamble, because preamble was a luxury she had given up sometime around the third requisition. "They have the carbon copies. Half the testimony."

"How?"

"Anselme." Corinne's voice was flat. She stared at her bandage as though reading from it. "He's been under questioning since Tuesday. He held four days. Four days is — " She swallowed. "Four days is longer than I'd have guessed. It isn't nothing."

No, I thought. It isn't nothing. It is four days, and we are going to spend them like misers.

"What exactly do they hold?" I asked, sitting down because my legs had opinions.

"Carbons of pages one through roughly twenty," Mrs. Vance said. "Which is to say, the early material — the requisitions, the conscription records, the general account of the occupation." She folded her hands. "Not the bridge. Not yet. The bridge material begins at page twenty-one, and Corinne kept it with her."

"And the clerk's list?" I asked. "The duplicate pages from inside headquarters?"

"With me," Corinne said. "Always with me." She touched her coat, where the paper lay against her ribs, and I felt a stab of something that wasn't quite admiration and wasn't quite pity — the recognition of one carrier of paper by another. We are a strange fellowship, the people who trust documents more than flesh.

"Here is our situation," Mrs. Vance went on, in the tone she must have used organizing rallies while men wrote editorials explaining why women couldn't organize rallies. "Brandt now possesses proof that testimony exists and is being compiled. He will assume — correctly — that copies survive outside his reach. Every soldier, every checkpoint, every informer in this town will be watching for paper moving toward the hotel. Ruth Ellery expects delivery in — " she glanced at the calendar on the wall, and I watched her face do the arithmetic and go carefully blank — "in however many days the armistice allows us. Perhaps five. Perhaps fewer. Perhaps, if the talks collapse, none at all."

"And the net?"

"Gone to ground. After tonight, no more meetings, no more children carrying messages between more than two known persons. The routes we planned are burned — Mill Road is certainly watched, and probably the north lane as well." She looked at me directly, and I understood that the next sentence had been prepared, weighed, and rehearsed, and that she hated it. "Which means the original plan is dead, Hazel. You cannot cross as a courier anymore. There will be no decoys, no safe houses, no relay. Whatever reaches Ruth Ellery now reaches her the way Brandt himself travels — in one car, driven by one man, through the front gate."

I sat with that for a moment. It had a certain terrible elegance. The best place to hide a fish, my father used to say, is in the river.

"He searches cars at the hotel gate," I said. "Randomly. And Brandt's aide is hunting the stolen ledger already."

"Yes."

"You're proposing I drive Captain Brandt to his own victory celebration with the evidence in the boot."

"I am proposing," said Miriam Vance, "that you consider it. I don't propose it lightly, and I want it recorded that I considered every alternative first, and that the alternatives are all worse." She paused, and for just a moment the general receded and the woman showed through — tired, sixty-some, afraid in the specific way of people who have sent others into danger and must now watch the door. "You've read enough of other people's letters, Hazel. You know better than anyone what happens to the truth when it stays in one place too long."

Outside, a patrol went by, boots ringing on the cobbles, and we all three sat silent until it passed, like people waiting out weather.

Corinne spoke then, quietly. "I can retype what they took. It'll take two days. The bridge section I can copy tonight — my hand remembers every word, I checked it against itself twice before the raid." She looked up. "But I'm finished as a courier. They'll have my description by morning. So it has to ride with you, Mr. Almeida. All of it. The ledger, the clerk's pages, everything. There's no one else left to carry it, and no time left to carry it slowly."

I thought about the tire well. About how I had built my whole life, these years, around the principle that anything worth keeping should fit in a satchel — portable, deniable, mine. Now the satchel had become a motorcar, and the motorcar belonged to a man who burned ledgers for a living, and tomorrow morning, God help me, I would polish his headlights.

"All right," I said. "Two days. Retype everything. Then I drive Brandt to the hotel, and the testimony rides under his feet the entire way, and we bet his arrogance against everyone's lives." I stood, and found my legs steadier than I deserved. "One question."

"Yes?"

"When they questioned Anselme." I kept my voice level. "Did he know about me?"

Mrs. Vance met my eyes and did not insult me by lying quickly.

"We don't know what he gave them," she said. "That's rather the point. Assume he gave them everything."

I walked out into the curfew-dark and drove home slow, past the square where the confiscated ledgers waited in the commandant's parlor for their fire, past the dark hump of the old bridge standing on its stones like a man pretending to sleep. Somewhere behind me a dog barked, and somewhere ahead of me a soldier was writing down descriptions, and somewhere in a tire well lay the only true account of the massacre on that bridge, riding through the night in a car that tomorrow would carry the man who ordered it.

Two days, I told myself, pulling the brake at the garage. Two days to make a chauffeur invisible again.

And then, through the open window of the office, the telephone began to ring — the private line, the one that connected only to the commandant's house, the one that rang, in all my months of service, exactly when something was about to go terribly wrong.
