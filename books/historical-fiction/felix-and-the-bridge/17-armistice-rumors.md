## 17. Armistice Rumors

The rumor arrived the way all rumors arrived in occupied towns that winter: on foot, wearing borrowed clothes, and slightly out of breath.

Hazel heard the first version of it from the baker's boy, who had heard it from a gendarme's cousin, who had heard it — allegedly — from a telegraph operator who was not supposed to say anything to anyone and therefore told everybody. The signing was set for Friday. Or possibly Monday. Or possibly, said one dissenting voice at the flour queue, it would never happen at all, because the delegation had walked out over some question of borders, or ships, or pride, and the whole business might stagger on into another spring.

"It's like watching three blind women describe the same elephant," Hazel said, wiping the windscreen of the motorcar that morning. "Except nobody has ever seen the elephant, and two of them insist there is no elephant."

Mrs. Vance stood on the step with her collar up and her hands jammed in her pockets, in the posture she adopted when she wanted to be told something she already knew. "Which version do you believe?"

"The one where it happens soon," Hazel said. "Not because I'm an optimist. Because Brandt's aide bought eleven bottles of wine yesterday and didn't pay for them."

"That does sound like a man planning to celebrate."

"Or a man planning to flee with a well-stocked cellar." Hazel worked the cloth along the glass. His hands were steady. He had trained them to be steady even when the rest of him felt like a kettle left too long on the stove. "Either way, Mrs. Vance, they're acting like time is running out. Which means it is."

She was quiet for a moment. The cold made her breath visible, small ghosts escaping one at a time. "The typist got half the pages north before the raid," she said, very low. "Half. And Ruth needs all of it, or enough of it, before the ink on whatever they sign dries. After that—" She did not finish. She didn't need to. After that, the victors would write their histories, and the histories would be smooth as river stones, and everything under them would stay buried.

"And now there's no network left to carry it," Hazel said. "Mill Road saw to that."

He said it without accusation. He had rehearsed saying it without accusation, because the courier they lost at Mill Road — Tomas, twenty-three, a stutter and a beautiful memory for safe-house addresses — had been introduced into the network partly on Hazel's recommendation, and Hazel carried that fact around the way he carried everything else: folded flat, close to the ribs.

"Tomas knew the risk," Mrs. Vance said.

"Everyone knows the risk. That's the trouble. Knowing it doesn't make the bullets miss."

She looked at him then, really looked, and something in her face softened by a degree — which, for Miriam Vance, was roughly equivalent to weeping. "You've been carrying this alone since the choir loft. You don't have to."

"I've been carrying everything alone since the floorboard," he said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it sounded like self-pity and he had promised himself he'd die of something more dignified.

But she only nodded, slowly, as if he'd confirmed a suspicion rather than confessed a wound. "Then you'll want to hear what I heard this morning before you decide anything else. There's a girl at the Grand Hotel — chambermaid, sympathetic, pays her rent in gossip. She says Ellery has been seen in the lobby every evening this week, sitting where she can see the door, with her typewriter case beside her like she's expecting someone to walk in carrying a parcel."

"She's waiting for us."

"She's waiting for *you*," Mrs. Vance corrected. "I'm too famous. My face is on posters in two provinces, and not flattering ones."

Hazel almost smiled. Almost. It was getting harder to find the smile lately; it kept hiding behind things.

* * *

The order came down at four o'clock, in triplicate, because everything dreadful came in triplicate that winter.

Hazel was in the motorcar outside the commandant's house, hands warming on the wheel, when Captain Brandt came down the steps two at a time with his coat unbuttoned and his aide scrambling after him holding papers. Brandt had been in a good mood all week — the dangerous, expansive good mood of a man who believed history was about to hand him a clean towel — and Hazel had learned to read his moods the way sailors read weather, because his survival occasionally depended on knowing whether to be furniture or a person.

Today Brandt was weather of the kind that made sailors tie down everything they owned.

"Auditors," Brandt was saying to his aide. "They'll send auditors. Peace comes, and the first thing anyone thinks of is paperwork." He took the papers, glanced at them, and handed them back as though they'd offended him personally. "Fine. If the records are going to be a problem, they will stop being records. The municipal ledgers, the parish registers, the tax rolls — all of it goes tomorrow morning. In the square. Publicly, so there's no confusion about what happened to them. A bonfire. Make it look civic."

"Look civic, sir?"

"Like housekeeping, Lieutenant. Like spring cleaning. Not like—" He stopped himself, which was interesting, because Brandt almost never stopped himself. He adjusted his cap and finished, quietly: "Not like what it is."

Hazel sat very still behind the wheel and watched the pair of them in the wing mirror, and inside his chest something happened that he could only describe as a door closing. Not slamming — closing. The soft, deliberate click of a man deciding.

Because here was the arithmetic he had been avoiding for weeks: if the ledgers burned tomorrow morning, then the pages hidden in the spare tire well of this motorcar became not merely valuable but singular. Half of the testimony already sat in a typist's satchel somewhere north, if Tomas hadn't given up its location, if the safe house hadn't been found, if, if, if. But the ledger itself — his ledger, four years of names in a hand small enough to fit a life inside a hatbox, plus the clerk's duplicate page list of the bridge dead — that lived six inches beneath his knees, wrapped in oilcloth, and it would live nowhere at all by this time tomorrow except in the memory of a chauffeur whom soldiers did not bother to search.

There will be no second chance. He thought it plainly, the way you think "the ice is thinning" while standing on the ice. No second chance meant now. Now meant Brandt. And Brandt meant—

"Hazel." Brandt had opened the car door without Hazel noticing, which was unforgivable, and stood looking down at him with those pale, unhurried eyes. "Tomorrow morning you'll drive Lieutenant Sorel to the square at seven. Then me to the hotel at nine. Understood?"

"Understood, Captain."

"Between those hours the car stays in the courtyard. Locked. No errands." Brandt paused. Something moved behind his face — not suspicion exactly, but the general wariness of a man who had spent a year learning that towns full of quiet people were towns full of arithmetic. "You've been useful, Almeida. More useful than most. When this ends, I may have work for a driver with your nerve."

"How kind, sir."

"I'm not being kind. I'm being practical." He shut the door. Through the glass, muffled, he said to his aide: "And find out whether the priest kept duplicates of the parish registers. If he did, I want them tonight."

The engine turned over. The town slid past the windows — shuttered shops, a woman beating a rug with the fury of the powerless, two boys chalking a hopscotch grid until a patrol made them scrub it off. Ordinary streets. Streets that did not know yet that tomorrow morning their own memory of themselves would go up in smoke in the square, and the crowd would be expected to find it civic.

Hazel drove. Behind his knees, six inches down, the ledger rode in silence, and for the first time in four years it felt less like a secret than like a passenger.

* * *

That evening he laid it all out on Mrs. Vance's kitchen table, because there was no longer any sense pretending the table wasn't already involved.

The ledger. The clerk's pages. A street map of the town with three routes drawn and crossed out and redrawn. The last of the network's codes, written on the back of a laundry list. And, in the center, a candle stub, because the electricity had become theoretical again.

"Tomorrow," Hazel said. "It has to be tomorrow."

Mrs. Vance did not argue. He had expected argument — schedules, contingencies, the word *reckless* deployed at volume. Instead she pulled out a chair, sat, and studied the map the way she studied parliamentary transcripts, which is to say like an enemy.

"Brandt drives to the hotel at nine," she said.

"He does."

"You drive him."

"I do."

"So the ledger is in the car when Brandt is in the car." She tapped the map. "Hazel. You're proposing to smuggle contraband past an army checkpoint using the man who signed the burn order as your reference letter."

"When you put it like that it sounds insane."

"How else would one put it?"

"Foolhardy? Bold? Characterful?" He turned the map toward himself. "Mrs. Vance, think about how searches actually work. They search strangers. They search carts and satchels and nervous men. They do not search a captain's own car, driven by the captain's own chauffeur, because doing so would imply the captain requires searching, and no lieutenant alive is going to imply that to a man like Brandt. The uniform isn't just cover. It's a spell. I've watched it work for months. I've simply decided to be on the other side of it for one afternoon."

"And if the spell fails?"

Hazel considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to Miriam Vance had never once worked. "Then I lose the ledger, my liberty, and quite possibly the use of my hands. In reverse order of likelihood."

"And you accept that."

"I accepted it weeks ago. I've just been waiting for the fear to catch up." He smiled, and found, to his surprise, that the smile came easily this time. "It hasn't. Mostly I feel like myself for the first time since Mill Road. Isn't that strange? All winter I've been invisible on purpose. Now there's one job left and I can't be invisible to do it, and instead of dread I feel—" He hunted for the word. "*Employed.*"

Mrs. Vance laughed — a short, startled bark, quickly suppressed, the laugh of a woman who had trained herself out of laughter during inconvenient wars. "Employed," she repeated. "Well. Better men have gone to their deaths for worse reasons."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true, which is better." She leaned over the map and began, briskly, to plan. This was the version of Miriam Vance that the posters never captured — not the fire-eater on the platform but the organizer, the woman who could take a mad scheme and give it a spine. "If you're driving Brandt, you leave at nine sharp and you are back by eleven, no exceptions, because Sorel expects the car at noon and a late chauffeur is a noticed chauffeur. The checkpoint on Quay Street rotates its sentries at ten; the new shift is always slower, colder, and more interested in their own coffee than in your boot. Use the captain's name early and often. Do not volunteer the tire well. And Hazel—"

"Yes?"

"If it goes wrong — if you're stopped, if they open the case — you say the ledger is yours alone. You invented it, you copied it, you never met me. You've never met me." Her voice did not waver, but her hand, resting flat on the map, had gone white at the knuckles. "Swear it."

"Mrs. Vance—"

"*Swear it.* One old woman's poster face is a fair price for four years of names."

He looked at her across the candle stub and understood, suddenly, that this was her version of what he'd done at the floorboard: the moment complicity became purpose, the moment a person stopped calculating odds and started calculating debts. He could refuse her nothing, and she knew it, which was why she'd asked.

"I swear," he said. "But only if you swear the reciprocal. If they come for you, I was a bad driver you regret hiring."

"Done." She snuffed the argument between them like the candle, then relit the candle, because they weren't finished planning. "Now. The route. There are three ways to the Grand Hotel and two of them are traps..."

* * *

He did not sleep. He had not expected to.

Instead he sat in the cold kitchen of his lodgings past midnight with the ledger open on his knees, reading names by the light of a shielded match, the way other men read scripture. Old habits. He touched each name the way the widow Okafor had asked him to touch her boy's — carefully, completely, the full name spoken once aloud in the dark where nobody could correct him.

*Emile Okafor. Born 1899. Bridge, November.*

Then the newer entries, the ones from the riverbank, descriptions instead of names because the water keeps names and returns everything else: a tall man with a rope burn on one boot; a boy of perhaps sixteen in a conscript's coat too big for him. He had written *perhaps sixteen* and then, unable to bear the perhaps, gone back and written *fifteen, I think, God forgive us,* which was not proper testimony but was honest, and he had learned this winter that honest was rarer and worth more.

Somewhere past two o'clock he caught himself smiling at the ledger, which alarmed him briefly, until he identified the feeling. It wasn't happiness. It was readiness — the specific, narrow peace of a man who has stopped asking whether he should act and started packing.

He packed. Oilcloth, twine, the tire iron that would sit innocently on top of the wrapping and explain any lump a suspicious sentry might feel. He ate the last of the bread. He wrote no farewell letters, because farewell letters were evidence, and besides, he intended to be back by eleven.

At the window, the town lay quiet under a thin skin of frost, and beyond the rooftops he could see the black line of the bridge against the marginally less black sky. Tomorrow it would still be standing. He would not. Not the man who had crossed it invisibly every day of this war — that man would be spent by noon, used up, burned through like the ledgers in the square. What walked away from the Grand Hotel, if anything walked away, would be somebody new: a man whose name existed in print.

A bell began ringing across the river. Just one church, one peal, then silence — probably a mistake, a sexton drunk or testing, but the whole valley held its breath for three seconds anyway, listening for the rest of the bells, the eleven of them, the end of everything.

They didn't come. The night closed back over its secret.

Hazel put the ledger in the oilcloth, the oilcloth in the satchel, the satchel by the door, and lay down fully dressed with his boots on, because tomorrow he would need to move fast, and there was no second chance coming.

At six-forty, a knock landed on his door — three quick raps, the network's old signal, the one the broken member of the choir loft had known too.

Nobody living should have been using it.
