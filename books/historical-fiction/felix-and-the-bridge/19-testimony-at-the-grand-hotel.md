## 19. Testimony at the Grand Hotel

The Grand Hotel had the particular shabbiness of places that expected to be important. Its lobby smelled of wet wool and cold coffee, its chandelier hung at a defeated angle, and its desk clerk — a small man with the eyes of someone who had watched three armies check in and out and intended to watch several more — waved Hazel through with the sublime indifference of a man who had decided that whatever was about to happen was not his department.

Room 214 was on the second floor, at the end of the corridor, and Mrs. Vance had chosen it herself because, she said, the window overlooked the service alley.

"If anything goes wrong," she'd explained, "we will leave through the kitchen, past the potatoes, and out into the alley like civilized people."

"And if it goes very wrong?"

"Then we leave through the potatoes quickly."

Hazel had carried the ledger up in his coat, against his chest, where it had ridden since the crossing — since the checkpoint at the canal bridge where he'd used Captain Brandt's own name like a borrowed key and the sentry had saluted *him*. The paper against his ribs had felt less like paper all morning and more like a second heartbeat. Now, standing in Room 214 while Ruth Ellery set up her typewriter on the writing desk, he found he could not immediately make his hands unclench.

"You'll want to sit," Ellery said, without looking up. She was perhaps forty, with ink-stained fingers and the unhurried manner of a woman who had filed dispatches from three collapsing fronts. Her typewriter was old, its keys polished silver by use. "Fainters always stand."

"I don't faint."

"Everyone faints their first time on the record. It's not weakness, it's gravity. Truth is heavy; you've been carrying it upstairs." She rolled a sheet into the platen with a practiced snap. "Sit, Mr. Almeida. Tell me everything. Start wherever it hurts most."

* * *

He began, as it turned out, with the first entry.

"October the ninth," he said. "Tomas Wren, cooper's apprentice, taken from the fish market by a patrol under Lieutenant Hessler. No charge recorded. Not returned."

The keys clattered. Ellery did not interrupt, did not look up, only typed, and there was something in the steadiness of that sound — the small mechanical faithfulness of it — that loosened something in Hazel's chest. He had read these names to himself a hundred times, silently, in the dark of the livery loft or behind the wheel of the car at checkpoints. He had never once said them aloud to another living person. Saying them aloud changed the weight of them. They stopped being stones he carried and became stones he could set down, one by one, on a table another person could see.

Mrs. Vance sat by the window in a straight-backed chair, hands folded, corroborating as promised. When he faltered on a date, she supplied it. When he misremembered which patrol had taken the miller's sons — October or November, Hessler or the other one, the one with the scar — she closed her eyes and said, "November the third. I remember because it was my sister's birthday and I thought, how absurd, to be celebrating anything."

"How do you know the date so precisely?" Ellery asked.

"Because I wrote it down too," said Mrs. Vance. "In my correspondence book. Under the guise of condolences. Forty-one condolence letters this winter, Miss Ellery. I have been keeping a record disguised as etiquette." A pause, dry as good sherry. "Suffragists learn early that women's work is invisible work, and invisibility has uses."

Ellery's typing slowed for one beat — the beat of a journalist recognizing a better story than the one she'd been promised — then resumed.

They worked through the morning. The boy conscripted at fifteen — Peter Sallow, who had lied about his age and whose mother had come to every office in town and been turned away from each one politely, in triplicate. The requisitioned grain, listed sack by sack from Hazel's memory of the requisition orders he'd carried sealed and read anyway. The men taken for labor details and never logged anywhere at all, names Hazel had gathered secondhand at doorsteps and over fences, and he flagged each of those aloud — "secondhand, Mrs. Duval told me, she saw it herself" — because testimony, he had learned, was only as honest as its hedges.

"Good," Ellery said when he did that. "Keep doing that. A list everyone knows is rumor. A list with its sources named is evidence."

Somewhere past noon — the hotel sent up bread and hard cheese and coffee the color of ditchwater, which they drank anyway — Hazel reached the bridge.

He stopped.

The room went quiet except for the radiator ticking to itself in the corner. Mrs. Vance rose and crossed to him and put her hand flat on the ledger where it lay open, not taking it, just resting her hand there as if stilling an animal.

"Take your time," she said.

"There were eleven," Hazel said. His voice came out lower than he expected. "That night. Soldiers sealed both ends at dusk. I heard the shots from the river path — nine, maybe ten, I counted them without meaning to, the way you count bells. In the morning there was a notice about bandit activity and the bridge was scrubbed." He swallowed. "There were no bandits. There were men from this town who had been asking questions about the requisitions. That's what they were guilty of. Asking questions."

"Names," said Ellery gently. "Give me the names."

And here was the thing Hazel had not anticipated: the names came easily. Eight of the eleven he knew whole — full names, trades, streets. For two more he had partials, and he said so plainly, and Ellery typed the gaps as gaps, *identity uncertain, described as a tall man with a limp, believed to be called Anselm.* The eleventh —

"The eleventh I never learned," Hazel said. "Young. Dark coat. I saw him only—" He stopped himself before saying *on the riverbank*, before saying *what the water returned*; some things belonged later in the record, in order. "I never learned his name."

"Then we write him as the eleventh," said Ellery, typing, "unnamed, and we say so loudly. An unnamed victim is an accusation with room left in it. Someone reading will fill in the blank someday. Blank spaces are patient."

Hazel looked at her. "You talk like Mrs. Vance."

"I'll take that as the compliment it is."

* * *

It was nearly three o'clock when the knock came — no, not a knock; Hazel registered the difference even through the fog of six hours' speaking. A knock asks. This one announced. Three blows, spaced like a parade drum.

Everyone in the room went still.

"That will be the aide," Mrs. Vance said calmly, as though identifying a birdcall. "Brandt's shadow. Captain Voss. Tall, humorless, carries a notebook in which he writes down other people's crimes, which is ironic given the company he keeps."

Through the wall, muffled but unmistakable: a voice demanding of the desk clerk whether a woman matching Miriam Vance's description had taken a room. And the desk clerk — bless him forever, thought Hazel, I will remember his name too, I will start a ledger of helpful hotel clerks — answering in a long, rambling, apologetic mumble about foreigners, Frenchwomen, possibly Swedish, so many this season, impossible to say.

"He can't stall forever," Ellery murmured. She was already lifting the typed pages off the platen, squaring them. "How many sheets?"

"Nineteen," Hazel said, because of course he'd counted. He counted everything now; counting was how he prayed.

"Carbon's in the drawer. Take the original. Where does it go?"

"It doesn't go anywhere yet," said Mrs. Vance. "Gentlemen who hunt papers search bags and coats and rooms. They do not search—"

"—the car," Hazel finished, and understood her at the same moment she said it. "In service to Captain Brandt. Nobody searches the captain's car. They salute it."

"Which means," said Mrs. Vance, with the smallest, sharpest smile Hazel had ever seen on her composed face, "that you must be sitting behind the wheel looking bored when Voss arrives downstairs. Can you be bored?"

"Madam, boredom is my native country."

Another blow on the door downstairs — closer now, at the foot of the stairs — and then footsteps.

* * *

What followed Hazel would replay for the rest of his life in the slow, clear motion of dreams: the pages going flat beneath the spare tire in the well of the boot, his fingers working the fasteners with a steadiness that surprised him; Ellery pressing the carbon copy flat between the leaves of a Baedeker guidebook on her shelf, in plain sight, because — she said — "no one ever opens a guidebook to Belgium, not even Belgians"; Mrs. Vance arranging herself in the chair by the window with her knitting, actual knitting, produced from a bag, needles clicking like a tiny typewriter of her own.

Then Hazel went down the back stairs, through the kitchen, past the potatoes, and out to the motorcar at the kerb, and sat behind the wheel, and was bored.

Captain Voss came around the corner of the hotel at four minutes past three, and he was exactly as advertised: tall, gray, precise, with the expression of a man perpetually auditing the universe and finding it short. Two soldiers trailed him. He stopped beside the car. He looked at Hazel the way a man looks at a piece of furniture that has been moved.

"You," said Voss. "Almeida. Brandt's driver."

"Yes, sir." He did not salute; drivers didn't, quite, and Voss did not correct him.

"What are you doing here?"

"Waiting on Mrs. Vance, sir. She's inside paying calls. I'm to have the car ready at half past." He let a note of grievance creep in, the whine of a man whose afternoon was being wasted, because Voss would believe a servant's resentment sooner than a servant's innocence. "She does take her time."

Voss's eyes narrowed a fraction. "We are looking for a ledger. Town records. Stolen property of the occupation authority. Have you handled any documents today? Any packages? Papers of any kind?"

"Papers, sir?" Hazel let his brow fold with the honest stupidity of a man being asked an unreasonable question. "I handle the captain's orders when he gives me his route, sir. Otherwise I mind the engine and the tires. Tires and engine." He said it twice, slowly, the way a simple man repeats the boundary of his understanding. Then, because the boldest lie is the true one wearing different clothes: "Is something stolen, sir? Only if the captain's motorcar is suspected of something, I'd take it kindly if you searched it yourself, proper, so there's no question later on me."

It was, he knew, a gamble of magnificent size. But he had learned in six months of checkpoints that suspicion feeds on refusal and starves on invitation.

Voss stared at him. Behind his eyes, Hazel could almost hear the adding machine: chauffeur, simple, offers search, simple men don't offer searches — but then again, why would Brandt's own driver steal records, what would he want with them, he can't even read—

Could he?

"Can you read, Almeida?"

"Enough to follow a route, sir. Words with roads in them."

A long pause. Down the street, a church bell began to mark the quarter hour, and Hazel counted the strokes the way he counted everything, and found his heart keeping perfect time with them, steady, steady.

"Open the boot," Voss said.

Hazel got out and opened the boot, and stood aside with his cap in his hands, and Voss looked into it: the tool roll, the jerry can, the folded tarpaulin, the spare tire seated snug in its well exactly as a spare tire ought to be, with nothing whatsoever visible beneath or around it, because paper laid flat under rubber in a dark well is paper the universe itself has agreed to forget. One of the soldiers lifted the tire halfway, and Hazel watched the man's hands, and the man's hands found nothing worth finding, because Voss was standing right there wanting to be somewhere else, and soldiers are exquisitely tuned instruments for detecting where an officer wants to be.

The tire went back. The boot shut.

"Nothing," said the soldier.

"No," agreed Voss. He gave Hazel one last look, the look of a man filing an index card he did not fully trust. "Tell Mrs. Vance the captain wishes to see her tomorrow. Ten o'clock. Regarding correspondence."

"Yes, sir. Ten o'clock. Correspondence." He would remember it word for word; word-for-word was what he was for.

Voss strode into the hotel with his soldiers behind him, and Hazel got back behind the wheel, and it was then — only then — that his hands began to shake, quietly, on the wheel, like something finally allowed.

Under those hands, beneath the tire, nineteen pages. Every name. Every date. The eleventh man, unnamed and therefore patient.

He sat in the cold car and laughed once, a short helpless bark of a laugh, and then put his cap back on and became a chauffeur again, because Mrs. Vance would be down presently and she took her time, but not that much time.

* * *

She came out at twenty past, knitting bag on her arm, face composed, and paused at the kerb.

"Well?" she asked, low.

"He searched the boot. Took the tire out himself." Hazel pulled the door open for her. "Found nothing."

"And upstairs?"

"Miss Ellery has the carbons hidden in a guidebook to Belgium."

Mrs. Vance settled into her seat and arranged her skirt. "Belgium," she repeated. "No one will ever look there. Half the tragedy of this war is that nobody looks at Belgium." She was quiet a moment as the engine caught. Then: "You offered him the search. I heard the shape of it in your voice just now. You invited the wolf to inspect the henhouse."

"It seemed wiser than letting him wonder."

"It was either very wise or very foolish, and I confess I cannot tell which, which is generally the sign of your best decisions." She looked straight ahead through the windshield at the gray street, the shuttered shops, the far gray line of the river and the bridge above it, standing. "Nineteen pages, Hazel. Eleven names from that night alone. Do you know what you've done?"

"Driven badly, according to several officers."

"What you've done," said Mrs. Vance, ignoring him with great warmth, "is make it expensive. From tonight, anyone who says those men were bandits has to argue with print. Print doesn't get frightened. Print doesn't disappear in the night. Print just sits there, in every newspaper that runs it, being true at people." She folded her hands. "Now drive me home before Voss remembers he dislikes you, and take the long way. I should like to see the bridge in the evening light, while it still stands."

Hazel took the long way. As they came along the embankment the bridge showed itself above the water, old stone, patched and patched again, holding as it had held for two hundred years against ice and flood and now soldiers — though not, he thought, for much longer, because he had seen the charges drilled into its piers and pretended not to, the way he had pretended not to everything until pretending was no longer possible.

He was turning the car onto Mill Road when Mrs. Vance spoke again, differently, quietly, all the iron gone out of her voice.

"Hazel. Tomorrow, ten o'clock, Brandt wishes to see me regarding correspondence. Voss said so in front of you deliberately. They know I've been writing letters. They may know more than that." Her hands were still in her lap, perfectly calm, and her knuckles were white around the knitting bag. "Whatever happens tomorrow, the pages stay with you. Not with me. You understand? If they take me, they take a woman with a correspondence book and forty-one condolences, and I will be tedious at them for hours. But they must not find the pages, and they must not find them near me."

"Mrs. Vance—"

"Drive, please," she said. "And Hazel — thank you for the eleventh man. Unnamed and patient. We will fill in his blank yet."

Ahead of them, at the corner by the shuttered bakery, a staff car stood at the kerb with its engine running. Beside it, checking his watch, stood Captain Voss — not waiting for them, not watching the road, merely present, the way a man is present when he has begun to suspect that a piece of furniture has been moved.

Hazel drove past at a respectful, boring, entirely innocent speed, and did not look back, and counted the seconds between the bakery corner and the next turning, and began — because it was how he prayed — to plan.
