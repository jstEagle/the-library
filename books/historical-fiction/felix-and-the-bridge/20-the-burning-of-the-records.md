## 20. The Burning of the Records

They announced it with a handbill nailed to the notice board outside the baker's, which struck me as a peculiar courtesy. The army had requisitioned our grain without so much as a receipt, and had taken Mr. Duval's horses with less ceremony than most people reserve for borrowing an umbrella — but when it came to destroying every written record of our town's existence, they wanted us to know about it in advance.

*By order of the occupying command,* the bill read, *obsolete municipal documents will be disposed of by fire this evening at seven o'clock in the market square. Citizens are reminded that duplicate copies remain on file at regional headquarters.*

That last line was a lie. I knew it was a lie because I had seen the parlor of the Commandant's house, where the "regional headquarters" consisted of a stack of ledgers gathering dust between a potted fern and a portrait of some general who looked as though he'd been carved out of a ham. There were no duplicates. There was only what was left after tonight, and what was left would be ash.

I read the handbill twice, then went back to the car and sat with my hands on the wheel, not driving anywhere, thinking about arithmetic.

Here is the sum as I worked it: everything in those ledgers — births, marriages, deaths, land deeds, the water rights from 1871 that old men still argued about over cards — all of it was about to become smoke. And everything I carried under the floorboard and inside my coat lining and folded into my boot heel — the names, the dates, the three I'd written at dawn after hearing shots from the bridge, the pages young Pell had slipped me with shaking hands — everything I carried was now, as of approximately seven o'clock this evening, the *only* copy in existence.

There is a particular feeling that comes with being the last remaining version of something. It is not pride. Pride is too comfortable a word. It is closer to what a man feels carrying a lit lantern through a dry field: very aware of his own hands.

* * *

The square filled early. That surprised me. I would have expected people to stay away — to draw their curtains and pretend not to hear the crackle, the way we had all pretended not to hear the shots two weeks ago. But by half past six there must have been two hundred of us standing in the cold, breath rising in little white flags of surrender or protest, I couldn't tell which.

Mrs. Vance stood at the edge of the crowd near my elbow, hands folded in her coat like a woman waiting for a bus. She had not spoken to me directly since the choir loft, since she'd caught me copying Brandt's routing order and instead of turning me in had said, in that dry voice of hers, *"Well, Almeida. It seems we've been conspiring separately for months. How inefficient."* Tonight she simply stood beside me, and her silence was its own kind of company.

"They came to watch," she murmured. "Why did they come to watch?"

I thought about it honestly, because Mrs. Vance's questions deserved honest answers even when they weren't really questions.

"Because it's theirs," I said. "The books. Even if they never opened one, even if they can't remember what's in them. You want to see the end of your own things. Even a bad end. Especially a bad end you're not allowed to argue with."

She made a small sound, not quite agreement. "You've been reading other people's correspondence for two years, Hazel. Has it ever once occurred to you that you might have been doing the same thing? Watching us watch?"

"Constantly," I said. "It's made me insufferable at funerals."

That earned me half a smile, gone before it finished arriving. Then the soldiers brought the barrels.

* * *

They had set up in front of the town hall — four oil drums cut lengthwise, propped over kindling, and behind them a handcart stacked with the ledgers themselves. I recognized them the way you recognize your own furniture in a stranger's house. The green register with the cracked spine where every birth in Saint-Aubin had been entered since before the war with the Prussians, the one my mother's name was in. The marriage rolls. The burial records, which had until tonight held the last official word on where certain people were buried — though not, I noted, the ones from the bridge, whose names appeared nowhere official at all except in my coat.

A lieutenant I didn't know presided; Captain Brandt stood slightly apart, hands clasped behind him, watching the crowd rather than the fire. He wore his dress collar and the expression he kept for public occasions, which suggested a man who found the entire world mildly damp.

They fed the ledgers in a few at a time. The fire took them slowly at first — a wet November wind kept pushing the flames sideways — and then all at once, the way paper goes, whole decades vanishing in a yellow rush and a smell like a library having a fever.

Nobody spoke. That was the remarkable thing. Two hundred people and nobody spoke, nobody wailed, nobody threw themselves at the soldiers. We just stood there and watched, and I understood that we were doing something together, all of us, something quiet and enormous: we were memorizing. Old man Ferrand by the fountain was moving his lips, reading the spines as they went in. Widow Marchetti had her eyes closed. The schoolteacher, little Madame Roux, was reciting something under her breath — the class registers, I'd have bet my satchel on it, names of children going into the fire and coming out through her mouth and into the heads of everyone standing close enough to hear.

The army thought it was conducting a disposal. What it was actually conducting was a memory exercise for two hundred reluctant students.

I felt a laugh start somewhere under my ribs — a dangerous place for a laugh to start that evening — and pressed it down. Not quite humor, but adjacent to it. My father used to say that the difference between grief and comedy was mostly timing, and God knew the timing tonight was absurd: here was the mightiest army on the continent, victors of a dozen engagements, spending soldiers and kerosene on the urgent military necessity of burning a book of sheep-grazing disputes from 1889.

*They are afraid of paper,* I thought. *An entire army, afraid of paper.* And then, less comfortably: *and they should be, because I am standing twenty feet away made almost entirely of it.*

* * *

It took forty minutes. The crowd stayed the whole time, which I think unnerved the soldiers more than shouting would have. When the last register had gone in — the burial book, I watched it curl — the lieutenant barked something about dispersing, and the crowd melted backward into the streets with the unhurried obedience of people who intended to obey the letter of an order while insulting its spirit.

I turned toward the car, and found Captain Brandt standing six feet away, examining my headlamp as though it had personally offended him.

"Almeida."

"Captain."

"You attended the disposal."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. Instead I said, "Car's warmed up whenever you need it, sir," which was true, and servile, and exactly the sort of thing a chauffeur says, and I had gotten very good these past weeks at saying exactly the right chauffeur things while my heart performed gymnastics that would have impressed a Hungarian judge.

Brandt didn't move. Firelight was still coming off the square behind him, orange against his cheek, making his shadow jump on the cobbles. He had intelligent eyes; that was always the problem with Brandt. Stupid enemies are a comfort. Intelligent ones look at you and see the shape of what you're hiding, even if they can't name it yet.

"Curious thing," he said. "Your employer's housekeeper tells me Mrs. Vance received no mail today. No mail at all. And yet her driver spent the morning at the post office."

"The network's slow since Mill Road," I said. "Went to ask after a parcel. There wasn't one."

"Mm." He looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere behind him a drum collapsed inward with a soft iron sigh, throwing up a column of sparks, and neither of us flinched, and I felt that flinchlessness itself was information he was filing away. "You know what I find curious about you, Almeida? Servants make poor witnesses and worse liars, and you are conspicuously both."

"I'll try to improve, sir."

"Do." He adjusted a glove, tugging each finger tight. "The armistice may come within days. Perhaps hours. Do you know what that means for men like me?" He didn't wait for an answer. "It means everything is provisional. Reports, orders, casualties — all of it becomes negotiable afterward, subject to whoever holds the documents. Documents, Almeida, decide what the war *was*. Whoever owns the record owns the peace." A thin smile. "Which is why we are thorough with ours."

"And very lucky for everyone," I said, "that no one else has any."

Something moved behind his eyes — not belief, not yet suspicion hardened into action, but the space where suspicion lived, warming itself. He glanced once at the car. At the spare tire mounted on the back. At me again.

"Take Mrs. Vance home," he said. "And Almeida — stay off the river roads at night. There have been incidents."

"There often are, sir," I said, "around rivers."

He held my gaze one second longer than politeness required, then walked back into the firelight and became a silhouette, and then a rumor, and then nothing.

I stood by the car with the engine ticking and let myself shake for exactly ten seconds. I counted them. It's a technique I recommend: fear is manageable if you schedule it.

* * *

Mrs. Vance was quiet the whole drive home. Halfway down the river road — which I took anyway, slowly, because the moon was generous and I wanted to see — she spoke without turning her head.

"He suspects you."

"He suspects everybody. It's his profession."

"He suspects you *specifically*. I watched his face." She paused. "I watched yours, too. You looked like a man attending his own funeral and pleased with the turnout."

"That's morbid."

"That's accurate." She turned then, and in the dashboard's faint glow her face was all angles and certainty. "How long until Ellery can wire the dispatch?"

"Tomorrow morning, she said. She has the cable office reserved first light — the hotel manager owes her a favor and she's the sort who collects favors like other women collect hatpins. The testimony's typed. Nineteen pages. The list is complete." I heard my own voice say it and marveled that it could sound so calm. "Every name. The boy Okafor. The three from the bridge at dusk. The clerk Pell's page list matches mine on every entry — we checked twice."

"Then it's done," Mrs. Vance said softly, "except for the part where it isn't."

That was precisely the shape of it, and we both knew it. The dispatch existed; it existed in Room 214 of the Grand Hotel, nineteen typed pages and a ledger's worth of names. But it also existed in the tire well of a motorcar currently traveling down a dark road beside a river, in the coat lining of a chauffeur whom an intelligent captain had just informed, with perfect courtesy, that documents decide what the war was. Between the paper in the hotel and the paper under the floorboards lay one night, one checkpoint's worth of distance, and whatever Brandt chose to do with his suspicions once he had time to sharpen them.

"He has no proof," Mrs. Vance said, as if reading my ledger upside down, which by now she practically could.

"He doesn't need proof. He needs a morning and a pair of boots."

"Then don't be home when his boots arrive." She faced forward again. "Drop me at the corner. I'll walk in the back way — if they're watching the house, let them watch me alone, an old woman with no mail. You take the car to the livery, sleep in the office, and be at the hotel at seven. Understood?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Hazel." She put her gloved hand briefly on my sleeve, the first time she had touched me in two years of employment. "Whatever happens tomorrow — the bells, the armistice, the crowds kissing strangers — you keep hold of one fact. They burned the books tonight because they had already lost. Men don't burn what's beating them. They burn what they cannot otherwise admit exists." Her hand withdrew. "You were the witness. Everything else is cleanup."

She stepped out at the corner and was swallowed by the dark street, upright as a lamppost and twice as illuminating.

* * *

I did not go straight to the livery. I drove instead to the bend above the river, killed the engine, and sat looking down at the black water and the pale scar of the bridge where it met its far bank. Below, at the waterline, the reeds moved the way they always do, indifferent and patient, keeping whatever the river gives them to keep.

Somewhere under that water and those reeds were answers the army had decided to file as *bandit activity.* Somewhere in my boot heel and my coat and the tire well were the same answers, filed properly, spelled correctly — Widow Okafor had made me spell her Théodore three times and I would spell him correctly until I died.

At seven o'clock tonight, an empire had burned a town's memory and called it tidiness. And it had failed, utterly and permanently, because for months a driver nobody bothered to suspect had been doing the one thing armies never plan for: paying attention, and writing it down.

Tomorrow at seven, Room 214. Tomorrow, the wire. Tomorrow, perhaps, bells.

Tonight I started the engine, checked the spare tire well with my fingers one last time — paper, string, wax, paper — and drove back toward town with my headlights hooded, past the square where the barrels still smoked, past two soldiers warming their hands over the ashes of everything we used to be.

One of them looked up as I passed.

I did not slow down. But I saw him turn and speak to his companion, and I saw the companion stand, and I saw them both look toward the river road, and I understood with terrible clarity that Captain Brandt's boots might arrive considerably earlier than morning.
