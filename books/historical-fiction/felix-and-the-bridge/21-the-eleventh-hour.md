## 21. The Eleventh Hour

I had always believed that the last hour of a war would announce itself with thunder — guns redoubling, or falling silent all at once like a held breath finally let go. Instead it arrived as a girl in a wool coat running across the hotel lobby with a piece of paper in her fist, shouting "It's signed! It's signed!" in the particular flat voice of someone who has been sent to deliver news and has not yet been given permission to feel it.

The time was 4:12 in the morning. I know because I had stopped sleeping sometime around midnight and had taken to watching the lobby clock the way a man watches a pot he is afraid will boil over.

Ruth Ellery's room on the second floor had gone quiet an hour earlier — that particular quiet of a typist who has finished. When I knocked she opened the door still holding her pen, ink on three fingers, and looked at me the way a surgeon looks at a patient after a long operation: relief and exhaustion sharing a chair.

"It went out," she said. "Ten minutes ago. The telegraph office opened early for armistice traffic. There was a queue of officers sending love letters home, if you can believe it, and one very confused corporal trying to send his mother a recipe." She stepped aside to let me in. On the desk lay the flimsy yellow copy of her dispatch, four pages of it, the last page carrying the thing I had spent a winter protecting: *attached, complete list of persons removed from the town of Saint-Aubin-sur-Mer and its district, November last, with such particulars as could be verified. Forty-one names.*

Forty-one. I had counted them so many times the number had worn a groove in me, like a stair tread polished by feet.

"You should sit down," Ellery said.

"I've been sitting down for six months," I said. "Professionally. It's what I'm known for."

She laughed — a short, cracked laugh, the kind that comes out of people when the pressure lets off before the grief has finished its arithmetic — and poured two glasses of the hotel's terrible coffee. We drank it standing at her window while the sky over the river went from black to the color of wet slate. Somewhere below, a soldier was singing very badly and very happily, and being told by his friends to stop, and refusing.

"The article won't print for days," Ellery said. "Maybe a week. Cables first, then the paper itself. But it's filed. It's *filed*, Mr. Almeida. Which means it exists now in more places than any one man can burn."

I said nothing, because there is a kind of speech that only spoils a moment, and because my throat had closed in a manner I found embarrassing. She pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

Downstairs, Mrs. Vance was already awake. Of course she was. I found her in the lobby in her good coat, dictating a telegram to a hotel clerk who wrote as fast as his pencil allowed and kept glancing up at her as though checking whether she really meant to send words like those through His Majesty's wires.

"Ah," she said, spotting me. "There's my driver. Sit down, Hazel. You look like a man who has recently been exhumed."

"I feel like one," I admitted. "But a well-documented exhumation. That's the difference."

Her mouth twitched. Then it did not twitch anymore, and she took both my hands in hers, which she had never done, and held them hard enough to hurt. "You did it," she said. "Whatever happens now — you did it."

*Whatever happens now.* She meant Brandt. We both knew she meant Brandt.

* * *

The bells started at seven.

Not church bells at first — the fire bell, then the school bell, then the great cracked bell of Saint-Aubin itself, which had not rung since before the occupation and which made a sound like a spoon dropped down a staircase. And then every church across the river answered, and then, faintly, bells in towns I couldn't see, until the whole valley was ringing and the sound came at you not through your ears but through the soles of your boots.

I stood in the square and watched soldiers become men again.

That is the only way I can describe it, and even now I cannot make it sound like anything but nonsense, though I saw it happen. They did not change their uniforms. They changed their *faces*. A sergeant who had terrorized the bread queue since September suddenly remembered how to smile, badly, like a man using a stiff hinge. Two privates sat on the steps of the requisitioned flour store passing a cigarette back and forth and laughing at nothing, at the bells themselves, at being alive on a morning when being alive required no explanation. An officer walked past me without looking through me, which was new, and tipped his cap to Madame Ferrand at the flower stall, which was unprecedented, and she sold him carnations for his lapel as though the last eight months were a stain that might yet come out in the wash.

I wanted to hate them. Some part of me had been saving up hatred all winter precisely for this morning, the way you save coins for a purchase you have planned, and when the moment came the shop was closed. They were twenty years old. They were cold. They had done what they were told by men whose names most of them never knew. It was not forgiveness — do not mistake me, it has never been forgiveness — but it was something worse than hatred, something that costs more: it was *recognition*. The boy laughing on the flour store steps might have been Théo Okafor, fifteen years old and conscripted, except that Théo was under the bridge and this boy was on the steps, and the difference between them was a patrol route and a bad night and a lieutenant with orders.

The bells rang and rang. Old women wept into their aprons. Somebody produced a bottle. The mayor appeared on the town hall balcony in his ceremonial sash, which he had hidden in a hatbox since October, and gave a speech about renewal that nobody listened to, and everybody cheered anyway, because cheering was the order of the day and the mayor, whatever else he was, had learned obedience thoroughly enough to apply it in reverse.

And through all of it, at the edge of the square, Captain Brandt stood beside his staff car and watched me.

He did not approach. He did not signal. He simply stood there in his best uniform, unsmiling amid the general delirium, the way a stone stands in a river. And when our eyes met he inclined his head perhaps two degrees — a gesture that could have meant anything, and meant, I understood immediately, *come here*.

* * *

The room they put me in was the commandant's own office, which struck me as either very flattering or very final. There was a desk, two chairs, a window overlooking the square, and on the desk, squared neatly at its center, a single folder stamped in red.

Brandt closed the door himself. He did not post anyone outside it. That detail I noted the way I have learned to note things — automatically, usefully, against my will.

"Do you know why the bells are ringing, Almeida?" he asked. Not *Mr. Almeida*. He had never granted me the honorific; I had long ago decided not to be wounded by it.

"The armistice, sir. Signed at dawn, they're saying. Effective at eleven."

"Eleven o'clock." He checked his watch, an expensive one, the crystal scratched. "So we are currently living inside the last hours of the war. After eleven, everything I do becomes a civilian embarrassment instead of a military necessity." He sat down behind the desk, leaving me standing, and opened the red-stamped folder. "Sit."

I sat. The chair was better than mine at the boarding house and I resented how much I enjoyed it.

"A ledger," he said. "A private ledger. Names. Kept by a chauffeur." He turned the folder toward me. Inside was a single sheet — the clerk's duplicate list, the pages that had crossed my palm in the dark ten days ago, photocopied — no, I am showing my age; *re-typed*, word for word, by an officer with good handwriting and, presumably, a guilty conscience of his own. My name was not on it. Neither was the clerk's. But the third line read: *source within records section — identity under determination.*

"You understand my position," Brandt said. "Two days ago a stolen document surfaced in a neutral journalist's room in this town. My aide searched Room 214 and found a chauffeur waiting politely with a car. Since then I have had time to think. I think very well at night; it is one of the few benefits of my profession."

I said nothing. In my experience the man who speaks first at a table like this one supplies the rope.

"My aide believes you took the ledger. The clerk who handled the copies has disappeared, which suggests either flight or a shallow grave, and I confess I have not investigated with vigor, because finding him answers questions I would prefer remain open." He folded his hands. "Here is what I believe, Almeida, and I ask you simply to listen. I believe you are clever, careful, and motivated. I believe you were in my house, in my parlor, among those ledgers, because my clerk reported a chauffeur asking about burning schedules. I believe Mrs. Vance's network carried your testimony to the hotel, and I believe that as of approximately four o'clock this morning, it does not matter what I believe, because it is in a cable office in the capital and possibly in two others."

Outside, the bells went on. Through the window I could see the crowd surging, hats thrown, a soldier hoisted onto shoulders. It looked like another country seen through glass.

"If this were September," Brandt said quietly, "you would be in a cellar by tonight and I would sleep fine. I want you to know that I know that, and that I am not proud of the man who would have done it, and that the man who would have done it is still here — he is merely out of work." He glanced at his watch again. "In fifty minutes, killing a chauffeur solves nothing. It produces exactly one more name on somebody else's list, written by somebody else, in somebody else's ledger. I have read Miss Ellery's earlier reporting — yes, we know who she is, we simply lacked a reason to care until this week — and I have noticed that she is very thorough with names. Yours would be the forty-second. It would be in print by Sunday. And unlike the others, mine would deserve it."

He closed the folder. For a long moment neither of us moved. I realized my hands were steady, and was surprised; some part of me had expected them to shake for this, the way they shook the first time I heard shots from the bridge.

"You may go," he said.

I stood. At the door I stopped, because there was a question in me that had been sitting all winter like a stone in a boot, and I found that I could not leave it there any longer, war or no war.

"The bridge," I said. "The night of the ninth. Whose order was it?"

Brandt looked at me for a long time. Outside, someone began to sing, and dozens of voices took it up, badly, gloriously.

"That file," he said finally, tapping the red folder, "was burned yesterday at noon, along with the rest of it, per the standing instruction. I supervised the fire personally." He almost smiled; it was the nearest thing to honesty I ever got from him. "So you see, Captain to chauffeur: I don't know anymore either. That is what burning does. It doesn't only destroy the guilty. It destroys the answer."

He returned to his paperwork — his discharge inventories, his transfer orders, the small tidy bureaucracy of ending — and I left him to it.

* * *

At five minutes to eleven I was in the square with Mrs. Vance and half the town, and at eleven exactly the guns across the river stopped.

They had been muttering all morning, desultory, half-hearted, the firing of men who had heard the rumors and were conserving themselves. Then, on the stroke, silence — a real silence, total, so complete that I could hear the river, which I had lived beside all winter without once hearing. Someone near me began to cry. Someone else began to cheer. Mrs. Vance took off her hat, held it against her chest, and stood absolutely still amid the tumult, eyes closed, lips moving slightly — not prayer, I think, but a list. She was reading names too. Everyone in that square who had lost someone was doing sums at that moment, and the sums came out different for each of us, and the same.

Hazel Almeida, standing in the crowd, felt — nothing, at first. Then the nothing filled up with something enormous and undifferentiated, and I understood that I was not going to weep, because I had spent six months converting weeping into handwriting, and the habit was too strong to break in a morning. Forty-one names. One bridge. One article, somewhere in the wires between here and the capital, moving at the speed of electricity toward Sunday's front page.

Release, yes. The word for what I felt was release. Absolution is a different word and I have not earned it and do not expect to. The clerk who slipped me the pages may be in a shallow grave; I have not dared to look. The widow Okafor has a condolence letter in her drawer written in my careful hand, delivering news I already knew she knew. Mrs. Vance and I trusted each other exactly as far as necessity required, which is a kind of love, but a lean one. I drove men to their dinners and served them wine and memorized their jokes about the paperwork problem, and I am not sorry, and I am not clean.

At noon Ruth Ellery found me at the hotel, fed, shaved for the first time in a week, holding a cup of coffee I had no intention of drinking. She dropped a newspaper proof sheet on the table in front of me — she had connections at a printer two towns over, of course she did — and there it was, in columns, in ordinary black type like a laundry list or a train timetable:

*IN MEMORIAM: THE DEAD OF SAINT-AUBIN BRIDGE. Complete roster follows, as recorded by witnesses.*

Forty-one names. Théo Okafor, fifteenth on the list, full name spelled correctly, age noted, trade noted — apprentice wheelwright — because his mother had made me repeat it twice and I had written it the way she said it, with the pride intact.

"There'll be an official history," Ellery said, sitting down across from me. "Committees, archives, footnotes. It'll say the record was destroyed in the confusion of war, which will be technically true and entirely false, and in fifty years nobody will check." She tapped the proof sheet. "This they'll have to check. This has a source. This has a witness willing to stand behind it." She looked at me over her spectacles. "Will you? Publicly? On the record, with your name attached?"

I looked at the list a long time. My whole winter fit in a satchel once; now it fit on one page of newsprint, and it weighed more than the motorcar.

"Mrs. Vance tells me," I said, "that they mean to hold a memorial on the bridge itself. What's left of it. Sunday week. She says the widow Okafor intends to read the names aloud, all forty-one, herself." I folded the proof sheet carefully, the way I had once folded other people's letters, and put it inside my coat, over the heart, where I had grown accustomed to carrying paper. "I'll need a ride there myself, Miss Ellery. I find I've developed a professional relationship with that riverbank."

Ellery smiled. "Then I'll drive you."

"No," I said, and discovered I was smiling too, the first time in months, and that it still worked. "Thank you — but I'll drive. It seems I'm rather good at getting where I'm needed."
