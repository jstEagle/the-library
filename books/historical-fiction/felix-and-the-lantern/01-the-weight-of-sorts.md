## 1. The Weight of Sorts

There is a particular weight to a drawer of lead type, and I have known it since I was small enough to sit inside one. My father used to lift me out by the collar like a kitten and say, *Quentin, you are sitting on the alphabet*, as though that were an ordinary thing for a child to do, as though most children did not spend their days sitting on things far less interesting than the alphabet.

I am not small anymore. The drawers have not gotten lighter.

I woke before the bells on the morning this story properly begins—though of course I didn't know it was the morning the story began; stories never announce themselves, they simply arrive smelling of ink and asking to be set—and I lay in my bed in the loft above the shop and listened to Vellamont cough itself awake. The river first, always the river, that long gray murmur under everything, then the fish carts, then the bells of Saint Ferrol's going six, seven, eight, because the bell-ringer had been drunk since the Assumption and nobody had the heart to report him.

Eight bells. I should have been downstairs at six.

This is the thing about living above your own livelihood: the commute is eleven steps and the temptation is infinite. I descended them, ducking the beam where Father cracked his head every winter solstice without fail, God rest him, and lit the lamp over the composing stone because the shutters let in light the color of dishwater and no compositor alive can set ten-point type by dishwater.

The job that morning was handbills for Harrow & Sons, Auctioneers. *EFFECTUAL CLEARANCE SALE OF HOUSEHOLD FURNITURE, LINEN, AND EFFECTS.* Someone was dead or someone was fleeing, and either way their spoons were going under the hammer on Thursday. I set it in a bold condensed face because auctioneers believe the world is deaf, and I will tell you something true about setting type: it is the only work I know where your mistakes are visible in your hands before they reach the page. A wrong sort sits in the stick like a pebble in a shoe. You feel it. You go back and find it or it goes out into the world wearing your name.

Well. Wearing somebody's name.

* * *

Let me explain the name, because the name is the whole architecture of the house I live in, and houses with unusual architecture deserve explanation before visitors arrive.

I was born Quentin Ashgrove, second child of Maren and Aldous Ashgrove, and I would show you the parish record if there were one. There is not. What there is, is a christening record for Felix Ashgrove, born three years before me, dead of the marsh fever at eighteen months, mourned with a stone in the churchyard at Ferrol's Cross that says only *Small Bird, Flown Home.* My parents grieved him the way people grieve—they ate nothing for a season and then ate too much for a decade—and somewhere in those years my mother looked at me and looked at the ledger of the world, which had a column marked ASHGROVE CHILDREN and a number in it, and decided the number was not negotiable.

So I became two children in ink and one child in fact, and when I was old enough to understand what she'd done, she sat me down and explained it the way you'd explain a load-bearing wall: this is what holds the roof up, don't knock it.

By day, in the eyes of the census and the tithe rolls and the guild registry, the shop belongs to Felix Ashgrove, sole surviving child of Aldous Ashgrove. Felix signs the customs declarations. Felix pays the paper tax. Felix is twenty-six years old, a compositor of good standing, quiet, respectable, unremarkable in every particular that attracts attention.

And Quentin? Quentin is the name the type knows. Quentin is who sets the letters at four in the morning. Quentin exists in the way a draft exists—you can feel it, but try writing it down.

My mother died when I was nineteen, my father when I was twenty-three, and I have kept the arrangement running ever since, the way you keep a clock wound that belonged to people you loved. It is not a lie exactly. Or it is exactly a lie, but it is a lie with a pedigree, built by two dead people I loved, and I maintain it the way you maintain anything inherited: with resentment, tenderness, and very little choice.

* * *

The hidden bed is in the wall.

Not behind a painting—that is for novels about other people. Mine is behind the type cabinet in the loft, the tall one with the drawers labeled A through Z in Father's handwriting, and if you pull the whole cabinet forward on its concealed runner—and you would only know to pull if you had watched my father do it, drunk once a year on the anniversary of my mother's death—the back panel opens on a space barely wider than a coffin and half again as long. A straw tick. A blanket Mother wove. A candle stub. A boy's jacket, outgrown decades ago, still hanging on a peg because neither of us could bear to move it.

It has never been slept in. That is the joke of it, if you want the joke: the bed exists so that if a soldier ever demands proof that Felix Ashgrove lives alone, there is a room for a brother that can be produced, made up, slightly rumpled. And it has never once been used, because producing it means admitting there is something to hide, and hiding it means there is nothing to show, and so it sits in the wall like a heart in a chest, doing its silent work, waiting for a day I pray stays hypothetical.

I air it anyway. First Monday of the month, I pull the cabinet out, open the panel, shake the tick, sweep the dust, and speak to it—not to anyone, just to the space, the way you'd greet an empty chair. "Still there," I say. "Still empty." Then I close it all up and feel, absurdly, better.

You will think this is a sad way to live. Everyone thinks so eventually, usually right before they say something unforgivable. But here is what I actually feel, most mornings, standing at the stone with the lamp turned low and the city muttering below: I feel *practiced*. Contentment, I have decided, is mostly vigilance that has gotten comfortable. You do the same small checks so many thousands of times they stop feeling like fear and start feeling like breathing. Lock the bolt. Bank the lamp. Count the sorts. Wear the name.

Felix fits me now the way my own shoes fit. Which is to say I forget I'm wearing him until I take a step and remember.

* * *

Midmorning brought Mr. Quill himself—Harrow's junior clerk, a damp young man named Pell who sweats proof sheets—to approve the galley. He held it up to the window, squinted, held it further away, held it close again, and pronounced it *serviceable*, which from Pell is a papal blessing.

"Father would've charged you extra for approving it that fast," I said, wrapping the forme.

"Would he?"

"He said clients who read carefully find problems, and problems cost money, so he charged for the reading."

Pell laughed, paid the account in coin—I insist on coin, coin leaves no trail of credit, no signature, nothing to compare across documents—and went off to ruin someone else's Thursday. And I stood there in the doorway of my father's shop, in my father's apron, holding money earned under my brother's name, and thought: *this cannot last.*

I think that roughly forty times a day. It has the quality of weather by now. It does not mean anything is ending. It just means I know how things are built—how type is built, how lies are built, how governments are built—and I know that everything built of small parts loosens eventually. A forme shakes apart in the press if you don't lock it tight enough. You check the quoins. You check them again. This is called craft.

Across the lane, the tailor Ostrov was hanging shirts in his window, and he raised a hand to me, and I raised one back, and I noted—because I note things, it is the whole trade—that there were two new men on the bridge at the end of our street wearing the gray coats of the Bureau of Records instead of the blue of the watch. Census men don't wear coats like that. Census men carry clipboards and look bored. These men carried nothing and looked at doors.

I hung my own sign—OPEN, in Father's favorite italic—and went back to the stone.

* * *

Afternoon: inventory. Every quarter I take stock of the shop, and every quarter I conduct a second, private inventory that appears in no book. It goes like this:

One satchel, leather, Father's, holds: the good quills (six), the burnisher, the whetstone, two shirts, the small tin of type-cleaning spirits, my mother's ring (which I do not wear, because rings invite questions), and room remaining for approximately thirty pounds of whatever matters most when the moment comes. Thirty pounds. I have spent years deciding what thirty pounds of a life weighs.

The chase with the Harrow forme: not portable. The press itself: certainly not, though God knows I'd try; a man does not leave his press the way he leaves a coat. The type cabinets: no. The ledgers—ah, the ledgers.

Here is the tender spot. Our business ledgers, kept faithfully for forty years by my father and now by me, record the household of Aldous Ashgrove as: Aldous (deceased), Maren (deceased), and children, two—one line for Felix, one line for the child whose name was never entered anywhere, because my parents weren't fools, they didn't write *Quentin* in a book. They wrote *two children* and left the second one a blank, and a blank, in a ledger, is a door.

A census man reads *two children* and asks: where is the other?

I have rehearsed the answer since I was nine years old. *Died young. Fever. There's a stone at Ferrol's Cross.* It is airtight. It has a gravestone in it. It is also, as of this quarter, possibly worthless, because the Bureau of Records has started doing something new—comparing. Comparing parish records against tax rolls against christening registers against each other, hunting for numbers that don't reconcile. And a blank child, a dead-child-without-a-death-record, is precisely the kind of arithmetic that doesn't reconcile.

I closed the ledger and sat in the lamplight—for the afternoon had gone dark, rain coming in off the river the way it does, turning the windows to smoked glass—and I did what I always do with dread, which is convert it into tasks. Tasks are just fear with its sleeves rolled up.

Replenish the spirits tin. Check the satchel. Move the reserve coin from the flour jar to the loose brick by the hearth, because flour jars are the first place anyone looks. Air the bed early this month; the cabinet runner was sticking. Sharpen the burin. Count the sorts.

Count the sorts. Always, at the end, count the sorts.

* * *

Night came down on Vellamont the way it does in autumn, early and apologetic, and I worked late because the Harrow job wanted distributing and distribution is meditation: each letter back to its compartment, the world put right one em at a time. The lamp hissed. The river murmured. Somewhere below, the two men in gray coats had been relieved by two more men in gray coats, which meant they were posted, which meant someone had assigned them, which meant paperwork existed somewhere with our street on it.

I finished the last case, brushed my hands, and was reaching for the lamp key when I heard the knocker.

Nine-forty at night is no hour for callers. Callers at nine-forty come holding clipboards, and clipboards at night mean the Bureau, and the Bureau at night means the arithmetic has finally failed to reconcile somewhere, and my rehearsed answer rose in my throat like bile—*died young, fever, there's a stone*—

I banked the lamp. I checked my voice in my chest, found Felix folded into it, ready. Through the shutter slats I saw a figure on the step: a woman, plain coat, official satchel, a lantern raised so its light fell patient and thorough across the shop door, across the sign, across the painted name above it.

ASHGROVE & SON.

She knocked again. Three measured raps. And pinned to her satchel, catching the lantern light, I saw the badge of the Bureau of Records, and beneath it a slip of paper with a list of addresses written in a neat bureaucratic hand.

Ours was third from the top.
