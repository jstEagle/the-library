## 2. The Census Taker's Question

The census taker came on a Thursday, which I have always considered an insult to the day. Thursdays were for cleaning the press and oiling the rollers and pretending, for one blessed hour, that the shop ran itself. Instead I spent that particular Thursday with my hands black to the wrist and a stranger's knuckles rapping the door glass hard enough to make the samples of our finest work — wedding invitations, funeral notices, and everything in between — tremble on their strings.

I should explain something about doors in Vellamont, because it matters. A door is a kind of sentence. Some say *come in*. Some say *go away*. Ours said, in faded gilt letters, **ASHGROVE & SON, PRINTERS**, which was itself a small lie with excellent posture: Ashgrove had been dead two years, and the son behind the door was not the son the letters promised. But the sign had been paid for, the paint was good for another decade, and lies, like type, hold up best when they are well-set.

I wiped my hands on my apron, which moved the ink around rather than removing it, and opened the door.

She was not what I expected, though I couldn't have told you what I'd expected. Younger than the usual government breed, for one. She wore the grey coat of the Bureau of Records with the collar turned up against the river damp, and she carried a ledger under one arm like a woman carrying a cat she did not entirely trust. Her hair was coming loose from its pins in a way that suggested the wind off the Weir had opinions about bureaucracy too.

"Household census," she said. "Ashgrove premises?"

"That's us."

"May I come in? It's cold, and your doorway faces the canal wind, which I'm told is the worst wind in Vellamont."

"It's certainly the dampest," I said, and stepped aside.

Here is what you must understand about being invisible: it is work. People imagine invisibility as a passive condition, like fog. It is not. It is a craft, and I had been apprenticed to it since I was twelve years old, which was when Father sat me down at the kitchen table and explained, with the patience he usually reserved for explaining kerning, that the world had made rules about children like me, and that our family would answer those rules with a fiction so consistent it would outlast all of us. Felix Ashgrove. Born, registered, schooled, confirmed — all in ink, all in a name belonging to a boy who had lived eleven days and left behind nothing but a name too good to waste.

I wore Felix the way other people wear coats. Buttons done up. Collar straight. And like any coat worn long enough, I had stopped feeling it — mostly.

The census taker looked around the shop with the particular gaze of her profession, which is to say she read the room the way I read a galley proof: looking for errors. Her eyes went over the press, the cases of type ranged in their oak cabinets, the drying sheets hung like laundry, the little stair to the gallery where we slept. Then they came back to me and performed some arithmetic I could not see.

"You're the compositor," she said.

"I'm the compositor."

"Alone now, I understand. Your condolences are overdue from the Bureau — your father's file was flagged for it and then mislaid." She said this without embarrassment, the way one reports weather or a delayed barge. "You've kept the business open."

"Someone has to print the city's apologies and invitations. Grief and joy both need setting in type, and neither waits."

That earned me half a smile. She set the ledger on the counter — our counter, the one Father had planed smooth himself, the one I'd leaned against at nine years old while he showed me how to justify a line — and opened it with a gesture that was almost tender. Whatever else Ilse Voss was, and I would learn there was a great deal else, she handled paper like someone who loved it, which is the one sin I cannot forgive in a person, and the one virtue I cannot resist either.

"Standard household schedule," she said. "Name of head of household, trade, premises held, number resident, number of children under sixteen. You'll forgive the questions; I only ask them, I don't write the form."

"Who does write the form?"

"A committee." She said the word the way other people say *plague*.

* * *

I made tea, because that is what one does with officials — you feed them, you warm them, you make yourself so ordinary that their eyes slide off you like rain off oiled canvas. Ordinary. That was always the whole trick. A compositor with ink in the creases of his knuckles, a dead father's chair, a pot of cheap tea. Nothing to see.

Except.

Except that the second bed was upstairs on the gallery, and I had made it that morning. Both beds. I always made both beds, the way Father had, the way one keeps a grave tidy — not for visitors but for the dead, or for the self that refuses to stop hoping. Felix's bed, with its blanket folded to military sharpness because Felix, in the family mythology, was a tidy boy. Eleven days old and already the neatest thing in the house.

If the census taker went upstairs — and census takers, I supposed, might do anything — she would find two beds and one resident, and she would do sums.

So I positioned myself the way you position a forme on the stone: carefully, deliberately, between her sightline and the stair.

"Number resident at this address?" she asked, pen poised.

"One."

Her pen did not move.

I want to be precise about this moment, because precision is my trade and I owe it to the truth. Her pen hovered over the line the way a compositor's hand hovers over a case when he has reached for a letter and found the wrong compartment — that fractional hesitation, the pause of someone who has caught a discrepancy between what is expected and what is set before them. Not long. A breath. Less than a breath. But I have spent my life reading hands, because hands tell you what a customer will pay before his mouth does, and hers hesitated.

"And the number of children under sixteen resident at this address?"

There it was. The question I had answered a hundred times in my life — to schoolmasters, to clerks, to the man who came round collecting the chimney tax. Always the same answer, worn smooth as a pebble.

"One," I said.

And I watched her write something else.

I don't know how to describe watching that without describing what it did to me. She wrote — I saw it, I am certain I saw it — not *one*. The stroke of her pen made a shape with more than a single vertical in it, and then she made a small mark beside it, a tick or a cross, I couldn't tell which, in the margin, next to a printed code I hadn't noticed until that moment, a column of tiny figures running down the page like stitches in a seam.

The kettle was screaming. I attended to it. My hands, I noted distantly, were perfectly steady, which was a lie my hands told on my behalf, bless them. I poured the tea. I carried it over. I smiled the smile of a man with nothing in his cellar but potatoes and grief.

"Milk?" I said.

"Please."

She took the cup. Our fingers did not touch, which I registered, and then resented registering.

"The shop's been here long?" she asked, conversational, pen still in hand, and I understood that the conversation was not over, that it had perhaps never been about residents at all.

"Sixty years. My grandfather cut the first 'A' of the sign himself, out of pearwood. It's still better than anything I could buy."

"And the family?"

"My father. Myself." I let the silence sit exactly as long as a natural silence sits. "My mother, years ago."

"Siblings?"

Ah. There it was, delivered between sips, aimed with the casualness of a woman asking after the weather. I had been asked that question a hundred times too, and I had a hundred answers ready, all of them Felix. *A brother. Died in infancy.* The standard answer, the sanctioned answer, the answer written into every register in this city in my father's careful hand.

"He died," I said. "Before I was born."

Something crossed her face — not suspicion. Curiosity, maybe. Or recognition. The look of someone who has caught a misprint and is deciding whether it matters enough to pull the sheet.

"Yes," she said quietly. "I saw the entry. Little Felix. Eleven days." She turned back a page in her ledger, unhurried, and I realized with a cold sliding feeling that she had read our file before she ever knocked. "It's a shame. A name like that."

"A name like what?"

"Felix." She looked at me over the rim of her cup, and her eyes were the grey-brown of the river in November, and they were not unkind, which frightened me worse than cruelty would have. "It means fortunate. Lucky. Someone chose it hoping, I think."

"My parents had a sense of humor," I said, and heard, too late, how thin that rang.

But she laughed — actually laughed, a short startled sound, quickly pressed flat — and something in the room loosened by a degree I didn't trust.

* * *

She worked through the rest of the schedule with the thoroughness of the truly conscientious, which is a rare and terrible thing in a bureaucrat. Trade: printer. Premises: owned, freehold. Number of presses: one, hand-worked, iron, built by Harrow & Sons, forty years old and ornery as a mule. She asked the value of stock. She asked whether I employed apprentices. (No. The truth was cheap there, so I gave it freely.) She asked whether I printed only commissioned work or also speculative matter.

"Speculative?"

"Political tracts. Pamphlets. Satire."

"I print weddings and funerals," I said. "In Vellamont, madam, those are the same market."

She wrote that down. I honestly could not tell whether she was writing it as testimony or as comedy.

And then, closing the ledger, she paused. Her thumb rested on the page edge, and she looked at me again — that reading gaze, hunting errors — and said, "You've ink on your jaw. Left side."

I rubbed at it. Of course it was the wrong side.

"Compositor's mark," she said. "My uncle was a compositor. In Corvenna, before the union was dissolved. He had it too — the smudge, low on the cheek, where you push the hair back with an inky wrist." She demonstrated, pushing her own hair back with an imaginary wrist, and the gesture was so precisely mine that for one absurd moment I felt seen the way a page feels seen when held to the light. "He used to say a man's hands remember more than his head does."

"Your uncle sounds like a wise man."

"He was arrested," she said, matter-of-fact, capping her pen. "In the third year of the registrations. He wasn't printed anywhere afterward, if you take my meaning. Which is why I mind the forms properly. Sloppy forms get people's uncles arrested."

I did not take her meaning, or rather I took it all too well and declined to show it. I showed her instead the face of a man thinking about milk and potatoes.

"Well," she said, gathering her coat, her ledger, her terrible conscientiousness, "you'll receive confirmation of registration by post within the month. Check it carefully. Errors should be reported within fourteen days." She paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and looked back at me with an expression I would spend many sleepless hours taking apart like a locked forme. "The Ashgrove household. One child."

"That's right," I said.

"Yes," she said. "That's what's right."

And she wrote something in the margin as she stood there — I saw her do it, quick, before the book closed — and then the door shut behind her, and the bell above it stopped its idiot jangling, and I was alone with two beds and one name and the sound of the canal wind worrying the window frames.

* * *

I did not go back to work immediately. I stood in the middle of the shop among all that honest machinery and thought about strokes of a pen.

Here is the arithmetic of a compositor's life: every word on every page got there because someone set it, letter by letter, and every letter was chosen. Nothing appears by accident. Even an error is chosen, in a sense — chosen by a tired hand or a cracked matrix or a distracted mind, but chosen. So when I watched Ilse Voss's pen make a shape that was not *one*, I knew — the way you know a line is set too tight before you ever measure it — that whatever she wrote, she wrote on purpose.

The question was: on whose purpose?

I climbed to the gallery because standing still felt dangerous, and because the gallery is where I think, pacing the narrow boards between the two beds like a man walking a beat. Felix's blanket lay folded in its perfect rectangle. Father's bed — my bed now — held its usual archaeology of proof sheets and string.

"Two beds," I said aloud to no one. "One child. That's what's right."

I had rehearsed this vigilance so long it had come to feel like peace. That is the trap of hiding well: you begin to mistake the success of your concealment for safety, the way a man in a deep boat mistakes the depth of the water for the strength of the hull. For six years I had been a misprint nobody had bothered to pull. The plate sat in the press, the error sat in the page, and the world rolled on printing its clean copies without me.

Now a woman with a grey coat and her uncle's knowledge of ink-smudges had held her pen over my name and hesitated.

Hesitation, in a compositor, means one thing only: she had found the discrepancy, and she had decided — for reasons of her own, in a margin of her own — not yet to correct it.

I went back downstairs. I cleaned the rollers. I oiled the press. I set type for a funeral notice for the Halloway infant, three lines, centered, with a small dove, and my hands did their remembered work beautifully while my mind ran ahead of them down dark streets, counting checkpoints that did not yet exist.

At dusk I locked the door, turned the sign, and stood a moment in the falling dark listening to the canal.

Somewhere across the water the lamps of the Bureau of Records burned in their long grey row, and behind each lamp, presumably, a pen was moving. Thousands of pens. Writing the city into existence, one household, one child, one careful marginal note at a time — and somewhere in all that ink, in some ledger I would never be shown, my name was being written differently than I had spoken it.

Fourteen days, she had said, to report an error.

I wondered how many days I would have, if the error was me.
