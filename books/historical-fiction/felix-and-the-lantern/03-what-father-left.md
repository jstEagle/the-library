## 3. What Father Left

Grief, I have learned, is a kind of inventory. You go through the dead man's things shelf by shelf, drawer by drawer, and every object is a small invoice: *here is what you owed him, here is what he never told you, here is the coat you cannot throw away because it still smells of him and also because coats are expensive.*

Father died on a Tuesday in March, between the second and third bell of the morning, which I know because I was setting type for a funeral broadside at the time—someone else's funeral, a merchant named Hollis who had been dying publicly for two years and finally made it official. The bell for Father never rang. He was too quiet a man for bells.

I finished the Hollis job first. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head: my father's heart stopped and I kept composing because the forme was locked and the ink was mixed and he himself had taught me that the work does not stop simply because the world has. He would have done the same over my body, probably. He'd have said something dry about deadlines. Then he'd have wept in the washroom with the door shut, the way he did everything emotional—privately, thoroughly, and slightly annoyed at himself for it.

* * *

The sorting began three days later, once the magistrate's clerk had stamped the death certificate and the neighbors had finished leaving bread on the doorstep as though baked goods were the traditional currency of condolence. (They may be. Nobody tells you these things. That is rather the problem with being nobody.)

The shop was his whole biography, arranged by shelf. The front room: commerce, respectability, the public face—wedding invitations, bills of lading, the occasional smuggled pamphlet he printed with the shutters closed and charged double for. The middle room: the trade itself, the cases of type he'd inherited from *his* father, brass reglets worn smooth as river stones, the great iron press he called *the Widow* because, he claimed, she demanded everything and gave back sheets. The back stairs: our living quarters, two rooms above the paper store. One bed made up neatly. One bed hidden behind a false wall panel, made up carelessly, because no one was ever supposed to see it and therefore no one ever troubled to straighten its blankets.

I started with the easy rooms. The front room yielded nothing but invoices and one drawer of confiscated pamphlets he'd apparently kept out of some mixture of evidence-hoarding and sentiment—a man who couldn't throw away a good argument, even one that could hang him. The middle room took longer, because I kept stopping to grieve individual objects. His cutting knife. His apron, hung on its peg with the sleeves still holding the shape of his arms. A proof puller's roller wrapped in oilcloth, labeled in his hand: *good for another year, whatever Elodie says.*

Wait. No. Scratch that name from the record. *Whatever Quentin says.* Ink remembers what mouths are made to forget; that is the whole trouble with my profession.

It was on the stairs landing, in the little cedar chest where he kept documents, that I found it. Under the lease. Under Mother's marriage lines. Under a bundle of Felix's christening papers that I touched exactly once, with two fingers, the way you'd handle a hot coal or a relic.

At the bottom, folded twice and sealed with plain wax, a document in his own careful compositor's hand—not typeset, which surprised me, but written, as though he hadn't trusted the press with it. As though some things must come from the body directly.

*I, Aldous Ashgrove, printer of Vellamont, being of sound mind and deteriorating body, do declare this my last will and testament.*

Then the standard clauses—the shop to whoever kept it running, the debts paid from the strongbox, the Widow to be oiled quarterly *or so help me.* And then this:

*And all the rest—house, stock, name, and whatever else I possess that cannot be weighed—to my child.*

Not *to my son.* Not *to Felix.* Not *to Quentin,* which would have required explaining Quentin to a clerk, and clerks explain things to magistrates, and magistrates have forms for people like me and none of the forms end well.

*My child.* Two words. Unspecified. Unnumbered. A door left open exactly the width of one person.

He was a compositor to his marrow; he knew better than anyone alive that words are chosen, that every line of text is a decision, that you can set *son* or *daughter* or *heir* and each choice builds a different house. He chose *child.* He sat at his desk—I found the draft versions beneath it, four of them, crossed out and corrected—and he chose the one word in the language wide enough to hold me without squeezing.

I sat down on the landing floor with the will in my lap and cried in the manner of my family: silently, thoroughly, and slightly annoyed at myself for it.

* * *

Here is the thing you must understand about the census, and ledgers, and the city of Vellamont generally: the law does not recognize ambiguity. The registries want a name, a sex, a parentage, entered in a fair hand, witnessed twice, filed in triplicate. Everything else—all the actual living mass of a person—is, legally speaking, decoration.

When Felix died of the winter fever—he was eight, I was six, and I remember the fever more clearly than I remember him, which is its own quiet horror—Mother wanted to register me under a new record, my own record, truthfully set. The registrar asked her questions. She answered them honestly, because she was honest, and honesty in a registry office is like water in a print shop: technically pure, catastrophically misplaced. The clerk's face went through several expressions, ending at the one I have since learned to call *the form face*, and he explained, kindly enough, that there was no provision. That corrections of that nature required a petition. That petitions of that nature required advocates. That advocates of that nature cost money and rarely succeeded, and wouldn't it be simpler—

Simpler. The word that has governed my entire life. It would be *simpler* if the household already had a son on its rolls. It would be *simpler* to let the dead boy stand in the doorway of the law while the living child stood behind him. Mother came home gray-faced and furious, and Father listened, and said very little, and the next day the false panel went into the wall, and the second bed became a secret, and Felix—who had been a name and a grief—became also a costume I put on whenever the world knocked.

I don't blame any of them. I want that understood too. They built me a shelter out of the only materials they had: a dead boy's name and a false wall. It kept the rain off for fifteen years. It's just that a shelter is not a life, and eventually you notice the difference, usually at night, usually when the lamp is low and the type cases are quiet and you catch yourself practicing saying your own name aloud to see if it still fits your mouth.

Quentin. Four letters I can set blind, upside down, in the dark. Qu-in. The *q* with its tail like a hook, always needing its companion letter to mean anything. Fitting.

* * *

Four drafts of the will, I said. Let me tell you about the drafts, because I have read them perhaps four hundred times since, and each reading is a small excavation.

The first draft read *to my son Felix Ashgrove.* Standard. Legal. A lie dressed in its church clothes. He must have gotten as far as the magistrate's office with it, because there's a note pinned to it in his hand: *clerk wants baptism record — NO.*

The second read *to my son.* Stripped of the name but still wearing the gender, and beneath it he'd written: *a half-truth is a misprint. Do not ship.*

The third—this one hurt—is in Mother's hand. *To my children,* it says, evenly divided, and there is a date from before Felix died, and I think it sat in the cedar chest unfinished for years, waiting for a future that the fever cancelled, and he could never bring himself to throw it away. Grief hoards. Ask anyone with a locked drawer.

The fourth is the one he sealed. *My child.* And in the margin, small, in that engraver's hand of his, almost too faint to read:

*Let the lawyers choke on it. Words belong to those who set them.*

That is a printer's theology, right there. That is the catechism I was raised in. Words are not facts; words are *made*, and making can be undone and remade, and the men who control the registries control only the registries—they do not control the language, whatever they believe. Somewhere in the city there is a ledger that says I do not exist. Very well. I am a compositor. I have lead, I have ink, I have a press older than the magistrate, and I have a father's testimony that the word *child* is wide enough if you're brave enough to set it.

* * *

I took the will to Master Corvin, the solicitor on Tanner's Row, a dried little man who smelled of vinegar and discretion. I wore my best coat and my most boring voice and presented myself as *Felix Ashgrove, executor,* and watched him read the document, and watched him arrive at *my child,* and watched him frown, and look up at me, and look down again, and make a decision.

"And the child in question?" he asked.

"Is present," I said.

A long pause. Vinegar and discretion doing their work. Then: "The instrument names no other claimant. The estate passes accordingly." He stamped it. He didn't ask. I think—I choose to think—he had seen enough of Vellamont's new thoroughness to know exactly which questions were becoming dangerous, and had decided that an old man's will was not the hill he cared to die cataloguing.

So I walked home lawful. On paper, in the eyes of the state, Felix Ashgrove owns the Ashgrove print shop, free and clear, by virtue of a document that says no such thing about any Felix at all. There is a comedy in that which Father would have appreciated. The law has accepted a will it cannot read correctly, because the law reads what it expects to read, and expectation is just a forme locked by lazier hands than mine.

But walking home along the canal with the stamped copy inside my coat, I felt less triumphant than I expected. Because I understood, with the particular clarity that arrives when you are carrying something valuable past strangers, that the will was not protection. It was evidence. It was the single sheet of paper in the city that affirmed me, and it was now sitting in a solicitor's copy-book where any clerk with a warrant might someday find it and ask the question Corvin was too tired to ask: *and who, precisely, is the child?*

Documents are like type, once they're distributed into the world—you cannot recall every sort. My father had spent his last months of life building me one line of defense, and the line was real, and the line was thin, and the times—I could feel it the way you feel weather in old joinery—were turning against thin lines.

* * *

That night I did two things.

First, I moved the original will from the cedar chest to the place I keep the things I actually cannot lose: sewn into the lining of the satchel I carry everywhere, beneath the false bottom where I keep my true shaving kit and my mother's ring. Everything I am, packed for a journey I hope never to take. Fifteen years of habit. One satchel, and the world fits inside it.

Second, I stood at the case and composed a line of type I did not print and then destroyed, just to feel it in my fingers:

*q u e n t i n   a s h g r o v e*

Set it, held it, looked at it reversed in the galley—mirror-writing, the way all true things look before they're pressed onto the world—and broke it apart and slung the sorts home.

It was a week later that the knock came at the door, and the woman on the step held a ledger and a pen, and said she was from the Office of Census and Enumeration, and my hand—the hand that had set and unmade my own name—went cold around the latch.

She wanted to know how many children lived in the household.

I said one.

I watched her pen hesitate.

And then I watched her write something else.
