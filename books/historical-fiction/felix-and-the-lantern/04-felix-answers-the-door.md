## 4. Felix Answers the Door

The Bureau of Enumeration gave me a leather satchel, a fountain pen, and a list of instructions long enough to hang a man with. Item eleven said: *When follow-up visits are required, the enumerator shall conduct them promptly and without sentiment.* I have always found that the Bureau's instructions read best as comedy.

Three days after my visit to the Ashgrove print shop, I stood in the lane outside it with my satchel and my prompt, unsentimental self, and asked myself why I'd taken the trouble to shave my boots clean of canal mud before coming. There was no reason. The Ashgroves were a two-child household reporting one, which made them a discrepancy, and discrepancies were ink work, not heart work. You wrote them down. You sent them up the stairwell of bureaucracy, floor by floor, until they arrived at whatever office dealt with them, and you never learned what happened at the top of the stairs because nobody who worked in the Bureau ever had.

Except that I had noticed something on my first visit, and noticing is the one vice the Bureau never managed to train out of me.

What I had noticed was this: the compositor at the press — the one called Felix, the surviving child, the one whose name sat in the ledger like a coin worn smooth from handling — had hands that did not match his voice. His voice was young and slightly too high, pitched the way a person pitches their voice when they are reciting something rather than saying it. But his hands were old. Old the way mine were old, though I am thirty-one and have done nothing with them but write. Ink in the creases, gone to a blue-black shadow no pumice stone fully lifts. A callus on the right middle finger, hard as a bit of type metal. A small white scar across the left thumb, the kind a composing stick gives you when it slips. I knew those hands. My father was a clerk at the *Vellamont Advertiser* for nineteen years, and I grew up watching men with exactly those hands carry trays of lead around like trays of bread, and I knew that whoever wore those hands had spent ten thousand hours standing at a case setting type, letter by letter, into words.

And Felix Ashgrove, according to every record the Bureau possessed, had been apprenticed to his father's trade at fourteen and died of marsh fever at seventeen.

That was the arithmetic that brought me back. Not item eleven. Arithmetic.

* * *

The bell over the shop door had a cracked note in it, like a coin dropped on stone. It rang twice before anyone came, long enough for me to take inventory of the front room: job posters for a funeral society, receipts for a chandler, a stack of playbills for a theater I was fairly sure had closed last spring. Everything printed neatly, everything slightly out of date. A shop living on its reserves, or a shop pretending to.

Then he came through the curtain at the back, and I watched him become Felix.

It was not a performance in any way I could have sworn to in an affidavit. He didn't change his face or square his shoulders. What changed was smaller and worse: the set of his eyes. On my first visit he had looked at me the way people look at census takers — past me, through me, at the door behind me, measuring how fast it could be shut. Now he looked at me directly, met my eyes, held them a half-second longer than comfort allowed, and I understood that the directness itself was the costume. Felix Ashgrove had nothing to hide, so Felix Ashgrove looked you full in the face.

"Monsieur — Madame — " He caught himself. "You've come back."

"Madame Ilse Verek," I said, though I'd told him this already. Repetition is a tool. You use it the way a printer uses a quoins: to hold everything still while you look for the loose piece. "Bureau of Enumeration. I have some follow-up questions about your household return. May I?"

He gestured me to the same chair as before, and I sat, and I opened my satchel with the ceremony of a surgeon opening his kit, and all the while part of my mind was doing something it had no business doing, which was noticing him.

He was not what the file suggested. Files never suggest anything; that is their virtue. The file said: *Ashgrove, Felix, b. 1187, apprentice compositor.* The young man pouring lamp oil into the little stove so the room wouldn't be cold for the government's convenience was perhaps twenty-two, narrow through the shoulders, with his father's heavy brows and none of his father's girth. Grief had been at him — there was a rawness under his skin that grief leaves, like the mark a bandage leaves when it comes off — but grief alone didn't account for the wariness. I have interviewed widows by the hundred. Widows look at you like weather, something to be endured and gotten through. This one looked at me like a blade being balanced on its point.

"Now." I uncapped my pen. "Your household return lists one child. Your father's death registration listed two. Can you account for the difference?"

"My sister died," he said.

Ah. So we had progressed. On my first visit there had been no sister at all; he had answered 'one' to my question about children with the flat finality of a man laying down a card he couldn't take back. Now, three days later, there was a sister, recently deceased, unrecorded anywhere in the Bureau's files. The story was growing a new room.

"I'm sorry for your loss," I said, and meant it, which annoyed me. "Her name?"

A pause. Not a long one. The pause of a man choosing between two answers rather than searching for one.

"Marguerite," he said. "She died in the winter. Before Father."

"And she wasn't registered? Vellamont requires deaths to be registered within—"

"—thirty days, yes. We — our household has not been diligent. With paperwork." Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice, and I got the absurd impression he was embarrassed, actually embarrassed, at lying badly. As if dishonesty were a craft and he resented doing poor work at it even in service of his neck. "After Father died there didn't seem to be a point."

"There is always a point, monsieur. The point is that the city keeps its accounts." I wrote *Marguerite Ashgrove, d. winter, unregistered* in my book, and watched him watch me write it. That is the trick of the pen, the one they don't put in the instructions: you don't write to record. You write to make the other person understand that recording is happening. Most confessions in Vellamont are written by the pen, not the mouth.

But Felix Ashgrove did not confess. He folded his hands — those hands, those telltale, beautiful, incriminating hands — in his lap, and waited, patient as furniture.

I should explain something about my position, so that what I did next can be judged fairly. In the Bureau, enumerators are meant to be instruments. You are a pen with legs. You do not interpret; interpretation happens above you, in rooms you will never enter, among supervisors whose names you learn only from memoranda. An enumerator who interprets is like a scale that decides what it weighs. And yet. Three days ago a man had told me he was an only child, and today he had a dead sister named Marguerite whom no church register, no burial society, no gravedigger's tally in this parish had ever heard of, and the question was not whether the discrepancy would go upstairs. It would. The question was what shape it would have when it got there.

I could send it up as fraud. Fraud was the word the Bureau liked, short and load-bearing.

Or I could send it up as error. Clerical error. Grief-stricken household, confused returns, no criminality implied.

The difference between those two sentences was, very possibly, the difference between a fine and a wagon. And I had known this man for twenty minutes total in my life, and I was sitting in his shop weighing his life on my tongue like a grocer weighing cheese, and the whole apparatus of the state was arranged behind me in ranks and columns so that I would not have to feel the weight of it at all.

"Your father," I said, changing course because I wanted to see him steer. "Marius Ashgrove. He held the shop license jointly with a partner once, did he not? Before the partnership dissolved?"

Something flickered. "Before my time."

"It would have been. The dissolution was eighteen years ago." I let my pen rest. "You were — what, four?"

"Yes," he said, and gave me the half-second of direct eye contact again, and this time I saw the seam in it, the tiny hesitation of a man doing division in his head. Because Felix Ashgrove, born 1187, would have been four in 1191, yes, the arithmetic worked, the arithmetic always worked, that was what made a good lie lie-shaped. But the man doing the arithmetic was not calculating from memory. He was calculating from a document. From a birth year he had read somewhere, perhaps in a ledger, perhaps on a stone.

Compositor's hands. Dead brother's birthday.

I want to be honest, since honesty is the luxury I'm spending this entire account on: at that moment I felt not triumph but something closer to vertigo. Because there are two explanations for a man wearing a dead boy's name, and one of them is fraud, and the other is fear, and in Vellamont these days the second explanation was becoming common enough that the Bureau had a form for it. I had seen the forms move through the office in bundles, tied with grey string. *Households requiring correction.* I had never asked what correction meant. Instruments don't ask.

"You'll forgive the questions," I said, and heard my own voice come out gentler than item eleven permitted. "The Bureau reconciles its registers after a death. When the numbers disagree, someone must ask. It isn't accusation. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," he repeated, and for just a moment the costume slipped — not the face, the voice. It dropped half an octave, went dry and tired and real, and he said, "My father used to say the city was just arithmetic that forgot it was counting people." Then he heard himself, and blinked, and the young voice came back like a curtain falling. "He said that. Once."

Silence, then. The stove ticked. Somewhere in the back of the shop a case of type sat in the dark holding ten thousand letters, each one identical to its fellows except for one small nick, and I thought, absurdly, about nicks. About how a printer tells his sorts apart from another foundry's. By the flaw. By the one thing that makes it distinguishable.

"Madame Verek." He leaned forward slightly. "Will there be — consequences? For the sister. For the late registrations. I can bring documents, if—"

"What documents?" I asked, sharper than I intended.

And there it was, the flinch, quickly mastered. "Whatever the Bureau requires."

I closed my book. I capped my pen. I did several things deliberately and slowly, the way one does when one needs a moment to decide what one is about to say, and in that moment I understood that I was already off the instrument's rail. I had been off it since I noticed the hands. An instrument does not choose which discrepancy to soften. An instrument does not sit in a liar's shop thinking, *he lies like a man who has never needed to lie before this year, and is teaching himself the craft in order to survive it,* and feel something that rhymed, horribly, with respect.

"Consequences depend on the file," I said at last. "The file depends on what I write. I write what I find." Which was true, and also a message, and we both knew it, and neither of us said so. "I may need to come back once more. There will be a reconciliation notice within the fortnight. Until then, monsieur — get the sister registered. Whatever the truth of your household is, get it onto paper. Paper is the only place this city believes people exist. Make sure it believes in all of you."

He stood to see me out, and at the door, as I settled my satchel, he said — quietly, without the young voice, without any voice I could attach to Felix or to anyone — "Why are you telling me this?"

Because you have your father's brows and a dead boy's name and hands like my father's colleagues, and because the grey-string bundles moved through the office yesterday, two more of them, and I signed the receipt without reading the contents, and I have begun to dream about strings. That is what I did not say.

"Because I am an enumerator," I said, "and I dislike inaccurate records. In either direction."

* * *

Outside, the lane smelled of canal weed and hot iron, and evening was coming down the way it does in Vellamont, sideways, off the water. I walked toward the customs house to file my day's returns and got as far as the corner before I stopped.

In my book, under *Marguerite Ashgrove, d. winter, unregistered*, my pen rested against the line where I was supposed to classify the discrepancy. Fraud, or error. Two words. One of them weighed nothing and the other weighed a wagon.

I thought about item eleven. *Without sentiment.*

I thought about a man answering 'one' when the true answer was more complicated than one, and writing, in effect, a misprint — and how the Bureau's whole edifice rested on the proposition that the printed word and the world were the same thing, and how every printer I had ever met knew, better than any clerk alive, that the plate and the truth were different objects entirely, and that the gap between them was where all the mischief and all the mercy of the world got in.

I turned back a page and wrote, in my best Bureau hand, *discrepancy clerical — follow-up pending — household cooperative,* and then I closed the book, and my hands were not quite steady, and I stood on the corner like a woman deciding which bridge to cross.

Above the print shop behind me, a lamp had been lit in the upstairs window. Two windows, I noticed now. Two rooms lit, in a household of one.

I began walking to the customs house, and I did not file my returns that night, and I told myself the reason was fatigue. It was the first lie I had ever written in my own interior ledger, and I found, uneasily, that my hand did not shake at all.
