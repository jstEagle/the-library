## 5. The Misprint

My father had a saying about cleaning: *a press room is never dirty, it is merely between editions.* He said this while sweeping ink dust into corners he would later call "archival deposits," and I have inherited both the saying and the corners.

It was the third of Frostmonth, and I was between editions in a way that would have made him proud. The winter orders were done—two hundred funeral cards for the Vellamont Undertakers' Guild, each one promising eternal rest with a crispness that suggested the guild had personally inspected the afterlife—and the spring contracts hadn't yet arrived. That left me with lamp oil, a bucket of lye water, rags gone gray as old snow, and no excuse left to avoid the press room's back wall.

The back wall is where presses go to confess. Every shop has one: the shelf where rejected plates, spoiled formes, and misprints wait to be melted down and reborn as something more obedient. Father called it the Purgatory Shelf. He'd let things sit there for years, on the theory that a mistake might someday become useful, which is either printer's wisdom or hoarding with a trade name attached.

I had been putting off the Purgatory Shelf since his death. Eleven months of putting it off, to be precise. There are griefs you can schedule around, and then there are shelves.

But the lye water was mixed, the lamplight was good, and the alternative was re-reading the funeral card copy for the fortieth time, so I rolled up my sleeves and started at the top.

* * *

You learn things about a city from its rejected plates. The first one I pulled down was a playbill from six years back—the year the theater burned—announcing a comedy that never opened. The second was a shipping manifest with a transposed tonnage figure that had allegedly cost a merchant four hundred crowns and cost him, when I checked the arithmetic, nothing at all. People will reject a plate over anything. Pride mostly.

The third layer down was census work.

That stopped me. Not because census work was strange—we printed for the Bureau of Records like every licensed shop in Vellamont—but because I remembered this job. Two years ago, spring, a runner had brought us a locked case and a supervisor who stood in the shop for three hours while we set and proofed it, then took the plates away himself, signed for them, and left a tip so large it felt like an apology. Father had said nothing about it except "well-paid" and "don't ask," which in my father's vocabulary were the same sentence.

Here was one of those plates, or its twin. Census master list, district seven. Households in columns, names in rows, and beside each entry a code in small capitals that I assumed meant something bureaucratic and dull.

I read the codes the way you read anything set in your own typeface: without thinking, the way you don't think about breathing.

Then I found our address.

*Ashgrove & Son, Printers, Lantern Row.* And beside it, in the same neat capitals as every other entry: **CORRIGENDA — VERIFY OCCUPANCY — SECOND NOTICE ISSUED.**

I want to tell you that I understood it immediately. I didn't. My first thought was that there had been a clerical error, which tells you something about how thoroughly a person can train themselves to believe in errors rather than intentions. Errors are weather. Intentions are architecture. Nobody wants to live inside someone's intention.

*Verify occupancy.* Verify what occupancy? We had occupied that building for thirty years. My father had set type in it until the week he died. If the Bureau wanted to verify occupancy, they could have asked me, and I would have told them, and—

And then I understood, all at once, standing in my own lamplight with ink drying on my hands, exactly what they wanted verified. Because the plate didn't say *Ashgrove & Son.* It said *Ashgrove, F., Printers,* and beneath that, in the household column, a number.

Two children.

* * *

Let me explain about the two children, though if you've lived in Vellamont long enough you may already know how a family acquires a ghost.

There was a Felix Ashgrove once. Born eleven years before me, died of the river fever at nine, mourned with the full apparatus of the trade—memorial cards, black-bordered broadsides, a funeral my father printed free of charge for himself as practice in the one craft no compositor wants to master. After Felix died, my mother went quiet in a way that never quite reversed itself, and my father went to the Bureau and did something that was either fraud or mercy depending on where you stand: he amended the household registration to read *two children,* and he raised me under Felix's name.

Not secretly. That's the part people get wrong. There was no hidden child, no second bed smuggled in and out through windows. I slept in the same room I sleep in now; I simply answered to Felix. School rolls, apprenticeship papers, the guild registry—all of it said Felix Ashgrove, and the boy who answered to it learned early that a name is just the most commonly used piece of type in your case, and that some sorts wear out faster than others.

Why? Partly grief—my father could not bear to strike his dead son from the record, and in a print shop, striking something from the record is the one sin. Partly practicality: a registered son inherits a license, a lease, a trade. An unregistered daughter inherits questions. And partly—I only understood this years later—because my father had looked at the world the Bureau was building, the world of lists and verifications, and decided that his family would keep one door open that no clerk could lock. A person who officially does not exist cannot be officially removed.

He thought it was armor. Standing there, reading his own address off a government plate stamped **CORRIGENDA,** I learned it was a target.

Because here is what the code column meant, once I made myself look at the other entries instead of hiding behind ours. Every household marked **CORRIGENDA** shared a family resemblance. The widow Hessel whose late husband's registration had lapsed. The Ferro family, whose eldest had left for the coast and been struck from the rolls by a clerk who apparently believed emigration was contagious. The three households on Tanner's Walk that the neighborhood knew, quietly, as keeping unregistered kin—cousins arrived from the provinces, a grandmother whose papers had drowned with her village's records in the flood year. People who existed in ink but not in law. People like me.

Forty-one entries. District seven alone. And this was a *rejected* plate—a proof, a draft, the version they'd pulled for correction—which meant somewhere in the Bureau there was a corrected version, approved, final, waiting.

Waiting for what? The plate didn't say. Plates never do. They just hold the words; the meaning arrives later, wearing boots.

* * *

I sat down on the melting pot's stool, which is not a thing a careful printer does, and I held the plate under the lamp for a long time.

Terror, I should say, is not a dramatic experience in a print shop. It doesn't arrive with trumpets. It arrives the way a misregistration arrives: everything still looks normal, but the colors have shifted slightly out of true, and you know that every sheet from now until you fix it will be wrong. I sat on that stool and looked around my press room—Father's press, Father's ink knife, Father's chair with the cane seat worn to the shape of him—and everything was normal, and everything was out of true.

They knew. Or they suspected, which for the Bureau amounts to the same thing with a delay attached. *Second notice issued.* Someone had come twice to verify our occupancy and had been satisfied—or bought, or lied to—and the entry remained. And Ilse—

Ilse. The census taker with the patient pen and the question that had hung in my parlor like smoke: *how many children?* I had said one. She had written something else. At the time I'd told myself she'd made an error, or abbreviated, or any of the hundred harmless things a tired clerk does with a pen. Now I knew what she'd written, or at least what the record already said, and the question rearranged itself: had she copied the old lie onto her fresh page, or had she caught it?

Either way, she'd find it again. People like Ilse always find things again. It's the whole profession.

And the purge—if that's the word, and I've had months since to search for a better one and never found it—wasn't coming. It was *typeset.* Somewhere in this city, right now, a compositor with steady hands and no imagination was setting the final orders, pulling clean proofs, locking the forme. The machinery of removal was already inked. All that remained was the pull of the lever.

Which meant the only question left in the world was the oldest question in my trade:

Who gets to print the truth, and how fast can they carry it?

* * *

Here is the thing about proof. I mean the word in both senses, because in a print shop they are the same sense.

A proof is a test impression—you pull it to see what the forme will do before you commit to the edition. And proof, the other kind, evidence, testimony, the thing that convicts—it works identically. It is an impression taken from reality before reality gets revised. This plate was a proof in both directions: a trial printing of the Bureau's intention, and hard evidence of what that intention was. Forty-one households. Codes. Second notices. A state's handwriting, in state-approved type, on state-owned metal.

You can deny a rumor. You can burn a letter. But a locked plate, cast and machined and numbered by the Bureau's own foundry—that is difficult to call a forgery when the serial numbers match your own inventory.

I turned it over. Foundry mark, yes. Plate number 7-C-114. Date of casting, fourteen months ago. Fourteen months. They had been assembling this list since before Father died. While I set wedding invitations and funeral cards and almanacs, the state had been setting its own edition across town, and mine was in it.

I did not cry. I want that recorded, even if only I ever read the record. I did not cry, because crying is water and water ruins paper, and I had suddenly become a man with a very specific job to do.

The job was this: the plate could not stay on the Purgatory Shelf, and it could not go back to the Bureau, and it could not be melted—not that plate, not while forty-one households were still standing and their names were still legible in the metal. It had to be *printed.* Pulled, editioned, scattered. Every name on that list needed to exist in hundreds of copies, passed hand to hand, tucked into bread loaves and hymnals and coat linings, until the list belonged to the whole city and could not be corrected out of existence. Because that is the one power a censor truly fears: not the truth itself, but the truth in plural. Burn one sheet and there are five hundred more. Arrest one printer and the type is already distributed across ten thousand kitchens.

Proof can be printed. That was the thought, arriving whole, the way the best thoughts do—already typeset, as it were.

The problem, of course, was logistics, and logistics in Vellamont currently meant checkpoints. Three of them between Lantern Row and anywhere worth being: the bridge guard, the customs house, the cathedral gate. Each one demanding papers. And the papers I could safely show were fewer than the papers I owned, which is a particular kind of poverty.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tomorrow's problem could wait its turn in the queue, like all well-behaved problems. Tonight's problem was simpler and heavier: get the plate out of this room, out of this building, somewhere no clerk would think to look, and do it before anyone came asking why the Ashgrove shop had a Bureau plate it had never been issued.

I wrapped it in a rag, then in oilcloth, then in a second rag, because paranoia is just craftsmanship applied to fear. Nine pounds of lead and truth. It fit in the satchel. Everything important in my life, I noticed, fit in the satchel; my father had taught me to measure the world by what could be carried, and I had resented the lesson, and here it was, saving me.

I pried up the floorboard beneath the type gallery—the loose one, the one Father always claimed was warped and never let anyone repair—and lowered the bundle into the dark. Then I swept the lye water over the shelves, erased the day's dust, closed the shutters, and trimmed the lamp.

Through the window, across the lane, the tailor's shop was dark too. The Halloran family, I thought. Were they on the plate? I tried to remember. I had read forty-one entries and retained perhaps six, because terror is a poor archivist. I would need to read it again. Carefully. Memorize it. A compositor's memory is a second press, and it never melts down.

Tomorrow I would start learning the route. Tomorrow I would find out who in this city could be bribed, blessed, or trusted, and in what proportions. Tomorrow I would need a smuggler, probably, and possibly a miracle, and I had no idea where either was sold.

But tonight, lying awake above my father's shop, listening to the river work at its pilings, I held one fact against the dark like a sort held tight in a composing stick: they had made their list in metal, and metal remembers, and I had it.

Somewhere across the city, a forme was being locked for the purge orders.

I intended to be faster than the lever.
