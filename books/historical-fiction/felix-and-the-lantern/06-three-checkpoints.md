## 6. Three Checkpoints

My father used to say that a city is just a sentence, and every street a word in it, and if you read it carefully enough you can tell where the grammar is about to turn ugly. He said this while setting type, usually, which is the posture of a man who believes he can fix any sentence by rearranging it. He was wrong about that. Some sentences you can only survive.

I spent the morning of the third day after finding the plate reading our city like a man proofing his own death notice.

The route to the underground printers was not written anywhere, of course. Father Anselm had given me only a name — *ask at the chandlery on Tanner's Row for the man who sells tallow by the pound and light by the inch* — and a look that suggested I should be cleverer than I felt. The rest I had to compose myself, the way one composes anything: line by line, checking each character before locking it into place.

The route ran from our door on Cooper's Lane down to the Weir Bridge, across to the customs house on the far bank, then up through the cloth quarter to the cathedral gate, and beyond it into the warren of streets where the old Augustinian priory had been gutted and forgotten. Three checkpoints between my press room and theirs. Three places where a man with a satchel might be asked a question he could not answer.

Three places where *Felix Ashgrove* might be asked to produce papers belonging to a dead boy.

I locked the shop, hung the CLOSED sign that had hung there since the funeral, and went out into a morning so bright and ordinary it felt like an insult.

* * *

The Weir Bridge first.

Every city has a bridge it loves to paint, and Vellamont's is the Weir: seven arches of honey-colored stone, lamp posts added forty years ago by a guild that wanted its name remembered, the whole span crowded from dawn to dusk with fishwives and wool factors and children daring the current below. On a market day you can cross it without being seen at all, swallowed whole by the crowd. But the soldiers had changed the arithmetic.

They'd built the guard post into the second arch — a timber hut that hadn't existed a month ago, squatting against the old stone like a barnacle. Two guards at the near end, one at the far, and a fourth who did nothing but look at faces. That last one was the dangerous kind: young, bored, and therefore attentive. Boredom is the sharpest instrument the state ever issues.

I crossed as a customer. There's a fishmonger halfway along the span, and nothing marks a man as harmless faster than haggling over eels. I bought half a pound I didn't want, wrapped in paper I would later burn, and let my eyes do their work while my mouth complained about prices.

The procedure was simple enough to be terrifying. No queue, no inspection of goods. The face-looker simply watched, and when something caught his interest, he beckoned, and the beckoned party stepped into the hut, and I watched a carter in a grease-stained apron step out again ten minutes later looking like a man who'd been asked to carry water in his hands. Papers shown. Papers copied? Papers *read*, at least — slowly, while the young guard watched the reader's face rather than the document, learning what fear looked like on this particular stretch of stone.

What papers would satisfy them? A guild card, maybe. A tax receipt. A letter of passage for merchants. All of those bore names, and all names could be checked against lists, and somewhere in the census office there was now a list with my address on it, flagged for correction, and I did not know what word they'd written beside it. *Unregistered.* *Anomalous.* *Correction pending.* The state has a genius for words that sound like clerical errors and mean the gallows.

And here was the thing I understood standing among the eel barrels with my heart going like a compositor's stick against the case: it wasn't the papers I couldn't show. It was the *name*. Any document I produced would have to say Felix Ashgrove, and Felix Ashgrove had died at nine years old of the winter fever, and if anyone in Vellamont kept records of the dead — and the state, I was learning, kept records of everything — then the moment my name touched their ledger, the sentence would resolve. The boy in the print shop who didn't exist would become, officially, a fraud wearing a dead child's coat.

I paid for the eels. I crossed back the way I came, unhurried, a man with nothing in his satchel but fish.

* * *

The customs house was worse, because the customs house was honest about what it was.

It sat on the far bank where the canal traffic came in from the river — a long gray building with the civic seal carved over the doors and a yard behind it where barges unloaded under the eyes of examiners. In my father's time, the customs house had been a place of arithmetic: duties assessed, ledgers balanced, bribes exchanged with the warmth of old friendship. Now there were soldiers in the yard, and a new sign painted in official black:

**ALL CARGO SUBJECT TO INSPECTION. ALL PERSONS ENTERING THE PRECINCT MUST STATE NAME AND PURPOSE.**

State your name. Such a small request. Such an enormous one.

I didn't enter the precinct. I walked the perimeter instead, along the public towpath, the way a woman might walk for her health or a clerk for his thoughts, and I counted. Two sentries at the water gate, checking barge manifests. One officer at the pedestrian door, seated at a table carried outside for the purpose, with a register open before him and a soldier standing behind his chair. Everyone entering wrote their name in the book. Everyone leaving was glanced at against what they'd written.

A book of names, sitting in the open air. I thought of Ilse's pen pausing over *number of children,* and how a pause is sometimes the loudest thing a person says.

But the customs house had a flaw, and flaws are the only things I trust anymore. The towpath continued past the precinct wall to a flight of stairs — the coal stairs, we'd always called them, though no coal had come up them in years — and the stairs climbed the embankment directly into the cloth quarter, bypassing the pedestrian door entirely. Anyone coming off a barge could vanish up those steps without writing a single letter in the officer's book. Which meant, surely, someone had noticed. Which meant the stairs were either unwatched because they were useless, or watched because they were useful.

There was one way to find out, and I hated it. I would have to come up those stairs carrying something worth stopping for, and see whether anyone stopped me.

Not today. Today I only stood at the bottom of the coal stairs and looked up at the daylight at the top and thought: *this is a corridor in a forme I haven't locked yet, and I don't know what's set in the lines above it.*

Then I made myself walk away, because a scout who lingers becomes a mark, and I had one more door to read.

* * *

The cathedral gate surprised me, and I don't love being surprised.

I had expected soldiers. What I found was Father Anselm himself, standing in the gatehouse arch in a cassock gone gray at the seams, arguing theology with a sergeant of the watch who clearly wished he were anywhere else.

"—and I'm telling you, Sergeant, that the cathedral close is consecrated ground, and the civil writ runs to the *gate*, not through it, and if your captain disagrees he's welcome to take it up with the bishop, whose opinion of your captain I will happily convey."

"The captain says—"

"I know what the captain says. The captain says many things." Anselm's voice was mild as milk and immovable as the stones around him. "You may search the alms baskets. You may not search the confessional. You may stand at this gate until your boots rot. Good day."

The sergeant withdrew with the expression of a man conceding a point he intended to raise again in a report. And Anselm turned, saw me standing at the edge of the square like a stray dog considering a doorway, and his face did something complicated — relief and worry wrestling to a draw.

"Compositor," he said. "Come to admire the architecture?"

"Come to count doors," I said. "How many ways out of your close?"

His eyebrows rose a fraction. Then he gestured me toward the gatehouse bench, out of the sightline of the retreating sergeant, and sat beside me with the groan of a man twice his age, which he may well have been.

"You've been walking the route," he said. Not a question.

"Bridge, customs house, here." I kept my voice low. "The bridge checks faces. The customs house wants names in a book. Your gate wants—" I nodded toward the square. "Him."

"Him is the least of it. They rotate the watch every six days; the next one won't argue theology, he'll simply bar the gate on the captain's order and dare the bishop to object. And when they bar it—" he tapped the arm of the bench, once, "—there's the crypt passage, which exits through the charnel house on Water Street, and which I will deny knowing about with great conviction and considerable detail."

"And the passage is wide enough for—"

"For whatever you're planning to carry, which you still refuse to tell me." He studied me with eyes that had heard ten thousand confessions and learned to wait out all of them. "You know, in thirty years of hearing sins, I have developed a nose for cargo. Guilt has a shape. Fear has a weight. Whatever you're carrying, you're carrying it like a reliquary."

I said nothing. He sighed.

"Very well. Keep your secret. But hear the rest, because the rest has a clock in it." He leaned closer. "The purge orders go to typeset within the fortnight. I know this because the man who sets them drinks at the Bell and Anchor and prays loudly enough that God tells me things. When the orders are set, every gate in this city will have a list nailed beside it, and every list will have been printed on a press, and the men who run those presses will believe what they print because printing makes it true. That is the devil's favorite sacrament, compositor. You of all people should understand it."

I did understand it. I had spent my life making ink tell truths and knew exactly how easily it tells lies.

"So the route is possible," I said. "Bridge, customs house, gate. Possible, with luck and forgery and your passage."

"It is possible," Anselm agreed. "Now let me tell you what it costs, because nothing in this city is free anymore. The bridge will cost you a face they don't remember. The customs house will cost you a name that survives their book. My gate will cost you—" he spread his hands, taking in the cassock, the close, the sergeant due back in six days, "—whatever I have left, which is less than you think and more than they suspect."

He looked at me then, straight on, and beneath the performance of the irritable priest I saw the other thing, the thing he'd lost his post for: a man who had decided, at some cost I could only guess at, that some lists must never be completed.

"Go home, compositor," he said gently. "Set your type. And when you're ready to tell me what we're carrying, bring bread. We'll want bread, where this is going."

* * *

I took the long way home, along the river, because I wanted to see the whole sentence at once.

From the far bank, Vellamont arranged itself the way it does in engravings: the bridge with its pretty lamps, the customs house flying the civic colors, the cathedral spire stitching sky to stone. A city that looked like a promise. And underneath the engraving, invisible unless you knew to look, the new ink: the guard hut squatting in the arch, the register book lying open in the yard, the sergeant's post at the gate. Three small marks, like printer's errors, except they weren't errors. Someone had set them deliberately, line by line, and locked the forme, and was waiting for the press.

That was the thought that followed me home across the bridge, past the young face-looker who glanced at me and dismissed me — a fish-buyer, a nobody, a ghost — and up Cooper's Lane to the dark windows of the shop.

Because here is what a compositor knows that a soldier doesn't: a forme is never finished until it's locked. Every line can be corrected. Every error can be caught at proof. The purge orders were not yet printed. The lists were not yet nailed to gates. Somewhere between the type case and the press, there was still room to reach in and pull a character, change a word, break the line.

They had made the city into a map of everyone they meant to erase.

But I had grown up in a print shop, and I knew something else about maps: whoever holds the plate decides what gets printed.

I unlocked the door, lit the lamp, and went down to the press room to start planning what I would need — and stopped on the stairs, because there was a folded note wedged in the door jamb, and the handwriting on it was one I recognized from a census form.

*Your household has been flagged. They come for the tailor's tomorrow. — I.*

Across the lane, in the tailor's darkened windows, a lamp was still burning.
