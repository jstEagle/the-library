## 7. A Smuggler's Rate

The first thing you learn on the canals of Vellamont is that everything has a rate. Brandy from the south shore, a coin a crate. Salt, two coins, because salt is honest and honest things cost more to move past dishonest men. A person who needs to be somewhere without being seen — well, that depends on the person, and on how hard the magistrates are looking that week, and on whether the person cries. I don't carry criers. Criers are ballast that talks.

I had worked the Ropewalk Canal for eleven years, long enough to know which moorings sagged and which constables took their tea at which hours and exactly how much of my cargo I could declare before declaring it became a confession. I was, if I say so myself, very good at not being caught, which is a profession like any other, with its apprenticeships and its hazards and its terrible pension plan, which is none.

So when the compositor came down the water stairs at Coalter's Slip on a gray morning with a satchel held against their chest like a breastplate, I knew before they said a word what kind of fare this would be. The expensive kind. The kind with eyes.

"You're Dario," they said. Not a question. That annoyed me slightly. My reputation is supposed to arrive ahead of me wearing better clothes.

"I'm whoever paid the boy at the fish market to tell you that," I said. "Get in. Mind the gunwale, she bites."

They stepped aboard my flat-bottomed barge with the careful feet of someone who had never been on a boat but had read about drowning. I noted the satchel again. Flat, wide, heavy in a way that wasn't books. Books shift when you carry them. Whatever this was moved as one piece, like a slab, like a thing cast rather than gathered.

"Where to?" I said.

"Upstream. Past the bridge."

"Past the bridge is customs water."

"I know."

"Knowing isn't paying." I put a boot up on the tiller and gave them the look I give all clients before the arithmetic starts, the one that says I am a reasonable man being reasonable. "Past the bridge is triple. Past the customs house is another double on top, and past the cathedral gate I don't go at night unless there's a festival, and there's no festival."

"There will be," they said, "if this goes right."

That got a laugh out of me, which I regretted immediately, because laughing meant I'd already decided to take the job and we both knew it. Compositors. Printers. They set type in your head the same way they set it in a forme — one letter at a time, until the whole sentence reads itself and you find you've agreed to something.

"What's the cargo?" I asked.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters the way weather matters. I don't need to know if it's raining. I need to know if it's the kind of rain that sinks boats."

They looked at the satchel. Then at me. Then at the water, which is what people do when they're deciding how much truth a stranger can hold without spilling.

"It's a printing plate," they said finally. "Copper. About so." They spread their hands the width of a hymnal. "It weighs less than a cat. What it *is* —" and here their voice did something interesting, dropped down into a register I hadn't heard yet, the voice under the voice — "what it is, is a list. Every household in Vellamont flagged for correction. For removal. It was struck by mistake at the census office and it ended up in my father's press room, and if it gets typeset into the purge orders instead of printed as proof of them, then a great many people are going to vanish off the face of this city and no one will ever know they were on a list at all."

I let the silence sit the way you let a knot sit before pulling it.

"And my address?" I said. "Is it on this list?"

Their mouth opened. Closed. And that, more than anything they'd said, told me the shape of the thing. Because a smuggler learns to read faces the way priests read scripture, and what I saw there wasn't *no*.

"Maybe not," they said. "Maybe not yet. But the man who owns the mooring I rent — the woman who sells me coffee — the tailor across the lane from the shop — they're on it. I've seen the letters. Their names are set in metal, Dario. Somebody *made* them, made them the way I make a page. And once a thing is set in metal, it wants to be printed. That's the whole nature of type. It exists to become many copies of itself. Either this plate becomes ten thousand proofs in every market square, or those names become empty houses by winter."

Now. Here is where an honest account requires me to confess something.

I did not take the job because of the names. I want that written plainly, in case anyone ever asks, in case anyone ever writes this down. I am not a martyr and I have never trusted anyone who volunteered to be one. Martyrs are just clients who've stopped negotiating, and a client who won't negotiate will get you killed twice — once by the authorities and once by their own conviction.

I took the job because of the fear.

Understand: I have carried fugitives. Priests, mostly, and once a tax auditor who had developed principles, which is worse than any crime. Fear is standard equipment on my barge. Most fear is wet fear, panicky, sloshing everywhere, wanting to be comforted. This was not that. The compositor stood in my boat holding a copper plate worth nothing as metal and everything as testimony, and their fear was *dry*. Set. Kerned. It had been measured and fitted and locked into the chase of them, and it was doing its work silently, the way good type does.

You cannot buy dry fear. But you can recognize it, and recognizing it changed the rate.

"Thirty coins," I said. "Fifteen now, fifteen on delivery."

"That's robbery."

"That's upstream, past two checkpoints, in a crate that soldiers might open, carrying evidence of treason. Robbery is my *discount* rate. Twenty-five of those thirty are for the part where I don't know what's in the box, and I intend to keep not knowing, which requires discipline, and discipline costs."

"I have eleven coins," they said. "And this."

And they reached into their coat and produced — I swear it on my mother's grave, who is alive and would be furious to hear it — a small tin lantern, dented, the glass fogged with age, and inside it a stub of candle.

"That's a lantern," I said.

"It was Felix's."

"Felix?"

"My brother," they said, in the tone of a door closing gently. "He died young. This was his. It's the only thing of his I have."

I looked at the lantern. I looked at the hands holding it — ink-stained, nicked, the fingers of someone who had spent a life picking twelve-point leads out of a composing stick without looking, and I thought: *there is no brother.* I didn't know it the way you know rain. I knew it the way you know a counterfeit coin — by the weight being slightly wrong, by the ring of it. People who grieve don't protect the name of the dead. They give it away freely, pin it to their coats. This one handled *Felix* like contraband.

But I'm a smuggler. Contraband is my favorite kind of goods. And everyone on the canals carries one name too many; mine happens to be Dario, which is not the name I was born with either, but that story costs extra and nobody's paid.

"Eleven coins and a dead man's lantern," I said. "Fine. Eleven now. Fourteen after. The lantern stays with you — I don't take heirlooms, they're haunted, and hauntings interfere with navigation."

"That's superstition."

"That's *rates*." I kicked off from the slip, and the barge swung her nose upstream, and the city began its slow rotation around us — the ropewalks, the tanneries, the laundry lines strung between windows like bunting for a country that didn't exist yet. "One condition. You ride as cargo. Under the tarpaulin from the bridge to the gate. If a guard asks, you're a shipment of wedding invitations from the Ashgrove press. Can you look like paper goods?"

"I can be still for four hours if I have to be. I set type for a living. Stillness is the trade."

"Hm." I steered us around a scow loaded with cabbages, waved at its pilot, who owed me money and pretended not to see me, which is also a rate. "And the invitations. Do you actually have invitations?"

"I can print some tonight."

"Good. Make them beautiful. Guards don't open beautiful things. Beauty is its own forged passport — nobody suspects a wedding of smuggling. Weddings are the most legal thing there is." I glanced back at them. "Who's getting married?"

"No one," they said. "Yet."

"Even better. Hope makes excellent ballast. Now get under the canvas and let me see how you fit."

*

They fit badly, which surprised me, given the stillness speech. Oh, they were quiet enough — quiet as a closed book — but they lay in the bottom of the barge with their spine rigid and their hand never leaving the satchel, even through the tarpaulin, even when I told them twice that the plate would survive a dunking better than they would. Copper doesn't drown. People do. It's the chief asymmetry of my trade.

We made the long bend past the tannery, where the water goes the color of weak tea and the smell does the guard work for free, and I used the quiet to do sums.

Here's what my sums told me. The compositor had come to me not because I was recommended — the fish boy was cheap advertising, and they'd known that, they were testing whether I'd lie, and I'd passed by lying immediately, which tells you something about the quality of my character and the quality of their judgment, both poor in instructive ways. No, they'd come to me because I move things past checkpoints, and they needed exactly one thing moved past exactly three checkpoints, and time was short in a way they hadn't said but wore like damp clothes.

Which meant someone else was involved. Nobody plans a three-checkpoint run alone. Alone, you plan a bridge crossing and a prayer.

And it meant the census office was leaking. A misprinted master plate doesn't wander into a private press room by accident like a cat. Someone at the census had let it wander, or failed to stop it, and that someone would eventually notice the leak and start plugging it, and the first place they'd plug is the river, because rivers are how everything guilty leaves Vellamont. I know. I'm the river.

So my professional assessment was: bad rates, worsening daily.

And my professional response to that assessment was, traditionally, to raise my prices or decline. Decline, ideally. Declining is the oldest tool in the smuggler's kit and the only one that has never once failed me.

Instead I found myself saying, as the bridge came in sight with its sentry box and its bored halberdier counting ducks, "When we pass, breathe slow and think about candle wax. Wax. Nothing melts trouble like a man thinking about wax."

The guard raised two fingers. I raised a hand and a yawn. "Wedding invitations, Ashgrove press," I called, unasked, which is either suspicious or disarming depending entirely on the boredom of the guard, and this one was bored unto holiness. He waved us through and went back to his ducks.

Past the bridge, I pulled the tarpaulin back. The compositor emerged blinking, hair full of canvas fibers, looking like something the tide had opinions about.

"You passed," I said.

"They didn't even look."

"Nobody looks. Looking is work. The whole empire runs on nobody looking." I steered us toward the north bank, under the willows where I moor when I'm thinking, and shipped the oar. "Here's the thing, compositor. I'll take your fourteen coins at the gate, and I'll take them gladly, and if you think that means I'm yours, you've been reading too much of what you print. I'm a ferryman. I go where the fare points. Tomorrow the fare could point elsewhere."

"I know," they said. And — this is the part I keep turning over, the part that ruined my evening coffee for a week — they *did* know. No flinch, no plea, no wounded faith. They just nodded, the way you nod at a price. "But you haven't taken the coins yet. So while you're still unspent — why did you say yes today? Thirty coins is high for a day's poling, but it isn't dying money. You could refuse this and live comfortably."

I should have lied. Lying is load-bearing in my line; pull it out and things settle in ways you don't expect. But the river was quiet, and the willows were doing their afternoon trick of making the light green and confessional, and there is a limit to how much counterfeiting one man can do before eight bells.

"Because of how scared you are," I said. "Not the amount. The *kind*. I've carried frightened people since before you had ink on your hands. They're afraid of guards, of dogs, of the wrong knock at the door. You're afraid of something else. You're afraid of being erased — of the whole city being erased, quietly, in metal, by men with clean hands — and you're afraid of it on behalf of people whose names you barely know. That fear doesn't come from a person. It comes from a *press*. From a shop where somebody taught you that what gets set in type becomes true. Somebody raised you to believe ink is a promise." I shrugged, embarrassed, and busied my hands with the mooring line. "My mother was a laundress. Nobody promised her anything in ink. But I've always wondered what it would be like to matter to somebody who keeps records. You people with your ledgers — you're the closest thing this city has to gods, and most of you don't even know it. So. There's my rate. The last coin isn't for the cargo. It's for standing next to the god for one day and finding out he's afraid too."

Silence from the compositor. Long enough that a heron landed, considered us, and left, judging us both.

"That," they said at last, "is the strangest reason anyone has ever given me for anything."

"Strangeness is included in the price."

They climbed out onto the bank, satchel hugged close, and turned back once. "The Ashgrove shop. After dusk, two nights from now. Bring the barge empty and bring rope for a crate. And Dario —" the lamplight from the street caught their face, and for a moment I saw it doubled, two faces in one, the living one and the printed one, Felix and whoever stood beneath — "don't ask me again what it costs to move a man's name across a city. You've already named it yourself."

Then they were gone up the water stairs, and I was alone with the willows and the tea-colored water and the distinct, unwelcome sensation of having acquired stakes.

Stakes. In eleven years I had kept my ledger perfectly balanced — coin in, cargo delivered, questions unanswered, conscience unconsulted. It was a clean book. Any compositor would have praised it.

And now some ink-fingered ghost of a person had walked aboard with a dead brother's lantern and a copper plate full of other people's names, and I could feel the entries shifting. Fourteen coins, due at the cathedral gate. One favor, unpriced, already accrued. One lie, pending, at the customs house, probably within the week.

I untied the line and poled back downstream toward the bridge, past the sentry, past the ducks, past the tailor's shop on the lane above the water where, I noticed, lamps were burning late — and not one lamp but several, moving, the way lamps move when a household is packing in the dark.

I had agreed to run one small crate past three checkpoints for a man who did not exist.

It occurred to me, watching those windows, that I had better find out soon what else had agreed to run alongside it — because whatever was coming down the river toward that print shop, it wasn't traveling at my speed.

It was traveling faster.
