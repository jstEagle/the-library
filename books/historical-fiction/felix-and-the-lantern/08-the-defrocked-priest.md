## 8. The Defrocked Priest

They took my collar the way you'd take a splinter from a child's finger—quickly, gently, with a great deal of reassurance that it was for my own good. Bishop Corvane himself did it, or rather he held out his hand and let me do it, which is the church's way of taking things: you are always permitted to hand over your own ruin so that no one need be seen to seize it.

That was four years ago. I have kept the collar in a drawer ever since, folded like a handkerchief at a funeral, because I could not decide whether throwing it in the river would be piety or vanity. Some nights I lean toward piety. Most nights I am honest enough to know it would be vanity—the dramatic gesture of a man who wants his disgrace witnessed.

I still say Mass. That is the joke of Vellamont, though nobody laughs where the soldiers can hear. The diocese struck my name from the rolls of the clergy, but no one has struck me from the rolls of the poor, and the poor do not care what the diocese thinks. They come to the cathedral gate at dusk because I am there, because there is bread, because Father Anselm—*defrocked* Father Anselm, *former* Father Anselm, *that man who used to be* Father Anselm—still knows the words. Grace does not require employment. I learned that the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything worth knowing.

What I lost my post for was this: I sheltered people without papers. Eleven of them, over three years, in the crypt and the bell tower and once, memorably, in the confessional for nine days while a customs clerk sat in the front pew waiting for God to move him along. A girl named Maren. A family called Ostrevant. A printer's apprentice whose name I never learned because he spoke only when spoken to and mostly not even then. When the diocesan inquiry came, I told them plainly: yes, I fed them, yes, I hid them, yes, I would do it again, and if your Grace requires a heresy for the record, write down that I believe Christ was undocumented in Egypt and the Holy Family did not present their papers at the border. That last sentence cost me the living. It was worth every syllable.

So now I keep the gate. Not the great doors—those belong to the chapter, locked and polished and attended by men still on the payroll—but the little postern gate in the north wall, where the beggars' bench sits and the drain runs out toward the canal. It is mine by the principle of adverse possession: I have been here so long, so uselessly, so faithfully, that the gate has become an extension of my person. Sweepers nod to me. Guards nod to me. Even the guards nod to me, because a defrocked priest is beneath suspicion the way a stone is beneath falling. Nobody watches the man who has already fallen.

I had noticed the compositor before I knew it was the compositor.

You develop an eye, keeping a gate. You learn to read people the way a toll-keeper reads carts—not their faces, which lie, but their loads, which cannot. Most people come through my gate carrying nothing heavier than hunger. But twice this week a figure had passed with a satchel worn high on the shoulder, the strap crossed the way printers cross them, so the weight sits against the spine and both hands stay free. And the hands—I confess I look at hands more than faces; faces can be turned away, but hands must work—a pair of ink-stained hands, the middle finger of the right calloused into a shiny ridge exactly where a composing stick rests. I have blessed enough printers to know that callus. It is the mark of a trade, the way my own palm carries the smooth spot of a breviary held forty years.

The first time, the figure went through the gate with the evening crowd and I thought nothing of it except the ordinary arithmetic: another person moving carefully through a city that has begun to require care. The second time, yesterday, the figure paused at the bench, hesitated over the bread as though deciding whether taking it would constitute evidence of something, and then took two pieces instead of one—and I thought, *ah, there is someone feeding a household of one who is pretending to be a household of none,* or possibly the reverse, and either way the arithmetic interested me.

I did not approach. Approach is a skill I have had to unlearn. For eleven sheltered souls I learned to wait—to let people come to the gate rather than going out to meet them, because a man walking toward you can be seen walking toward you, and in Vellamont these days being seen approaching someone is halfway to being accused of something together. So I swept my stretch of pavement, which is the oldest disguise in the world, and I waited, and this evening the figure came back a third time, and this time stopped.

"You're the priest they unfrocked," the compositor said. Not a question. The voice was pitched low and even, a voice managed the way you manage a press—firm pressure, no slippage. I had heard about the Ashgrove shop, of course. Everyone in the printing quarter had heard about the Ashgrove shop: old Ashgrove dead these two years, and the son Felix carrying on, a quiet young man, a careful young man, a young man who never gave the guild a reason to look twice. I looked at the hands again. Ink in the creases, ground in past washing, the stain of a life spent setting other people's words.

"I'm the priest they unfrocked," I agreed. "Though technically they didn't unfrock me. They declared I had never properly been a priest at all, retroactively, which is a remarkable feat of theology—unmaking a sacrament after the fact. If they can do it to ordination, mind, they can do it to baptism, and then to birth, and then—" I caught myself. "Forgive me. You didn't come for homiletics."

"No," said the compositor. "I came to ask about the third checkpoint."

Well. There it is, I thought, laid on my bench between us like a coin. The cathedral gate is the third checkpoint on the canal route to the tannery district, and everyone who needs to know knows it, and this person needed to know it, and had just told me so in front of God and the sweepers and one guard who was pretending to read a proclamation posted on the wall, a proclamation I happened to know was printed in our shop—or in Ashgrove's shop, I should say, set by those very callused hands, and I wondered whether the compositor felt the irony of typesetting the instruments of one's own surveillance. Probably did. Printers always feel the irony. It comes with the trade, like ink under the nails.

"The third checkpoint," I repeated. "And what would you want with the third checkpoint?"

"To know who keeps it."

"Officially? A rotation of city watchmen, six on the roster, changed monthly so they don't grow roots." I leaned on my broom. "Unofficially? Me. The watchmen check papers. I check nothing. I stand here, and things pass through my gate that shouldn't, and the watchmen have decided that watching me watch nothing is more trouble than it's worth. A corrupt arrangement, in its way. The most reliable kind."

The compositor's eyes went to the guard by the wall, then back. "And if someone needed to carry something through. Something small. Heavy in ways that aren't weight."

I had spent four years learning to hear sentences like that one without flinching, and I flinched anyway. Not at the request—at the familiarity of it. I had stood at this gate and heard versions of that sentence from eleven different mouths, and eight of those eleven were gone now: scattered, fled, or—Maren—worse than gone. Every time, I had told myself the same thing: *this time I will ask fewer questions, this time I will feel less, this time I will be a gate and not a heart.* And every time, within a week, I knew their names and their fears and what they ate for breakfast, and when they were taken I grieved like a father, which is to say uselessly and at length.

"Something heavy in ways that aren't weight," I said slowly. "In my experience, that means paper."

The compositor said nothing, which confirmed it.

"Paper is the heaviest cargo in Vellamont," I went on. "A sheet weighs nothing and can hang you. I've carried a lot of paper through this gate. Names, mostly. Lists. Once a whole register of baptisms I wasn't supposed to have copied, back when I still had a diocese to defy." I studied the young face—because up close, in the lamplight spilling from the wall sconce, the compositor was young, younger than the careful voice suggested, with the particular pallor of someone who has not slept properly since some specific night and counts the nights since. "You're Ashgrove's," I said. "Felix."

Something moved across the face then, quick as a misprint—there and corrected before you could swear you'd seen it. "Everyone says that," the compositor said.

"Everyone says it because it's true. Or because it's written down, which in this city has become the same thing." I set the broom against the wall. Here is where I should have been cautious. Here is where a wiser man—one of those sensible priests who kept his collar by keeping his mouth shut—would have said, *come back Sunday, speak to the sexton, I'm only a sweeper.* Instead I heard myself say: "Walk with me to the end of the wall. The guard's eyes are bad and his interest is worse."

We walked. Ten paces, no more, to the corner where the buttress shadows deepened and the drain whispered its way toward the canal.

"I'll tell you what I tell no one," I said quietly. "Not as a bargain. As a warning, because you've asked about checkpoints, and a man asking about checkpoints deserves to know what's coming behind them." I made sure of the guard's position first—he had given up on the proclamation and was picking his teeth, a man entirely at peace with the world's affairs. "There's a new census. You'll know this. What you may not know is what the census is *for*. It isn't counting. Counting is what governments do when they want to tax you. This is something else. This is the state learning where everyone sleeps."

The compositor had gone very still. Stillness, in my experience, is not calm. Calm breathes. Stillness holds its breath.

"There are lists," I said. "Being compiled now, in the census bureau, under seal. Households flagged for review. And when the review is finished—which will be soon, a fortnight, perhaps less—the findings go to the government print works, and the purge orders get typeset. I know it goes to the print works because everything in this city goes through print eventually, and because a clerk who owes me money drinks too much and talks too much, and last week he talked about a schedule. Typesetting begins within the fortnight. After that, the orders go out with the morning patrols, door to door, and the flagged households are 'corrected.'" I used the word the way you'd handle a dead rat, by the tail, at arm's length. "Corrected. As though a family were an error in a ledger."

The silence between us lasted long enough for the cathedral bells to mark the half hour, their bronze voices rolling over us, indifferent and magnificent. My bells. I still counted them as mine, though the chapter would dispute it.

"Why are you telling me this?" the compositor asked at last, and the voice had slipped at last, just slightly, the managed pitch fraying at the edge. "You don't know me."

No, I thought. I don't know you. I know your hands and your satchel and the way you take two pieces of bread, and I know that look—young people wear it when they have just understood that the world intends to kill them for a clerical error—and I know why I am telling you, and the why is not noble, and I could lie about the why, I have lied in the confessional booth facing better liars than myself, but standing in the shadow of my own cathedral I found I couldn't manage it.

"Because I buried what I saved," I said. "Do you understand? Eleven people through this gate. Eight gone. Maren was seventeen. They took her from the crypt on a Tuesday morning and I stood in my tower and watched the cart go and I did nothing, because doing something would have meant dying, and I told myself God required me alive. Perhaps He did. Perhaps He requires me alive now, for you, for whatever you're carrying. But I will tell you the truth of my situation, compositor, since you've been truthful with yours: I am not offering you sanctuary out of courage. I'm offering it out of debt. Everything I did for those eleven, I did too late, too briefly, too cowardly, and the interest on that debt compounds nightly. You are the first soul to walk up to my gate and ask directly, and I find that I would rather die paying than live defaulting. That's not heroism. That's bookkeeping. But it's honest bookkeeping, and in Vellamont at present, honesty of any kind is contraband."

The compositor looked at me for a long moment. In the shadow, the ink-stained hands had closed around the satchel strap, knuckles pale.

"The fortnight," the compositor said finally. "You're certain of the schedule?"

"As certain as a drunk clerk makes a man."

"Then it has to move sooner." Said half to me, half to the air, the voice of someone recalculating a layout, shifting type from one galley to another. "Everything has to move sooner."

"And what is *everything*?" I asked gently. "What is it you're carrying that weighs nothing and can hang you?"

For a moment I thought the answer would come. The compositor's mouth opened—and then closed, and I saw the discipline reassert itself, the press clamped down, the pressure held. "Heavy in ways that aren't weight," the compositor said. "That's all you need to hold. Can you hold it?"

I thought of Maren's cart. I thought of the drawer with the collar in it, folded like a shroud. I thought of the bishop's soft hand extended, and my own placing the collar into it, and how light it had seemed, and how heavy it had been every day since.

"I can hold it," I said. "Come Thursday, after compline, when the watch changes. Come through the gate with your cargo and don't stop to pray, however much you're tempted—stopping to pray at my gate is the one thing that would draw attention, since everyone knows I'm beyond the reach of prayer. And compositor—" they had already turned to go, and my voice came out rougher than I intended, the voice of the tower on Tuesday mornings, the voice that watched carts— "whatever your name is. The real one. The one that isn't on any census. You should know that this gate doesn't check papers. It never has. Whoever walks through it is whoever walks through it. That's the whole liturgy."

The compositor paused at the edge of the lamplight, half-turned, and for a heartbeat the managed face was open, and what was underneath it was young and terrified and resolved, all three at once, the exact expression I had seen on Maren the night she came through this gate with nothing but a bundle and a false name.

"Thursday," the compositor said. "After compline."

And then the shadows took them, and I was alone at my gate with the sweepings and the bells and the guard picking his teeth, and I went back to my broom, and I swept, and I prayed—for the first time in four years, actually prayed, not recited—and what I prayed was not for deliverance.

It was: *let me be worth the risk this time. Let this one count.*

Behind me, inside the cathedral I no longer belonged to, the bells rang the hour, and somewhere across the dark city a press waited, and a plate, and a fortnight that had just become ten days.

* * *

I did not sleep that night, which was unremarkable; I slept badly most nights, in the narrow cell the chapter grudgingly left me above the vestry, a room they could evict me from with a week's notice and hadn't yet because evicting me would mean admitting I existed. But this night the sleeplessness had a different texture. Usually my insomnia was a low fire, banked coals of old grief. Tonight it was a forge.

I got up and opened the drawer and looked at the collar.

Four years I had kept it there, unable to bury it, unable to wear it, and tonight I understood at last what the drawer had been for. Not preservation. Postponement. I had been keeping the collar in escrow, waiting for a moment when putting it on would mean something again—and I had imagined, in my more self-pitying hours, that the moment would come as vindication, the diocese reversing itself, some future bishop with a softer heart. It had never once occurred to me that the moment would arrive as a frightened compositor at my gate asking about checkpoints.

I did not put it on. Not yet. Wearing it Thursday would be theater, and theater gets people killed. But I took it out and held it, the linen gone soft at the fold, and I made myself say the words I had not said since the inquiry: *I am a priest.* Not *was.* Not *allegedly.* The diocese could retroact whatever it liked; a sacrament, once sealed, does not unseal, and if the church disagreed then the church and I would settle our accounts in a higher court.

Then I sat down with the stub of a pencil and a scrap of wrapping paper, and I did what I had done for eleven souls before and sworn never to do again, because knowing names is how grieving starts: I began making a list. Who else needed to know. Which watchmen on the third checkpoint's roster could be bought, which merely bribed, and which—there was one, a young one named Perrin with a stutter and a mother in the tannery district—might be turned for free, with truth alone. Where the crypt passage let out. How many lamps the route would need.

It was a map of my own vulnerability, drawn in pencil on rubbish paper, and it was the most alive I had felt since Maren.

Toward dawn, the bells rang, and I heard, faint and far off, the particular rhythm of hooves that meant a mounted courier, and I stood at my window and watched a rider in census-bureau grey clatter across the square below and rein in at the chapter house—not my gate, never my gate—and hand over a sealed dispatch. And I remembered the drunk clerk's talk of schedules, and I counted days on my fingers like a schoolboy, and the number I arrived at was worse than ten.

Eight, perhaps. Maybe seven.

The fortnight was shrinking. Whatever the compositor carried, whatever weighed nothing and could hang us all, it would have to fly sooner than Thursday now—and I had just promised a stranger my gate, my name, and the last shred of my usefulness, and somewhere across the sleeping city, in a print shop that smelled of ink and lamp oil, that stranger was presumably awake too, doing the same arithmetic, and neither of us yet knew that the schedule we were racing had just been shortened by the very state we were stealing from.

I blew out my lamp and knelt, because kneeling was the one posture they had never managed to confiscate.

*Let this one count,* I said again, to the dark, to whatever listened.

And the dark, which in Vellamont these days kept excellent records, said nothing—but it wrote it down.
