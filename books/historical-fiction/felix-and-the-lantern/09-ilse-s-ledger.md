## 9. Ilse's Ledger

My mother used to say that a census taker's hands were the cleanest hands in Vellamont, because they touched nothing but paper, and paper never bled. She said this with a certain pride, as though I had entered a holy order rather than a bureau. She is dead eleven years now, and I have come to understand that she was wrong in every particular but one: the paper does bleed. It simply does it later, and somewhere else, and never where anyone official is obliged to watch.

I live in two rooms above a chandler's shop on Rue des Oubliettes, which is either an ironic name or an honest one depending on the week. The rent is modest because the stairs are wicked and the chandler, Monsieur Perrin, is a widower who believes all government workers are functionally damned and treats me with the brisk courtesy one extends to the terminally ill. In the evenings he leaves a candle on the landing for me, which I have never decided whether to interpret as kindness or commentary.

It was by such a candle—my own lamp having guttered in a draft that comes through the window frame like an unpaid debt—that I sat down that night to reconcile my rolls.

This was not unusual. Reconciliation is the spine of my work. Every name collected in the field must be checked against the master registry, every birth entered, every death struck through with the single clean line we are taught to draw—the *deletion stroke*, they call it in training, as though it were a calligraphic flourish rather than what it is. I am told I have the neatest deletion stroke in the Third District. There is a commendation in a drawer somewhere that says so. Some nights I take it out and look at it the way one looks at a photograph of oneself from before an illness.

The work itself I have always loved, and this is the confession I cannot make at the bureau, where love of the work is considered either ambition or weakness depending on the day. But there is something in it—a household of eight rendered truly, a midwife's name spelled correctly at last, a street I walked a hundred times made permanent in ink. When I do the reconciliation properly, Vellamont exists on my desk. All of it. The whole crooked, arguing, candle-hoarding city, present and accounted for. I have thought for years that this was the closest thing to prayer my life contained: *everyone counted, everyone kept.*

You can see, perhaps, where the night went wrong. Prayer said over a falsified altar is still prayer. It is only the god who changes.

* * *

The discrepancy announced itself politely, as discrepancies do. A sum. My Tuesday route had yielded forty-one souls across six households; the master roll recorded thirty-seven. I ran the figures twice, then a third time with my finger under each line like a schoolgirl, because Ilse Verrec, who has never in nine years of service been off by so much as a chimney-sweep's apprentice, was off by four.

Four people. Not ink errors. Ink errors come in ones and are corrected in the margin in red. This was a block adjustment—four names removed from the Ferrand tenement on Clocher Lane, their lodgings reassigned on the register to "vacant pending survey." I knew the Ferrand tenement. Three floors, thin walls, a woman on the second floor who took in washing and whose husband had died in the spring floods. I had counted those four myself. I remembered the youngest, a boy with a cleft chin who had answered the door holding a wooden horse by its tail.

Vacant pending survey.

I pulled the prior quarter's roll. The family was there. I pulled the district index. The family was there, but stamped—newly stamped, the ink still smelling faintly of the press room, that hot-metal smell I had always found comforting and which now sat in my sinuses like a warning—with the survey mark. The mark that meant *flagged for correction*. We had been taught about the survey mark only six weeks ago, in a closed session conducted by a man from the Second Bureau who never told us his name and who used the phrase "administrative hygiene" four times, which I had privately considered excessive even for a bureaucrat.

Hygiene. As though people were soot.

Here is the thing about discovering you have been lied to: it does not arrive as thunder. It arrives as arithmetic. Forty-one against thirty-seven. I sat with my pen poised over the reconciliation sheet, and I realized that my next act would be the smallest possible act, almost nothing, a hair's breadth—and that it would divide my entire life into before and after. I could enter the adjustment as found. *Discrepancy noted, master roll confirmed.* Nine years of habit would carry my hand through it without my conscience being consulted at all. That is the genius of procedure; it lets your hand commit treason while your mind is elsewhere.

Or I could ask questions.

I want to be honest, since honesty is about to become expensive: I hesitated. I sat in that cold room with the chandler's candle burning down and I *hesitated*, and the hesitation lasted longer than I will ever admit to anyone. Because I knew what asking meant. Asking meant becoming visible. I had spent nine years perfecting invisibility of the other kind—not Quentin Ashgrove's kind, though I did not know about that yet, or only half-knew, in the way one half-knows a neighbor's grief—but the bureaucratic kind: the woman who is diligent, punctual, unremarkable, whose neatness is her camouflage. The Bureau protects the invisible. It devours everyone else.

And underneath the fear, colder and quieter, was the other thing. The boy with the wooden horse. *Vacant pending survey.* Where, precisely, did one survey them to?

I asked the question the only safe way I knew: sideways. The next morning I filed a routine query—clerical inconsistency, sum mismatch, requesting verification of the Ferrand entry, respectful in every comma. It was the sort of query any careful taker might file. It cost me nothing. That is what I told myself. It cost me nothing.

The reply came back within a day, which should have been my second warning, because nothing in the Bureau moves that fast except punishment. Supervisor Brant's office: *Adjustment verified. Do not re-canvass flagged households. Recanvassing flagged addresses constitutes interference with administrative hygiene.*

Interference. I had asked whether four human beings still existed and been told that wondering was a crime.

That evening I did not go straight home. I told myself I was taking the long way for the air, and the lie was so flimsy that even I didn't buy it, and I bought lies wholesale. I walked past Clocher Lane. The Ferrand windows were dark. Not empty-dark—occupied buildings breathe, you learn this in the census trade, a lived-in window fogs differently—but *dark*, the deliberate dark of people who have heard something. The washing-line was bare. The boy's wooden horse, which had spent the whole autumn wedged in the stairwell rail where he always left it, was gone.

I stood in the street like a fool for a quarter hour, and then I went home, and lit no lamp, and sat in the dark with my rolls on my knees like a breviary.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of sleeping.

Brant kept the master adjustment ledgers in his office, but Brant himself was a creature of habit, and habits are just confessions written in behavior. He left the bureau at six sharp on Thursdays—he played cards at the Sign of the Two Anchors, badly, and tipped the landlady excessively, and I knew this because knowing things is my entire profession—and returned at nine to "close out the week," which meant he signed things alone in his office for an hour while the night clerk dozed over the dispatch box.

Thursday came. At seven I returned to the bureau with the excuse I had prepared—a misfiled route sheet, genuine, I had made sure to genuinely misfile it—which bought me access to the records room adjacent to his office. The door between the records room and Brant's office had been installed by a carpenter and not a soldier; it had a keyhole you could drive a coach through.

I am not going to pretend my hands were steady. They were not. But they were *neat*, because nine years of discipline lives in the hands even when the rest has gone to water, and I copied.

What I found in the adjustment ledger, I will set down plainly, because plainness is all I have left to offer some of those names. It was not a list of errors. It was a list of intentions. Households flagged across all three districts, sorted not alphabetically but by trade—printers first, then ferrymen, then midwives, then a category labeled simply *irregulars*, and I did not need the training session with the nameless man to tell me what irregular meant. It meant people who existed in some ledger's doubt. Bastards with no filing father. Widows whose papers burned. Foreigners' children. And—one entry, one address, flagged twice, underlined in Brant's own cramped hand, which I recognized because I had once admired it—a print shop. Ashgrove. Two children registered, one child canvassed. A note beside it: *verify occupancy; correct registry to one.*

Correct registry to one. As if a child could be subtracted like a bad figure. As if ink were the person and the person were the error.

I copied four pages. Not everything—I am many things but not yet a martyr, and everything would have been noticed—but the Ferrands, two printer households, and the irregulars' column entire, because the irregulars had no other defender and I understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like falling, that nobody else was going to copy these pages. That was the whole of it. The lists were accurate. The Bureau knew exactly who everyone was. The crime was not ignorance; the crime was the correction.

My pen scratched in the dark of the records room and every scratch sounded like a knock at my own door, pre-emptive, from the future. Twice the night clerk stirred and twice I stopped breathing entirely, and between the two stoppings I had time to consider that what I was doing had a name, and the name was not *diligence*.

When I finished, I folded the pages small—they went into the lining of my left glove, where they stayed against my pulse the whole walk home, warm as a living thing—and I restored the ledger exactly, to the millimeter, because if Brant ever learned to read anything let him read a tidy shelf.

Then I walked home past the chandler's, past Perrin's candle on the landing, up the wicked stairs, and I sat down at my desk with my own honest rolls spread before me, forty-one souls, six households, everyone kept, everyone counted, and I understood that I could never again write *confirmed* at the bottom of a reconciliation sheet without perjury.

Duty, I had always believed, was a wall you built your life against. It had not occurred to me that a wall could be moved—that the same stones could be carried to the other side of the road and stacked into something else entirely. That was Thursday. On Friday morning I would pin the reconciliation sheet to my board, and my hand would be steady, and my face would be the bureau's face, and inside my glove—or rather, inside my glove's lining, transferred there overnight with flour-paste and a prayer—four stolen pages would sit between me and my skin like a second conscience.

There remained only the question I had been avoiding since the records room: what does one *do* with a stolen truth? You cannot file it. You cannot publish it—I knew what happened to unauthorized presses; everyone in the Bureau knew, we were shown photographs. You cannot give it back to the people it concerns without delivering them to the survey. Truth, it turns out, is like contraband; it needs a route, and smugglers, and someone foolish enough to carry it.

And there was the other matter. The address flagged twice. *Verify occupancy. Correct registry to one.* I had been to that print shop. I had watched a compositor's hands move over the type case—ink-stained, sure, wrong in some way I could not then name—and I had watched a pen pause over the word *children* and heard a voice say *one* while something behind the eyes said otherwise. I had written my own small falsehood that day, or my own small mercy; I still had not decided which, and the undecidedness of it had been following me home for weeks like a stray.

Two children in ink. One child at the door. One child flagged for correction.

I looked at my glove on the peg by the fire, ordinary as any glove in Vellamont, and understood that I already knew where the pages were going. I had known since the records room. Perhaps since the door of the print shop.

Some errands you choose. Some choose you, and merely wait, with terrible clerical patience, for you to pick them up.

Tomorrow, after my rounds, I would go back to Rue de l'Imprimeur. After hours. And God help me, I would knock.
