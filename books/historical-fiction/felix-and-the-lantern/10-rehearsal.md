## 10. Rehearsal

There is a particular kind of patience required to set type by hand, and I had always believed it was a patience of the fingers. It is not. It is a patience of the spine. You stand at the case for six hours and you do not hurry, because hurrying is how you pick up a sort backwards and print ten thousand upside-down letters, and my father—may he rest, though I suspect he's too busy proofreading the clouds—used to say that a compositor who rushed was a compositor who didn't respect the reader. *Respect the reader,* he'd say, and then charge extra for rush work, which I have come to understand is not a hypocrisy but a philosophy: virtue is expensive, and should be.

I thought about this at four in the morning, standing over the press with a forme of wedding invitations that did not announce any wedding that would ever occur.

The plate lay at the bottom of it.

Let me explain, because the explanation is where the craft lives. A forme is the locked-up assembly of type and blocks that goes into the press—the whole page, made physical, wedged into its iron frame with quoins tightened until nothing shifts. The census plate was thin enough to sit beneath a layer of furniture, under the packing, invisible unless you dismantled the forme entirely. Above it, in lovely fourteen-point Baskerville with flourishes I am genuinely proud of, went:

*Mr. & Mrs. Aldous Wren request the honour of your presence at the marriage of their daughter Cordelia*

—and so on, through two hundred words of invented bliss. I chose the Wrens because they were a family name common enough that no guard could confirm or deny it, and because Cordelia gave me an excuse to set swashes, and because if I was going to be shot carrying contraband I wanted to be shot carrying beautiful typography.

The plan, insofar as I had one yet, was simple in the way that plans are simple before they meet the world. Take the plate out of the shop. Carry it to the underground printers past three checkpoints. Do not get caught. My brother Dario—who is not my brother, who is a smuggler who attached himself to me the way barnacles attach to hulls, with an insistence I mistook at first for commerce—had offered his boat for the first leg. Father Anselm had claimed the cathedral gate as his own burden. But between those two mercies stretched a mile and a half of Vellamont that belonged to the Provisional Authority, and every yard of it was watched by men with rifles and lists, and I was on one of the lists.

So before I trusted the plate to anyone's boat or anyone's gate, I did what a printer does before a press run. I ran proofs.

I walked the route myself, empty-handed, at hours when guards expect empty hands, and I watched them the way I watch a new apprentice watch the stone—looking for where they were careless, where they were human, where the rhythm of their attention broke down into something exploitable. Fear, I had decided, was going to be typeset like anything else: measured, justified, locked into place. You cannot keep fear from existing. You can only decide where it sits on the page.

* * *

I left by the delivery door at dusk, wearing my second-worst coat and carrying a satchel full of nothing but bread, ink rags, and the confidence of a man with nothing to declare. Felix Ashgrove walked out of the Ashgrove print shop. Quentin stayed home, folded up small under the floorboards with the plate, being nobody.

The bridge came first.

The Meridian Bridge spans the canal at its narrowest point, and the Provisional Authority had claimed both ends of it with sandbags, a barrier, and a guardhouse the size of a confessional. There were two soldiers on the evening shift. One was young—young the way dogs are young, all shoulders and uncertainty, checking papers with the excessive care of someone terrified of missing something. The other was older, gray at the temples, and he did not look at papers at all. He looked at people. That was the one who frightened me.

I joined the foot traffic—a fishwife, two clerks, a woman with a pram—and shuffled forward with the studied boredom of the innocent. The young one took the fishwife's permit, squinted at it, stamped it. The older one stood slightly apart, hands clasped behind him, doing nothing except looking, and his looking had a quality I recognized from my own trade. He was proofreading. Not reading for content—reading for error. For the thing that doesn't match the rest of the line.

I adjusted nothing. That was the discipline. Every instinct said hunch my shoulders, drop my chin, become smaller, but I had learned already that a corrected letter stands out worse than a correct one. If the whole line is set properly, the eye slides over it. So I let my shoulders do what Quentin's shoulders do, which is carry a compositor's stoop, and I looked at the middle distance with the expression of a man thinking about dinner.

He looked at me for perhaps three seconds. Then his gaze moved on, snagging instead on the woman with the pram, whose baby was crying, whose papers she was digging for with rising panic. I crossed the bridge with my heart performing percussion and my face reporting, falsely, mild interest in the weather.

On the far side I stopped in the shadow of a warehouse and wrote in my head, the way I write corrections in a galley margin: *Young one checks paper. Old one reads people. Pram = disturbance of attention. Cross in company. Never alone.*

You will forgive me if I say that there is something obscene about being good at this. I had spent thirty-one years perfecting the arrangement of other people's words, and now the same faculty—attention, pattern, the cold pleasure of finding the flaw—was turned on the question of which guards might kill me. But obscenity and necessity are old friends in Vellamont these days, and I did not have the luxury of refusing the acquaintance.

* * *

The customs house sat at the junction of the canal and the processional road, a fat gray building with the architectural charm of a strongbox. This was checkpoint two, and it was worse, because the customs house did not merely look at people; it looked at cargo. Carts queued at the loading bay. A bored officer with a ledger walked the line, poking at sacks with a brass rod.

I bought roasted chestnuts from a brazier near the corner and ate them slowly, watching the queue move, and here is what four hours of observation taught me: the customs house ran on paperwork, and paperwork has a grammar. A cart with a manifest got waved through in ninety seconds. A cart without one got the brass rod, the officer's full attention, and an average delay of eleven minutes—which meant that the officer's tolerance for delay was finite, and the queue's pressure was real, and a man with a plausible document and a boring story was functionally invisible. They weren't hunting truth at the customs house. They were hunting inconvenience.

That was when the shape of the plan arrived, fully set, like a line of type snapping into the composing stick. The plate could not travel naked. It needed a manifest. It needed to be something dull, expected, unworthy of the brass rod. And what travels through Vellamont's checkpoints unquestioned, in this season of enforced normalcy? Wedding goods. Linen, cake boxes, printed invitations. The Authority wanted the city calm, and nothing says calm like nuptials.

I stood there with chestnut shell fragments on my coat, and I understood that I had just designed my own funeral announcement or my own alibi, depending entirely on execution, and I felt—not terror, exactly. Something colder and more useful. The feeling I get when a difficult job comes through the shop door and I know, before quoting the price, that I can do it. Father called it the printer's arrogance. I call it the only prayer that has ever worked for me.

* * *

The cathedral gate was third, and it nearly undid me, but not for the reasons I expected.

Anselm had told me to come at the hour of vespers, when the flow of penitents gives cover to any loiterer, and I arrived to find the old priest sitting outside the gate itself, on a stool, shelling peas into a bowl. This was either the most transparent surveillance cover in the history of the church or a genuine retirement hobby, and with Anselm it is genuinely impossible to know.

"You again," he said, without looking up.

"I'm walking. For my health."

"Your health," Anselm repeated, in the tone of a man filing a heresy for later review. He tipped his chin toward the gate. Two soldiers stood there, checking bags at the cathedral entrance—checking bags at a *church*, which tells you everything about the current state of Vellamont's soul. "They started that Tuesday. Looking for seditious literature."

"In a cathedral."

"The Virgin apparently publishes pamphlets now. Nobody tells me anything." He dropped a pea into the bowl with a small, precise click. "You're counting them."

I was. Shift change at the half hour. One checks bags, one watches faces, and during the handover there is a gap of perhaps forty seconds where neither one is certain whose job the stranger is. Forty seconds. In my trade, forty seconds is enough to pull a sheet, check the register, and feed it back. In Anselm's courtyard, forty seconds would have to be enough for a man to walk through a doorway carrying a stolen census plate inside a wedding.

"Come to vespers Thursday," Anselm said, still shelling. "Sit near the back. Sing badly. Nobody suspects a man who sings badly of anything except crimes against music." He glanced up then, briefly, and his eyes were not amused at all. "And Quentin. When you run your rehearsals—because I know that is what this is—vary the hour. You've walked past this gate twice today in the same coat. The old soldier at the bridge noticed you the first time. He'll notice the third."

My blood did something complicated. "How—"

"Because I notice too. It's the same job. I watched faces for thirty years and told them their sins. Now I just tell them the weather." He went back to his peas. "Vary the coat. Vary the hour. Be boring, and be boring *differently* each time. That's the whole catechism."

I walked home the long way, by the canal, and let the dark come down over the water, and I reviewed the day like a pulled proof, looking for flaws in the form. The bridge: crossable, in company, with a disturbance. The customs house: crossable, with paperwork and dullness. The gate: crossable, in forty seconds, singing badly. Three problems, three solutions, all of them fragile, all of them dependent on things staying exactly as rehearsed—and the world, as any printer knows, never stays exactly as set. Something always shifts in the chase. A letter moves. A quoin loosens. A supervisor gets suspicious and follows a census taker through streets she thinks are safe—

I stopped walking.

I had not heard from Ilse in four days. Four days, after she stood in my shop with lamp oil and copied pages spilling out of her coat like molting feathers, after she said *the orders go to typeset within the fortnight* in a voice that shook only at the end. Ilse was punctual the way the state is punctual, which is to say pathologically, and four days of silence from Ilse was a misprint in a line that had been clean.

I told myself she was being careful. Careful people go quiet; that was the whole point of carefulness. I told myself the silence was discipline, hers and mine, and that a rehearsal was not the place for messages anyway.

Then I turned onto our lane and saw the light burning in the shop's front window, which I had extinguished when I left, and my whole body understood the situation several seconds before my mind agreed to.

I did not run. That is the strangest part, the part I keep turning over like a coin I suspect is counterfeit. Ten years of hiding had taught me that running is the loudest thing a person can do. I walked, at a compositor's pace, past my own front window, and through the glass I saw a man in the gray uniform of the Bureau of Records standing at my father's type cases, drawing out a drawer and tilting it to read the labels.

He was holding the household ledger. My father's ledger. The one that recorded two children, born four years apart, in a neat dead man's hand.

And beside him, taking notes, stood a second man—and it was not until he turned toward the lamplight that I saw his face, and understood that the rehearsal was over, and the press run had begun without me, and somewhere across the city Ilse's silence had a reason after all.

The man at my father's cases closed the drawer very gently, the way one handles another man's property one intends to confiscate, and said something to his companion that I could not hear through the glass.

But I could read lips. Thirty years above a composing stick teaches you to read everything.

He said: *"Find the brother."*

They were looking for Felix. Felix, who had never existed; Felix, whose name was worn smooth as a stair from all my borrowing of it. And the plate lay under the floorboards twenty feet from where I stood, beneath a forme of wedding invitations, waiting for a courier who could no longer go home.

I stepped back into the shadow of the doorway across the lane, and began, very carefully, to think.
