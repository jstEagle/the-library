## 11. The Raid Next Door

### Quentin

The boots woke me before the shouting did, which tells you something about the order of things in Vellamont: the state arrives on its feet and finds its voice later.

I had fallen asleep in the type gallery again, cheek on a case of long primes, the plate beneath the floorboards three feet from my ribs. I'd taken to sleeping there—upstairs, among the sorts—rather than in the bed behind the shop wall. The hidden bed was for hiding. The gallery was for watching. Lately I could not tell you which instinct was stronger.

Across the lane, lamplight spilled from the tailor's doorway in a wide, rude rectangle. Bram Voss's shop. Bram, who had hemmed my father's mourning coat without charging for it, saying grief was bad enough without a bill attached. Bram, his wife Marta, their son Piet, who was eleven and could whistle through his teeth loud enough to stop traffic.

The lanterns came first—a whole procession of them, carried low, so that the soldiers' faces stayed dark while everything around them blazed. That is a thing I have thought about often since: they light the world and keep their own faces out of it. It is not cowardice. It is procedure.

I did not decide to go to the window. My body went, the way a body goes to a sound in the night, and I pressed myself into the shadow of the casement and looked down at a scene I would give a great deal never to have seen.

They were not rough. That was the terrible part. In the pamphlets the underground sometimes smuggled past the customs house, raids are described as fury—doors kicked, crockery smashed, mothers screaming. This was nothing like that. Four soldiers, one corporal with a clipboard, and a man in civilian grey who held the census ledger the way my father used to hold a proof sheet, tilting it toward the light.

"Household of Voss," the grey man read out, conversational, almost bored. "Three registered. Correction ordered. Come along, please."

Bram stood in his doorway in his nightshirt with his arms folded—not defiantly, exactly. More like a man waiting for a tailor's error to be acknowledged. "There's been a mistake," he said, reasonably. "Our papers are in order. We've been on the rolls since before the Marshal's accession."

"The rolls have been amended," said the grey man.

Amended. Such a gentle word. A compositor amends. A priest amends. Somewhere in an office I will never see, someone had taken a pen—or a plate, God help me, a plate like mine—and simply removed the Voss family from the city's memory, and now four men with rifles were the punctuation.

Marta came out with Piet by the hand. She had put her coat on over her nightdress and she had made him put his shoes on, and I understood, watching her, that she had known this might come, someday, the way we all knew it, and had rehearsed it in her head the way I rehearsed checkpoint routes. You dress the child. You do not cry in front of the child. You hold the child's hand as though you are merely crossing a street.

Piet looked up at our windows. I stepped back before the light found me, heart slamming, and then—I don't know why, call it stupidity or witness—I leaned out just enough to see him again. If someone must remember, let it be someone across the lane. Let it be ink if it cannot be anything else.

They walked them down the lane toward the customs house road. No wagon even. Walking, in shoes Marta had laced herself. The door of the shop stood open behind them, lamplight pouring out onto the cobbles like something spilled.

The grey man paused at the threshold, turned back, and looked up—at the lane, at the shuttered windows, at mine—and I dropped below the sill and stayed there, breathing through my mouth, until my lungs burned.

When I dared look again, the street was empty. The lamp in the tailor's shop still burned. Nobody had turned it off. It occurred to me, in the absurd corner of my mind where I keep such observations, that somewhere in the bureaucracy of Vellamont there was no form for *extinguish the lamp*, and so it would burn until the oil gave out, a small domestic light marking an absence, burning for no one.

I sat on the gallery floor until dawn. I did not sleep. At some point I pried up the floorboard and put my hand flat on the plate, the cold tin of it, the raised reverse of names and numbers pressed into metal like braille for a blind god. *This is what failure looks like,* I told myself. Not dramatic. Not bloody. Just a family walking down a lane in their shoes, and a lamp left burning, and a neighbor watching from the dark with the proof in his hands and doing nothing, because doing anything was not yet possible, or not yet planned, or not yet brave enough.

By morning I had stopped shaking and started counting. Guards' shifts change at six. The bridge is thinnest-manned at the change. Dario's boat runs the canal at slack water, which is near noon. I wrote none of it down. I set it in type in my head—capital letters, tight leading—and I will tell you what every compositor knows: what is properly locked in a chase does not scatter, no matter how the case is shaken.

### Ilse

I heard about the Voss removal at half past seven, from a clerk named Odilon who told it like weather. "Vosses gone this morning. Tailor, by the bridge." He buttered his bread. "Correction order."

I said, "I took that household myself. In spring. Three registered, papers consistent. There was no discrepancy."

Odilon shrugged with his whole philosophy, which was: not my table, not my type. But the words were out of my mouth before I'd decided to say them, and once said, they could not be unsaid, and Odilon looked at me a half-second longer than he had ever looked at anyone in his life.

At the census office, Supervisor Grempe was already at my desk. Not near it. At it, seated, turning pages of my district roll with one finger, unhurried as a man reading love letters.

"Ilse," he said pleasantly. "You flagged the Voss entry?"

"No, sir. I verified it. Someone else amended it."

"Mm." He closed the book and squared it on the desk, and I watched him do a curious thing: he turned it so the spine faced him, not me. Small. Meaningless. I have thought about it every hour since. "Verification is a service," he said. "Amendment is a policy. You are not employed to audit policy. You are employed to count."

"To count *people*," I said, and heard how it sounded—young, principled, doomed—and Grempe smiled like a teacher whose pupil has recited the wrong lesson correctly.

"Precisely," he said. "People. Counted. As instructed." He rose, patted the roll once as if it were a dog that had behaved, and left me my desk back.

I worked all morning with my hands steady and my mind running like a press at full speed. Here is what I could not stop seeing: the Voss entry in my own hand. *Bramuel Voss, tailor. Marta. Piet, b. 9th of Harvest.* I remembered Piet because he had answered the door and lied about his age—he wanted very much to be counted as twelve, a whole year in advance, the little ambitious fraud—and I had written eleven anyway, and he had sighed like a man ruined.

Eleven years old, entered in ink by my hand, and by evening that same ink was a warrant.

I have taken oaths. I want that understood; I am not a woman who drifted into the census service for the pension. I believed the count was a covenant—that a state which numbers its people has promised, by the very act of numbering, to *keep* them. Every name in a ledger is a claim: *you exist; we have noted it; you are therefore ours to protect.* What I learned at that desk, in the hour after Grempe left, is that the covenant reads both directions and only one of us signed.

At midday, when the office emptied for the meal, I copied three pages of the amendment schedule into shorthand on the inside of my cuff-pattern, then thought better of the cuff and copied them again, cleaner, onto a slip I sewed into the lining of my bag. Amendments initialed not by any censitor but by a single mark I did not recognize—a small hooked glyph, repeated forty times, forty households, forty families walked out of the city's memory like the Vosses.

Forty lamps, I thought, that somebody will leave burning.

I should have stopped there. Copied evidence is already treason; I knew the statute number, I had helped draft the filing categories for it. But my hand kept moving, and here is the confession I will make plainly: it moved because of a print shop. Because a compositor with ink-stained hands had answered my questions with perfect courtesy and one lie too many, and because when I had written *two* in my field book instead of *one*, some part of me had already chosen a side, and the rest of me was only catching up, the way the rest of a body catches up to a stumble on stairs.

Before the bell rang for afternoon session, I had addressed a note. No salutation. Six words.

*They amend at night. Check your floor.*

I did not sign it. I am not yet ready to hang, and besides, I suspected he would know the hand. Censitors and compositors write alike—all those years of making characters behave.

### Dario

I was poling my barge past the bridge landing at slack water when I saw the tailor's boy.

Understand: I see everything on the canal, and I file it under two headings, *cargo* and *not my cargo*. The boy Piet Voss I had ferried twice, once with a coil of rope he was very proud of. Now here he was walking between two soldiers on the customs house road in good shoes, holding his mother's hand like a man being escorted to a surprise party he'd already deduced the nature of. Heading: not my cargo. Filed. Moved on.

Except I didn't move on. I held the barge against the current, which costs money, muscle, and time, and I watched until the lane swallowed them, and I said aloud, alone on my boat like an idiot, "Well. Hell."

I don't hold causes. Causes sink boats. I hold rates, and routes, and a talent for looking boring. But there is a species of transaction I have always refused, quietly, without advertising it, and that is the trade in people. Contraband is contraband—brandy, Bibles, broadsheets, I'm your man—but the day I carry a person against their will is the day my boat becomes a slave ship, and a man has to draw his waterline somewhere or he's just ballast.

What I was watching wasn't cargo. That was the problem. Those were customers of the state, being delivered.

That evening I poled over to the Ashgrove wharf, which is technically not a wharf, it is a loading step and a mooring ring, and knocked the pattern I always knock, and Ashgrove—the young one, the one who is either Felix or is not, I've stopped spending effort on the question—opened the door before the third rap, pale as fresh paper.

"You heard," they said.

"Saw."

For a moment neither of us did anything, and then they stood aside and I came in and there was bread and there was lamp oil and there was, on the table between us, unremarked upon, a length of new cord and a canvas sheet cut to size, which in my trade is called *packing for something you don't want described.*

"Rate's doubled," I said, mostly to hear myself be a professional.

"Agreed," said Ashgrove, instantly, and I felt the peculiar sensation of a man setting a price and having it met too fast, which means you asked too little, which means you care more than you claimed. I poured myself their bad tea and glared at it.

"Route's blown at the customs house," I said. "They walked the Vosses down the customs road. There'll be a post there now, extra eyes. They'll open crates."

"They won't open wedding invitations."

"They'll open *anything* after today. Fear makes them thorough." I drank the terrible tea. "Thoroughness is a phase. It passes. Question is whether it passes before or after your box goes through."

Ashgrove looked at me across the table with those compositor's eyes that read a face like a galley proof, hunting broken type, and I realized I had said more than I meant to, and that the difference between this run and my other runs had quietly changed shape while I wasn't pricing it. Other runs, I feared the guards. This run, I noticed, I feared for somebody.

"Don't look at me like that," I said. "I haven't decided to be a hero. Heroes drown. I'm deciding to finish a job."

"Of course," said Ashgrove, and smiled faintly, the liar, and I drank more of the awful tea because it gave my hands employment.

### Anselm

They brought me the news at the cathedral gate, which is where all news comes eventually, carried by the poor, who are faster than couriers because they have nothing to slow them down.

"A family taken off the lane by the print shop," said the widow Callas, setting down the candle she brings me every week although I can consecrate nothing anymore, defrocked as I am, reduced to a layman with keys to a side door. "The tailor. They walked them out on foot."

"On foot," I repeated. Even the Marshal's men usually use wagons. On foot is what you do with cargo you're not ashamed of, because shame requires acknowledgment and acknowledgment requires paperwork you might someday answer for. On foot says: *this is ordinary.* Ordinary is the most obscene word in Vellamont this season.

I lit a candle for the Voss family at the shrine of Saint Aldric, who is patron of scribes, which the Church assigned him centuries ago and the state has recently tried to revoke, along with several other saints' commissions. The candle caught on the second match. I took that for whatever a man takes such things for.

Here is my arithmetic, done nightly, in the crypt, among the bones of men who at least died certain of their office: I lost my living because I sheltered the unregistered—eleven souls in the rectory cellar, one winter, and I would do it again tonight if the cellar were mine. I was punished. I repented nothing. And yet I have spent the months since in a fog of uselessness, guarding a gate, keeping my head, telling myself that survival is also a vocation. Meanwhile the lists grow. Meanwhile a young compositor two streets away carries proof of the whole rotten machine under their floorboards and has not yet asked me for the one thing I can actually give: the third gate, my gate, the cathedral checkpoint where the guard owes me a debt older than the Marshal.

Tonight, hearing of Bram Voss walking in his shoes down a public road, I understood that my penance was not being performed. It was being *postponed*, and postponement is just pride wearing humility's coat. I want to pay quietly. I want to be redeemed discreetly, receipts kept, no witnesses. But the fortnight is nearly spent—the purge orders go to typesetting within days, a canon at the chancery whispered it to me thinking my collar still granted him safety—and if I do not stand at my own gate, soon, and mean it, then every candle I light is just fire.

I took the plate of bread the widow left, and I broke it, alone, and ate it standing up, which is how penitents eat.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would walk to the print shop and offer the boy—Felix, Ashgrove, whichever name is truest this week—the thing I have: a gate. And I would ask, in return, the only fee my conscience would accept.

That my name go on what gets printed.

### Quentin

The lamp in the tailor's shop burned until nearly two in the morning. I know because I timed it against the cathedral bells, lying on the gallery floor with the floorboard loose beside me, listening to the oil gutter down the wick across the lane like a pulse slowing.

By morning the shop stood empty and sealed, the official placard already pasted to the door—*AMENDED, BY ORDER*—and the seamstress from two doors down was already, horribly, measuring the window glass with her eye, because commerce abhors a vacancy, and Vellamont's griefs are inventory opportunities within eight hours.

Ilse's note came with the morning milk, folded inside nothing, tucked under the bottle. Six words in a censitor's tidy hand: *they amend at night. check your floor.*

I checked it. Of course I checked it. I knelt in the press room like a penitent and pried the board and there it was, cold, intact, real: the master misprint, forty addresses, the Ashgrove shop among them, my own name—*both* my names, Felix and the empty space where a child should be—struck for correction in the same hooked glyph I would later learn meant Grempe's office.

And underneath the terror, quiet as a third color in a duotone, sat the instruction. Because that is what horror is, if you survive it and keep your eyes open: instruction. The raid taught me the guards' spacing. The lamp taught me how long neglect lasts. Marta's coat taught me that preparation looks like ordinary dressing. Piet's upward glance taught me the only thing worth printing.

By noon I had the forme half-built—wedding invitations, blank names, innocent lead—and a route sketched in my skull, and a smuggler's rate agreed, and a note of my own going out to the cathedral gate asking a defrocked priest for a favor I intended to spend my father's name on.

The Vosses were gone by morning. The truth would be gone by midnight—out of my floor and into the city, where it could not be amended.

I locked the chase. I pulled the board snug over the plate one last night. And upstairs, across the lane, a seamstress tapped at the tailor's empty window frame, measuring another family's ruin for curtains, while in the dark under my knees the names of forty households waited, in reverse, in tin, patient as type, for morning.
