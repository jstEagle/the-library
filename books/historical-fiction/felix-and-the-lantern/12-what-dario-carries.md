## 12. What Dario Carries

There are three rules to moving cargo on the canals of Vellamont, and I learned all of them before I was old enough to shave: never ask what's in the box, never look like it matters, and never, under any circumstance, lie to a soldier. The first two keep you solvent. The third keeps you alive. Soldiers forgive smugglers the way a magistrate forgives weather—it's simply what we are—but they never forgive being lied to by someone whose whole trade is built on the honest exchange of dishonest goods. A smuggler who lies is a smuggler with something worth hanging for. Every guard in this city knows that arithmetic better than I do.

I want that understood before I tell you what happened at the customs house, because what I did there broke twenty years of discipline, and I have spent every hour since trying to work out why.

* * *

The morning started ordinary, which should have been my first warning. Ordinary mornings are the ones that kill you; the bad ones you see coming from across the water.

I had the crate lashed amidships under a consignment of church candles—Anselm's arrangement, and a clever one, because no customs man in Vellamont will dig through wax when there's a chance of brandy. Candles are the perfect cargo. Nobody wants them, nobody taxes them, nobody looks at them. They go into churches and come out as nothing at all. I'd told the compositor as much when we loaded at the print shop dock in the grey hour before dawn, and Quentin had stood on the quay with their arms wrapped around themselves, watching me settle the crate like a mother settling a child in blankets.

"You don't want to know," I said, by way of reassurance. It's what I always say. It's usually true.

"I know exactly what's in it," Quentin said. "That's the difference between us."

And off they went, back up the lane toward the shop, walking like a person carrying something much heavier than empty hands. I remember thinking: there's a creature that has been hunted long enough to learn the hunter's habits. I remember thinking: not my problem. I was paid—I was paid well, paid at rates I quote to nobody—and the cargo was small and heavy and none of mine.

The river was kind. Fog sat low on the water like an old dog too tired to beg, and I made good time past the tanneries and the granary stairs, humming a little tune my mother used to sing about a girl who married a pike. By the time the customs house came into view—the long low building with its chain across the channel and its lantern already burning in daytime, which was wrong, that lantern burning in daytime, but I didn't read the omen then—I was in as fine a mood as a man of my profession ever permits himself.

Then I saw the soldiers.

Not the usual pair. The usual pair is Corporal Voss, who takes his bribe with the enthusiasm of a man collecting rent, and a boy whose name changes depending on how recently he's been demoted. Today there were six of them, and a sergeant with a clipboard, and a table set up on the quay as if for an inspection of the entire canal trade, which had never once happened in the history of the canal trade, because inspections imply standards and the customs house has never pretended to any.

"Boat ahoy," said the sergeant, without looking up. "Manifest."

I gave him my manifest. He gave it the kind of attention a man gives a love letter written in a language he doesn't speak—turning it over, holding it to the light, as though the fog might have printed secrets between the lines.

"Dario Ferro," he read. "Canal carrier, license fourteen. You do a lot of work for the churches?"

"God-fearing city," I said. "Somebody has to ferry all that fear around."

He didn't smile. That was the second wrong thing. Voss would have smiled. Voss would have taken his coin and told me about his wife's opinions on candles. This one just looked at the crates, and then he looked at me, and I watched him perform the little calculation every soldier performs on every boat: *what is this man hiding, and is it worth the paperwork?*

"Open the forward crate," he said. "The candle one."

Now. Here is where I must be honest with you, whoever you are, wherever this account ends up—probably wrapped around sausages someday, knowing my luck—because honesty is the only luxury I've never traded away. When he said *the candle one*, something happened in my chest that I haven't felt since I was nine years old and my father's boat was boarded by press-gangs. Not fear exactly. Fear is a professional tool; I keep mine sharpened and oiled like everything else. This was something below fear. This was stakes.

Because I knew—suddenly, completely, in the way you know things your mind hasn't caught up to—that the compositor hadn't packed candles at all. Anselm's people had packed candles. But the crate in the middle, lashed under the canvas with the wax on top of it, weighted so carefully, padded so lovingly, that crate was the thing Quentin had carried down the quay steps at first light with both arms and a face like a person lowering their own coffin into water.

A printing plate. I hadn't seen it. I didn't need to see it. You don't spend thirty years on these canals without learning what cargo weighs in the hands of the person carrying it. That crate held the weight of a city's names. I could feel it through the canvas like a cat feels a mouse through a wall.

"Problem?" said the sergeant.

"Not at all," I said, and began unshipping the forward crate, buying seconds the way other men buy wine.

Here's what I did, and I did it fast, and I did it well, God help me. The candle crate comes apart a particular way—Anselm's people build them for this, slats that lift out, wax trays beneath. I opened it wide and cheerful and let him see candles, hundreds of them, ranks upon ranks of fat white candles smelling of honey and sanctuary. And while he looked, I talked. I have a gift for talk; my late mother called it a curse and charged accordingly.

"—and the sacristan at Saint Ferrol's will tell you himself," I was saying, "these are the Lenten order, and if they're short by even a dozen the canon writes letters, and I mean *letters*, sergeant, letters with seals on them, and last time it took me four months to get the smell of incense out of my ledger—" all of it patter, pure patter, and underneath the patter my hands doing something no manifest will ever record: tipping the third tray of candles sideways so it leaned against the crate lid at an angle, casual as spilled sugar, so that anyone reaching past it into the dark space behind would have to move it deliberately, visibly, with me standing right there watching them do it.

The sergeant reached toward the back of the crate.

And stopped.

Because—and I swear this on my mother's grave, which is a stone I stole, so take the oath for whatever it's worth—he stopped, and looked at me, and I saw him decide. Not about the candles. About me. He was weighing whether I was the sort of man who'd hide anything worth finding, and I watched him arrive at the answer, and the answer was wrong, gloriously wrong, and it was wrong because of my face.

Twenty years I've kept my face like still water. Boatmen learn it. You cannot run cargo past a customs house wearing your heart on your jaw. And now, at the precise moment when still water would have saved me, my face did something else entirely. I don't know what. Something that said *this matters*. Something that said *please*. And Sergeant Clipboard looked at my face and thought: *a man this nervous about church candles is a man with a mistress in the sacristy or debts to the diocese*, and both of those were more interesting to him than duty, because duty doesn't pay.

"Third tray's crooked," he said.

"Tide-set," I said. "Everything on my boat is crooked. Including me."

He laughed—one bark, involuntary, the laugh of a man surprised into humanity—and closed the lid himself. "Move along, Ferro. And tell your canon his Lenten letters can go to the devil."

I pushed off. I poled out into the channel. I rounded the granary point, and only when the customs house was fully out of sight did I discover that my hands were shaking so badly I had to sit down on my own cargo and breathe like a man surfacing from a deep dive.

* * *

That evening I delivered the crate to the address Anselm had given me—a cellar door behind a cooper's yard, received by a woman with flour on her forearms and a printer's squint, who signed nothing and said less. She cracked the crate open just enough to confirm the plate was there, wrapped in oilcloth like a relic, and something crossed her face when she saw it. Recognition. Grief, maybe. Then she closed it again and paid me double the arranged rate without being asked, which frightened me worse than the sergeant had.

Double rate means the seller knows the buyer is taking a bigger risk than advertised.

I walked home the long way, along the embankment, and I did my accounts in my head the way I always do, and here is where the trouble really starts, because the accounts wouldn't balance. Not the money—the money balanced beautifully; I had made in two days what an honest man makes in a season. The other ledger wouldn't balance. The one I didn't know I kept.

Let me lay it out plainly. I have moved contraband of every description. Lace, gunpowder, priests, pregnant duchesses, one time a live bear, though that was a mistake on everyone's part. Never once have I asked what was in the box, and never once have I cared, and I have told myself this is professionalism, but tonight, sitting on the embankment steps with the fog coming in off the river and my hands finally steady, I had to admit the uglier truth: it isn't professionalism. It's cowardice wearing professionalism's coat. Not asking is a way of not being responsible. If I don't know the box holds a man's whole existence, then I can't be hanged for a man's whole existence. I'm just a boatman. The box is just a box.

But now I knew. The knowledge had gotten into me the way river water gets into a hull—not through any opening I consented to, just through the seams, because seams exist. I knew that the crate held a list of names, and I knew that somewhere across the city soldiers were setting type for orders about those names, and I knew—I knew the way I knew the weight in Quentin's arms—that among those names were people who had paid me fares, sold me eels, lent me an umbrella in the rain outside the cathedral one November I've never forgotten.

You cannot un-know a thing like that. Believe me, I've tried. There's a great deal of my past I keep in a sealed compartment below the waterline, and it has served me admirably, and tonight the compartment flooded.

And here is the part that genuinely disturbs me, the part I've turned over fifty times since midnight. At the customs house, when the sergeant's hand went past the third tray of candles, I lied. Not misdirected—misdirection is my native tongue, that's just breathing. Lied. He asked me if I'd carried cargo for Saint Ferrol's before, and I said yes, and I never had. I invented a canon. I invented Lenten letters. I fabricated an entire ecclesiastical history to protect a box whose contents I claimed—had always claimed—to ignore.

Why? I've run the calculations. Fear doesn't explain it; fear would have had me sweating and stammering, and stammering men get searched. Self-interest doesn't explain it; self-interest would have had me offering the sergeant a cut to skip the formality, which has worked before. Some flicker of principle? Please. I'm a smuggler. My principles float.

No. What I felt, standing on that deck with a soldier's hand a foot from a plate stamped with the names of the condemned, was something I have successfully avoided feeling for forty years: ownership. Stakes. Somewhere between the print shop dock and the customs house, without signing anything, without being asked, I had become a man with something to lose. And the worst of it—the truly unforgivable part—is that I think I knew it at the print shop dock, this morning, watching Quentin walk away up the lane with their arms wrapped around themselves. I think I took the job already knowing I wasn't going to stay detached. I think some traitorous corner of me chose the crate the moment I saw the size of the grief carrying it.

My mother had a word for men like what I am becoming. She used it about my father, who once went back into a burning tenement for a neighbor's cat and came out with broken ribs and no cat. She called such men *compromised*, and she said it the way you'd say *drowned*, and she was right both times. Compromised men die young. Compromised men also, occasionally, live long enough to matter, which is worse.

* * *

I slept badly and woke before dawn with my decision already made, which annoyed me, because I like to be consulted about my decisions. I rowed back upriver to the Ashgrove dock in the grey light, told myself the whole way that I was only going to renegotiate my fee—there's a second delivery needed, I'd heard, over the checkpoints, past the cathedral gate, deeper into the maze than candles can plausibly explain—and told myself the fee was going to be ruinous, extortionate, the kind of number that buys a man absolution through sheer greed.

Quentin opened the door before I knocked. Up close, in daylight, they look like a person who has been awake all night listening to the floorboards, and there was ink on their fingers and something new in their eyes when they saw it was me. Relief, quickly buried. People bury relief fast in this city; it's a liability, like gold worn openly.

"The soldiers were at the customs house today," they said. "Six of them. A sergeant with a list."

"They were," I agreed. "Your candles arrived beautifully."

Something moved across their face—understanding, maybe, of what my beautiful candles had cost. "There's another run," they said. "Bigger. Through three checkpoints. Dario, I won't lie to you about what it is anymore. You've earned the truth and the truth might get you killed."

And there it was. The question I'd been dodging since the embankment, handed to me on the doorstep like a parcel. Ask what's in the box, my first rule, except reversed now, inverted, ruined: they were going to tell me what's in the box whether I wanted it or not.

I should have quoted a price. That was the move. That was always the move; it's practically my signature. Instead I heard myself say, in a voice I barely recognized as belonging to the sharpest bargainer on the Vellamont canals:

"How soon do you need me?"

And Quentin Ashgrove smiled—tired, ink-stained, grateful as sin—and said:

"Sooner than you'll like. Ilse sent word this morning." They glanced down the lane, lowered their voice. "She's been arrested."
