## 13. Anselm's Bargain

I had been a priest for twenty-two years and a defrocked one for three, and I will tell you which of the two taught me more about the mercy of God: the first gave me the vocabulary, but the second took away my pulpit and left me with nothing to do but practice.

They take the collar, you understand. That's the first thing they do—the Bureau of Registers sends a man with a clipboard and two soldiers with bored faces, and they cut the collar off your shirt with scissors while you stand very still, because struggling gives them something to write down. Then you are free. Free of the cathedral's payroll, free of its protection, free to sleep in the crypt you used to sweep and eat bread that parishioners leave on the sill like offerings to a stray dog. There is a joke in there somewhere about how Christ also kept no house, but I have found that most jokes about voluntary poverty stop being funny the moment poverty becomes involuntary.

My name was struck from the clergy rolls on a Tuesday in Lent. Father Anselm Corvin, removed for the crime of sheltering the unregistered—fourteen souls, over three years, hidden in the crypt passage behind the reliquary wall, fed on soup and lies and the occasional smuggled hymnal. The fourteenth soul was a girl of nine who had been born in a boat on the canal and therefore, according to the ledgers, had never been born at all. Her name was Maren. I baptized her anyway, in secret, with canal water, because I am a vain man and could not bear the thought of an unbaptized Christian in my care even while I was committing crimes for her sake.

She is gone now. Across the border, I am told, into a country where paper does not outrank flesh. I pray it is true. I pray it is true the way you press your thumb on a wound—not to check if it hurts, but because pressing is the only proof you have that the wound is yours.

* * *

The compositor came back to the cathedral gate on a Thursday, which pleased me, because Thursdays were when I still said the office alone in the side chapel, and there is a particular humiliation in being interrupted mid-psalm by someone whose soul you have been worrying about all week.

I had met Quentin Ashgrove twice before—once by daylight at the gate, once after dark in the shadow of the buttresses where the beggars' alcove keeps its secrets. The first time, they were Felix Ashgrove, son of the dead printer, and they asked me questions a son would ask: whether the cathedral still rang the workday bells, whether the sexton remembered their father's shop printing the diocesan almanac, thirty years gone. The second time, they were nobody at all—hood up, voice down, asking in a whisper what I knew about the underground presses and whether the men who ran them were fools or cowards.

"Fools," I told them then. "Brave ones. Which is the more dangerous combination."

Now they stood at the gate in the grey afternoon light with ink on their fingers—I noticed it the first time too, compositor's hands, stained black to the second knuckle as though they had shaken hands with the devil's typographer—and said, "Father Anselm. I need your gate."

"My gate," I said, "is a door. Doors open for anyone. It is one of the few doctrines I still hold."

"I mean the checkpoint. The third one. Soldiers at your gate, checking papers against your face. If I'm going to move something through the cathedral close, I need it to move under your eyes, not around them." They spoke quickly, the way people speak when they have rehearsed courage and are afraid it will wear out before the performance. "There's a plate. A census plate. A misprint from the Bureau's own press room—it's the master list, Father. Every household flagged for correction. Mine is on it. Two hundred others. The purge orders get typeset within the fortnight unless this reaches the printers on Tanner's Row first."

I looked at them for a long moment. Old habit, that look—I used to give it over the confessional grille, waiting for the sin beneath the sin. What I saw beneath the fear was not desperation. It was craft. A plan with corners on it, measured twice.

"You've thought about this," I said.

"Every hour since I found the plate."

"And Dario?"

"Dario moves the crate through the water. Ilse—" They stopped, and something crossed their face like weather. "Ilse has her own part. The customs house pass comes through her. And then the last stretch is yours. Your gate, your ground, your eyes. No one searches the cathedral close hard. Out of respect, or superstition, or because the Bureau hasn't decided yet whether God is a threat to the state." A thin smile. "Have they?"

"They're still commissioning the study," I said.

But I did not say yes. Not yet. I made them come inside instead, into the side chapel where the candles burn down to stubs because I buy them myself now, with coins begged from widows, and I sat them in the pew where Maren used to swing her legs, and I told them what my yes would cost.

* * *

Here is the thing you must understand about defrocked priests, or at least this one: we did not lose our posts for lack of faith. We lost them for an excess of it, poorly budgeted. I spent my whole ministry believing that humility meant hiding—that the righteous hand should not be seen, that the good deed done in secret is worth double. It is a beautiful doctrine. It is also, I have come to believe, a coward's theology dressed in vestments.

When they cut my collar off, the clerk asked me, with genuine bureaucratic kindness, whether I wished to make a statement for the record. A recantation, he meant. A small statement. *Former Father Corvin acknowledges his error and asks the Bureau's forgiveness.* One line. One line and I would have kept my pension, my rooms, my name in the diocesan calendar, my place at the table of respectable men.

I opened my mouth to say it. I want you to know that. I opened my mouth, and the words were there, lined up like type in a composing stick, and all I had to do was pull the lever.

And then I thought of Maren's legs swinging in that pew, and the fourteen names I had sheltered, and I heard myself say instead: "I regret nothing except that I did not shelter more."

It felt magnificent for approximately four seconds. Then the scissors came out, and I discovered that magnificence, unlike charity, covers no rent.

Three years I have lived in the margin of my own church. Three years of doing good invisibly—passing messages, feeding fugitives, blessing babies with canal water—and telling myself it was humility. But I will confess it here, in the dark, to whoever reads this: it was not humility. It was the opposite of pride, which looks identical to it from the outside and is rotten at the core. I hid my penance because I wanted to survive it. I did my atonement in the shadows because the shadows don't keep records, and a man who has been struck from a ledger develops a taste for places without them.

The compositor sat in my pew and asked for my gate, and I understood, sitting there among the candle stubs, that God had finally sent me the bill.

* * *

"I'll give you the gate," I said. "The close, the sexton's silence, the crypt passage if it comes to running. My word on it. But I have a condition, and it is not negotiable, and you will want to argue, so let me finish first."

Quentin folded their hands. Compositor's patience. I have always suspected that printers and priests are the same trade—both of us arranging other people's words into salvation.

"When you print the plate," I said, "when the true census comes off that underground press with all those two hundred names on it and the lie laid bare beside them—you will print my name at the foot of it. Anselm Corvin, formerly of this cathedral, defrocked for sheltering the unregistered. Set it in type. Large enough to read across a square."

They blinked. "You want credit?"

"I want *witness*." I heard my own voice climb and made myself come down. "Listen to me. For three years I have paid my debt in the dark. Quiet rooms, quiet passages, quiet blessings. Nobody sees. Nobody knows. And I told myself that was the point—that God sees, and God's seeing is enough. But I have begun to suspect," and here my hands were shaking, and I gripped the pew rail like a drowning man grips driftwood, "that I chose the dark because the dark is comfortable. Because a hidden penance costs exactly as much as you can afford and not a sou more. Because every night I go to sleep having done *enough*, and enough is the Devil's favorite word."

"Father—"

"The Bureau erased my name. Fine. Erasure is their sacrament; let them have it. But this"—I pointed toward the gate, toward the city beyond, toward the press room where a misprinted plate waited like a live coal in a satchel—"this is my chance to be erased *out loud*. You want to slip through my gate like a ghost, print your truth anonymously, scatter it in the night, and vanish. Everyone protected. Everyone deniable. That is the old way, the prudent way, the way I have practiced since the scissors. And it is a way of staying half-alive."

I sat back down. My knees had opinions.

"Print my name," I said, quieter now. "Let the Bureau find it. Let them come for me with their clipboards and their bored soldiers. Let the whole city see an old fool standing in the light paying what he owes. I have been spending my penance in installments, child, and installments are how the soul cheats. I want to pay it all at once, in currency everyone can read."

Quentin looked at me for a long time. Then they said something I did not expect, because I had expected the argument.

"My father used to say," they began, and stopped, and started again, "my father said a printer has one duty above all others: never set a word you wouldn't stand behind. He said anonymity is sometimes mercy—for the frightened, the hunted, the ones who'd die for a sentence. But the person who pulls the lever on the press, who feels the platen come down—if they hide their own name while setting everyone else's, the page knows. Ink remembers the hand that hesitated."

"Ink remembers," I repeated. "Yes. That's the doctrine exactly."

"So I won't argue," Quentin said. "Your name goes at the foot of the plate. Anselm Corvin. And mine goes beside it."

I stared. "Yours? The compositor's name? Child, the entire point of your alias—"

"Is ending. Soon. On terms I choose." Something moved in their face, some decision made in a room I wasn't shown. "Felix Ashgrove died of scarlet fever at six years old. I've worn his name for fifteen years because a ledger only ever counted one child in our house. If we're printing truth, Father, we print all of it. Q. Ashgrove. Both names. Let the Bureau choke on the arithmetic."

I laughed. I couldn't help it—an honest-to-God laugh, the first in months, echoing disgracefully in a chapel built for whispers. The candle flames leaned.

"Oh, you'll do," I said. "You'll do very badly and very well, both at once, which is the only way anything holy gets done."

* * *

We settled the particulars over bread and the last of the communion wine, which I am assured is sacrilegious to drink alone and doubly so to share with a fugitive, and which tasted better than any wine of my ordained years. The plan had corners on it, as I said. Dario's boat through the canal to the customs house; the crate inside a legitimate shipment of wedding invitations—compositors think of everything, God love them; Ilse's pass at the second checkpoint; my gate for the third. From the close, the crypt passage runs two hundred feet to the tanner's yard behind Tanner's Row, and from the tanner's yard it is forty paces to the underground press. Forty paces. I measured them once, carrying a nine-year-old girl who deserved to be born somewhere better than a boat on dirty water.

Forty paces between my failure and my redemption. I intend to walk every one of them in the open.

One matter remained. I saved it for last, because I am a coward in small things even now.

"There's something you should know about my gate," I said. "The sergeant who commands the checkpoint. Corporal Vassek. Young. Thorough. Frightened, the way they make them. He has a list of faces—deserters, unregistered, agitators. And he has been seen at the Bureau offices twice this week, coming out with papers."

Quentin went very still. "Purge orders?"

"Possibly. Or possibly requisitions for his men's winter boots. I can't read him yet. But if the orders are typeset within the fortnight, as you say, then Vassek's little list becomes a killing document, and my gate becomes the door it stands in front of." I turned my cup slowly on the table. "I'll hold the gate. That's sworn. But you should know that when the day comes, I may not be holding it for long. A man my age, barring a door with his body—he is buying minutes, not victories. Whatever happens at that gate, you run. You do not turn around. You do not come back for me. Promise me the printer's discipline: the job goes out, whatever happens to the shop."

Quentin looked at me across the candle stub, and I saw them weigh the promise and find it heavy, and lift it anyway.

"The job goes out," they said.

"Good." I drained the cup. "Then we're agreed. My name at the foot of the page, yours beside it, and God's mercy distributed according to need."

* * *

That night I went up to the belfry, which I still climb though I no longer have authority to ring anything, and I looked out over Vellamont—the river black and patient, the lamps of the checkpoints burning like a rosary of small suspicions, the Bureau tower with its windows lit past midnight. Somewhere in that tower, a compositor was setting the purge orders right now. Some other pair of ink-stained hands, pulling a lever on other people's deaths.

Two presses in one city, I thought. Two sets of type. Everything this place will become is already wet ink on somebody's platen, and the only question left is whose hands hesitate.

I prayed, briefly, in the manner of professionals—quickly, and expecting results. Then I did what I have learned to do instead of praying for courage, which is to rehearse the moment until fear wears smooth. I stood at the belfry rail and said my own name aloud to the dark city, testing it, the way Quentin must have tested theirs a thousand times.

"Anselm Corvin," I said. "Defrocked. Unrepentant. Present."

The city did not answer. But below, far off, a bell tolled the hour—the cathedral's great Bourdon, swung by the new young sexton who pretends not to see me and leaves bread on my sill—and the sound rolled over the rooftops and through the checkpoint lamps and into the Bureau tower itself, and I thought: *they can strike my name from their rolls, but they still ring my bell.*

Three days later, a note came to the crypt, written in a hand I did not recognize, in ink the color of dried blood:

*The orders are being typeset now. They start with the flagged households. — A friend at the Register.*

I read it twice, put it in the candle flame, and watched my fortnight shrink to days.

Then I went to find a compositor, and tell them that rehearsal was over.
