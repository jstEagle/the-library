## 14. Ilse Comes Back

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that keeps a census taker awake at night. It is not the arithmetic of the ledger—ledgers are honest, in their way, and I have always found a certain comfort in columns that add up. No, the arithmetic that keeps me awake is the other kind: the kind where you count nine households on your route and eight doors open in the morning, and you sit at your desk with your pen hovering over the ninth line, wondering whether the missing family was ever there at all, or whether you simply never asked the right question.

For eleven years I have been the woman with the ledger. Ilse Vantorre, Assistant Census Officer, Third Ward, Vellamont. It is not a title that inspires poetry. People see me coming up their lane with my satchel and my ink-stained cuffs and they perform a small calculation of their own—how much trouble am I, how quickly can I be made to leave—and then they open their doors and answer my questions, because the alternative to answering is worse, and everyone knows it.

I have counted 4,112 souls in the Third Ward. I could tell you which houses have sick children behind them and which have stillborn secrets. I know that Widow Kessing lies about her lodgers because she cannot afford the lodging tax, and I have written down her lie every year for six years without once correcting it, because correcting it would cost her the roof over her head, and some arithmetic a person does in her own private ledger and nowhere else.

That is what I told myself, anyway, for eleven years. Private arithmetic. Small mercies. A lie here and there left uncorrected, like a splinter left in the thumb because removing it hurts more than carrying it.

Then Supervisor Gremmelink took my rolls out of my hands on Tuesday evening, dipped his pen, and began striking names.

* * *

I want to be precise about what I saw, because precision is the only virtue I have ever managed to keep, and I find I am hoarding my virtues now like a woman hoarding coin before a devaluation.

Gremmelink's office smells of sealing wax and cold tea. He had my ward book open on his desk—not my fair copy, the working book, the one with my marginal notes, the one where I record things like *Widow Kessing: two lodgers, both declared*—and he was working through it with a red pencil the way a man shells peas, briskly, without appetite, as though it were beneath him and necessary all at once.

"You've been thorough," he said. Not praise. An indictment wearing praise's coat.

"I try to be."

"Mm." His pencil paused at a name. Struck it. Moved on. Paused again. "The Ashgrove household. Print shop, Tanner's Lane. You recorded one child."

"One child appeared," I said. "The ledgers in the parish record two."

"And which do you believe?"

It was an odd question from a supervisor. Supervisors do not care what clerks believe; they care what clerks write. I remember thinking, in the half-second before I answered, that he had never once in four years asked my opinion on anything, not on ink suppliers, not on routing, not even on the placement of a comma, and that a man who suddenly wants your belief wants something else entirely and belief is merely the door he's trying it against.

"I believe what appeared at the door," I said.

"Which was?"

"One young man. Felix Ashgrove."

"Felix Ashgrove." He wrote something in the margin—not a correction, a mark, a small hook like a fisherman's tick—and I understood, with the slow cold spreading that precedes fainting or revelation, that I was not being consulted. I was being catalogued. My certainty was being entered into *his* ledger, the one I had never seen, the one that apparently sat underneath mine the way the cellar sits under the house, holding everything up and seeing nothing.

"The Ashgrove entry will be reviewed," he said, closing the book. "Along with several others. You needn't concern yourself."

"Reviewed for what?"

"For accuracy." And then he smiled—the smile of a man who has practiced it in front of a glass until it stopped resembling anything human—and said, "You look tired, Vantorre. Go home."

I went home. I did not sleep.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about complicity: it does not arrive with trumpets. It arrives with a closed book and a polite instruction to go home. For eleven years I told myself that my work was neutral, that counting is not choosing, that a census is a mirror and mirrors cannot be blamed for what stands before them. It is a comfortable philosophy. It fits neatly in a satchel. But on Tuesday night I sat in my rooms above the chandler's on Bellwether Steps with my own copies of the ward rolls—I keep copies; I have always kept copies; a clerk who does not keep copies is trusting someone else's memory, and memory is the first thing a state revises—and I did what I have done ten thousand times: I checked the arithmetic.

The numbers did not add up.

Not in Ashgrove's row. In others. The Ferreiras, rope-walk, four souls recorded in spring, three struck by Gremmelink's pencil in autumn—no deaths filed, no removals, no transfer of registry. The Odink family off Saltmarsh Row, five reduced to four, the fourth being a daughter aged nineteen whom I myself had interviewed, whose handwriting I had watched form the letters of her own name. The tailor Brann and his wife. A widow named Sorel. Eleven entries across three wards, each one struck not in error but in pattern, each one marked in Gremmelink's hand with that same little hook, and when I laid my pages side by side and read the hooks as a sequence instead of marks, I felt the floor of my profession open under me, because I recognized the shape of what I was looking at.

A list. Not a census. A census counts. This selected.

And every name on it was a name that had once hesitated at a door. Every name was a household where someone had answered my questions with a fraction too much care, or a fraction too little. Every name was a person who existed in ink but not—or soon, not—in law.

I did not cry. I want that on the record. I sat at my table until the candle guttered, and I did not cry, because crying is a luxury for people whose households are not about to be reviewed, and mine, I now understood, might be among them—because Gremmelink had asked what I *believed*, and belief, in the new arithmetic, was itself an entry.

So instead I did the only thing eleven years of training had equipped me to do. I sharpened my pen, pulled a fresh sheet, and copied. Page after page, in my small clerk's hand, fast and exact: names, ages, trades, the dates the entries were altered, the initials doing the altering. When the candle died I finished the last page by the light of the chandler's sign outside my window, and when it was done I folded the sheets into oilskin and sewed them into the lining of my winter coat, because I am a coward in the ordinary ways but not, it turns out, in the ones that matter, and a coward with a copying hand is a more dangerous animal than anyone at the Bureau has yet accounted for.

Then I sat in the dark and thought about a pair of hands.

* * *

They had been compositor's hands—that was what I noticed the second time, standing in the doorway of the Ashgrove shop while "Felix" answered my follow-up questions in a voice pitched like a borrowed instrument. Ink in the creases, a shine on the first two fingers of the right hand where brass type is gripped ten thousand times a day. I grew up above my uncle's bindery; I know what printers' hands look like the way farmers know weather. And the hands had known me. That was the thing. In the first interview, the compositor who served me had met my eyes exactly once, and in that single glance there had been a flicker of something I mistook at the time for mere shyness, and understood later, turning it over in the dark like a coin, was recognition. They had looked at me and seen not a visitor but a verdict.

I had written down *one child* anyway. Or rather: I had watched my own pen hesitate, and then I had written what the parish ledger said, because writing what appeared at the door would have required me to write a question I was not authorized to ask, and I was, on that morning, still a woman who did not ask unauthorized questions.

Eleven years. One hesitation. It seems a flimsy foundation for treason. But every building I have ever admired was flimsy at its footings, right up until the day it wasn't.

* * *

Thursday evening, after the Bureau lamps went dark, I walked to Tanner's Lane.

I will admit to rehearsing. I am not a spontaneous creature; I rehearse arguments the way actors rehearse soliloquies, and I had prepared three openings on the walk over—one forthright, one apologetic, one administrative—and I abandoned all three within four seconds of knocking, because the door opened and Quentin Ashgrove stood in the lamplight with a composing stick still in one hand and fear rising in their face like water in a glass, and there is no opening for that. There is only the truth, delivered plainly, the way you would deliver a death notice, because that is essentially what it is.

"You're flagged," I said. "Your household. It's on the list."

Quentin did not move for a moment. Then they stepped back from the doorway—not an invitation, exactly; more the gesture of a person who has decided that whatever comes through the door, it may as well come all the way in—and I crossed the threshold into the shop that smelled of oil and paper and the particular metallic sweetness of ink, and I heard the bolt slide home behind me with a sound like a full stop.

"Ilse Vantorre," Quentin said. "Census officer. Come after hours. Unannounced." Their voice was steady in the way of things held steady by force. "Say what you've come to say."

So I said it. All of it, standing there among the cases of type with my coat still buttoned, because if I sat down I was afraid I would never manage to stand up again. I told them about Gremmelink's red pencil. About the hooks in the margins. About the eleven households, and the tailor Brann, and the Odink girl whose handwriting I knew better than my mother's. About the review schedule I had seen pinned inside the supervisor's cabinet—typeset orders, drafted, awaiting the press, within the fortnight. And then I unpicked the stitching in my coat lining with a seam-ripper I had brought for the purpose, because I had planned even for this, and I put the folded pages on the workbench between us like a woman laying down a hand of cards she had cheated to get.

"What are these?" Quentin asked, though their fingers were already moving toward the pages, the way hands move toward proof.

"The roll," I said. "As it was before he altered it, and as it was after. Names, dates, initials. Everything needed to show the lists were falsified. Everything needed—" and here my rehearsed eloquence failed me entirely, and what came out was the plainest sentence I have ever spoken, "—to show that you were marked by a man's pencil and not by any truth."

Quentin read. I watched them read, and I want to describe it honestly, because honesty is all I have left to offer anyone: I watched a person learn the date of their own erasure, and I watched grief arrive and be set aside, deliberately, like a tool returned to a rack, because there was work to do. I have seen soldiers receive worse news with less discipline.

"My father's shop," Quentin said finally. Quietly. "He built this to last two hundred years. He used to say type is the only inheritance that can't be stolen, only misused." A pause. "And they mean to erase the people who can inherit it."

"Yes."

"Why?" And they looked up, and their eyes were wet and furious and terribly clear. "Why bring me this? You're the Bureau. Your oath—your position—you could be hanged for the ink on these pages. Why carry them to a printer's shop at night?"

I had an answer prepared. Of course I had an answer prepared; I rehearse. The answer involved duty, conscience, the moral architecture of the state versus the moral architecture of the soul, and it was a good answer, well-constructed, load-bearing.

What I actually said was: "Because I counted you. Four thousand one hundred and twelve souls in the Third Ward, and I counted every one, and I told myself counting was neutral, and then a man showed me what my numbers were for." I pressed my palms flat on the workbench, over the pages, as if to warm them. "I put you in that book, Quentin Ashgrove. Both times. Once as two children, once as none. I don't get to wash my hands of where the book goes. So I'm not washing them. I'm bringing you the errata."

The lamp between us fluttered. Somewhere in the type gallery above, a loose sort settled with a small click, the way old houses comment.

"Errata," Quentin repeated, and something moved through their face—grief and exaltation sharing a bench, taking turns—and then, unbelievably, they almost laughed. "Do you know what we call it, in the trade, when the printed word contradicts the truth? We don't burn the edition. We print a correction. We make the error famous."

"I know," I said. "I've read your father's colophons. He signed his corrections. Most printers don't."

"He said an unsigned correction is an apology whispered. He wanted his apologies heard across the square."

For a while neither of us spoke. The shop ticked and breathed around us. And I became aware—we become aware of such things slowly, the way one becomes aware of weather—that I was no longer standing in the house of a stranger I had once audited. I was standing in the house of a person to whom I had just handed my life in oilskin, and who was holding it carefully, the way you hold another person's child.

"They'll come for you too," Quentin said. "When they find these pages missing. Gremmelink will know it was you."

"He already suspects. He asked me today what I believed." I straightened my coat, rebuttoned it over the empty lining, and found that my hands were almost steady. "I told him I believe what appears at the door. Which is, I've decided, the whole of my creed now. What appears at the door. Who answers it. Whose name is on the page."

"That's a dangerous creed."

"It's cheaper than the other one," I said. "The other one costs everything, paid out slowly, in installments, forever. This one costs everything at once, up front." I allowed myself the smallest smile, the first in days. "Clerks prefer lump sums."

Quentin did laugh then—a short, startled sound, gone as quick as it came—and crossed to the window and checked the lane through the gap in the shutter, an old habit performed with new urgency. When they turned back, their face had arranged itself into something I would learn to recognize in the hard weeks ahead: the compositor's calm, the patience of a person accustomed to solving enormous problems one character at a time.

"There's an underground press," they said. "Beyond the third checkpoint. If these pages reach it—if the true roll is printed beside the false one—"

"Then the purge orders die in the cradle," I finished. "An order typeset against people the whole city can name and number and vouch for is not an order. It's a confession."

"And you'd testify? Openly? Your name attached?"

"I've spent eleven years signing other people's existences into the record." I took the seam-ripper from my pocket and set it on the bench next to the pages, a small absurd gesture, a craftsman laying down tools. "I think it's time I signed my own."

Quentin studied me for a long moment. Then they reached up to the shelf above the bench and took down a lamp—unlit—and set it beside my things, an offering or a welcome or a promise; I didn't ask which.

"Stay tonight," they said. "We'll need to plan the route. There are people you should meet—a smuggler who works the canal, a priest who holds the cathedral gate." A pause, and the ghost of a wry twist at their mouth. "You've been counting this city for a decade, Mistress Vantorre. Tomorrow you'll meet the part of it that doesn't fit in your book."

"I look forward to it," I said. "I've always distrusted books that add up too neatly."

Below the floor somewhere, water moved in the old cistern, patient as a press. And I thought, hanging my coat on the peg by the door with its lining empty for the first time in a week, how strange it is that a life can turn on a hesitation—one pen pausing over one line—and how the state builds its lists on certainties, and how certainties, unlike hesitations, never once stopped to wonder if they were wrong.

We worked until dawn. And near morning, when the first carts rattled past on Tanner's Lane, Quentin blew out the lamp and said, in the dark, in a voice stripped of all its careful steadiness:

"They're typesetting the orders Monday. Whatever we do, we do before Monday."

Three days. I have faced deadlines before. I have simply never before faced one with my whole life set out on a printer's bench beside it, waiting for ink.
