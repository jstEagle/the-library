## 15. Found Family

There is a particular hour past midnight when a print shop stops smelling of ink and starts smelling of itself—oil and metal and old paper and the ghost of every job my father ever ran. I had lived in that smell my whole life without once thinking of it as company. Then I looked up from the forme of wedding invitations I was pretending to study, and there were three strangers at my father's composing table eating bread like they owned the place, and I thought: *ah. So this is what the smell has been waiting for.*

Ilse had arrived first, hood up, carrying her copied pages sewn into the lining of her coat like contraband, which is precisely what they were. Dario had come by water, poling his flat-bottomed skiff up the delivery channel to the loading door with the ease of a man who has been arriving places he shouldn't since before he could walk. Anselm came last, on foot, in civilian clothes so bad—a brown coat the color of regret, a hat two sizes small—that he looked less like a defrocked priest traveling incognito than like a priest being punished by a tailor.

I let them in one at a time, four minutes apart, the way you distribute weight across a bridge. Old habit. Father's rule: never let the press bear more than it must.

The bread was yesterday's, from Mother Corvi's stall, bought by Dario because none of us could be seen buying anything tonight. He tore it into quarters with his hands and distributed it like a man paying wages.

"House rules," I said, standing at the head of the table with my back against the type cabinets, where the lead sat cool and heavy against my shoulder blades. "Nobody's real name gets spoken above a whisper. Nobody leaves by the front door. And nobody—" I looked at Dario, "—nobody touches the galley on the far bench. It's locked for a reason."

"What's in it?" Ilse asked.

"Nothing good."

"That's usually what's in locked things," said Anselm mildly, and bit his bread.

* * *

We planned the way I set type: piece by piece, each element checked against its neighbors, nothing trusted until it sat true in the line.

Dario went first, because Dario always goes first; it's how he avoids being talked out of things. He spread a hand-drawn map of the canal district on the table, weighted at the corners with pieces of bread crust, which struck me as either thrift or blasphemy.

"Here's the shape of it," he said. "Your plate travels in a crate marked as church candles—Anselm's parish still owes me favors, God help him. We go by water to the Salt Bridge, which is your first checkpoint. Then the customs house at Tanners' Quay, second. Then the cathedral gate, third, where our reverend friend here performs whatever miracle he's been saving up."

"Not a reverend anymore," said Anselm. "They were very thorough about the word 'not.'"

"Then perform whatever miracle a *not*-reverend can save up."

"The gate opens for deliveries before dawn bell," Anselm said, ignoring him with the practiced serenity of a man who had spent thirty years ignoring congregants. "A candle shipment going into the crypt stores is ordinary. A candle shipment accompanied by a defrocked priest is slightly less ordinary, but the guards at the gate are paid to notice cargo, not men. Men are someone else's department."

"And if their department notices?" Ilse asked.

"Then I will remind them that excommunication removed my authority but not, regrettably, my stubbornness."

I found myself almost smiling. It was an alarming sensation, like pulling proof too early—something that might yet come out wrong, but promising.

Ilse unrolled her own contribution: the copied pages. She laid them flat and turned them so we could all read them, though only she and I could read the census shorthand fluently. Her finger moved down the columns.

"Here. Households flagged for 'correction.' The mark is this hook-shaped stroke in the margin—look for it. There are forty-one households in the Vellamont rolls alone. Forty-one families." Her voice stayed level, but her finger pressed harder on the paper. "And here's what I found in my supervisor's office. The flags aren't assessments. They're assignments. Someone above him chose these houses before any census taker walked any street. My whole survey—it wasn't counting anyone. It was confirming a list already written."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from under the door. Nobody spoke. In the silence I could hear the press clock ticking, the one my father wound every night of his life, which I keep winding now mostly out of argument with him.

"So the plate in my press room," I said slowly, working it through, "isn't just evidence. It's the master copy. If the orders get typeset from the corrected rolls and the plate is destroyed—"

"Then no one can prove the lists were falsified," Ilse finished. "No one can prove those forty-one families ever existed on paper at all. That's the point of 'correction.' You don't just remove people. You remove the record that removal happened."

"That," said Anselm, "is why it must not merely reach the underground press. It must be printed. Widely. Loudly. With attribution."

Everyone looked at him.

"With attribution," I repeated.

"My name on it," he said calmly. "F. Anselm, formerly of the Cathedral of Saint Alderhelm, attests that this census is true and this government lies. Put it at the bottom of every sheet."

"Dario's right about you," I said. "You do want to be seen paying."

"I want to be seen *paying attention*," he corrected. "There's a difference. The first is vanity. The second is testimony. When they unfrocked me, the bishop read the sentence aloud in an empty cathedral, because they wouldn't risk a congregation hearing why. Empty room, sealed doors, my name struck off in silence. I have had quite enough of things done quietly. Let this one be loud."

* * *

Somewhere in the middle of arguing over the candle crate—Dario insisted on false bottoms, Anselm insisted false bottoms were "theology by other means"—Ilse stood, went to the window slit, and pulled the shutter aside a finger's width.

Across the lane, the tailor's shop stood dark. It had stood dark for two days now. The awning sagged where the sign used to hang; the sign itself was gone, unscrewed by soldiers with methodical patience while the family watched from upstairs. Or didn't watch. I don't know. They were gone by morning, whichever.

"You knew them," Ilse said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"The Bravens. Tailored my father's funeral coat." I joined her at the window. "Old Bren never once got my measurements right. Always cut for someone broader. I used to think he was doing it on purpose—dressing me as a person I wasn't." I paused. "Turns out he was just bad at shoulders."

Dario laughed, short and startled, and then stopped himself, and then we were all quiet again, because grief does that—lets a laugh in the door and then charges in behind it.

"They're on the list," Ilse said. "Number nineteen."

"Then they'll be number nineteen in the printed sheets," I heard myself say. "Every name. All forty-one. If the state won't count them honestly, we'll count them for the whole city to see."

That was the moment, I think, when the plan stopped being mine and became ours. I could feel it happen—the way you feel a forme lock into the chase, everything tightening at once from loose parts into a single solid thing that can take pressure.

* * *

We broke bread properly then, or rather Dario broke it again, having apparently appointed himself quartermaster of carbohydrates. Ilse produced a hard cheese from her coat with the guilty expression of a woman smuggling more than census pages. Anselm had a flask of communion wine he claimed was "deconsecrated by circumstances," which everyone understood to mean stolen, which everyone drank anyway.

"So," Dario said, wiping his mouth. "Four people. One plate. Three checkpoints. A fortnight, maybe less, before the purge orders go to typeset. Any of us caught, and it's not just the purge for us—it's the list for everyone we've ever nodded at." He raised the flask. "To modest ambitions."

"To modest ambitions," Anselm echoed solemnly, drinking like a man taking sacrament and enjoying the sacrilege.

Ilse drank. I drank. The wine was terrible. It was the best thing I'd tasted in a year.

I watched them in the lamplight and tried to remember the last time this room had held more than one person who knew I existed. Father, obviously. Customers, who knew Felix or knew nothing. The ledger in the back recorded two children born to Ashgrove; the law recognized one; the truth had spent twenty-six years folded between those figures like a misprint everyone agreed not to see.

Now look. A smuggler who had seen my hands shake and priced his loyalty anyway—and then, somewhere between the canal and the customs house, stopped pricing it. A priest buying redemption with his own name. A census taker who had counted forty-one families and decided she was the forty-second, counted among us now, sworn in over stale bread and bad wine.

My father left me a shop, a trade, a dead brother's name, and a will that said *my child* without troubling itself over which child. I had spent years resenting that blank space. Standing there, watching Dario show Ilse how to read the tide tables and Anselm argue theology with a man who believed in nothing but favorable currents, I understood the will differently. He hadn't left a blank. He'd left room. Room for exactly this—for whoever would stand at that table and refuse to surrender alongside me.

Family isn't what the ledger says. Family is who eats your bread at midnight and doesn't ask you to be anyone else to deserve it.

"All right," I said, and heard my father's voice in mine, the one he used before running a difficult job. "Here's how it goes. Two nights from now, we rehearse. Empty crate, no plate—I'm not risking the real cargo on a drill. Dario takes the water route, I walk the bridge as a courier with wedding invitations for cover. We time every guard change. Then home, sleep, and two nights after that, we run it for true."

"And the plate?" Ilse asked. "Until then?"

I looked at the floorboards beneath the type gallery, where I'd pried up the plank two nights ago with my heart in my throat.

"Asleep," I said. "Where dead things in this family are kept."

Anselm raised the flask one last time—to the dead, he said, and the not-yet-caught—and Dario drained it, and Ilse laughed for the first time since I'd met her, a real laugh, surprised out of her, and I thought: whatever happens at those checkpoints, they cannot take this hour back from us. It happened. It's printed. Somewhere in the great forme of the world, this night is locked in tight and true.

It was nearly three when I doused the lamp. Dario left by the loading door, sliding into his skiff like ink into paper. Anselm went up the alley whistling something hymn-adjacent and deeply forbidden. Ilse lingered at the threshold, hood half-raised, and looked back at me in the dark doorway.

"Two nights," she said. "Quentin."

Not Felix. Not compositor. Just the name, given back to me like a key returned.

"Two nights," I said.

I barred the door and stood a long while in the dark shop, listening to the press clock tick, and did not hear the knock until it came—three quick raps, low on the lower panel, the kind a fist makes when its owner is trying not to be heard making fists.

I crossed the room and put my eye to the shutter slit.

A boy stood in the lane, soaked through, holding a scrap of paper up toward the window. Even upside down I recognized the handwriting, because I had watched that hand make it for eleven days: Ilse's neat census script, hurried now, the letters leaning like runners.

And above the scrap, above the boy's head, at the far end of the lane, a lantern swung—soldiers' lantern, army issue, coming this way.
