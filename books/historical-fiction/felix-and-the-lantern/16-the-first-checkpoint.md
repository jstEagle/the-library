## 16. The First Checkpoint

*Quentin*

There is a particular kind of silence a man makes when he is trying not to think about the thing in his boat. Dario had been making it since the Saltworks Cut—humming, actually, which is worse than silence, because humming is silence wearing a costume. He hummed an old canal song about a drowned toll-keeper, cheerful as a funeral bell, and I sat in the bilge with my hands folded over a crate of wedding invitations and tried to look like a woman's cousin who had never once committed a crime.

The plate was beneath the invitations. The invitations were beneath a bolt of grey linen. The linen was beneath Dario's reputation, which was the only thing on that boat with any legal standing whatsoever.

"Stop praying," Dario said without turning around. He poled us out of the cut and into the current of the Vell, where the bridge came into view like a held breath. "I can hear you praying. It sounds like arithmetic."

"It *is* arithmetic," I said. "Three guards on the near span. Two on the far. One lantern, badly hung. If they board us, we have perhaps forty seconds before they lift that linen."

"And how long does a prayer take?"

"Longer than forty seconds."

"Then pray faster." He grinned at me over his shoulder, that grin of his that suggested the world was a joke he'd already heard the ending of and found adequate. "Besides. Nobody boards the *Merry Widow*. I've run this crossing eleven years. Old Brannigan down at the customs house calls her 'the widow' because she's taken everything from him except his dignity, and he hasn't got any."

The fog was coming off the river in slow grey ribbons, which was good. Fog is a compositor's friend; it blurs the fine print of a city. What was not good—and I saw it before Dario did, because I had spent three nights learning this bridge the way one learns a difficult passage of type, letter by letter—was the fourth figure on the near span. A new guard. Young. Standing too straight, the way men stand when the uniform is newer than their judgment.

"Dario," I said quietly. "Who is that?"

Dario squinted. And then I watched something happen to his face that I had never seen happen before: the grin died. Not faded. Died, cleanly, like a snuffed wick.

"That's Hessler's boy," he said. "That's—no. No, no. That's Corporal Voss. He worked the Saltworks patrol last spring." He poled us toward the bridge stairs anyway, because there was nowhere else to go, and his voice dropped into something flat and careful. "He knows my boat. He fined me twice for unmanifested cargo."

"What was the cargo?"

"Cheese."

"Was it cheese?"

"It was very good cheese." He set the pole and brought us alongside the stone steps with a gentleness that would have moved me under other circumstances. "Listen to me, Felix. Whatever happens up there, you say nothing. You are a wedding guest. You are a *bored* wedding guest. Boredom is the best disguise ever invented—no soldier in history has ever arrested a bored man. Can you be bored?"

"I've been bored my whole life," I said. "It's the one thing I'm qualified for."

He laughed, but it was a thin laugh, stretched over fear like parchment over a frame, and we went up the stairs together into the lantern light.

* * *

*Dario*

Here is what nobody tells you about a life of small crimes: they accumulate interest. Every cheese, every untaxed bale, every night you slipped past old Brannigan with a wink—that all goes into a ledger somewhere, and ledgers, as a certain ink-stained friend of mine keeps telling me, always get balanced eventually.

Corporal Voss stepped out of the lantern light with his hand resting easy on his belt, and I gave him the smile I give customs men, the one that says *you and I are both professionals here, and professionals don't make work for each other.*

"Evening, Corporal. Cold night for standing on stones."

Voss did not smile back. That was the first bad sign. The second was that he looked past me, at the boat, and stayed looking.

"The *Merry Widow*," he said. "Dario Venn."

"You remember me. I'm touched."

"I remember the fines." He came down two steps, boots ringing on the wet stone. "Two citations for unmanifested cargo. Your file says you're a smuggler who's too clever to get caught with anything worth hanging."

"Your file flatters me."

"Your file also says you only run the evening crossing when you're carrying something." He looked at me then, directly, and I felt the whole plan tilt like a badly locked forme. "So what's in the boat, Venn?"

And here is the thing I will tell you honestly, because I tell everything honestly eventually, usually after the money changes hands: I had a lie ready. A good one. Canal freight, household goods, moving a widow's furniture upriver, papers in order, want to see them? I'd rehearsed it against the ceiling of Anselm's crypt until I could say it asleep.

Instead I heard myself say: "Wedding invitations."

Silence. Even the river seemed to lean in.

"Wedding," said Voss.

"Invitations," I confirmed, because retreat at that point would have been its own confession. "For the Harrowgate match. Big doings. The printer's running late and the family wants them delivered tonight or the bride's mother will burn the shop down, and between you and me, Corporal, I'd sooner face you than her."

Now, why did I say that? Why, with a perfect serviceable lie sitting in my mouth like a coin under my tongue, did I swap it for the truth? Because it was nearly the truth, I suppose. Because the crate really did hold wedding invitations, four hundred of them, printed by the finest compositor in Vellamont on paper I'd paid too much for, and because I have learned—one learns things, running cargo past men with rifles—that the best hiding place for a thing is inside a slightly larger version of itself.

Also, God help me, because Felix was standing behind me being so magnificently bored that I wanted to see if the performance would hold.

"Open the crate," Voss said.

* * *

*Quentin*

I have set type for funerals, indictments, and one particularly vicious divorce notice, and I will tell you what I told myself on those cold stone steps: a lie, like a line of type, must be composed in advance. You cannot improvise letters. You can only lock up what you have already cast.

So when Voss said *open the crate*, I stepped forward—the bored cousin, roused to mild irritation—and said, "Must you? There's a wax seal. My aunt sealed it herself. She'll know if it's been broken, believe me, she has a gift."

"A gift," said Voss.

"For noticing things," I said, and let him hear, just underneath, the smallest possible suggestion that he might be noticed too.

It is a strange craft, misdirection. Father taught me that the eye goes where the contrast is strongest. So while Voss frowned at me—at my ink-stained fingers, which I had thoughtfully smudged further that morning, a compositor's hands dressed as a courier's—I watched Dario work the crate lid loose with the ease of a man opening his own pantry, and I watched the lantern light fall across the top layer: cream stock, gilt edges, silver ribbon, and there in the center of each card, in type I had cut myself from Father's matrices, the intertwined names *Marguerite Harrowgate* and *Edmund Fell*, with the date, the church, the hour.

All of it false. All of it beautiful. I had pulled proofs by lamplight until my eyes burned, and I confess that some traitor part of me was proud of them even now, even here, while a corporal of the Vellamont garrison lifted one card to the light and read it with the slow suspicion of a man who has never been invited to anything.

"Harrowgate," he said. "The wool family?"

"The wool family," Dario agreed solemnly.

"My sergeant's sister does laundry for them." Voss turned the card over. Turned it back. And I stood there doing sums again—forty seconds, thirty-five, thirty—while the most dangerous object in the city lay under a false bottom six inches beneath his thumb, a census plate with the names of every household marked for removal, including mine, including, I would learn later, quite possibly his.

Then he did the worst thing. He looked at me.

Not at the cards. At me. At my hands, and then up, at my face, and something moved behind his eyes—not recognition exactly, but the animal unease of a man who senses a misprint without being able to say where. Ilse had looked at me the same way, months ago, over the census book. People who count things for a living develop the instinct.

"You're not family," he said. "You're the printer."

"I'm the courier," I said, and pitched my voice into the flat boredom I'd practiced against the wall of the type gallery. "The printer's brother, if you must know. Family enough to carry the box, not family enough to eat at the head table. Such is my station in life. Do you want to inspect the ribbon too, Corporal, or shall we let a bride's mother sleep tonight?"

A long moment. The river slapped the stones. Somewhere above us, on the far span, another guard called out the hour.

And then Voss laughed—a short, defeated laugh, the laugh of a young man realizing he has been made ridiculous by ribbon—and dropped the card back into the crate. "Get it off my bridge," he said. "And Venn—" he jabbed a finger at Dario, "—next time you cross at night, I'm opening everything. Everything. Tell your wool people whatever you like."

"God bless the Harrowgates," Dario said piously, and took up the pole.

We went down the stairs. We pushed off into the current. We rounded the first bend, where the lantern light of the bridge shrank to a smudge and then to nothing, and only then did either of us breathe.

* * *

*Dario*

Felix made a sound I'd never heard from him—half laugh, half the noise a rope makes when it finally takes a load—and sat down hard on the thwart among the wedding invitations like a man whose legs had resigned without notice.

"He read the card," Felix said. "He *read* it. He could recite Marguerite Harrowgate's fictional wedding date to his dying day."

"Better than reciting yours." I kept poling, because my arms needed something stupid to do while my heart caught up. "You were good. The aunt with the seal. Where did she come from?"

"She didn't come from anywhere. She came from terror." Felix looked at their hands—those lovely ruined compositor's hands—and I saw the triumph start to curdle, right there on their face, the way milk curdles in a thunderstorm. "Dario. Those invitations are real objects now. Real paper, real type, my actual hand in the cutting. Four hundred of them. If anyone ever traces the Harrowgate job—if anyone asks the wool family whether they're getting married—"

"They'll find no record," I finished. "Because there is none."

"Because there is none," Felix echoed. "Which means the forgery points backward. To whoever printed it. To this boat. To Voss, who touched it, who'll swear to it in front of his sergeant if anyone ever asks." They looked up at me, and the lamplight—or rather the starlight, since we'd left the lamps dark—caught the exhaustion there. "We bought our crossing with evidence against ourselves."

"Well," I said. "That's marriage for you."

They stared at me.

"Think about it," I said, warming to it, because humor is how I keep from thinking, and someone on this boat had to keep from thinking. "You bind yourself to a fiction, you put your name to it, you spend your substance on it, and years later it turns up as proof of everything you did. Weddings, smuggling—same trade, different freight." I shipped the pole and let the *Merry Widow* drift with the current, quiet and black, toward the second checkpoint. "Besides. It's not evidence yet. It's only evidence if somebody comes looking. And nobody comes looking unless—"

"Unless Ilse's supervisor finds the copied pages," Felix said. "Unless the raid next door turns up something. Unless Anselm's name on the broadside draws the wrong kind of readers. Unless." They exhaled. "The whole plan is built of *unlesses*."

"That's what a plan is. A plan is just hope with a schedule." I took up the pole again and swung the bow toward the customs house lights, yellow and patient in the fog ahead. "One down. Two to go. And the next one's worse, so save your boredom—you'll need it."

But I'll tell you what I didn't say, what I kept under my tongue like that coin I mentioned: I hadn't lied to a customs man in eleven years. Eleven years of cheese and calico and convenient blindness, and tonight, for a box of paper and a stranger with ink under their nails, I'd told the truth dressed as a lie and felt it cost me something. Stakes, Felix would call it. I call it the beginning of the end of a perfectly comfortable career.

Ahead, the customs house clock began to strike ten, and each stroke sounded, to my ear, exactly like a ledger closing.

Behind us, on the bridge we'd just bought our way across, a lantern bobbed into motion—Corporal Voss, leaving his post, walking fast toward the far bank.

Toward the customs house.

Toward us.
