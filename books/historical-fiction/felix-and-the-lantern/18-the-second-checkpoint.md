## 18. The Second Checkpoint

The note Ilse had sent arrived folded inside a delivery of printing ink, which was either the most brilliant misdirection I have ever benefited from or the laziest, depending on how much you respect the intelligence of customs clerks. It said, in a hand pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*Pass void. They have my pages. They will be watching for a compositor named Felix Ashgrove. You cannot cross as him anymore. I am sorry. I am not sorry about everything else.*

Twelve words and eleven more, and they dismantled three weeks of planning the way a compositor dissolves a forme—one piece lifted out at a time until nothing holds.

I sat in the back room of Anselm's crypt passage staging area—which is to say, a wine cellar that smelled of candle grease and old apologies—and read the note four times while Dario watched me with the particular stillness he reserved for weather he didn't like.

"So," he said finally. "The pass is dead."

"The pass is dead."

"And they're looking for Felix."

"They're looking for Felix."

Dario turned his cup in his hands, around and around, like a man trying to read the bottom without drinking. "Then who are you going to be?"

It is a strange thing, to be asked that question by a smuggler over watered wine, and to realize you do not have an answer ready. I had spent nine years being Felix Ashgrove—dead brother, convenient fiction, a coat worn so long it had taken the shape of my shoulders. I had answered doors in his voice, signed receipts in his hand, let neighbors believe in him so thoroughly that when the census came asking after children, I had nearly forgotten which of us was the ghost.

And now the state had caught up with the fiction. They were hunting a name, and the name wasn't mine.

"I don't know," I said.

"Yes, you do," Dario said. "You've known since she walked through your door with her little book. You just haven't liked knowing."

He was right, which was irritating, because I prefer it when people are useful rather than correct. But there it was, sitting between us on the barrel: if Felix was burned, then the only papers left in the world that could carry me across the customs house floor were the ones my father had made. The will. *To my child.* No name. No brother. Just the word itself, unadorned, the only sentence in all of Vellamont's endless registers that had ever said I existed.

"You can't show them that," Dario said, when I told him. "A will naming no one? They'll laugh you into the river."

"Or they'll look at it long enough for me to get past the desk."

"That's not a plan, printer. That's a coin toss."

"Every plan this month has been a coin toss," I said. "At least this one lands on something true."

* * *

I want to be honest about what that night was, because memory softens things and I refuse to let it soften this.

That night was terror dressed up as industry. We worked in the cellar by lantern light—Anselm praying loudly enough that any listening ear would hear only piety, Dario repacking the crate with the plate nested in false bottom among vestment linens, and me rehearsing. That's what I called it, anyway. Rehearsing. As though I were setting type instead of walking into a building full of soldiers whose job was to find exactly what I was.

Here is what the rehearsal looked like: I stood in front of a mirror Anselm used for checking his collar, holding my father's will, and I said my own name out loud.

"Quentin Ashgrove."

It came out wrong the first time. Thin. Apologetic. The voice of someone asking permission to exist, which is more or less the voice I'd been using for nine years.

"Again," I told the mirror.

"Quentin Ashgrove." Better. Still wrong. There was a hitch in it, a flinch at the end, as though I expected someone to interrupt.

"Again."

By the twentieth repetition Dario had stopped pretending not to listen. By the fortieth, Anselm had come down from his prayers to stand at the foot of the stairs, watching me the way you watch someone learn to walk again after a bad break—wanting to help, knowing better.

"You keep saying it like you're bracing for correction," Anselm said at last. "Like it's an argument you expect to lose."

"It is an argument," I said. "Nine years of one. The whole city has been telling me I'm Felix. My own mouth has been telling me I'm Felix."

"And what does your father's will tell you?"

I looked down at the paper. The ink had gone brown with age, the flourishes faded, but the center line held firm the way good type always does. *To my child, and to no other, I leave the shop, the press, the sorts, and the name of Ashgrove entire.*

"He doesn't say whether it's Felix or Quentin," I said. "He couldn't. Legally he couldn't write what I actually was. So he wrote the only thing that was true for both of us. *My child.*"

"A clever lawyer's dodge," Anselm observed.

"My father wasn't a lawyer. He was a compositor." I ran my thumb along the line. "We spend our lives choosing exactly the words that fit. He chose these."

Anselm was quiet a moment. Then he said, "Then say it the way he set it. Not as a plea. As a proof."

* * *

Morning came gray off the river, the kind of light that makes everyone look guilty of something. Dario walked me as far as the fish market and no farther—"Two of us together reads as conspiracy; one of us alone reads as Tuesday"—and pressed something into my palm before letting go. A coin. Not money. A token, brass, stamped with a barge number I recognized as one of his legitimate runs.

"If it goes badly," he said, "flash that at the desk clerk, Marlow. He owes me from a card game and doesn't know it yet. It won't save you. It'll buy you ninety seconds, maybe. Enough to swallow a note or start a fire. Something."

"Dario."

"Don't thank me. It makes the merchandise feel expensive." His grin did its usual work of hiding whatever was underneath, but underneath, this time, I could see the shape of something. Concern, maybe. In a smuggler, that's practically love.

I put the token in my left pocket and the will in my breast pocket, over my heart, where it sat like a coal.

The customs house rose at the end of the quay the way it always had—a fat stone ledger of a building, windows like ruled lines, the republic's flag hanging limp as a dropped comma. I had passed it a thousand times as Felix, invisible, unremarkable, one more body in the crowd of bodies. Today every stone of it seemed aimed at me.

The queue outside was short. Short queues at checkpoints are never good news; it means the guards are bored, and bored guards look for entertainment.

I took my place behind a woman with a market basket and practiced breathing. Four counts in. Six counts out. Compositor's patience. You cannot rush a line of type. You cannot rush a line of anything.

The guard at the door waved the woman through, then me. Inside: the great hall, marble gone yellow with age, the long desks, the smell of wax seals and damp wool. And there, at the second checkpoint proper—the document desk—a clerk I did not know, flanked by a soldier who was doing the thing soldiers do when they've been told to watch for someone: looking at faces instead of papers.

*They will be watching for a compositor named Felix Ashgrove.*

Good, I thought, and the thought steadied me like a quoined forme. Let them watch for a ghost. Ghosts are easy to miss. I would give them the living instead.

"Next."

I stepped up. Put my satchel on the desk—it contained wedding invitations, thirty of them, genuine work from a genuine client, my cover story intact and useless, because cover stories only cover you until they ask the wrong question. The plate rode elsewhere, in the crate Dario had already moved ahead through arrangements I wasn't told about and preferred not to know. My job today was simpler and worse. My job was to be passable.

"Purpose of crossing?"

"Delivery of printed goods to the parish of Saint Ferrell." Keep it flat. Keep it dull. Boredom is armor.

"Business papers."

I produced the invoice, the client's order, the shop's registration—all in the name Ashgrove & Son, Printers. The clerk skimmed. The soldier, behind him, watched my face.

"Ashgrove," the clerk said. "Which Ashgrove? There's a notation here. Shop registered under two principals. Father and son. Felix Ashgrove, compositor." His finger tapped the register. "Is that you?"

This was the hinge. This was the moment the whole plan had been built to avoid, and Ilse's arrest had broken the mechanism, and here it was anyway, arriving on schedule the way catastrophe does.

I could feel Felix rising in my throat like a tide. *Yes. I'm Felix. Been Felix all my life, sir, ask anyone.* Nine years of reflex pushing the words up, smooth, practiced, safe—

And underneath the reflex, cold and clear, Ilse's handwriting: *You cannot cross as him anymore.*

"No," I said.

The clerk looked up. The soldier's gaze sharpened.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Felix Ashgrove is dead," I said. "He died nine years ago. Same year as his father. It's in your own registry—I'd have thought a census office kept better books." My voice was steady. Some part of me observed this from a distance with considerable astonishment, the way you watch your own hands pull proofs. "I am the surviving principal of Ashgrove & Son. I've been running the shop alone since."

"Name," the soldier said. Not a question. A requisition.

"Quentin Ashgrove."

Saying it felt like pulling the first print off a freshly inked plate—you press, you hold your breath, you lift, and you don't know yet whether you've got a clean strike or a ruin. The name hung in the marble air of the customs house, mine, spoken aloud in daylight in front of the state itself, for the first time in nine years.

Nothing happened. No thunderbolt. The clerk merely frowned and reached for a ledger, which is the state's version of a thunderbolt.

"Quentin Ashgrove," he repeated, hunting through pages. "No record under that name in the household rolls."

"There would not be," I said. "As I explained. The registry lists the household under the son's name because the son handled the public business. After his death the trade continued under the same license. That's common enough. Ask any guild clerk."

It wasn't common at all. It was, in fact, precisely the sort of irregularity that got households flagged for review, which meant I was confessing to a small crime to avoid a large one, and the soldier knew it—he had the expression of a dog that has caught a scent but not yet decided whether it's worth chasing.

"Documents," the clerk said. "Proving succession. Or proving identity. Preferably both."

Here it was, then. The coin, tossed.

I took out the will.

My father's will. Brown ink, faded flourishes, the seal cracked but legible, witnessed by two men long since dead or dispersed. I laid it on the desk the way you lay a fresh sheet on the tympan—with care, because it mattered, because it was the best thing I had ever pulled.

The clerk read it. His lips moved slightly on the key phrase, which I could recite in my sleep: *To my child, and to no other, I leave the shop, the press, the sorts, and the name of Ashgrove entire.*

"This names no one," he said. "'Child' isn't a person. It's a category."

"It's a legal term, sir, in use in every testament in this city. 'Child' binds the estate to whoever lawfully fills the role. The role is filled." I heard my father's cadence coming out of my mouth—compositor's cadence, exact-word cadence—and realized I had inherited more than a shop. "One child. Deceased. One survivor of the household, named in the shop's guild registration and in nine years of tax receipts, all bearing the signature you're welcome to compare against the one on this instrument."

Because that was the thing—the thing I had thought of at three in the morning and hadn't dared tell Dario, in case he talked me out of it. My father had taught me letters. He had cut my first steel pens himself. And a man who spends forty years judging type recognizes his apprentice's hand anywhere. The signature on the will—witnessed, sealed, nine years old—was close enough to mine that any clerk with eyes and a grudge against sloppiness would see it. Close enough to be arguable. Arguable was all I needed. Arguable buys time, and time buys the door.

The clerk held the will up to the window light. Then he drew out my invoice, my registration, my tax receipt, and set the signatures side by side like sorts in a composing stick.

The soldier leaned in too. "Looks similar," he said.

"Similar isn't identical," the clerk muttered.

"No," I agreed, pleasantly, while my heart performed feats of engineering. "Ink ages differently than intent. But you'll notice the peculiar loop on the Q—that's a compositor's habit, we make our letterforms the same in everything. My father taught me." Which was even true.

Silence. The hall ticked along around us—stamps, murmurs, a cart rolling somewhere beyond the arches. Somewhere upstairs, I knew, there were lists with my address on them, flagged for correction, waiting only for typesetting. Every second this clerk spent squinting at signatures was a second borrowed against that press.

The clerk looked up. Behind the official suspicion, something else flickered—an old clerk's private amusement, perhaps, at a puzzle well-posed. Or pity. I've wondered since which it was, and I've stopped needing to know.

"Succession appears... irregular but supportable," he said slowly. "Pending verification." His stamp hovered. The soldier shifted. "However—there remains the matter of the secondary principal. If Felix Ashgrove is deceased, why does the household census—" he consulted a slip of paper that appeared in his hand as if summoned by my dread "—filed last month, record two children residing at the premises?"

The hall went very quiet in my ears. The queue behind me had stopped shuffling.

I had prepared for this question in every form except the one in which it was actually asked, which is the way all important questions arrive. Two children. Ilse's pen. Her pause over the entry. Whatever she wrote that day—truth, suspicion, mercy, trap—was sitting now in front of this clerk in summary form, and I could not know which.

"An error," I said.

"Whose?"

"Mine," I said. "I gave the return. I was confused about the form. Grief makes bookkeeping strange." All true. None of it sufficient. I watched the stamp hang in the air, and the soldier's hand drift toward the slip of paper, and understood with terrible clarity that the next thirty seconds contained my entire life.

Then, from my left pocket, the brass token, which my fingers found without consulting me.

Or not the token. Because before I could decide whether to gamble on a smuggler's card debt, another voice entered the hall—a clerk's voice from an inner office, calling, "Marlow! These correction schedules need countersigning before noon, the supervisor wants them to press today—"

*Correction schedules. To press today.*

The clerk—Marlow, apparently, debtor to Dario or not—glanced toward the inner office, and in that glance I saw his whole hierarchy of concerns reshuffle. Schedules due at noon outranked one irregular compositor by a comfortable margin. He stamped my documents with the brisk violence of a man reclaiming his own time.

"Goods pass. Identity provisional, pending registry verification. Don't make me regret the ink." He pushed the papers back. "Move along."

I moved along. I did not run. Running is a confession, and I had just spent five minutes establishing myself as a person who files accurate paperwork, more or less. I walked—four counts in, six counts out—across the marble, under the arch, past the soldier whose eyes followed me the whole way like a splinter working toward the skin.

And then I was through. Out the far side, onto the quay, into the gray ordinary noise of the city, carrying a stamped pass, a dead brother, and my own name, newly claimed and already provisionally filed away in some drawer, pending verification, in the archives of a state I was on my way to humiliate.

* * *

Dario found me two streets later, materializing alongside as smugglers do, as if he'd been walking with me for miles.

"Well?"

"Through."

His eyebrows asked the rest.

"I told them my name," I said. "My actual name. To their faces. With paperwork."

Dario stopped walking. People jostled past us. He looked at me with an expression I had never seen on him—not the grin, not the merchant's appraising squint, but something open and briefly young. "You mad bastard," he said, with feeling. "You glorious, impossible—" He clapped my shoulder hard enough to bruise. "How did it take?"

"A will, a forged-by-inheritance signature, and a clerk who wanted his lunch."

"That's how everything worth doing gets done," Dario said. "Somebody wants their lunch." Then, quieter: "The soldier. Did he buy it?"

"He bought the delay. Not the story. He'll pull the thread."

"Then we pull faster." He fell into step beside me, and we headed upriver together, toward the cathedral, toward the third checkpoint, toward Anselm and the gate and whatever waited past it. The crate with the plate was already moving, somewhere ahead of us in the veins of the city. The purge orders were already moving too, somewhere behind us, toward a press.

Two races, one finish line, and the clock striking noon somewhere over the rooftops.

"We've got until they verify the registry," I said. "Maybe a day. Maybe an hour."

"Then don't waste it being careful," Dario said cheerfully. "Careful is for cargo. You're family now, and family we move fast."

Ahead, above the gray slate of the city, the cathedral towers stood up into the light like type raised out of the galley—tall, black, waiting to be read. At their gate, small in the distance, a figure in plain black stood watching the road.

Anselm had seen us. Anselm had been waiting.

And behind him, I could see now, the cathedral doors stood open, and the street between us and them was already filling with soldiers.
