## 19. The Cathedral Gate

The plate weighed less than a prayer book. That was the cruelty of it—four pounds of lead and the whole architecture of my survival, and it sat in my satchel as innocently as a loaf of bread. I had carried heavier formes up the type gallery stairs a thousand times without thinking. Now every step toward Saint Verena's gate felt like walking with a bell tied to my ankle.

Dario had gone ahead an hour before, poling his barge down the canal with that unhurried slouch of his, whistling something off-key to advertise himself harmless. The wedding invitations—the real ones, forged for a couple who did not exist, addressed to guests who had never been born—were already distributed as cover stories across three checkpoints' worth of guards. Ilse's note, scrawled in her small upright hand and pressed into mine at the tanner's alley, said only: *They have me. The pass is void. Go as yourself.* Beneath it she had copied out the number of my father's will from memory, because of course she had memorized it. She was the sort of person who memorized things on principle.

*Go as yourself.* As if I knew how. I had been Felix for eleven years—Felix who answered doors, Felix who signed receipts, Felix who existed in ink but not in law. Going as myself meant standing in front of a guard with nothing but a dead man's document and my own face, which no ledger had ever confirmed belonged to anyone.

Father Anselm was waiting at the cathedral's side door, in the shadow of the buttress where the lamplight gave out. He looked older than he had at our first meeting, or perhaps I had simply never looked properly before. His cassock was patched at both elbows. His hands were folded over his belly like a man at a funeral who has decided to be comfortable about it.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm early. Dario's late. I'm merely the wrong kind of punctual."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't, which frightened me more than any expression he could have chosen. "They came to the cathedral office this afternoon," he said quietly. "A lieutenant and two men. Asking after a compositor. They had a description." He looked at my ink-stained fingers, curled around the satchel strap. "It was not a flattering likeness, but it was accurate."

"Then we go now. Through the crypt, while it's dark."

"No." He said it the way my father used to say *no* when I wanted to run a job twice through the press to save ink—not cruel, just final. "You'll never make the far gate. They've set a watch on the canal stairs since sundown. I watched them settle in like cats outside a mousehole." He straightened, and there was something in the motion—a settling of weight, of intention—that I didn't understand yet. "The crypt passage empties behind the almshouse, a quarter mile east. But between here and there is the cloister corridor, and between here and the corridor stands whatever soldier they send to knock."

"Then I go around—"

"Quentin." My name. Not Felix. He had learned it from Ilse, and he used it now like a key turning in a lock I'd forgotten I carried. "How long do you imagine a defrocked priest is useful to God? I have been waiting three years for something worth being useless for."

I didn't understand him then. I understand him now, and I would give a great deal to un-understand it.

"The passage door is the third arch past the ossuary," he went on briskly, as if discussing drainage. "Take the lamp but keep it shuttered until you're below. The steps are uneven—count them, there are thirty-one, and mind the twelfth. And Quentin." He took my shoulder. His grip was stronger than his hands suggested. "Whatever you hear behind you—and you will hear something—do not come back. Do not open that door once it closes. A door opened twice is a door opened for everyone."

"What will I hear?"

"The truth," he said, "being spoken at volume."

* * *

From Father Anselm's reckoning, he had been useless since the winter they stripped his office from him—three years ago, for the crime of baptizing children whose names appeared on no register, marrying couples whose unions the state declined to count, burying the unnumbered dead in consecrated ground because he judged God's arithmetic superior to the Chancery's. He had kept the cathedral keys anyway; no one had thought to take them from a man nobody officially employed. He wandered the cloisters like a ghost who had neglected to haunt anything in particular.

Tonight, for the first time in three years, he stood in front of a door with his whole weight behind it.

He heard them coming before he saw them—boots on flagstone, the particular rhythm of men who believed themselves owners of whatever ground they crossed. Four of them this time, torchlight sliding along the arcade. The lieutenant from the afternoon was among them, a young man with the unfortunate combination of ambition and diligence, which Anselm had learned, in forty years of parish politics, was far more dangerous than either vice alone.

"Father." The lieutenant stopped short of the threshold. Even soldiers hesitated at church doors; the habit outlived the faith. "We're looking for a fugitive. We believe they've entered the cathedral grounds."

"Then you believe more than I do," Anselm said. "I believe it's going to rain. Beyond that, I keep my convictions modest."

"This is not a jesting matter."

"Very little is. That has never stopped the rain."

The lieutenant's jaw tightened. Behind him, one of the soldiers shifted, torch dipping, and in its light Anselm saw the courtyard beyond—saw, briefly, a figure crossing the far colonnade with a satchel and a shuttered lamp, moving with the careful economy of someone counting steps. Thirty-one, boy, he thought. Mind the twelfth. He let none of it reach his face. Three years of saying nothing had taught him precisely what his face could hold.

"The cathedral is consecrated ground," he said aloud. "And I am, whatever the Chancery chooses to call me, still a priest of this diocese. No officer enters these walls without a warrant signed by the bishop."

"The bishop signs what we put in front of him."

"How convenient for you both."

"That is not how this conversation concludes," the lieutenant said, and produced a paper. "This is. A writ of entry, sealed by the Provost's office, authorizing search of all ecclesiastical premises in the quarter." He held it out. "Stand aside, Father."

Anselm did not take the paper. He considered, instead, the strange mathematics of his position: a man without office, defending a door he had no right to guard, for a child the law insisted did not exist. Every term of it was absurd. He found, examining the absurdity, that it fit him like a well-made coat—better than the cassock had, honestly, which had always ridden up at the shoulders.

"No," he said.

"Father Anselm—" The lieutenant had done his homework; the name was deliberate, a small blade. "You are defrocked. You have no authority here. Step aside, and perhaps the Provost's office takes a lenient view of tonight's obstruction."

"My son," Anselm said gently, "you are mistaken about nearly everything, so allow me to correct the smallest error first. Defrocked means they took my title. It does not mean they took the door." And he stepped back onto the threshold itself, filled the archway with his body, folded his hands, and waited.

They could have moved him. Any two of them could have moved him; he was sixty-one years old and had the build of a man who considered bread a hobby. But a priest barring a church door is a different problem than a man blocking a street, and the lieutenant knew it, and hesitated, and in hesitating spent the only currency that mattered.

In the courtyard behind them, very faintly, a door closed on its hinges. Thirty-one steps, and the twelfth survived.

Anselm smiled at the lieutenant with genuine warmth. "Now," he said, "let us discuss the writ you're holding, because I believe you'll find the seal is a month old and re-issued under the previous Provost, which makes it worth rather less than the vellum it's written on. Shall I fetch my reading glasses? I keep them in the sacristy. It's a long walk. There are many interesting vestments along the way."

* * *

Thirty-one steps down into the dark, and I counted every one, because Anselm told me to and because counting was the only prayer I knew. Above me, muffled by stone, I heard voices rise and fall—the low murmur of argument, then Anselm's voice lifting into something sonorous and infuriatingly unhurried, a man reciting canon law to people carrying swords. I heard the crack of a boot against wood. I heard him say, clearly enough to reach me through four feet of medieval masonry: *"Gently, gentlemen. The door is the property of God, and He itemizes."*

I wanted to go back. Every nerve in me wanted to go back. My hand was on the twelfth step and the satchel was heavy and somewhere above me an old man was spending himself like coin so that I could walk away, and the whole animal machinery of my body screamed to turn around and help.

But he had told me. *Do not open that door once it closes. A door opened twice is a door opened for everyone.* Courage, I understood then, standing in the dark with my palm flat against cold stone, was not a thing you felt. It was a thing that got handed to you, like a baton in a relay, and your only duty was not to drop it—to carry it forward at whatever pace you could manage until someone else's hand closed around it. Anselm had taken his leg of the race. Ilse had run hers, into a cell. Dario, gods knew where, was probably running his with a stolen boat and a magnificent lie. Mine was thirty-one steps long and ended at an underground press.

I ran it. I counted the steps aloud, whispering, and at the bottom there was the almshouse door, and beyond it the wet dark of the east quarter, and the canal breathing its slow tide-smell, and the city lying unaware beneath its lamps with the purge orders twelve days from the press.

The plate rode against my hip. Four pounds of lead. The true census, every name the state meant to erase, cast in metal by some clerk's careless misprint—proof that all those people existed, that the Ashgrove household held a child, that I was somebody's daughter and somebody's son and somebody's own self all at once.

Behind me, above, the cathedral door groaned. I did not look back. Anselm would have approved; he struck me as a man with firm opinions about runners who glanced over their shoulders.

Ahead, at the mouth of Tanner's Row, a lantern swung once, twice—the signal we'd agreed on. Then a voice, pitched low and familiar, wry even in extremis:

"You're late, compositor. And I have news that isn't good, and a boat that isn't fast, and about four minutes to choose between them."

Dario stepped out of the shadow, and the look on his face told me the night had only begun to collect its debts.
