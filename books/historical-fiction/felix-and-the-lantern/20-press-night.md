## 20. Press Night

The underground press smelled like everything I had ever loved and most of what I had spent my life avoiding: ink, damp stone, tallow, other people's unwashed conviction. It lived beneath a chandlery off Tanner's Row, down a stair so narrow that Dario had to turn the crate sideways and mutter the whole way about cargo that fought back.

"Careful," I told him. "That cargo has a better education than you do."

"That cargo weighs forty pounds of lead," he said. "Education doesn't weigh anything, or scholars would be harder to rob."

The press itself was an old hand-pull, older than Father, older probably than the city's habit of lying to itself. Its previous owner—a chandler who printed seditious almanacs between batches of candles—had died the previous winter, and the network had inherited the machine the way orphans inherit coats: gratefully, and without anyone quite knowing if it fit.

It fit well enough. That was the whole philosophy of the underground, as far as I could tell. Nothing fit perfectly. You pulled the lever anyway.

I set the crate on the stone floor and knelt beside it and did not open it right away. This is something non-printers never understand. There is a moment before you see the type—your type, the type you set or found or stole—when it could still be anything. When the forme might be damaged in transit, when the plate might have cracked, when the whole enterprise might collapse for reasons that have nothing to do with courage or its absence. For one held breath, you are not yet responsible.

Then I lifted the lid, and the lamplight caught the metal, and there it was: the census plate, the misprint, the master list of households flagged for *correction*, with the Ashgrove address sitting in it like a splinter in my own palm.

Responsible again. Well.

* * *

Father used to say that setting type was the only honest prayer he knew. You kneel. You handle small cold things one at a time. You assemble them in an order that means something, knowing that the order is the entire point—that the same lead letters, rearranged, become either a wedding announcement or a death sentence. He said God was in the spacing. I said God was in the kerning, because I was insufferable even at fourteen, and he threw a piece of quoin at me, gently, the way he threw everything.

He never set a plate like this one. No compositor had, in the history of Vellamont, because no compositor had ever been asked to print the state's own confession back to it.

The plate was a rejected proof—the census office had caught the error, or what they called an error, before distribution. Some clerk with a steady hand and a guilty conscience—or possibly just bad luck; the two are indistinguishable from behind—had let the wrong forme go to casting, and the result was a full record: every household in the city marked for removal, with the reasons coded in the state's tidy shorthand. *Irregular documentation.* *Discrepancy of persons.* *Anomalous registration.*

Anomalous registration. That was us. That was me, before I had a word for it. Somewhere in a government office, a man I'd never met had looked at the Ashgrove ledgers—two children recorded, one child presented—and stamped a code, and that code meant men with rifles at our door before winter.

I ran my thumb along the row where we lived. My hands were shaking. I want to be honest about that, because everyone in this story tends toward heroism in the retelling, including me, and the retelling is a lie. In the moment, kneeling under a chandlery with ink under my nails and forty pounds of evidence between my knees, I was not brave. I was a compositor reading a proof, and the proof said: *you were always going to be found.*

And then, the way grief does—it turned over and became something else. Because the proof also said: *they wrote it down.* They wrote all of it down. Every family, every name, every euphemism. The state had done the hardest part of the work already. It had composed the truth, in lead, in mirror image, waiting only for someone to pull it into the light.

You don't waste a clean forme. Father taught me that too.

* * *

Anselm arrived at midnight, smelling of candle smoke and incense he was no longer entitled to burn. He came down the stairs sideways, like everyone did, and stood at the bottom catching his breath with his hand pressed flat against the stone, and for a moment he looked so much like Father in the last year of his life that I had to turn away and pretend to inspect the tympan.

"The gate held?" I asked.

"The gate held." He straightened. "A sergeant asked why a defrocked priest was standing in a doorway at two in the morning. I told him I was praying."

"And?"

"He apologized for interrupting." A pause. "Being disgraced has unexpected privileges. Nobody suspects a man of ambition anymore."

Dario laughed from his corner, where he was doing something to a wheel of cheese that I chose not to witness. "That's your whole strategy, priest? Confuse them with humility?"

"It's everyone's strategy. Yours involves a boat and a smile. Mine involves a doorway and God's apparent absence. We all improvise."

Anselm crossed the room and looked at the plate where it lay locked in the chase, ready for the bed of the press. He read it the way he must once have read scripture—slowly, lips barely moving—and I watched him find the names. The tailor's family from across our lane, gone by morning three weeks ago. The Widow Castellan and her sister. The ferryman's boys. Names I knew and names I didn't, column after column, each one preceded by a code and followed by nothing, because the dead and the disappeared don't get follow-up lines.

"They're all here," he said quietly.

"All of them. Even the ones the supervisor erased from Ilse's working copies. The original plates kept everything. Whoever cast this made a mistake, or made a choice, and either way—" I touched the edge of the chase. "Every name they tried to unwrite is still written."

Anselm put his hand flat on the metal, the way you'd touch a coffin lid or a sleeping child. "My name is on it?"

"In the colophon." I had set it myself, three nights ago, in this room, by this lamp: *Printed by order of no one. Anselm Ferrand, priest, claims the fault.* "As demanded. You realize it's an admission of guilt to sedition, forgery, and about four flavors of heresy."

"I've been defrocked for less," he said. "It seemed a shame to let the vestments take all the credit."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about printing testimony: it is exactly like printing invitations. That was the revelation of that night, the thing I keep returning to even now, months later, when people ask me—people do ask me now—what it felt like. It felt like work. Gloriously, redemptively, exactly like work.

I had run the route with the plate hidden inside a forme of wedding invitations, and perhaps that rehearsal taught me more than the guards' rhythms. Perhaps it taught me that a plate is a plate. That the same hands, the same pressure, the same paper, the same prayerful attention to packing and impression serve a lie and a truth equally well, and the difference lives entirely in the arrangement—which is to say, in the choices, which is to say, in the person making them.

So I did what I have done every working day since I was nine years old. I checked the quoins. I tested the ties. I laid the first sheet—the good paper, the paper Dario had smuggled past two checkpoints under a manifest declaring it *rag stock, inferior, unsuitable for official use*, because the best contraband is trash—and I set the points, and I pulled the bed under the platen, and I adjusted the frisket with the tips of my fingers, the way Father had guided them when I was small enough to need guiding.

"You'll want to pull the first one," Anselm said.

"It's your name on it."

"It's your father's press craft on it." He glanced at Dario. "And your boat got it here. But the boy set the type."

"Not a boy," I said, automatically, the correction worn smooth as a coin.

"No," Anselm agreed, without missing a beat, without looking up. "Not a boy."

I took the handle.

* * *

The first pull is always the worst pull, because until it comes up you know nothing—you suspect, you hope, you fear, but you don't *know*. The platen descends. The press groans through its old joints. There is that half-second of total commitment when the lever is down and the sheet is under and whatever is going to happen has already happened and simply hasn't reached your eyes yet.

Then the lift. Then the sheet, peeling away from the type.

Mirror-wise, reversed, the world set right: households, codes, columns, names. The Ashgrove shop. The tailors. All of it, sharp-edged, faintly over-inked because of course the first proof of anything is over-inked, and I didn't care, I will never care, because it was legible. It was *legible*, which is the entire religion of my trade, and underneath the legend of codes I had added a line in ten-point, small enough to fit, large enough to read:

*These are your neighbors. The city counted them. Count them too.*

Dario came and stood at my shoulder. For a long moment none of us spoke. The lamp hissed. Water dripped somewhere in the dark beyond the press, patient as a metronome.

"That's my cousin's street," Dario said finally. "Third column. The Marinos." He tapped the sheet, twice, softly, the way you'd knock on a door you weren't sure you wanted answered. "They told everyone the Marinos moved to Halden. There was a story. Everyone repeated it." He looked at his finger, at the fleck of ink it had picked up from the wet proof. "There wasn't a story. There was a list."

"There was a list," I said.

He nodded slowly, and then he did something I had never seen him do in all our dealings: he rolled up his sleeves without being asked, without negotiating a rate, and said, "Show me how to feed the sheets. If I'm going to hang for this, I want it done neatly."

* * *

We worked until the candles failed and were replaced, until the second batch of candles failed, until time stopped meaning anything except impressions. Anselm fed. Dario stacked and turned the drying sheets, handling them like the contraband they'd been born as. And I pulled, and pulled, and pulled, and somewhere around the third hundred I stopped counting proofs and started hearing the names instead of reading them.

That sounds like madness, maybe, or sentiment. It was neither. Any compositor knows it: set the same words often enough and they stop being words. You begin to feel their weight in the stick. The Widow Castellan became a rhythm—so many ems, so many units of space, a specific weight my wrist learned. The ferryman's boys, both names, both codes. My own address, over and over, until it stopped being a threat and became a fact among facts, one line of many, and there was a strange mercy in that. On the plate, I was not anomalous. On the plate, I was listed. Included. Counted. It took a government purge document to give me the ordinary dignity of appearing on a roster with everybody else, and I laughed out loud the first time I noticed that, alone at the lever in the small hours, and Anselm looked over with real concern and I waved him off. Grief and exaltation, in the same breath, in the same body. The breath manages somehow. The breath is more accommodating than we are.

Near dawn, my hands gave out—not from the weight of the lever but from the weight of everything else, the accumulated tremor of three weeks of fear finally discharged into honest labor. I sat down on the crate the plate had come in and let Anselm pull a few dozen while I watched the stack grow.

It grew the way stacks do, which is to say slowly, then suddenly, then unstoppably. Five hundred sheets. Eight hundred. Rag paper, inferior, unsuitable for official use, better than anything the state had ever printed, because it had us behind it.

"Enough for every market stall, every church door, every tavern table," Anselm said, setting the lever home. "Dario's people can move a thousand sheets through the canals in a night."

"Dario's people haven't agreed to anything yet," Dario said, from the drying rack. "Dario's people will require an explanation." He considered. "Though I find that showing them the third column saves considerable negotiation."

I picked up a single proof—the very first pull, over-inked, slightly crooked, worthless as an artifact and priceless to no one but me—and I folded it and put it inside my coat, against my chest, where Father's will had ridden across the customs house checkpoint two nights ago in my own hands, under my own name, spoken aloud to a guard who read it and hesitated and let me pass. Both documents, then, carried against my heart. The one that affirmed me. The one that would have erased me. I intended to keep both forever, as proof that the difference between them was made of nothing but choices.

"We should rest before distribution," Anselm said. "Even the faithful sleep."

"The faithful," said Dario, "have never met my schedule."

But it was as he said it that we heard it: boots on the stairs above, more than two sets, moving fast, and the muffled voice of the chandler's widow overhead—shouting, not the frightened shout of someone robbed but the deliberate, carrying shout of someone performing an alarm for witnesses—and beneath it, unmistakable, the smell beginning to thread down through the floorboards.

Smoke.

Dario was already at the foot of the stairs, sniffing like a man appraising goods. "That's the shop district," he said quietly. "That's Tanner's Row. That's *our* row."

I climbed far enough up the stairwell to see through the coal hatch: orange light on the opposite facades, sparks climbing past our own door, and across the lane, silhouetted against the fire, soldiers feeding papers—cartons of papers, armload after armload—into the flames of what remained of the Ashgrove print shop.

They were burning the records. Burning the originals, the duplicates, every copy of every ledger that could contradict the lists we had just printed five hundred times over.

They were late by one night, and somewhere behind me, drying on racks in the dark, the truth was already stacked and ready to run.

"Get the sheets to the boats," I said. "Now."
