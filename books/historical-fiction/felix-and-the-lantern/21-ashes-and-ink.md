## 21. Ashes and Ink

*Quentin*

I have always believed that a building is only a kind of book—rooms for chapters, stairs for sentences, a door that opens the way a cover opens. So when I watched the Ashgrove print shop burn, I did not watch a house die. I watched a volume being returned to its elements, the paper and the timber and the forty years of my father's labor going up in a language of smoke, and I found—I am still finding this difficult to confess—that I felt very little grief.

What I felt was arithmetic.

The soldiers had come at dusk, six of them with a brazier and an official-looking document, and they had gone about it with the bored efficiency of men setting type they hadn't chosen. They stacked the furniture in the lane. They broke the windows so the fire could breathe. One of them, a boy no older than Felix would have been, stood a long moment in the doorway of the press room before he came out, and I watched from the attic window of the cooper's house across the lane—the same window from which I had watched them empty the tailor's shop—and I understood that he had read the sheets. That he had run his thumb down the columns of names and found one that belonged to someone he ate supper with. That was why he lingered. That was why, when he threw his torch, he threw it gently, almost apologetically, the way you'd set down something borrowed.

The fire took the shop the way ink takes cheap paper: fast, blooming, indifferent. It lit the lane orange. It made shadows of everyone who came out to look, and half the lane came out to look, which was precisely the point, though the soldiers didn't know it yet. You cannot burn a building in front of a city without the city asking itself what else can burn.

I stood in the dark with the cooper's family and their neighbors, and I kept my face composed into the proper grief, because a compositor knows that spacing is everything—that a lie reads true if you kern it carefully enough. Inside, beneath the floorboards where the plate had lain for eleven days, there was nothing now but ash and the smell of scorched lead. The plate was across the river. The plate was being inked at that very moment by hands I trusted, pulling proof after proof after proof, and every proof was already moving through Vellamont like water through a cracked cistern—under doors, into market baskets, folded inside hymnals, tucked into the satchels of children who had been paid a copper apiece and who had, I suspect, read every word before delivering a single one.

My father built that shop with his hands. He set its first page when I was too small to reach the composing stick, and he named me for a dead boy because the law left him no other door, and he died in the room above it with his will folded under the ledgers like a held breath. It was right, I decided, watching the roof beams go, that the building should pay our debt. Buildings are only paper with pretensions. We had already taken everything out of it that mattered: the sorts, the will, the name, the truth.

The heat reached us even across the lane, a breath against my cheek like the city exhaling.

"Shame," said the cooper beside me, quietly, not looking at me. He had a sheet of printed paper in his apron pocket. I had seen him fold it there that morning.

"Terrible," I agreed.

Neither of us blinked at the other. In Vellamont, that night, that was a sacrament.

* * *

*Ilse*

They put me in a room with a table, a lamp, and a chair, which is how the state imagines guilt is produced: furniture first, confession second. My supervisor, Magistrate Corven, sat opposite me with my own copied pages spread between us, smoothed flat, arranged with the fussy precision of a man who has never once had to carry anything heavier than a pen.

"You understand what this is," he said.

"It's a copy of the census rolls," I said. "Which is my job."

"It's a copy of the *altered* census rolls." He tapped the page where the Ferro household had been, where four names had become none, where a family of four had been subtracted from existence between my second visit and my third. "You copied evidence of tampering. Do you know what happens to clerks who collect evidence?"

"I assumed a promotion," I said, "given the general direction of this government."

He did not laugh. He had never once laughed, Corven, and I want it recorded that in nine years of service I never once made him, and that this seemed to me, even then, the truest measure of the man.

He left me with the lamp and a guard at the door, and I sat in the chair and did what I have done every night of my working life: I reviewed my files. Except my files were now a locked room and my own fear, and the inventory went like this: Quentin Ashgrove, arrested in absentia, location unknown. Father Anselm, defrocked, holding a cathedral gate against six soldiers with nothing but his body and his office, both of which the state had already taken from him once. A smuggler whose name I refused to give because giving it would have been the only thing I could have done for Corven, and I had stopped doing things for Corven somewhere around the moment I saw him erase a mother with two strokes of his pen.

And the lists. Always the lists.

I heard the guard shift outside the door. Then I heard him do something no guard in that building had ever done in my presence: I heard him unfold paper. The dry whisper of it. The small clearing of a throat. And then silence—not the silence of a man standing post, but the silence of a man reading.

I knew what he was reading. Of course I knew. By evening the proofs had reached everywhere; they had reached even here, passed hand to hand through the barracks like contraband bread, because soldiers have families too, and the census had counted them too, and the purge orders being typeset did not distinguish between the households of the condemned and the households of the men sent to condemn them. Corven's alterations had been efficient. They had also been careless. Carelessness, given enough copies, becomes evidence.

The whispering stopped. A long minute passed. I heard the guard stand, sit, stand again. When the key finally turned in the lock, it turned wrong—too quick, too shallow, the rattle of a man whose hands were not steady—and the door opened on a young face I had seen a hundred times in the corridor, gray uniform, gray expression, and eyes that were not gray at all just now but wide and wet and burning.

"My sister," he said, as though I had asked, though I had said nothing. "She's on the third list. She's fourteen."

"I know," I said. "I copied her entry."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he stepped aside from the doorway, and gestured down the corridor with the strange, stiff courtesy of a man surrendering his entire career in a single motion, and said, "The east stair. The yard gate is unwatched until the second bell."

I walked past him. At the corner I stopped, because it seemed suddenly important, and said, "What's your name?"

"Petar," he said. "Why?"

"Because when this is over," I said, "someone should write it down."

Then I ran—down the east stair, across the yard, through the gate that swung shut behind me like the last page of a bad book—and out into streets that were glowing orange at the far end and rustling, everywhere, faintly, endlessly, with the sound of paper changing hands.

* * *

*Dario*

In thirty years of moving cargo on the canals of this city, I have developed certain professional principles, chief among them: never deliver anything you haven't priced, never carry anything you can't deny, and never—never—work for free. I mention these principles so that history understands exactly how far I fell, and how fast.

By midnight I had delivered four hundred sheets. I had not charged for any of them.

This is what happened to me. I rowed the Rosalia down the Lantern Cut with the cargo hold full of printed truth and my heart full of something I did not recognize and did not care for, and at every landing there were hands waiting—bakers' hands, washerwomen's hands, one pair belonging to a customs officer who took his copy, read it by the light of the burning print shop across the water, folded it very carefully, and saluted me. Saluted. Me. A man whose boat has been boarded at that same dock eleven times.

"The fire," he said, nodding at the glow. "That was the print shop. The Ashgrove place."

"That's what it looks like," I agreed, which was true.

"They say the compositor died in it."

"Do they," I said.

"Pity," he said, and handed me a twist of tobacco, which I accepted, because a man must keep his hand in.

The compositor, you understand, was at that moment three streets away in the cooper's attic, alive as sin and twice as stubborn, and I was the only person on that dock who knew it, and I held that knowledge the way I had once held stolen brandy—carefully, warmly, with a constant low terror of dropping it. Somewhere between the bridge checkpoint and the cathedral gate, between Quentin's white knuckles on my gunwale and Anselm planting himself in that doorway like the last honest sentence in a corrupt ledger, I had acquired stakes. Stakes are fatal to a man in my trade. Every smuggler knows this. And yet there I was, rowing home at two in the morning with an empty hold, an unpaid debt, and the unmistakable sensation of having joined a family, which is the most expensive cargo there is.

I passed the last sheets to a boy on the Cut who couldn't have been twelve. He read faster than any customs man I've ever bribed.

"What is it?" he asked, though he'd clearly read it.

"People," I said. "It's people."

"There's a woman on here," he said. "Third column. She does my mother's washing."

"Take it to your mother, then," I said, "and tell her to pass it on."

He ran. The whole city, that night, was boys running, and old women folding, and strangers pressing papers into each other's palms like communion, and above it all the print shop burned beautifully against the smoke, and I thought: they think they're destroying the evidence. They've got it exactly backwards. The building was the alibi. This—this rustling, this whispering, this whole city turning over in its sleep—is the document.

* * *

*Anselm*

They came to the cathedral at dawn to arrest me, all seven of them, and I received them in the wrecked antechamber with my bruised ribs wrapped in a stole I no longer had the right to wear, wearing it anyway.

"Father Anselm," said the lieutenant, reading from his orders.

"Not for some years," I said. "But proceed."

"You sheltered an unregistered fugitive. You obstructed three checkpoints. You permitted the printing of seditious material."

"All true," I said. "You've missed one. I demanded it be printed with my name on it. Check your copies. You'll find me in the colophon, taking credit, which is more than I can say for your magistrate, whose forgeries required anonymity to function. There is a lesson in that comparison, Lieutenant, if your education included reading."

His jaw worked. His sergeant, behind him, was holding a folded sheet with conspicuous looseness, as though it might go off.

"We're to take you to the magistrate," the lieutenant said at last.

"He isn't there," I said. "Your magistrate fled at the second bell. You didn't know? Well. That is what happens, son, when you serve men who burn the news rather than read it." I rose, slowly, because the ribs objected, and I let them object audibly, because a little theater costs nothing. "Take me wherever you like. But understand that you are late. Everything I protected is already out of my hands, which means, for the first time in my life, everything I protected is safe."

As they bound my wrists—a formality, almost gentle, the sergeant avoiding my eyes—I heard, distantly, through the broken doors, the bells of Vellamont ringing unsanctioned, unrung by any sexton, pulled by whoever felt like pulling them, which is the sound a city makes when it remembers it has a voice.

I had spent eleven years doing penance badly, alone, for the families I failed to hide. I had imagined redemption as something I would earn in private, expiating in installments. Instead it had arrived as a frightened compositor at my gate, and a lying smuggler, and a census clerk with stolen pages, and a burned building, and my name—my actual name—in print, attached at last to something worth the ink.

God, I have decided, has an excellent sense of composition. Terrible timing. Excellent composition.

* * *

*Quentin*

At noon, from the cooper's attic, I watched the smoke over the lane thin into ordinary weather, and Ilse climbed the ladder and came in smelling of the canal, soaked to the knees, her hair down, her official bearing left somewhere at the bottom of the east stair. Dario followed with bread and the last bottle of anything. Anselm's absence sat at the table with us like an extra chair; we had his message, passed along by a choirboy: *Taken, unharmed, insolent to the last. Print anyway.*

So we printed anyway. There was nothing else in the world we knew how to do.

Ilse laid her copied pages on the table and smoothed them flat, and I looked at them—at the erased households, the forged totals, Corven's careful little subtractions—and felt my father's hand settle on my shoulder the way it used to when I set my first line crooked. *Measure twice,* he'd say. *Ink is forever.* Well. Their ink was forever too. Their lies were typeset and locked in the state's own plates, and we had proofs of both the lies and the truth, and a city full of hands.

"They'll rebuild," Ilse said quietly, nodding toward the window, toward the black skeleton across the lane. "The shop."

"No," I said, and surprised myself with how easily it came. "We will. Somewhere. Not the same shop. Better light." I touched the satchel at my feet, where my father's will rode against the spare sorts—*to my child, nameless in law but not in me*—the one document in Vellamont that had never lied about me. "Buildings are just paper with ambitions. Let them keep the ashes."

Dario raised the bottle. "To the Ashgrove print shop," he said. "Dead of telling the truth."

"To Petar," said Ilse.

"To the colophon," I said, and we drank, four names short of a family and fuller than any ledger ever made us.

Below us, in the street, a child ran past with an armful of printed sheets, shouting nothing, saying nothing, simply holding the pages up so that anyone could take one, and the hands came out of doorways to meet them like flowers turning.

And then the church bells began again—all of them, every tower, ragged and joyous and completely unscheduled—and Ilse went very still, listening, and then she smiled in a way I had never seen on a census taker's face, and said:

"That's not a fire bell. Quentin—that's the free quarter ringing. They've read the lists. The garrison has *read the lists.*"

We looked at one another across the table, across the bread and the copied pages and the ash-flecked light coming through the cooper's window, and I understood that whatever came next—whatever new city we would have to build out of this one—it would need a press in it, and a lantern over the door, and a name on the first page. Possibly two names.
