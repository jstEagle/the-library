## 22. Felix and the Lantern

The lantern came from a junk barge in Harrow Quay, and it cost me three coppers and an argument.

The bargeman wanted five. I told him it was dented, which it was—the tin had been kicked in on one side so that it sat crooked no matter how you hung it, like a man leaning into a wind that wasn't there. He told me that was character. I told him character was worth two coppers at most in this city, and he laughed, and we settled at three, and I carried it home through streets that smelled of salt and tar and nobody's census rolls.

That was the trick of the free city, I was learning. Nothing here smelled of ledgers.

I hung the lantern above the door of what had been, until six weeks ago, a chandler's shop—narrow-fronted, deep as a coffin, with a cellar perfect for a press and a back stair for leaving by. The hook I'd driven into the lintel held. The lantern swung. The dent made it list to starboard, casting its light slightly off-true, so that anyone standing beneath it stood half in gold and half in shadow.

I decided that was fitting. Most of us lived our whole lives in that split of light.

"You'll want to oil the hinge," Dario said from the doorway behind me, "or you'll be listening to that thing scream all winter." He had a crate on one shoulder—the good crate, the one with the rollers wrapped in wool—and he set it down inside without being asked, because Dario never asked; he delivered, and then he named his price, and then he stayed for dinner anyway.

"It gives the door character," I said.

"Everything in this city gives itself character because it's broken," he said. "That's why I love it."

* * *

My father built his first press bench before I was born—or before I understood I was born, which in our house amounted to the same era. He built it low, so children could reach the type cases, though only one child ever did, and even that was a fiction we maintained together, the two of us conspiring in ink: Felix at the counter, Quentin at the case, one name answering the door and the other setting the letters, until neither of us could always say who was speaking.

In Vellamont, that ambiguity was armor. Here in Corvenna—in the free city, where the harbor taxes are posted openly and the magistrates publish their own accounts and get shouted at in pamphlets for it—the ambiguity is something else. Not armor. Inheritance.

I spent the morning arranging the shop. The press went against the north wall where the light is steadiest. The cases went where my father's had always gone, capital sort within arm's reach of the composing stone, because some habits are not habits but spine. And beside the press, on the wall where the chandler once hung his ledger of tallow and wick, I put up a frame.

It is not an expensive frame. It is pine, stained dark, with glass I paid too much for because I refused to have it glazed with anything warped. Inside the frame is a single sheet of old paper, foxed at the edges, written in a lawyer's careful hand:

*I, Aldous Ashgrove, printer of Vellamont, being of sound mind, leave all that I am and all that I hold to my child.*

No name. No *Felix*, no *Quentin*. My father, who measured words for a living, who could justify a line of type to within a hair of perfection, chose in the last document of his life to leave the most important line unjustified—open, ragged, roomy enough for whoever stepped forward to claim it.

For years I thought that vagueness was a mercy or perhaps a cowardice. Now I think it was the finest piece of composition he ever set. He left me a blank space in the world and trusted me to fill it without overrunning the measure.

Ilse came by while I was hanging it. She does that now—arrives without knocking, the way family does, still startled sometimes by her own freedom of movement. She stood looking at the will for a long moment.

"In Vellamont they would have called this document invalid," she said. "Insufficient identification of beneficiary."

"In Vellamont they burned the shop it was written in."

"Yes." She touched the glass lightly, the way you touch a gravestone or a cheek. "Here it's just a will."

"Here it's just a will," I agreed, "and a broadside is just a broadside, and a census is just a count of people rather than a list of targets." I looked at her. She had circles under her eyes that hadn't fully left since the prison, and probably wouldn't; some things the body keeps like ink keeps in the grain of wood. "Are you going to finish the education column? You promised me the education column."

"I'm getting to it. A woman needs breakfast before she educates anyone."

She wrote for me now, three mornings a week—plain accounts, school primers, and once a month a quiet little column on civic arithmetic: how many households the old regime had vanished from its rolls, computed in figures a child could follow. Ilse had discovered that numbers, honestly kept, were more dangerous than any pistol, and she wielded them with the grim delight of a woman settling accounts. Her supervisor was in prison awaiting trial. His ledgers were exhibits. She had testified for four hours and come out pale and upright and had gone straight from the courthouse to my typesetting stone and asked, *what do you need copied first.*

You do not forget a person like that. You build a print shop around them.

* * *

By afternoon the shop had filled the way it does now, the way it seemed to fill everywhere I went since the night we planned a rescue over bread and lamp oil. Anselm arrived first after Ilse, carrying a box of candles "liberated," he said, "from the cathedral of Saint Ferren, whose bishop owes me a sermon." He has a post again here—a small parish by the tanners' row, nothing grand—but he still says *liberated* about everything, still wears his guilt the way other men wear medals, and still insists on blessing the press before every big run.

"The plate is clean," I told him. "It doesn't need your incense."

"The plate is metal," he said, shaking the thurible anyway. "Metal remembers what hands have done. Ask it to carry truth and it will. But bless it first, because asking is not the same as thanking."

He blessed it. He thanked it. Then he rolled up his cassock sleeves and hauled crates with the rest of us, because Anselm's theology has always been simple: God helps those who lift.

Dario came at dusk with the last of the paper—good stock, bought through channels he declined to specify, "because specifying," he said, "is how men end up paying customs duties twice, once with money and once with their necks." He looked around the shop, at the press bolted down, at the cases full of type I had cast myself, at the framed will catching the lamplight, and he did something he almost never did.

He didn't name a price.

"Well?" I said.

"Well what?"

"Aren't you going to tell me what I owe you?"

He considered the question seriously, the way he considers everything, chewing on it like a man checking a coin for clipping. "You owe me a wedding invitation," he said finally.

"A—"

"That run you used for cover. The forged invitations. Ashgrove & Ashgrove, joyfully announcing." He grinned, and the grin had lost none of its wickedness in transit across the border, but it had gained something underneath, some ballast it hadn't carried before. "Nobody ever sent me mine. I did a man's work on that bridge, dodging the guard's eye, and I never got so much as a card. Print me one. Frame it. I'll hang it on my boat."

"Dario. It was forgery. It's evidence."

"It's evidence of the best day of my life," he said. "Print it anyway. Put my name on it. Dario Fenn, guest of honor."

And because it was Dario, and because refusing him has never once worked in the history of the world, I set the invitation that evening while the others argued cheerfully about where the stove should go. I printed a single copy on the best stock in the shop, and when it came off the press warm and damp, I held it a moment before handing it over, because presses make small ceremonies of everything they touch.

He took it in both hands, actually read it, and said, quietly, without any wink in it at all: "Funny. First thing anybody's ever put my name on that wasn't a debt."

Then the wink returned, and he pocketed it, and went to help Anselm move the stove twice, because Anselm was wrong the first time and wronger the second.

* * *

We pulled the first proof of the new shop near midnight.

It was nothing grand—a prospectus, a promise of services, the printer's traditional opening word to a city. *Q. Ashgrove, Printer, Corvenna. Books, broadsides, forms, and honest work.* Below it, the line I had composed and recomposed eleven times, testing it against my eye the way my father taught me, reading it upside down, reading it backward in the mirror of the polished stone, until the letters stopped being letters and became simply true:

*Proprietor: Q. Ashgrove, called Felix.*

Two names. One line. Justified flush left and right, no overruns, no gaps. I had set my own name in metal, both of my names, and locked them into a forme, and there was no census taker in any city who could unpick the quoins.

Anselm blessed it. Ilse read it aloud and pronounced the spacing sound, which from her is a knighthood. Dario asked whether guests of honor got free printing for life, and was told no, and appealed, and was denied, and helped himself to wine.

I pulled the lever. The bed came up to meet the platen, the press breathed its familiar sigh, and the sheet came away black and certain.

There is a moment—I have known it since I was small enough to need a stool at the case—when the proof comes off and you see your work outside yourself for the first time, fixed, finished, no longer yours alone. Most compositors feel pride there. Some feel fear. That night, standing in my own shop in a free city with my found family drinking my bad wine around me, I felt what I can only call continuity. My father's hands on the same lever motion. My brother's name on the same page as mine. The dead and the invented and the living, all pressed flat into one sheet of paper, all legible.

I hung the first copy by the door, under the crooked lantern, where the light falls half gold and half shadow.

"For luck," Anselm said.

"For evidence," Ilse said, at the same moment.

They looked at each other. "Both," I said. "In this trade it's usually both."

* * *

Near dawn, when the others had gone—Ilse to her rooms, Anselm to his parish, Dario out the back stair toward the harbor with the unhurried gait of a man who owns every alley between here and anywhere—I stayed alone in the shop and did the last thing.

I wrote a letter. Not to mail. To set.

*To the reader in Vellamont, whoever you are, whichever name you are forced to wear:*

*We know your names. We kept them. They are printed below, all nine hundred and forty-one, exactly as the state recorded them before it tried to unrecord you. A list cannot be burned out of every memory in a city. We are that city's memory now.*

*Come north when you can. There is work here, and a press, and a lantern over the door that hangs slightly crooked, which means it works.*

*— Q. Ashgrove, called Felix*

Below the signature I set the names. All of them. It took me four days, and my eyes ached, and twice I found my hands shaking over the case—not from fear anymore, but from the sheer weight of saying each name aloud in lead, giving each vanished household its letters back, the tailor and his daughters, the widow from Tanner's Lane, the boy whose only crime was a birthdate entered wrong on purpose by a clerk who was later kind enough to die of conscience. Nine hundred and forty-one lines of type. The longest forme I have ever locked.

At the bottom of the list, in smaller type, a final line, the one my father should have lived to set himself:

*This record printed from the master plate recovered by the people it named.*

The sun came up over Corvenna harbor while I cleaned the press. Through the front window I watched the light reach the lantern hanging over my door—dented, crooked, lit anyway—and I thought about my father measuring everything by what could be carried in one satchel, and how wrong he'd had it, and how right. Because you can carry a lot in a satchel if what you're carrying is a craft, a name, a will, and nine hundred and forty-one reasons to keep the press oiled.

Someone knocked. Early for customers. I wiped my hands, left the ink-blackened rag on the stone where it belonged, and opened the door on a young woman I didn't know, travel-stained, with the look of someone who had walked a long way past a great many doors before choosing this one. Behind her, half-hidden in the doorway's shadow, waited two more figures, thin and watchful, wearing names I couldn't yet guess.

"We saw the broadside," she said. "On the boat coming over. Somebody was passing copies hand to hand." She swallowed. "Our names are on your list."

"Then you're expected," I said, and held the door wide, and beneath the crooked lantern the light caught the first page of tomorrow, still warm.
