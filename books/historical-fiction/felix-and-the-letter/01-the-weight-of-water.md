## 1. The Weight of Water

The sea off Point Sallow was the color of a struck coin that year, and my father loved it the way some men love a woman who will certainly kill them.

I was nine the first time he let me watch from the boat. He sat on the gunwale with his feet bare, tying his weight belt, and he talked to me the whole time—not to comfort me, I understand now, but because talking was how he breathed before breathing stopped being possible. He told me about the pearl beds, how they lay in forty feet of green-dark water like a field nobody had ever plowed right. He told me about the light down there, how it came down in ropes instead of sheets. He told me that when you dove deep enough, your own heartbeat became the loudest thing in the world, and you had to make peace with it or panic.

"What's it like," I asked him, "when you're down there?"

He thought about it. My father was not a man who answered quickly; he had been born on the other side of the ocean, in a place whose name he gave only in fragments—a province, a river, an aunt with a clay oven—and he treated most questions like small fish he meant to release unharmed.

"It's quiet," he said finally. "Quiet like a church where everybody agrees."

Then he spat over the side for luck, put the bone mouthpiece between his teeth, and went over backward into the water with barely a splash, the way big men can when they've made peace with gravity. I watched his shadow get smaller and smaller until it wasn't a man anymore, just a darkness among darker rocks, and I held the rope and counted, because counting was what you did. One minute was nothing. Two minutes was fine. Three minutes and your hands started to sweat.

At three minutes and forty seconds he came up gasping and laughing, holding aloft an oyster the size of a fist, and the whole boat cheered, and I thought: this is what my father does. This is all he does. He goes where the air isn't, and he comes back.

I was seventeen when the coming back failed.

They never found him, which is its own particular cruelty, because the sea around Port Meridian gives things back if it feels like it—boot soles, ship's figureheads, once an entire church bell—and it kept him. For two years afterward I dreamed of the pearl beds the way other people dream of falling: the green dark, the rope slack in my hands, the count going past four minutes, past five, past any number that means anything, and waking with my lungs burning as if I'd been the one underwater.

Now I am twenty-three, and I do not dream of it much anymore. Not because grief leaves—it doesn't, it just moves out of the bedroom and into the walls—but because I have become very good at something else. Something my father never learned, being a man who talked his way through every door he ever met.

I have become good at being no one.

* * *

The Meridian Trading Company's counting house smelled of ink, lamp oil, and the particular sourness of men who eat their lunches at their desks. It was a long room on the second floor of the company headquarters on Harrow Street, forty desks in eight rows, and at desk six of row five—close enough to the window to get the light, far enough from the overseer's platform to be beneath sustained attention—I had spent six years tallying.

Tally clerk is not a job anyone dreams of. There were girls in the dormitories who had come to Port Meridian dreaming of marrying harbor pilots or opening dress shops, and there were boys who dreamed of ships, and none of them, not one, had ever dreamed of tally clerk. You got the job by needing it. You kept it by disappearing into it.

That morning—this was the third Tuesday of October, though I didn't yet know what that Tuesday would bring—I arrived at seven o'clock sharp with the other clerks, filed through the door in the gray parade we made of ourselves, and took my seat. My hands knew the work without me. That is the secret mercy of routine: done long enough, it requires nothing of you but presence. The ledgers came in from the warehouse in canvas satchels; I copied weights of copra, cases of copal gum, bales of rubber, casks of pearl shell; I entered figures in a hand so regular the chief clerk had once held a page of mine up as an example, saying, *This is what entries should look like*, and I had felt not pride but a small cold satisfaction—the satisfaction of a stone that has been praised for being still.

"Oscar."

It was Mr. Prewitt, the under-overseer, a soft-bodied man with a mustache like a dead moth. "Sterling. You're wanted to carry these down to Receiving." He dropped two ledgers on my desk, heavy as bricks.

"The runners usually carry the ledgers," I said. It came out before I could stop it, which was rare enough that Prewitt blinked.

"The runners are carrying the mail today. Go on."

So I carried them. This was the trouble with being good: goodness got noticed exactly enough to be used. I went down the back stairs with a ledger under each arm, past the map room where the company's whole watery empire hung in framed charts, past the registry room with its locked door and its brass plate—*REGISTRY OF PERSONS AND BONDS*—which I passed every day of my working life without a single thought, the way you pass a wall.

Receiving occupied the ground floor, a cavern of crates and hemp and the shouting of men who needed things moved. I signed the ledgers over to the receiving master, a half-deaf old islander named Tuilagi who terrified new clerks by making them repeat everything twice.

"Sterling girl," he said, squinting at the signature. "You're Sterling's daughter."

Everyone said it that way, as if my father were the more definite person and I the derivative. "Yes, Mr. Tuilagi."

"He was a good man. Dived the Sallow beds, didn't he. Best diver on the point, some said." Tuilagi leaned close, lowering his voice as if the crates might repeat what he said next. "Some say the beds are fished out now. Some say they're not. Some say there's shell down there big as dinner plates, and no one diving them because—" he tapped the side of his nose.

"Because the best diver on the point drowned in them," I said. "Yes. That's generally considered bad luck."

Tuilagi laughed, delighted, and waved me off. But walking back up with my empty arms, I felt the old ache settle in, dull and familiar as a healed break that aches before rain. Six years, and people still handed him to me in passing, casually, the way you'd hand someone a package. *Here. Your father. Hold this forever.*

* * *

Here is what invisibility actually looks like, since everyone believes it means doing nothing. It means doing everything, quietly, correctly, and first.

Busy hands, lowered eyes—that was my catechism, learned young. When the overseers walked the rows, I bent over my ledger. When the clerks gossiped at lunch about who had been dismissed from the shell-grading shed, I listened and asked nothing. When the matron at the dormitory read out the weekly notices—curfew hours, chapel attendance, the wages docked for broken crockery—I stood with the others, eyes down, and let the words wash over me like weather. In six years I had been written up twice: once for a smudged entry (unfair), once for attending chapel in the wrong shawl (fair, but petty). Twice in six years. Most girls managed twice a month.

"You're a ghost, Oscar Sterling," my friend Nell used to tell me, back when Nell still worked the sorting tables before she married a cooper and moved to the north shore. She meant it kindly. Being a ghost, she thought, was safety, and she was nearly right. It was nearly right the way a shallow grave is nearly deep.

But here is what I knew that Nell did not: ghosts are safe until someone comes looking for them. And when someone comes looking for a ghost, they find one, because a ghost is exactly what she appears to be. Visibility was danger, yes. But perfect invisibility had a cost too, a kind of interest paid slowly. Every year I grew a little more practiced at being unseen, and a little less sure there remained anyone visible underneath to save.

I thought about this more than was healthy, sitting at desk six of row five, copying the weight of the world's cargo into books. Perhaps that is what happens to fatherless girls raised by institutions—we mistake our survival strategies for personalities.

At noon I ate bread and cheese on the window seat at the end of row five, the one spot in the counting house where a person could look out over the harbor without being observed from the overseer's platform. Below, Port Meridian lay in its usual October glitter: masts like a stripped forest, the great chain-tower at the harbor mouth, lighters and pearling luggers jostling at the quay. Somewhere out beyond the chain, I knew, ships rode at anchor that hadn't been allowed into port in weeks—there was talk of war somewhere, there was always talk of war somewhere, and the papers printed edicts, and the edicts mostly meant that things got slower and more expensive and the company charged more for the delay.

And there—my eye found it without permission, the way it always did—a brig with tan sails furled and a hull the color of wet slate, riding at anchor inside the blockade line, flying some flag I couldn't resolve at this distance. I had noticed her weeks ago. I noticed her every day. I had no idea why my eye returned to her, except perhaps that she looked stubborn, sitting there where slower vessels had already given up and warped away. Something in her angle at anchor suggested a horse turned to face a lion.

"Eat your crusts, Sterling." Prewitt, passing behind me. "Window seat's for messengers."

"Yes, Mr. Prewitt." Busy hands, lowered eyes. I ate my crusts.

* * *

The dormitory stood on Cabbage Lane, four stories of salt-stained brick housing sixty indentured women of the company, and I had lived in it since I was sixteen, when the orphanage released me directly into Meridian's keeping the way you transfer a parcel. My contract ran four more years. After that—well. After that was one of those territories I did not survey, along with *what you would do if you were free* and *who you would be*. Unsurveyed territory keeps a person humble.

That evening the common room smelled of boiled mutton and lamp black. Girls crowded around the one newspaper, and someone read aloud the items: a customs seizure at the east wharf, a speech by the governor—Crane, his name was, Governor Crane, though I confess at that time I knew him only as a name attached to proclamations and a portrait in the company boardroom, a handsome stern face like a ship's figurehead—edicts about foreign vessels and registration of persons. Registration of persons. Someone giggled nervously. Somebody else said her mother had been registered twice in the old country and it hadn't killed her.

I sat by the stove darning a stocking, hands busy, eyes lowered, thinking about nothing in particular, which is to say thinking about everything in the way you think about a stone in your shoe you've decided to walk on.

"Oz." Tilda Hearn, from the next stool, a wiry shell-grader with a laugh like a cart on gravel. "You listening? They're saying the census men come round tomorrow. To the dormitories and all. Counting everybody proper—where born, parents born, the lot."

"Why?" asked a girl named Ada, nineteen and easily alarmed.

"War," said Tilda darkly, with relish. "Spies everywhere, aren't there. Probably sleeping in our very chimney."

"They'll not find many spies in Cabbage Lane," I said, and Tilda laughed her gravel laugh, and the conversation rolled on to whether the census man would weigh you as well as count you, and whether you could lie about your age to a government official (you could lie about anything, Tilda maintained, if you committed to it).

I finished my stocking. I banked the stove. I went up to the room I shared with three others, and I lay in my cot listening to the harbor bells through the window, and I thought—as I had not thought in months, in years, with the whole day's ordinary weight pressing it down—about names.

My father had a name from the other side of the ocean. A real one, from a real place, with a river in it and an aunt with a clay oven. On all my documents—in the orphanage rolls, in the company's bonds, in the ledger where I myself recorded the comings and goings of cargo—my place of birth read *Port Meridian*, which was true, and my parentage read *O. Sterling, mariner*, which was true, and none of it mentioned rivers or provinces or aunts, because my father, in the last year of his life, had grown very quiet about where he came from. I had assumed, being then fifteen and knowing nothing, that this was simply the way of fathers.

Now, lying in the dark with the census coming like weather, I felt the first faint pressure behind the thought, the way you feel rain before it arrives. *Where was your father born?*

I had never been asked. Not once in twenty-three years had anyone official cared. And I discovered, in the dark of that narrow bed, that I did not know what I would say. Not because I didn't know the answer.

Because I didn't know the answer I could safely give.

* * *

Morning came gray, with a wind out of the northeast that sent the harbor chop whitecapping against the seawall. I walked to Harrow Street with the other clerks, our gray parade, and the city felt different—tighter somehow, brighter-eyed. Soldiers at the chain-tower. A company launch with a mounted gun I didn't recognize, chugging importantly between the quays. At the counting house door, Prewitt was posting a notice, and the clerks gathered to read it with the solemnity of people reading their own horoscopes:

*BY ORDER OF THE GOVERNOR'S OFFICE: A GENERAL ENUMERATION OF ALL PERSONS RESIDENT WITHIN THE PORT DISTRICT SHALL BE UNDERTAKEN...*

and so on, particulars, schedules, penalties for giving false information—*penalties* got its own line, bolded, which seemed excessive—and at the bottom the seal of the crown and the countersignature of the Meridian Trading Company, because in Port Meridian the company's word sat just under God's and slightly above the governor's, depending whom you asked.

"A census," said Tilda, at my shoulder. "Proper counting at last. Wonder if they'll find me interesting. Probably not. Nobody's ever found me interesting in my life."

"They found you interesting enough to move your workstation twice for talking," I said.

"That was discipline, Oz, not interest."

We took our seats. The ledgers came in their satchels. I opened mine, dipped my pen, and began the day's entries, busy hands, lowered eyes, invisible as Sunday—and it was precisely then, at nine minutes past eight in the morning of the third Tuesday of October, with my pen moving and my mind safely stowed, that the door of the counting house opened and a voice announced:

"Mr. Aldous Finch, census enumerator for District Four, by authority of the Governor's Office. I'll want each of you individually, please. In order of seniority."

And I felt it before I understood it—a sensation like the moment on the boat when the rope goes slack in your hands, and the count has run long, and the water above you has gone from green to gold to something with no color in it at all.

Mr. Prewitt began alphabetizing us with the enthusiasm of a man given a small power he'd always wanted. I sat at desk six of row five, copying figures with steady hands, and watched the door, and waited my turn, and told myself the thing I had told myself for six years: *Be no one. Be the ledger. Be the gray.*

Somewhere below us, out past the chain, that stubborn slate-colored brig rode at anchor, facing its own lion.

My name was called at twenty minutes to eleven.

I picked up my pen, set it down again, and went in to be counted.
