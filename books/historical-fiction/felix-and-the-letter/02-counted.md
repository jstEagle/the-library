## 2. Counted

The morning the census taker came to Meridian House, Oscar Sterling knew three things before she even saw him: that the porridge was burnt again, that Prue Hackett had been crying in the washroom again, and that something in the air had the particular tightness of a day about to go wrong.

The first two things were ordinary. Burnt porridge was as reliable as tide; Prue cried most Mondays, over letters from home or the lack of them. But the third thing—the tightness—was a sense Oscar had inherited along with her father's dark eyes and her mother's stubborn chin, and in eleven years of tally-clerking it had rarely lied to her.

She'd learned to read days the way other people read weather. A day could come in soft and gray and give nothing away, or it could come in with its collar up and its hands in its pockets, looking for trouble. This one was the second kind.

"Finch," said Mrs. Pettibone, the dormitory matron, appearing in the doorway of the long eating hall with a clipboard clutched to her chest like a breastplate. "Census man from the Crown Office. He'll be taking names in the common room, first shift girls first, no dawdling. Wash your hands. Some of you look like you've been counting fish with your fingers."

A low groan went down the tables. Censuses meant standing in lines, and lines meant Mrs. Pettibone's rules about posture, and Mrs. Pettibone's rules about posture meant an hour of pretending one's spine was made of ship's mast.

Oscar ate her burnt porridge without complaint and thought about nothing at all, which was a skill.

* * *

Being unremarkable was not something that happened to Oscar Sterling. It was something she did, the way a weaver does cloth, thread by thread, every day, until the fabric of her life was so uniformly gray that even God might misplace her in a crowd.

She had gotten very good at it. Busy hands, lowered eyes. She answered questions with exactly as many words as required and not one more. She laughed at jokes a half-second after everyone else, so her laughter blended into the general noise like one drop of water joining a bucket. When the supervisors walked the tally floor, their eyes slid past her the way a man's eyes slide past a fence post he's seen ten thousand times. She had been at Meridian Trading since she was nine years old, and there were clerks who had worked beside her for six years who would have to think hard to remember her last name.

This was not cowardice, whatever Prue Hackett said when they were being honest with each other after lights-out. This was arithmetic. Oscar's father had been loud once—a big laughing man with a foreign accent he never bothered to sand down, a man who whistled on the docks and argued prices out loud and called company magistrates "friend" in a tone that made it clear he thought they were anything but. Her mother, before the fever, had sung in the street in a language nobody else in Salt Harbor spoke, loud and unashamed.

Loud people got noticed. Noticed people got counted. And counted people—

Well. Oscar's father had drowned in the pearl beds off Corvo Point, and the company had docked his final wages against the cost of the dive suit he hadn't come back in. That was what being counted had bought him: a line in a ledger, red ink, *suit unrecovered*, and a daughter indentured to pay off the difference.

So Oscar kept her head down and her hands busy and her name spoken as seldom as possible, and if there was grief underneath all that grayness, she had worn it smooth as a beach stone. You can carry anything, she had found, if you make it small enough and carry it every day until your hand forgets it's heavy.

The common room smelled of floor wax and nervousness when the census taker arrived. He was not what she expected. Census takers, in Oscar's experience of rumor, were either fat and bored or thin and vicious. Mr. Aldous Finch was neither. He was a narrow, precise sort of man, perhaps forty, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles that sat slightly crooked on his face as though he'd adjusted them while thinking about something else and never corrected the error. He set up his table by the window with the fussiness of a surgeon laying out instruments: pen tray, blotter, ledger, a second smaller notebook whose purpose was not explained to anyone.

He did not look bored and he did not look vicious. He looked, oddly enough, *interested*. That was worse.

"Name," he said to Prue Hackett, who stood first in line, spine masted, eyes forward.

"Pruence Hackett, sir."

"Parents' places of birth."

"Both born here, sir. Salt Harbor, both."

He wrote. Next girl. Born here. Born here. Born here, except her mother born upcoast in Marsh Junction, which Finch received with a small nod, as though Marsh Junction were a fascinating novelty. The line moved. Oscar, third shift tallies, stood near the end with her hands folded and her heart doing a slow, deliberate impression of a stone sinking in calm water.

It was a simple question. It should have been a simple question. Both born here. Say it the way Prue said it, flat and true as a coin on a counter.

"Name," said Finch.

"Oscar Sterling." Even now, saying it aloud felt like stepping out from behind a wall.

His pen paused. "Oscar," he repeated. Not mocking—curious, the way a man is curious about a shell he hasn't seen before.

"After my grandfather, sir."

"Mm." He wrote her name in a small, neat hand, each letter given its fair share of ink. Then he looked up, and behind the crooked spectacles his eyes were pale and quick and missed nothing, and Oscar felt them travel over her face like fingers checking goods for flaws. "Parents' places of birth."

Here it was. The simple question.

Her father had been born under a different sky entirely—in a port across the ocean whose name she only half-knew, in a country that had since gone from trading partner to something colder, the kind of colder that filled newspaper columns with words like *edict* and *registry* and *enemy-blood*. Her mother had been born here, or near enough; that much was safe. But the question asked for both, and lies, Oscar knew from the ledgers she reconciled daily, were like sums: one wrong figure invites scrutiny of all the rest.

"Both born here, sir," she said. "Salt Harbor."

The lie came out steady. Eleven years of practice will do that.

Finch looked at her for a moment longer than he had looked at anyone. It was not a long moment—it was perhaps the length of two heartbeats—but Oscar felt it the way you feel a draft under a door.

Then he bent his head and wrote.

She watched his pen. She couldn't help it. She had spent her life watching pens, because pens were where decisions happened in this city; nobody ever ruined by word of mouth alone, it always ended in ink. And she watched Mr. Aldous Finch write *Salt Harbor* for her mother—and then keep writing. His hand moved on, adding something after the place of birth, a notation, small and cramped, tucked into the margin where a person's eye would not naturally fall.

He blotted the page. "Next."

Oscar walked back to the tally floor on legs that felt borrowed. She sat at her desk. She pulled the day's shipment records toward her and began checking weights against waybills, because busy hands, lowered eyes, because the fabric must stay gray, thread by thread.

But a crack had opened somewhere inside the ordinary morning, thin as a hairline fracture in porcelain, and through it she could feel cold air moving.

What had he written?

* * *

The rest of the day behaved itself, mostly. A crate of tea came in short-weighted and Oscar spent a satisfying hour proving it, which was her favorite kind of hour—one where the numbers agreed with her and the world briefly made sense. Prue reported that the census man had smelled of ink and peppermints, and had thanked each girl by name after questioning her, which everyone agreed was unsettling behavior in an official.

"He remembered my name," Prue said, hugging her knees on her bunk that night. "Not read it off the list. *Remembered.* What kind of man remembers?"

"The kind you should worry about," said Bess Odum from the next bed, "or the kind you shouldn't," and they argued it in circles until lights-out, and one by one the girls of Meridian House sank into sleep, breathing in the dark like a single sleeping animal.

Oscar lay awake.

It was the notation. It was the two-heartbeat pause. It was the fact that he had written something in a margin where writing was not wanted, and she had spent eleven years learning exactly how company paper worked, and margins were where clerks put things they didn't want examined but couldn't bear to lose.

At half past eleven, when the house had been still for an hour, Oscar did something she had done perhaps four times in eleven years.

She got up.

* * *

The registry room occupied the ground floor of the administration building, across a yard of packed mud from the dormitories. It was supposed to be locked. It was locked, in fact—Oscar had known since she was thirteen that the lock was theatrical rather than functional, that a wire pin from any decent broom persuaded it open in under a minute, and that this knowledge was best kept the way one keeps a knife wrapped in oilcloth: not used, not mentioned, but there.

Inside, the dark smelled of paper and lamp oil and dust, the particular smell of a room where the past was stored in triplicate. Moonlight came through the high windows in pale rectangles, and the filing cabinets stood in ranks like patient animals, and Oscar stood just inside the door and let her eyes adjust and listened to her own heart argue with itself.

*Go back,* said the part of her that had kept her alive and invisible for eleven years. *You don't know what he wrote. You're risking everything for maybe-nothing.*

*You watched him write it,* said another part, quieter, older, with her father's accent. *You watched him hide it.*

Her father had a voice in her memory like warm gravel, always faintly amused, as though the world were a joke he almost approved of. She hadn't let herself hear it clearly in years—it hurt too much, like pressing on a bruise—but tonight it came through clean.

She crossed the room.

The census records wouldn't live in the registry room permanently—they'd be copied and sent to the Crown Office—but the working files, the company's own personnel records, lived here in the S-through-T cabinets, and it was those she cared about. Because here was the thing Oscar understood better than Finch could know: the census was just a net dragged through shallow water. Whatever it caught, the company would compare against its own files. And her file, she knew—she had helped shelve it once, years ago, anonymous as everyone's—was thin. Father: pearl diver, drowned, wages docked. Mother: deceased, fever. Nothing in it worth a second glance from anyone.

Unless someone had given it a second glance already.

She found the drawer. Sterling, S-t-e. Her fingers, so sure with waybills, trembled among the folders, and she hated them for it. There—thin folder, company buff, her name in block letters. She carried it to the moonlight rectangle by the window like a woman carrying a candle she dared not light.

The folder had been disturbed. She knew it the way she knew a re-weighed crate: the papers sat wrong, the fold creased at a new angle, the whole thing carrying the faint suggestion of other hands. Someone had pulled it recently. Recently enough that the cabinet's neighbors had not yet settled fully back against it.

And threaded through the top left corner of the file, punched neatly through the paper, was a length of red thread.

Oscar stood very still.

She knew red thread. Every tally clerk knew red thread. Red thread flagged disputed shipments, customs holds, cargo under investigation. Red thread meant *do not release without authority above the floor supervisor*. Red thread meant the machinery had turned its attention toward something, slow and heavy and indifferent as a millstone, and the thing it had turned toward, in the entire vast ocean of Meridian's paperwork, was a thin buff folder with her name on it.

She touched it. Just the once, with one fingertip, the way you touch a thing to confirm it's real.

Behind her, in the yard, gravel crunched.

Oscar moved without deciding to move—the body older and wiser than the mind in these matters. There was a gap between the cargo crates stacked against the far wall, samples awaiting assessment, teak and camphor wood breathing their faint spice into the dark, and she folded herself into it sideways, pulling her nightgown close, becoming crate-shadow, becoming nothing, the old skill rising in her like water into a dry channel.

The door opened. Lamplight swung a yellow arc across the ceiling.

"—told him, didn't I, the lock wants replacing, and does anyone listen to a watchman?" Mr. Colm, night watchman, talking to himself as watchmen do, his boots crossing the floor with the unhurried tread of a man checking boxes because checking boxes is the job. "Whole building could be carried off piece by piece. Whole *company* could. Paper, that's what it all comes down to. Paper and who's holding it."

He made his circuit. He paused, horribly, near the crates—Oscar counted her own heartbeats, seven, eight, nine—and then the lamplight swung away, and the door closed, and the key turned, though the lock needed no key, and his boots crunched away across the yard toward the warehouse row.

Oscar stayed in the crate-gap a long time. Long enough for the moonlight rectangles to creep a handspan across the floor. Long enough to be sure.

Then she slipped the file back into its drawer—red thread and all, for removing it would announce her presence louder than any footprint—let herself out, crossed the yard in the shadow of the wall, and climbed back into her bunk, and lay with her eyes open in the dark, listening to forty girls breathe.

Red thread. Enemy-blood investigations, the older clerks called it, in the lowered voices reserved for such phrases. Since the edicts, since the newspapers grew teeth, the red thread had appeared more and more often in the registry—flagging cargo, flagging crews, flagging men with accents and families with the wrong grandmothers. It had never, in eleven years, touched her. Invisibility had been her armor, her craft, her whole architecture, and she had believed in it the way some people believe in God: without evidence, but completely.

Tonight a census taker with crooked spectacles had looked at her face for two heartbeats and written something in a margin, and somewhere upstairs the machinery had turned, and the red thread had found her name the way iron filings find a magnet.

*If the company ever asks about my blood,* her father had said once, years ago, in a tone she'd mistaken for joking because she'd needed it to be a joke—*you remember your papa loves you, little fish. Remember that before you remember anything else.*

She lay in the dark and made her plans the way she made her sums: carefully, twice, in order.

Tomorrow she would find out what the notation said. Tomorrow she would discover who had pulled the file and why and where the thread led. Tomorrow she would become, if the arithmetic demanded it, remarkable.

But tonight—for one more hour—she lay quietly in the gray dark of Meridian House, a fence post among fence posts, and grieved a little for the girl she had been at supper, who had still believed that being unseen was the same as being safe.
