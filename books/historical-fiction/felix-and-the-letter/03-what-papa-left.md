## 3. What Papa Left

The year Papa drowned, the summer came in early and stayed late, and the harbor smelled of hot tar and ripe mangoes and the particular green salt of the shallows where the pearl beds lay. I was eleven, and I believed my father could breathe underwater.

This was not entirely foolish of me. He could stay down longer than any diver on the coast—four minutes on a good day, five once, though he never repeated the feat because Mama had boxed his ears for it—and he moved through the water the way other men moved through their own kitchens, unremarkably, comfortably, as if the sea were simply a room where he happened to keep his living. The other divers said it was his blood. Foreign blood, island blood, whatever that meant to them, which mostly meant nothing and sometimes meant everything. Papa himself said it was simpler than that.

"Oscar," he told me, sitting on the dock with his feet in the water while I pretended not to listen, "the trick is not to want air too badly. Want it badly and your heart pounds and your chest burns and you go up like a cork. Stay curious instead. Curious lasts longer than desperate."

He said this around his pipe, which was unlit, because Mama forbade lit pipes anywhere near the beds, and he obeyed her in all things except the ones she never found out about.

I have thought about that sentence many times since. *Curious lasts longer than desperate.* It is a fine philosophy for a diver. It is also, I have learned, a terrible philosophy for a girl trying to disappear, because desperation is invisible—you can drown in it quietly—but curiosity makes you lift your head. Curiosity makes you look at things you were not supposed to see.

But that is getting ahead of the story, and Papa always said a story told out of order is a lie wearing a nice coat.

* * *

The Fairwind came into port on a Thursday, riding low with cargo and flying Captain Elias Vane's pennant, which was gray with a black fish on it, chosen, rumor held, because Vane could not draw anything more complicated than a fish and refused to pay someone who could.

Papa heard the lookout call her name from the beds, and I watched him surface early—which he never did, not for weather, not for sharks, not for Mama calling him to supper—and haul himself onto the deck of the tender grinning like a man who has just remembered it's his birthday.

"Elias is in," he said, wringing out his queue. "Oscar. My girl. We're going to eat like kings."

"We eat like kings every time the Fairwind docks," Mama said, appearing behind him with the flat look she reserved for the two of them together, which was most of the time. "And then we eat like paupers for a month after they leave, because you drink half the profits and give the other half away."

"That," said Papa, kissing her wet forehead so she'd shove him, "is why we are rich in friends."

"And poor in rent."

"Rich in friends," Papa repeated serenely, "who are poor in rent also, and therefore never ask us to lend them money."

I record this exchange faithfully, because it is how I choose to remember them: bickering on the dock, the whole afternoon ahead of them, the sea calm and the season fat and nobody flagged in any registry anywhere.

* * *

Captain Elias Vane was younger then—not young, but younger—with a beard he trimmed himself and therefore trimmed badly, and hands like anchor chain. He was not a large man but he took up space anyway, the way certain people do, filling a room without raising his voice. He called Papa "Sterling" even though Papa's name wasn't Sterling—that had been the company clerk's invention, an English word slapped over a foreign one, the way they slapped English flags over everything else—and Papa answered to it cheerfully, having decided long ago that a name was just a coat, and you might as well wear the one that fit the weather.

They had met, according to the version I was allowed to hear, when the Fairwind ran out her anchor line during a squall and fouled the diving grounds, and Papa swam down in the middle of the storm to cut her loose rather than let her drag the beds. According to the version Marta the fishwife told, there had also been a fight, a wager, and a woman whose identity changed with each telling. According to the two men themselves, they had simply taken a liking to each other and seen no reason to complicate it with explanations.

Whatever the truth, by sundown of docking day they were installed at our table, which wobbled, with a jug between them, and I sat on the stool by the stove doing sums I wasn't doing, listening.

"—and I tell him, Harrowgate, I say, the man signed for cargo he never saw. That's not trade, that's confession." Vane knocked his pipe out against the table leg. Mama pretended not to mind. She minded everything and pretended not to; Papa noticed everything and pretended not to; between them they had built a marriage the way you build a seawall, out of things that individually would strike you as useless.

"Harrowgate," Papa said, "is a fool with excellent handwriting, which in this city is the more valuable flaw."

"In this city," said Vane, "a fool with excellent handwriting ends up governor."

They laughed. I did not know then that it was a joke. I know now that it was a prophecy, and I wish I could reach back through the years and stop both of them laughing.

"Soon," Papa said, refilling the cups, and here his voice changed—went quiet and careful, the voice he used below the water. "There's talk of edicts. War talk. Company charters, fleet registrations, the whole rigmarole. They'll come for independents like yours, Elias. They'll say it's for security, and fools will believe them, because it sounds tidy."

Vane looked at him across the candle. "You worried?"

"For me?" Papa smiled his crooked smile. "I'm nobody. I dive, I tally, I keep my head down. Nobody investigates a man who owns nothing but a knife and a daughter." He reached over and ruffled my hair without looking at me, the way he did, as if I were a fixed feature of the room, like the stove, dependable and always warm. "For you, though. A brig and twelve crew and a flag with a fish on it? You're a list waiting to be written."

"Then let them write it," Vane said. "Ink dries. Wind doesn't."

I remember thinking, even at eleven, that this was a very fine thing to say, and also that Papa didn't laugh, and that Papa not laughing was rarer than a five-minute dive.

* * *

It was later that same visit—I am nearly sure it was that same night, or the next morning at first light before the Fairwind sailed again—that Papa gave him the letter.

I only learned the details years afterward, pieced together from what Elias told me and what Marta remembered and what Papa himself had let slip in the weeks before. But I will set it down as if I saw it, because in some sense I did; I have replayed it so often in my head that I have furnished the room.

They were on the dock. Dawn-gray, the harbor empty of everyone but gulls and one fishwife hosing down her slabs. Papa had a package wrapped in oilcloth, small, the size of a prayer book, and inside it a letter sealed with wax—the cheap tallow wax, not proper sealing wax, because proper sealing wax cost money and Papa considered money spent on wax to be money wasted.

"You'll hold it," Papa said. Not a question.

"Sterling." Vane turned the package over in his big hands. "What is it?"

"A letter."

"I can see it's a letter. Who's it to?"

"My girl."

Vane waited. He was good at waiting; it is a captain's chief virtue, along with swearing fluently. When Papa offered nothing more, Vane said, carefully, "You're asking me to hold your correspondence. On my ship. Through whatever's coming. You want to tell me why?"

Papa looked out at the water. I know he looked at the water because he always did when he was deciding something; Mama used to say he checked whether the sea approved.

"Because," he said finally, "if the company ever asks about my blood—and they will ask, Elias, maybe not this year, maybe not me, but they will ask—give this to my girl. Only to her. Not to a magistrate, not to a friend, not to her mother, God rest her patience. To Oscar. She'll know what to do with it. Or she won't, and she'll keep it anyway, and that will be enough."

"That's a strange instruction, Sterling."

"It's a strange world, Captain." Papa clapped him on the shoulder, and the sound of it was like a hatch closing. "Sworn?"

Vane swore. I don't mean cursing—he did plenty of that—but the actual oath, the old-fashioned kind, hand on the rail, spoken low. Sailors still did it then, for promises that mattered, and it embarrassed them terribly, like dancing. I am told Vane's exact words were few: that he would hold it, and keep it, and deliver it into her hands and no others, and that if he failed, the sea could have him.

Papa laughed at that last part and said the sea already had him, had had him since he was born, and that was the whole trouble.

* * *

Three weeks later came the last dive.

I was not there. This is the fact that sits in me like a swallowed stone: I was not there. I was at my sums in the company schoolroom, being taught by a thin woman with chalk on her sleeves that the Empire's rivers were numerous and its grievances justified, while my father went down into the green water off Serpent Reef and did not come up.

They told me afterward that it was a cramp. Then they said it was his air line fouled. Then a man who had been in the tender that day drank too much at Marta's stall and said, before Marta clapped a hand over his mouth, that Sterling had gone down somewhere he shouldn't have, somewhere marked off the beds, and had been under too long when he surfaced, and had surfaced wrong, quietly, without splashing, the way a man does when he isn't surfacing at all but merely arriving at the top of a longer descent.

Marta hushed him. Marta brought us casseroles. Marta never said a word, not then. But I remembered the drunk's sentence the way you remember a splinter—too deep and too small to dig out, too sharp to forget.

Papa's effects came back to us in a company crate, itemized: one knife, one pipe (unlit), one slate with figures on it rubbed nearly away, one pair of boots, no watch (never owned), no papers (noted, with what I now recognize was a small, hungry disappointment in the clerk's handwriting). No letter. Whatever he'd carried on his body that day, the sea kept, or the company did, and I was eleven and grieving and did not think to ask.

The Fairwind was at sea when it happened. By the time Elias Vane returned, there was a grave to stand at and a widow to sit with and a girl who had stopped speaking above a whisper. He stood at the grave. He sat with Mama. And he looked at me—this enormous man with the badly trimmed beard, crouched down to my height on the dock where Papa used to sit with his feet in the water—and he said:

"Your father left you something, little fish. It's safe with me. When you're grown, or when you need it—whichever comes first—you come find the Fairwind, and I'll have it. Understand? Whichever comes first."

I understood almost none of it. I nodded anyway, the way children nod, to make adults stop looking at them with that terrible softness.

Then he sailed, and the years did what years do.

* * *

Here is what I knew, growing: that Papa was foreign-born, though born-where was a question Mama answered with silence and neighbors answered with rumors, none matching. That the company had tolerated his difference because he was useful, and useful men are allowed quirks the way prize horses are. That tolerance, I would learn much later, is not safety; it is a lease, and leases expire.

Here is what I did not know: what was in the letter. Whether it was love or evidence or both. Why a simple diver—a nobody, a man with a knife and a daughter—had gone gray-voiced and serious over a scrap of tallow-wax seal. Why he had trusted it to a captain rather than a strongbox, why he had said *if the company ever asks about my blood* in the tone of a man reading weather.

I did not know any of it, and so I did the only thing an orphaned indentured girl can do with an unknown inheritance: I forgot it was mine. Or persuaded myself I had. I became the tally clerk, the gray girl, the one with busy hands and lowered eyes. I filed the memory of that dawn on the dock beside the memory of Papa's laugh, in the place where I kept things too bright to look at directly.

Elias Vane kept the letter. That is the whole of the miracle, and I did not appreciate it for years: a man kept a promise for no payment, against prudence, against interest, past the point where keeping it made him a fool and nearly past the point where it made him a dead man. He kept it the way Papa dove—curiously, stubbornly, wanting the air less than he wanted the truth of the thing below.

* * *

I tell this all now from the far side of it, and from the far side I can see what I could not see at eleven, standing at the grave in my borrowed black dress: that my father knew. Not everything—nobody knows everything—but enough. He had seen something from the bottom of the sea that frightened him more than drowning, and he had spent his last weeks putting his affairs in order, and the order of his affairs was: the girl, the letter, the friend.

He had even, Marta told me once, years later, over her stall, in a voice pitched under the fish-haggling, said something odd the week before he died. She'd asked him why he looked happy when the times were turning ugly, and Papa had said—

Well. I have carried his words a long way. Let me set them down where someone else can carry them too.

"He said: 'A lie needs guarding, Marta. Every hour, every ledger, every year—someone has to keep feeding it or it starves. The truth only needs to be written down once, and hidden well, and it outlives everybody.' And then he laughed, the rascal, and bought a mackerel, and asked after my mother's hip."

She wiped her hands on her apron and would not meet my eyes.

"He knew he was going out deep again," she said. "That's the part I can't forgive him for, dearie. He knew, and he whistled about it."

* * *

But all of that was ahead of me. On the day the story properly begins—gray dawn, roll call, the smell of ink and tar in the Meridian counting-house—I knew only three things.

That I was nobody's daughter now, officially, only the company's.

That somewhere on the water a gray-flagged brig came and went, and a bearded captain held something of mine he had never described, and I had never asked.

And that invisibility, which had served me six years, was about to fail me—though the crack in it came, as cracks do, from a direction I never thought to watch: not the sea, not the company, but a mild little man with a census book and a pen.

His name was Finch. He arrived on a Tuesday.
