## 4. The Ledger Room

The Meridian Trading Company kept its secrets the way the sea kept its dead: deep, dark, and theoretically undisturbed.

Oscar knew this because she had spent four years as a tally clerk in its belly, counting crates of copra and tin and pearl shell by lamplight, and in four years she had learned exactly one useful truth about large institutions: they were run by tired men, and tired men forgot things. They forgot to lock the side door of the registry annex on Thursdays, when the coal delivery came through the alley. They forgot which clerk had borrowed the good pen. They forgot that a girl who had made herself small enough to disappear could also, on occasion, make herself small enough to slip through a door.

She stood across the alley from the registry annex at half past eleven that Thursday night, hood up, hands jammed in her pockets, feeling like the world's least convincing criminal.

"Right," she whispered to herself. "You've counted ten thousand crates of tallow. How hard can one file be?"

The answer, it turned out, was quite hard, but she was getting ahead of herself.

* * *

She had not planned this. Planning implied boldness, and boldness was a thing Oscar Sterling had carefully bred out of herself the way one breeds a bad temperament out of a dog. What she had done instead was worry, for six days, in her particular methodical way—worrying being simply counting performed on fears instead of barrels.

Item: Mr. Aldous Finch, census taker, a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and the apologetic posture of someone perpetually asking to borrow money, had asked her where her father was born. She had said Port Harrow, which was true of his landing but false of his beginning. Finch had looked at her for one second too long—the look of a man doing arithmetic—and written something anyway.

Item: her dormitory bunk had felt colder all week, though the nights had been mild.

Item: on Tuesday, when she'd gone to request a copy of her indenture record for the standard wage review, the junior registrar—a pimply boy named Tewson who normally tripped over himself to be helpful, mostly because Oscar was the only person in the building who ever said thank you to him—had gone pale and said the file was "with the magistrate's office," and had he mentioned how well she looked lately? He had. Twice. While not meeting her eyes.

Item, and this was the item that tipped her over: Tewson, flustered, had said "red-tagged" before catching himself, as if the words had escaped without authorization and he'd had to slam the door on them.

Oscar did not know what red-tagged meant. But she knew the company's filing system better than anyone alive, including, she suspected, the registrars themselves. Red thread in the Meridian ledgers meant disputed title. Disputed cargo. Goods held pending investigation.

She had never once heard it applied to a person.

So on Thursday night, when the coal cart rattled through the alley and young Pem, the watchman's nephew, propped the side door open with a brick while he argued with the driver about the weight ticket, Oscar—who happened to be walking through the alley with a parcel of bread and cheese she had absolutely purchased for herself and no one else—happened to drift inside.

* * *

The registry room by night was a different country from the registry room by day. By day it smelled of dust and beeswax and the Registrar Hobbes's terrible onion sandwiches. By night it smelled only of paper and lamp oil, and the shelves rose up in their long ranks like something between a library and a forest, silent and patient and full of names.

She allowed her eyes a moment to adjust. Then she got to work, because sentiment was a luxury and the coal cart would not argue forever.

The S files. Sterling. Fourth shelf, third case, in the section for indentured personnel. She knew the geography of those shelves the way other girls knew the steps of dance cards; four years of fetching and carrying and re-shelving left marks on a body. Her fingers found the gap where her file should sit before her eyes found anything at all.

The gap was there. The file was not.

Her stomach performed a slow, cold roll, like the harbor swell before weather.

"All right," she breathed. "All right. It's been moved. Moved files go to the investigation annex." The investigation annex was the locked cage at the far end of the room, the one with the brass grille and the lock that Registrar Hobbes polished more lovingly than he had ever polished anything belonging to his wife.

She crossed the room on soft feet, heart tapping out a quick irregular rhythm against her ribs, and stood before the cage. Through the brass grille she could see the investigation files in their special folders, grey board with colored thread through the top corner. Green thread for disputed debts. Yellow for customs queries.

And there—third folder from the left, in the row marked CURRENT—a slim grey folder with a twist of crimson thread through its punched corner, bright as a wound.

For a long moment she simply looked at it, the way you look at a snake sunning itself on a path you need to walk.

Then practicality asserted itself, because it always did. The cage was locked. The key hung on Hobbes's belt by day and lived in Hobbes's desk drawer by night, and Hobbes's desk was—

Hobbes's desk was right there, actually. And Oscar, who had fetched Hobbes's spectacles, his cough lozenges, his spare nibs, and on one memorable occasion his reading cushion, knew precisely which drawer stuck and how one jiggled it.

"I'm going to regret this," she told the empty room.

The room said nothing. Rooms rarely disagreed with her; it was one of the reasons she liked them.

The drawer stuck. She jiggled it. It surrendered with a squeak that sounded, in the midnight hush, like a scream muffled with a pillow, and she froze for a full thirty seconds, hand hovering, listening to her own blood move.

Inside the drawer: keys, a tin of peppermints, a bottle of gin that explained some of Hobbes's afternoon moods, and a small brass ring with perhaps forty keys on it, each labeled with a tiny stamped tag.

She took the ring into the cage area and tried keys by feel, matching tag letters to lock plates. *I-4... I-5... I-6.* The sixth key turned with a click so soft it might have been an apology.

The folder was lighter than she expected. That somehow made it worse. Heavy folders meant lots of evidence; light folders meant somebody hadn't needed much to ruin you.

She carried it to the reading table at the center of the room, set the lamp low, and opened it.

* * *

The first page was her own census entry in Finch's careful hand. *Sterling, Oscar. Age 19. Father: Sterling, Josiah, pearl diver, deceased. Place of birth of father:* —and here Finch had written her lie, *Port Harrow*—*amended per source interview.*

Amended per source interview. He had gone and asked someone else. Someone who had known Papa. Someone who had answered honestly, because honesty costs nothing to people who have nothing else to lose.

Beneath it, in a different hand entirely—thick, official, unhurried, the handwriting of a man paid by the page—was a note:

*Foreign birth confirmed via dockside informant. Subject's bloodline falls under Section Twelve. Recommend classification: enemy-blood, dormant. Flagged for observation. Referenced party: VANE, E., master, brig FAIRWIND — see cross-file.*

Cross-filed. Her name, tied with a red thread to Elias Vane's, sitting in a locked cage in a locked room under a company whose new wartime edicts had turned *enemy sympathizer* from an insult into a sentence.

There was more—a copy of Papa's old diving license, his indenture papers, her mother's death notice—but it was the last document that stopped her breath. A single sheet, dated eight days ago, bearing the seal of Magistrate Hargrove's office:

*Maintain subject in current employment and residence. Do not detain at this stage. Subject may prove useful to higher purposes.*

Useful. The word sat on the page like a hook sits in water: quiet, patient, baited.

Oscar discovered she was gripping the edge of the table. Her knuckles had gone the color of candle wax. She made herself breathe—in for four, out for four, the rhythm she used when a tally ran long—and thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than fear did:

*They're not going to charge me. They're going to use me. And whatever they mean to use me for, it has Captain Vane's name on it.*

Papa's friend. The man who had wept openly at the funeral and then stood at the harbor wall afterward, saying nothing, for an hour, while she stood beside him saying nothing either, and it had been the kindest hour of that whole gray year.

She closed the folder. And here she did something she had never done in her careful, invisible life: she took out the little pencil stub she used for tallies, and copied down, on the back of her own grocery list, the cross-file number, the date, and the phrase *Section Twelve, enemy-blood, dormant*, in her neat clerk's hand. Evidence. If they were keeping records on her, then by God she would keep records on them. It was the only weapon she owned and it weighed less than a sardine, but she pocketed it like a knife.

She returned the folder to the cage, locked it, replaced the key ring exactly as she'd found it—one tooth of the ring resting against the peppermint tin, just as before—pushed the drawer home, and was turning toward the door when the floorboards near the main entrance announced a visitor.

Heavy boots. A lantern's glow swinging pale fingers across the tops of the shelves. And a voice humming—not well, not in tune, but with tremendous commitment—"Ohhh, the maid went down to the har-bor..."

The night watchman. Old Brann, who patrolled the registry wing twice nightly and whose rounds Oscar knew to the minute, except that tonight, apparently, he had decided to be early, or thorough, or cruel.

Her exit lay straight down the central aisle. His lantern lay at the entrance of the same aisle.

Oscar did what any sensible woman raised among cargo would do: she put out the lamp and folded herself into the dark between the stacks of sample crates stored along the east wall—copra samples, tin ingots, one crate of dried fish that announced its presence with the enthusiasm of a drunk uncle. She drew her knees up, pressed her back against cool planking, pulled her hood low, and became, with four years of devoted practice, nothing at all.

Brann's boots came down the aisle. The lantern light slid along the shelf ends, throwing moving stripes across the floor. The humming stopped.

"...smell like someone's been at the lamp," Brann muttered to himself. To her vast distress, he sniffed. "Or someone's been smoking. In the registry. If it's you, Pem, I'll have your ears."

*It's the lamp oil,* Oscar screamed internally. *It's always the lamp oil, you enormous walrus, go back to bed.*

A boot scuffed close. Close enough that she could hear the creak of his belt and the tick of his watch chain. The dried-fish crate chose this exact moment to release a fresh wave of its opinion into the air.

Brann paused. Considered. "Fish," he pronounced at last, with the satisfaction of a detective closing a famous case. "Somebody's been in the fish crates again. Rats, most likely." He raised his voice to address the room at large, and possibly the afterlife: "You hear that, rats? I'll be back with poison, and then we'll see who's laughing."

He resumed humming and moved off down the far aisle, lantern bobbing away like a departing moon.

Oscar stayed where she was for another five minutes, counting her own heartbeats because it was that or laugh, and laughing would kill her. When silence had thoroughly settled, she unfolded herself limb by limb, slipped out through the shelving, eased the side door open onto the alley, and walked out into the night air with the measured pace of a woman with nowhere particular to be and certainly no crimes behind her.

The coal cart was long gone. Pem had gone with it. The brick still held the door, and she nudged it loose with her heel and let the door swing shut behind her, soft as a confession retracted.

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the harbor wall, because her legs wanted motion and her mind wanted water. The tide was out; the exposed flats stank gently of weed and salt, familiar as her own hands. Somewhere out there, under that black shine, lay the beds where Papa had drowned. Somewhere beyond the heads, if the rumors on the docks were true, the Fairwind rode at anchor under the guns of the blockade, flying colors the government no longer approved of.

Enemy-blood, dormant.

Dormant, she supposed, was meant as mercy. As if her father's blood were a fever that might pass if she kept quiet and took plenty of fluids. As if she could sweat out eleven generations of somewhere-else.

She stopped at the wall and gripped the cold stone and let herself feel, briefly and completely, the true size of it: they had taken her lie, weighed it, found it wanting, and threaded it in red. She was cargo now. Disputed goods, held pending investigation, marked *useful*.

And then, because she was her father's daughter and her father had been a man who dove alone into dark water on the strength of a good lamp and better arithmetic, she felt the fear turn over inside her, the way a keel turns, and come about.

They thought she would stay hidden because hiding was all she'd ever done. That was, in fairness, excellent reasoning. It was also, she realized with something that was almost laughter and almost grief, their first mistake. Because nobody—*nobody*—knew how to find things in the Meridian's records better than she did. Not Hargrove, not Hobbes, not the whole ink-stained congregation of them. Four years of carrying their files had taught her every route through that paper forest, every shortcut, every misfiled drawer, every secret a bureaucracy keeps even from itself.

If there was a cross-file on Elias Vane, she would find it. If there was a reason—a real reason, an original sin—that her father's blood mattered to men like Hargrove, it would be written down somewhere, because such men wrote everything down. It was their religion.

She pushed off from the wall and walked on, and by the time she reached the dormitory steps she had stopped rehearsing excuses and started composing plans, which was how she knew the fear had finished changing shape.

She climbed to her bunk quietly. Marta's message could wait until morning market—she'd been putting off visiting the fish stalls all week, telling herself she was too busy. She wasn't busy. She'd been afraid. But the fishwives heard everything on this waterfront, everything, and there was a question she now very much needed answered: whether word could reach a blockaded captain, and what a girl must do or give to make it travel.

She lay in the dark with her hand pressed flat over the pocket where her grocery list lay, numbers and dates and one dangerous phrase written on its back, and listened to the dormitory breathe around her.

*Dormant*, they'd written. Let them think so. Let them sleep soundly on it.

Tomorrow she would go see Marta, who had known her mother, and who owed her mother favors that outlived the grave. Tomorrow the invisible girl would start asking visible questions.

Tonight, she slept better than she had any right to.
