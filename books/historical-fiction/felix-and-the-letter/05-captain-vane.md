## 5. Captain Vane

Captain Elias Vane had survived forty-one years at sea by observing a simple rule: when the world grows complicated, tend to your ship. Rigging first. Hull second. Crew third. Everything else—the weather, the admiralty, the whims of governors and the edicts they signed with flourish pens—could wait until the deck was sound beneath your boots.

The Fairwind was sound. The rest of the world was not.

He stood at the stern rail that gray morning and counted, as he did every morning, the ships that had no business being there. Three gun-brigs of the Crown sat in a lazy arc across the harbor mouth, their cannon ports closed but their presence unmistakable, the way a man leaning against your doorframe with his arms crossed is unmistakable even if he never says he's blocking it. Beyond them, at anchor in the outer roads, lay the blockade tender *Resolute*, flying the double pennant of wartime authority.

"Still three?" asked Silas, coming up the companionway with two mugs of coffee so weak it was more an apology than a beverage.

"Still three."

"They added a fourth last week."

"That fourth is a coastal packet with a cracked mast. She couldn't chase a rowboat uphill." Elias took his mug and drank anyway. Bad coffee was one of war's smaller indignities, and he had made a discipline of noticing small indignities rather than large catastrophes. It kept a man sane.

The wartime edicts had come down six weeks past, in that particular season when the chancelleries of great nations decide they have exhausted the pleasures of diplomacy. Every independent hull in the harbor was to be impounded "for the duration," requisitioned into crown service at rates to be determined later by persons to be named later. Most captains had grumbled and complied. Captain Marlowe of the *Gannet* had complied so quickly he'd reportedly tripped over his own anchor chain getting ashore.

Elias Vane had sent back a letter. It was not an insolent letter—he prided himself on his penmanship and his restraint—but neither did it contain the word *yes*. It contained, instead, several paragraphs about the sanctity of private tonnage, the rights of free masters under the old harbor charter, and a closing line he'd been rather pleased with: *"The Fairwind carries cargo, Your Excellency, not allegiance, and I intend to keep her that way."*

The governor's reply had been the blockade.

"Kerr wants to see you," Silas said. "He's got the face on again."

"What face?"

"The face. The one like he's swallowed a ledger and it's disagreeing with him."

Elias sighed. "Tell him I'm on deck."

"He's standing right behind you, Cap."

Elias turned. Boatswain Kerr—a man built like a capstan, with a beard you could lose a biscuit in—stood at the head of the companionway holding his hat in both hands, which was itself an omen. Kerr wore his hat everywhere, including, on one memorable occasion, to bed during a hurricane. A hatless Kerr meant a Kerr assembling words with care, and Kerr assembling words with care usually meant trouble for somebody.

"Captain," Kerr said. "A moment below?"

"A moment above. It's the only kind we've got."

Kerr glanced at Silas, who examined the horizon with the studied innocence of a man who had heard every conversation ever held on this quarterdeck and intended to hear this one too.

"Alone, Cap."

Silas drifted aft without complaint, though not so far aft that a raised voice wouldn't reach him. Elias respected this. A good first mate eavesdrops professionally; it's half the trade.

* * *

They walked the deck as they talked, because Kerr thought better in motion, like a clock that only runs when you carry it downstairs.

"You know what I'm going to say," Kerr began.

"The letter."

"The letter."

"We've had this conversation eleven times, Boatswain."

"And I'll have it a twelfth, because eleven times you've nodded politely and done nothing, and I'm hoping the twelfth takes." Kerr stopped at the rail and faced him. In the flat morning light his face looked older than its years, which was saying something; the sea had already taken its toll from it decades ago and was now charging interest. "Cap, there's a paper in your strongbox that could hang every man aboard us. You know it, I know it, and now the whole harbor knows there's a reason the Crown hasn't simply boarded us and been done with it. They're waiting to see what you do with it. That's why those gun-brigs sit out there with their ports shut. We're not blockaded, Elias. We're *baited*."

"I'm aware."

"Then burn it."

Elias said nothing for a while. Below them the harbor water slapped the hull in its usual patient rhythm, the same rhythm it had kept through peacetime and would keep through whatever this became. He found the sound steadying. Water didn't take sides. Water just waited, and eventually everything else came to it.

"It isn't mine to burn," he said finally.

"It's paper, Cap. Paper doesn't belong to anybody."

"This paper belongs to a dead man and his daughter, and I gave my word on both counts, and you were standing next to me when I gave it, so if my word's worth nothing then yours witnessed nothing, and where does that leave us?"

Kerr grimaced. This was the trouble with arguing with Elias Vane: he remembered things. Whole conversations, verbatim, from twenty years back, produced at need like coins from a tight fist.

"That was brandy," Kerr said. "Promises made over brandy don't count the same."

"Why not?"

"Because—" Kerr waved a hand, searching. "Because brandy makes a man generous, and generosity's a fair-weather wind. It doesn't hold once the sky turns."

Elias smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth. "You told me something once over brandy too, Tom. Do you remember?"

"No," Kerr said immediately.

"You told me that if you ever got rich you'd buy a farm inland, someplace where you couldn't hear gulls, because gulls remind you of debts. And then you laughed until you spilled a third of a very good bottle, and I paid for it."

"That's not the same class of thing."

"Everything's a different class of thing when it's ours and not someone else's. That's the whole of human nature, Tom, and I've never met a man who escaped it, including me." He set down his mug on the rail. "But here's the part I keep, and I keep it sober: a drowning man handed me a sealed letter and asked me for one thing, and I said yes, and he died within the hour believing me. I will not make that yes into a no after he can't hear it."

Kerr was quiet. Then, lower: "And the girl? The Sterling girl? She's under investigation, Cap. Harbormaster's clerks talk, even bribed ones. If the company's pulled her file, she's in the machine now, and machines like that don't stop out of mercy. They stop when they've finished."

"I know it."

"So the letter doesn't save her. It damns whoever holds it near her. Burn it, and she's just a clerk with a foreign father and bad luck. Keep it, and she's a conspirator."

"She's his daughter."

"She's also a grown woman who's survived that company for years without our help—"

"She's nineteen."

"She was sixteen when he drowned, and nobody helped her then either, and she lived." Kerr's voice gentled despite himself. "Cap. I love you like a brother, which is why I'm saying this plain instead of going behind your back like a sensible mutineer. That paper is a loaded pistol in a house full of children. Prudence says drown it."

Elias looked out at the gun-brigs. One of them was lowering a boat—not toward the Fairwind, merely toward the tender, routine traffic, but every movement on the water drew the eye these days like a cough in church.

"Prudence," he said, "has kept you alive thirty years at sea, Tom, and I'll not knock it. But I've noticed something about prudence. It always knows exactly what to do right up until the moment it matters. Afterward it just stands there holding its hat." He nodded at Kerr's hands, still wrapped around the hat he'd carried up from below. Kerr looked down at them as though discovering them for the first time, and put the hat on with as much dignity as the maneuver allowed.

"There'll be a council," Kerr said. "The crew's talking. You can't stop men talking in a becalmed harbor, Cap, it's the one cargo that ships itself."

"I know. Call it for tonight. I'd rather hear it to my face than through the bulkhead."

Kerr hesitated at the companionway. Whatever he said next, he said to the deck planks. "If it comes to choosing, Elias—her, or the rest of us—I hope you'll remember there's forty souls aboard who also trusted you."

"That's fair," Elias said. "That's fair, and I'll carry it."

He watched his boatswain go down, and stood alone at the rail, and admitted to himself what he'd admit to no one else: Kerr wasn't wrong. That was the misery of it. His oldest friend aboard was wrong the way a sound chart is wrong—it shows every rock truly, and still can't tell you where to sail.

* * *

In the afternoon, when the light slanted low enough to make the cabin gloomy, Elias opened the strongbox.

He did not open it often. Once a month, perhaps, to oil the lock and confirm the world hadn't changed overnight—which it never had, and which he knew perfectly well it wouldn't, and which he did anyway, because ritual is how sailors pray without admitting to it.

Inside, beneath the ship's articles, the spare chronometer, and a small fortune in honest coin, lay a letter.

It was a modest thing. Good paper, better than a pearl diver would ordinarily buy, sealed with plain wax and wrapped once in oilcloth against damp. On the outer wrapping, in a careful hand that must have been written before the last dive, cold and dry and on land: *For Oscar, from her father.*

Elias had held it for four years now.

He lifted it out and sat with it on his knee the way you'd sit with a sleeping cat you had no intention of disturbing but couldn't help thinking about. Four years. He'd carried it through two voyages, one near-wreckage on the northern shelf, one marriage proposal (declined, hers, amicably), and the entire slow rot of the harbor's politics from nuisance to siege. Through all of it the letter had stayed sealed, because Sterling had asked one thing and Elias had promised it, and the promise had a shape: *if the company ever asks about my blood, give this to my girl.* Not before. Not unless. The letter had a door, and the door had a condition, and Elias Vane was a man who honored conditions.

Except now the company *was* asking. The condition had arrived. And the door, when he reached for it, opened onto a room full of armed men.

He almost laughed at himself. Forty years afloat and the hardest navigation he'd ever attempted was this: a piece of paper, a girl he hadn't seen since she was sixteen, and a harbor full of bayonets between them.

There was a tap at the cabin door, and Silas came in without waiting, which was Silas all over—polite enough to knock, confident enough not to care about the answer.

"Harbormaster's boy came alongside in a skiff," Silas said. "Wouldn't speak aloud. Passed me this." He held out a scrap of paper folded to the size of a thumbnail.

Elias unfolded it. The handwriting was the harbormaster's own, hurried, the ink blotted where a man writes fast and frightened:

*THEY'VE PULLED THE STERLING GIRL'S FILE. RED THREAD ON IT. INVESTIGATION FORMAL AS OF TUESDAY. MOVE QUICK OR DON'T MOVE AT ALL. — J.*

Elias read it twice. Then he read it a third time, not because the words were difficult but because reading them again postponed the moment he'd have to do something about them.

"Well?" Silas asked.

Elias set the letter on the desk, and beside it the note, and looked at the pair of them together—the sealed promise and the ticking clock—as though arranging them might reveal a third option hiding between the folds.

Four years he'd kept his word intact, sealed and safe, and the keeping had cost nothing but vigilance. Now the word itself demanded payment, and payment meant breaking every prudent rule Kerr had spent the afternoon reciting. To reach the girl he'd have to send someone ashore through a blockade, into a city where Hargrove's tails watched the fish market and the salt sheds and probably the churches. To warn her without reaching her was to half-drown her—frighten a girl whose entire survival strategy, if he remembered young Oscar Sterling rightly, was never being noticed. And she *hadn't* been noticed, not for years, not until somebody pulled a thread.

But a red thread didn't unravel on its own. Somebody pulled it, and whoever pulled it would pull until something tore.

"Cap?" Silas said quietly. "Your face is doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you've already decided and you're grieving about it."

Elias huffed out something that was nearly a laugh. "Am I that legible?"

"To the crew? You're a broadsheet, Cap." Silas leaned against the doorframe, arms folded, and waited—and that was the other thing about Silas, the thing that made Elias thank whatever saint kept drunken printer's apprentices and turned them into steady hands: he waited without pushing. He'd follow the captain off a cliff or back to bed, whichever was ordered, and he'd do it without flinching, and he'd never once in eight years asked Elias to be anything other than what he was.

"Get me the boat log," Elias said. "I want to know which of our shore runs looks most like errantry and least like conspiracy. Fish, water, letters home—something dull. Dullness is the best disguise left in this harbor."

Silas straightened slowly. "You're sending someone to the girl."

"I'm sending someone to the *fish market*, Mr. Silas, who may happen to encounter a fishwife named Marta who knew the Sterling family, and who may pass along, entirely by accident, that Captain Vane inquires after the health of his old friend's daughter. That is all. That is legal, harmless, and dull."

"And if the company follows whoever we send?"

"Then we send someone clever enough not to be followed." Elias paused. "Which is why I'm sending you."

Silas's eyebrows went up, and then, gradually, into something warmer. "Aye," he said. "All right."

"That's it? No argument?"

"About going ashore? Cap, I've been cooped up in this harbor six weeks drinking coffee that's legally classified as a threat to morale. You'd have had to tie me down." He gathered the mugs, paused at the door. "What do I tell her, if I get close enough to tell her anything?"

Elias looked down at the desk, at the letter lying in the lamplight with its careful wax seal and its patient, terrible weight.

"Tell her," he said, "that her father's friend keeps his promises. Tell her the letter is safe. And tell her—" he stopped, hearing his own voice, hearing how much there was to say and how little of it fit in a message carried through a hostile port. What could a stranger's sentence possibly mean to a girl who'd been invisible for four years? *Your father loved you*—she'd know that. *You're in danger*—she'd learn that soon enough on her own, and fear arriving early serves nobody. What was the truest thing?

"Tell her," Elias said slowly, "that she should watch the tide tables. Nothing more. If she asks what I mean, say the captain will explain when he can. Let her decide whether to trust that much."

Silas nodded once, and slipped out, and his footsteps went up the ladder light as a man who'd been given shore leave rather than a mission, which Elias supposed was the same kindness either way.

Alone, the captain picked up the letter one more time. He turned it over in his hands. *For Oscar, from her father.* Four years of salt air and the wrapping was still sound; Sterling had known his trade even at the end, wrapping against the wet the way he'd wrapped his whole life against discovery.

"Not yet," Elias told it, the way he'd been telling it for four years, and slid it back beneath the ship's articles, and locked the box, and pocketed the key.

Above, through the skylight, he could hear the evening watch gathering, Kerr's voice calling the order in its usual gravel, and beyond the hull the harbor water slapping its patient, sideless patience. Out past the point, three gun-brigs sat with their ports closed, waiting to see what he would do.

Let them wait, Elias thought. Tomorrow night, someone quiet would row for the salt sheds, and the first plank of whatever came next would be laid down in the dark.

He only hoped the girl would read the tide tables—and trust a dead man's friend enough to follow them.
