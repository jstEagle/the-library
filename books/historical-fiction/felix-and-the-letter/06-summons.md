## 6. Summons

The note arrived on a Tuesday, which Oscar would later think was fitting. Tuesdays were the days the company took inventory of everything it owned, and she supposed she had finally been counted among the ledgers' contents: one girl, used, condition fair, value under review.

It came folded inside her wage packet, which was itself an insult dressed as efficiency. Eleven years of wages had come to her in those packets—coins wrapped in a slip of paper stamped MERIDIAN TRADING CO., ALL SUMS FINAL—and never once had the paper said anything but the amount. Now it said:

*Miss O. Sterling to present herself at the Office of the Magistrate, third floor, Customs House, at half past two o'clock on Thursday. Punctuality is appreciated.*

*Appreciated.* Not required. Not compelled. As though Hargrove were inviting her to tea.

She stood in the wage line with the paper going soft and damp in her fist, and around her the queue shuffled forward in its usual order: dockhands first, then the tally clerks, then the sorters, then her, because invisibility had a hierarchy too and she had spent a decade climbing to its middle rung, where nobody looked up or down.

"Bad news?" muttered Prue Calloway beside her, a rope-sorter with forearms like mooring lines and a face like Sunday dinner—open, warm, impossible to lie to. Prue was the closest thing Oscar had to a friend, which meant Prue did all the friendship's work while Oscar performed the part of a wall that occasionally laughed.

"It's nothing," Oscar said, and tucked the summons into her apron where it lay against her ribs like a swallowed fishhook.

"That's the fourth nothing you've said this week. You're hoarding them."

"I'm frugal with them."

Prue snorted and heaved her coil of rope onto the counter. But her eyes stayed on Oscar a moment longer than comfortable, because Prue Calloway had never once in her life mastered the art of not looking at people, and she could not understand—truly could not comprehend, the way a fish cannot comprehend fire—that some of us survived by doing the opposite.

Oscar finished her shift in the gray way she finished every shift. Numbers in, numbers out. Bales counted, barrels counted, hands counted at the gate. Her father had once told her that the sea kept no ledgers, that it simply took what it took and gave what it gave and never sent a man a bill. She had been seven. She had thought this was poetry. Now she understood it was the only honest accounting in the world, and she worked for the dishonest kind.

That night in the dormitory she lay awake listening to thirty women breathe, and made her plans the way she made all her plans: small, reversible, quiet.

If Hargrove wanted to know about her father, she would know less. Less than anyone alive knew. Less than the stones on the harbor bed knew. She would be a daughter of nowhere, born of nobody, sprung fully formed from a company ledger like a bad debt. It was a good plan. It had kept her alive for eleven years.

She would learn, in forty-eight hours, exactly how little a good plan was worth against a man with a signature.

* * *

Thursday came in gray and drizzling, the harbor dissolving into mist until the blockaded ships were only suggestions—the masts of the fishing fleet standing out of the fog like the spines of drowned animals. Oscar presented herself at the Customs House at twenty past two. Ten minutes early. Punctuality might only be *appreciated*, but she had survived by over-delivering on other people's small expectations.

The building smelled of ink, floor wax, and the particular staleness of rooms where decisions about other people's lives were made by men who went home at five. A clerk with green eyeshades checked her name off a list without looking at her face, which she appreciated; it was easier to be a name.

"Second door," he said. "He'll see you now."

"He" was already seeing her, she realized. Through the gap in the magistrate's door, a pair of eyes had been tracking her since she crossed the threshold, with the patient attention of a man watching a clock he owned.

Magistrate Corwin Hargrove's office was larger than any room had a right to be, and arranged so that whoever entered had to cross the full length of it—past the globe, past the model ship in its glass case, past the framed commendations—before arriving at the desk, like a pilgrim approaching an altar. The chair across from him was placed at an angle, slightly lower, slightly farther away than courtesy demanded. Everything in the room was slightly wrong in ways you felt before you identified.

"Miss Sterling." He rose—actually rose, a plump man in a good coat, extending a soft hand. "Thank you for coming. May I offer you tea?"

"I—no, thank you, sir."

"Do sit."

She sat. The chair was lower than his. Of course it was.

Hargrove resettled himself, laced his fingers over his waistcoat, and regarded her with an expression she could only call benevolent appraisal—the look of a gardener considering whether a plant was worth the trouble of watering or ought simply to be dug up and burned before it spread.

"You're wondering why I've asked you here," he said warmly.

"No, sir." Then, catching herself: "I mean—I assume it concerns the census."

"Ah! You've heard about Mr. Finch's work." He smiled as if the census were a charming community project, a fete, a raffle. "A tiresome business, counting everyone, but the times require it. Wars do make arithmetic of us all." He sighed with genuine feeling. "You know, Miss Sterling, I've reviewed your file. Eleven years with the company. No absences unexplained, no debts, no disturbances. Your tally accuracy is—" he consulted a paper, though Oscar would have bet her last coin he had never read it, "—remarkable. Ninety-nine point six percent. Do you know, that figure puts you above three of my own clerks?"

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me. Accuracy is its own reward, they tell me." He chuckled at his own small joke, and waited for her to smile. She arranged her mouth into something adjacent to one. "Which is precisely why this business pains me. Because when irregularity touches a record as clean as yours, one wants very much to find it a clerical error."

Her hands were in her lap. She kept them there. Hands that fidget gave testimony.

"The census entry," Hargrove continued, drawing a folder toward him but not opening it—a conjurer's restraint, letting the audience imagine the dove, "lists your father's place of birth as—" and here he said the foreign place-name lightly, carelessly, the way one names a distant country in a travel lecture, "—is that correct?"

"My father is dead, sir. Five years drowned. The beds off Gull Point."

"Yes. So I've read. A diver. Dangerous trade." Sympathy moved across his face like weather over flat water—visible, surface-level, touching nothing beneath. "But you'll appreciate my difficulty. The question concerned *birth*. Place of birth. And your answer to Mr. Finch was, according to his notes—" he peered at the folder without opening it, "—somewhat evasive."

"Mr. Finch asked where my parents were born. My mother was born here, in the parish of Saint Aldric's, and my father—" She paused. In the pause she built the wall, brick by brick, the same wall she'd built at eleven years old when the company matron asked who her people were. "My father died when I was young. I don't recall what he said of it. Children don't ask their fathers such things, sir. They ask for supper."

"Mm." Hargrove studied her. His eyes were pale, the color of weak tea, and they did not blink as often as eyes ought to. "And yet Mr. Finch records that he observed in you a hesitancy consistent with concealment."

For one absurd moment, grief for Mr. Aldous Finch rose in her breast—he had seemed decent, bumbling, apologetic, and now his pencil scratches were being recited in a warm room as evidence. "He wrote something down after I answered him. I couldn't say what."

"No," Hargrove agreed softly. "You couldn't. That is rather the theme of our conversation, isn't it? There is a great deal you cannot say. Where your father was born. What he said of it. What he did, beyond diving." He opened the folder at last—delicately, two-handed, as though it might startle—and turned it so she could see a single page. He did not push it toward her. That was the trick; she was meant to crane. She did not crane. "Tell me what you know of your father's associates."

The question slid into the room like cold water finding the crack in a boot.

"Associates, sir?"

"His friends. His shipmates. Men he kept company with." Hargrove's voice remained pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a man discussing drainage. "Divers have crews. Crews have captains. Did your father ever speak of Captain Vane?"

There are silences and there are silences. There is the silence of thinking, and the silence of refusing, and the silence of terror, and a skilled interrogator can taste all three. Oscar had been silent for eleven years; she knew the grammar of it the way sailors know weather. She let the pause stretch just long enough to suggest confusion rather than calculation, and said:

"Vane. The blockade captain? Everyone knows the name, sir. It's on the broadsheets."

Something flickered in the pale eyes—not anger. Enjoyment. Dear God, she thought, he *likes* this.

"Everyone knows the name," Hargrove repeated agreeably. "Yes. Captain Elias Vane, master of the brig *Fairwind*, currently at anchor in our harbor in defiance of a lawful edict, holding four vessels and forty-one crew outside crown authority. A traitor, in plain terms, though the lawyers prefer 'enemy of the procurement acts.'" He leaned back and pressed his fingertips together. "And a sworn friend of one Silas Sterling, pearl diver, deceased, whose daughter sits before me claiming to remember nothing of him at all."

The word *sworn* went through her like a splinter. Who had told them that? Who watched two men share brandy on a dock fifteen years ago?

"I never met Captain Vane," she said. It was even true. Papa had kept his two worlds salted apart like split fish—the family world and the fleet world—and had done it, she understood now with a chill, deliberately. "My father dove. He didn't run with ship captains."

"Didn't he." Hargrove closed the folder with a soft pat, as one pats a dog that has performed adequately. "Miss Sterling, let me describe a situation to you, and you tell me if I've got it wrong, because heaven knows I'm often wrong; ask my wife." He beamed at this, inviting her into the marriage's little joke, offering intimacy the way a poacher offers an apple through a fence. "A girl of no family and modest prospects works eleven spotless years for this company. Then a war starts, and a census taker writes one line in a register, and within a week that same girl is summoned to this office. From her point of view, that must feel like misfortune. Like lightning." He spread his hands. "But from *my* point of view, it looks like a door. A door that opens onto a corridor, and the corridor runs down through the dockworkers' quarter, past the fish market, along the waterfront where certain persons congregate, and arrives eventually at the berth of the *Fairwind*. All I am asking, Miss Sterling—all I have *ever* asked—is to walk that corridor quietly, with your help, and see where it ends."

"And if I don't know the way, sir?"

"Then we shall map it together." He said it kindly. That was the worst of it; he said every monstrous thing kindly, wrapping each one in courtesy like a pill in jam. "You may begin with the smallest thing. Names. Dockworkers who spoke well of Vane. Fishwives who carried messages. Men who complained about the new levies. Trifles. Nobody hangs for trifles." He smiled. "And in exchange—Miss Sterling, look at me."

She looked at him. It cost more than the whole interview so far.

"In exchange, this file—" he tapped it, "—becomes what it ought to be. A clerical curiosity. A dead diver's daughter, blameless, employed, accurate to the ninth of a percent. There are positions in the head office for girls of accuracy, you know. Dry rooms. Good light. Wages that arrive without arithmetic errors." He paused delicately. "Whereas if the file remains flagged—if it goes upstairs, to the governor's people, with all the questions unanswered—then it stops being a file and becomes a *case*, and cases, Miss Sterling, have a way of gathering weight as they roll downhill. They gather witnesses, and informants, and testimony, most of it false, all of it damaging. By the time a case reaches the bottom, the person inside it is rarely recognizable." He sighed, genuinely regretful, a man lamenting the weather he himself had ordered. "I should hate to see that happen to ninety-nine point six percent."

The room was very quiet. Rain ticked at the window. On his desk, between the inkwell and the model ship, sat a small brass bell, and Oscar understood with perfect clarity that somewhere behind one of these paneled walls a man sat beside that bell's twin, waiting, and that whatever she said next would decide whether she left this building through the front door or another one.

So she did the only thing she had ever been any good at.

"I'm sorry, sir," she said, "but I don't understand what you want from me. My father dove pearls. He drowned. My mother died of fever two years prior. I was taken into indenture by the company and I have worked ever since. I don't go to the fish market except Thursdays, for herring, from Mrs. Odell's stall, the second one, because Mrs. Odell doesn't shortchange the count." A beat. "I don't know any captains. I count things. That's all I am."

Hargrove regarded her for a long moment, and then he laughed—a real laugh, round and delighted, and somehow that was worse than everything before it.

"*That's all I am,*" he repeated, savoring it. "Oh, you're your father's daughter, right enough. Silas Sterling, they tell me, could hold his breath four minutes and smile the whole while." He stood, signaling dismissal, and the warmth in his voice did not diminish so much as reveal its machinery. "Think it over. Not indefinitely—we're at war, and wars move quickly—but a few days. If anything should *occur* to you, anything at all, however trivial, you may send word through the clerk downstairs. Discreetly." He walked her toward the door himself, a hand hovering near her elbow without landing, a gentleman escorting a lady across a chasm. "And Miss Sterling—"

She stopped.

"—the interesting thing about silence," Hargrove said pleasantly, "is that everyone assumes it means innocence. But I've sat in this chair nineteen years, and I'll tell you what silence actually means." He smiled down at her, mild as milk. "It means the person is still deciding."

He opened the door. The clerk with the green eyeshades looked elsewhere with tremendous professional discipline.

"Give my regards to Mrs. Odell," Hargrove said.

* * *

She did not go straight back to the dormitory. She walked, because her legs wanted motion and her thoughts wanted none, and the rain had thinned to a drifting haze that softened the whole waterfront into watercolor. Past the rope walks. Past the salt sheds, dark and locked. Along the harbor wall where the fishing fleet rode at anchor under the guns of a revenue cutter, masts bare, sails furled, the *Fairwind* somewhere among them with her captain presumably pacing his quarterdeck and knowing nothing—nothing—of what his old friend's silence was costing his friend's daughter.

*I don't know any captains. I count things. That's all I am.*

She had given him nothing. She turned it over as she walked, testing it, the way she tested knots. Nothing, surely. A dead father, a herring stall, an alibi measured in percentages.

And yet.

Silence had been safety for eleven years, and she had believed in it the way other people believed in God—without proof, without question, because belief was easier than the alternative. But Hargrove had sat there in his warm room and explained, with the patience of a man describing arithmetic to a child, that silence was not a wall at all. It was a door, and doors opened from either side, and he had a key and time and the whole apparatus of a company and a crown to turn them with.

She stopped at the harbor wall, where the tide slapped the stones below, and looked out at the fog-bound fleet.

*Everyone assumes it means innocence.*

Somewhere out there, invisible in the murk, a bell buoy rang—slow, patient, marking a hazard that could not be seen, only trusted to exist. Papa had taught her that sound when she was small. *When you hear the bell and can't see the rock, love, that's when you mind your heading hardest.*

She pulled her collar up and turned back toward the dormitory, minding her heading, and it was only halfway down the dark lane by the cooperage that she noticed the footsteps behind hers—soft ones, unhurried ones, matching her pace and stopping when she stopped.

They stopped, too.

And then, from the shadow of the cooperage doorway, a woman's voice—rough, low, familiar as herring brine and Sunday bread—said quietly: "Not here, girl. Keep walking. Turn left at the shrine, and don't look back, whatever else you do."

Marta. Old Marta, who had known her mother, who sold fish and gossip in equal measure and had never once, in eleven years, spoken to Oscar anywhere a third soul might witness it.

Oscar kept walking. Left at the shrine. Heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird.

Behind her, the other footsteps turned away into the dark.
