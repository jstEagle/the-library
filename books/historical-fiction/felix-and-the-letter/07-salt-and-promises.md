## 7. Salt and Promises

The harbormaster of Port Meridian was a man named Cobbins who had three great loves in life: his wife's cooking, his own importance, and money—though not necessarily in that order, and Elias Vane had long suspected the order changed depending on the day. For eleven years, Captain Vane had kept Cobbins fed on a steady diet of small bribes, and in return Cobbins kept him fed on small truths: which customs officer was in a mood, which warehouse had room, which patrol boat was likely to be drunk by Thursday.

It was Thursday morning when Cobbins came alongside the *Fairwind* in a skiff that smelled powerfully of mackerel, wearing the expression of a man about to deliver either good news or terrible news and hoping to be paid either way.

"Captain," Cobbins said, hoisting himself over the rail with less grace than he'd managed a decade ago. "Might we speak privately?"

Elias looked at him for a moment—the sweating brow, the hands wringing an oilcloth cap, the eyes that wouldn't settle anywhere above Elias's collar—and felt something cold slide down his spine, the way it always did when a man who talked freely for a living suddenly wanted privacy.

"My cabin, then," Elias said.

* * *

In the cabin, Cobbins accepted brandy he didn't need and held it without drinking, which for Cobbins was as alarming as a church bell ringing at midnight.

"There's talk at the registry," Cobbins said at last, staring into the amber liquid as though the words might be written there. "Investigations. Enemy-blood inquiries, they're calling them. The magistrate Hargrove has pulled files on half the waterfront."

"That's government business," Elias said carefully. "I deal in cargo, not gossip."

"It's your business." Cobbins finally looked up, and what Elias saw in his face made the brandy in his own glass go still. "The girl Sterling, Captain. The tally clerk. Sterling's daughter."

The cabin seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way it did in heavy weather. Elias set his glass down on the chart table with deliberate care, because his hand wanted to do otherwise.

"What about her?"

"She's flagged. Red thread in her file—I saw it with my own eyes when I went to renew our berth papers. Her father's birthplace recorded foreign, and now she's evidence." Cobbins lowered his voice as though the bulkheads might testify against him later. "They'll question her, Captain. And when questioning doesn't satisfy them—and it never does, these days—they'll use her. A girl like that, alone in the world, indentured to the company? She's leverage. Leverage against anyone who ever knew her father."

Elias stood and walked to the stern window. Below, the harbor lay gray and restless under the blockade chain that had been strung across its mouth six weeks ago, when the war edicts came down and every independent hull in port became a prisoner of paperwork. Beyond the chain, the open sea glittered indifferently, close enough to break a man's heart.

He thought of Josiah Sterling the way you think of weather from years ago—a whole climate of memory arriving at once. Josiah laughing in this very cabin, boots up on the bench Elias had since reupholstered twice. Josiah teaching Oscar to whistle through a blade of grass on the quay while she frowned in concentration like a scholar attempting Greek. Josiah on the last evening of his life, pressing a sealed letter into Elias's hands with a grip that said more than the words that followed.

*If the company ever asks about my blood, give this to my girl.*

That had been eight years ago. Eight years of carrying the letter through storms and inspections and one memorable incident with a corrupt inspector in Santalos who patted down every man aboard. Eight years of it sitting in the false bottom of Elias's sea chest, wrapped in oilcloth, weighing nothing and everything.

"I thank you for the news, Cobbins," Elias said quietly. "And I'll double your usual."

Cobbins's eyebrows rose. "You'll what?"

"You heard me. But there's a condition. You'll tell me everything else you hear about the Sterling file. Everything. If a clerk so much as sneezes near it, I want to know which way the wind carried the sneeze."

"That's—" Cobbins hesitated, calculating. Elias could see the arithmetic behind his eyes: the danger rising against the payment rising to meet it. Money won, as it usually did. "Done, Captain. But mind yourself. These are bad waters. Men are drowning in them who never got their feet wet before."

After Cobbins had gone over the side, Elias remained at the window a long while, watching a gull hang motionless over the water. Then he rang for Silas.

* * *

Silas Okafor arrived with the particular walk he used when he suspected he wasn't going to enjoy what came next—which, Elias reflected, meant Silas knew him very well indeed.

"You want tea or trouble?" Silas asked, folding his long frame into the chair across from the chart table.

"Both, ideally."

"We're out of tea."

"Then trouble it is." Elias told him. All of it: the red thread, the investigation, the girl marked as evidence in whatever purge Hargrove was running beneath the flag of patriotism. Silas listened without moving except for his thumb, which worked steadily along the seam of his trousers, a habit he'd had since he was a printer's apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a head full of dangerous opinions.

When Elias finished, Silas was quiet for a moment. "So. What are we doing?"

"*We* aren't doing anything. I'm going to get word to her."

Silas exhaled through his nose—not quite a laugh, but related to one the way a wolf is related to a dog. "Captain. We are blockaded. Our papers are held hostage to a war nobody declared. Half the crew hasn't been paid since the edicts, and the other half is composing poetry about mutiny in the fo'c'sle. And you propose to reach into the middle of a company investigation, through a chain across the harbor, to send a message to one girl."

"Yes."

"One girl."

"One girl," Elias agreed.

"Whom you've seen perhaps four times in eight years."

"Five. There was the christening of Marta's second stall. She ate too much honey cake and was sick over the harbor wall, and Josiah pretended not to notice out of respect for her dignity." The memory surfaced whole and warm, and Elias found his mouth pulling sideways despite everything. "She had her father's frown even then. Like the world was a sum that refused to come out right."

Silas studied him. He had a way of looking at Elias sometimes that made Elias feel like a document being checked for forgery. "This is about the letter."

"This is about a promise."

"The promise is about the letter."

"The promise is about Josiah," Elias said, and heard his own voice come out rougher than intended. He got up and poured himself more brandy he didn't need, because Cobbins hadn't needed his either and look how well that had gone. "Silas, when a man drowns, they don't find his body for weeks, if ever. The sea keeps what it takes. So the last thing Josiah gave me wasn't paper. It was his daughter's future, folded into my hands. He asked me for one thing in fifteen years of friendship. One thing. And I have sat here with it in my sea chest for eight years telling myself that no news was good news, that invisibility was armor, that the best way to honor the promise was to leave it sleeping." He turned to face Silas. "Well. The company has stopped letting it sleep. They've put a red thread around that girl's neck and called it law. So the question isn't whether I keep the promise. The question is whether I keep it in time."

Silas was silent for a long moment. Outside, someone on deck shouted an order about the jib, and the ship answered with creaking timbers, alive even at anchor.

"There'll be those who say we should stay out of it," Silas said finally. "Kerr especially."

"Kerr especially," Elias agreed grimly. Boatswain Kerr had served with him twenty-two years and had lately taken to speaking about pensions the way other men spoke about religion—with fervor, and a hint of menace toward nonbelievers.

"Then let's do it properly," Silas said, unfolding from the chair. "Call them together. Let them argue. Better they argue it now than whisper it later."

Elias nodded, and hated how much he dreaded it. He had commanded men through two gales, a grounding on the Corvus shoals, and one truly catastrophic encounter with a whale that neither he nor the whale had enjoyed. But nothing tested a captain like fear, and fear was what the blockade had been breeding in his crew, patient as rot.

* * *

They gathered in the great cabin that evening—all hands off duty, which meant thirty men packed shoulder to shoulder among the charts, and the air thick with salt wool and grievance. Kerr stood at the back with his arms crossed, which was where he always stood when he intended to disagree with whatever was about to happen.

Elias laid it out plainly: the investigation, the red thread, the girl.

"And why," asked Tam Reyes, the sailmaker, in the reasonable tone of a man who already knew he was being unreasonable, "is she our problem? No offense to the dead, Captain, but Sterling's been eight years gone. His troubles drowned with him."

"Her troubles didn't drown," Elias said. "They've just been given a file number."

"Aye, and hundreds of others besides, from what Cobbins says. It's a purge. Purges roll over everyone in their path. Best thing a small ship can do is lie flat." Reyes looked around for agreement and found some; Elias watched it move through the crowd like wind through barley.

"Best thing a small ship can do is remember what she is," Elias said. "This crew has taken on Sterling's family before. Remember the winter Marta's boy took the fever, and who ran medicine through a customs cordon at two in the morning?"

"That was different," Kerr said. His voice was quiet, which from Kerr was worse than shouting. "That was a child dying. This is a grown woman with a file. And Captain—no disrespect to your friendship—but the letter. That letter's sitting in your sea chest like lit powder. Every day we stay in this harbor with it aboard, we're moored next to a fire. Now they've flagged the girl. What happens when they start connecting threads? Girl to father, father to captain, captain to letter? We don't go poking at that thread. We pray nobody pulls it."

A murmur of assent. Kerr had a gift for making cowardice sound like seamanship.

"Or," Elias said, "we pull it ourselves, on our terms, before someone else does. Because here is what your pension arithmetic leaves out, Mr. Kerr: they don't need proof to hang a man anymore. They need a reason to want proof. And I am a fleet captain who refuses to sign his ships over to the company, married to nothing, heir to nothing, friend of a foreign-born dead man. If Hargrove wants me, he needs no letter. He needs only a Tuesday afternoon and a tribunal with quills."

"Then burn it!" The words burst out of Kerr before he could dress them up. The cabin went very quiet. Kerr's jaw worked, and pride kept him going. "Burn the letter, Captain. It's eight years old. Whatever's in it—words, sentiments, a dead man's wishes—it isn't worth thirty lives. Give the girl her silence and let her keep her head down, same as she's done all these years. That's mercy too."

For a moment Elias let himself feel the temptation at full weight, the way you let yourself look over a high edge precisely because you know better than to jump. Burn it. Be done with the weight. Tell himself silence was safety, the lie he'd been telling himself for eight comfortable, cowardly years.

"No," he said.

"Captain—"

"No, Kerr. And I'll tell you why, and then I'll never explain it again." Elias came around the chart table so he could see all their faces. "Eight years ago a dying man's friend shook my hand and took charge of the most valuable cargo I've ever carried, and I promised delivery. Not someday. On demand. The demand has come. Now, I've sailed with most of you for a decade or more, and I know what you'll say: that promises don't feed crews, that sentiment sinks ships. But hear me. A ship is a strange thing. She floats because of what's underneath the waterline—invisible, unglamorous, holding everything up. Honor is our ballast, gentlemen. Throw it overboard and we ride higher for a while, lighter, faster, easier. And then the first real storm comes, and we turn over, and everyone drowns anyway." He let that sit. "I will not ask any man to risk his neck for my promise. Any man who wants out at the next lawful opportunity will have my blessing and a fair share. But while you sail the *Fairwind*, we do it my way. And my way includes the girl Sterling."

Silence. Somewhere above, the watch bell rang eight times, unhurried, indifferent.

It was young Pellip, the cook's mate, sixteen years old and permanently surprised by the world, who broke it. "My mum works the fish stalls," he blurted. "She knows everyone. Fishwives, oyster girls, the lot. If anyone can get a message to a tally clerk without the company seeing, it's the fish market. It's got more hidden channels than the harbor."

Elias looked at Silas. Silas's mouth twitched—the smallest suggestion of a smile, there and gone like a fish under a swell.

"There's hope for this crew yet," Elias said. "Pellip, you'll write to your mother tonight. Discreet, no names on paper beyond what's needed. She's to reach a woman named Marta—fishwife, big laugh, stall by the third arch. The message goes to Marta, and Marta gets it to the girl. Four words only: *Your father's friend knows.* Nothing more. Can your mother manage that?"

"Aye, Captain. Mum would smuggle contraband in her sleep. Probably does."

Kerr hadn't moved from the back of the cabin. His arms stayed crossed, and his face had settled into the expression of a man filing something away for later—not anger exactly, but a cold little ledger of his own. Elias met his eyes and held them, and neither man said anything, and both understood perfectly well that the argument had merely gone below decks to sulk.

"Dismissed," Elias said. "Mr. Kerr, stay a moment."

* * *

When the cabin emptied, the two old sailors regarded each other across the chart table like chess players who had forgotten whose move it was.

"You think I'm wrong," Elias said.

"I think you're loyal," Kerr said. "Loyalty's a fine wind, Captain. It's also blown more ships onto rocks than reefs have." He rubbed his jaw, gray-stubbled and stubborn. "Twenty-two years, Elias. I've buried men with you, married with you nearby, raised my pension dreams on your deck. I'm not your enemy. I'm just the only one aboard who counts what things cost."

"I know it," Elias said, and meant it, and that was somehow the worst part—that he and Kerr were not opposites but the same coin, flipped. "Count this, then. If they take that girl, they'll squeeze her for names. Dockworkers first, then sympathizers, then anyone who ever shared bread with a Sterling. And whose name sits at the top of that list, attached to a brig full of independent-minded sailors? Mine. Yours. Pellip's mother's. There is no lying flat, Boatswain. There's only choosing whom we stand beside before the tide chooses for us."

Kerr was quiet a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its edge, worn down to something almost gentle. "You always did make it sound simple."

"It isn't simple. It's just true." Elias reached across the table and gripped the older man's forearm, the old way, the way they'd sealed watches and wages and griefs for two decades. "Get some rest. Tomorrow we count what things cost together, and we make sure we can pay."

Kerr left without answering, and Elias stood alone in the lamplight among his charts, listening to the ship breathe around him.

* * *

Late that night, when the cabin lantern had burned low, Elias knelt at his sea chest and lifted the false bottom. The oilcloth bundle came up light as a bird's bone. He unwrapped it just enough to confirm what he already knew: the seal unbroken, Josiah's thumbprint pressed in wax gone dark with age, the paper inside yellowing toward the color of old sail.

*If the company ever asks about my blood, give this to my girl.*

"Well, Josiah," Elias murmured to the empty cabin, to the ghost who presumably haunted it out of habit rather than malice, "somebody's asking."

He rewrapped the letter and stowed it, then sat a while longer with his hands laced over one knee, watching the lantern flame steady itself against the ship's slow motion. Out beyond the blockade chain, the tide was turning. It always turned; that was the one promise the sea kept without being asked.

By morning, Pellip's letter would be in his mother's hands. By tomorrow night, if the fishwives were worth their salt, it would reach Marta's stall. And somewhere in the gray rows of the company dormitory, a girl who had spent eight years perfecting the art of being unseen would receive four words that would change everything.

Elias only hoped they wouldn't frighten her into running. Hope, and something fiercer underneath it—the settling feeling of a debt finally, after eight years, coming due. He had promised Josiah Sterling delivery on demand.

The demand had come. Come hell, blockade, or Hargrove himself, Elias Vane intended to pay it in full.

He pinched out the lantern and went up on deck, where Silas stood at the rail watching the dark water.

"Well?" Silas asked.

"Message goes ashore at dawn."

"And after that?"

"After that," Elias said, "we wait, and we trust a fishwife." He paused. "God help us all."
