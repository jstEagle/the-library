## 8. The Fishwife's Errand

The herring had opinions on Tuesday.

Oscar knew this because Marta said so, loudly, to anyone within earshot of her stall at the fish market—which was everyone, whether they wished to be or not. "Opinions!" Marta declared, slapping a silver-bright fish down onto the ice like a magistrate pronouncing sentence. "This one has *opinions*. You can tell by the mouth. Look at that mouth. That is the mouth of a fish that argued with the net."

"Maybe it won," Oscar said.

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. That was the trouble with Marta's stall: it loosened things in her. She'd only come for her weekly half-kilo, the same order she'd made every Tuesday for six years, delivered in the same flat voice, with the same lowered eyes. Busy hands, quiet mouth, no ripples. That was the whole of her philosophy, carved into her by grief and by Meridian's dormitory rules in equal measure. But Marta Brannigan had never once treated Oscar Sterling as an invisible thing, and after seven years of trying, invisibility simply gave up around the woman and went home.

Marta laughed—a great chest-deep laugh that scattered gulls off the pilings. "It won! And now you'll eat it, and who won then? Eh?" She wrapped the herring in brown paper, and her hands—huge hands, scarred from thirty years of knives and lines and winter water—went suddenly careful. Slow. The paper crinkled longer than paper needed to crinkle.

"There's a bit of cod under the herring," Marta said, quieter now, not meeting Oscar's eyes. "No charge. It looked lonely."

"I didn't order cod."

"No," Marta agreed. "You didn't." She pushed the parcel across the scale, and beneath the brown paper Oscar felt something stiff and small that was neither herring nor cod. A rectangle. Paper on paper. "My mother always said the sea sends what you need, not what you ordered," Marta went on, loudly again, back in her marketplace voice, telling the whole row of stalls about the weather coming up from the south. "Take your fish, girl, before it turns. Go on. Go on home."

Oscar took the parcel. Her heart had begun doing something unprofessional—hammering against her ribs like a tally clerk demanding an audit.

She walked out of the market at her ordinary pace. It took everything she had. Behind her she heard Marta resume an argument with a customer about the moral character of mackerel, at full volume, as though nothing in the world had happened at all.

* * *

She did not open the parcel until she was in the dormitory stairwell, in the dead hour after supper bell and before lights-out, when the corridors belonged to mice and the memory of cabbage. There was a window on the third-floor landing where the gaslight didn't quite reach, and she stood there with the package in her lap feeling like a burglar in her own life.

Under the herring, under the cod, wrapped in oilcloth: a scrap of paper, folded twice, and inside the fold a small brass button, naval pattern, worn smooth.

Her father had carried buttons like that. Elias Vane's crew wore coats with buttons just like that. She had spent enough of her childhood on the Fairwind's deck, perched in the rigging while the men shouted at each other about knots, to know the weight of one in her palm. Her throat went tight. Papa's friend. Papa's promise. All those years she had told herself the letter was a story, a comfort invented by a drowning man's daughter to make a drowning man's absence bearable—and here was its weight in her hand, real as a coin.

She unfolded the note. Marta's handwriting, which had clearly been learned from shipping manifests and resented every letter of it:

*Girl. A man came to my boat before dawn, wouldn't give a name but paid in buttons off his own coat, one by one, till I believed him. He says the captain holds what your father left. They can't come into the city — the company watches the gates like cats at a mousehole. Salt sheds past Tanner's Walk, tomorrow after the nine o'clock bell. Come alone or don't come. I'm sorry to be the one carrying this. Your mother would have boxed my ears for it, but she'd have gone too.*

Below that, smaller, almost squeezed out of the margin:

*I know you'll go. You're your mother's girl if you're anyone's.*

Oscar read it four times. Then she folded it small, smaller, smallest, and pressed it flat between two bricks of the landing wall where a mortar joint had crumbled years ago and no mason had ever bothered—the same gap where she kept her emergency shilling and, once, a stolen sugar lump.

Hope arrived first, warm and enormous, filling her up like spring tide. Then terror followed, because terror always followed hope the way the bill followed the meal, and terror had a great deal to work with.

Come alone or don't come.

Company magistrates didn't leave notes in fish. Company magistrates summoned you to offices with good chairs and bad intentions; Hargrove had already done exactly that, with his oily courtesy and his questions about her father's blood. If this were a trap, it was a strange kind—but strange traps caught invisible girls precisely because they assumed nothing strange could want them.

And yet. *The captain holds what your father left.* Seven years of gray mornings, of tallying other people's fortunes with a pencil stub, of being no one so thoroughly that even the mirror sometimes forgot her—and somewhere past the blockade line, on a brig with a button-wearing crew, sat a piece of paper with her name on it. Her actual name. Not Sterling-the-indentured, not Sterling-the-tally-clerk, not Sterling-the-evidence-in-an-investigation-she-wasn't-supposed-to-know-about. Just—his girl.

She could not go. It was madness to go. Everything sensible in her lined up and said so in one voice.

She went to bed. She lay awake until the bell, listening to the dormitory breathe.

Then she got up, retrieved the note and the button, and put on her oldest boots.

* * *

Tanner's Walk earned its name honestly: the tanneries along it stank so ferociously that respectable people crossed the street rather than walk it, which meant the street belonged entirely to the disreputable, which suited Oscar fine. Past the last tannery the ground fell away toward a stretch of mudflat where the salt sheds stood in a crooked row—low, tar-black buildings where fish salting was done far enough from town that nobody official had to smell it.

The nine o'clock bell rang from St. Aubin's as she came down the path, and its sound rolled over the flats and out across the gray water and came back changed, softer, like something remembered instead of heard.

There were perhaps ten people visible in the whole world: two boys digging lugworms, a dog engaged in private business, an old man mending a weir basket who never once looked up. No one waiting by the sheds. Her stomach dropped and then scolded her for hoping so easily.

She made herself walk the length of the sheds anyway. Foolish, probably. Reckless, certainly. At the far end, in the shadow between the last shed and a stack of salt barrels, someone cleared his throat.

He was young—younger than she'd expected—with a deckhand's rope-burned hands and a sailor's rolling stance, and he held his cap in both hands like a man in church. Dark hair cut badly, in the way of someone whose barber was also his bosun. He looked at her the way people look at a photograph they've been told about for years.

"You're her," he said. "You're Sterling's girl."

"And you're wearing our coat buttons," Oscar said.

He glanced down at his own jacket as though betrayed by it. "Ah," he said. "Well. That was—" He stopped, visibly regrouped. "Silas, miss. Silas Perrin. Deckhand, Fairwind. The captain—he couldn't come himself. Couldn't send anyone senior either, they're watched, every officer on that ship's got a shadow like a second coat. So they sent me, on account of I'm nobody." He said it without self-pity, almost with pride, the way one might say *I'm the lightest, I fit in the boat.*

"Nobody," Oscar repeated.

"Best kind of crewman there is," Silas said solemnly. "Nobodies get all the errands worth doing."

Despite everything—the fear still fizzing in her blood, the flats' cold wind coming off the water, the sheer lunacy of standing in a salt shed's shadow talking treason with a stranger—something in Oscar's chest unknotted half a turn. Gentle humor. Papa's crew had been full of it. She'd forgotten how it felt, that particular warmth, like sun through a cabin window.

"The captain holds the letter," she said. "Marta's note said as much. Why drag me out here to tell me what I already know?"

"Because knowing isn't having, is it." Silas turned his cap in his hands. "Captain wants to give it to you proper. Swore an oath over brandy, he says, and oaths over brandy are binding, he says, though Mr. Kerr says oaths over brandy are just hangovers with ambition." He caught himself. "Point being. He can't bring it into the city, and you can't be seen going out to the ship—the blockade line's got patrols, chain across the harbor mouth at night, company cutters rowing patterns like they're darning a sock. So."

"So."

"So there's a way out to her. Thursday night, if the fog cooperates. Fog usually cooperates; fog's the only honest employee left in this port." Silas held her eyes, and his young face went older and more careful. "I won't lie to you, miss. It means crossing water the company watches. It means if we're taken, they won't ask nice questions like Hargrove does in his office with his tea. It means your name gets attached to ours, and ours is already attached to enough trouble to float a fleet." He paused. "The captain said to tell you true, all of it, and then say: it's yours anyway. What he holds. Yours by right, whenever you can come for it, or—if you send word you'd rather not risk it—he'll find another way, even if it takes years. But he thought you should choose. He said—" Silas frowned with the effort of exact recall. "He said, *her father never once let the sea decide for her. Don't you do it either.*"

The wind moved through the salt sheds and set the tar-paper walls creaking like old ships.

Oscar looked out over the flats, at the gray water, at the far line of the blockade where—though she couldn't see them from here—company cutters rowed their patient darning-needle patterns. Somewhere beyond them, a brig at anchor, and on that brig an envelope seven years sealed, with her father's hand on it. She had spent her whole life since the diving accident learning to want nothing loudly. Wanting quietly. Wanting in pencil, erasable.

*You're your mother's girl if you're anyone's,* Marta had written. Her mother had wanted things loudly. Had laughed loudly, argued loudly with fishmongers and captains and once, memorably, with the entire congregation of St. Aubin's regarding the interpretation of a hymn. Oscar had spent seven years being her father's daughter instead—patient, watchable, silent as the pearl beds.

But her father had also, apparently, left instructions.

"Thursday," Oscar said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "After dark. Where do I—"

"There's a stairs behind the net loft on Coal Quay, down to a skiff. Be there at the change of tide, quarter past eleven. Knock twice on the hull." Silas was already retreating into the shed's shadow, pulling his cap on, becoming with remarkable speed exactly the sort of person one's eye slid off without catching. He paused at the corner. "Miss? Wear clothes you can swim in."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. Captain's orders: tell her true." And he touched his cap brim and was gone around the shed, and a moment later she heard nothing at all, which for a boy who claimed to be nobody was a considerable professional accomplishment.

Oscar stood a while longer, letting the wind take her hair apart, looking at the water. Hope and terror had stopped fighting in her chest and settled into something worse and better: agreement. Both of them pointing the same direction, out past the chain, toward the Fairwind.

Then she squared her shoulders, arranged her face into its usual blankness, and started the long walk back toward the city—past the worm-digging boys, past the indifferent dog, past the old man at the weir who, she noticed with the very last flicker of her attention, had begun, very quietly and without any change in his posture, to follow her.

She did not spot him. She walked home believing herself alone, the whole way rehearsing Thursday in her head like a prayer, while fifty yards behind her a company man with a notebook matched her pace step for step, writing down the date, the time, the place—

—and underlining, twice, the words *salt sheds.*
