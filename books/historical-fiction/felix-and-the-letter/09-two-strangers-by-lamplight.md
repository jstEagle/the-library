## 9. Two Strangers by Lamplight

### Oscar

The salt sheds smelled like everything I had ever refused to feel.

That was Marta's phrase, not mine—she'd said it years ago, laughing into her apron, the way she laughed at most things: "That place smells like everything you refuse to feel, girl. Salt and rot and old rope. You'd hate it." She was right. I hated it. I stood in the shadow of the nearest shed with my collar up and my heart going like a drum in a parade I hadn't agreed to join, and I hated every second of it.

The message had come wrapped inside two mackerel. Not *in* them, exactly—that would have been theatrical even for Marta—but tucked beneath them in the paper, written on a scrap torn from the margin of a tide table. *Your mother's friend sends her love. The salt sheds, Thursday, after the second bell. Come alone. Tell no one, not even her.* The handwriting was cramped and hurried, the letters of a man who wrote more often with rope than with pen.

I had spent three days deciding whether to go. That is not quite true. I spent three days pretending to decide, because some part of me—the part that had kept me alive and unremarkable for eleven years at Meridian Trading—had already gone, had already been standing in the salt smell waiting, while the rest of me filed requisitions and lowered my eyes and counted crates as though counting could keep a girl safe.

Counting couldn't. I knew that now. Mr. Aldous Finch had counted me, and the red thread in the registry room had counted me, and Magistrate Hargrove's oily courtesy had counted me, and there was no number I could be that would make me invisible again.

The second bell rang across the harbor, flat and brassy through the fog.

He stepped out from between the sheds before I saw him come—a small man, quick, with the rolling walk of someone who had spent his whole life compensating for decks that moved under him. He carried a lantern unlit, which told me something. A man who lights his lantern only after he knows whose eyes are watching. He stopped six feet away, which told me something else. A man who understood that strangers need their distance the way fish need water.

"Oscar Sterling," he said. Not a question. Which frightened me more than a question would have.

"Who's asking?"

"Silas." He said it the way other men say their full names, titles and all. "Deckhand, *Fairwind*, out of—" He stopped himself. "Out of nowhere in particular, lately. Captain Vane sent me."

The name went through me like cold water through cloth. Elias Vane. Papa's friend. The name from the flashback that lived behind my ribs—the brandy, the laughter, the big man with the gray in his beard who had lifted me onto his shoulders once when I was six and called me *little starfish* because I wouldn't stop clinging.

"You're lying," I said, because caution demanded I say something, and my voice came out steadier than I felt. "Vane's fleet is blockaded in harbor. No boat leaves, no boat boards, and no captain under edict sends deckhands wandering into the city to find tally clerks."

"No," Silas agreed pleasantly. "He doesn't. So either I'm a very bad liar or a very good one, and you'll have to work out which, because I've been asking myself the same question since Tuesday."

Despite everything—the fear, the wet dark, the company tail I did not yet know existed somewhere behind me in the fog—something in my chest loosened. You can learn a great deal about a man from the shape of his honesty. Papa had been like that too, all bluntness and warmth, terrible at cards because he couldn't hold a lie in his face longer than a hand of play.

"Prove it," I said.

"Happy to." He lit the lantern then, and held it up between us like a man presenting credentials. I saw him properly for the first time: youngish, maybe thirty, with ink stains on his fingers that no honest deckhand should have had, and a face built for wry expressions. "Except I can't, can I? That's rather the whole trouble. Sit down, Miss Sterling. This takes telling."

There was an upturned crate by the shed wall, salt-scabbed and solid. I sat. I did not sit because he told me to. I sat because my knees had been negotiating with me since the second bell and had finally won.

I had rehearsed the speech the whole way in, rowing. It had been very good, that speech. Well-ordered, persuasive, opening strong and closing stronger. Then she looked at me with her father's exact gray eyes—the same eyes that had watched Elias Vane swear an oath over a sealed letter seven years ago—and every word of it evaporated like spray off a hot rail.

So instead I told her the truth, which is what happens when preparation fails me, and honestly it usually works better anyway.

"The captain can't come ashore," I said. "You know the edict. Any officer of an impounded fleet who sets foot on company soil is taken on sight. They've got men watching the harbor stairs, the customs gate, the fish market—especially the fish market, which broke Kerr's heart, because he says the fish market is the last honest institution left in this city and now it's crawling with Hargrove's spies."

"What's the Fairwind's boatswain doing commenting on the honesty of fish markets?"

"Philosophizing. He does it between curses." I crouched down so we were level, lantern between us, and lowered my voice though there was no one near enough to hear, old habit. "Miss Sterling, seven years ago your father put a letter into my captain's hands. I wasn't there—I signed aboard four years after—but I know the story the way the crew knows the story, which means I know it wrong in twelve places and right in the ones that matter. Elias Vane has carried that letter through peace, war, blockade, and one memorable hurricane that cost us our jib and nearly our mast. He has never opened it. Half the crew thinks that's loyalty. The other half thinks it's madness. Kerr thinks it's both, which is why he and the captain argue the way married couples argue."

Her hands were folded tight in her lap, knuckles white, and she was listening the way sailors listen for weather—not to the words but underneath them.

"Why now?" she asked. "If he's kept it seven years, why send for me now?"

"Because of you, ma'am. Because the harbormaster—who costs us a fortune and is worth every coin—told us a girl named Sterling was flagged red in the company registry. Enemy-blood investigation. And the captain went quiet in a way I'd never seen. Quiet like ballast shifting." I hesitated. "He made me promise to tell you this next part exactly. He said: 'Tell her I have held her father's trust for seven years without breaking the seal, and I will hold it seven more if she says so, but I'll not have her carry this danger alone when the letter was always meant for her.' Those words, exact. He made me repeat them back twice on the crossing and once more on the stairs, and if I got one word wrong he'd know, because he knows everything, it's insufferable."

Something crossed her face then—grief and its cousin, tenderness, wearing each other's coats.

"He remembers me," she said. Quietly. Not a question either, but closer to one.

"Ma'am, he talks about you the way other men talk about religion. Sparingly, and with conviction."

### Oscar

We sat in the salt-smell and he told me the shape of the problem, and the shape was this: I could not go to the letter, and the letter could not come to me.

"I can't bring it into the city," Silas said. "Even if I dared—which I don't, and the captain doesn't—if they took it off me, off goes the only proof your father left, into Hargrove's files, and God knows what happens to it there. Paper has a way of disappearing inside Meridian Trading. Paper has a way of disappearing *into* Meridian Trading, if you take my meaning."

"And I can't come out to the ship."

"Not past the blockade chain, not without a row in the dark and patrol boats counting hulls, and not—" he paused, choosing his words with visible care, "—not without the captain knowing you'd be risking your neck, which he won't abide. He said, and again these are his words: 'She's carried her father's name in silence long enough. If she wants the letter, she comes as herself, proven, on her own footing—or not at all.'"

"On my own footing," I repeated. "What does that mean?"

"It means he needs to know you're you. Truly you. The daughter of Tomás Sterling, pearl diver, born—" and here Silas squinted, dredging, "—born in a coastal village I can't pronounce, godchild to nobody, sworn friend to Elias Vane over brandy in the year of the great mullet run. He needs proof before he puts that letter in any hands, because if the wrong hands are reaching for it—and Miss Sterling, we both sleep better assuming the wrong hands are always reaching—he'll die before he hands your father's trust to a stranger wearing your face."

The fog had thickened around us. Somewhere out in the harbor a patrol boat's lantern swung, a slow pendulum, and I thought about how a city can be full of light and still be dark.

"There's nothing," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, thin. "Papa's papers burned with the dormitory fire, year before last. His diving license, his indenture release—all of it. There's no record of me that isn't Meridian's record, and Meridian's records say I'm nobody's daughter. They say I sprang fully formed from a tally desk." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You want proof of identity from a girl the company spent eleven years teaching not to exist."

Silas was quiet a moment. Then he reached into his coat and brought out something small, wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with a deckhand's economy of motion. He held it out on his palm.

A ring. Cheap brass, diver's style, the kind worn to mark working depth—notches filed along the band. This one worn nearly smooth, except for three deep notches still legible, and a fourth half-cut, abandoned mid-file.

"Do you know it?" he asked.

I could not speak. I nodded instead, and even nodding felt like breaking something open.

"He wore it under his glove," I managed, when I could. "Always. Said a diver wears his luck where the water can't argue with it. He was cutting the fourth notch the season he—" I stopped. "He never finished it."

"He never finished it," Silas agreed softly. "Elias took it off his wrist himself, the morning after. He's worn it on a cord round his neck ever since. He sent it as surety. He said—" and here Silas's voice did something complicated, and I realized the wry deckhand was fighting not to be moved, "—he said, 'Tell her the fourth notch is hers to cut, if she wants it. A depth isn't done until it's marked.'"

I held that little ring in both my hands like a coal, like a bird's egg, like the smallest possible piece of my father returned across seven years of drowned water. I am not a girl who cries. I had not cried—not once, not at the funeral that wasn't, not at the dormitory fire, not in the registry room among the crates—because crying is visible, and visibility kills. But my eyes stung, and I let them, there in the dark where only a stranger could see, and somehow that made it bearable.

"All right," I said finally. "Say I believe you. Say I believe all of it—the ring, the captain, the letter. What do you want from me?"

### Silas

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying someone else's hope: it weighs more than cargo, and it doesn't stack neatly.

She asked me what we wanted, and I gave her the answer I'd rowed in with, the rehearsed speech resurrecting itself just in time to be useful, and I watched her listen to terms that were, when you laid them out plainly, completely unreasonable.

"Three nights from now, low tide, there's a gap in the patrol rotation off the northern chain—forty minutes, maybe less, and the harbormaster swears it's reliable, and the harbormaster has been wrong twice, so draw your own conclusions. We bring a boat. You come out to the Fairwind. You show the captain who you are—tell him something only Tomás Sterling's daughter would know, something he can weigh against seven years of memory. If he's satisfied, he puts the letter in your hands, and it becomes yours entirely, and whatever you do with it after that is yours to decide, and none of ours."

"And if he isn't satisfied?"

"Then he apologizes, I row you back, and everyone pretends this never happened." I paused. "Though between us, ma'am, he'll be satisfied. He's been satisfied for seven years. He just needs the ceremony of it. Captains are like that. All that authority, and underneath it they're just men who need things done properly."

She looked down at the ring in her palms for a long moment. The lantern threw our shadows huge against the salt shed, two giants hunched over one small circle of brass.

"There's a problem," she said. "Getting out past the chain. Getting back in before roll call. Being seen on the water at night during an investigation with my name on it." She said it flatly, inventory-style, and I recognized the habit—she was a clerk, she counted risk the way she counted crates, one at a time, eyes lowered. "Any one of those things could end me. All of them together are practically an invitation to the gallows."

"Yes," I said. "They are."

"You're not going to pretend otherwise? Reassure me? Tell me it'll all be fine?"

"Ma'am, I've known you for a quarter hour. Lying to you this early seems disrespectful."

And she laughed—a short, startled bark of a laugh, quickly strangled, like a sound escaping from a room that had been locked a long time. I found myself grinning in the lamplight, which was absurd; nothing about that shed was funny. But there it was.

"I'll need to think," she said. "About whether I trust you."

"Take the time you need. Three nights."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You misunderstand. I mean I need to think about whether I trust *him*. You're just the messenger. The messenger is easy—it's the man holding my father's letter and my whole life in evidence that I have to gamble on." She turned the ring over. The notches caught the light. "Seven years he's kept it sealed. Anyone else would have opened it. Sold it. Burned it out of fear. He didn't."

"That's the captain."

"How do I know that's the captain? How do I know any of this isn't Hargrove's web, baited with my father's own ring?"

It was a fair question, and it deserved better than reassurance, so I gave it the only thing I had.

"You don't," I said. "Not for certain. Faith isn't certainty, Miss Sterling—it's a wager placed without seeing the cards. I placed mine four years ago when I signed aboard a blockaded brig for a captain with a price on his principles, and I'll tell you what I've learned: you can't ever really know another person's heart. You can only watch what they do when it costs them. And Elias Vane has been paying for your father's letter every single day for seven years—in patrols and pardons refused and a boatswain who argues with him about fish markets—and he has never once tried to spend it on himself. That's all the proof I can offer you. It's not much. But it's everything I have, and I'd stake my own neck beside yours on it, and I don't say that lightly, because my neck is my best feature."

She studied me for a long moment in the lamplight. Then she slipped the ring into her pocket, and something in her posture changed—the settling of a decision, the way a ship settles when cargo is finally lashed down.

"I'll be here," she said. "Three nights. Northern chain, low tide. If I'm not there within twenty minutes of the bell, row away and don't look back."

"Understood."

"And Silas—" I straightened up. She was looking at me with her father's eyes, and there was iron in them now where the fear had been. "If this is a trap, I want you to know something. I'm a tally clerk. I count things for a living. And sooner or later, every trap gets counted."

"Yes, ma'am," I said, and meant: *God help whoever traps you.*

She melted into the fog toward the city, walking quiet as foam, and I doused the lantern and stood a moment in the salt-smell dark, thinking about wagers.

Then I heard it—far off, muffled, wrong. Footsteps. Not hers. Someone moving through the sheds behind me with the careful, deliberate tread of a man following rather than leaving.

Somebody had been listening.

* * *

### Oscar

I did not look back. Looking back is for people who can afford to be seen looking back.

I walked home through the waking streets with my father's ring against my hip and my heart conducting its parade again, and I told myself the prickle between my shoulder blades was only the fog, only the cold, only an ordinary fear that belonged to an extraordinary night.

But faith, I was learning, is a wager placed without seeing the cards.

And somewhere behind me in the gray dawn, unhurried, patient as arithmetic, someone was counting too.
