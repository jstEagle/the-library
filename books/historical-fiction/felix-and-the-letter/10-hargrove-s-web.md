## 10. Hargrove's Web

Company Magistrate Josiah Hargrove believed in three things: order, arithmetic, and the fundamental malleability of other people. He did not believe in cruelty for its own sake—cruelty was inefficient, left stains on carpets and grudges in ledgers—but he did believe that a man who would not bend could be made to break, and that the difference between bending and breaking was mostly a matter of applying pressure to the correct joint.

He applied pressure the way his late father had applied varnish: thinly, patiently, and always in the direction of the grain.

On the morning after his interview with the Sterling girl, Hargrove arrived at his office at half past six, as he always did, before the clerks, before the gulls had properly organized themselves over the fish market, before the harbor fog had decided whether it intended to be weather or merely mood. He hung his coat. He filled his pen. He opened the ledger that mattered—not the company's ledger, which was a performance, but his own, bound in green calfskin, in which every thread of the present enterprise was recorded in a private shorthand of his own devising.

The girl's name he had entered under *Assets*.

He drew a small, satisfied tick beside it.

* * *

To understand what Hargrove was doing—and he flattered himself that almost nobody did—one had to understand what the war was actually for. The official account, printed in the *Gazette* and read aloud in churches, held that the crown faced a foreign threat, that loyal merchants must rally, that independent shipping interests operating outside company charter were at best suspect and at worst treasonous. This account was true in the way that a fence is a tree: it occupied the same space and served an entirely different purpose.

The truth, as recorded in green calfskin, was simpler. Governor Crane needed money. The war he had done so much to encourage was expensive, and the men who had financed his elevation expected to be repaid in something more durable than gratitude. What they wanted was tonnage. Hulls, berths, routes, contracts—the physical machinery of coastal trade, currently scattered among two dozen independent fleet-owners who had grown fat and sentimental during the long peace.

The edicts had solved the problem elegantly. A blockaded captain could not sail. A captain who could not sail could not pay his crews, his chandlers, or his debts. And a captain who defaulted on debts secured against his vessel discovered, upon reading clause fourteen subsection nine of the Emergency Maritime Provision, that his vessel now belonged to the Meridian Trading Company, which had drafted the provision, lobbied for it, and printed ten thousand copies of it in a typeface small enough to constitute an ambush.

Patriotism, Hargrove had found, was the cheapest raw material available in the colonies. It required no import duties and replenished itself endlessly. Every seizure dressed in its colors cost nothing and paid handsomely.

Except for Elias Vane.

Hargrove tapped his pen against the ledger and regarded Vane's entry with the particular displeasure he reserved for sums that refused to come out even. Vane owed money—everyone owed money—but Vane's brig Fairwind carried no debt the company could call. Her papers were immaculate. Her crew signed articles year upon year, which spoke either of exceptional wages or exceptional loyalty, both of which Hargrove found distasteful. And Vane himself had come to the customs house in person, three weeks after the edicts, stood in Hargrove's outer office with his hat in his hand like a farmer, and declined—politely, unhurriedly, with the terrible patience of a man who has survived storms—to sign his fleet into company charter "for the duration."

"For the duration," Hargrove muttered now, testing the phrase. It had a sound to it. Like a door closing.

One could not ruin a man who owed nothing. One could not starve out a crew that loved him. But every man had a joint at which pressure told, and Hargrove had spent twenty years in the counting houses of empire learning where to look. Sometimes it was debt. Sometimes it was drink. Sometimes it was a daughter.

Or, in this case, a father's blood.

* * *

At seven o'clock his clerk brought tea and the morning files, and Hargrove worked through them in order of dullness, saving the interesting one for last, the way a careful diner saves the sweet. The census irregularities file had grown pleasingly thick since Finch—that strange little counter of heads—had begun his rounds through the dormitories. Hargrove considered Aldous Finch a minor marvel: a man who had performed a genuinely useful act entirely by accident, out of nothing better than pedantry. Finch wrote down what people told him. Most census takers fudged, abbreviated, lost things. Finch wrote everything down and filed it correctly, and so when Oscar Sterling had said her mother was born here and her father had been born—Finch's neat notation was unambiguous—somewhere else, somewhere across the water, the fact had traveled from Finch's book into the registry, from the registry onto a card, and from the card onto Hargrove's desk, where it had become, through no effort of the girl's own, a lever.

Enemy-blood investigations were delicate instruments. They were also wonderfully flexible ones, because *enemy*, in wartime, meant whatever the governor's office said it meant this week, and the governor's office took its meaning from Hargrove.

He reread his notes from yesterday's interview. The girl had sat with her hands folded like a woman waiting out rain. She had given him nothing—not a name, not a nod, not so much as a flinch at the word *sympathizer*. Hargrove had interviewed hundreds of people in his career and could count on one hand the ones who had given him truly nothing, and most of those had been unconscious at the time.

She was, he noted, her father's daughter. Marcus Sterling had been the same. Quiet, punctual, obliging right up until the moment obligingness ended, and then a wall where a man used to be. Sterling had witnessed something on a night eleven years ago—a shipment, a pier, a signature—and had declined, with that same folded-hands politeness, to describe it in any detail worth having. The investigation had stalled. Then Sterling had drowned, conveniently, and the file had gone into the deep stacks to gather the particular dust of unfinished business.

Until business, as it does, had finished coming around again.

Because here was the elegant part, the part Hargrove had assembled over brandy with Crane like two men building a ship in a bottle: the letter existed. He was nearly certain of it. Sterling the elder had been a close friend of Vane's—all the harbor gossip confirmed it, old men at the taverns still told the story of the two of them singing on the seawall—and men like Sterling, men with walls inside them, entrusted things to friends before they went into dangerous water. And if Sterling had hidden something once, on that pier, eleven years ago, then whatever he had put into his friend's keeping might very well be the same something.

Which meant the girl was not merely evidence. The girl was bait.

He wrote, in his shorthand: *S.G. = door. E.V. will come through it.* He looked at the sentence with satisfaction. It had the compactness of good engineering.

* * *

At half past eight came the complication, because complications kept office hours more faithfully than any clerk.

"Magistrate," said his secretary, a young man named Pell whose entire soul lived in his handwriting, "a Mr. Finch is downstairs. From the census office. He says he's found an irregularity."

"In his own work?"

"He says it's an irregularity in someone else's response to his work. He says—" Pell consulted his note, because Pell trusted no memory, including his own, "—he says the registry has altered one of his entries."

Hargrove set down his pen. "Altered."

"Changed. After filing. He brought the original."

There followed Mr. Finch himself, a narrow man with ink on three fingers and the eyes of an accountant confronting a column that would not sum. He laid two documents on Hargrove's desk side by side, aligned to the millimeter, which Hargrove approved of even as he resented the occasion.

"This is what I recorded," said Finch, tapping the left page. "This is what sits in the registry." The right page bore a small amendment in a hand Hargrove recognized with a cold prickle of professional annoyance as one of Pell's sub-clerks, working under instruction. An innocent notation had become a pointed one. A place of birth had acquired an editorial flourish it had not earned.

"You'll appreciate," said Finch, in the mild voice of a man describing weather to an idiot, "that I take responsibility for what goes out under my name. If the record says I wrote a thing, I ought to have written it. If I didn't write it, the record is wrong. Records that are wrong are worse than no records, because they wear the costume of records." He paused. "I've been doing this eleven years. I have never once had an entry amended after filing. Until this family's."

*This family's.* The word landed harder than Finch could know—or perhaps he knew exactly; there was something unnervingly precise about the man. Hargrove composed his face into an expression of administrative sympathy, a face he kept polished for exactly such occasions.

"Clerical error, Mr. Finch. Wartime volumes. I'll have it corrected."

"I'd rather correct it myself, if it's all the same. In my own hand. So the archive stays honest." Finch collected his pages, squared their edges, and added, on his way to the door, in a tone of perfect innocence: "It's a funny thing about corrections, Magistrate. They always seem to travel in one direction. Toward whoever's being looked at." And he was gone, leaving behind the faint smell of cheap tobacco and the distinct sensation of having been counted.

Hargrove sat for a while without moving.

Then he wrote in the green ledger, beneath the Sterling entry: *Census clerk = watch later.* Not a threat yet. A note. Hargrove never wasted a threat on a man who might still prove useful by accident.

* * *

The rest of the morning was devoted to the pleasant mechanics of the web. He dictated letters. He signed orders. At ten, the harbormaster's report confirmed that the Fairwind remained at anchor, coals low, crew restless, and that two of her men had taken shore leave contrary to the edict's restrictions—Hargrove noted their names with tenderness, the way a collector notes stamps slightly damaged and therefore affordable. At eleven, he received Governor Crane, which was less a meeting than a mirror.

Crane was a handsome man gone soft at the edges, the sort of politician who mistook his own reflection in a window for an audience. He settled into the chair across from Hargrove's desk and said, "Well? Does she bite?"

"She doesn't bite at all, sir. That's her quality." Hargrove poured the sherry Crane liked and did not drink himself. "I offered her the standard trade—her neck for names. She gave me silence of a very high grade. I've had magistrates crack faster."

"And you're sure the diver's friend will hear of it?"

"I'm making certain of it today." Hargrove touched, lightly, the copy of the girl's investigation flag that would, by evening, find its way into the hands of a dockworker who owed the company money and talked too much in taverns. "Rumor travels to the waterfront by Friday. The waterfront travels to the blockade line by Sunday. Vane has held that letter—I'm convinced he holds it—for eleven years out of sentiment. Sentiment, Governor, is a debt that compounds." He smiled at his own phrase and made a note to use it again.

Crane swirled his sherry. "And if he doesn't come ashore? If he's cleverer than you think?"

"Then we take the fleet anyway—there are procedures, ugly ones—and the girl goes to the penal registry, and we lose nothing but time." Hargrove spread his hands, palms up, a gesture of reasonable men everywhere. "You see the beauty of it. Every branch of the tree bears fruit. Her blood makes her actionable. His loyalty makes him predictable. And the whole apparatus—the flags, the tribunals, the red thread in the registry—wears the uniform of patriotic duty. If anyone ever asks why the Sterling girl was pursued, the answer is *foreign birthright*. No one will ask what foreign birthright has to do with independent shipping charters. Nobody reads clause fourteen, Governor. That was rather the point of clause fourteen."

Crane laughed, pleased, and drained his glass. "You're a comfort, Hargrove. You make the necessary things look like bookkeeping."

"That is the entire art, sir," said Hargrove modestly. "Anyone can do a necessary thing loudly. Doing it quietly, in double-entry, with receipts—" he inclined his head, "—that is government."

After Crane had gone, Hargrove stood at his window a while, looking down at the harbor. The fog had burned off into a fine bright day. Masts crowded the anchorage like reeds; among them, distinguishable to a practiced eye by the set of her canvas covers and the insolent healthiness of her lines, lay the Fairwind. Small, at this distance. Almost toylike. He thought about toys, and levers, and doors.

Eleven years ago, a pearl diver had looked at him across this same desk—no, across the desk of Hargrove's predecessor; Hargrove had only inherited the file—and had declined to talk, and had gone home to his daughter, and had died in clean water doing honest work, and had thereby escaped the ledger. Hargrove did not resent the man for it. Resentment was another inefficiency. But he did remember him, because Hargrove remembered everything, and there was a kind of respect, he felt, in remembering.

The daughter would fare worse than the father unless she proved useful, and usefulness, in Hargrove's experience, bloomed most reliably under pressure. He did not wish her harm, particularly. Harm was a tool, like any other, to be kept clean and used sparingly. What he wished was simply to reach through her, gently, the way one reaches through a doorway, and take hold of what waited on the other side: one stubborn captain, one unregistered brig, one sealed letter that might contain anything from an old love note to the end of Governor Crane's career—and therefore the beginning of Hargrove's ascent, though that last he wrote nowhere, not even in green calfskin. Some entries a man kept only in himself.

* * *

In the afternoon he summoned Pell and gave instructions with the smooth economy of a man laying a table.

"The Sterling girl is to be watched. Quietly—no tails she can spot; she has sharp eyes for someone who pretends not to have any. Where she goes, whom she speaks to, how often she visits the fish market. Especially the fish market." The fish markets were the harbor's nervous system; messages moved through them the way blood moves through a body, and Marta the fishwife had appeared in enough marginal memoranda over the years that Hargrove kept a thin folder on her labeled, in his shorthand, *loudmouth, useful*. "If she goes near the water after dark, I want to know within the hour."

"And Vane's sailors on leave?"

"Leave them alone. Let them hear the rumor. Let them carry it home." He capped his pen. "Fear travels fastest when it thinks no one is chasing it."

Pell copied it all into his notebook in his flawless hand, and hesitated, pen hovering. "And the census man, sir? Finch? He's asking questions about the amendment."

Hargrove considered. Somewhere below, faint through the floorboards, came the ordinary music of the counting house—pens, coins, the mutter of men moving numbers—and beyond that the harbor, full of ships, full of doors.

"Finch counts people," Hargrove said at last. "Let him count. Counters are harmless so long as they only count." He turned back to the window, to the bright water and the little brig at anchor, and permitted himself the day's single genuine smile. "Besides, he may yet prove useful. He found me my bait in the first place."

By nightfall the rumor was loose on the waterfront, wearing its work clothes, telling anyone who'd listen that the Sterling girl's file had the red thread in it, that the magistrate had questions about her father's blood, that there'd be a tribunal if the truth didn't come out soon. By midnight it had reached the tavern by the chain-ferry where Fairwind men drank when they could get ashore. And by the time the tide turned, a boat's crew was pulling back out through the dark toward the blockade line, carrying the story the way sailors carry everything worth carrying—carefully, and with curses, straight to Captain Elias Vane.

In his office above the emptying docks, Hargrove entered the day's final tick in the green ledger, blew out his lamp, and went home to sleep the untroubled sleep of a man whose books balance.
