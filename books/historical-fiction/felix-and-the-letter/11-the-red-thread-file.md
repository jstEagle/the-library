## 11. The Red Thread File

There is an art to being forgotten, and Oscar Sterling had spent nine years perfecting it.

The trick, she had learned, was not to hide but to be useful in a boring way. A clerk who is always at her desk is furniture. A clerk who never asks questions is a lamp: present, unremarkable, lit only when someone needed something from her. For nine years she had been the best lamp in the Meridian Trading Company's counting house, and she had believed—right up until the night watchman nearly caught her crouched among cargo crates with her heart hammering like a dockhand's mallet—that the belief itself would keep her safe.

It did not. Belief, it turned out, weighed nothing against a red thread.

* * *

Three nights after the registry room, Oscar went back.

Not because she was brave. She wanted that recorded somewhere, even if only inside her own skull, where no magistrate could subpoena it. She went back because she had spent two days trying not to think about the thread and had discovered that not thinking about a thing is like trying not to think about water when you are drowning in it. Every ledger line she copied blurred into the same question: *why me?* And beneath that question, quieter, older, wearing her father's face: *why him?*

Because here was the thing she hadn't told anyone, the thing she'd barely admitted to herself while hiding behind a barrel of Sumatran pepper with a watchman's lantern swinging six feet from her boots: the red thread had been tied around her file in a particular way. A double loop, knotted twice. And she had seen that knot before, years ago, on a different file, in her father's hands.

She had been eleven or twelve. Papa had come home late from the counting office—he took clerical work in the off-season, when the pearl beds were too cold—and he'd carried home a folder he should have left behind. She remembered it because he burned it in the stove, and because before he burned it, he held it up to the lamplight and looked at the red binding for a long time, and his face did something she had no name for then. Now she did. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic about his own life.

He'd caught her watching and smiled, quick as a card sharp, and said, "Paperwork, little fish. The company drowns in paper so nobody has to drown in water."

Then he'd fed the folder to the fire page by page, making sure each one curled and blackened completely, and told her a story about a crab who kept a beautiful shell that was much too big for him, and how the wisest thing a crab can do is leave the fancy shell for some other fool crab.

She had thought, at twelve, that the story was about a crab.

So on Thursday night, when the counting house emptied and the harbor fog came up over the seawall like a tide of milk, Oscar waited through the supper hour, and then through the change of shifts, and then she took the copy key she had pressed in wax three days earlier—her first act of genuine criminality, and she'd been surprised to find it didn't feel like anything at all—and let herself into the registry room.

* * *

The registry smelled of dust and camphor and old decisions. Rows of filing cabinets stood in ranks like a silent congregation, and somewhere above them a single gas jet burned at half-pressure, the eternal pilot light of bureaucracy. Everything in this room had outlived somebody. That was what files were, really: the parts of people that stayed behind when the rest of them drowned.

Oscar knew exactly which cabinet. Sterling, O. — indentured, tally clerk, third dormitory. She had pulled files from that drawer a thousand times in daylight for magistrates too important to pull their own. Her hands knew the motion: slide, lift, fan, extract. Her hands were steady. It was only the rest of her that shook.

The red thread was still there. Double loop, knotted twice.

She untied it—slowly, the way you'd disarm something—and slid the file free, and then she did something no sensible invisible person would ever do: she sat down on the floor between the cabinets, cross-legged in her gray work dress, and read her own life by pilot light.

The first pages were ordinary enough to make her almost laugh. Her indenture papers. Her wage record, annotated with the sort of comments that follow a competent clerk like seagulls after a trawler: *Punctual. Quiet. Does not fraternize.* Nine years of being good, reduced to four words. Somewhere in her chest, a small stubborn voice pointed out that she had worked very hard to earn those four words, and another voice, her father's voice, said *little fish, the fanciest shells are the emptiest.*

Then the investigation memorandum, dated eight days after Finch's visit, and the room got colder.

*Subject's parentage irregular. Father: Lin Sterling, pearl diver, deceased. Place of birth: [entry contested]. Note prior flag.*

Prior flag.

Oscar turned the page and there it was, cross-referenced in a clerk's precise hand: *See also: Sterling, L. — File 1189-C (sealed).*

File 1189-C lived in the sealed cabinet. The sealed cabinet required a magistrate's signature, and Hargrove's signature was currently attached to her own investigation, and Oscar stood there in the dark doing arithmetic about her own life and understanding, finally, her father's face.

She could not open the sealed cabinet. Not tonight, not without a forgery good enough to fool the day registrar, who had eyes like a fishmonger weighing your coin. But she could do something else, something smaller and meaner and entirely within her power.

She could steal her own file.

Not the original—the original's absence would be noticed by lunchtime, because she was, apparently, interesting now, and interesting things get counted. No. There was a copying clerk's tray by the door, the way-station where duplicates waited to be filed or binned, and the company made copies of everything, because the company trusted no one, including itself. And there, near the bottom of the tray, sitting fat and patient as a spider:

A duplicate of her investigation memorandum. Already copied. Waiting for its second filing.

Someone upstairs was building a case in triplicate.

Oscar folded the pages flat and slid them inside the lining of her bodice, where they lay against her heart like a hot coal wrapped in cloth. Then she re-tied the red thread—double loop, knotted twice, exactly as found; if the company wanted theater, she could give them theater—and put her empty folder back in its drawer, and walked out of the registry room at the pace of a woman going to fetch more ink.

The night watchman nodded at her in the corridor. She nodded back. *Quiet,* said his face. *Does not fraternize.* She nearly laughed, and that frightened her more than anything else that night, because laughing in corridors is the first symptom of a woman who has stopped being careful.

* * *

Back in the dormitory, she did not light her candle until the matron's footsteps had faded and the third-year girls had stopped whispering about a boy at the rope works. Then she lay on her cot with the blanket over her head like a child afraid of thunder, and read by the leak of light under the blanket's hem, and the thunder was all her own.

The memorandum ran six pages. Page one established that she existed suspiciously. Page two catalogued her father: diver, deceased by drowning, wages garnished against his indenture, *no surviving kin of note.* Page three discussed the census entry—Mr. Aldous Finch's neat hand reproduced in full, including the lie Oscar had told and the truth Finch had written anyway—and Oscar felt again that peculiar sensation of being turned into an exhibit. Pages four and five were procedural boilerplate, signatures pending, Hargrove's name appearing with the frequency of damp in a hull.

Page six was a single paragraph, and it was the paragraph that changed the shape of her whole life, though she did not know it yet the way you don't know an earthquake's size while the floor is still moving.

*Note: subject's father previously entered the Company's attention in connection with the *Meridian Rose* matter (see 1189-C). Recommend discretion. The Governor's office has expressed interest in ensuring past irregularities remain settled.*

*The Governor's office.*

Governor Crane's office.

Oscar lay very still under the blanket. Around her the dormitory breathed—thirty girls, thirty sleepers, none of whom would believe her if she woke them. *The Governor's office has expressed interest.* Governors did not express interest in dead pearl divers. Governors did not express interest in tally clerks with four-word reputations. Governors expressed interest in ships, cargoes, money, and witnesses, and her father had been, by every account except the sea's, the last kind.

*Witnessed something,* she thought. *Twelve years ago. Something called the Meridian Rose matter. And whatever he saw, it scared him enough to burn a file in his own stove and tell his daughter a story about crabs.*

And then the other thing, arriving slowly, the way deep cold arrives in water, from the feet upward: *If the company ever asks about my blood, give this to my girl.*

Her father had not given Elias Vane a letter because he was a sentimental man who loved stationery. He had known. He had known that someday the company might come asking about his blood, and he had known—or hoped, or feared—that the asking would be about something other than blood. The letter wasn't just a father's farewell pressed into a friend's hands. It might be the reason the file was flagged. It might be the thing the Meridian Rose mattered for.

The letter may hold more than sentiment.

Oscar pushed the blanket off her face. The ceiling of the dormitory was a wash of gray, and somewhere beyond the shutters the harbor lapped at the pilings, patient as ever, keeping whatever secrets the sea had taken and giving back nothing but cold. She had spent nine years believing that the safest place in the world was the middle of the flock, gray among grays, counted by no one.

Now the company had counted her, and the count went upward—to Hargrove, to the Governor's office, to a sealed cabinet and a ship's name she'd never heard of—and there was no un-counting herself. The only direction left was through.

She folded page six into a tight square and hid it in the hollow seam of her mattress, where generations of girls had hidden love notes and stolen sugar, and she lay back down, and she made herself a promise with all the solemnity of eleven years old and none of a nineteen-year-old's caution: *I will find out what Papa saw. Even if it drowns us both.*

Sleep took its time. When it finally came, she dreamed of her father holding a letter up to the lamplight, and the letter was sealed with wax the color of blood, and when she reached for it the dream gave her a crab instead, walking sideways out of the flame, carrying a shell much too big for him.

* * *

Morning arrived with roll call, porridge, and Marta.

The fishwife had taken to leaving her stall cart parked near the dormitory gate on Fridays—"coincidence," she claimed, with the innocence of a woman selling contraband mackerel out of the bottom crate—and Oscar came out into the gray morning light smelling of salt and herring and found Marta arranging her display with the fierce concentration of a general positioning troops.

"You look," Marta announced without looking up, "like a girl who slept in a stove."

"I slept fine."

"You slept like a herring in a net. I have sold fish to liars for thirty years, girl, and liars have a smell." Marta slapped a mackerel onto the scale with unnecessary violence. "Two for a penny. And maybe while you're buying fish you'll tell me why your hands are shaking."

Oscar looked down. Her hands were shaking. She hadn't noticed.

"It's nothing," she said, which was the first lie, and then, because Marta's black eyes had a way of prying lids off things: "I found something out about Papa. Something old. Something the company buried."

Marta went still—not dramatically, not like theater, but the way water goes still before weather. Thirty years of docks lived in that stillness. "Buried how?"

"Sealed. Flagged. Tied with a red thread." Oscar lowered her voice as two third-years clattered past with their laundry baskets. "Marta—did you know my father saw something? Before he died? Something worth a governor's attention?"

For a long moment Marta said nothing. She gutted the mackerel with a single practiced stroke, and Oscar watched her decide something, watched the decision move across her broad weathered face like a shadow of birds.

"I knew he was frightened," Marta said at last, quietly. "That winter. Your mother used to sit with me of an evening, and Lin would walk the seawall instead of drinking with the other men, and once I asked her what ate him, and she said—" Marta's voice caught on the old grief, briefly, like a skirt on a nail— "she said, 'He saw the hold of a ship he shouldn't have seen, and now the ship sees him.'"

*The hold of a ship.* Oscar's skin prickled. *The Meridian Rose.* A cargo manifest, a witness, a governor's interest, a drowned man. The pieces didn't fit together yet, but they were the same pieces; she could feel it, the way you feel a pattern forming under your fingers before the picture shows.

"There's someone I need to reach," Oscar said. "Out past the blockade. Captain Vane."

Something flickered in Marta's expression—recognition, yes, and worry wearing recognition's clothes. "Elias Vane is a name that gets a girl followed," she said. "You know that."

"I know it better than most, lately."

Marta studied her for a long moment, this girl she'd known since she was in swaddling, this quiet clerk who had just confessed to theft and sedition over a penny mackerel. Then she wrapped two fish in brown paper, pressed them into Oscar's hands, and closed Oscar's fingers over the bundle hard enough to hurt.

"Sunday," she said. "Come to the stall at first light, before the market fills. If you're going to step off the safe path, little fish, at least don't step blind. I know people who know boats."

Oscar walked back toward the counting house with the fish warm against her ribs next to the folded page, and the fog was lifting off the harbor, and somewhere beyond the chain and the patrol boats the Fairwind rode at anchor with a letter aboard that was not, perhaps, a letter at all.

Behind her, unnoticed as always, a man with a census taker's satchel paused at the corner and wrote something small in a notebook, and crossed the street, and was gone.
