## 12. Night Crossing

Silas Peck had been a printer's apprentice for exactly eleven months of his life, and in those eleven months he had learned two things that mattered: how to set type without looking, and how to lie to his mother about where the ink stains came from. Everything else he knew—knots, tides, the particular patience required to sit still in a small boat for hours—he had learned aboard the *Fairwind*, from a captain who believed that a man who couldn't wait quietly was a man who would die loudly.

He was thinking about that now, sitting very still indeed, because fifty yards off his port oar a patrol boat was cutting its lamp across the black water, and the man beside him—a boy really, no older than Silas himself, with hands too big for his wrists—was breathing like a bellows.

"Breathe through your nose," Silas whispered. "Slow. Like you're smelling bread."

The boy—the girl, rather; Oscar Sterling, though Silas kept forgetting, because she wore her brother's coat and her hair bound flat under a cap and she moved like someone who'd spent seven years practicing not being noticed—obeyed. Her breathing slowed. Her eyes never left the patrol boat.

"Good," Silas said. "Bread's important. My mum made it Sundays. You think about bread, the rest of you goes quiet."

"What kind of bread?"

That surprised a laugh out of him, half a breath, immediately smothered. "Dark rye. With caraway."

"Caraway," she repeated, as if filing it away for later, which, he was beginning to understand, was what she did with everything. He'd met her an hour ago at the salt sheds and already he could tell: this was a person who kept accounts. Not on paper. In her ribs.

The patrol boat's lamp swept past them, caught the underside of a gull sleeping on the water, and moved on toward the mole. Silas counted to thirty out of habit, then to thirty again out of fear, and only then did he lean forward and dip his oars.

* * *

Getting out to the *Fairwind* was the easy part, in theory. The harbor chain—new iron, company-forged, raised three weeks ago under the wartime edicts—blocked the main channel, but the chain's western anchor sat in shallow water over the old oyster flats, and at low tide there was a gap beneath it wide enough for a determined rowboat and a crew with short tempers and long memories. Elias Vane had found it the first night of the blockade, standing at the rail with his arms folded, looking at the chain the way other men looked at insults.

"The water doesn't care about edicts," he'd said. "Water remembers where it wants to go. We'll just go with it."

But the patrol boats were new since then. Two of them now, plus a picket sloop anchored mid-channel with a swivel gun that nobody had fired yet but everybody thought about constantly. And tonight there was fog coming off the flats in patches, thick as wool in places, thin as regret in others, so that you rowed from blindness into visibility into blindness again and never knew which came next.

"You've done this before," Oscar said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Twice," Silas said. "Once with salt pork. Once with the harbormaster's cousin, who screamed when a seal bumped us." He paused. "Don't scream."

"I don't scream."

"People say that." He shifted his weight to keep the boat trimmed. "Kerr screamed. Kerr's the boatswain. Forty years on water and he screamed at a seal."

She almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost, but Silas had spent enough time around people in fear to know what it cost her. She was frightened the way quiet people are frightened—not with noise but with weight, sitting on her chest like ballast—and the almost-smile was her throwing something overboard to lighten the load.

"Your captain," she said. "Tell me about him."

"Elias?" Silas considered. The question deserved better than the obvious answers, and anyway talking kept the terror at manageable size. "He's not what you'd expect. People hear 'Captain Vane' and they picture somebody shouting. He doesn't shout. I've seen him angry maybe four times, and once was at a seagull."

"At a seagull."

"It stole his breakfast egg. He gave it a speech." Silas grinned in the dark, knowing she couldn't see it. "About property and the dignity of labor. The seagull was unmoved. But the point is—" He steadied as a patch of fog swallowed them, cold and close. "The point is he keeps things. Promises, mostly. He's been holding your father's letter for—I don't even know how many years. Kerr told him to burn it when the war talk started. Told him straight: *that paper will hang us all.* And Elias said—" Silas dropped into an imitation, low and deliberate, "—*'Then we shall be careful how we hang.'*"

Oscar was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone strange and careful. "He remembered my father. All these years."

"He talks about him," Silas said. "Not often. But when the rum's out and the watch is long. Sterling, he says. Sterling dove like the sea owed him money." He heard her breath catch, and wished he hadn't said it, and then decided he was glad he had, because some things needed saying even when they hurt. "You look like him. Around the eyes. He showed me a sketch once."

"He didn't draw."

"No. Your mother did. He kept it inside his hatband until the sweat took it."

The fog thinned. Ahead, low and black against the lesser black of the sky, lay the flats, and beyond them the pale seam of open water. And somewhere out there, past the chain, past the picket, the *Fairwind* rode at anchor with a letter in her captain's cabin that had crossed half a decade and one man's stubbornness to arrive at this exact hour, this exact tide, this girl with caraway bread filed away in her ribs.

* * *

They heard the patrol boat before they saw it—oars muffled, which was regulation stupidity, because muffled oars creak differently and every working waterman knew it. Silas shipped his own oars and let the boat drift, and put one finger to his lips, and pointed.

It came out of the fog wall on their starboard side, close enough that Silas could smell the lantern oil, could see the watch officer's profile tilted over a clipboard, counting nothing in the middle of nothing. Two men at the oars, one at the lamp. The lamp swung.

Silas did the only thing he could think of, which was to do nothing at all, magnificently. He slumped his shoulders, tipped his cap over his brow, and became a tired man asleep in a leaky skiff, which required no acting whatsoever regarding the leaky skiff, because she'd taken on a hand's width of water since the flats and was sloshing gently around his boots.

Beside him, Oscar had done something even better. She had simply stopped existing. No slump, no performance—she was just gone, folded into shadow and stillness, as if the space where she'd been sitting had always been empty. It unnerved him more than the patrol boat did.

The lamp swung past them. The officer yawned audibly. And then, cruelly, absurdly, the current chose that moment to carry them in a slow, stately arc directly across the patrol boat's bow.

Nobody spoke. Nobody breathed, except Silas, who breathed through his nose and thought about dark rye bread with caraway and his mother's kitchen and the way ink smelled, and discovered that terror and nostalgia are nearly the same sensation.

The current carried them through. The fog closed behind them like a curtain drawn by a merciful stagehand. A hundred yards on, when his hands finally stopped shaking enough to pull, Oscar leaned over and whispered:

"The seal story. Tell me the rest later."

"There's no rest to it. That's the whole story. Kerr screamed."

"Good," she said. "I want something to hold over him."

And Silas Peck, who had known her for ninety minutes, laughed silently until his eyes watered, and understood that he would follow this girl into considerably worse situations than this one, probably soon, possibly at her request.

* * *

The gap under the chain was worse than he remembered. The tide had turned early, or the moon pulled wrong, or the sea had opinions tonight—it was hard to say—but where last month there had been room to spare, now the water sucked at the iron links above them and the boat wanted to lift and pin them against the barnacled mass. They had to lie flat, both of them, while Silas guided her by hand along the anchor cable, pushing the gunwale down with his palms, and the chain groaned overhead like something waking up.

"If we get stuck," Oscar whispered, "do we drown quietly or make a scene?"

"We swim under and come up on the far side and pretend to be otters."

"Noted."

They got through. Of course they got through—Silas would tell it differently later, in the telling versions where the chain came down to within a finger's width of the mast step and the whole harbor held its breath—but they got through, and came out onto the long swell of the open roads, and there she was.

The *Fairwind*, riding at anchor under a single stern light, brig-rigged and beautiful in the way that only hard-worked ships are beautiful: patched sails furled square, hull streaked with salt, a rope fender hanging crooked where somebody had meant to fix it for a week. Fog wreathed her decks. Her watch-keeper stood at the rail like a statue that occasionally smoked.

Oscar didn't say anything. She had turned in the bow and was looking at the ship with an expression Silas couldn't read, and after a moment he realized why: this was the first time since her father died that she had looked at anything belonging to him. This ship had carried Sterling on her decks. These ropes had run through his hands. The wood grain of her rails had been split and worn by the same weather that had weathered the man.

"Hoy!" Silas called, soft, the two-note hail of the *Fairwind*'s own people. "*Peck, coming alongside. Quiet-like.*"

The statue at the rail stiffened, vanished aft, and reappeared with reinforcements, and by the time Silas brought the skiff bumping against the chains there were half a dozen faces peering down through the fog, and one of them, high above, was old and seamed and attached to a voice like gravel in a barrel.

"That'll be Kerr," Silas murmured. "Try not to mention seals."

They came up the side in the ordinary way, hand over hand, and the deck of the *Fairwind* received Oscar Sterling the way a house receives family it never expected: awkwardly, warmly, with everyone suddenly finding reasons to be nearby. Sailors drifted in from the forecourt of the fog. Somebody produced dry blankets from nowhere, the way sailors do. A boy younger than either of them stared at her cap and whispered something to his neighbor about *the Sterling girl, the actual Sterling girl,* and was elbowed into silence.

And then the crowd parted, and Captain Elias Vane came down the deck.

He was broader than Silas had described, grayer, with a beard going to iron and hands that looked like they'd been carved by someone in a hurry. He walked slowly, not from age but from the habit of a man who has learned that arriving slowly gives everyone time to decide how they feel about seeing him. He stopped three paces from Oscar, and the fog moved between them and moved away again, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all.

Then Elias Vane took off his hat.

"You have his eyes," he said. His voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough. "I'd forgotten how much. Or perhaps I never forgot, and only pretended to, the way a man does, so he can keep walking about."

Oscar's chin came up. She'd been holding herself together with both hands since the salt sheds—Silas had watched her do it, admired it, worried about it—and now, here, on this deck, with this man, the hands slipped for just a moment.

"You knew my father," she said. It came out steady. Mostly steady.

"Twenty years," Elias said. "I knew him before I knew my own wife. I knew him drunk and sober, rich and poor, lucky and drowned." He stopped. Corrected himself, gently, like setting a misprinted line of type. "Before he drowned." He looked at her a long moment. "He used to say you could hold your breath longer than any child on the coast, and that you did it out of pure stubbornness, and that you'd grow up to be either a great diver or a terrible negotiator."

"A terrible negotiator," Oscar said. "So far."

Something eased in the circle of them—the crew exhaled as one body, Kerr muttered something that might have been a blessing or a complaint, and Elias Vane smiled for the first time in weeks, a real smile, weather-worn.

"Miss Sterling," he said. "Welcome aboard the *Fairwind*. I have something of yours."

He said it lightly. But his hand went to his breast, to the inner pocket where—for Silas knew, having seen it there at anchor and at sea, in calm and in the worst weather of the autumn—the letter had ridden for five years, sealed, unopened, heavier than any cargo the brig had ever carried.

Elias drew it out. Oilcloth-wrapped, wax-stoppered, small enough to disappear in his broad palm. He held it a moment, looking at it the way a man looks at a debt he has carried so long it has become part of his skeleton.

"Your father gave me this at the end of his last shore leave," he said. "We argued about it. I told him to give it to you himself, like a normal man, over dinner. He said—" and here the captain's voice did the thing voices do when they quote the dead, went soft and careful, handling each word like glass— "he said, *'If I give it to her now, she'll read it now. And it isn't time. If the company ever asks about my blood, Elias—give this to my girl. She'll know when.'*"

He held it out across the small space between them.

Oscar reached for it. Her fingers touched the oilcloth.

And that was when Kerr, at the rail, said quietly: "Cap'n. Lamp. Coming fast. From the eastward."

Every head turned. Out on the black water, past the chain they'd slid beneath, a patrol boat's lantern was burning steady—not sweeping, not searching, but fixed, aimed, patient, like an eye that had already seen what it came to see.

Elias closed his hand back over the letter, and his smile did not fade, exactly. It simply went somewhere else and waited.

"Well," he said. "It seems the letter and the girl arrived together. Let's hope the news did not beat them here."

Below decks, the watch bell rang twice, soft as a heartbeat, and the *Fairwind* creaked at her anchors like a woman turning over in her sleep.
