## 13. The Unopened Letter

The Fairwind's cabin had not been built for two people, and it had certainly not been built for two people carrying eleven years of silence between them. Elias Vane stood with his back against the chart table because there was nowhere else to put himself, and watched the girl—no, the woman, he corrected himself, though the correction sat awkwardly—wring seawater from the hem of her coat.

She'd come aboard soaked to the knees, salt-stung, shivering, and furious with herself for shivering. He recognized the fury. It had been her father's, too. Tom Sterling had once rowed out to the Fairwind with a fever that would have felled an ox, and when Elias had ordered him below to the surgeon, Tom had stood in this very spot and declared that shivering was a matter of opinion.

"Sit," Elias said now, and pushed the single chair toward her with his boot.

"I'm fine."

"You're blue. Sit down before you fall down, and then I'll have to explain to a magistrate why a drowned girl is in my cabin, and I've had enough explaining to do this decade."

That earned him the ghost of a smile. She sat. She was small in the chair the way her father had been small in it, all coiled economy, hands that never stopped working. Even now her fingers found a loose thread on her cuff and worried at it.

Elias poured brandy from the bottle he kept for occasions he dreaded. Two glasses. He'd been pouring two glasses for eleven years, one of them for a dead man, and tonight the arithmetic finally came right.

"To Tom," he said.

She looked at the glass a long moment. "You knew him well."

"Better than I knew myself, some years." He sat down across from her, the cabin so narrow their knees nearly touched. "He pulled me out of the Pearl Street riots when I was nineteen and too proud to thank him. He taught me to read a tide table. He cheated at cards so badly it was almost a form of honesty—everyone knew he was cheating, and he'd grin while he did it, so no one could take offense." Elias turned the glass in his fingers. "I have spent eleven years waiting to tell someone that story, and now that you're here I find I can't remember the good parts. Only the shape of him."

"The shape's enough," she said quietly. "I've only got the shape."

The lantern swung with the swell, throwing their shadows up the bulkhead, two figures leaning toward each other like conspirators, which, Elias supposed, was now precisely what they were.

He reached into the drawer beneath the chart table.

He had kept the letter in that drawer through two refits, one mutiny scare, a hurricane off the Sable Shoals, and three years of a blockade that was slowly strangling the harbor. He had kept it through the night his first mate, Marta's network, half the waterfront, and now apparently the whole machinery of the Meridian Trading Company had conspired to make a simple promise complicated. He had kept it the way Tom had pressed it into his hands on the last morning—quickly, casually, the way men hand over things they cannot bear to explain.

"If the company ever asks about my blood, give this to my girl."

That was what Tom had said. Elias had thought it a strange thing to say at the time. Tom Sterling was the least secretive man Elias had ever known; he'd narrate his own dreams at breakfast. But there had been something in his face that morning, something Elias had filed away under *diver's superstition* and never thought to look at again until it was too late to ask.

He set the letter on the table between them.

It was a plain envelope, oilcloth-wrapped, the wax seal unbroken, the paper gone soft and amber at the corners from years of handling. Elias had taken it out a thousand times and a thousand times had put it back. He'd held it up to lamplight once, in a weak moment, and seen only the ghost of folded pages inside, and had felt like such a scoundrel that he'd gone on deck in a gale and taken the wheel himself until dawn as penance.

Oscar stared at it.

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "You never opened it."

"Wasn't mine to open." He slid it toward her. "It was meant for you alone. Your father's words. I've carried them long enough; the rest of the journey is yours."

She didn't touch it. Her hand hovered over the oilcloth the way a diver's hand hovers over something glinting in deep water—wanting, wary, knowing that reaching changes everything.

"Eleven years," she said. "You carried it eleven years, through a blockade, with the company hunting you, and you never once—" Her voice caught. She pressed her lips together and tried again. "Why?"

Elias considered lying, or at least softening it. He decided Tom's daughter deserved better than soft.

"Because he asked," he said. "And because a man's word is the only cargo he can't insure. I've watched better sailors than me lose everything else—ships, fortunes, names. The word's the last thing to go, and when it goes, there's nothing left to salvage." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own sermon. "Also, I was afraid of it."

She looked up sharply.

"Afraid?"

"He gave it to me the morning of his last dive. He was laughing, joking about my terrible coffee, and then he pressed this into my hand and said that, and his eyes were—" Elias shook his head. "I've stood at a hundred gravesides and never once seen a man look the way he looked. Like he was reading something written on the far side of the water. I was afraid of what a man writes when he knows. So I kept it sealed, and I told myself I was honoring him, and maybe I was. Maybe I was just a coward with good manners."

The lantern swung. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the deck; Silas's voice murmured an order to the watch. The blockade chain groaned against its moorings out in the dark harbor, a sound Elias had almost stopped hearing, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

Oscar picked up the letter.

She held it in both hands, small as a bird, and Elias watched her thumb trace the wax seal—the same deep red as the thread he'd learned, from Silas's report, was tied around her file in the registry room. The company marked its investigations in red. Tom had sealed his last words in red too, though Elias doubted he'd meant anything by it. Or maybe he had. Tom had been a man who noticed things.

"He wrote my name on it," Oscar said. "Under the wax. Look."

Elias leaned in. There, in Tom's blunt sailor's hand, half-hidden by the fold of the oilcloth: *For Oscar, when she needs it. Not before.*

"When she needs it," Elias repeated. "He knew you'd be stubborn. He knew you'd try to get by without it."

"He knew me," she said, and there was a whole grief in it, compressed, the grief of a person who has been rationing herself for years.

"Open it," Elias said. "Or don't. It's yours either way. But I'll tell you this—" he tapped the table, "—whatever's in there, you don't have to read it alone. If you want me to look away, I'll look away. If you want me gone, I'll go stand in the rain, which is where I spend most of my time anyway, being a captain."

She didn't answer right away. She turned the letter over, once, twice, the way she might turn a shell in her fingers, reading its underside.

Then she said, "Read it with me."

Elias blinked. "It's meant for—"

"I know what it's meant for. I've been alone with my father's ghost for eleven years, Captain. I'm not doing this one alone too." She met his eyes, and hers were wet and furious about being wet. "Besides. If it's bad news, I'll want a witness. And if it's good news—" the ghost of the grin, Tom's grin, sudden and crooked, "—I'll want someone to tell."

Elias found his throat had gone tight. He nodded, because words had temporarily failed him, and that was a rare enough occurrence that Oscar almost smiled at it.

She broke the seal.

The wax parted with a small sound, like a knuckle cracking, and eleven years of waiting came undone in a second. She unfolded the oilcloth, then the outer paper, and inside was a packet—several sheets, folded small, and beneath them, wrapped around them like a second skin, something stiffer. Parchment. A document of some official weight, the kind of paper that had borders and stamps.

She drew out the letter first. Three pages, both sides, Tom's hand so dense and slanting it looked like rigging in a storm.

"Read it aloud," Elias said. "If you want. Or I can—"

"No. I'll read it." She steadied herself. "But you look too. I can feel you not looking, and it's worse."

So Elias looked, and she read, and the cabin was very quiet except for her voice and the sea.

*My Oscar,* it began.

*If you're reading this, then either I'm dead or the company has finally come asking questions it has no business asking, and I honestly don't know which would worry me more, because you are a girl who will take a death with better grace than an injustice.*

Elias heard her breath catch. He kept his eyes on the page.

*I have watched you grow up careful, my love, and I hate it. I know where you learned it. You learned it from watching what the water took from me and what the company gave back, which was nothing. You learned to make yourself small because small things don't get noticed and noticed things get taken. I can't scold you for it. I did the same, all my life, and it kept me alive, and it kept me a stranger in every room I ever stood in.*

*But I have to tell you something, and I need you to be brave in a different way than you've been brave so far. I need you, if the day comes, to be seen.*

Elias glanced up. Oscar had gone very still. She read on.

*You will find something wrapped around this letter. I took it from the *Meridian Rose* the night of the arms shipment, the one they told the harbor board was machine parts for the cannery. I was diving the wreck off Gull Point to check the pearl beds, and I found the transfer crate instead, and inside it, this. I am not a clever man, but I know a bill of lading when I've forged enough of them in my day, and I know whose signature is on it.*

*Governor Crane's.*

Elias felt the bottom drop out of the evening. The lantern light seemed suddenly very far away.

*I hid it in the reef where only a Sterling could reach it, and I kept a copy, and I am giving the copy to Elias Vane, because he is the only man I trust to keep a promise past the end of his own convenience. If the company ever asks about my blood, it will not be about my blood. It will be about this. They will come for you to get to the paper, or for the paper to get to me, and either way, my girl, you must know what you are carrying: not a keepsake. A weapon.*

*Use it when the moment is right, not before. And when it's done—when it's truly done—come home to the water. It's the only honest thing left on this coast.*

*Your father, who was proud of you every single day, including the days you made it very difficult,*

*Tom.*

Silence.

The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears like deep water. Elias became aware, distantly, of the ship's sounds continuing without them—the creak of timbers, the watch calling the half-hour, the blockade chain groaning—and of how completely none of it mattered.

Oscar set down the letter with hands that were not quite steady. Then, slowly, she unwrapped the stiff paper beneath it.

A bill of lading. Meridian Trading Company letterhead, the rose-and-anchor seal, columns of cargo: *crated machinery, 40 tons, consigned to Cannery No. 3.* And beneath the columns, in the space where the harbor board's countersignature should have been, a different hand entirely. Elegant. Confident. The signature of a man who had never once doubted that his paper would be believed.

*Edmund Crane, Governor.*

And a date. Elias did the arithmetic without wanting to. The date was eleven days before Tom Sterling drowned. Eleven days before a pearl diver who had witnessed an arms shipment under the governor's own signature went down into the water off Gull Point and did not come up.

"They said it was an accident," Oscar whispered. Her voice was flat, careful, the voice of a tally clerk reading figures. "The inquiry said his line fouled. They said the beds were dangerous that season. They said—" She stopped. Her thumb was pressed white against the edge of the parchment.

"Oscar." Elias kept his voice low, the way you speak to a diver coming up too fast. "We don't know that. Divers drown. It happens. It happened before Crane ever—"

"He *witnessed* it." She looked up at him, and her eyes were dry now, which was somehow worse than tears. "He wrote it himself. He found the crate. Eleven days, Captain. Eleven days later my father's line fouled in beds he'd worked since he was a boy, in water he knew better than the company's own charts, and every man on the salvage crew that day was on the company payroll, and the inquiry was closed in a week by a magistrate who—" she stopped, and Elias watched the arithmetic finish behind her eyes, watched it arrive at its terrible total. "Magistrate Hargrove's predecessor. Who was promoted. To the capital. Under Crane."

The lantern swung. Elias found that his own hands had closed into fists without his permission, and that he was looking at the bill of lading the way a man looks at a thing that has just rearranged the furniture of his whole world.

He had spent eleven years thinking he was carrying a dead friend's last words. A keepsake. A kindness. He had kept it sealed out of reverence, and all the while it had been sitting in his chart drawer like a powder keg in a bread box—enough, if it was what it appeared to be, to hang a governor. Enough to explain the red thread on a clerk's file. Enough to explain why the company wanted Oscar Sterling found, questioned, and quietly erased. She wasn't evidence of foreign blood.

She was evidence of murder.

"We have to get you off this ship," Elias said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Tonight. Before dawn. If Crane learns you were ever aboard—"

"He already knows I'm under investigation. The whole registry knows. The tail they set on me knows." She was still looking at the signature. "Captain. This isn't just my father's letter anymore. This is—" She looked up, and Elias saw it happen: the awe curdling, the wonder going gray at the edges, the moment when a gift reveals its price. "This is a death warrant. Whoever holds this is holding the thing Crane will kill to bury. My father knew. That's why he hid it in the reef. That's why he gave me only a copy. He wasn't being sentimental." Her voice cracked, just once. "He was being *careful*. He was trying to make sure that whatever happened to him, I'd have a weapon and they'd have nothing."

"And now we have everything," Elias said slowly, "and I'm no longer sure that's better."

Above them, boots on the deck—quick ones, more than one set. A knock at the cabin door, Silas's particular triple-rap, and then his voice, low and urgent through the wood:

"Captain. You'll want to come up. There's a launch in the water off our stern, no lights, and it's been sitting there since the girl came aboard. It's not moving on. It's *watching*."

Elias and Oscar looked at each other across the table, across the letter, across the governor's signature.

"Get the papers," Elias said, rising. "All of them. And pray, Miss Sterling, that whoever's in that launch is merely curious—because if Crane already knows what your father carried, then we haven't just found the truth."

He blew out the lantern.

"We've walked into the middle of it."
