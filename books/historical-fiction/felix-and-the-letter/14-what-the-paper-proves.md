## 14. What the Paper Proves

The dark on Elias's cabin was total for perhaps four heartbeats. Then Silas's knock came—three quick raps, their agreed signal—and the lantern bloomed again behind a shielded wick, low and orange as a coal.

Oscar had not moved. She was still looking at the place where the bill of lading lay on the table, though in the dark she could not see it. She found she did not need to. The thing had a weight now that had nothing to do with paper.

"Under the floorboards," Elias said quietly. "Silas, the aft section. Miss Sterling—the letter, if you please. Wrap it in the oilcloth first."

She did, her fingers steadier than she had any right to expect. There is a peculiar calm that arrives when a thing has gone too far to be undone, and Oscar had met it once before: at fourteen, in the company clerk's office, signing her name beneath the word *indentured* while a man with kind eyes and no power whatsoever looked away.

The board came up. The papers went down. Above them, boots crossed the deck—the watch changing, ordinary footsteps, though every one of them sounded like an accusation tonight.

"Now," Elias said, settling back into his chair as though he were about to discuss cargo tonnage, "let us consider what we are actually holding, before whoever is in that launch decides to come and hold it with us."

* * *

He laid it out the way he must have laid out a hundred voyages: plainly, in order, without decoration.

"One sheet of paper bearing Governor Crane's signature, authorizing the shipment of rifles under Meridian's flag, dated eleven years ago. One manifest showing those rifles logged as pearl-oil barrels, landed at Port Callow, which was officially receiving nothing but salt that year. And one letter, written by your father, enclosing both."

"Yes."

"Which means we hold proof that the governor of this colony armed a faction during the Border Troubles and let the company carry it for him. Proof that would end him. It would also—" Elias's voice stayed level, maddeningly level, the voice of a man reading tide tables— "end us, if we use it wrong."

Oscar frowned. "Wrong how? It's the truth."

"The truth is not a fish, Miss Sterling, that comes out of the water already cleaned." He almost smiled. "Ask yourself: to whom do we deliver it? A magistrate? Every company magistrate answers to Crane—he appoints them, he pays them, and Hargrove in particular strikes me as a man who has never once bit the hand holding the ledger. The colonial courts sit in Crane's pocket. The garrison takes its orders from Crane. The press here prints what Crane permits. If I walk this paper into any official hand in this colony, within the hour it will be in Crane's fire, and by midnight you and I will be in irons on charges of forging documents to slander His Excellency. Which, I promise you, is exactly the charge we would face. Traitors, forgers, conspirators. He'd hang us with the same rope and call it loyalty."

The cabin felt smaller. Oscar looked down at her hands—tally-clerk's hands, ink-stained, unremarkable—and thought about how many hours she had spent making herself into a person no one wrote things down about. Now there was a sheet of paper with her father's care folded into it that made her, potentially, the most written-about person in the colony.

"So we can't show anyone here," she said slowly. "And we can't keep it, because there's a launch sitting off your stern full of people who may already know it exists."

"Now you're thinking like a quartermaster," Elias said approvingly. "Yes. The paper cuts whoever holds it unless it reaches someone above Crane's reach entirely. And the only someones above Crane's reach are in the capital. Judges of the High Bench. The Colonial Secretary's own auditors. Perhaps certain newspapers there that his money hasn't bought—which is a shorter list than you'd hope."

"The capital." Oscar said it flatly. "Four hundred miles. Through waters he controls, into a city none of us know, past officials we've never met, carrying evidence against the most powerful man in the colony." She heard her own voice climb half an octave and reeled it back down. "Forgive me, Captain, but that isn't a plan. That's a funeral itinerary with extra steps."

For the first time all night, Elias laughed—a real laugh, short and rough, startled out of him. "Aye. That it may be. But consider the alternative." He tapped the floorboard over the hidden papers. "We sit on it. We wait. And Crane, who likely already knows, does not sit. He moves. First the launch outside becomes a boarding party. Then the Fairwind is impounded for 'irregular documentation'—there is always irregular documentation if a man like Hargrove looks long enough. Then your father's letter is found in my strongbox, entered as evidence of my treason, and used to bury the very truth it carries. The paper doesn't stop being dangerous because we refuse to swing it, Miss Sterling. It just starts cutting *us* instead."

* * *

They talked until the ship's bell marked two hours gone. Oscar kept expecting exhaustion to flatten her; instead her mind ran sharper, the way it always had over a stubborn column of figures that wouldn't balance. Somewhere past midnight she caught herself sketching on the back of a cargo slip—an old habit, mapping problems—and realized she'd drawn the harbor, the blockade chain, the coastline running north.

"It wants a chain of custody," she said, mostly to herself. "That's what Papa would've called it. Not one messenger carrying one paper—one paper can be seized, or doubted, or burned. Copies. In different hands, going different ways, so that seizing one only proves the others true."

Elias looked at her across the table with an expression she couldn't name at first. Then she could: it was the look of a man watching a ghost pour tea.

"You have his head," he said quietly. "Sterling used to talk like that. *Assume the sea will take anything you carry,* he'd say, *and pack accordingly.*"

"He said that about diving?"

"He said it about everything. Including letters." Elias's mouth twisted. "Which is why, I begin to suspect, there may be more in that envelope than even we've seen. Sterling never trusted a single copy of anything in his life."

But that was a thought for daylight. What they settled on, in the small hours, was this: nothing could move until they knew what the launch knew. If Crane's men had merely noticed a rowboat visiting a blockaded brig—if they suspected smuggling, or a rendezvous, but not the contents—that gave them days, maybe weeks. If a spy aboard had already read the papers, they had until sunrise.

"We need to know," Oscar said. "And we need to know whether anyone else on this crew knows what I came aboard carrying. Because Captain—I'm sorry, but I counted faces tonight, and more than one man looked at this table before he looked away."

Elias was quiet a long moment. "Kerr," he said at last, heavily, saying the name like a weight set down. "My boatswain. Twenty years. He's been strange since the blockade edicts—talks of pensions, of how a man's earned his rest. I'd tell myself it was fear talking, nothing more." He rubbed his jaw. "I'll watch him. Silas watches the rail. And you—"

"Me?"

"You go home before dawn, as planned. You walk through those gates as the grayest little tally clerk the company ever hired, and you learn what's moving on *that* side of the water. Hargrove summoned you once. Men like Hargrove summon twice."

"And if they search me on the way out of here?"

Elias reached under the table and produced something she hadn't seen him take: a small packet, oilskin again, flat as a hymnal. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, a single page—not the bill of lading, not the manifest, but a careful copy in Silas's printer-perfect hand of the manifest alone, marked in pencil along the margin with three dates.

"A decoy," Oscar said.

"A decoy with teeth. If you're taken, they find a document that proves nothing on its own—but enough to make them believe they've got the whole of it. Men stop hunting when they think they've finished the hunt." He held her gaze. "It's a risk. Carrying anything is a risk. But a girl rowed out here tonight and rowed back empty, well—that's worth wondering about too. Better you're caught with a plausible secret than an inexplicable errand."

Oscar took the packet and slid it inside her boot, against her calf, where the shape disappeared into the line of her stocking. Her father had taught her that trick, actually—for hiding coins from dormitory thieves. It seemed fitting that his lessons should come in useful for treason.

* * *

The row back was longer than the row out. Everything is longer at the end of a night; the oars weighed double, the cold came up through the boat boards, and Silas, pulling the second oar, kept glancing over his shoulder at the dark shape of the launch, which had finally, mercifully, drifted off toward the chain around one o'clock—or pretended to.

"Do you think they saw us?" Oscar asked, between strokes.

"I think," Silas said, breathing carefully, "that if they saw us clearly, we'd already be boarded. I think seeing and knowing are different animals, and somebody's going to have to reconcile them in a report by morning." He was young—younger than she'd guessed on the crossing out, maybe twenty, with ink still faintly staining the creases of his knuckles despite five years at sea. "Captain says you're a clerk."

"Tally clerk. Eleven years."

"Then you'll appreciate this." He nodded at the boot where the packet hid. "I copied that document three times tonight. Once for the captain's strongbox, once for the oilskin you're wearing, once to burn. And the whole time I kept thinking—in the print shop, copying was the bottom rung. The master never let apprentices touch original plates. Copies were what you gave the cheap customers." He shook his head, half a grin in the darkness. "Turns out copies are the whole revolution. Nobody burns a revolution by burning one copy."

"Grief makes philosophers of us all," Oscar said dryly, and was rewarded with a real laugh, quickly smothered against the oar handle.

But underneath the joke, the thought lodged in her and would not come loose. *Nobody burns a revolution by burning one copy.* And the reverse of it, colder: nobody stops a revolution by catching one courier either. She was, right now, expendable by design. The knowledge was meant to comfort her. Mostly it made her feel like a coin her father might have hidden in a boot—small, valuable, and passed hand to hand by people with plans of their own.

The city wall rose out of the pre-dawn fog, and beyond it, faintly, the smell of the fish market waking up. Marta's stall would be lighting its lamps about now. Oscar's chest tightened. Marta, who had carried one message and was already being watched; Marta, whose whole livelihood hung from the same thread Oscar was now weaving into a net.

"Here," Silas murmured, shipping oars as the boat kissed the pilings under the salt sheds. "Same ladder. Mind the wet stone."

She paused, one hand on the lowest rung.

"Silas. The letter—my father's letter. When I asked the captain to open it with me, I thought... I don't know what I thought. That it would be words. Forgiveness, maybe, or instructions about where he buried the good brandy." She heard her voice go unsteady and hated it. "Instead it's a weapon. And I can't decide whether Papa left me a gift or a curse."

Silas considered this with the seriousness of the young.

"My mother left me a recipe box," he said. "After she died. And inside it, under the recipe cards, was her savings book and a debt note my father had signed and never told her about. First thing I ever read in it was proof the man I loved had been lying for six years." He shrugged. "People leave us the truth because it's all they have that lasts. Doesn't make it comfortable. Still counts as love, I think. Maybe especially then."

Oscar looked at him for a moment. Then she climbed.

* * *

The wall gate took her back without comment—the sentry knew her face, or knew her category, which amounted to the same thing—and by half past four she was walking the long gray avenue toward the company dormitories with her wet hem slapping at her ankles and her mind running its columns.

The problem arranged itself, as problems did, into a ledger:

*Debit:* one document proving treason, currently in her boot, worthless everywhere within four hundred miles.
*Credit:* several copies, location distributed, plus whatever else Papa might have hidden in that envelope that they hadn't yet examined.
*Outstanding:* one governor who might or might not know, one launch whose report she couldn't read, one boatswain whose strangeness the captain refused to fully weigh, one fishwife already touched by the danger, and one census taker who had watched her file with more interest than a counting-man ought to spare.

And one more entry, small, that she almost didn't write down: the summons. Elias was right. Men like Hargrove summoned twice. And if the second summons came—if it came today, with the launch's report fresh on his desk and her absence-from-bed a matter of record to anyone who cared to check—

She stopped walking.

Ahead of her, at the corner by the chandlery, a figure stood under the lamp that was always lit there before dawn. Thin. Correct. Holding a black satchel with brass clasps, and consulting, of all things, a pocket notebook, as though the street itself were a population to be enumerated.

Mr. Aldous Finch raised his head. Their eyes met across forty feet of empty cobbled morning.

He didn't smile. He didn't frown. He simply looked at her—wet-hemmed, salt-stiff, arriving from the direction of the harbor at an hour no tally clerk had any business keeping—and then, with terrible gentleness, made a small mark in his notebook, closed it, and walked away.

Oscar stood very still in the middle of the avenue and understood, with the clarity of a column finally summing, that her invisibility had not cracked.

It had shattered—and somewhere behind her eyes, she could hear the sound of a pen beginning to count the pieces.
