## 15. Return Before Dawn

The row back was harder than the row out.

Silas had warned her it would be. "The tide turns against you around three," he'd said, shipping the oars with the easy economy of a man who had done this a hundred times and would do it a hundred more. "Water doesn't care what you're carrying. It only cares that you're slow."

So Oscar rowed. She rowed until her palms blistered and her shoulders screamed and the harbor resolved itself out of the darkness in gray slabs and black masts, and all the while she kept her eyes on the lantern of the customs house, which was the fixed point Silas had told her to keep. *Steer by the light that doesn't move,* he'd said. *Everything else lies.*

She had left the Fairwind an hour before, after a night that had rearranged the furniture of her entire life. Elias Vane. The letter. The bill of lading with Governor Crane's own signature folded inside it like a knife in a bread loaf. Her father, it turned out, had not simply been a pearl diver who drowned; he had been a witness, and the sea had kept his testimony for six years, waiting for his daughter to come and collect it.

She had not said any of this out loud on the row back. There was no one to say it to. Silas had put her over the side at the coaler's mooring with a grip on her wrist that was half farewell and half warning, and said, "Walk slow. Wet hems hang people in this town."

*Wet hems hang people in this town.* She turned the phrase over as she climbed the sea-stairs, testing its weight. It was meant kindly. It was also entirely true.

* * *

The city at four in the morning was a different animal from the city she knew. By day it was a machine of noise and commerce, all winches and shouting and the smell of tar and frying fish. At this hour it was a sleeping animal, breathing slow, and Oscar moved through its ribs like something small and quick that had learned long ago not to wake it.

She knew the back ways the way she knew her own hands. The alley behind the chandlery where the cat with the torn ear kept a kingdom of fish heads. The gap between the cooper's yard and the salt sheds where a girl of her size could pass sideways without disturbing a single barrel. The drainpipe on the dormitory's north wall that she had climbed a hundred times in her mind and never once in her body—until tonight, apparently, was to be the night for first things.

She had planned it, of course. She always planned. That was the whole architecture of her survival: plan, proceed, return, and be unremarkable by morning. Roll call in the women's dormitory was at six. She would be in her cot by half past five, her boots dried and her stockings hung behind the boiler where the damp would look like ordinary sweat, and by seven she would be at her tally desk with her ink-stained fingers and her lowered eyes, and no one—not Hargrove, not the matron, not God himself—would know that the quiet little clerk had spent the night aboard a blockaded brig holding papers that could hang a governor.

That was the plan. The plan was good. The plan had survived contact with patrol boats, a suspicious harbor watch, and her own terror, which had at one point somewhere off the mole become so complete that she had seriously considered lying down in the bottom of the boat and letting the tide take her where it liked.

The plan failed at the coaler.

Not in any way she could have foreseen. The coaler's mate, a Welshman named Prosser with a face like a fist, had simply been awake—sitting on his hatch cover with a pipe, watching the water, because he was the sort of man who could not sleep in harbor. He had watched her come up the ladder. He had watched her wring out her hem. And he had said, in a voice of profound unconcern, "Cold night for a swim."

"I fell in," Oscar said.

"Aye," said Prosser, drawing on his pipe. "Fell in. Off a boat, I'd say. A rowboat, from the way your hands are blistered." He looked at her for a long moment, and she braced herself for the whistle, the watch, the end of everything. Then he said, "My girl's about your age. In Cardiff. Works in the tinworks." He knocked out his pipe against the rail. "Mind how you go."

And that was all. She walked away from the coaler with her heart hammering and her mind already composing the story she would tell if it ever mattered—*fell from the stairs at the fish market, the ice, you know how it is*—and it was only three streets later that she understood what Prosser had actually given her, and had to stop in the shadow of the chandlery and press her hand over her mouth until the shaking stopped.

Kindness. Unasked, unearned, from a stranger. The world was full of it, apparently, hidden in odd corners like coins in a mattress. She had simply never gone looking before.

* * *

The dormitory wall took her longer than it should have. Her arms had nothing left; her fingers closed on the drainpipe and simply refused to negotiate. She hung there in the dark, three feet off the ground, contemplating the unique humiliation of being caught not by the company watch but by gravity, and then she thought of her father—of his hands on the pearl lines, of the way he'd told her once that the sea only takes the ones who hurry—and she climbed.

The dormitory was a long room of forty cots, and it breathed. That was the thing nobody who hadn't slept in such a room could understand: it had a sound, a collective tide of snoring and sighing and the creak of bed frames, and into that sound Oscar folded herself like a coin into a purse. Her cot was by the wall, third from the window. She toed off her boots. She hung her stockings behind the boiler. She lay down.

And then, in the dark, with her body finally still, her mind would not be still at all.

She saw the bill of lading again. She had read it twice by Elias's lantern, and both times her eye had snagged on the same line—the same impossible, damning line—*Shipment consigned per order of Gov. A. Crane, to be landed at the salt sheds, manifest suppressed.* The salt sheds. She had walked past the salt sheds every day of her working life. She had pressed her shoulder against their walls to squeeze through the gap between the barrels. Her father's last dive had been within sight of them.

*If the company ever asks about my blood, give this to my girl.* Papa had known. Not everything, maybe, but enough. Enough to seal a letter and put it in the hands of the one man he trusted, and enough to hide a manifest in the reef like a pearl in an oyster, and enough to teach his daughter to be invisible without ever telling her why.

She had thought invisibility was her own invention. Her own clever trick, learned young, perfected over years. Now, lying in the dark listening to forty women breathe, she wondered if it had been an inheritance all along. A coat he'd made for her before she was born, because he knew what was coming and couldn't stop it, only dress her for it.

The thought was so large and so sad that she had to lie very still and breathe through it, the way you breathe through a cramp, until it passed.

* * *

She did sleep, in the end. Not enough, and badly, but some—the body's mercy, which does not consult the mind about whether this is an appropriate time. She woke to the matron's bell and the gray light of five o'clock, and she went through the motions: wash, dress, queue, roll call.

"Sterling."

"Here," she said, to her own shoes.

The matron's eye passed over her and moved on. Unremarkable. Successful. She felt the little victory settle into her chest like a warm coal, and she carried it down the hill to the registry, through streets just waking, and she was actually—actually—beginning to believe the night had been survived cleanly when she saw the census taker.

Mr. Aldous Finch was standing at the corner of Quay Lane and the fish market steps, consulting a small notebook, and there was nothing remarkable in that, because Finch was always consulting a small notebook; the man seemed to conduct his entire existence through it, eating and walking and presumably sleeping with one hand on the page. What was remarkable was that he looked up as she passed. What was remarkable was that his gaze went, with the unhurried precision of a man reading a ledger, from her face to her hem.

Her hem, which she had dried behind the boiler, and which had therefore dried *wrong*—stiff with salt, ridged with a tide line of white mineral crust that any dockside child could have named for what it was. Seawater dries differently than wash water. Seawater leaves a signature.

Finch looked at the hem. Then he looked at her. Then he took out his pencil, made a small mark in his notebook, put the pencil away, and said, in a voice of mild administrative interest:

"Good morning, Miss Sterling."

"Good morning, Mr. Finch," said Oscar, and kept walking, at exactly the speed of a woman with nothing whatsoever on her mind, which required more concentration than she had ever spent on anything, including arithmetic.

She did not look back. Looking back was for amateurs. She walked the last two hundred yards to the registry door, climbed the stairs, sat at her desk, opened the day's ledger, and dipped her pen.

Her hand was steady. She noted this with detached professional interest, the way one notes the weather. Her hand was steady and her pen was moving and the numbers were coming out correct, column after column after column, and behind all of it, running underneath like groundwater, was a single thought, repeating:

*He saw. He saw and he wrote it down.*

* * *

The morning went on. Mornings, in her experience, always did; it was one of their most irritating qualities. Cargo came up the gangways and she counted it. Barrels of salt pork, cases of cotton, a shipment of Manila hemp whose invoice disagreed with itself in a way she would normally have flagged and did not, because her attention had been entirely consumed by the image of a small pencil mark in a small notebook.

What did he write? That was the question that would not leave her alone. She turned it over and over like a stone in the pocket. *Sterling, O., hem damp, 5:40 a.m., Quay Lane.* Was that what it said? Or something worse? Or something better? Or nothing at all—was it possible the mark had meant nothing, that Finch marked things the way other men coughed, compulsively, meaninglessly, a tic of the profession?

No. She had looked at his face. There had been nothing in it, which was itself information. She knew that look; she wore it herself, professionally, forty hours a week. It was the look of a man who has noticed something and decided, for the moment, to file it rather than say it.

The question was: file it where? And: until when?

By noon she had constructed and demolished four separate theories. Theory one: Finch was a company man and would carry his observation straight to Hargrove, in which case by evening there would be watchmen at the dormitory and her night's work would be ash. Theory two: Finch was a harmless obsessive, a collector of trivialities, and the mark would molder in his notebook among ten thousand other trivialities until the end of time. Theory three: Finch was something else entirely—she didn't know what, but the man gave her a itch she couldn't scratch, a sense of a locked drawer in an otherwise open desk.

Theory four she did not let herself think until the afternoon, when the light through the registry window had gone long and gold and the day's tally was nearly done. Theory four was the one that made her put down her pen.

Theory four was that Aldous Finch had seen a wet hem on a clerk at dawn, and had understood exactly what it meant—because Finch, who counted people for a living, who had written down her father's birthplace in his careful hand and thereby started this entire avalanche—Finch had *known*, and had said nothing, and had made his small mark, and had gone on his way. And the question of what that meant was a question she could not answer from behind a tally desk, and could not stop asking, and would very likely lie awake asking until it drove her mad.

She looked down at the ledger. In the margin of the hemp shipment's page, without any memory of doing it, she had written a single small word in her own careful hand:

*Finch.*

She looked at it for a long moment. Then she did not cross it out. She was, she reflected, becoming a person who kept files now. It seemed to be going around.

* * *

She took the long way to the dormitory that evening, which was to say she took three wrong turns on purpose and doubled back twice, and watched reflections in shop glass, and by these arts satisfied herself that no one was following her—except that this was exactly what a person being followed would hope, and the thought circled back on itself until she gave up and simply walked.

Marta's stall was shuttered. It had been shuttered since the arrest, and the sight of it still landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. Someone had chalked a fish on the boards—a little mackerel, drawn quick and sure. The fishwives' sign, Silas had told her: *we see you, we remember.* She stood in front of it for a moment in the evening crowd, an unremarkable woman looking at a shuttered stall, and then she went on.

The dormitory. The queue. The bell. The cot.

She lay in the dark and did the thing she had decided, somewhere around the hemp invoice, that she must do: she reviewed the day as a ledger. Entries only, no feelings. *Item: returned before dawn, undetected by watch. Item: observed by Finch, hem, 5:40 a.m. Item: no action taken by Finch as of 8 p.m. Item: Marta still held. Item: Elias still afloat. Item: letter still unopened—no.* She corrected herself in the dark. *Item: letter opened. Item: what it proves still burning a hole in the world.*

And item, the last one, the one she saved for the bottom of the page where the totals went: *Item: the census taker knows something, and has not yet said it.*

He would say it. Everyone said it, eventually; silence in this city was never a wall, only a door held shut by someone's hand, and hands got tired. The question—the question that bloomed in her like ink in water as she finally, finally drifted toward sleep—was not *whether* Aldous Finch would come to her with that small pencil mark.

It was whether, when he came, he would come as a hammer or as a hand.

She dreamed of the reef that night, and of her father's hands on the pearl lines, and of a small notebook sinking slowly through green water, its pages opening one by one, every page bearing her name.
