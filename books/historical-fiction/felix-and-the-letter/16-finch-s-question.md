## 16. Finch's Question

Aldous Finch had been counting things since before he could properly name them, which meant he had been counting things for nearly forty years, and in that time he had learned one great truth about the world: everything, given enough attention, confesses.

Numbers confessed most readily. A merchant's ledger that rounded every figure *down* to the nearest convenient sum; a harbor tax roll where three ships of identical tonnage paid wildly different duties; a census column where a widow's household shrank by two souls the very year her late husband's brother came into an inheritance. Finch collected these confessions the way other men collected stamps or grievances—quietly, systematically, and with a pleasure he did not entirely examine.

He kept them all in a garret room above a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, in notebooks arranged by year, then by district, then by the particular *flavor* of the irregularity. He labeled these flavors himself, since no official taxonomy existed: *shaved tonnage*, *ghost sailors*, *widows' arithmetic*. There was a shelf devoted entirely to Meridian Trading Company filings, and it was, he often thought while climbing his narrow stairs with a candle, the fullest shelf in the room.

His landlady believed him to be a hobbyist entomologist. He saw no reason to correct her. In a sense he was.

The irregularity of Oscar Sterling occupied a green notebook, third from the left on the Meridian shelf, and Finch thought about it more than was strictly reasonable. It was not a large irregularity. It would not have troubled a lesser man at all. But Finch had spent four days after his visit to the company dormitories turning it over like a coin of suspicious mint, and the longer he held it up to the light, the more it refused to behave.

Here was the trouble: the girl had lied to him.

This was unremarkable in itself. People lied to census takers the way gulls lied to each other about fish—constantly, reflexively, without malice. Nine out of ten households adjusted something. Ages shaved, trades elevated, bastards folded into nephews. Finch catalogued the lies and moved on; they were the small change of his profession.

No—the trouble was *how* she had lied.

She had told him her father was born in the capital. A clean, sensible lie, delivered with lowered eyes and busy hands. But her hands had been mending a stocking at the time, and she had finished the row *exactly* as the lie ended—a small human rhythm interrupted by nothing, because there was nothing inside her that believed what her mouth said. And when Finch had written his note—noting only that the father's place of birth was *unverified*, a mark he made perhaps six times in a hundred entries—she had looked at the pencil, not at his face. Guilty people watched faces. Innocent people watched faces too. Only people who understood precisely what a record *was*—what it could do, where it traveled, who might someday pull it—watched the pencil.

That was not the look of someone hiding a bastard nephew. That was the look of someone who knew the machinery.

And then, this morning, the second confession had arrived, unbidden, wearing wet boots.

Finch rose early—he always rose early; sleep, in his experience, was merely counting you did lying down—and took his usual walk along the harbor before his rounds. He liked to count the fishing boats out, though he never wrote the numbers down. It was purely for pleasure, like a man humming.

At the foot of the coal-stairs near the north wall, he had seen a girl come up out of the pre-dawn gray, moving fast, head down, hem soaked to the knee, salt dried white along the seam of her skirt. She had passed within six feet of him. She had not seen him; nobody ever saw Aldous Finch, which he considered a professional accomplishment and a private sorrow in roughly equal measure.

She had come off the water. Before dawn. Through, if his knowledge of the blockade chain was correct—which it was; he had counted its links once, out of curiosity, and found the company had reported eleven more than existed—past the patrol line itself.

And the girl had been Sterling. The tally clerk. The liar with the steady hands.

* * *

The garret smelled of paper and lamp oil and, faintly, of the chandler's stock of tallow drifting up through the floorboards, which gave the whole room the atmosphere of a very honest candle. Finch sat at his slanted desk with the green notebook open before him and made a new entry in his small, upright hand:

*S. Sterling, O. — observed returning from harbor quarter, 05:40, garments wet below the knee. No legitimate traffic crosses the chain between dusk and dawn. Conclusion pending.*

He looked at "conclusion pending" for a long moment and disliked it. He disliked unfinished sentences the way some men disliked crooked pictures. But the truth was he did not yet know what conclusion he was reaching toward, and Finch had a personal rule against writing conclusions he hadn't earned. A man who wrote conclusions first was not a counter. He was a clerk. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of his life.

The red thread bothered him too. Not the girl's thread—that was none of his doing; the registry flagged its own investigations and he merely transcribed what households told him—but the *shape* of it. In nineteen years of taking the census, he had developed a private theory: the government counted people the way a butcher counted carcasses, by weight and by use. When a file sprouted a red thread, it meant the person had stopped being a person and become a *useful weight*. Finch had transcribed the flags of eleven red-threaded souls over the years. Eight were dead now. One had vanished. Two worked, last he checked, in the company's own counting house, with the careful blankness of men who had learned what pencils could do.

He did not wish to add Sterling to the list. This surprised him. He examined the surprise the way he examined all data—turned it over, weighed it, noted that it was heavier than expected.

It occurred to him, somewhere past midnight, candle guttering, that what he actually wanted was not her file. Her file was a fact; facts were cheap. What he wanted was to know whether she was a carcass or a person, and no registry in the empire recorded that distinction.

There was only one way to find out. He would have to do the thing he was worst at in all the world.

He would have to have a conversation.

* * *

He chose the fish market for it, partly for the crowd—witnesses made conversations honest, in theory—and partly because Tuesday was the slow day, when the mackerel was gone and the haddock hadn't come in, and a man could stand still without obstructing commerce. He wore his good coat, which was indistinguishable from his ordinary coat except to him, and he carried his satchel, because a census taker without his satchel was a priest without vestments and besides, the notebook inside steadied him the way a railing steadies a man on a high deck.

He found her at the tally shed, checking crates with a stub of pencil behind her ear. She counted the way he imagined she did everything: efficiently, invisibly, as though the world might fine her for being noticed.

"Sterling," he said. "Miss Sterling. Good morning."

She turned, and he watched the sequence cross her face like weather: recognition, alarm, suppression of the alarm, and finally a smooth gray politeness laid over the whole of it like paint over damp plaster. It was, he had to admit, excellent work. It would have fooled anyone.

It fooled no one, because he already had the wet boots.

"Mr. Finch." Her voice was flat and pleasant, a coin rubbed smooth. "Have I a form outstanding?"

"No forms today." He positioned himself beside the crate stack with the studied casualness of a man who had never in his life been casual. "I'm—hm. I am revisiting certain households. For accuracy. The bureau prizes accuracy."

"The bureau prizes numbers."

"Yes," Finch said, with feeling. "Yes, it does. Numbers are easier. Numbers don't ask anything back."

She blinked at that—one quick blink—and he realized he had startled her by agreeing, which was not the effect he'd intended but was possibly a useful one. Honesty, deployed unexpectedly, functioned rather like a flash of light on water: it showed what swam beneath.

"I'll be brief," he said. "I've been doing this work for nineteen years. I count houses, and hearths, and souls, and trades, and ages, and I record where everyone's fathers were born, and I will tell you quite plainly that in nineteen years I have learned that almost nobody tells me the truth, and that this is fine, because the truth is rarely what the ledger is for." He paused, gathered himself. He was, he knew, proceeding badly. He always proceeded badly. Conversations did not come in rows one could finish neatly. "But I have also learned to tell when the lie matters. And yours, Miss Sterling, mattered. To somebody. Not to me. To somebody."

The dockyard noise went on around them—gulls, winches, a mate cursing a crane—but between the two of them there opened a pocket of stillness, and Finch had the peculiar sense of standing in a glass bell with her, both of them watching each other through curved and distorting walls.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Your father," Finch said gently. "Where was he born?"

"Port Anselm. In the capital. As I told you."

"Mm," said Finch. He took out his pencil, and he did not write anything, and he watched her watch the pencil. There it was again—the flick of her eyes downward, the tracker's eye, the eye that knew what graphite on paper could cost a person. "You know, most people, when I ask that question twice, get angry. Anger is honest. Indignation is honest. Even fear, properly worn, is honest—it says *I am afraid of the wrong answer*, which tells me there is a wrong answer, which is itself information. But you've done neither. You've simply repeated yourself. Like a woman reading a sign."

"And what does that make me, Mr. Finch?"

"I don't know yet," he said, and meant it so plainly that something in her face shifted—the paint cracking, just slightly, at one corner. "That is what I have come to ask. Not for the book." He tapped the satchel. "The book can go hang. I keep my own records, Miss Sterling, and my records are mine, and they are kept in a room above a chandlery on Rope Walk, and they are the only files in this city, I suspect, whose purpose is that I should *know things* rather than that somebody should *use* them. I count people. It has recently come to my attention that in nineteen years, you may be the first one I've actually wanted to count correctly."

Silence. A gull screamed somewhere overhead, outraged about something, as gulls were.

"You're mad," she said at last, but softly, the way one tests ice.

"Frequently. Obsession will do that." He sighed and adjusted his spectacles. "I'll tell you a thing I don't tell anyone. When I was nine years old, my family was missed by a census. Entirely missed. The taker walked past our door—my mother had died that spring, and the house looked shut-up, and he couldn't be troubled to knock—and so we did not exist, officially, for ten years. My father couldn't claim relief funds during the famine winter because there was no relief for men who weren't on the rolls. My sister went hungry in a country that had mislaid us." He looked out at the harbor, at the ships sitting fat and patient on their reflections. "I became a census taker so that no one else would be invisible. And I discovered, in time, that the counting is used for the opposite. That the rolls are a net, and I am the one holding the pole. So I keep my own books. Every irregularity. Every shaved tonnage, every ghost sailor, every name that vanishes from a dormitory roster the week after a magistrate takes an interest. Someday—I don't know when or how—those books are going to matter. And when that day comes, Miss Sterling, I should like to know which side of my own ledger you belong on."

She was looking at him now fully, and it was the strangest sensation of his adult life—like being counted himself, itemized, entered into some private register behind those gray eyes. He stood straight under the audit. Let her put him in her book. Fair was fair.

"My father," she said slowly, "was born across the water. On the pearl coast."

The words fell into the space between them, and Finch felt them land with the particular gravity of a stone dropped into a well he'd been listening to for weeks.

"And your mother?" he asked, keeping his voice level, though his pulse had begun doing something undignified.

"Died when I was small. Why do you care? You said the book can go hang."

"The book can go hang," Finch agreed. "But I cannot. Not until I understand why a tally clerk with a drowned father and a foreign birthright is wading out of the harbor before dawn with salt on her skirts, while the registry office runs a red thread through her file and Magistrate Hargrove—" he caught himself, but only barely; some sentences escape even the careful—"has pulled eleven other files this month, all of them dock families, all of them independent-fleet kin, none of them, I note, any actual threat to anything but somebody's ledgers."

Her breath went shallow. "How do you know about Hargrove's files?"

"Because I read the returns, Miss Sterling. Nobody reads the returns. That is my entire advantage in this world: I read the things nobody reads." He closed his empty hand around the pencil, a habit of his when excited, and heard it creak faintly. "I am not your enemy. I am possibly the least dangerous man in this city, which is a low bar but I clear it honestly. All I want—"

What he wanted, he found, he could not say cleanly. He wanted the pattern completed. He wanted the sentence finished. He wanted, absurdly, to be useful, which was a hunger he had never once permitted himself to name aloud, and here he was, nearly naming it to a stranger by a fish stall on a Tuesday morning.

"All I want," he tried again, "is to be told the truth once. Before someone uses it against you. Because they will, you know. They're going to. Whatever it is you're carrying—and you are carrying something, I've watched enough smugglers' shoulders to know the posture—they will find it, and when they do, the only thing standing between you and a red thread pulled tight will be whatever record exists of who you actually are. And I keep," he said, "the only honest records in this city."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, quietly, with the weariness of someone setting down a load only to pick up a heavier one:

"If I told you—if I told you any of it—you couldn't write it down. Not anywhere. Not in your garret, not in your little notebooks. Knowing it would be dangerous. Do you understand? People who know things near me have started coming to harm."

Something cold walked down Finch's spine, and he recognized it, because he had met it before, in famine winters and missing-household files. Fear, but not for himself—for the first time in many years, for someone else.

"People," he repeated carefully. "Which people?"

But her face had closed again, the paint reapplied, smooth and gray, and he understood with sinking certainty that he had gotten exactly as far as trust could carry him in one conversation, and that the rest of the distance would have to be bought with something rarer.

"Thank you for your time, Mr. Finch," said Oscar Sterling, in her coin-smoothed voice. "If the bureau needs me, you know where the dormitory is."

She turned back to her crates. Dismissed, politely, completely.

Finch stood a moment longer, then touched his hat brim and withdrew, walking back along the quay with his satchel bumping his hip, his mind running ahead of him like a dog scenting rain. Across the water the blockade chain glinted, and beyond it, riding at anchor under the white flag of impound proceedings, sat a brig he had entered into his private books eleven months ago under the heading *tonnage discrepancy — Fairwind — see also: Governor's discretionary waivers, q.v.*—a note he had made long before any of this began, back when it had been only a curiosity, only a number refusing to sit still.

*See also*, he thought. Everything confessed, eventually. The trick—the art, the whole vocation—was in the cross-references.

He was halfway up Rope Walk, already composing the evening's entry in his head, when he noticed the boy waiting by the chandler's door: one of Marta's fish-market runners, cap in hand, shifting from foot to foot.

"You're the counting gentleman?" the boy said. "Marta sends word. She says—she says they took her this morning, and she says you should tell the Sterling girl the net's closing, and she says—" the boy screwed up his face, reciting a message that had clearly been drilled into him through tears—"she says *don't come for me, come for the letter*."

Finch stood very still on his own doorstep, the key forgotten in his hand.

Then he went inside, took down the green notebook, and began, in his small upright hand, to earn his conclusion.
