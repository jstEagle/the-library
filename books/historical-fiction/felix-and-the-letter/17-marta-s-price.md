## 17. Marta's Price

The news came the way bad news always comes to people like me—not with trumpets, but with a gap. A missing thing. An absence shaped like a woman.

I had gone to Marta's stall at the morning market out of pure habit, carrying two coins and an appetite for her smoked herring, which she claimed—loudly, to anyone within earshot—was the only herring in the harbor fit for human consumption. "The rest of them smoke fish like they're punishing it," she liked to say. "Like the fish owes them money."

But when I came around the corner by the customs house, where her stall had stood every morning for as long as I could remember, there was nothing. Not a table, not an awning, not a barrel. Just the pale rectangle of clean cobbles where the stall had sat for years, and old Tom Braddock selling turnips in the space beside it with the studied blankness of a man pretending very hard not to see anything at all.

"Marta's ill," he said to no one in particular, before I'd even asked.

"Tom."

"Terribly ill. Contagious." He arranged his turnips with tremendous concentration. "You shouldn't stand so close, Miss Sterling."

There were perhaps four feet between us. I understood him completely and hated understanding him.

"What happened?"

He didn't answer. He picked up a turnip, examined it as though it had personally disappointed him, and put it back down. Around us, the market went on with its ordinary roar—fishwives shouting prices, gulls committing their usual crimes—but there was a pocket of quiet around Tom's stall, a careful deafness. Nobody looked my way. Everybody was very busy not looking my way, which is its own kind of looking.

I walked home through the market with empty hands and that cold, dropping feeling in my chest, the one I remembered from the day they pulled my father's coat out of the water and I knew, before anyone said a word, what it meant.

* * *

By afternoon, the shape of the absence had filled in.

It came to me at the tally desk, as everything did eventually, because Meridian Trading was a company that believed all information belonged on paper and all paper belonged to them. A runner brought the internal circular to Mr. Prew, who read it, went slightly gray, and then passed it down the row of clerks the way you'd pass a dead rat—everyone touching it briefly and wanting it gone.

Detained, pending inquiry: one Marta Okafor, fishwife, on suspicion of conveying intelligence to enemies of the crown. Any person holding information regarding said individual's associations should present themselves to Magistrate Hargrove's office without delay.

*Conveying intelligence.* As if Marta, who could not read and signed her name with a fistful of crooked crosses, had been caught passing coded dispatches to a foreign fleet. As if her network of women who knew whose husband was drunk, whose daughter was with child, and which patrol boat took its tea at four o'clock sharp constituted a web of espionage.

Well. I suppose it did, in a way. That was rather the point.

Mr. Prew caught my eye across the row—a flicker of it, quickly shut down. He was a small, damp man with ink-stained cuffs and a conscience he kept mostly in a drawer, but even he couldn't quite meet me without flinching. Everyone in that office knew. That was the design of it. Hargrove hadn't arrested Marta despite the office knowing about me; he'd done it *because* they knew. The circular wasn't information. It was a message, addressed to exactly one person, delivered via everyone else.

*We have your friend. Come and be useful.*

I finished my column of figures. My hand did not shake, because hands are loyal creatures and mine had been trained since childhood to keep working no matter what the rest of me was doing. Then I asked leave to step out for air, and Mr. Prew granted it with suspicious speed, and I walked out into the gray drizzle of the harbor afternoon and let myself shake where nobody could see.

* * *

I want to be honest here, because this is the part of the story where honesty costs something, and if I fudge it now I'll fudge it everywhere after.

My first thought was not *how do I save her.*

My first thought was *she told them about the salt sheds.* Because Marta was the one who carried Silas's message to me; Marta pointed me toward the meeting place; Marta had been the thread connecting me to the Fairwind from the very beginning, threading quietly back and forth through the market stalls while the company watched the obvious routes. Of course they'd pull that thread. Of course. I had known, in some chamber of myself that I kept carefully unlit, that touching other lives meant handing other people to the fire. And the moment I read that circular, that unlit chamber lit up all at once, and my very first thought—the thought before thought, the animal thought—was *thank God it wasn't worse,* followed instantly by shame so hot I tasted it.

Because it could have been worse. And it would get worse. And the arithmetic of it was simple and obscene: Marta was in a company cell because I had wanted a letter delivered, and every hour I spent being invisible was an hour she spent paying my bill.

I stood under the eave of the counting house and watched the rain stitch the harbor flat, and I thought about my father's philosophy of water. *The sea doesn't hate you,* he used to tell me, hauling me up from my first clumsy dives. *It just doesn't notice you. You make yourself noticeable, or you don't come up.* I had built my whole life on the second half of that sentence. Be small. Be gray. Let the sea not notice.

But Marta had noticed me. That was the whole trouble. She'd fished me out of the dormitory line when I was fourteen and motherless twice over, fed me herring off paper, taught me which constables took bribes and which took offense, laughed at my jokes in a voice that carried three stalls away. She had made me visible to herself, and now the company had made her visible to itself, and there was no version of invisibility left that saved either of us.

I dried my face with my sleeve, which was already wet, rendering the exercise pointless, and went back inside to finish my columns. Busy hands. Lowered eyes. The old religion, recited by someone who no longer believed a word of it.

* * *

I tried the fishwife network that evening, and that is how I learned what silence sounds like when it is frightened.

Marta's stall had been more than a stall. Behind it, woven through the market like a net through water, was a lattice of women—herring-sellers, oyster-openers, crab-pickers—who moved goods, gossip, warnings, and occasionally people along routes no magistrate had ever mapped. When I needed word to reach the blockade line, it was that lattice that carried it. And so, desperate, I went to the places where the lattice lived: the back steps of the oyster house, the alley behind the smoke sheds, the corner bench where old Widow Cass always held court with her mending.

And found them closed to me like doors.

At the oyster house, a girl named Prue—sixteen, gap-toothed, devoted to Marta in the way of apprentices everywhere—was scrubbing shells with red knuckles. When I spoke her name she didn't look up.

"Prue. Is there word? Anything about where they've taken her, whether she's—"

"Don't know anything."

"Prue, please. I'm not asking you to carry anything. I'm asking—"

"I don't know anything," she said again, louder, and then, barely moving her lips, with her eyes fixed furiously on her work: "*They're asking everyone who talks to her. Go home, miss.*"

Widow Cass's bench was empty. The alley behind the smoke sheds had two men leaning in it who were not usually men who leaned in alleys. Even the gulls seemed to have been briefed.

I understood. Of course I understood—I had invented the strategy, hadn't I? Busy hands, lowered eyes, let the sea not notice. These women survived by exactly the doctrine I had lived by, and I had come among them trailing danger like a wet hem, and they had done the sensible thing. They had stepped back. The net had drawn in around me, mesh by mesh, each woman closing her own little gap, until the whole beautiful, illegal, generous machinery of Marta's world went silent as a held breath.

And the worst of it—the part I still can't say without wincing—was that I couldn't even blame them, and some small, ugly sliver of me was relieved. If they wouldn't carry my messages, then no more of them would be arrested. My guilt and my gratitude arrived holding hands, and neither would look me in the eye.

I walked home along the seawall in the dark, past the harbor chain and the lanterns of the patrol boats, and I did sums in my head the way I do when I can't bear the actual numbers. One letter. One promise to a dead man. One girl stepping off the safe path at the salt sheds. Against that: one fishwife in a cell, forty women struck dumb, a captain blockaded, a deckhand hunted, and a magistrate somewhere in his warm office moving pieces on a board only he could see, waiting for me to make the next move he had already planned for.

*Come and be useful.*

The rain stopped. The clouds tore open over the water, and there was the moon doing its indifferent silver work, lighting the pearl beds where my father had drowned, lighting the cell—somewhere over there in the company quarter—where Marta was sitting on a plank bunk wondering what she'd raised me into.

"You terrible old woman," I said to the moon, or possibly to myself. "You've got me talking to the sky now."

* * *

Sleep declined to attend me that night, so I sat up in the dormitory cot with a blanket around my shoulders and did what I do best: I worked. Not company work. My own.

Here is what I knew, laid out like a ledger:

One. Hargrove had taken Marta not for what she knew but for what she *was*—pressure applied to a specific point on a specific map. Which meant he did not need her testimony to proceed. He needed my cooperation. Her arrest was a door left open with a light behind it.

Two. Every hour the door stayed open cost Marta something. Company cells were not cruel in the dramatic ways—they were cruel in the accounting ways. Cold, short rations, indefinite sentences measured in paperwork. Marta was sixty-two years old and had lungs full of smoke-shed air from thirty years over a fire pit. Time was a currency she had less of than I did.

Three. I could not reach her by force, could not buy her out, could not smuggle her a crust without confirming to Hargrove exactly how much she mattered to me—and thereby how much the next person mattered, and the next, until everyone I loved was inventory.

Four. Therefore the only move that saved Marta was the one Hargrove wanted: walk through the door, sit in the warm office, offer to be useful.

Which meant the question was not *will I go to Hargrove.* That was decided the moment I read the circular; grief just hadn't caught up with the paperwork yet. The question was *what do I bring him when I go.* Because a girl who walks in empty-handed gets used and discarded, and everyone she's connected to gets catalogued. But a girl who walks in with something to trade—

I turned the blanket around my shoulders and stared at the dark ceiling and thought about what I had to trade, and the honest inventory was short. I had a letter whose contents I half-knew and could not surrender. I had a name—Silas—that I could burn, though burning it bought maybe a week and killed a man. I had my father's memory, my own silence, my own neck.

And I had, filed away in the registry room of Meridian Trading, under a red thread, a document that said the company had investigated my father once before—for witnessing a shipment Governor Crane had wanted buried. A shipment. A signature. A paper trail that led, if you tugged it hard enough, all the way upstairs.

Hargrove served Crane. Hargrove's whole machine ran on Crane's authority. And somewhere in that machine's own files was the record of the thing Crane most needed buried—which meant the company's own paper could be turned against the company, if a girl with ten years of tally-clerk training could get into the registry room a second time and get out again.

It was a mad plan. It was barely a plan at all. But it had one virtue that outweighed its madness: it meant that when I walked into Hargrove's office, I would not be coming empty-handed and bare-necked. I would be coming to bargain. The invisible girl, learning at last to play visible games.

Somewhere below my window, a bell marked the middle watch. Somewhere across the black water, Elias Vane slept—or didn't—aboard a brig he couldn't sail, holding a letter he couldn't open. Somewhere in a cell, Marta Okafor coughed smoke-damaged lungs in the cold and probably, being Marta, was already running a black-market economy among the guards.

"For you," I told the ceiling. "And God forgive me for what it costs."

Then I lay down, closed my eyes, and began, methodically, to memorize the registry room's night schedule—the watchman's rounds, the lamp hours, the twenty minutes between his passes that I had counted once before with my heart in my mouth among the cargo crates.

Tomorrow, I would go back in.

Tomorrow, I would find out what my father had seen.

And the day after, I would walk through Hargrove's open door—carrying, I prayed, something worth a fishwife's freedom.
