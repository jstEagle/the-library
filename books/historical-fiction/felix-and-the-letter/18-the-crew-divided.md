## 18. The Crew Divided

Captain Elias Vane had learned long ago that a ship told you her troubles before her crew did. The Fairwind told him this one at dawn: a wrong note in her timbers, a restlessness in the way she tugged at her anchors, as if even the vessel had heard what was being whispered below decks after the lamps went out.

He stood at the stern rail with his second cup of coffee going cold in his hand and watched the blockade chain glint across the harbor mouth. Three company frigates sat beyond it, fat and patient as spiders. Eleven days now since the edict. Eleven days of eating salt pork and watching his fleet — what remained of it — swing at anchor like horses in a stable they couldn't leave.

"You're doing the thing again," said Silas, appearing at his elbow with the particular lightness of a young man who has been running errands all morning and would like credit for it.

"What thing?"

"The staring-at-the-chain thing. You get a line right here—" Silas drew a finger between his own brows "—like you're trying to bore through it with sheer disapproval."

"It hasn't worked yet?"

"Not even a little."

"Then I'll keep at it. Persistence is a sailor's virtue."

"Stubbornness is a sailor's vice. You told me that yourself, my first week. You also told me never to trust a calm sea, a cheap rope, or a man who says trust me twice." Silas helped himself to Elias's coffee, which was either insubordination or affection depending on how one filed it. "Cap. There's something you should hear before you hear it from someone angrier."

Elias turned. Silas was twenty-four, quick-fingered, once apprenticed to a printer before the sea got him, and he had a face built for concealing things — but not this morning.

"Say it."

"There's talk in the fo'c'sle. About the letter."

The word *letter* sat between them like a live coal. Elias kept his voice level. "There's always talk."

"Not like this. They're saying Crane's offering pardons. Full pardons, back wages, maybe even prizes for the fleet. All we have to do is hand over what you're carrying." Silas hesitated. "Kerr's name is on the talk, Cap. Not around it. On it."

* * *

Elias found his boatswain on the middeck, coiling line with the mechanical perfection of forty years' habit. Boatswain Kerr was sixty-one years old, built like a capstan, and had pulled Elias out of the water exactly twice in their shared life — once from a storm off the shoals, once from a bottle after Elias's wife was buried. He was the closest thing to a brother Elias had, which is precisely why the sight of him coiling rope too carefully made Elias's chest go tight.

"Walk with me," Elias said.

They walked to the rail, away from the hands. Kerr spoke first, which was unlike him.

"You've heard."

"I've heard."

"Then let me spare us both the dance." Kerr set down the rope and faced him squarely. "Eleven days, Elias. Eleven days at anchor, and the men are counting what's left in the bread room same as I am. We can't run the chain. We can't fish the beds — company cutters take any small boat past the buoy line, ask Tammy's crew. We are sitting in a barrel with three cats outside it, and the only thing between us and the chopping block is a piece of paper you won't even show us."

"It isn't mine to show."

"It isn't yours to *hang* for, either, but I expect that's where it lands if this goes wrong!" Kerr's voice stayed low, which somehow made it worse. Kerr shouting was weather; Kerr quiet was a warning. "A girl's father gave you that letter over brandy seven years ago. A kind thought. But that man's dead, Elias, drowned and done, and the living crew of this brig didn't swear anything to him. Pardons, Captain. Real ones. And the fleet stays ours — Crane's man said so, said it plain through the harbormaster's boy."

"And you believe Crane." Elias kept his tone mild, almost conversational, the way he did when the deck was tilted and someone needed to stay calm. "You believe the governor who declared half the harbor enemies of the crown because their grandfathers were born on the wrong side of a line will honor his word to a boatswain he's never met."

"I believe a bird in the hand," Kerr said stubbornly. "I've got a pension, Elias. Thirty years of it owed to me. My knees are gone, my sister's alone up the coast, and I'd like to see her before I'm too stiff to make the road. That paper of yours is worth nothing to us and everything to them. Trade it."

"Or?"

Kerr's jaw worked. "Or the men may decide to trade it without asking you."

The words dropped into silence. Around them the ship worked — blocks creaking, water slapping the hull, a cook's boy banging a pot below — all the ordinary noises of a morning that had stopped being ordinary.

Elias looked at his oldest friend and felt something crack, quietly, the way ice cracks before it goes.

"So that's where we are. Mutiny with a pension attached."

"Don't dress it up fancy," Kerr growled. "It's arithmetic."

"It's fear wearing arithmetic's coat." Elias stepped closer. He kept his hands loose at his sides and his voice pitched for one man only. "You want to know what's in that letter? Fine. Here's what I know, and I'll say it once. Sterling gave it to me because he'd seen something in the pearl beds he wasn't meant to see, and he trusted me when there was no reason on God's green water to. If I sell it — if I trade a dead friend's last wish to the very men who'd have buried whatever he found — then everything I've told these boys about being a fair captain is bilge, and they'll know it, and so will I. And Crane still hangs us, Kerr. Because men like Crane don't pardon witnesses. They *file* them."

For a moment — just a moment — something flickered in Kerr's eyes. Doubt, maybe. Or memory: the two drownings, the storm, the bottle, thirty years of watching each other's backs.

Then he shut it away like a drawer.

"You always did think being right was the same as being safe," Kerr said. "Call your muster, Captain. Before sundown. Because if you don't, I can't answer for the foremast men, and I'd rather settle this with words than otherwise."

* * *

He called the muster at four bells.

The whole crew came aft — forty-two souls minus the two ashore — and arranged themselves in the loose, shuffling semicircle that sailors use when they want to look mutinous and mostly manage to look cold. Elias counted the faces. Young Tam with his burned hands. Old Pietro who'd sailed with him fifteen years. The Delacroix cousins. Little Finnegan who still slept with his boots on. And front and center, arms crossed, Kerr, flanked by six men whose eyes kept sliding toward the captain and away.

Silas stood at Elias's shoulder, uninvited and immovable.

"You've been talking," Elias began. No preamble. He'd learned from his mother that the fastest way through a hard door was to stop pretending it was soft. "And I don't blame any man here for talking. Fear talks. Hunger talks. So before anyone does something we'll all be ashamed of, I'm going to do something I should have done eleven days ago."

He reached inside his coat.

Every man straightened. Kerr's hand moved to his belt. Somewhere in the crowd somebody whispered *he's got it* — and Elias, who was carrying nothing but his watch and a stub of pencil, let them believe it for exactly as long as it took to feel the weight of what he actually carried.

He held up his empty hand instead.

"That letter has hung over this ship like weather. Every man guessing, every man afraid, and me standing here like a miser guarding a strongbox nobody's seen. Well. Here's the truth of it, plain." He looked down the line of faces, meeting eyes. "I cannot show it to you, because I swore to a dying man's friend that it would pass unopened to his daughter, and a promise that bends when the wind picks up is no promise at all. But I can tell you this much, and I'll swear it on my name, which some of you know I'm particular about."

He paused. Gulls wheeled overhead, screaming their idiot opinions.

"That paper is not a treasure map and it is not a pardon. It is *evidence*. Evidence against Governor Crane, of a kind that frightens him enough to blockade a harbor over it. It is worth nothing sold — because the moment we offer it, we become loose ends, and loose ends on Crane's ledger get trimmed. The only safety in that letter is in its *delivery*, to the right hands, by the right people. Which means every man aboard who wants a future is already holding the most valuable thing he owns: his patience."

Silence. Then Tam, the young one with the burned hands, said, "And if the right hands never come, Captain? If the girl doesn't come?"

"She'll come." Elias said it with more certainty than he felt, and watched Silas nod along beside him like a seconding motion. "Sterling's daughter has her father's spine. I've had letters. She's moving."

"You've had letters?" Kerr's eyebrows climbed. "From inside a blockaded city?"

"Harbormaster's boys aren't the only ones who can carry messages for coin," Silas said smoothly, and Elias blessed him silently for the improvisation, because in fact the fishwife network was a rumor wrapped in a hope, and he'd promised Marta's cousin a favor he hadn't figured out how to repay yet.

Kerr stepped forward. The six men behind him shifted like a single animal.

"With respect, Captain," Kerr said, and his voice was terrible in its steadiness, "that's a lot of faith resting on a dead man's paper and a girl neither of us has seen in years. I've followed you through weather no sane man sails. I'd follow you into hell itself and fetch my pipe on the way. But I won't follow you into the sea with the ship full and dry under my feet — not for a promise made over brandy. Call it to a vote. Fair and square. Majority carries."

"A vote," Elias repeated softly. "On my deck."

"On the crew's fate."

Something old and cold settled in Elias's chest. He had imagined this moment, in the black watches, the way a man imagines his own funeral — knowing it must come, hoping to miss the date. Now it stood in front of him wearing his best friend's face.

"No," Elias said.

Kerr's chin came up. "No?"

"No vote. Not because I'm afraid of losing — though I notice you brought your six friends to the front row, Boatswain, which suggests you weren't sure of the count yourself." A ripple went through the assembled men; two of Kerr's six suddenly found the rigging fascinating. "But because a ship is not a tavern. When the storm comes, we don't vote on whether to shorten sail. I am captain of the Fairwind until the sea, the company, or God Almighty relieves me of it, and not one minute sooner. If any man believes otherwise, he knows where the longboat is, and I'll see him over the side with my own hands and a fair share of provisions."

His voice never rose. That was the trick of it, the thing he'd learned from his mother, who had raised four children through a famine without ever once raising her voice: quiet makes men lean in, and leaning-in men are listening men, and listening men are not rushing men.

"But hear me, all of you, because I'll say this once." He let his gaze travel the whole line, slow as a lighthouse beam. "Every man who stands with this ship through the next fortnight, whatever falls out of it, I will stand before any tribunal on this earth and say *he was true*, and I'll spend the last coin of my name buying his freedom. That is my vote. It's the only vote a captain gets. And Kerr—" he turned to his oldest friend, and made his voice gentle, because this part deserved gentleness "—you taught me that, thirty years ago, when you fished me out of the shoals and never once asked what I'd done to fall in."

Kerr stood very still. His hands, those great knuckled hams that had spliced cable in hurricane winds, opened and closed at his sides.

"Thirty years," Kerr said quietly. "Aye." He looked at the deck. Looked at the blockade chain, far off and glittering. Looked at Elias, and Elias watched him choose — saw the choosing happen, saw it go the wrong way, and could do nothing about it but stand there and let it hurt.

"My sister's up the coast," Kerr said, as if that explained everything. As if it did. "Pension's owed. I'm sixty-one, Elias. I haven't got another thirty years of *patience* in me." He squared his shoulders, and his voice went formal, quarterdeck-formal, which was how Kerr said goodbye to things. "Request permission to go ashore, Captain. On leave. Permanent."

The crew stirred. Someone hissed. Elias raised one hand and the hissing stopped.

"Permission granted," Elias said. His throat felt like he'd been swallowing rope. "With full pay through month's end, and my thanks for every year of it."

"You're letting him just walk?" Tam blurted. "After—"

"He's walking," Elias said, "because I asked him to choose, and he chose, and a choice that costs a man nothing teaches nobody anything. See it cost him, Tam. Watch it. That's the lesson."

Kerr flinched as if struck. For one heartbeat he looked old — older than the sea had ever made him, older than his father must have looked. Then he gathered himself, touched his brow in something between a salute and a wound, and went down the ladder without looking back.

The sound of the shore boat pulling away carried across the water for a long time.

* * *

That evening, Elias sat in his cabin with the letter — the actual letter, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of his spare coat — and allowed himself, briefly, to be an old tired man instead of a captain. He poured one finger of brandy, the last of the bottle Sterling himself had opened seven years ago, and drank it slowly.

A knock. Silas entered without waiting, which was either insubordination or affection.

"Well," Silas said, dropping into the opposite chair. "Half the crew thinks you're a saint and the other half thinks you're a lunatic, which by my reckoning is the best ratio you've ever achieved. Pietro's recaulking the longboat 'just in case,' which I've chosen to read as loyalty."

"And the six?"

"Gone quiet. Quiet as church mice who've remembered the cat." Silas turned his cup in his hands. "Cap. Can I say a thing you won't like?"

"You're going to regardless."

"Kerr's not the type to sulk ashore with his sister. He's the type to fix a problem the way he fixes rigging — cut out the bad stretch and splice in new line." Silas looked up. "If Crane's people were talking to the harbormaster's boy, they were talking to more than one person. And a boatswain with debts and grievances, walking free in that city, knowing exactly what we carry and where we sleep..."

Elias looked at the dark window, where the harbor lights of the enemy glittered like a spilled jewelry box.

"I know," he said.

Because he did know. He'd known since the boat pulled away, known it in the timbers, in the way the Fairwind pulled at her anchors. His oldest friend had gone ashore carrying thirty years of Elias's secrets, and fear, and the coordinates of everything.

Silas leaned forward. "So what do we do?"

"We do what Sterling did." Elias reached into his coat lining, felt the oilcloth crackle, and made his peace with it one more time. "We trust the girl. Get word to Marta's cousin tonight — tell her the situation's changed, tell her the window's closing. Whatever Oscar Sterling means to do about that letter, she'd better mean to do it fast."

Above them, on deck, the watch changed, and somewhere beyond the chain the company frigates burned their lamps late into the night, waiting — as Elias now understood — for a message that might already be traveling ashore in a boatswain's pocket.
