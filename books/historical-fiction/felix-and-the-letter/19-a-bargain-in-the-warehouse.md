## 19. A Bargain in the Warehouse

There is a particular kind of quiet in the Meridian counting house at the end of the day, when the clerks have gone and the lamps have been trimmed and the ledgers sit in their shelves like sleeping animals. I had spent seven years learning to love that quiet, because it was the only thing the company ever gave me for free.

I did not love it anymore. Now it only sounded like Marta's empty stall on the fish market row, and the way her neighbors drew their shutters when I walked past, as though grief were a contagion you caught from looking at the wrong girl too long.

Three days since they took her. Three days of rehearsing one sentence in my head until it had worn smooth as sea glass: *I can get you Captain Vane's letter.*

It was a lie, mostly. That was the trouble with lies—they were so much harder to carry than silence. Silence weighed nothing. A lie had edges.

I had spent those three days doing what I had always done best: watching. I watched the runners who carried messages between Hargrove's office and the harbor lockup, and learned that Marta was moved twice, once to be questioned and once to be counted—everyone got counted now; Finch's census had made counting fashionable. I watched the guards' rotation and found that it changed at the changing of the tide, which was either stupidity or arrogance, and with Hargrove I suspected both. Most of all I watched Hargrove himself, because if I was going to walk into his web on purpose, I wanted to know how many strands there were and which ones hummed.

He kept evening hours in the ledger room—the very room where I had once hidden among cargo crates with my heart hammering like a loose block on a deck. He liked to work late, I had learned, because that was when the building belonged to him alone, and Magistrate Hargrove was a man who mistook solitude for power. He would learn the difference eventually. Men like him always did, but usually not before they ruined somebody.

I meant to be the somebody who taught him. Or died trying. The two outcomes felt closer together these days than I would have liked.

* * *

Silas argued with me about it, of course. He had been hiding in Marta's back room since the raid, because the city was too hot for him and the Fairwind too watched to return to, and he spent his days pretending to mend nets while actually doing nothing of the sort. His hands were made for finer work. Over the past week they had been making finer work indeed—I had watched him mix inks by lamplight, aging paper with tea and smoke, copying the company's own watermarks until I could not tell his bills of lading from the real ones without holding them to the sun.

"You're walking into the man's parlor," Silas said, "with nothing but nerve."

"I'm walking into his parlor with the best forgery in this hemisphere."

"That's still nothing but nerve. Nerve dressed up in nice handwriting." He set down his pen and rubbed his eyes, leaving a faint gray smudge at his temple that made him look younger, which was unfair, because I needed him to seem older and wiser than me right now, and he wasn't managing it. "Oscar. If he looks too closely—if he holds it up to a window—"

"Then we're finished, and Marta stays in a cell, and Elias hangs, and I have spent my whole life being invisible for absolutely no reason." I heard my own voice come out flat and steady and was faintly surprised by it, the way you're surprised by your reflection in a shop window when you'd expected someone else entirely. "I've done the arithmetic, Silas. Doing nothing costs more."

He looked at me for a long moment. Lamplight does kind things to most faces; it did something better to his—it made the fear in his face visible, and honest, and mine to answer for.

"Finch's papers came through," he said finally, which was his way of surrendering. "The transfer orders are good. If this works, we move on the lockup during the tide change tomorrow night."

"If this works," I said, "there won't *be* a lockup problem. Hargrove will release her himself. That's the shape of it—he trades me Marta, I trade him the letter, and everyone walks away believing they've won."

"And which everyone is going to believe it?"

"The one holding the paper," I said. "That's the whole trick of paper. Papa taught me that without meaning to. He trusted a letter to keep us safe, and it will—but letters only work on people who believe what they want to believe, and Hargrove wants so badly to hold Elias Vane's secret that he'll swallow anything shaped like it."

Silas almost smiled. "You've gotten frightening, do you know that? When you first rowed out to us you couldn't look anyone in the eye."

"I still can't," I said. "I've just learned that nobody checks whether you mean it. They only check whether you showed up." I gathered the forged packet—wrapped in oilcloth, tucked against my ribs where a girl carries the things she cannot afford to lose—and felt its small weight settle against my heart like a second heartbeat. "Wish me luck."

"Better," said Silas. "I'll wish you Hargrove's exact vanity. It's worth ten lucks."

* * *

The Meridian warehouse at dusk smelled of tar and tallow and old rope, and beneath all of it, faintly, the sea—which the company had never managed to own, though God knows it tried. I had worn my good dress, the gray one, because gray was my color and I saw no reason to announce myself with anything brighter. But I wore it differently than I used to. Straighter. As if the girl inside it had been let out a few inches.

Hargrove's clerk admitted me without question, which told me Hargrove had already sent word that I might come. Of course he had. We had been circling each other for weeks; he knew I would come to him for the fishwife eventually, the way water knows to find a drain.

The ledger room was lit warmly, which surprised me. He had even had wine set out—two glasses, the second one waiting, patient as a snare.

"Miss Sterling." He rose behind his desk with that oily courtesy of his, the bow of a man who has decided in advance exactly how much respect the occasion requires and not a drop more. "You'll forgive the theater. I find negotiations go more smoothly over something drinkable."

"I don't drink on business," I said, and sat without being asked. Small things. They mattered, the small things. He noted my sitting; I watched him note it; neither of us blinked.

"No?" He poured one glass after all, for himself. "Your father did, I'm told. Pearl men usually did—something about the cold coming out of the bones afterward." He sat, folding his hands, and let the name hang in the air between us like smoke. He had done this before, in the summons; I remembered the sensation of being turned over and examined. But I had practiced being turned over since then. "You've come about the woman. Marta. The fishwife."

"I've come to make you an offer."

"Hargrove lifted an eyebrow, the precise instrument of a collector hearing a rumor of a rare piece. "From you? What could you possibly have that I want?"

"Elias Vane's letter."

I laid the word down gently, the way you lay a card you've been holding all night. And I watched it land. Watched his stillness go from professional to genuine—a half-second where the magistrate evaporated and only the man remained, hungry, leaning forward before he could stop himself.

"You know about the letter," he said softly.

"I know my father sealed it into Captain Vane's keeping six years ago. I know Vane has held it through blockade and edict rather than give it up. I know that whatever's in it, it frightened you enough to burn half the harbor's independence to get at." I folded my hands in my lap, mimicking his posture, a mirror held up at an unkind angle. "And I know that you've been trying to catch him ashore using me as bait, which is insulting, frankly, because I'm worth more than bait."

"Are you."

"I'm the only living person Elias Vane keeps a promise to." I let that breathe. "He won't come in for you. He won't come in for a pardon or a threat. But he'll come in for me—and I'm the one person alive who can ask him for the letter itself. He'll open his safe for me. He told me so, out on the Fairwind, the night I visited him. Did your spy aboard mention that part? I imagine not, or you'd have moved already."

A flicker—there and gone—at the word *spy*. Good. Let him wonder how much I knew. A man checking his own blind spots is a man not checking yours.

"You're proposing," Hargrove said slowly, "to deliver it to me yourself."

"I'm proposing a trade. The letter, hand to hand, authentic, unopened—or opened, if you insist, I don't care what's in it, I never have—for the woman Marta, released, unharmed, with charges withdrawn. And a clean file for me. Red thread cut. My father's name struck from your lists of enemies." I leaned forward. "You get your prize, Magistrate. Crane gets his. And two women nobody important ever cared about get to stop drowning."

He swirled his wine and studied me over the rim, and for a long moment the room was so quiet I could hear the harbor breathing outside, the slap of water against pilings, the groan of the chain across the channel.

"You understand my difficulty," he said at last, pleasantly. "Trust. You could bring me any paper. How would I know it was genuine?"

"How would *I*?" I countered, and had the pleasure of watching surprise cross his face again. "Magistrate, I'm a tally clerk. I know numbers, not treason. You'll verify it—you'll have experts, you'll compare hands, seals, watermarks, whatever men like you compare. That's your half of the risk. Mine is trusting you release Marta. Neither of us gets to be certain. That's what makes it a bargain instead of a surrender."

Something moved behind his eyes then—not suspicion exactly, but appraisal, the cold pleasure of a man discovering that the mouse has teeth. He had planned, I realized with a chill that reached all the way down to my shoes, to take the letter from me tonight if I offered. He had the wine, the privacy, the patience for it. And now here I was offering, on nearly his own terms, and some part of him was almost disappointed, the way a cat is disappointed when the bird lands in its mouth voluntarily.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Sunset. Pier nine, the customs shed. You bring the letter. I bring the woman and her freedom. Agreed?"

"Agreed." I stood. So did he, and extended his hand, and I shook it—dry, cool, careful—and thought of all the stories where a handshake means something, and how stories are written by people who have never needed to shake hands with a spider.

"One more thing, Miss Sterling." He held my gaze, mild as milk. "Whatever's in that letter—your father's words, his last wishes—I hope you understand I'll read every line. Sentiment doesn't survive an archive. Nothing does."

"I know," I said truthfully, and smiled, and the smile was the realest thing I had given him all evening, because it was the smile of a girl who had already read every line and knew exactly what the letter would tell him.

Which was nothing. Which was ink aged with tea and a watermark copied by a printer's apprentice with steady hands and a bill of lading for a shipment that never existed, bound for a port that had burned down years ago, under signatures belonging to three men who had never sailed anywhere at all.

* * *

Outside, the fog had come in off the water, softening the lamps into halos, and I walked back through the fish market with my heart going like a drum in a parade. I had done it. I had sat across a table from the most dangerous man in the city and lied to his face for a quarter hour, and he had shaken my hand.

It should have felt like victory. It felt like stepping off a dock.

At the corner by Marta's shuttered stall, a figure detached itself from a doorway, and my whole body went to iron before I recognized the stoop of shoulders and the umbrella carried like a surveying instrument. Finch. The census taker. Watching me come out of Meridian's warehouses at dusk with Hargrove's handshake still warm on my palm.

He didn't speak. He only looked at me—that terrible, gentle, cataloguing look—and touched the brim of his hat as though confirming an entry, and melted back into the fog.

I stood a long moment in the wet dark, listening to my own breath.

Tomorrow at sunset I would put forged paper into the hands of a man who verified things for a living. And somewhere between now and then, I had a growing, sickening certainty that the census taker had just counted me into a column I did not yet understand.

I walked faster.
