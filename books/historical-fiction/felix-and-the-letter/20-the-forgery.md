## 20. The Forgery

Silas Wren had not touched a composing stick in nine years, and his hands remembered it anyway.

That was the thing about craft—it lived in the fingers, not the head. His head was busy enough these days, what with the blockade chain creaking outside the porthole and the memory of Kerr's face at the mutiny still sitting in his chest like a swallowed fishhook. But when he laid out the type tray on the upturned crate in the Fairwind's hold, tilted the lantern so its light fell in a long gold bar across the lead letters, his hands went quiet and sure, like they had somewhere better to be than fear.

He'd been a printer's apprentice at fourteen. Compositor at sixteen, if the master had been believed, though the master had been a drunk and a flatterer and dead of both by the time Silas was seventeen. Still—four years of setting type, four years of pulling proofs until his forearms burned, four years of learning that a page was only ever as honest as the man who inked it. It was a strange education for a deckhand. It was about to become a very useful one.

The hold smelled of tar, salt pork, and old rope. He'd cleared a corner behind the water barrels where the lantern wouldn't show through the seams, and he'd spent two days assembling his arsenal: a sheet of company letterhead lifted from the purser's locker (the purser would notice eventually; Silas planned to be elsewhere), a stick of sealing wax in Meridian red, a brass seal that was almost—not quite—the magistrate's office mark, and three bottles of ink of slightly different blacks, because official paper never used one black. It always used two, one for the body and one for the stamps, and getting that wrong was how forgers hanged themselves. Literally, he supposed, though he tried not to suppose it too loudly.

The door hatch scraped.

He knew her step now. That was the trouble. Nine days she'd been coming aboard under cover of Marta's fish cart, and nine days he'd been telling himself that the small lift he felt at that particular scrape of boot on ladder rung was just relief that she hadn't been caught. Relief. That was all it was. A man could feel relief about anything.

"You're doing it again," Oscar said, appearing at the bottom of the ladder with her collar up and salt in her hair. "The staring-at-nothing thing."

"It's called thinking," Silas said. "Printers do it between words."

"What's between your words?"

"Come see."

* * *

She sat cross-legged on the crate beside him, close enough that her sleeve brushed his whenever either of them reached for something, which was often, because everything on the work surface was within arm's reach of both of them. He chose to believe this was logistics.

"Explain it to me," she said. "Like I'm stupid."

"You're not stupid. You count cargo for a living."

"Cargo counts itself. Paper lies for a living." She nodded at the letterhead. "That's why I want to understand it."

So he explained. How the eye read a document before the mind did—the weight of the stock, the depth of the impression a real seal made versus a wax imitation, the way clerks' hands got tired toward the bottom of a page so the last lines always drifted down and to the right, like men walking home drunk. How no forger worth his gallows copied a hand exactly, because exactness looked like tracing, and tracing looked like guilt. "You want it nearly right," he said. "Nearly right says 'a clerk wrote this.' Exactly right says 'somebody wanted you to think a clerk wrote this.'"

Oscar frowned at him. "That's insane."

"That's bureaucracy."

"No—I mean—" She picked up one of the practice sheets he'd ruined already, a half-set page of transfer orders with a smeared date. "I've filed hundreds of these. Thousands. And I never once thought about any of this. The drift. The two inks. I just... read the words and moved my eyes along." She looked at the ruined sheet with something like respect, or maybe grief. "I made myself invisible inside this paper my whole life. And here's you, reading the paper itself like it's a person."

"How's the paper strike you, then?" he asked, mostly to keep the conversation off the way she'd said invisible, like it was a word she'd worn smooth from handling. "As a person."

"Tired," she said immediately. "Overworked. Underpaid. Lied about constantly." A pause, and then, quieter: "It doesn't deserve it."

* * *

They worked. That was the miracle of those nights in the hold—they worked, and the working was calm.

He set the type for the transfer orders first, because that was the piece they needed regardless of how Hargrove's bargain went: documents that would move a prisoner named S. Quill from company lockup to harbor duty, signed by an officer who existed, countersigned by one who didn't. Oscar supplied the details from memory—the correct form numbers, the rotation of the lockup watch, the particular officious phrasing Meridian favored, which she could recite like scripture. "Per directive 114-C," she dictated, "prisoner transfer to be effected without public notification during shift changeover, such changeover being defined as..."

"You memorized the directives?"

"I memorized everything," she said simply. "In case I ever needed to disappear inside it." Then she caught herself, hearing the word again, and made a face, and he was kind enough not to say anything, and loved her a little for knowing he wanted to.

By the third night the type was set and locked in the chase, and they were pulling proofs by lamplight, and Silas discovered that teaching was its own strange pleasure. Her hands were quick—diver's daughter, strong fingers, no wasted motion—and when he showed her how to judge the pressure of the burnisher, how to age paper with weak tea and a candle held at just the wrong distance, she absorbed it the way she seemed to absorb everything: silently, completely, and with an intensity that ought to have frightened him more than it did.

"You'd have been good at this," he said. "Printing. In another life."

"In another life I'm a pearl diver's daughter with a name nobody wants," she said. "This is the other other life."

"Then this life suits you. Your margins are even."

She laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, because sound carried in a hold and they were forging treason. But it escaped anyway, a little. He collected it. He was collecting them, he realized. Every laugh she let slip, every time she forgot to lower her voice, every moment her shoulders came down from around her ears—he stored each one away like coin against a bad winter.

* * *

The fear came back on the fifth night, and it came back wearing boots.

Boots, on the deck above. Two sets, unhurried, passing. Patrol rounds—and everyone aboard the Fairwind knew the patrol boats had been circling closer each week since Kerr left, since whatever Kerr might be saying ashore found ears that mattered. Elias had doubled the watch. Elias had also, Silas noted, said nothing at all about what happened in the hold after midnight, which was its own kind of permission and its own kind of risk.

Oscar froze mid-motion, the tea-dampened transfer order held aloft, dripping. Her whole body had gone rigid in a way Silas recognized from gulls, from deer, from anyone whose survival had once depended on being overlooked. He watched her become furniture. It was astonishing and awful.

The boots passed. The hatch stayed shut. Somewhere above, a man coughed and moved on.

"Breathe," Silas said quietly.

She breathed. The paper trembled once in her grip and then stilled. "Sorry."

"Don't apologize for that. You did that right. That's the whole trick, isn't it? Being nothing on demand." He took the dripping sheet from her, blotted it against his knee. "Me, I go the other way. When I'm scared I get loud. Ask anyone. Ask the mast, I apologized to it personally during the last storm."

"Why the mast?"

"It was there. And unlike most things aboard, it doesn't hold grudges." He smoothed the sheet flat. "You know what I can't figure out? You're braver than me and you don't even enjoy it. I at least enjoy it afterward. Telling the story. Me and the mast."

Oscar looked at him across the lantern light for a long moment. "What do I get afterward?"

And there it was—the question underneath everything, asked lightly, in a hold full of forged paper, by a girl who had spent six years practicing invisibility because visibility had cost her father his life. Silas considered several answers. Most of them were jokes. One of them was true.

"Afterward," he said, "you get to sit somewhere warm with people who know your actual name, and eat something that isn't ship's biscuit, and nobody checks your papers. That's the whole prize. That's everything I've got."

"That's a lot," she said.

"It's a terrible lot. But it's mine."

Her hand was on the crate between them, near his, not touching. He looked at it. She didn't move it and she didn't move hers away, and in the economy of the two of them—two people fluent in the language of almost—that counted as a paragraph.

The lantern guttered. He trimmed the wick. They went back to work, and their sleeves brushed, and neither of them fixed it.

* * *

On the seventh night the forgery was finished, and neither of them knew what to do with a finished thing.

There it lay on the crate: four transfer orders, aged and creased, bearing the tired-drift of an imaginary clerk's hand, sealed in Meridian red with an impression so close to the magistrate's mark that Silas himself felt a flicker of vertigo looking at it. Beside them, wrapped in oilcloth, the second set—papers for the larger game, the warehouse bluff, the trap-within-a-trap that Oscar had proposed and Elias had argued against and lost, because arguing with Oscar Sterling about her own plan turned out to be like arguing with tide.

"It's good work," Oscar said.

"It's adequate work."

"Silas. Look at it and say it's adequate."

He looked at it. The drift was right, the inks were right, the seal had the hairline flaw of the genuine article that he'd reproduced from memory of a document he'd glimpsed for eleven seconds in Hargrove's outer office eight days ago, and he did not recommend asking how. "It's the best thing I've ever made," he admitted, "and I once printed five hundred wedding invitations without a single typo, which in my old trade was regarded as a minor miracle."

"Who was getting married?"

"Nobody. The printer invented the wedding. Paid us himself. Said a wedding announcement was the only honest forgery—paper pretending two people are happy." He heard himself and winced. "That came out darker than intended."

But she was smiling, small and sideways, the smile he'd been collecting all week. "You're nervous," she said.

"I'm always nervous. It's my best quality. Keeps the rigging honest."

"You're nervous about tomorrow."

Tomorrow. The warehouse. Hargrove's terms, delivered through channels: the letter for the fishwife, midnight, no tricks. Oscar's counter-bluff: deliver the forgery, free Marta, buy time. Everyone aboard had opinions about it. Elias had gone quiet in the way that meant he was losing an argument with himself. And Silas had said nothing at all, which for him was a feat of restraint comparable to the wedding invitations.

"Yes," he said. "I'm nervous about tomorrow."

"Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is not mine to say."

"Say it anyway. I've spent my whole life around people deciding what wasn't theirs to say to me. Say it."

The lantern hissed. Above them the Fairwind creaked at her moorings like an old woman turning in bed. Silas set down the burnisher, wiped his inky hands on a rag that was beyond saving, and made the mistake of being honest.

"If it goes wrong tomorrow, you're the one standing in the room with him. Not me. Not the captain. You." His voice did the loud thing. He let it. "And I have to sit on this boat and row and wait and forge nothing, because there's nothing left to forge, and waiting is the worst work there is. That's the rest of it. I hate the waiting and I'm frightened for you and I'd trade places in a heartbeat except you'd never allow it, because you're the bravest person I've ever met and brave people are terrible at being protected."

Silence. Long enough for him to hear his own pulse.

Then Oscar said, "You know what's funny? All week I've been thinking you were calm."

"I'm never calm. I'm loud on the inside."

"Well." She picked up the oilcloth bundle and began wrapping the papers, precise, unhurried, her diver's hands steady as stones. "For what it's worth—I'm glad it's your rowboat tomorrow night. Out of everyone's."

"That's practically a compliment."

"That was a compliment. Don't get greedy." She tied the cloth, and then, in the smallest voice he'd yet heard from her: "If it goes wrong—if he knows, if the whole thing turns over—you'll come for me? Even if the captain says no? Even if it's stupid?"

Silas thought of Kerr, who had chosen safe. Thought of the mast, which held grudges for no one. Thought of every sensible thing he had ever declined to do.

"I'll come for you if it goes right," he said. "I'll come for you especially if it goes right, because you'll be insufferable and someone should witness it. If it goes wrong, I'll come faster."

She nodded once, businesslike, as though he'd confirmed a delivery schedule. But her hand crossed the last inch of crate and pressed his, ink to ink, briefly, before she stood and took up the lantern.

At the foot of the ladder she paused. "Midnight tomorrow," she said. "The salt sheds. If I'm not back by two—"

"I know where you'll be."

"And if I'm not anywhere?" she asked, which was the question, the real one, hanging in the tar-smelling dark.

Silas looked at her—the invisible girl holding the forged papers that would make her the most visible person in the port—and gave her the only answer he had.

"Then I'll start asking the paper," he said. "Paper remembers everything. You taught me that."

She climbed. The hatch closed. And the Fairwind rocked on black water while somewhere below, in a hold that still smelled of tea and lamp oil and her, Silas Wren sat alone with four perfect lies and one true thing, counting hours until midnight.

Above him, unseen in the rigging, the lookout was already watching a longboat put off from the quay—flying no light, pulling hard, making straight for the blockade line.

Someone else knew about tomorrow too.
