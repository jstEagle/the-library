## 21. Midpoint: The Exchange

Marta's stall was empty again that morning. Third day running.

Oscar had walked past it twice on her way to the counting house, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. The awning was lashed down, the scales hung in their leather wraps, and a chalk sign—*back soon, God willing and the herring willing*—had faded from someone's joke into something closer to a prayer. The fishwives had gone quiet around her, too. Not hostile. Worse. *Careful.* As if she were a wet plank on a slippery deck, and everyone had learned to step wide.

She had made her bargain three nights ago in Hargrove's warehouse office, and every hour since had been spent rehearsing this one. The letter for Marta. Elias's letter—the letter her father had sealed against his own chest before diving into green dark water—handed over like a sack of smuggled tea, and in its place, thanks to Silas's clever hands and a stolen press-roller, a forgery good enough to fool a man who thought himself unfoolable.

*Good enough,* she told herself for the hundredth time, and then, because honesty had become a habit she couldn't quite break: *it has to be.*

The note had come at dawn, folded inside a bloat of dried cod that Marta's neighbor thrust at her without meeting her eyes. *Warehouse Nine. Salt Row. Six o'clock tonight. Come alone, come plainly, and bring what we discussed.* It was signed with Hargrove's own neat hand, which somehow made it worse. A summons written like an invitation to supper.

So she came plainly. Her second-best dress, the gray one, hair pinned without ornament. The forged letter wrapped in oilcloth and bound with the same tarred twine her father used to use for his dive lines—Silas's suggestion, a detail of authenticity she hadn't thought of herself, and which had made her look at him rather longer than she'd meant to.

"You're staring," he'd said, not looking up from the drying ink.

"I'm admiring," she'd said. "There's a difference."

He'd turned pink to the ears and kept working, and she had filed the moment away somewhere safe, the way you pocket a coin you don't dare spend yet.

*Later,* she thought now, crossing Salt Row as the lamps went up. *If there is a later.*

* *

Warehouse Nine smelled of brine and rope and old decisions. Two lanterns burned at a trestle table set absurdly in the middle of the floor, as though someone had transplanted a magistrate's chamber into a cathedral of cargo. Crates rose around them in shadowed cliffs. Somewhere above, water ticked against the hull of the building itself—it stood on pilings over the tide channel—and Oscar found the sound steadying. Water she understood. Water kept honest time.

Hargrove sat behind the table, hands folded, looking for all the world like a man about to award prizes at a church bazaar. He was smaller than she remembered, or perhaps she had grown. His clerk hovered at his shoulder with pen poised, and two company men flanked the door with the studied blankness of furniture.

"Miss Sterling." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Punctual. Your father would be proud—or alarmed. Divers are famously casual men."

"Divers learn what happens when they're late," Oscar said, sitting. "The tide doesn't negotiate."

"No," Hargrove agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't." He let the word sit while he poured two cups of coffee from a battered pot, sliding one across. She didn't touch it. He didn't seem offended; if anything he seemed to approve. "You have it?"

"The letter. Sealed as it was given to Captain Vane nine years ago. Unopened." She placed the oilcloth bundle on the table between them but kept two fingers on it, an anchor weight of her own. "Marta first. I want to see her walk out that door before my hand leaves this table."

Hargrove's smile deepened by a degree. "You've been reading novels, Miss Sterling. Very well." He nodded to the door. One of the furniture-men left, boots echoing off the crates, and for a long minute nobody spoke. Hargrove drank his coffee. The clerk's pen scratched nothing in particular. Oscar counted her breaths the way her father had taught her to count fathoms—one, two, steady, don't rush the surface.

Then footsteps, and Marta came through the door.

She looked smaller, grayer, her knuckles scuffed, her jaw set in the particular way of a woman who has decided crying is a luxury. When her eyes found Oscar they went wet anyway, pride and fury fighting it out openly on her face.

"You great foolish girl," Marta said, voice cracking exactly halfway through. "Both of us."

"Go home, Marta," Oscar said softly. "Water your cat. Argue with the herring merchant. Don't look back."

Marta looked back, of course. She looked back twice at the door, once at Hargrove with an expression that could have curdled milk at forty paces, and once more at Oscar—a long look, a mother's look, saying everything the fishwife network's silence couldn't. Then she was gone into the lamplit fog, and something in Oscar's chest unlocked that had been jammed shut for three days.

Her fingers left the bundle.

"There," Hargrove said, almost gently. "A clean transaction. You see? The company honors its arrangements. We are not monsters, Miss Sterling. We are bookkeepers."

*That's what frightens me,* she nearly said, and bit it back. Instead she watched him draw the bundle toward him, watched him turn it over, weigh it in his palm like a man judging a pear. He worked the twine loose. Peeled back the oilcloth. The letter beneath was yellowed, wax-sealed, creased with nine years of careful keeping—and underneath the seal, invisible unless you knew, the faint ghost-lines where Silas had steamed open the original, copied every water stain and fox mark onto fresh-aged paper, and resealed the true pages elsewhere.

Hargrove held it up to the lantern. Light glowed through the paper, and Oscar watched his face the way she used to watch the surface from below—for the shimmer that meant weather coming.

What she saw was satisfaction. Warm, unhurried satisfaction, the expression of a man confirming a figure he had already entered in his ledger.

"Well," he said. "Well, well."

He set the letter down. He did not break the seal.

Every instinct Oscar owned went cold at once. A man who believed he had won would open it—greedily, right there, hungry for whatever leverage it contained. A man who did *not* care what was inside—

"Something wrong?" Hargrove asked, watching her watch him. "You've gone pale, Miss Sterling. I'd offer more coffee, but it's dreadful stuff. My wife sends it. She believes suffering improves character."

"What's wrong," Oscar said, and heard with distant interest that her voice came out level, "is that you're not reading it."

"Mm." He folded his hands again. "No. I don't suppose I need to. Bill of lading, isn't it? Arms shipments under Meridian's flag. Dates, ports, signatures. Governor Crane's signature, most damningly." He recited it the way the clerk beside him might recite tonnage figures. "Dated the spring before your father died. Hidden in the lining of a letter home to a little girl who presumably couldn't read cursive yet. Sentimental men make excellent couriers—they never suspect the parcel matters more than the words."

The tide under the floorboards kept its honest time, one tick, another, and Oscar could not make her lungs follow it.

"How—" Her voice failed on the first attempt. She started again. "How do you know what's in it?"

Hargrove reached beneath the table and produced, with the air of a conjurer producing a dove, a second document. He laid it beside the forgery. Side by side they were near twins—same stains, same seal, same tarred twine—and yet the copy in Hargrove's hands was *right* in some way Oscar couldn't name and Silas would have spotted in a heartbeat.

"This arrived by pilot boat yesterday morning," Hargrove said. "Transcribed in a familiar hand, I'm told. Neat fellow. Careful with his letters. Boatswain, by rank. Kerr, I believe the crew calls him. Twenty-two years with the company before Vane poached him, and a pension he was very keen to protect."

*Kerr.* The name landed like a dropped anchor. Oscar had never met the man; she knew him only as a shape at Elias's elbow in old stories, the boatswain who'd taught Silas his knots, oldest friend, loudest laugh. *Loyalty fracturing under fear,* some quiet part of her noted, cataloguing even now, even here. *Even the keel has seams.*

"So you see my difficulty," Hargrove continued, warming now, a lecturer with a favorite subject. "Or rather, you see my *ease*, which is rarer and much pleasanter. Three days ago I had a problem: a girl, a captain, and a document I needed but couldn't search for openly without alerting Crane's people that I wanted it for myself. And then you walked into my office with your little proposal—bring me the letter, free the fishwife—and you solved everything. You brought me the letter. Or a copy of it, very prettily done—I really must compliment your printer, the aging work is remarkable—which hardly matters, because I already hold a faithful transcript of the original, and now I hold your forgery too, which proves intent. Forgery of a corporate instrument. That's a hanging offense all by itself, quite apart from the treason."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the channel, a bell buoy clanged twice, patient as ever.

Oscar became aware, distantly, that she was still sitting upright with her hands folded in her lap like a girl at Sunday service, and that some stubborn submerged part of her was *proud* of it. Busy hands. Lowered eyes. Except her eyes weren't lowered anymore. They were fixed on Hargrove's face, and she let him see everything in hers, because hiding had just stopped being available and she refused to flinch in front of the man.

"You released Marta," she said.

"I did. A bargain's a bargain." He shrugged. "I keep my small promises precisely so that no one believes I'd keep a large one. It's a useful reputation. You should cultivate it."

"Why let her go at all? If you'd caught me out regardless—"

"Because you'll do it again," Hargrove said simply. "Next week, next month, whenever I need you. People forgive almost anything in a man who pays his debts. Now." He stood, buttoning his coat, and nodded to the furniture-men, who came alive around her chair. "Miss Sterling, you'll return to your dormitory. You'll go to work tomorrow. You'll say nothing to anyone, because the fishwife network has learned its lesson, your captain sits in my harbor under my guns, and the young printer's apprentice—" he paused delicately, and Oscar's blood turned to ice water, "—is currently sleeping aboard a ship I can impound at will. Am I understood?"

*He knows about Silas. He knows where Silas sleeps.* The trap hadn't sprung tonight; it had sprung days ago, quietly, while she and Silas laughed over ink stains in a lamplit hold, and she had been walking around inside it ever since, congratulating herself on her cleverness.

"And the letter?" she asked. "The real one. What will you do with it?"

Hargrove paused at the door, and for a moment something almost human crossed his face—not remorse, but recognition, the look of a man seeing a younger version of himself across a table.

"Burn it, eventually," he said. "But first I'm going to use it to buy the governor's silence and half his shipping concessions, and then I'm going to retire to a farm far from this damp harbor. That's the trouble with people like you, Miss Sterling—you think truth is a weapon. Truth is *inventory*. It only matters who holds it at the end of the quarter." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Give my regards to the tide."

The door boomed shut behind him. The clerk snuffed one lantern, took the other, and followed, and Oscar sat alone in Warehouse Nine among the crates and the ticking water with a forged letter and a heart hammering out fathom after fathom of fear.

Think, she told herself. The way Papa taught her to think underwater: panic spends air; spend it on thinking instead.

Hargrove had a transcript. Kerr had sold it—he must have copied the letter's contents during the near-mutiny, when papers moved from hand to hand. But a transcript wasn't the original. Hargrove needed the original eventually—Crane's own signature in Crane's own ink, no transcription standing up forever against men who checked things. Which meant Hargrove still needed something. And Elias still held the original, sealed, hidden somewhere only he knew, and Elias was under guard on a ship full of a crew Kerr had proven could be bought.

And Hargrove had said the dangerous thing without knowing it was dangerous. *Your captain sits in my harbor under my guns.* But the Fairwind was a sailing vessel at a blockaded pier, and blockades changed, and captains escaped, and boatswains slept badly when their consciences got loud.

Oscar stood. Her knees shook; she let them, briefly, privately, the way you're allowed to shake below the waterline where no one counts.

Then she straightened her gray dress, pinned her hair more firmly, and walked out onto Salt Row into the fog and the lamplight, moving at the pace of a woman with nowhere urgent to be, past the shuttered stalls, past the empty place where Marta's chalk sign hung, toward the docks and the dark water and the blockade chain.

She had gone in playing invisible.

She was beginning to understand, with a clarity that felt almost like her father's hand on her shoulder, that the next move would require her to be seen—and that the man she had to warn was guarded by guns, surrounded by traitors, and running out of tide.
