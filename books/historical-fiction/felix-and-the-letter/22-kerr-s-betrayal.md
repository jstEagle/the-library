## 22. Kerr's Betrayal

Boatswain Kerr had not slept, which was not unusual for a man his age and profession, but tonight the sleeplessness had a different texture. Usually his wakefulness was the honest kind—the kind that came from forty years of listening to rigging complain in a rising wind, of knowing by the sound of the hull whether the sea beneath it was friendly. Tonight he lay in his bunk aboard the *Fairwind* with his eyes open and his hands folded on his chest like a man in a coffin who had some objections to file.

He had sold them. That was the plain shape of it, and no amount of rolling over would change its outline.

Not the letter itself—he hadn't been fool enough or brave enough to put his hands on the thing—but the existence of it. The fact of it. He'd sat across a table from a soft-spoken company man in the back room of the Anchor and Rope, three nights past, and for the price of his pension and the promise that his name would stay off any list, he had told them what Captain Vane carried in the strongbox beneath his cabin floor.

*There's a paper,* he'd said. His voice had sounded very far away even as he used it. *Something the old man Sterling left him. Something the captain thinks can hang a governor.*

The company man—Kerr never learned his name, only that he stirred his tea far too long and thanked Kerr with the courtesy one extends to a servant—had smiled the way an accountant smiles when the columns finally agree.

Three nights. Kerr had spent three nights telling himself stories. That the information would go nowhere. That Hargrove would sit on it, cautious as ever, waiting for something better. That the whole thing might blow over like a summer squall, and in ten years it would be nothing but a bad taste Kerr couldn't name when he drank his morning tea.

Then, at first gray light, he heard the oars.

* * *

You learn to hear a harbor, after enough years. You learn that a harbor at dawn has a vocabulary: water slapping pilings, gulls arguing over their opinions, the cough of a cook lighting a galley stove, someone's boots on deck because someone always has boots on deck before anyone else does. The *Fairwind* rode at anchor two hundred yards off the customs wharf, blockaded, idle, her sails furled and her crew restless as cats in a sack—and still she had her own sound, and Kerr knew it the way he knew his own breathing.

The oars did not belong.

They came muffled, wrapped in cloth or sacking, which was worse than loud oars. Loud oars meant fishermen up early, honest men grumbling about bait. Muffled oars meant men who did not wish to be heard until they were close enough that hearing made no difference. Kerr swung his legs out of the bunk and stood, and his knees announced their grievances as they always did, and he crossed to the porthole of the boatswain's berth—a luxury of a berth, honestly, wide as a coffin and twice as comfortable—and looked out at the water.

The light was the color of pewter gone dull. Mist lay on the harbor in low ropes. And coming through it, four ship's boats in line ahead, each one packed with men in company blue, oars dipping quiet as spoons in soup.

Kerr's stomach performed a maneuver he had last felt during the typhoon of '91, when the *Cormorant* had gone down with all hands off the Saddle Rocks and he alone had lived to be blamed for it.

He should wake the captain. Of course he should. That was the arithmetic any child could do: soldiers in boats plus sleeping crew equals disaster, therefore ring the bell. And if he rang the bell, perhaps the captain could slip over the side in the confusion, take to the dory, vanish into the maze of the fish market before the soldiers' boots touched the deck. Elias Vane was sixty years old and built like a windlass, but he had swum harbors before, in worse light than this, and the mist was thick enough to hide a cathedral.

But if the captain fled—if soldiers boarded the *Fairwind* and found her master gone—they would burn her. That was the standing edict; Kerr had read it posted on the customs house himself, pretending to squint at it while actually reading it. Vessels whose masters evaded lawful process were subject to forfeiture and destruction. Forty years of Kerr's life were caulked into those planks. His pension, the pension he had sold his soul to keep, was drawn against the *Fairwind*'s earnings. If she burned, there would be no earnings. There would be nothing.

So the thought arrived—not fully formed, not dressed in honest language, but slinking into his head the way rats come aboard, through the hawsehole in the dark: *if the captain is here when they board, they take him, but they leave the ship.*

And then the second thought, the truly unforgivable one, arriving so quickly on the heels of the first that they might have been twins: *I arranged this.*

Kerr stood at the porthole with his hand on the frame and watched the boats come on, and he understood with terrible clarity that he was going to do nothing. Not ring the bell. Not pound on the captain's door. Not even make noise enough to disturb the mist. He was going to stand there, an old man in his nightshirt, and let it happen, because he had already decided it should happen three nights ago in the back room of the Anchor and Rope, and everything since had merely been theater.

The theater was the worst part, he would think later. The performance he put on for himself.

Because he did move, after all. He dressed—slowly, deliberately, taking time with each button as though buttons required a boatswain's expertise—and he came out onto the deck in the gray light, and he found young Tam Whistler at the rail, and he said, easy as you please, "You're up early, boy."

"Can't sleep, Boatswain," Tam said. "Too much quiet. A ship ought to creak more than this one does."

"Aye," Kerr said. "She's sulking." And he almost laughed at that, because it was true and because the boy had a feel for the vessel, and the laugh caught in his chest like a fishhook in a net. "Get below and rouse the watch, then. No sense in all of us being asleep when—" He stopped. He had been about to say *when the day starts*, which was innocent, and the innocence of it nearly choked him.

Tam went below. The boats came on.

* * *

Here is what Kerr would have said, if anyone had asked him—which no one did, and which he was too much a coward to offer—that he had never intended any of it. That is what every traitor says, and Kerr knew it was what every traitor says, and he hated that he knew it, because knowledge of your own cliché is no defense against committing it.

It had begun small, as such things do. It began with the blockade wages.

Six weeks the *Fairwind* had sat at anchor earning nothing. Six weeks of crew drawing pay against a hold full of unsold cargo, and the owners in the capital growing cold in their letters, and the chandlers growing cold in their credit. Kerr had a sister in the almshouse at Point Marrow with bad lungs and worse care, and the little he sent her monthly was the difference between medicine and prayers. When the company man had approached him—bought him a drink, complimented his service, spoken warmly of pensions well earned—Kerr had told himself he was only listening. Listening costs nothing. A man can listen to anything.

Then the man had mentioned Kerr's sister by name, and Kerr had understood that the conversation had started long before he entered the room, and somewhere in the fog of that realization—somewhere between the terror and the flattery and the arithmetic of his sister's lungs—he had nodded at whatever was asked.

*Is there truth aboard the Fairwind?* the man had asked, and it was such a strange question, such a ledger-book question, that Kerr had answered it like one. *There's a paper,* he'd said.

He had walked back to the harbor afterward feeling lighter, which was the cruelest joke of all. Guilt should weigh. Everyone knows guilt weighs. But for three days he had felt lighter, unburdened, the way a man feels when he has finally confessed a debt to a creditor—right up until the moment the interest comes due.

The interest came due at dawn, in four muffled boats.

* * *

They boarded without fuss, which somehow made it worse. Kerr had imagined shouting, resistance, drama—some grand moment where the betrayal became visible and therefore real. Instead a lieutenant in company blue came up the side, saluted the quarterdeck out of pure habit, and said to the watch, "Wake your officers. Captain Vane to be taken into custody by order of the Meridian Trading Company magistrate. All hands will muster on deck. No one leaves the ship."

The watch, bless them, froze. Young Tam stood at the mainmast with his mouth open like a landed cod. And Kerr—Boatswain Kerr, keeper of order aboard the *Fairwind* for nineteen years—heard himself say, in his best parade-ground voice, "You heard the officer. Rouse the watch. All hands. Move smartly now."

Move smartly now. Nineteen years of habit, deployed on behalf of the enemy. His body simply did what bodies do: it obeyed the last standing order, and the last standing order was cooperation.

Elias Vane came out of his cabin dressed, as though he had known—as indeed he probably had known, in the way old captains know weather before it shows. He stood on his quarterdeck in his worn blue coat with his white hair tied back, and he looked at the soldiers filling his deck like ants at a picnic, and he looked, last of all, at Kerr.

Just a look. Two seconds, maybe three. The look of a man doing sums.

And Kerr, who had prepared for this moment without admitting he had prepared for it, found that his face did exactly what faces do: nothing useful. He looked back at his captain with guilt written across him in letters a blind man could read, and Elias Vane—who had sailed with him through two wars and one mutiny and the death of half their messmates—took him in, and understood him, entirely, in silence.

Elias did not rage. That was the unbearable part. Kerr had rehearsed for rage. Rage he could have answered, could have shouted against, could have turned into a fight where his own guilt got lost in the general thunder. What he got instead was a small nod, almost gentle, and the words, quietly pitched for Kerr alone as the lieutenant read the charges:

"You look tired, Bosun. Get some sleep when this is done."

And then they took him down the side, and the mist closed behind the boat, and the *Fairwind* rocked gently on the gray water as though nothing whatsoever had occurred.

* * *

What does a coward do with his morning? This was the genuine philosophical puzzle Kerr faced, standing on his own deck among a prize crew of soldiers who regarded him with the mild contempt soldiers reserve for cooperative civilians. He could not stay—the company man's promise notwithstanding, his presence aboard was awkward now, evidence of inside knowledge. He could not flee—that would forfeit the pension, the entire point of the enterprise, the whole rotten architecture he had built his sister's lungs upon. So he did what cowards have always done. He went to breakfast.

He sat in the Anchor and Rope with a plate of kidneys going cold in front of him and did not eat them. Around him the dockside crowd buzzed with the news—word traveled fast when soldiers rowed anywhere—and every table within earshot was talking about the *Fairwind*.

"They say they took Captain Vane in irons," said the tinker to the coal-heaver.

"For treason," said the coal-heaver. "For what treason? The man refuses to hand over his own ships and that's treason? Then every widow who won't give up her house is a traitor too."

"There's papers, my cousin says. Papers with the governor's name on them."

"Papers." The coal-heaver spat, philosophically. "Half the misery of this world comes from papers."

Kerr stared at his kidneys. He had imagined, in his three nights of storytelling, that the betrayal would at least accomplish its purpose cleanly—that the letter would be seized, the danger ended, the account closed. But the soldiers had torn the captain's cabin apart and come up empty. He'd watched from the deck as they'd brought out the strongbox pried from under the floorboards and opened it on the quarterdeck, and the lieutenant had gone through it twice, and then a third time, and then had looked at Kerr with a expression Kerr would carry to his grave.

The letter wasn't there. Elias Vane had moved it, or hidden it, or given it away—and Kerr, with a lurch that had nothing to do with the kidneys, remembered the girl. Sterling's girl. The tally clerk. The one the whole harbor whispered was mixed up in it. The captain had been seen talking with her at the salt sheds; Silas had rowed her out one night past the blockade chain, and Kerr had pretended to be asleep for every minute of it.

Which meant the paper was loose. Which meant everything Kerr had sold his soul for had purchased nothing at all. The soldiers had the captain, but not the crime; the company had the man, but not the proof; and somewhere out there a girl with gray eyes and busy hands was carrying the one document in the world that could hang a governor—and could, incidentally, hang a boatswain, once anyone thought to ask how the company had known to board the *Fairwind* in the first place.

Kerr pushed the plate away and pressed his palms flat against the table, as if checking that the world was solid.

He thought of his sister at Point Marrow, and the thought no longer comforted him; it only invoiced him. He thought of Tam Whistler's open mouth at the mainmast, of the cook weeping quietly into her apron when they mustered the hands, of Silas—good lad, steady as a keel—now hunted, because Kerr's tea-stirring friend had names to work with now. Every man he had served beside for nineteen years was compromised, pursued, or chained, and the sum total of Kerr's gain was a pension payable by an institution that might itself be dissolved before the next quarter day, if the rumors about the governor had any salt in them.

He had betrayed his captain to save his pension from a company that his betrayal was helping to destroy. The elegance of it almost made him laugh. Almost. What came out instead was a sound the barman politely ignored.

* * *

Toward evening he walked the wharf. He had no destination; cowards rarely do, because every direction has consequences. The *Fairwind* rode at anchor under a prize crew, her lines wrong somehow—ships have a set to them when strangers handle them, the way a house smells different when another family moves in—and the sight of it hurt in a toothache sort of way he couldn't rub away.

At the end of the wharf, an old fishwife was packing up her stall for the night. Marta, the harbor called her; Kerr had bought sprats from her for twenty years. She worked slowly, favoring one wrist, and she did not look up when Kerr stopped near her stall, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

Then Marta said, without raising her eyes from her baskets, "Funny thing. Company men knew just where to board. Just the right ship. Just the right dawn. Nearly like somebody told them."

"Marta—"

"I'm not asking who." She stacked a basket with great care. "A man who'd answer that question wouldn't be worth asking. I'm only saying the harbor's small, Boatswain. Smaller than people think. And the water remembers."

She picked up her crates and went, leaving Kerr alone at the end of the wharf with the tide coming in and the lamps coming out and the whole glittering, gossiping harbor laid out before him like a witness list.

He stood there a long while. And it must be recorded, in fairness, that no angel visited him and no revelation struck—it was nothing so clean. It was only the slow accumulation of small weights: the memory of Elias's gentle nod, the taste of uneaten kidneys, the image of Tam Whistler's frozen face, the fishwife's flat voice saying *the water remembers*. Cowardice examined without excuse is a heavy cargo, and Kerr had spent his whole life loading ships, and he knew a vessel overloaded when he stood in one.

Somewhere out in the dark, past the blockade chain, a girl was carrying a paper that could break everything open—or get everyone she loved killed trying. And the company would be looking for her now, hunting her with the same patience that had sent four muffled boats through the morning mist. They would find her, unless someone who knew the harbor's every current, every patrol rotation, every gap in the chain—someone with nineteen years of keys and codes and quiet arrangements still folded in his pockets—

Kerr took his hat off. Ran a hand through what the years had left of his hair. Put the hat back on, straighter.

"Well," he said aloud, to nobody, to the tide, to the ghost of a captain who had told him to get some sleep. "Damn me for a fool, but I expect I'll want to be awake for this."

He turned toward the fish market, where the night boats came in, where a certain fishwife would be selling her sprats by lamplight—and where, if the water remembered anything, it might remember how to forgive.
