## 23. Seized

Elias Vane had always believed that a man could tell the shape of his day by the smell of the wind off the water before sunrise. That morning the wind smelled of tar and cold iron, and he knew, before his boots hit the deck of the *Fairwind*, that the day would be a bad one.

He'd slept in his coat, which he hadn't done since the blockade began. He told himself it was because the cabin was drafty. He told himself a great many things these days.

Silas was on deck already, coiling line with the particular carelessness of a young man pretending not to be worried. Elias had watched that pretense for three weeks now—the way Silas checked the harbor mouth every quarter hour without seeming to, the way his hands went still whenever oar-locks creaked in the fog. The boy thought he was subtle. Nobody aboard the *Fairwind* was subtle anymore. Fear had seen to that.

"Morning, Captain," Silas said. "Fog's thick."

"Fog's always thick."

"This fog's thicker."

Elias grunted and looked out at the harbor. Silas was right. It wasn't river fog—it sat wrong, too low and too still, like something holding its breath. And through it came the sound Elias had been waiting three weeks to hear and hoping never to hear: the measured slap-slap-slap of oars keeping military time.

"Get Kerr," he said quietly. "Wake the others. Don't run about. We're not going to give them a show."

Silas's face did something complicated—fear and fury fighting for the same ground—and then he nodded and went below without a wasted step. Good lad. Elias turned back to the rail and counted the shapes resolving out of the fog. Two longboats, then a third. Behind them, the gray bulk of a company cutter, its guns run out. Thirty men, perhaps forty. Enough.

He thought, absurdly, of his mother's kitchen, of the way she'd said *well then* when the roof beam cracked one winter night. Well then. You put your shoulder under what you can hold up, and you let the rest fall where it falls.

By the time the first longboat bumped against the hull, the crew was on deck in an untidy line—not armed, not resisting, just present. Kerr stood at Elias's left shoulder, big as a door, his jaw working around nothing. He hadn't met Elias's eyes since the night of the near-mutiny. He didn't meet them now. Elias noted that the way he noted everything these days: quietly, and filed under *wounds that don't bleed*.

The boarding party came up the side with more noise than competence. At their head climbed a lieutenant in company blue with a spotless coat and the unhappy expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. He unfolded a paper with great ceremony.

"Captain Elias Vane," he read, "you are taken into custody by warrant of the Meridian Trading Company, charged with treason against the Crown, conspiracy with foreign agents, and sedition." He glanced up. "You'll come quietly, I hope."

"I hope so too," Elias said. "It's early."

The lieutenant blinked, thrown by the absence of either bluster or begging. Elias had found over a long life that most officials prepared themselves thoroughly for resistance and not at all for courtesy. Courtesy disarmed them. It also bought time, and time was the only cargo he had left to protect.

"The brig is impounded," the lieutenant went on, recovering. "Effective immediately. Crew will be held aboard pending disposition."

"Pending disposition," Elias repeated. "That's a fine phrase. Means you don't know yet what you're allowed to do to us."

"Captain—"

"Lieutenant." Elias gestured pleasantly at the fog-bound harbor. "While your men are poking about my ship, let me save them some trouble. There's no letter. There's no paper of any consequence aboard. Whatever Hargrove thinks he's going to find, he sent forty men to rummage through salt pork and spare canvas."

"We have information—"

"I'm sure you do. Information travels. That's the trouble with information." He kept his voice light as a weather report while inside him something turned over, slow and heavy, like a whale sounding. Because it was true: the letter itself was gone from the *Fairwind*, but not gone from the world. And there was the other thing—the thing that had been living in his chest since the raid plans had first become rumor on the docks. The location. The second copy of the bill of lading, the corroboration, hidden where only Elias knew.

He had made a decision three nights ago, lying awake in his drafty cabin, and he had hoped he wouldn't have to keep it. Hope was a luxury for younger men.

* * *

They searched the *Fairwind* the way ants dismantle a dropped biscuit—thoroughly, chaotically, leaving a trail. Elias stood on his own quarterdeck as a prisoner in irons—he insisted on irons, actually, over the lieutenant's embarrassed objection, because shackles made a statement to his crew that words couldn't: *I am not fighting this, so neither shall you*—and watched soldiers open his sea chest, shake out his charts, pry up floorboards.

Kerr hovered nearby, and finally, when the soldiers were below, muttered without looking at him: "Cap'n. Say the word and half these boys go over the side."

"And the other half hangs us all for murder. No." Elias flexed his wrists in the iron. "Kerr. Look at me."

Kerr looked, at last, and Elias saw the shame in him plain as paint, and understood it in a way that surprised even himself. Kerr had been frightened. Frightened men sell things; it's the oldest trade in the world. What Kerr hadn't reckoned on was having to stand beside the man he'd sold afterward.

"You've a pension coming," Elias said quietly. "A wife with bad lungs. I know why you did what you did. I'd be lying if I said I never ran the sums myself."

"Cap'n—" Kerr's voice broke sideways. "I didn't think they'd take the whole ship."

"They always take the whole ship. That's how companies think—one ledger line at a time until the ledger's all there is." Elias looked out at the water. "Listen to me now, because I'll only say this once, and then they'll march me off and we may not speak again. Whatever happens—whatever they offer you, whatever you're afraid of—there's a thing I know. A place. One place. Do you understand me? They can search this ship to the keel and they will never find it, because it isn't here, and it isn't written anywhere, and the only man alive who knows it is standing here in irons being extremely polite to a lieutenant."

Kerr went very still.

"They'll try to make you tell them where I'd hide something," Elias went on. "They'll try to make me tell them. Neither of us will. But if you remember nothing else, remember this: whatever they promised you for selling a letter's existence—that debt dies with my silence. And my silence is the one cargo I can still deliver."

"Cap'n, I—"

"Elias Vane!" The lieutenant was back, red-faced. "My men have found a locked compartment beneath your berth. You'll open it."

"Will I?"

"You have the key or you'll regret—"

"It's empty," Elias said. "I keep the brandy in it when the Methodists come aboard. Take the whole cabinet if it makes your report fatter."

The compartment was, in fact, empty, and had been since the night he'd moved everything that mattered. The lieutenant knew it was empty the moment the lock clicked, and knew that Elias knew he knew, and the two of them stood there in a silence of mutual professional embarrassment while soldiers behind them smashed a perfectly good barrel of pickled herring on the theory that treason might be pickled inside it.

"Well then," Elias said softly, to no one. His mother again. She got everywhere, the dead.

* * *

They marched him down the gangway at midmorning, past the crew drawn up along the rail, and Elias did the thing he'd done at every parting of his sailing life: he touched two fingers to his brow, unhurried, as though catching a hat against the wind. Silas returned it with his chin up and his eyes wet and furious. Several of the younger hands copied the gesture badly, elbows everywhere. It was, Elias reflected, the closest thing his crew had to a religion, and he was glad to leave them practicing it.

The company cutter took him not to the civil jail but to a hulk anchored mid-harbor—the *Resolute*, an old line-of-battle ship stripped of masts and purpose, now serving as a floating warehouse for problems the company preferred not to have on land. Elias had helped provision her twenty years ago, back when she was a proud thing. Now her ports were shuttered and her lower deck smelled of mildew and despair, and the despair, he noticed, had been arranged in neat rows of hammocks.

They put him in a cabin—a real cabin, with a desk bolted to the floor, which told him they intended to interrogate rather than merely rot him. A guard took up station outside. Through the gunport he could see the *Fairwind* across the gray water, small and riderless, and beside it the company flag going up the impound halyard, and he felt the sight like a hand pressing on a bruise.

Thirty years. He'd been younger than Silas when he'd first signed aboard her predecessor. Ships were supposed to outlive their captains; that was the natural order, the comfort of it. But watching her sit captured at anchor, he understood that ships don't belong to anyone, really—they belong to the water, and the water lends them, and the lending can be called in.

He sat at the desk. He had, he estimated, several hours before anyone official came, and a decision to finish making.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain, as he liked to lay out everything:

The girl had the original letter—or Hargrove had it, or it was ash; he couldn't know, and wondering was a current that led nowhere useful. The manifest she'd risked the sea for—Silas had carried word of it before the nets closed, breathless, triumphant—was somewhere between here and the capital in the hands of the bravest printer's apprentice who ever lived. So the evidence existed. The evidence could survive without him.

But the location of the second copy—the duplicate he'd cached years ago, in the old days, when Sterling had first pressed the letter into his hands and Elias, being a careful man, had made arrangements of his own—that location lived in exactly one vessel: his own skull. He'd never written it down. Writing things down was how honest men got honest men killed.

And Hargrove would want it. Hargrove, who had verified the letter's contents through a traitor's mouth, who had seized the ship and taken the captain, who was not a fool however much Elias wished him stupider—Hargrove would understand quickly that a man who hides papers once hides them twice. And Hargrove would have tools for extracting such knowledge. Companies always did. That was nearly the definition of a company.

Elias looked at his own hands, folded on the desk like a settled argument. Sailor's hands. They'd tied knots in a hundred thousand storms, held drowning men, buried his friend Sterling at sea with a pearl diver's grace. They were steady now.

There was a way to make sure the location stayed delivered—delivered to the one authority no company agent could ever raid, subpoena, or bribe. It was not a pleasant way. It was, in point of fact, slightly mad, and he laughed aloud at it, alone in the mildew-smelling cabin, a short bark that startled the guard outside into opening the door a crack.

"Everything all right, Captain?"

"Fine," Elias called. "Remembered a joke. Old one. You wouldn't know it."

He tested the theory of it against every other option one last time. Refuse to talk, and they'd eventually believe there was nothing to tell—but they'd believe it faster if the telling became impossible. Talk, and die anyway, after. Escape? From a hulk, at fifty-eight, with a bad knee? He'd swum the harbor in his youth; he could maybe swim it now, but not carrying anything, and everything worth carrying was in his head.

Well then, his mother said, in the kitchen of his memory, wiping flour from her hands. Well then.

When the footsteps finally came—two sets, one military, one soft-soled and administrative, which meant Hargrove or someone wearing Hargrove's manners—Elias rose from the desk and stood at ease beside it like a man awaiting guests rather than judgment.

The door opened. The soft shoes belonged to a company examiner named Prosser, thin as a rule and twice as rigid, carrying a writing case. No Hargrove. Interesting. Hargrove sent instruments when he expected compliance and came himself when he expected theater.

"Captain Vane," Prosser said, setting the case on the desk with a click. "This needn't be lengthy. The company has evidence sufficient for conviction. What remains is mitigation. Cooperate fully—answer questions completely—and the tribunal may weigh it in your favor. The first question concerns documents removed from your possession prior to this vessel's impoundment. Where have you concealed them?"

"My goodness," Elias said. "Straight to business. I admire that in a clerk."

"Examiner."

"Mm." Elias smiled at him, and it was the smile he used on green hands the first week out, warm and rueful and faintly sorry for them. "Let me save us both the afternoon. You want to know where I hid things. Here is what I know about hiding things at sea, Examiner: the best hiding places aren't places at all. They're commitments. A hiding place can be found. A commitment—" he tapped his breastbone with one finger, gently, almost fondly— "a commitment goes where it goes and does not stop for questions."

Prosser's pen paused above the paper. "Are you refusing to answer?"

"I'm answering better than you asked."

"Captain, I am obligated to inform you that obstruction carries penalties independent of the primary charge."

"I should think everything carries independent penalties with your company," Elias said. "You charge the tide for coming in." He sat down uninvited, folding his hands, and let the warmth drain out of his voice by degrees, the way he'd do before a gale, so the boy sailors would understand the weather had changed. "Examiner. I'm going to tell you exactly one true thing today, and you may write it in your tidy hand and carry it to whoever sent you. The documents you're hunting cannot be found, because they are no longer located anywhere you can reach. Every hour you spend pursuing them is an hour given freely to the people who will use them. Tell Hargrove that. Tell him he has spent his career reading ledgers, and he has learned their lesson too well: he believes everything can be balanced, collected, seized." Elias leaned back. "Some entries, Examiner, are kept in trust. And some trustees keep their accounts in the one vault no writ can open."

Prosser stared at him. "That is gibberish."

"Not yet," Elias said agreeably. "Give it a few years and a little salt water."

They took him up on deck after that, into the gray afternoon, and there was a moment—a small one, easily missed—when the guards' attention divided between him and the business of securing the launch, and Elias Vane, enemy of the crown, stood at the rail of the dead ship *Resolute* with the whole harbor laid out before him like a chart.

He looked at the water. He looked at the *Fairwind*, at the flag flying over her that had no right to her. He thought of Sterling underwater with the pearls, of a girl with busy hands and lowered eyes learning, God help them all, to lift both. He thought of a promise sealed in peacetime over brandy, and of how promises, unlike ships, cannot be impounded.

Then Elias Vane tipped his head back, and swallowed.

Not the papers—those were safe, and would stay safe, in the dark wet reef-cache only his memory had ever marked. But the *location*—the bearings, the depths, the three landmarks in their secret relation—he took those down deliberately, in the manner of a man taking a bitter medicine he has prescribed for himself, sealing the account in the one vault no writ can open, as he'd told the startled examiner with more foresight than the poor clerk could possibly have appreciated.

The guards saw only an old captain standing oddly still at the rail. Prosser, watching from the cabin stair, wrote in his case notes that the prisoner appeared *composed to the point of insolence*.

Elias composed himself further. When they led him below, he went with perfect courtesy, two fingers touching his brow at the rail in farewell—to the *Fairwind*, to the harbor, to thirty years—and if his step was slower than it had been that morning, well. Gales change a man's walk. Everyone knows that.

In his new cell, in the dark that smelled of tar and tides and other men's endings, Elias Vane sat on the cot and permitted himself, at last, one minute of honest fear. Then he counted it—because a man who counts things owns them instead of being owned—and put it away.

*Keep it safe,* Sterling had said, years ago, brandy in hand, eyes bright with the terrible prescience of drowning men. *If the company ever asks about my blood, give this to my girl.*

Delivered, Elias told the darkness. Every entry accounted for. And now let them ask their questions, and let Hargrove come in person next time, with all his oily courtesy, and find that the vault before him opens for nobody—

Above, muffled through the deck, came the sound he'd been waiting for all day: the ship's bell, ringing not the watch but the summons. Word traveled fast in harbors. Somewhere across the black water, the girl Sterling had heard he was taken.

Elias smiled into the dark, slow and unrepentant, a man who had just made himself the most valuable piece of evidence in the harbor—and the least recoverable.

*Come then, girl,* he thought, settling back against the bulkhead as boots thundered overhead. *Come and ask the captain what her father carried. And mind you ask loudly enough for the whole square to hear.*
