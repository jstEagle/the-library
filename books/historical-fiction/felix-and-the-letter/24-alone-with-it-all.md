## 24. Alone With It All

The morning after the raid, the harbor smelled of tar and shame.

Oscar stood at the edge of the fish market with her collar up and her hands buried in her pockets, watching men she had known all her life look away from her. Not glance away — that would have been something, that would have been human. They turned their whole bodies, slow and deliberate, like compass needles refusing north. Old Tomas, who had taught her to gut a mackerel when she was nine and still owed her father three coppers for a lost wager, spat into the water when she passed. He didn't spit *at* her. He simply spat, as if her presence had put the taste in his mouth.

She couldn't blame them. She'd tried, on the walk down from the dormitories, to assemble the argument in her own defense the way she assembled ledgers: column by column, entry by entry. *I did not bring soldiers to the Fairwind. I did not know about the spy aboard. I offered Hargrove a forgery, which was, if anything, an act of loyalty dressed in treason's clothes.* But arguments require an audience willing to read them, and audiences were exactly what she had spent nineteen years learning to avoid. Now, when she finally needed to be seen, the whole city had learned her trick. Everyone was invisible to her at once.

Marta's stall was shuttered.

That was the thing that undid her, more than the glances, more than the spitting. Marta's stall had never been shuttered — not through storms, not through the fever year, not the time the harbormaster's nephew had come around demanding protection money and Marta had met him at the stall's edge holding a filleting knife and a smile that made him reconsider his career. Rain or ruin, that stall opened. The awning went up before dawn and the fish went out silver-bright on beds of chipped ice, and the whole western row set its clock by the sound of Marta arguing prices with God Himself.

Shuttered, it looked like a held breath. A mouth that had been slapped shut.

Oscar stood in front of it for a long moment, and then, because standing in front of it felt like a kind of trespass, she walked on.

* * *

She found Marta that afternoon, and she wished she hadn't.

They had released her the night before — Hargrove's arithmetic being what it was, a freed sympathizer was worth more than a jailed one when you wanted a girl to come running — and Marta was back at her lodgings above the net-mender's shop, sitting in a chair by the window with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a photograph to be taken of her.

"Marta."

Nothing. Not a blink. Marta had always been loud; it was her signature, her weather. She called across harbors. She called across arguments she wasn't party to. When Oscar's mother had still been alive, Marta's voice had come through the floorboards of their rooms some evenings like a warm tide, carrying gossip and recipes and opinions nobody had asked for, and Papa used to say you could navigate home from the open sea by the sound of that woman complaining.

Now her voice had gone somewhere, and what sat in the chair was the case it used to come in.

"I brought—" Oscar had brought nothing. Her hands had needed to do something so they had carried her here instead. "Marta, it's me. It's Oscar. Sterling's girl."

At that, something moved behind Marta's eyes — a fish turning under ice — and her mouth opened and closed twice before any sound came out of it.

"They asked me questions," she said finally, and her voice was small and flat and wrong, a voice for saying names off a list. "For two days they asked me questions. In a little room with no window. And I told them nothing, girl, I want you to know that, I told them nothing, but—"

Her hands came apart in her lap and Oscar saw they were shaking, fine and constant, like rigging in a wind too small to see.

"—but my hands won't stop remembering it. Two days and one night. My hands are still there." She looked at them with mild, terrible curiosity, as if they belonged to a stranger who had wandered in and refused to leave. "You go and you win your war or whatever it is you're fighting. But don't you come back here needing anything from me. I've got nothing left to give anyone. They took the pot empty."

Oscar knelt beside the chair. She wanted to say: *it was for you, all of it, the forgery and the risk and the stepping off the safe path — it was to get you out.* She wanted to say it because it was true and because true things are supposed to be a comfort, and she understood, kneeling there, that it was not a comfort at all. It was an indictment. Marta had paid for Oscar's gamble in the only currency she had left, two days and a night in a room without a window, and telling her so changed the price of nothing.

So instead she said, "Your awning rope is frayed. Left corner. I noticed on my way past. If you don't mend it, the first real blow will take the whole frame down."

And Marta looked at the frayed rope of the silence between them and said, quietly, "I know," and let Oscar let herself out.

* * *

Silas was gone.

Not arrested — that, at least, she could have borne, because arrested meant a place, a wall, a door she could stand outside of with a forged pass and a plan. Gone meant hunted. After the raid, when the Fairwind was seized and Elias taken off his own quarterdeck in irons, Silas had vanished into the city the way a drop of water vanishes into the sea, which was sensible and correct and left her with no way to say goodbye or be brave together or do any of the hundred small things people do for each other when the world has come apart. She checked the salt sheds twice. She left no messages, because messages could be read. She walked past the print shops where he'd once served his apprenticeship and saw, in the window of the third one, a company notice with fresh ink still smelling of it:

**BY ORDER OF MAGISTRATE HARGROVE. REWARD FOR INFORMATION LEADING TO THE APPREHENSION OF THE FUGITIVE KNOWN AS SILAS, DECKHAND OF THE SEIZED VESSEL FAIRWIND, WANTED FOR QUESTIONS PERTAINING TO TREASON.**

*Treason.* She read the word four times. It had begun with a census form and a lie about her father's birthplace, and now the word treason hung in print-shop windows like bunting, and somewhere in this city a man with careful hands and ink-stained cuffs was moving between hiding places because he had helped her, and every hour he moved was an hour he could be seen, and being seen, she knew better than almost anyone, was fatal.

She thought about the hold of the Fairwind, the lamplight swinging, the smell of ink and pitch, his shoulder warm against hers as they worked the press. *If we live through this,* he'd said, not looking up from the plate he was etching, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather, *I'm going to take you somewhere with no harbor at all. Inland. Hills. Nothing to sail on for a hundred miles. You'll hate it inside a week.*

*I'll love it inside a week,* she'd said, and he'd said, *that's what I'm afraid of,* and neither of them had laughed, because fear had been sitting right there with them at the worktable, but they had both smiled at the plate like fools.

Now she stood in the street reading his description — *youngish, dark-haired, answers to no known family name* — and understood that the future they had sketched in the hold now depended entirely on whether he stayed invisible long enough for the world to change around him. She had taught him her oldest trick without meaning to. She prayed to a sea she didn't believe in that he was better at it than she had ever been.

* * *

She went to see the trial date posted, and that was where the dockworkers found her.

Elias's tribunal was set for the harbor square — public, of course; Hargrove understood theater better than he understood shipping, which was why Meridian's books bled while his gallows ran full. The notice was already nailed to the customs house board, and a knot of men stood before it, and Oscar, out of nineteen years of habit, slowed to read over shoulders rather than push through, and that was her mistake, because slowing made her visible, and visible was what she could no longer afford to be.

"So it's her." The voice belonged to a stevedore named Brann, a big man with forearms like mooring posts, whose son had signed aboard the Fairwind two seasons back. He didn't raise his voice. That was somehow worse. "The Sterling girl. Come to see what her errands bought."

"I didn't bring them to the Fairwind," Oscar said. "I'd never—"

"Never what?" He turned, finally, and gave her the full weight of his regard, and it was heavier than any cargo she'd ever tallied. "Half my boy's wages came off that ship. Now she's impounded and he's ashore with no berth and a black mark, and the captain's in chains, and it all started moving the week *you* started moving. Fishwives talking, papers flying, a girl who never said boo to anybody suddenly going everywhere and asking everything." He shook his head slowly. "We watched you grow up on these docks, girl. Quiet as ballast. Turns out ballast can be bought."

"I wasn't bought."

"No," said an older voice, and old Tomas was there again, arms crossed, eyes like gull's eggs. "Worse. You weren't bought. You just didn't think about what the wake would do to the rest of the boats. Bought we can understand. Careless, out here, drowns people."

There was nothing to say to that. That was the discovery of her afternoon: there was nothing to say, because they were not wrong. She had stepped off the safe path knowing other people were on it. She had counted the risks to herself with a clerk's precision and waved the rest through as acceptable losses, the way companies do, the way Hargrove does, and hearing her own arithmetic recited back to her in Tomas's gravel voice made her stomach turn over.

"Trial's Friday," Brann said, turning back to the notice. "Anybody speaks for the captain hangs beside him, by order. So nobody'll speak. And you—" he didn't look at her "—you ought to be gone before Friday. For your own sake. Some of the lads are angrier than me."

She walked away down the row with their silence at her back, and it followed her the whole length of the quay like a tide coming in.

* * *

The northbound coaler loaded at Pier Nine.

She had known that for years, the way everyone on the docks knew it — the *Cormorant*, tramping coal from the southern fields to the northern yards, in port four days, sailing on the evening tide Thursday. What she had never known, until tonight, was the second mate's name, or the fact that he drank at the Anchor and Chain and complained, loudly and to anyone, about being short-handed since two stokers had jumped ship in the spring.

Tonight she knew it because she had gone to find out. That was the whole of her evening's ambition. She had not decided anything; she wanted to be very clear with herself about that, and failed to be clear with herself about anything. She had only gone to find out whether leaving was possible, the way a drowning woman checks the depth of the water without committing to it.

The second mate bought her a cider she didn't drink and heard her out with professional disinterest. No papers needed for stokers, not on a tramp like his, not north. Hard work, black work, four hours on and four off. Paid at the first coaling port, cash, no questions. Sail Thursday, seven o'clock, be at the pier by six.

"You'll want a name that isn't yours," he added, examining his glass. "Company's got watchers at the gates these days. Give me something dull. Dull names travel light."

She walked back along the waterfront under the lamps with the word rolling around in her chest. *Thursday.* Three days. Before the trial. Before Elias stood in the square and said *ask the girl what her father carried,* which he would say, she knew he would say it, because he was a man who kept promises past all reason, and if she wasn't there to answer then the words would fall on empty air and the tribunal would do what tribunals do to empty air.

If she stayed, she might hang beside him — Brann had said it plainly enough — and what would the letter prove then? What would the manifest prove? Dead girls testify poorly. Dead girls' truths get filed under sedition and burned with the rest.

If she left, she lived. Dull name, black work, four on and four off, hills maybe someday, a place with no harbor at all — no, that had been Silas's dream, and Silas was a fugitive, and Marta was a husk in a chair, and Elias was in chains because he had kept faith with a drowned pearl diver's memory, and every single one of those threads ran back through her hands.

*Careless, out here, drowns people.*

She stopped at the harbor wall, at the stretch of it where the water deepened fast, over the old beds. Papa's water. Somewhere down there the reef held the scar of his last dive, and somewhere above it she had spent nineteen years learning to be nothing, and the lesson had saved her right up until the moment it hadn't, and now both paths — visible and invisible — seemed to end in the same gray place.

She thought about her father's letter. She had read it a dozen times now, the evidence pages and the private ones beneath, and there was a line in it she returned to the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: *A Sterling should live loudly, girl. The sea doesn't honor the quiet ones. It only fails to notice them.*

Papa. Who had died diving, visibly, loudly, reaching for something worth reaching for.

She looked down at the black water and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for six hours. Thursday, seven o'clock. Trial, Friday morning. The coaler would clear the headland while Elias was being marched to the square, and whatever happened there would happen behind her, growing smaller, unprovable, unwitnessed. Crane would keep his office. Hargrove would keep his ledger. Finch's archive would rot in a garret. Marta would sit by her window with her shaking hands until her hands forgot how to shake, which was worse.

And somewhere in the city, Silas was trusting her to finish what they'd started in the hold.

She gripped the wall's cold stone and leaned into the wind coming off the water and let herself, for exactly one minute by the harbor clock, want it — the coaler, the coal dust, the dull name, the blessed gray anonymity of four hours on and four off, a life with no one in it to lose. She let the wanting have her whole chest. It tasted like safety and it tasted like ash and she could not tell the difference anymore.

Then the minute ended, because the harbor clock struck nine, and Oscar Zhou Sterling wiped her face with the heel of her hand, straightened, and admitted the truth she had climbed the wall to avoid: she was going to stay. She was going to be visible. She was going to walk into that square on Friday and speak, hang beside him or no, because some debts didn't clear at any port north of here.

She just didn't know how. She didn't know how to make a city listen to a girl it had already decided to forget, with three days, no allies, and a warrant-shaped hole where her future used to be. Every road she mapped dead-ended at a locked door, a shuttered stall, a printed notice.

"There you are."

She spun, heart slamming, hand going to the knife at her belt before her eyes caught up with the voice — a dry, precise, faintly apologetic voice she had heard exactly once at close range, on a wet dawn she'd spent weeks trying to forget.

Mr. Aldous Finch stood a few feet down the wall, hat in his hands against the wind, notebook nowhere in sight, looking at her with the expression of a man who had counted something very carefully and found the total troubling.

"We should talk," said the census taker. "Before Thursday."
