## 25. The Census Taker's Offer

Aldous Finch had counted eleven thousand four hundred and six souls in the port of Meridian since spring, and he could tell you something particular about every one of them.

The harbormaster's wife, for instance, had given her age as thirty-four on three consecutive censuses, which was either a miracle of biology or a lesson in the elasticity of truth. The lamplighter on Cordwain Street had listed his occupation as "gentleman," though Finch had personally watched him trim forty wicks in the rain. And the tally clerk Oscar Sterling—daughter of the drowned diver, ward of the Meridian Trading Company, entry flagged with red thread in the registry—had lied to him about her father's birthplace with the flat, steady voice of someone who had practiced lying the way other people practiced scales.

Finch knew lies. He collected them, in a sense. Not out of malice—he wished that understood, though it never was—but because a lie was simply a number that refused to add up, and Aldous Finch had never in his life been able to walk past a sum that wouldn't balance.

He had watched her come down the gang stairs at dawn three days after the raid on the Fairwind, hem dark with harbor water, hair plastered to one cheek, moving through the fish-market crowd like a ghost who hadn't yet been told she was dead. He had said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing. It was one of his few social graces.

But he had gone home to his garret above the chandler's shop, and he had sat among his shelves—the ledgers, the cross-references, the twenty-two years of irregularities he had recorded in a hand so small and neat that his mother, were she alive, would have wept with pride—and he had asked himself a question he had never once asked in two decades of counting:

*What is the count for?*

* * *

It had started, as most ruinous things do, innocently.

His first posting had been a village so small the census took an afternoon. He had counted one hundred and twelve people, four goats (disputed), and one lie: a widow who claimed her son was a sailor when everyone knew—and this came out years later, over gooseberries—that the boy had never left, but had lived hidden in her root cellar for seven years rather than be conscripted.

Finch had written "occupation: sailor" in his ledger because the law required it. But beneath it, in his tiny hand, he had also written: *"Occupation, actual: loved too much to report."*

That was the first entry in what would become, without his quite intending it, an archive.

Because the thing was—once you started noticing discrepancies, you could not stop. A ship listed as carrying grain that docked riding three feet too high in the water. A tax abatement granted to a warehouse that had burned, according to the fire brigade's own records, on a date eighteen months before the fire. A governor whose household accounts showed the purchase of sixty dozen wine glasses in a single year, which suggested either spectacular breakage or spectacular guests, and Governor Crane was not, by all accounts, a clumsy man.

Each anomaly went into its ledger. Each ledger went onto its shelf. And there they sat, year after year, like seeds in a drawer, because Aldous Finch was a census taker and not a magistrate, and no one—not once in twenty-two years—had ever asked him what he knew.

Until now, nobody had. But now the girl existed. Now there was a captain in chains in the company lockup, scheduled for tribunal in nine days. Now there was a red thread tied around one Oscar Sterling's name, and threads, Finch had observed, do not merely mark a page. They are pulled.

He found her at the docks on a Thursday, because she was always at the docks on Thursdays; he had charted her movements for eleven days, which he recognized even as he did it was not entirely normal behavior, and had resolved to mention it to no one, including himself.

She was sitting on a bollard with a bread roll she wasn't eating, watching the northbound coaler take on cargo with the fixed attention of someone pricing escape.

"Good morning," Finch said. "Or possibly afternoon. I've been up since four and time has gone soft."

She startled badly. Her hand went into her coat—to the knife, he supposed; the drowned diver's daughter would carry one—and Finch raised both hands, palms out, holding his umbrella aloft like a white flag.

"It's the census man," she said. Not a question. Her voice was flat as slate.

"Mr. Finch. Yes." He lowered the umbrella. Rain had begun, that fine coastal mist that didn't fall so much as accumulate. "You wrote 'Meridian-born' for your father's place of birth."

"I did."

"You didn't. Your father was born in—" he consulted nothing, because he never needed to; the number was simply there—"a port your people call Saltmere, on charts your government doesn't print. I know because I interviewed a pearl buyer in 'ninety-one who traded there and described it in detail, chiefly the taverns. I have it in the green ledger. Third shelf. It's very well organized."

Oscar Sterling stared at him the way one stares at a dog that has begun reciting poetry.

"What do you want?"

"May I sit?" He gestured at the bollard's far end. She didn't answer, which he correctly took as permission. Sitting, he discovered, put them nearly eye to eye, as he was not a tall man; he was, by his own measurement, precisely average, a fact he had confirmed twice.

"I want," he said, "to make you an offer that will sound insane, and I'd like you to hear all of it before you walk away, because you're going to want to walk away almost immediately."

Her eyes narrowed. Up close he could see the exhaustion in her, the particular grayness of someone running on stubbornness alone. It was, he thought—not for the first time—a terrible waste. She had excellent bone structure for a portrait. Nobody painted tally clerks.

"I know about the letter," Finch said.

The change in her was instant and total—the stillness of prey, the hand back inside the coat. "You don't know anything."

"I know a sealed packet left the Fairwind's hold in the custody of Captain Vane eight years ago, per the harbormaster's outbound log, which lists it under 'personal effects, weight: half a pound.' Half a pound is heavy for sentiment. It's about right for paper and wax and perhaps a document or two." He paused. "I know Hargrove believes he already has the contents, because someone aboard sold him their existence, if not the papers themselves. I know the tribunal is set for the ninth, and that trials scheduled that quickly are not trials, they're funerals with paperwork."

"You've been spying on me." Her voice shook slightly. "For the company?"

"For no one." And here was the crux, and Finch felt, oddly, nervous—which he noted with clinical interest, as he had not known he could be nervous; he had assumed the capacity had atrophied. "Miss Sterling. May I tell you what I am? Truly? No one has ever asked, so you'll be the first to hear the full account, and I warn you it is dull right up until it isn't."

She said nothing. He proceeded anyway. In his experience, silence meant listening ninety percent of the time, and the remaining ten percent meant running, and she hadn't run.

"I count people. That is the entire job. Where they were born, whom they belong to, what they do, whether they exist. The crown wants the numbers so it can tax them and conscript them, and for twenty-two years I have provided honest numbers to a dishonest system, and told myself that accuracy was a virtue regardless of who held the ledger." He looked out at the coaler, its holds yawning black. "And then I watched a girl walk out of the harbor at dawn with wet hems and a face like the last day of a war, and I realized I have spent my whole life recording what people are, and it has never once changed what happens to them. Twenty-two years. Eleven thousand entries. And every person in my books who mattered got ruined while my figures stayed perfectly, uselessly correct."

The rain thickened. She pulled her collar up. She did not leave.

"So here is the offer," Finch said. "I have a private archive. Twenty-two years of Crane's irregularities—the ships that vanished, the cargoes that never existed, the taxes remitted to ghosts, the wine glasses. Sixty dozen, incidentally, in one year. Either the governor entertains enormously or he's selling crystal to someone across the water, and I can show you which, arithmetically. It's all inadmissible, technically. Census records aren't evidence. But patterns are patterns, and magistrates are men, and men can be made to see a pattern if you hit them with enough of it."

"Why?" The word came out raw. "Why help me? You don't know me. You marked my file yourself."

"Yes," Finch said quietly. "I did. And I have thought about that entry every day since, which for me is a considerable amount of thinking. Miss Sterling, I marked you because the law required marking you, and I have just finished explaining why that excuse no longer serves. Also—" and here he hesitated, because the next part embarrassed him, and he was a man constitutionally unaccustomed to embarrassment, having long ago concluded that feelings, like errors, should be acknowledged promptly and corrected in the next edition— "also, you are the first one who counted."

She frowned. "What does that mean?"

"When I asked your parents' places of birth, you lied. Everyone lies. Sailors lie about their ages, widows lie about their sons, the harbor-master's wife lies about arithmetic itself. But they lie carelessly, the way people breathe." He turned to look at her fully. "You lied carefully. You chose a town adjacent to your father's actual birthplace—not the same, but close enough to seem plausible, far enough to break the trail. Do you know how many people understand that a good lie must be *structured*? None. In eleven thousand people, none. You understood that being unremarkable was a discipline. You had studied invisibility the way I study ledgers. And for one moment across that table I thought: *there is another person in this city who counts.* And then I wrote the red thread anyway, because I was a coward with good penmanship."

For a long moment she said nothing. The coaler blew its horn, a low animal sound. Somewhere behind them the fish market was waking up—gulls screaming, Marta's stall standing empty and dark, which Finch saw Oscar notice, and saw her flinch at.

"If I say yes," she said finally, "what happens to you? Hargrove would hang you beside Elias. Beside me. He hangs sympathizers, that's the whole point of the square."

"Oh, I've considered it." Finch adjusted his spectacles. "I've actually made a list of probable outcomes, weighted by likelihood. Would you like to hear it? I find lists comforting."

"No."

"Hangings, mostly. Various." He cleared his throat. "But here is the thing about being a census taker that no one appreciates: I am *everywhere*, Miss Sterling, and I am *no one*. I have stood in ten thousand doorways asking my little questions, and no door has ever closed on me, because a man with a ledger is furniture. Hargrove knows my name the way you know the name of your broom. If I am careful—if we are careful—I can move through this city collecting testimony, tax rolls, shipping manifests, and the wine glass receipts, and by the time anyone wonders why the furniture is walking, the tribunal will already have heard more arithmetic than it can survive."

"And if you're wrong? If they catch us?"

"Then I shall die having done, for the first time, something with the numbers besides keeping them." He said it lightly, but his hands, he noticed, had gone cold on the umbrella handle, and he catalogued the sensation for future reference. "I would prefer not to. I have gooseberry cuttings coming in spring. But preference is not policy."

Oscar Sterling looked at him for a long, long moment. Rain dripped off the bollard between them, counting its own small time.

"The green ledger," she said at last. "The pearl buyer. Saltmere."

"Ninety-one. Tavern section is particularly thorough."

"My father was born in Saltmere." Her voice cracked on it, just barely, a hairline fracture in slate. "You had it right the whole time. In a book. On a shelf. While they put a red thread on my file for hiding what you already knew."

"Yes," Finch said. And found, to his surprise, that the apology rose in him like floodwater, twenty-two years of it, every careful cowardly entry. "Yes. That is exactly the size of it. I'm sorry is insufficient; I've drafted better versions on the walk down, all of which have abandoned me. So I won't insult you with one sentence. I'll insult you with six ledgers instead, and a promise: from tonight forward, every true thing I know goes where you point it. Consider me... recalculated."

Something moved in her face—too fast to name, grief and fury and something that might, in better weather, have been laughter. She wiped her cheek with the back of her wrist, briskly, as though the rain were solely responsible.

"The pearl buyer," she said. "Your interview from 'ninety-one. Did he ever mention shipments out of Saltmere? Grain ships, he'd have said, except they weren't."

Finch went very still. Then he smiled—the rare, genuine article, the smile that made children distrust him—and reached into his coat for his notebook.

"Miss Sterling," he said, flipping to a page dated nineteen years back, "I have been waiting two decades for someone to ask me that question. There were four such ships. One of them sank exactly where your father dove. Shall we begin?"

* * *

They began, of course, with arithmetic—because everything with Finch began with arithmetic—and by the time the rain stopped, the two of them sat shoulder to shoulder on the bollard like old conspirators, while a census taker taught a tally clerk the true ledger of Port Meridian: four ghost ships, sixty dozen wine glasses, one warehouse fire that predated itself, and a pattern in the missing tonnage that pointed, with terrible precision, straight out to the reef where Sterling's father had gone down.

Somewhere across the harbor, in the company lockup, a captain slept—or didn't—in irons, nine days from the gallows.

Somewhere above the chandler's shop, six ledgers waited to be carried, one pocket at a time, into a war.

And Aldous Finch, average height, twenty-two years of service, gooseberries pending, closed his notebook and felt—for the first time in his meticulous, invisible life—perfectly, dangerously *counted*.
