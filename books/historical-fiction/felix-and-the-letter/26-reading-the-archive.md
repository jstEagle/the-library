## 26. Reading the Archive

Oscar had expected a hideout. What she got was a hermit's nest balanced over a chandlery like a teetering stack of ledgers.

Finch's garret smelled of ink, dust, and something faintly medicinal—camphor, perhaps, deployed against the moths that had clearly lost the war. Every wall was shelved floor to ceiling, and every shelf groaned under boxes, folios, and bundles tied with the fraying string of a man who never threw away a piece of paper in his life. Papers covered the single table like snowfall. A narrow bed stood wedged into one corner, made up with military precision, the only tidy thing in the room.

"You live here," she said, "with all of this."

"I work here," Finch said, hanging her wet coat on a hook with the fussy care he brought to everything. "I sleep here also, briefly, between counting. Sit. Mind the 1897 mortality rolls; they are fragile and they are friends of mine."

She sat. The chair creaked but held.

He had carried his archive down from a false panel behind the wardrobe in three armfuls, wrapped in oilcloth, moving with the breathless urgency of a man who had waited years for someone to ask. She had told him everything on the walk from the docks—the letter, the bill of lading, Elias in chains, Silas locked in the company lockup, the manifest waiting somewhere in the cold green dark where her father had died. He had listened without interrupting once, which she suspected was the longest silence of his adult life.

"Now," he said, rubbing his long hands together, "we count."

"Count what?"

"Everything." His eyes, behind their smudged spectacles, had lit like harbor lamps. "The census is not merely names, Miss Sterling. It is arithmetic. People arrive, people depart, ships come heavy and leave light or the reverse, taxes are assessed on cargo that does not appear on manifests. Individually these are errors. Together—" he drew a box toward him and lifted its lid with the reverence of a priest opening a reliquary "—they are a confession."

* * *

Finch had known something was wrong with Governor Crane for eleven years.

He had not known it as a scandal knows it, with shouting and headlines. He had known it the way he knew everything: by subtraction. In the spring of 1889, the *Cormorant* had cleared the port with a declared crew of twenty-three and returned, per his door-to-door canvass of the sailors' quarter, with nineteen. No deaths recorded. No wages paid out to widows. Four men simply ceased, arithmetically speaking, to exist.

He had written a memorandum. It had been received, initialled by a junior clerk, and never seen again.

In 1892 there had been the matter of the warehouse on Rope Walk, assessed for storage of raw sugar at a rate suggesting full capacity year-round while the customs house recorded imports amounting to barely half that. In 1895, six vessels registered to Meridian subsidiaries whose founding papers named the same three men, none of whom appeared to exist in any census Finch had ever taken—and Finch had taken all of them. He had knocked on the address given for one *A. Pemberton, shipping magnate*, and found a vacant lot where pigeons conducted their business.

"Paper ghosts," Oscar said slowly, turning a tax roll toward the lamp.

"Precisely. Companies that own companies that own wharves. Follow the ownership and you follow nothing; the trail dissolves like sugar in tea. So I stopped following ownership." He tapped the roll of 1897. "I followed tonnage instead. Cargo weighs, Miss Sterling. Weight cannot be incorporated, cannot be assigned a fictitious director, cannot vanish into a holding structure. Weight goes over a quay on a crane and onto a ship, and someone must be paid to lift it, and stevedores are paid in cash and remember cash."

"So you asked the stevedores."

"I am a census taker," Finch said, with a small, dry smile. "Asking questions and writing down answers is the whole of my profession. Nobody guards against a census taker. I am furniture with a notebook. It has been my great advantage in life to be underestimated by everyone, including, until this week, myself."

Something in Oscar's chest turned over. She knew that trick of living—busy hands, lowered eyes, the long patience of the unnoticed. She had thought herself its sole practitioner. Here was a narrow, spectacled man who had spent a decade doing exactly the same thing, one question at a time, in service of an obsession no one had asked him to pursue.

"Why?" she asked. "You could have stopped. You did stop, mostly—you wrote your memoranda and filed them. Why keep going?"

Finch was quiet for a moment. He took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and put them back on, buying himself the interval.

"In 1894," he said, "I counted a family in Cutter's Lane. Father a rigger, mother took in washing, four children. The following year I returned and the father had been arrested as a foreign sympathizer on the testimony of an anonymous informant. Deported to a country he had left at age two. The mother and children were evicted inside a month; I know because I recounted the lane and found them gone." He aligned a pencil with the table edge, very precisely. "The informant's statement, when I eventually saw it through channels I will not describe, contained an error of fact regarding the man's birthplace. A clerical error. Mine. I had transposed two digits in the register the year before, and the investigation had built itself upon my mistake like a house upon sand."

Oscar said nothing. Her father's file, flagged with red thread, rose in her memory.

"A number," Finch went on, softly, "that passed through my hands ruined a family. I could not unmake it. But I could make certain I understood precisely how many such numbers were passing through other hands, deliberately, and into whose pockets they fell. It became—not a penance. Penance implies forgiveness is possible. A debt schedule, perhaps. I keep accounts." He glanced up. "You would be astonished how many of us there are. Quiet people with pencils. We are everywhere. We are the reason empires bother with forms at all."

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low and Finch lit a second, and then a third, constructing a little city of light on the table.

The method, once Oscar understood it, was almost embarrassingly simple—the way a riptide is simple. Take each anomaly Finch had collected: a ship clearing port heavier than her manifest allowed, a warehouse taxed beyond its recorded trade, a crew list that shrank, a subsidiary that existed only on paper. Plot each one by date. Plot each by berth. And a shape emerged.

"Look." Finch laid three documents side by side. "*Meridian's* berths take ordinary traffic, all aboveboard, all dull. But every anomalous shipment—every one, going back to '89—loaded or discharged at Pier Nine. Company records show Pier Nine leased since 1891 to the Halcyon Shipping Concern." He raised his eyebrows. "Which, as we have established, consists of three gentlemen who do not exist."

"Pemberton and friends."

"The very phantoms. And who countersigns Halcyon's lease renewals?" He slid across a brittle page, and there at the bottom, in the cramped self-satisfied hand she had seen on proclamations nailed to every post in the city: *Nathaniel Crane, Governor.*

Oscar sat back. The garret seemed very quiet, just the tick of the stove and the murmur of rain beginning against the skylight.

"All right," she said. "Pier Nine. Ships heavier than they should be. What was being loaded?"

"That depends which direction." Finch spread a hand-drawn chart of the coastal waters, annotated in his tiny upright script. "Outbound anomalies cluster in spring and autumn—small, dense cargoes, loaded at night per the watch logs I obtained, crated to a uniform size. Inbound anomalies are fewer but larger, and here is the beautiful part—" he said *beautiful* the way another man might say *atrocious* "—the inbound ships always arrive from the south, and they always arrive *lighter than they departed their last port*. Yet they pay duty on full tonnage."

Oscar frowned. "They're paying duty on cargo they don't have?"

"They are paying duty on cargo that officially exists and materially doesn't. Which means someone profits twice: once on whatever the outbound crates contain, sold wherever they go, and again on the phantom inbound tonnage, because Meridian claims the duty rebate against losses at sea." He tapped the chart. "Storm-loss claims, Miss Sterling. Six ships in nine years have been written off as lost with all hands in the waters here—" his finger traced a stretch of coast south of the harbor "—and here, and here. Yet I have canvassed every fishing village along that shore. Wreckage washes up, you see. Locals remember wrecks. There is no wreckage. There are no dead. Those ships never sank."

"Then where did they go?"

"That is the question I have been unable to answer for nine years." He sat down heavily on his stool. "Until tonight. Because tonight you told me about a bill of lading hidden inside a dead man's letter, naming Crane. And tonight you told me—" and here his voice changed, gentled "—where your father dove. Say it again for me. Precisely."

Oscar's mouth had gone dry. "The Serpent's Teeth. The reef beds two days' sail south. Papa worked the deep beds there for years—he knew them better than anyone. He said—" She stopped. Started again. "He said the deep water past the third spine was strange. That he'd found things down there that shouldn't be on a pearl bed. Machinery. He thought it was salvage from some old wreck. He went back for it."

Finch turned the chart toward her. On it, in his meticulous hand, marked with a small pencilled cross, was the location where six ships had officially drowned without drowning.

The cross sat squarely among the Serpent's Teeth.

"He wasn't diving pearls at the end," Oscar heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away, as though it belonged to someone standing on a dock watching her. "He was diving their graveyard. They scuttled those ships themselves—stripped them, sank them over the reef to hide them. And my papa found them. He found the proof lying on the bottom in forty feet of water, and he didn't even know what he'd found, and they—"

She couldn't finish. Ten years of grief rearranged itself in her chest, not softer but sharper, taking a new shape with edges. Her father had not drowned by accident or by the sea's indifferent mathematics. He had drowned because he was good at his work, honest, and curious, and because curiosity aimed at the wrong patch of ocean was, to men like Crane, a threat to be managed.

And then—the thought arrived cold and clear, like a diver breaking surface—

*Papa knew.* He had pressed a letter into Elias Vane's hands before his last dive. *If the company ever asks about my blood, give this to my girl.* He had known the water might close over him. He had gone anyway, and hidden the manifest in the reef, and written his daughter a letter, and trusted the sea and an old friend with the rest.

"Grief," Finch said quietly, into her silence, "is the worst possible archivist. It misfiles everything. For ten years you have kept your father in a folder marked *accident*, because accident requires nothing of you but endurance." He pushed the chart across the table until it touched her hands. "I propose we re-catalogue. Methodically. With citations."

Oscar looked up at him—at this strange, brave, ridiculous man surrounded by his paper children—and felt something she had not felt since childhood: not hope exactly, but its working cousin, competence. Grief transformed into method.

"Then let's re-catalogue," she said. "Show me everything you have on Pier Nine."

* * *

By the fourth hour, the table had become a map of a decade's crime.

Finch worked like a surgeon and narrated like a schoolmaster pleased with his best pupil, and Oscar discovered that ten years of tally-clerk drudgery had made her fluent in exactly his language. She read columns the way he did. She spotted the discrepancy in the 1896 wharfage fees before he pointed it out—a duplicate entry, same barge, same date, double the fee, the sort of padding that bought silence dockside. She found, buried in a crew wage ledger, the name of the night foreman at Pier Nine, a man called Dree, whose salary tripled in the same month the Halcyon Concern came into being.

"Dree," Finch repeated, writing it down. "Dree. Yes. He testified once before a company tribunal, in a matter concerning pilferage, and his testimony contradicted a watch log I happen to possess." His pencil moved swiftly. "Dree is a thread. We pull him later."

Outside, the rain thickened. The stove ticked. Somewhere below in the chandlery a dog dreamed audibly. Oscar spread the final sheet flat and weighted its curling corners with two inkwells and Finch's teacup, and together they looked at what they had made: dates running left to right, berths and ships and phantom directors, six fake sinkings, one real lease, one governor's signature, and at the far right margin, marked with a fresh cross in her own hand, the Serpent's Teeth.

Every line of it converged on the water where her father had died.

"It corroborates," she said. Her voice was steady. She was rather proud of that. "Elias swallowed the knowledge of where the bill of lading copy sits, and Hargrove can't extract it, but even if he never gives it up—the manifest is the second proof. Paper in Crane's own hand, recovered from the site. One document alone can be called forgery. Two, from independent sources, matching this—" she touched the chart "—cannot."

"Correct," Finch said. "Legally, morally, and arithmetically correct. Which brings us to the difficulties." He held up fingers, ticking them off with the relish of a man who had waited years to enumerate difficulties to a captive audience. "One: the manifest lies beneath forty feet of cold water in a reef famous for drowning men, currently patrolled, I should note, within an inch of its life, because Crane believes—correctly—that something is hidden there. Two: the only person alive who knew those beds well enough to find it is dead. Three: the person who learned the beds at his knee is an indentured clerk under active investigation, recognized now, watched now, whose face Hargrove has likely committed to memory. And four—" he paused, and for once the schoolmaster left his voice entirely "—even if all of the first three difficulties resolve perfectly, we still require a tribunal willing to hear it, a press willing to print it, and enough hours between discovery and trial for none of it to be intercepted. Which is to say, Miss Sterling, the plan is impossible in four distinct ways, and I have not even begun on the weather."

Oscar looked at the cross on the chart for a long moment.

"My father taught me those reefs," she said. "Serpent's Teeth, the third spine, the cut at slack tide. He started teaching me before Mama died, when I was eight. I haven't been under since he drowned, but you don't forget water like that. You don't forget the way home." She rolled up her sleeves—her father's daughter, busy hands, but no longer lowered eyes. "So we take the difficulties in order. Tomorrow I find out when Elias is tried, because that's our clock. Then I need a boat, weights, lines, and someone ashore who can watch Hargrove's patrols and tell me when the water's clear."

"And the diving itself?"

"Is mine." It came out flat and certain, and she surprised herself with it. "You've done your part for ten years, Mr. Finch. This part was always going to be mine. Papa left it in the sea for me. I'm the only one who can bring it up."

Finch studied her over his spectacles. Then he nodded, once, the precise nod of a man entering a figure into a ledger he finally trusted to balance.

"There is," he said, rising to fetch a battered tin from the shelf, "one further matter. If you are to dive the Serpent's Teeth, you will need to leave the harbor, and the harbor gates log every hull that passes. I have taken the liberty—" he opened the tin, revealing folded papers, forged passes in a hand remarkably like various officials', "—of anticipating several impossibilities. I find it saves time."

Oscar stared at the passes. Then at him. "Where did you learn to forge a harbormaster's signature?"

"I count signatures, Miss Sterling," Finch said mildly, handing her the tin. "It is a short step from counting them to producing them. Now. The trial date first, as you say—but before that, tea, because method runs on tea, and because you have been awake for thirty hours and I intend for you to survive long enough to be impossible."

She laughed—a rusty, disused sound, startling them both—and reached for the cup. On the table between them, the little pencilled cross sat on the Serpent's Teeth like a promise.

Two days' sail south, under forty feet of patient green water, her father's truth waited in the reef. All she had to do was go down and get it—and slip past every patrol boat in the governor's navy to reach the dive site, before Hargrove's tribunal hung Captain Vane to keep the sea from ever giving up its dead.
