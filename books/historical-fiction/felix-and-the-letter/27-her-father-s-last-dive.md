## 27. Her Father's Last Dive

The sea did not want me back.

I knew this the way you know weather in your bones—by the ache of old bruises, by the way my hands went cold before my feet ever touched water. It had been nine years since I'd worn a diving dress, nine years since the morning the company men came to our door with their hats in their hands and their pity arranged just so on their faces, and I had promised myself a great many things in the years since, most of them variations on *never again*.

But promises made to yourself, I was learning, are the easiest kind to break.

"You don't have to do this," Silas said for the fourth time since dawn. He was crouched at the bow of Marta's cousin's fishing skiff, checking the air line's couplings with the fussy thoroughness of a man who had once been a printer's apprentice and therefore believed all disasters began with a smudged detail. "We could send word to Elias. He knows these reefs better than anyone living."

"Elias is in chains in the company lockup," I said. "His knowledge is not presently available for consultation."

"Finch, then—"

"Finch can't swim." I said it with more fondness than the statement strictly deserved. "He tried to demonstrate this to me last night. In a wash basin. There were charts involved."

Silas smiled despite himself, and then put the smile away, because we were approaching the point past which joking would have been cowardice dressed in better clothes.

The reef rose ahead of us out of a gray-green sea, a broken crescent of rock and coral that the charts called the Serpent's Teeth and the divers had always called, more simply and more honestly, Papa's Beds. Not because he owned them—he'd owned nothing in his life but a knife, a suit, and a letter—but because he had worked them longer and deeper than any man or woman before him, and because when other divers' baskets came up light, his came up full, and nobody could explain it except by saying he *listened* to the water.

I had inherited his hands. Everyone said so. I had spent nine years pretending otherwise, keeping those hands busy with ledgers and tallies and ink, because busy hands and lowered eyes kept a half-blood girl invisible, and invisibility had been the only safety the city sold cheap.

The sea took the rest of the inheritance whether I signed for it or not.

* * *

Marta's cousin—a wide, silent man called Tomás who smelled permanently of brine and pipe smoke—anchored us over the site with the unhesitating certainty of someone who had done it a hundred times for someone else. He didn't ask questions. The fishwife network had gone quiet after Marta's arrest, but it had not gone dead; it had gone underground, the way an eel goes under a rock, still there, still watching. Tomás knew what the manifest meant. He knew what it would cost if we failed. He shook my hand once, hard, before I began to dress, and that handshake contained an entire speech.

The old diving dress came out of its oilcloth like something exhumed. Papa's suit. I had sold everything else after his drowning—the house, the boat rights, even his good coat—but I had paid a warehouse keeper to hold this in storage, and I had never let myself think too carefully about why.

"It smells like the hold of a ship," Silas observed.

"It smells like him." Pipe smoke and lanolin and the particular salt-rot of old rubber. I worked my arms into the sleeves and felt nine years collapse. "Help me with the breastplate."

He lifted the copper collar onto my shoulders and I heard the old familiar clank of the world narrowing to a bolted circle of metal. He fastened the bolts with the spanner, four turns each, checking each one twice, and then stood looking at me through the faceplate with an expression I couldn't afford to examine.

"How's the air?" he asked.

"Like standing inside a church organ," I said, which was true and also a joke, and he huffed almost-laughter, and we both pretended that was comfort enough.

The plan was simple, as plans made by desperate people usually are. Finch's reconstructed route—the census anomalies, the tax rolls, the ships that vanished from harbor records between one quarter and the next—all of it converged on this reef. Papa had witnessed something on his last dive. Something Crane wanted buried, in every sense of the word. And Papa, being Papa, had buried it deeper: hidden it in the reef itself, at a spot only I could find, because he had taught me the reef the way other fathers taught their children prayers.

The letter had said as much, in its careful evidence-laden pages. But evidence needs corroboration, Finch insisted, in the tone of a man whose entire soul was organized around corroboration. A bill of lading alone could be dismissed as forgery. A bill of lading *and* the physical manifest recovered from the site, bearing dates and signatures and the honest rot of nine years underwater—that, even a tribunal bought and frightened could not wave away.

So here I stood, at the rail, in my father's suit, with the sea doing its slow gray breathing below me.

"Signal line's rigged," Silas said. "One pull means you're well. Two means coming up. Three—" He stopped.

"Three means trouble," I finished for him. "I know the signals, Silas. I was diving before you could set type."

"That's not actually comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

I stepped onto the ladder, and the cold came up through the rungs to meet me like an old acquaintance with unfinished business.

* * *

The first ten feet were the worst and have always been the worst: the shock of cold closing over the helmet, the world going green and wavering, the sound of your own breath becoming enormous, a bellows, a tide. Then the pressure settles on your shoulders like a firm hand, and the strangeness becomes ordinary, and you descend.

I had forgotten how quiet it was down there. Not silence—the dress groans, the air valve hisses, your blood makes its own drumming—but a quiet, the way a cathedral is quiet. Busy. Solemn.

The reef unrolled beneath me exactly as memory had kept it, and that nearly undid me. The brain coral mounds like sleeping oxen. The fan corals waving their slow amber fans. A school of silver fish parted around my helmet and reformed behind me, incurious. Nine years, and the reef hadn't noticed my absence at all. The reef does not keep files. The reef does not flag entries with red thread. It simply continues, indifferent and generous, holding everything and judging nothing.

*Come on, Papa,* I thought, and followed the beds down the seaward slope, counting landmarks the way he'd taught me. *Past the third coral head with the split crown. Past the ledge where the crayfish hide. Down to where the sand gives way to rubble.*

He had shown me this place when I was twelve, on my last dive with him, though neither of us knew it would be the last. He had pointed at a jumble of fallen rock beneath an overhang—nothing to look at, less than nothing—and said, in his slow way, the way he saved words like pearls:

*"If anything ever happens to me, girl, remember this pile. Remember it looks like every other pile. That's the whole trick of it."*

I'd laughed at him. Twelve-year-olds laugh at everything their fathers say that sounds like wisdom, because wisdom in a parent's mouth is unbearably embarrassing. And then I had memorized the pile anyway, without meaning to, the way you memorize the shape of a face.

I found it in eleven feet of murk by feel as much as sight, my gloved hands reading the rubble like a page. And there—wedged where an overhang met stone, screened by a curtain of dead coral branch—was the oilskin packet, small as a hymnal, lashed with cord gone stiff as wire.

My heart was pounding so hard I could hear it competing with the air valve. I worked the packet free. It came loose easier than it should have, and I understood a half-second too late why: the cord had rotted, and with it whatever anchoring held the overhang's rubble screen in balance.

The rocks came down quietly, the way disasters do underwater. Not dramatically. Just a soft slither, a shifting of weight, and then the edge of the slab caught my air line against the ledge and pressed it, and the hose kinked, and the sweet steady hiss of air stopped.

I pulled the signal line once—no, twice—no, my hands weren't answering orders properly anymore. The valve wouldn't open my lungs. The world outside the faceplate dimmed at the edges the way a lamp dims before it dies, and I thought, with a strange floating clarity: *this is how he felt. This is the last thing he felt. The pressure and the quiet and the breath that won't come.*

And then I thought, with equal clarity, and considerably more anger: *No.*

Papa had not drowned because he panicked. Papa had drowned, the divers always said afterward, shaking their heads, because he went down one more time than he should have, on a day the current was wrong, because there was a shell bed he hadn't worked yet and pride and poverty make a poor pair of swimming companions. He had died of ordinary things. Of need and stubbornness and bad luck. Not of the sea's malice, because the sea has none. The sea is only ever exactly what it is.

I stopped fighting the panic and started working the problem, the way he would have. My hands found the hose where it kinked. My hands lifted—lifted, not shoved, lift with the legs, girl, the arms are for holding—the slab's edge just enough. An inch of clearance. The hiss returned, thin and blessed, and I drank it in ragged pulls while the dark at the edges of my vision retreated grudgingly, like a tide going out.

Then I gathered the packet against my chest with both arms, the way you hold something you have decided to keep, and I walked myself up the reef's slope, step by deliberate step, following my own bubble-rain toward the light.

* * *

Breaking the surface is being born twice. The sky slammed down green and then gray and then blazing white as they hauled me up the ladder, and the helmet came off, and the ordinary world rushed in—wind, gull-noise, Silas's voice cracking on my name—and I sat on the deck of the skiff in a puddle of my own making, gasping like a landed fish, laughing.

I didn't decide to laugh. It arrived on its own, from somewhere deep and rusted.

"You were down too long," Silas said furiously. His hands were shaking as he worked the bolts. "Your line went slack, then taut, then slack again—I couldn't read it—Oscar, I thought—"

"I know what you thought." I coughed seawater into the scuppers, elegantly, I felt. "I thought it too. Briefly. Then I got annoyed about it."

"Annoyed."

"The sea and I needed to renegotiate the terms of our relationship." I peeled the breastplate off my shoulders and stood, wobbling, in the wind, lighter than I had felt in nine years. "It wants my father. It can't have him. It had nine years of me by accident and it doesn't get another hour."

Tomás handed me brandy without comment. I drank it and it burned all the way down and lit lamps in rooms I'd shut.

The packet sat on the thwart between us, dripping.

None of us moved for a moment. There is a superstition among divers that some things brought up from the bottom should be acknowledged before they're handled—asked permission, in a manner of speaking. Papa used to touch his helmet to the rail before every descent, quick, like a man kissing his own forehead. I put my hand flat on the wet oilskin.

"All right, Papa," I said. "Let's see what you were so sure about."

The cord crumbled when I cut it. Inside the oilskin, wrapped in a layer of tarred canvas and a layer of waxed linen—three defenses against the water, three layers of a careful man's love—the manifest lay dry as the day it was written.

Dry. Nine years under the sea, and dry as a Sunday sermon.

I unwrapped it on my knees in the bilge-water and the spray, and Silas crouched opposite me, and Tomás leaned over the side pretending to check the anchor, watching everything.

It was a cargo manifest, handwritten, initialed at the foot in a hand I recognized from a hundred company documents: **A. Crane**, in his neat, self-satisfied script. Dated nine days before Papa's last dive. Listed beneath: forty crates, marked as pearl shell and dried fish outbound for the northern ports. Weights that matched no fish catch possible. Destination codes that corresponded, item for item, to the missing ships and falsified tax rolls in Finch's archive. Arms, Silas breathed, when he worked it through—crates of rifles and powder moving under Meridian's flag, smuggled north to buyers Crane had been supplying in secret for years, and my father had seen the crates loaded, had seen them with his diver's eyes that measured and weighed everything, had understood what the false weights meant, and had copied what he saw.

Not stolen the original. Copied it, in his own careful hand, and hidden it in the reef he trusted more than any man alive.

At the bottom, beneath his own mark—the diver's mark, a shell and anchor scratched in the old way—he had written one line, small, in the space where manifests sometimes carry remarks:

*Witnessed and weighed true. Tell my girl the sea keeps honest books.*

I read it aloud, because I couldn't not. My voice cracked in the middle and Silas kindly looked elsewhere until it mended.

"Well," Tomás said at last, straightening up from the rail, in the longest sentence I would ever hear him deliver, "now we know. Now everybody will know."

"Now everybody will know," I agreed.

The wind came around with the morning and pushed us toward the city, and I sat wrapped in a blanket with the manifest inside my shirt, against my skin, feeling it warm slowly to the temperature of a living body. Somewhere under my ribs, something that had been clenched since I was fourteen years old let go—not grief exactly, and not peace either, but the thing that lives between them and rarely shows its face.

I had come to the sea to take something. I left having taken back more than paper.

* * *

We made the harbor by mid-afternoon, running easy among the other boats, flying no signals, drawing no eyes. That was the plan, and the plan held right up until the quay.

Silas spotted it before I did—he grabbed my elbow, and I followed his look, and my stomach dropped through the hull.

Company constables at the landing. Six of them, and a company wagon, and a clerk with a ledger checking every skiff that came in, every basket, every face. A net, thrown across the harbor's mouth, and us swimming straight into it.

"They're not fishing for us," Silas said low. "Look—they're not searching. They're waiting."

He was right. The constables weren't inspecting cargo. They were studying faces, comparing them against something—one constable held a paper, glancing down at it, then up at the arriving crews. A description. A warrant drawn for specific people.

And then I saw why the net had been cast today, of all days, and the warmth the manifest had kindled under my ribs turned to ice water.

Because there, at the end of the constable's line, wrists bound, flanked by two men, head bloody but unbowed, being marched toward the wagon they had surely come to collect him in—

They had Silas already. Or rather, they had caught him somewhere else, hours ago, in the city, before we ever reached the wreck—and the man they were loading into the wagon now was not Silas at all.

It was worse. It was Finch.

He scanned the quay as they pushed him along—the little obsessive counter of people, the keeper of irregularities, my impossible ally—and his eyes swept across our skiff without pausing, deliberately, a man performing ignorance. But his mouth moved, just slightly, shaping words with no voice, words he knew I could read because I had spent my whole life reading mouths across crowded rooms:

*They found the lockup empty. They know about you. Run.*
