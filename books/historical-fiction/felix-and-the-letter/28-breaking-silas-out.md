## 28. Breaking Silas Out

Finch's garret smelled of ink, dust, and forty years of other people's lives, and by the third night of working in it I had stopped noticing the first two.

"Again," Finch said, not looking up from his own copying. "You've written the date wrong."

I looked down at the transfer order in front of me. The date was fine. The date was *perfect*. I had written it four times and it had been perfect every time, which was precisely the problem, because perfect was suspicious. Prison clerks did not write perfect dates. Prison clerks wrote dates with a tired loop on the final numeral and a blot where their wrist dragged through the wet ink, and if a company inspector ever compared my forgery against a hundred genuine orders, the absence of that drag-mark would be the thread that unraveled everything.

"It has a blot," I said.

"It has a blot shaped like a continent," Finch said. "Yours looks like an accident. A real clerk's blot is a signature. You must *inhabit* the blot."

"You cannot be serious."

"I have spent thirty years reading handwriting for a living, Miss Sterling. I am extremely serious about blots." He set down his pen at last and rubbed his eyes under his spectacles, and for a moment he looked less like a census taker and more like what he actually was: a thin, stooped man of fifty-odd who had been so long ignored by everyone in this city that he had turned invisibility into a science. He was, I realized with a start, the only person I'd ever met who had done it longer than I had. "Do it again. Drag your wrist this time. Be a tired man named Pemberly who wants his supper."

I picked up the pen. I thought about Pemberly, shift clerk, second watch, a man I had invented two nights ago and was coming to know rather well—bad knee, fond of pickled herring, counting the minutes until relief came. I thought about wanting supper. That part took no imagination at all. And when I wrote the date this time, my wrist sagged, and the blot bloomed fat and ordinary at the edge of the eight, and Finch made a small satisfied sound like a bird settling on a branch.

"There," he said. "Pemberly lives."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had been built out of the same materials as the forged papers: small irregularities, fitted together.

Silas was being held in the Meridian Company lockup on Cordage Street—a low brick building attached to the warehouse yard, holding mostly dockworkers held over for brawling and one customs officer arrested for drunkedness, because the company had lately decided it could arrest anyone it liked. Silas had been in three days. Three days was survivable. Seven was when they transferred men like him—printer's apprentices caught carrying seditious paper, boys who had helped spring a hunted woman onto a blockaded brig—to the stone cells under the magistrate's court, and nobody came back from those cells with a whole story.

So we had to move him before the seventh day, and we would move him in daylight, in the open, wearing the company's own face.

"The shift change is at four o'clock," Finch said, laying out the street plan he had drawn from memory, because of course he knew every building on Cordage Street; he had counted its inhabitants twice. "Day watch goes off, night watch comes on, and for approximately eleven minutes the desk is manned by one man—Sergeant Doyle—who is responsible for two buildings, six keys, and a kettle. Doyle is not corrupt. Doyle is worse than corrupt. Doyle is *procedural.*"

"Then how do we get a procedural man to release a prisoner?"

"We don't ask him to release anyone." Finch's eyes glinted behind the smudged glass. "We present him with paperwork so ordinary, so correctly filled out, so blessedly routine, that releasing the prisoner becomes simply the last chore between him and his supper. We are not breaking Silas out, Miss Sterling. We are *transferring* him, with all appropriate signatures, to the lockup at the North Quay annex—which does not exist, but is far enough away that no one will check on him until morning."

"And if someone checks the annex register?"

"Then by then Silas will be on the evening packet boat," Finch said, "wearing a hat, and you and I will be at my table eating a truly regrettable stew, and I will be saying I told you so, or possibly being arrested. It is one of the two."

He smiled when he said it. For a man whose entire life had been led in margins, he seemed remarkably cheerful about finally writing in them.

* * *

I did not sleep the night before. Not from fear—or not only. I lay in my narrow cot in the dormitory listening to the other girls breathe, and I found myself thinking about Papa's hands.

It had surprised me, these past weeks, how much of him kept surfacing. When I practiced the forged signatures, I remembered him teaching me to hold a pencil on the *Cormorant*, my small fist inside his large one, both of us bent over the logbook in lamplight while the ship rocked. *"Don't fight the motion of the deck,"* he'd said. *"Work with it. The wave writes the letter with you."* I had thought he meant handwriting. I think now he meant most things.

My father had hidden a manifest in a reef with his own hands, underwater, in the dark, trusting the sea to keep his secret for eleven years. The least I could do was drag my wrist convincingly.

At dawn I checked the recovered manifest again—the real one, wrapped in oilskin, tucked in the false bottom of my sea chest where Marta's brother had once hidden contraband tobacco. The paper was water-stained but stubbornly legible: cargo weights, dates, ports of call that did not match any honest trade, and there at the bottom, in ink gone brown, the governor's own signature, neat as a hymn book. My father had carried proof of Crane's treason down into the water rather than let it surface, and the sea had given it back to me. It seemed only right that I should now carry it the rest of the way.

Because here was the truth Finch and I had worked out between cups of terrible tea: the manifest alone, in this city, was worthless. Every tribunal, every magistrate, every newspaper editor worth the name answered to Crane or feared Hargrove. Paper changed nothing unless it reached people who could print it and could not be silenced. The sympathetic press existed—one paper in the capital, *The Harbor Light*, run by a widow named Okafor who had already printed three careful stories about missing ships. But getting the manifest there meant putting it in a hand I trusted, on a road I couldn't watch, and there were exactly two hands in the world I trusted just now, and one of them belonged to Elias, chained in a cell waiting for trial.

That left Silas.

* * *

Four o'clock, Cordage Street, and the rain had settled in with the patience of something that intended to stay the week.

I stood under the awning of the chandler's across from the lockup, dressed in the gray wool dress Marta had once altered for me, carrying a leather document case exactly like forty others in this city, and I watched the day watch come shuffling out into the rain with their collars up. Finch was already inside. That had been the argument—the only argument—we'd had. I wanted to deliver the papers myself. He had pointed out, with infuriating gentleness, that Miss Oscar Sterling was currently the single most investigated person in the harbor district, and that a census taker carrying transfer orders was merely a dull man doing a dull errand.

"Census takers go everywhere," he'd said. "Nobody remembers seeing us. It is our single great advantage, and I have waited twenty years to use it for something."

So I stood in the rain and watched the door and tried to breathe like a woman whose heart was not attempting to escape through her ribs.

Eleven minutes, Finch had said. At minute six, a figure came out of the lockup door beside Sergeant Doyle, and even at that distance, even hooded against the rain, I knew the walk—loose-kneed, rolling slightly with an imaginary swell, the walk of a boy raised on decks who had never quite gotten his land legs back. Silas paused at the top step while Doyle locked the door behind him, and I saw him say something, and I saw Doyle glance at the paper in his hand, and then Silas came down the steps into the rain like any released man walking toward his supper.

He didn't look at the chandler's awning. He crossed the street half a block down, as arranged, and turned into the alley behind the cooperage, and I counted to thirty, gave the awning one last idle glance—just a woman waiting out the weather—and followed.

* * *

He was waiting in the dark beside stacked barrels, wringing out his sleeve, and when I came around the corner he didn't grab me or shout. He just went very still the way sailors do, listening, and then he said, quietly, "Oscar?"

"Here." I struck the match I'd brought and shielded it, and in the small flame we looked at each other. He was thinner, there was a bruise going yellow along his jaw, and he was grinning like a fool.

"They wrote me a transfer order," he said. "To an annex that doesn't exist. Finch says hello, by the way. He told Doyle all about it. Ten full minutes about the annex's coal supply. I thought the man was going to adopt him." The grin faded a little. "Is it true? What they're saying—that they've set the trial for the harbor square?"

"Nine days."

"And you're going to get him out."

"I'm going to make sure the truth gets there first," I said. "Which may amount to the same thing."

I unwrapped the oilskin there in the alley, rain drumming on the barrel heads above us, and showed him the manifest by matchlight. He read it the way he'd been taught to read cargo papers, quickly and completely, lips moving slightly, and I watched his face go from curiosity to stillness.

"This is your father's hand. The annotations."

"Yes."

"He hid this in the reef." Silas looked up at me, and the match guttered and I lit another. "Oscar. You went down for this yourself."

"It seemed rude to ask anyone else."

He laughed—one short, disbelieving breath—and then pressed the oilskin package flat against his chest with both hands, careful as a man handling a chart in a gale. "Where do I take it?"

"North Road to the coach depot at Millbridge. Don't use your name anywhere—Finch has made you a new one, it's in your coat pocket, left side, along with money enough to be boring. Boring is safe. In the capital, ask at *The Harbor Light* for Mrs. Okafor, and give her this phrase—" I made him repeat it twice, because Finch insisted phrases be memorized and never written: "*The census counts everyone; count this.* She'll know what it means."

"And then?"

"Then she prints it, and the world becomes a different shape," I said. "Or she doesn't, and we improvise."

"That's a plan?"

"It's the best kind of plan. The kind with room in it."

He put the package inside his shirt, next to skin, and buttoned his coat over it, and for a moment neither of us moved. Rain ran off the barrels. Somewhere down the street a cart rattled past, unhurried, ordinary, carrying somebody's cabbages.

"Your father," Silas said suddenly, "once lent me his knife when I'd lost mine over the side, and when I tried to thank him he told me a crew is just a family that agreed to be one." He shifted his weight, embarrassed. "I'm bad at speeches. But I'm going to get this there, Oscar. Weather, patrols, whatever they've got on the road—I'm getting it there."

"I know," I said. And I did know, the way you know a mooring line will hold—not because of the rope but because of the hands tied into it.

"One more thing." He hesitated. "When this works. When he's free. Ask the captain about the night he met your father. He'll pretend he doesn't remember. Make him tell it anyway. It's a good story, and—" he shrugged, awkward and young, "—you should have it. Whatever happens."

"Nothing's going to happen to you."

"Probably not," he agreed cheerfully. "But it's a good story regardless." Then he touched two fingers to his brow, sailor's salute, and slipped off down the alley toward the north gate and the coach road and the capital, and the dark folded around him, and he was gone.

* * *

Finch was waiting at the corner of Cordage and Salt Lane, beneath a lamp that hadn't worked since the last administration, and he fell into step beside me without greeting, which was his way. We walked four blocks before either of us spoke.

"Doyle asked me about the coal supply at the annex," Finch said at last, with the satisfaction of a man reporting a personal triumph. "For nine minutes. I described the coal cellar in detail. There is no coal cellar. I based it on one I inventoried in 1889."

"Did it hurt?" I asked. "Lying?"

"Profoundly. I intend to do it again at the earliest opportunity." He glanced sideways at me. "The boy is away?"

"Away."

"Good. Good." We crossed Salt Lane, dodging a fish cart, and he added, in a lighter tone that fooled neither of us, "You know, of course, that Hargrove will discover the transfer order within a day. Two days at most. He audits everything—it is his one virtue. When he finds the order, he will find Pemberly, and Pemberly does not exist, and then he will begin asking who filed the paperwork."

"Then he'll find a census taker who signed a dead man's name in a living man's hand," I said. "And he'll come for you."

"Oh, almost certainly." Finch adjusted his spectacles, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not fear, and not bravado either. Something quieter. The look of a man who had spent thirty years recording other people's courage and had finally been handed a page of his own. "I find I don't mind as much as I expected. Though I would appreciate it, Miss Sterling, if matters concluded before Sunday. I have an invitation to dinner."

I stopped walking. "You don't have an invitation to dinner."

"Not yet," said Finch, and continued on toward the garret without breaking stride. "But I'm told the household takes in strays, and I count people for a living. By my arithmetic, I qualify."

I stood a moment in the wet street, laughing before I could stop myself—the first laugh in days, startled out of me like a bird from cover—and then I hurried after him through the rain, past the shuttered shops and the sleeping warehouses, toward the lamplit garret where the stew was indeed regrettable, and the work was nearly done, and somewhere north of us a boy with a borrowed name was carrying my father's truth through the dark toward morning.

What none of us knew—not Finch with his ledgers, not Silas on the north road, not me—was that Hargrove was already auditing. Even now, in the warm office above the registry room, he was drawing his finger down the transfer ledger, pausing at the entry for the North Quay annex, and frowning at it the way a man frowns at a sum that will not balance.

By breakfast, he would have Pemberly's name.

By noon, mine.
