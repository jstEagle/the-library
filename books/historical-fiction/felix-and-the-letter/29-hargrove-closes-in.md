## 29. Hargrove Closes In

Magistrate Corwin Hargrove believed, as a matter of principle and long practice, that every lie left a residue. Ink did not vanish; it smudged. Money did not disappear; it moved, and movement could be followed the way a hunter follows blood across snow. He had built his career on this conviction, sitting late in his office above the harbor with a decanter of good brandy and the company's ledgers spread before him like an atlas of human weakness.

So when Silas Wren escaped the company lockup during the Tuesday shift change—walked out between two guards in a company uniform, carrying transfer orders that no one had signed—Hargrove did not rage. Rage was for men without method. He poured two fingers of brandy, opened a fresh page in his private notebook, and wrote at the top, in his small, immaculate hand: *Who benefits?*

The list was short. Wren himself, certainly—he benefited by not hanging. Captain Elias Vane, who would want his deckhand back. And one other name, which Hargrove underlined twice before he allowed himself to say it aloud to the empty room.

"The Sterling girl."

* * *

He began, as he always began, with paper.

The forged transfer orders had been recovered from the lockup clerk's wastebasket—the originals, or rather the practice copies, discarded by someone careless or hurried. Hargrove laid them flat on his desk and examined them under his desk lamp the way a jeweler examines a stone. The printing was very good. Too good, in fact, for a girl who spent her days tallying cargo. The serif on the company seal had been reproduced with a printer's eye, someone who understood kerning and ink weight and the particular way the Meridian crest's anchor dragged slightly to the left because the original die had been cut crooked forty years ago and never corrected.

That was a deckhand's work. Silas Wren had been apprenticed to a print shop in his boyhood—Hargrove had read the file three times now, and knew it the way he knew his own signature. Fine. Wren had made the papers. But papers were not the whole of it.

The escape itself required knowledge. Someone had known the shift change schedule, known that Corporal Doyle took his supper at half past six and that the east gate guard doubled as turnkey only when the duty roster thinned. Someone had known which cell Wren occupied. Someone had walked those orders past two guards whose eyes slid over official documents like water over oiled stone.

And here was the detail Hargrove found genuinely delicious, the detail he turned over in his mind like a sweetmeat: the transfer orders named the correct prisoner. Cell eleven, S. Wren, remanded on the charge of forgery. A stranger breaking in could not have known the cell number. The guards themselves hadn't known it until they read it. Which meant the person who drafted those orders had either seen the lockup registry or—

Hargrove set down his glass and smiled at the ledger room's darkened windows.

—or had stolen it. Along with everything else.

He sent for the registry clerk first, a nervous young man named Pell who arrived in his office wringing his hat and leaving damp crescents on the chair arms. Hargrove let him sit for a full minute while he finished writing something unimportant, because fear softened people faster than questions did.

"Pell," he said at last, pleasantly. "When was the Sterling investigation file last accessed?"

The clerk blinked. "The—the red thread file, sir?"

"I assume I don't keep many files tied with red thread. Yes. That one."

Pell's mouth worked. "I couldn't say, sir. Access is logged, of course, but—" He stopped, and his face did something complicated.

"But the log has been checked out," Hargrove finished for him. "Or mislaid. Or borrowed."

"Signed out three weeks ago, sir. By your office. It hasn't come back."

Ah. So she had been thorough. Hargrove made a small note. He did not actually mind being outmaneuvered in the early stages of an affair; it made the eventual closing of the trap more satisfying, the way a difficult negotiation made the signature sweeter. What he minded was being *embarrassed*, and there was a difference between the two that he guarded like a family heirloom.

"Thank you, Pell. That will be all." And then, as the man reached the door: "Incidentally. The Sterling girl. Oscar Sterling. Does she still report to the tally office?"

"Yes, sir. Every morning. She's—well, she's never missed a day, sir. Even after all the trouble."

"Conscientious," said Hargrove, and heard Pell's footsteps hurry away down the corridor, quick as a rabbit escaping a snare it had not yet noticed.

* * *

The tail he assigned her was a dull, reliable man named Griggs, who had once worked harbor security and retained the useful talent of looking like furniture. For four days Griggs followed Miss Sterling from the dormitories to the tally office and back again, and reported, each evening in his flat voice, nothing whatsoever of interest.

On the fifth day, the pattern broke.

"She went to the fish market, sir. Not the main stalls. The back row." Griggs frowned, assembling the sentence with visible effort. "Talked to a woman. Old one. Sells eels. They talked maybe ten minutes, and then the girl came home, but—" he shifted, uncomfortable, "—she was crying, sir. Or near enough. Walked past the chapel and stood there a while."

Hargrove thanked him and dismissed him, and sat for a long time with his brandy untouched.

Marta. Of course. The fishwife who had carried messages between the girl and the Fairwind, whom Hargrove had arrested weeks ago precisely because she mattered to the girl—and whom he had then, against his own better judgment and at considerable cost to the architecture of his plan, released. He had released her because terror, applied too early, taught people to run, and running girls were hard to find. Better to let Marta go home broken and quiet, a lesson written in flesh, and let the girl draw the correct conclusion: *touch anyone, and I will take them.*

But the girl had gone back anyway. Had wept at a chapel afterward, which told Hargrove something more valuable than any document: she had a conscience, and consciences were levers. A clever girl with no conscience would have to be hunted. A clever girl who loved people would, given the right pressure, walk into a trap on her own feet, holding the door open behind her.

He opened his notebook again and wrote, beneath the underlined name: *Loves: father (dead), Vane (surrogate), Wren (new), Marta (maternal). Fears: being seen. Will be seen if others pay for her silence.*

Then he crossed out the word *fears* and replaced it with *hates*, because he was beginning to suspect, from the quality of her recent work, that the girl had stopped fearing anything at all, and that this was far more interesting.

* * *

Governor Crane received him in the residence's long gallery, where the portraits of previous governors hung in their gilt frames and looked down with the mild, embalmed disapproval of men who had died rich. Crane himself stood at the window with his hands clasped behind him, watching the harbor, where the masts of the blockaded fleet rose like a crop of black winter reeds.

"Hargrove. Tell me you have the girl."

"I have her contours, Your Excellency. Her shape is clear even where her person is not." Hargrove joined him at the window. Below, dockworkers moved cargo with the slow, watchful rhythm of men who had heard rumors and were waiting to see which way the wind sat. "She freed Wren. She has access to the Fairwind. She is, in short, the thread that connects every loose end in this affair—including, I am confident, the original bill of lading your predecessor's correspondence has been so anxious about."

Crane's jaw tightened. It always tightened at the phrase *bill of lading*, which was one of the small pleasures Hargrove permitted himself in these meetings. "You assured me the letter was secured when we took the ship."

"The letter, yes. The letter we have." Hargrove did not add *or believe we have*; a governor did not need to hear the seams of a thing. "But letters persuade, Your Excellency. They do not convict. What convicts is corroboration—a manifest, witnesses, the physical proof that turns a story into a verdict. And I believe the girl went looking for exactly that. Why else steal her own investigation file? Why else free a forger? She is building a case, and she is building it for the tribunal, not for us."

"Then arrest her."

"On what charge? Forgery—we cannot prove the transfer orders were hers. Sedition—she has said nothing seditious to anyone, because she never says anything to anyone. She has spent nine years making herself invisible, Excellency, and invisible women are terribly difficult to hang." Hargrove turned from the window and let the pleasant mask settle fully into place. "No. We do not hunt her. We make her come to us."

He laid his plan on the long table the way he laid out ledgers: line by line, column by column, each figure supporting the next.

Elias Vane would be tried publicly, in the harbor square—not in the tribunal hall, where doors could be closed and reporters kept out, but in the open air, on scaffolds facing the water, so that the entire port could watch. Treason trials were ordinarily dreary things conducted in whispers; this one would be theater. Bunting. Soldiers in dress uniform. The governor's own box draped in the colors.

"And the sympathy element?" Crane asked. His voice had gone soft, which meant he understood.

"Announce it plainly," Hargrove said. "Any person who speaks in the traitor's defense will be charged as an accomplice. Any person. Man, woman, child, clerk, fishwife. We will print the notice in the Gazette and nail it to every customs post in the harbor." He paused delicately. "The girl cannot bear to be the reason other people suffer. You saw what she did for the eel-seller. So we do not threaten her. We threaten everyone she might possibly love, publicly, in advance, and we give her exactly one door out of the burning building."

"Which is?"

"Silence. Or—" and here Hargrove allowed himself the smallest smile, "—if her conscience proves larger than her sense, a scaffold with excellent acoustics, positioned directly beside Captain Vane's."

Crane was quiet for a moment, watching the harbor. Then he said, "Vane will not talk. He's refused counsel already. He stands in that cell like a fence post."

"He will not need to talk. I intend to speak *for* him." Hargrove drew a folder from his case and set it down. Inside lay the documents recovered from the Fairwind—or rather, the documents that would be sworn to have been recovered from the Fairwind, including certain correspondence between Vane and foreign agents that did not exist yet but would exist by Thursday, produced by the same talented hand that had produced the transfer orders. Silas Wren's hand. There was a pleasing symmetry in it. "The trial will be short, Your Excellency. The evidence overwhelming. And if anyone rises in the crowd to dispute it—" he tapped the folder, "—we shall have a second defendant before lunch."

Crane looked at the folder for a long moment, and then at Hargrove, and something almost like warmth passed across his dry face. "You are wasted on trade disputes, Corwin."

"I have always thought so, Excellency."

* * *

The notice appeared in the Gazette on Saturday morning, set in bold type where even the semi-literate could not miss it, and nailed to every customs post, warehouse door, and chapel wall in the lower city by noon. Hargrove read it standing in the square itself, beneath the scaffolds that were already rising—raw pine planks and fresh hammering, the gallows frame at the eastern end not yet dressed but unmistakable in its purpose, visible from the water, from the fish market, from the dormitory windows of the Meridian Trading Company.

He wanted her to see it. He wanted her to see it every day.

*BY ORDER OF THE TRIBUNAL: the traitor ELIAS VANE, sometime captain of the vessel FAIRWIND, having refused lawful surrender of his fleet to the Crown, shall stand trial for HIGH TREASON in the Harbor Square on the morning of the twenty-ninth instant. Let all persons take notice: whosoever shall speak, write, or act in defense of the said traitor, or shall present any document, testimony, or petition on his behalf, shall be taken and charged as ACCOMPLICE to the same treason, and shall suffer the extreme penalty of the law together with him.*

*There is no appeal. There is no exception. There is no mercy in wartime for those who choose the enemy's friendship over the Crown's.*

He read it twice, checking the phrasing the way he checked invoices, and found it good. The words *any document* had been his own addition, inserted over the objections of the tribunal's legal clerk, who worried it was overbroad. Overbroad. As if precision were the point. The clause existed for exactly one reader, and Hargrove trusted that she would understand its meaning perfectly, because she had proven herself, throughout this entire affair, to be the most attentive reader in the port.

A small crowd had gathered at the foot of the scaffold—dockworkers mostly, come to gawk, hats in their hands, faces carefully empty. Hargrove stood among them unnoticed, in his plain gray coat, listening.

"They'll hang him sure," a stoker muttered.

"Trial's just the rope getting measured," said another, and spat.

An old woman near the eel stalls—not Marta, but of Marta's tribe—crossed herself and turned away, and Hargrove watched the gesture travel down the row like wind through wheat: one after another, hands rising briefly to foreheads, the old country's blessing passed in silence down a line of strangers. He noted it. Noted it and filed it. Fear he could manage. But grief moving through a crowd like weather—that was a current, and currents deserved respect.

Griggs materialized at his elbow. "She's read it, sir."

"Has she."

"Came out of the tally office at the noon bell. Stood at the customs post on Quay Street. Read it through twice." Griggs hesitated, which for Griggs constituted eloquence. "Then she went back inside, sir. Didn't cry. Didn't speak to anyone. Just—went back to work."

Hargrove considered this. A girl who wept at chapels had gone back to her ledgers instead, and that silence told him more than tears would have. She was not deciding whether to act. She had decided weeks ago, probably on the night of the escape. What she was doing now was arithmetic—counting the cost, weighing the lives—and Hargrove needed only to ensure the scales tipped the way he wanted.

"Anything else?"

"One thing." Griggs lowered his voice. "The census man. Finch. He's been asking around the docks. Quiet-like. Asking about ships that went missing years back. Cargo tonnage. Tax rolls." A pause. "He asked about me, sir. Whether I'd been following the Sterling girl. Paid me a shilling for my answer."

Hargrove turned his head slowly and regarded his subordinate with genuine interest. "And what did you tell him?"

"Told him nothing. Took the shilling, though." Griggs shrugged apologetically. "Seemed rude not to."

Hargrove laughed—one short bark, surprising them both. "Keep the shilling, Griggs. If Finch is buying answers about the girl, then he is already halfway into her pocket, and I want to know every coin he spends. Follow *him* for a few days. Quietly." He looked up at the scaffold, at the fresh pine gleaming in the afternoon light, at the harbor beyond it where the Fairwind rode at anchor under prize crew, her sails furrowed like a folded wing. The noose was nearly dressed. The square was nearly ready. Every piece on his board was where he had placed it, and the girl—whoever else she was, whatever cleverness she had learned in the dark of the registry room—was about to discover that visibility ran both ways.

"There is one flaw in her character I have not yet exploited," he said, mostly to himself.

"Sir?"

"She thinks this is about her father's name." Hargrove smiled at the scaffolds, warm as a parish priest. "It isn't. It's about mine. And by Friday, everyone in this port will know both our names—the drowned pearl diver's daughter and the magistrate who closed his file." He straightened his coat. "Send flowers to the tribunal clerk. Tell him to prepare two indictments. One we will use."

He walked back toward his office through the gathering dusk, past the fish market where the lamps were being lit one by one, past the chapel where the girl had wept, past the customs post where her eyes had read his words twice and returned, obediently, to work. Somewhere out there she was sharpening her plan against his, the way a knife is sharpened against a stone that does not care.

Let her come. The square would be ready. The rope would be ready. And the door out of the burning building, as promised, would be standing wide open—painted, padlocked, and pointed directly at him.

Behind him, the hammers drove the last nails home into the scaffold, and the sound carried all the way down to the water.
