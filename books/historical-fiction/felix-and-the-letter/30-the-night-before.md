## 30. The Night Before

Marta's stall was shuttered when Oscar came down Fish Gutter Lane, but the shutters themselves told a story. They were new. Someone had pried the old ones off their hinges during the arrest—Oscar had heard about that secondhand, in the guilty way she'd heard everything about Marta's two weeks in custody—and the fishwives had clubbed together to buy her fresh pine, and hung it, and painted it the same faded blue as before, because Marta would have noticed if it wasn't.

The door to the back room was ajar. Oscar knocked anyway, because you knocked at Marta's, even now. Even when there was hardly anything left of her to disturb.

"Come in before the cold does," said a voice from inside. It was Marta's voice, but thinner, like a net through which most of the fish had already passed.

She was sitting in her old chair by the stove, wrapped in two shawls despite the mild evening, gutting nothing. Her hands were in her lap. That was the wrongness of it, Oscar thought. Marta's hands were never in her lap. They scaled and salted and haggled and pointed at your chest when they made a point. Now they lay there like two pale fish that had given up swimming.

"You're supposed to be resting," Oscar said.

"I'm supposed to be many things." Marta looked up, and some of the old iron came back into her face, just around the eyes. "Sit down, girl. You've got the look of someone about to do something stupid tomorrow."

"That's an accurate look, then."

"Good. I hate being wrong." Marta nodded at the kettle on the stove. "Pour us both something. My hands ache."

It was a lie, or half a lie. Oscar poured, and watched her friend's fingers curl slowly around the cup, and understood that the ache was real but not the reason. The reason was that Marta had learned, in the company lockup, to be afraid of what her own hands might shake while someone watched. She'd told Silas this once, and Silas had told Oscar, in the flat careful voice he used for things that hurt him to say.

"They took my statement eleven times," Marta had reported. "Eleven times the same questions. By the ninth time I was making up answers just to have something new to say. That's how they get you, girl. Not with the rope. With the repetition. They make you so tired of yourself you'll confess to anything for the novelty."

Now Marta sipped her tea and studied Oscar over the rim.

"So," she said. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

"They'll hang him?"

"Not if I can help it."

"Hmph." Marta set the cup down. "You know what I did, in there? Two weeks, mind, and me innocent as a boiled egg?" She didn't wait for an answer. "I counted. Days, hours, the cracks in the ceiling, the steps the guard walked. Four hundred and six steps every hour, give or take. Because as long as I was counting, I was doing something. Counting's not the same as waiting. Waiting is what they do *to* you. Counting is what you do back."

Oscar thought of Finch. Of course she did. She thought of a thin man in a shabby coat who had spent twenty years counting a city, and how his private madness had turned out to be the only weapon anyone had against Governor Crane. "There's more counting in this world than people realize," she said.

"There's more counting than living, is the trouble." Marta leaned forward. Her hand found Oscar's wrist—not gripping, just resting, like a bird landing. "Listen to me. Tomorrow, whatever happens in that square, you listen for one thing. When Elias Vane speaks—he'll speak maybe twice, he's not a man who spends words—when he speaks, don't watch the magistrate. Watch the soldiers. The young ones. They'll be the ones who flinch. Flinching is where all revolutions start, in my experience. Somebody important flinches, and then somebody with a rank notices, and then it's arithmetic from there."

"Marta—"

"And one thing more." Her grip tightened, just slightly. "Whatever happens, you come back here Sunday. There'll be chowder. If there's no you by Sunday, I'm leaving your portion to the gulls, and they're ungrateful company."

Oscar laughed—a short, wet sound she hadn't expected. "You'd feed my portion to gulls."

"I'd feed it to them *angrily*. It's not the same as forgiveness." But Marta's eyes had gone bright, and she pulled Oscar down and pressed her forehead to hers, the way she had when Oscar was nine and motherless and selling oysters on this same lane. "You survived me being taken," she whispered. "That was the worst fortnight of my life, but I survived it too. We're a pair of survivors, you and me. Survivors eat chowder. Promise."

"Promise."

"Then go home. Sleep. And if sleep won't come—" she flapped a hand vaguely toward the harbor "—count waves. They never end. It's very restful, losing count forever."

* * *

The garret above the cooperage where Oscar had lodged since the raid smelled of lamp oil and cedar shavings, and tonight it felt smaller than usual, which was fine, because she wanted small. Small was manageable. The world outside those walls had grown enormous—the whole city talking of tomorrow's trial, the harbor square already sprouting scaffolding for the gallows like some terrible spring flower—and Oscar had no use for enormous tonight.

She laid her father's diving knife on the table under the lamp.

It had been her mother's wedding gift to him, though everyone in their world knew better: she hadn't given it, she'd made it, folding steel in a forge she wasn't supposed to touch, because a woman of the islands didn't need permission to make things. The blade had gone down with him—or nearly. Divers always kept knives lashed where a line could be cut in one motion, and when they'd brought Papa up, the knife had still been at his belt, and Oscar, fifteen and hollowed out, had claimed it before the company clerks could list it as salvage.

Ten years of salt had found every seam. The handle wrappings were gray and fraying, the blade spotted dark along the edge, the rivets loose enough to click when shaken. Tonight it clicked like a small impatient clock, and Oscar decided she was done letting it click.

She had no forge. She had a lamp, three sizes of pliers borrowed from Silas, a spool of waxed cord, a whetstone, and a tin of oil. It would be enough. Her father had maintained this knife on boats, in dormitories, once—she remembered this with sudden vividness, the memory arriving like a fish surfacing—in the back of a wagon during a rainstorm, using his thumbnail and a rag. A man who could mend a knife in a rainstorm could have his knife mended by lamplight by his daughter. It was simple courtesy, almost.

She unwound the old cord first, working slow, and the smell that rose from the handle was salt and old leather and underneath it something faintly of him, or something she had decided long ago was faintly of him, which amounted to the same thing. Her eyes blurred. She let them. Nobody was watching.

*The night before*, she thought. People always talked about the eve of battle as if it were dramatic. It wasn't. It was cord and oil and a whetstone whispering against steel, stroke after stroke after stroke, until the rhythm carried her somewhere quiet.

When the blade could shave the hair off her forearm—she tested it the way he'd taught her, breath held, pleased when it worked—she rewound the handle in fresh cord, tight and even, sealing each pass with wax the way he'd shown her when she was seven and allowed, for the first time, to hold the far end. *"Even tension, little star. Slack anywhere means failure everywhere. Same in a line, same in a knot, same in a promise."*

Same in a letter, she thought, and stopped, because the other thing sat beside the knife on the table, waiting its turn.

Paper. Ink. The good pen Silas had bought her, saying printers judged pens and this one passed judgment.

She had been avoiding this all week, the way you avoid a cold swim. But the swim had to happen. Tomorrow she would stand in that square and speak—against every instinct bred into her bones, out loud, visibly, her name in the mouths of magistrates and soldiers and God knew how many company informers—and people who spoke in that square might hang beside Elias Vane. Hargrove had arranged it precisely so. The invitation was a trap dressed as a courtesy.

So: a letter. To whoever survived her. It seemed only fair. Her father had left her one; the least she could do was keep the family business going.

*Dear whoever,* she began, and crossed it out. Too much like a tally form.

*Marta gets the stall's debt cancelled if anyone owes me money, which nobody does, so instead Marta gets my apology for every silence I ever kept while she shouted at customs men on my behalf.*

*Silas gets—*

The pen stopped. She sat with it stopped for a long moment.

Silas got the truth, then. That was the only thing she owned that he actually wanted. She wrote: *Tell Silas he was right about everything except the timing, and even the timing he was mostly right about, and that the fortnight I spent forging papers in the Fairwind's hold with ink on my knuckles was the happiest work of my life, and I never once said so out loud, because I am my father's daughter and we hoard our tenderness like pearls. Tell him to say it out loud. To whoever's left. Life is short and harbors are dangerous.*

*Elias gets the knife. He'll pretend he doesn't want it. He wants it.*

*Finch gets my file—all of it, the real story, corrected and signed, because a man who keeps twenty years of archives deserves one honest entry about himself, namely: that everyone thought he was mad, and he was merely early.*

*And whoever finds this and isn't any of these people: I was Oscar Sterling, daughter of Wen Sterling the diver, and I spent ten years trying to be invisible, and I want it recorded that it didn't work. The sea saw me. The census saw me. One stubborn fishwife saw me. In the end I will have been seen by thousands, on a scaffold, in a square, and I find—I am surprised to find—that I mind it less than I expected. Visibility, it turns out, is only terrible the first time.*

She read it over. It was too honest, probably. It was exactly honest enough.

She folded it, sealed it with a drop of candle wax, and wrote on the outside: *For whoever survives Oscar Sterling.* Then, because humor was the last thing standing between her and weeping, she added beneath it: *If nobody survives, burn unread. No point embarrassing myself posthumously.*

* * *

She was trimming the lamp low past midnight when the stairs creaked in the third step from the top—the third step, always, because Silas moved through buildings like a man reading them, and had memorized her staircase within a week of knowing her.

He shouldn't be here. He should be across the harbor, behind the blockade chain, where the Fairwind lay impounded and watched and where his presence ashore was worth a hanging on its own. Those were the arrangements. He'd sworn to them with printer's solemnity.

She opened the door before he knocked.

"You're early," she said.

"For what? I'm not due anywhere."

"Silas."

"Inigo's covering my watch." He came in, shaking rain off his shoulders—when had it started raining?—and stood dripping just inside the door with the particular stiffness of a man rehearsing a speech he hopes won't be needed. "Before you say anything else: yes, it's dangerous. Yes, Kerr's watch rotation covers me till dawn. Yes, I checked twice." He paused. "Three times."

"I wasn't going to scold you."

"You were going to scold me."

"I was going to *consider* scolding you," Oscar said, "and then decide against it, because I'm glad you're here, and lying about that has never once worked between us."

He smiled then, and the stiffness went out of him, and he looked around the garret—at the whetstone and oil rags, at the knife gleaming on the table with its new white cord, at the sealed letter propped against the lamp—and something in his face softened further, into an expression she'd seen him wear over finished print jobs. Craft appreciated.

"The knife looks new," he said.

"It sounds better, anyway. It used to click."

"Everything clicks before it's mended."

They divided the night without discussing dividing it, the way they'd divided forgery work in the hold—her tasks, his tasks, no negotiation required. He fetched bread and hard cheese and a jar of pickled onions from his coat pockets, having apparently stopped at three different vendors on his way through the rain, and they ate cross-legged on the floor by the stove because the table belonged to the knife and the letter and neither of them wanted to crowd those.

"Do you want to know what I wrote in mine?" Oscar asked, nodding at the letter.

"No," Silas said. "Yes. No. Tell me the parts you want to tell."

"I told you to say tender things out loud."

"Did you? That seems unlike me." But his ears went pink, and he reached into his breast pocket and produced a folded paper of his own, creased soft from handling. "I wrote one too. Three drafts. The first two sounded like shipping manifests." He hesitated, then held it out. "You can read it after. After matters more than now, I think. Now it would just be—"

"Yes," Oscar said quietly. "After."

Because they both understood the shape of the arrangement: letters written on the night before were promises made to the darkness, and reading one aloud would break its spell, turn a charm into a claim. Better sealed. Better patient. Better waiting on the other side of tomorrow like a lamp in a window.

So instead they kept vigil, which is a word for two people staying awake together so that neither is alone with the hours.

Silas sharpened the small paring knife she used for food, humming an off-key shanty. Oscar re-oiled the hinges of the window latch, which shrieked, and had shrieked through every escape and return she'd made this month, and would shriek no longer. They talked in the low register of sailors and conspirators—not about the trial, or barely. About whether eels could taste bitterness (disputed). Whether Finch would wear his good coat tomorrow or the shabby one (they bet a penny; Oscar took the shabby coat; she'd have staked her indenture papers on it). Whether Marta would actually serve chowder on Sunday or simply threaten to.

"She'll serve it," Silas said. "Threatening is just how she seasons it."

Around three, the rain quit, and through the window the harbor showed itself: black water, the riding lights of the blockading ships strung across the roads like a low crooked constellation, and beyond them, dim and captive, the silhouette of the Fairwind. Somewhere on her deck, Inigo was keeping Silas's watch. Somewhere below, in the brig or the hold or wherever they'd put him, Elias Vane was spending his last free night, and Oscar found herself wondering if he was sleeping. Probably he was. He had a sailor's gift for sleeping when sleep was available and fighting when it wasn't, and she envied it with her whole tired spine.

"If it goes wrong tomorrow," Silas said, into the quiet, "the part after will still go on. That's what I keep arriving at. However bad the square gets, Sunday comes. Chowder comes. The tide doesn't read tribunals." He turned the paring knife over, inspecting his edge. "My master the printer used to say: the press doesn't care if you're frightened. Set the type true and pull the lever. Frightened type sets crooked."

"Frightened divers drown," Oscar said. "Papa's rule. You breathe slow and you check your line, and you save the fear for afterward, on the surface, where it can't reach your hands."

They looked at each other, and laughed—small, exhausted, genuine laughter, the kind that lives next door to crying and borrows its sugar.

At four, Silas spread his coat on the floor by the stove and insisted she sleep first. At four-thirty she insisted back. At five they gave up and sat shoulder to shoulder, not sleeping, not quite talking, watching the sky over the harbor go from black to iron to the color of an old bruise fading.

At a quarter to seven, the bells began. All of them, every church and every company tower at once, ringing the hour and then ringing past it, ringing and ringing, and down by the water the crowd was already flowing toward the square like the harbor itself rising.

Silas stood and held out his hand.

Oscar took it. Then she picked up her father's knife, slid it through her belt where it rode light and silent, newly mended, and went with him down the stairs—past the third step, which creaked anyway, and made them both stop and look at each other and almost laugh again, because some things you cannot fix by tomorrow, and some things you fix by walking through them.

The square was full when they arrived. The gallows stood against the morning glare. And at the tribunal table, spreading his documents like a man laying out a feast, sat Magistrate Hargrove—already smiling.
