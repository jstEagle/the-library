## 31. Trial in the Square

The soldiers came for Elias at dawn, and he thanked them for the punctuality.

It was not sarcasm—or not entirely. A man who has spent thirty years reading tides learns to appreciate a schedule kept. The sergeant who shackled his wrists did not know what to do with the thanks and settled for scowling, which Elias found restful. Scowls he could navigate. It was pity that turned a man's stomach.

They marched him through the company gates, past the Fairwind riding at anchor with a prize crew aboard her like lice on a gull, and down to the harbor square, where carpenters had spent two days building a tribunal platform that looked, in Elias's professional opinion, like a badly ballasted ship. Too much weight at the back, where the magistrates would sit. Anything weighted wrong at the stern broaches in a crosswind. He suspected the morning would prove him right about that too.

The square was already filling. Fishwives with their baskets set down as barricades of their own. Dockworkers who should have been at the morning shift, standing with their caps off. Children on their fathers' shoulders, because a hanging is a spectacle even when it's dressed up as justice, and nobody had told the children otherwise.

Elias walked to the platform with his spine straight and his hands loose in the irons, and he did what he had done every day of his command: he looked at his people so they would know they were seen.

* * *

From three rows back, Oscar watched him do it.

She had planned, all night, not to come. Every sensible voice in her head—the ones trained by nine years of lowered eyes and busy hands—had drawn up a list of reasons. You will be recognized. You will be seized. You are bait, and bait belongs in the trap. Marta herself had gripped Oscar's wrist at the fish stall that morning and said, *Stay home, girl. Stay small,* which from Marta was a sermon delivered by a woman who could no longer follow her own scripture, her stall half-empty, her laugh gone quiet since the lockup.

Oscar had stayed home until eight o'clock. Then she had put on her grayest dress, pulled her cap low, and come anyway—because she had realized sometime around dawn, staring at her father's diving knife on the table, that staying small had never once saved anyone. It had only delayed the counting. Finch had counted her whether she lied or not. Hargrove had flagged her file whether she hid or not. The sea had taken her father without consulting her invisibility at all.

So here she stood, wedged between a cooper and an old woman selling roasted chestnuts out of sheer defiance of the proceedings, close enough to see the salt-and-pepper stubble on Elias Vane's jaw and the way his eyes moved across the crowd—not searching for escape routes, but taking attendance.

His gaze passed over her once. Twice. Stopped.

He did not smile. That would have condemned her. But something at the corner of his mouth moved, one sailor's millimeter, and his eyes went on, and Oscar felt it like a hand briefly on her shoulder: *There you are. Good.*

She bit the inside of her cheek and did not weep, because chestnut sellers notice weeping, and the whole plan—her plan, the desperate half-formed thing she and Finch and Silas had built over three sleepless nights—depended on her remaining exactly unremarkable enough to be underestimated.

*Ask the girl*, Elias was going to say. She had made him promise it the night before, via Silas, in words carried out to the brig sewn into the collar of a coat. Whatever they did to him in there, however they twisted the forged papers Silas himself had made—papers now turned traitor, evidence against the man whose life they were meant to save—he was to say one sentence. One sentence, and then stand aside and let the invisible girl be visible.

Her hands would not stop shaking. She folded them, the old habit, busy hands, lowered eyes—and then deliberately raised her chin instead. Practice. She would get one chance to do it in front of a thousand people.

The trumpets brayed. Governor Crane's tribunal filed onto the ill-ballasted platform.

* * *

Hargrove was having an excellent morning, and he knew better than to trust it.

Company magistrate, chief investigator, architect of the purge—all titles he had earned by understanding one simple principle that other men found distasteful: institutions did not run on truth, they ran on paperwork. Truth was weather; paperwork was climate. And this morning he held a folder containing climate itself.

The papers were good work. He had examined them twice with a jeweler's glass. Captain Vane's hand on the correspondence, the Meridian seal properly cracked and resealed, dates consistent, ink aged convincingly with what Hargrove guessed was iron salts and a clever oven. The documents placed the Fairwind squarely in arms traffic with foreign buyers, which justified impoundment, treason charges, and—most usefully—retroactive cover for everything Crane and Hargrove had done to acquire the independent fleets. One folder, and five years of patriotic theft became lawful prudence.

There was a wrinkle. There was always a wrinkle; Hargrove had built a career on wrinkles. The girl Sterling had vanished from her dormitory bedroll four nights running, according to the matron's reports, and returned before roll call each time like a cat through a window. His tail had followed her to the salt sheds weeks ago and lost her. She was doing something, the invisible little clerk was doing *something*, and Hargrove disliked not knowing what the way a man dislikes a splinter under a fingernail.

But today she didn't matter. Today the machinery was too heavy to divert. He had personally arranged the seating, the trumpets, the press of crowd permitted through the cordon—large enough to demonstrate public consensus, controlled enough to contain it. Crane wanted spectacle; Hargrove had provided spectacle the way he provided everything, on budget and documented in triplicate.

He glanced at his notes one last time and rose to open proceedings.

*Let it be clean*, he thought, and had the prisoner brought forward.

* * *

Elias declined counsel in eleven words.

"I have no need of a lawyer," he said, "for a conversation I intend to keep short."

The junior magistrate presiding—a nervous man named Pell whom Hargrove had chosen precisely for his malleability—blinked at him. "Captain Vane, the charges are grave. Treason carries—"

"I know what it carries. I've carried heavier."

A ripple went through the square. Not laughter, quite. Something adjacent to laughter, the sound of a thousand people deciding simultaneously that they were allowed to enjoy themselves. Hargrove, seated to Pell's left, smoothed his papers and waited. Let the man posture. Posturing was ballast; it kept a hull steady while you loaded the cargo that sank it.

Pell read the charges. Conspiracy to traffic in armaments. Correspondence with foreign agents. Violation of wartime edicts. Each count was accompanied by an exhibit passed forward—Silas's forgeries, crisp and damning—and each time, Pell held the paper up to the crowd as if displaying a fish of respectable size.

"Do you contest the authenticity of these documents?"

"They're authentic enough," Elias said. "My man printed them in my own hold. I'd be ashamed to disown his work."

That landed strangely. Pell faltered, glancing at Hargrove, who frowned faintly—the confession was odd, but a confession was a confession, and half the square heard the captain admit his crew had produced the documents. Guilt by craft, if not by intent.

"The court takes that as acknowledgment of authorship," Pell said carefully.

"Acknowledge whatever pleases you." Elias shifted his weight, chains clinking, and surveyed the tribunal with the frank appraisal he'd once given a leaking hull. "Magistrate. May I ask how long you've served the company?"

"Four—four years."

"Then you'll have signed the edict exempting company vessels from harbor inspection. Did it trouble you, signing that? All those crates going out under Meridian's flag that no one was allowed to weigh?" He paused. "No. Never mind. Wrong conversation for today. Save it."

"Captain Vane—"

"No, I'm sorry. Genuinely." And he did look sorry, in the mild way of a man apologizing for correcting a helmsman's grammar while the ship took on water. "You're reading from papers someone handed you, in a building someone else paid for, on behalf of men you've never dined with. I've hanged men for less initiative."

The ripple again, wider this time. Somewhere in the crowd an old woman said "oh my" with evident satisfaction, and Oscar, chestnuts at her elbow, felt her heart hammer against her ribs with something dangerously near joy. He was magnificent. He was buying time with contempt, spending it coin by coin, and the crowd was leaning in like water finding its level.

Hargrose rose. The platform tilted almost imperceptibly under him—that cursed stern-weight—and he gripped the rail for a half second before pretending he hadn't.

"Captain," he said, oiled and reasonable, "the court is grateful for your candor regarding authorship. Perhaps you'd like to explain *why* your crew fabricated documents implicating the governor in smuggling. Some personal grievance? A dispute over wages?"

It was well played, and Oscar hated him for the skill of it. Any answer Elias gave—denial, silence, irony—would curdle in the record. Hargrove had built the question the way you build a net: any direction was capture.

Elias looked at him for a long moment. Then he did the thing Oscar had promised him a crowd would remember for the rest of their lives.

He turned his back on the tribunal.

Not dramatically—there was nothing theatrical in it. He simply rotated, slow and deliberate, chains whispering, until he faced the square. Faced the fishwives and the dockworkers and the children on shoulders and the cooper and the chestnut woman. Faced, last of all, the gray-dressed girl with the low cap who stood frozen between strangers with her heart in her ears.

"Ask the girl," Elias Vane said, in a voice pitched to carry to the seawall, "what her father carried."

Silence, one full breath of it, the kind of silence a harbor knows between a wave breaking and the water arriving.

Then Pell, bewildered: "What girl? Captain, identify yourself—what girl do you mean?"

And Oscar Sterling stepped out of the crowd.

* * *

Later, she could never reconstruct how it happened—whether her legs decided without her or whether some part of her had been walking forward since the night in the ledger room among the cargo crates. What she remembered was the strangeness of air. Nine years of making herself thin enough to slip between gazes, and now gazes caught on her from every side like burrs, hundreds of them, and she did not dissolve. She simply moved through them, up the cordon line, past a soldier who reached for her arm and then, seeing a hundred dockworkers shift forward in the same instant, thought better of it.

"Name," said Pell weakly, as she mounted the platform steps.

"Oscar Sterling," she said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her more than anything else that morning. "Tally clerk, third grade, Meridian Trading Company. Daughter of Wen Sterling, pearl diver." She let that sit. "Who drowned in company waters, diving a site no company chart accounts for, six weeks after he witnessed a shipment the governor wanted buried."

Hargrove moved first, because of course he did. "This girl is a person of interest in an ongoing investigation—"

"Then arrest me," Oscar said, turning to face him, and saw—for the first time, and forever after—the flicker in his eyes when a ledger refused to balance. "Arrest me in front of all of them, Magistrate. Or hear what I carried out of the sea."

The platform leaned. The crowd pressed. And at the harbor mouth, unseen from the square, a long gray flagship began, very quietly, to make steam.
