## 32. Oscar Speaks

The harbor square smelled of tar and fear.

Oscar stood at the edge of the crowd with her back against a fishmonger's barrow, and she counted things, because counting was what her hands did when the rest of her wanted to shake apart. Fourteen soldiers in company blue along the tribunal steps. Two officers with sabers they kept adjusting, as if the weight of them embarrassed everyone. One gallows scaffold, freshly built, still smelling of cut pine. One captain in chains.

Elias Vane stood on the platform with his wrists bound before him and his spine as straight as his own mainmast. They had dressed him in his own coat — Hargrove wanted him to look like a pirate, and instead he looked like a man who had been interrupted mid-work and expected shortly to resume it. His eyes moved over the crowd once, unhurried, and Oscar ducked behind the barrow before they could land on her.

She had promised herself she wouldn't come. She had promised Marta, too, and Marta had grabbed her sleeve at the stall this morning and said, *Girl, if you go to that square, you go as a stranger, not as mine.* But Marta's hands had been shaking when she said it — they hadn't stopped shaking since the lockup — and a promise made to trembling hands is only half a promise.

The tribunal crier bawled the charges. Treason against the crown. Refusal to surrender vessels under edict. Conspiracy with foreign interests. Each word landed like a stone dropped down a well, and the crowd received each one with the particular silence of people doing arithmetic about their own necks.

Hargrove sat at the tribunal bench in his gray suit, looking less like a magistrate than an accountant reviewing someone else's sums. He had, Oscar knew, every reason to be comfortable. The forged papers Silas had slaved over in the Fairwind's hold were sitting somewhere in that man's evidence chest, and Hargrove believed them genuine — believed Elias himself had forged documents to sell Crane's secret back to him. The trap had inverted weeks ago. Today it would close.

"Prisoner," said the presiding officer, a dry stick of a man named Corbett whose voice suggested he'd rather be anywhere, "have you counsel?"

"I have a witness," Elias said.

Murmurs ran through the square like wind through wheat.

"Name your witness."

Elias turned his head slowly until he was facing the crowd — facing all of it, no one in it, everyone in it. And then he said, in the voice of a man announcing the day's weather:

"Ask the girl what her father carried."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted three full heartbeats. Oscar counted them. She had learned long ago that terror moves in threes.

Then Hargrove laughed. It was a soft, pleasant sound, the laugh of a man watching a dog chase its tail. "There is no girl," he said. "There is no letter. There is only a diver's brat who has already been dealt with according to procedure." He rose, buttoned his coat, and let his gaze drift across the front rows of the crowd like a lantern being passed along a line of held breaths. "Unless," he added, and here his voice went gentle, which was the most frightening thing it did, "unless some sympathizer wishes to step forward and hang beside Captain Vane. The tribunal extends its generosity."

Nobody moved. A gull screamed somewhere over the masts. Somewhere near Oscar, a woman whispered a prayer to a saint who had never once been near salt water.

And Oscar discovered something strange happening inside her chest, which was that all her life's discipline — lowered eyes, busy hands, the whole gray architecture of invisibility she had built plank by plank since her father drowned — was simply refusing orders. Ten years of practice, and it walked off the job in one morning.

She thought of her father's hands on the letter. Of Marta's shaking ones. Of Finch saying, in his dusty garret, *I count people. You're the first one who counted.* Of herself, twenty feet underwater at the wreck site, lungs burning, fingers closing around oilcloth while the sea pressed on her chest like a question: *What will you do with it?*

She thought, finally and absurdly, of tally ledgers. Of how a number hidden in a column can change everything, but only when somebody reads it aloud.

Oscar Sterling stepped out from behind the fishmonger's barrow into open daylight.

The first thing she felt was not courage. It was nakedness — a physical sensation, like having her skin removed. Every eye in the square swung to her, hundreds of them, and she felt each one as weight, as heat. Her legs wanted very badly to carry her back behind the barrow. She made them carry her forward instead, up the square's center aisle between packed bodies that parted for her the way water parts for a hull, and she heard the crowd's sound change behind her — a low collective intake, the sound of people realizing that history was going to happen to somebody standing close enough to touch.

"Name," said Corbett, flustered, consulting nothing.

"Oscar Sterling," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Tally clerk, third registry, Meridian Trading Company. Daughter of Wen Sterling, pearl diver, born in Canton, drowned off the Gullwing reef eleven years ago. Investigated by this company twice — once after his death, and once before it, though nobody ever told me that until I read my own file by lamplight in a room I wasn't supposed to be in."

A ripple of something — not quite laughter, not quite alarm — crossed the soldiers' ranks.

"Remove her," said Hargrove mildly.

Two soldiers started down the steps. Oscar kept walking toward the platform, and it was Elias who stopped them — not with words, but by simply turning his chained wrists toward the soldiers and lifting one eyebrow, the way he might at a deckhand fumbling a knot. The soldiers hesitated. Crowds hesitate louder than anyone, and the two men caught the hesitation like a disease.

"She has the floor," Elias said quietly. "For now."

"The floor is not hers to have—"

"Captain Vane asked a question of the public," Oscar said, and here she did something she had rehearsed exactly zero times: she turned her back on the tribunal and faced the crowd. Their faces hit her like weather — dockworkers with rope-scarred hands, fishwives in Marta's style of headscarf, clerks and coal-heavers and a boy perched on a mooring post with his mouth hanging open. She had spent ten years learning these faces from below her own eyelids. Now she looked at them straight on, and found that they looked back like people waking up.

"My father died carrying something," she said. "Not pearls. Papers. He hid them in the reef where his boat went down, and he gave a letter to the only man he trusted with his blood's business — Captain Elias Vane, who has kept that letter sealed for eleven years because he swore to my father he would put it in no hands but mine. That is his entire crime, gentlemen. He kept a promise."

"Allegedly," said Hargrove.

"The letter contains more than words." Oscar reached into her coat — and every saber in the square came out of its scabbard at once, which would have been comic if it weren't lethal — and drew out, slowly, folded in oilcloth gone stiff with age and salt, the manifest. "It contains a bill of lading. Dated four months before my father's death. Signed by Governor Ambrose Crane. Shipping arms under Meridian's flag to a port the company told the crown it had never touched."

"Lies," Hargrove said, but he said it a half-second too late, and Oscar, who had spent a decade catching errors in columns of numbers, heard the delay the way she heard a misplaced digit.

"Captain Vane never opened that letter," she went on, raising her voice. She was surprised to find she didn't have to raise it much; the square had gone quiet as deep water. "Eleven years. He could have burned it. He could have sold it. He could have traded it to any of you—" she pointed at the tribunal without turning around— "for a pardon and a pension. Instead he kept it dry in an oilskin wrap and waited for a girl he'd last seen at nine years old. I ask the people of this harbor: does that sound like treason to you? Or does it sound like the kind of man you'd want owning a ship?"

From the back of the crowd, somebody — she was almost certain it was Marta, though she couldn't see through the blur in her own eyes — shouted: "It sounds like a Vane!"

The cheer that followed was small and ragged, but it was real, and Oscar felt it pass under her feet through the cobblestones like a swell under a hull.

Corbett banged his gavel twice, uselessly. "These are grave allegations. Produce your evidence properly or stand aside."

"I'm not standing anywhere," said Oscar. "I dove for this myself. Three days ago. Forty feet down on the Gullwing reef, in water cold enough to stop your heart, to fetch the second copy my father hid before he died. Because a letter alone can be called forgery." She unfolded the manifest with hands that were, finally, perfectly steady. "But paper signed by Crane, matching the letter line for line, date for date — that's corroboration. Shall I read the names?"

"You will do no such—"

"Wen Sterling. Master diver, the Fairwind's consort dives. Cargo entered: 'machine parts, forty crates.' Destination entered: blank. Signature authorizing entry—" and here Oscar's voice did break, just slightly, because she had traced that signature a hundred times in the ledger room at night, memorizing it the way other daughters memorize a father's handwriting from letters home— "Ambrose R. Crane, Governor, by his own hand."

The square did not erupt. It was quieter than that, and worse. It was the sound of several hundred people all arriving at the same arithmetic at the same moment. Oscar watched a foreman she'd known all her life look at a constable, and the constable look at his boots, and neither of them look away in shame so much as look *around*, taking the census of the crowd, measuring it.

Hargrove stood. For the first time since she'd known him, his face lost its ledger-book calm, and what came up beneath it was older and sweatier. "This document is unprovenanced. Any forger could—"

"Any forger could," agreed a new voice, high and precise and utterly unexpected, "but no forger did. I can prove it."

Every head in the square turned toward the man pushing his way up the aisle with a leather satchel clutched to his chest like a breastplate. He was small, round-shouldered, ink-stained at the cuffs, and he wore the expression of a man who had rehearsed this moment in a mirror and was horrified to find the moment lacked a mirror.

"Mr. Aldous Finch," Oscar breathed. "Census taker."

"Assistant registrar, retired, currently census taker, fourth district," said Finch, arriving at the platform steps and addressing the tribunal in the tone of a man correcting a misfiled record. "I take issue with the defendant's characterization of this document, and I am prepared to resolve the matter with data."

"Mr. Finch," said Hargrove slowly, "you are a crown employee."

"I am a counter of persons, Mr. Magistrate, which is a different employment entirely, whatever the pay schedule says." Finch unlatched his satchel and began removing papers with the ceremony of a surgeon laying out instruments. "For nineteen years I have kept records the crown did not ask me to keep. Irregularities. Anomalies. Ships that left the harbor heavier than their manifests allowed. Warehouses whose tax assessments never changed despite three expansions. Persons who appeared on no census and departed on no passenger list, yet drew company wages." He looked up, blinking mildly at the assembled soldiery. "You would be astonished, gentlemen, what a man can learn simply by noticing that numbers refuse to agree."

"Mr. Finch—" Hargrove began.

"For example." Finch selected a single sheet and held it aloft. "The brig *Cormorant*, listed as lost with all hands off the southern shoals in the spring of the year Wen Sterling drowned. Yet I have personally conducted the census of a fishing village where three of the *Cormorant*'s supposed dead crew now live, married, with children who appear in my registers. Ships don't sink and then attend christenings, Mr. Magistrate. Ledgers that lie always leave footprints, and I have been following these footprints for nineteen years without knowing whose feet made them." He turned, found Oscar, and inclined his head with grave formality. "Until this young woman taught me what I was counting."

"Sir," said Corbett wearily, "the tribunal cannot accept the private papers of a census clerk as—"

"Then accept mine," said another voice, and Boatswain Kerr shouldered out of the crowd.

Oscar's breath caught. Elias's oldest friend — the man who had sold them, who had walked off the Fairwind with Hargrove's pension promise in his pocket — stood at the foot of the steps holding a bundle of letters tied in twine, and his face was gray as old rope.

"Kerr," Elias said. Just the name. Nothing else. It was not forgiveness and it was not condemnation; it was simply the fact of him, spoken aloud, and Kerr flinched as if struck.

"I corresponded with Magistrate Hargrove," Kerr said hoarsely, holding up the bundle. "Nine letters. Terms of my pension. What I was to report from aboard the Fairwind. Dates, sums, his seal." His hands shook. "I did it to keep my bread, and I'll answer for that however it's asked of me. But I won't stand here and let a good man hang on paper that man wrote." He hurled the bundle up onto the platform, where it landed at Corbett's feet with a slap like a caught fish.

The crowd's sound now was not a murmur. It was a low continuous note, rising, the sound the harbor made before weather.

Finch, undeterred and apparently invigorated, had climbed two of the platform steps and was distributing papers to Corbett, to the second officer, to a bemused sergeant-at-arms. "Cross-reference, if you please. The *Cormorant*'s false loss against the manifest's blank destination. Note the dates align within a fortnight of Sterling's final dive. Note further that Magistrate Hargrove's own hand appears on a warehouse requisition dated—"

"Arrest her," Hargrove said, pointing at Oscar, and his voice cracked on it, which was the loudest thing he'd said all day. "Arrest the girl, arrest the clerk, clear the square—"

No soldier moved.

They stood in their blue ranks on the tribunal steps, fourteen of them — Oscar counted again, because counting steadied her even now — and they looked at Hargrove, and then they looked at the crowd, which had stopped being an audience and become a wall, and then they looked at the papers in Finch's hands, signed by their own governor, and the arithmetic finished itself in their faces.

A young corporal — Oscar would learn his name later, Tammas Reeve, nineteen years old, three months in the company colors — lowered his bayonet to rest and said, not loudly, to no one in particular:

"I didn't sign on to guard gallows for smugglers."

The sergeant beside him didn't correct him. That was the whole battle, right there, decided in a silence: no one corrected him.

"Read the rest," someone shouted from the crowd. "Read it all!"

"Yes," said Elias Vane from the platform, and his voice carried the way a bell carries, cutting clean through the swelling noise. He looked at Oscar — really looked at her, for the first time since she'd stepped into the open, and there was something in his face that undid her more thoroughly than all the sabers had. Pride, and grief, and underneath both a fierce, patient gladness, as if he had been waiting eleven years to see exactly this girl stand exactly here. "Read it all, Oscar. Let them hear your father's name in the daylight."

Oscar lifted the manifest over her head.

And that was when the bells began — not the harbor bell, not the tribunal's chime, but the great cracked bell of the Governor's residence, tolling fast and ugly, the signal every soul in this port had been taught to dread since childhood and had never once heard used.

The crowd's roar faltered. Down at the harbor mouth, oars flashed in the morning sun as a longboat packed with marines pulled hard for the quay. From the residence hill, a column of regulars was already descending at double time, bayonets fixed.

On the far side of the square, a door banged open, and a runner in the governor's livery went past at a dead sprint, shouting to no one and everyone:

"Martial law! By order of Governor Crane — the tribunal is dissolved! Clear the square or be fired upon!"
