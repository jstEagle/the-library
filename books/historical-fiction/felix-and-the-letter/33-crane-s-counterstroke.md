## 33. Crane's Counterstroke

Governor Ambrose Crane had always believed that trouble, like weather, could be outwaited.

He had risen before dawn for forty years — first as a junior clerk in the customs house, then as a man whose name appeared on shipping contracts no one dared audit — and in all that time he had learned one lesson above all others: scandals were tides. They came in loud, they soaked everyone's boots, and then they went out again, leaving the sand smooth and the powerful still standing on it. He had survived three market panics, two wars of rumor and one of cannon, a poisoned harvest, and the unfortunate affair of the *Larkspur*, whose manifest he had personally burned in a fireplace not unlike the one warming him now.

So when his aide de camp, a pale young man named Prewitt, knocked at the residence door at half past five in the morning with the words "Sir, there's been a development at the tribunal," Crane did not look up from his tea.

"Developments," he said, "are the tribunals' whole purpose. I appointed them to develop."

"Yes, sir. It's only — Captain Vane's trial, sir. The Sterling girl has spoken. There was a document read aloud. Your name appears on it, sir. Twice. With your signature."

Crane set down his cup with great care, because a man who rattled his crockery was a man who had already lost.

"Fetch me the morning reports," he said, "and send for Colonel Ashe. And Prewitt — how many people are in the square?"

Prewitt consulted his slate. "As of the last count, sir, between four and five hundred. Growing. The dockhouses turned out at shift change. The fishwives have come up from the strand." He hesitated. "They're singing, sir."

"Singing what?"

"The old harbor song. The one about the drowned men. The one your late wife used to—"

"I know the one," Crane said quietly.

* * *

By seven he stood at the window of the residence overlooking the harbor square, and what he saw displeased him in a way he could not immediately name.

It was not the crowd itself. Crowds were cattle; he had dispersed larger ones with a single company of marines and a well-timed rumor about quarantine. It was not even the girl — though the girl troubled him more than he cared to admit. Oscar Sterling stood on the tribunal steps with her father's papers held above her head like a lantern, and she did not look frightened, which was a clerical error on somebody's part. Somebody had been supposed to frighten her months ago. Hargrove had assured him it was being handled. Hargrove, Crane thought, would be having a very instructive morning.

No — what displeased him was the texture of the thing. A mob has a certain sound, a heat, an appetite. This gathering had none of that. Dockworkers stood with their arms folded, listening. Fishwives passed bread along the back rows. Old Marta, released from custody and thinner than her wanted posters had promised, sat in a chair someone had carried down for her, right at the front, where the soldiers could see her. Even the census man — Finch, the little gray ghost who counted every soul in the city twice over out of some private madness — stood at the edge of the steps reading aloud from notebooks in a dry, uninflected voice, as if reciting laundry lists: dates, tonnages, missing ships, payments routed through accounts with numbers instead of names.

Laundry lists with the governor's signature on every third line.

"They believe each other," Crane said aloud, to the empty room, and understood at last why the sight bothered him. Belief was the one commodity he had never managed to corner. He could buy testimony, silence, uniforms, and judges. He could not buy a fishwife's certainty that she had seen the wrong ships come home light.

Colonel Ashe arrived at half past seven smelling of wet wool and bad coffee. He was a solid man, not clever, which Crane had always counted as a virtue in a soldier. Crane laid the situation before him the way he laid out ledgers: plainly, without adjectives.

"A tribunal convened under my authority has exceeded its mandate. It is now, functionally, an instrument of sedition — public accusation of the crown's appointed governor, incitement of the dockhands, obstruction of lawful process. The legal remedy is dissolution. The practical remedy is martial law, because dissolution alone will leave five hundred people in the square asking questions I'd prefer they didn't ask."

Ashe studied the square through the glass. "And if they don't disperse?"

"You are a colonel, Colonel. Tell me what you do with a crowd that will not disperse."

"Depends on the crowd, sir. That one's got grandmothers in it."

"It has also got the man who refused to surrender six ships to the crown in wartime, and a girl who has just committed forgery, theft of company documents, and obstruction of justice in front of two hundred witnesses. Grandmothers notwithstanding, the square contains criminals. Clearing criminals is not tyranny. It is Tuesday."

Ashe did not laugh. Crane noted that. For forty years people had laughed at his Tuesdays.

"The marines can clear it," Ashe said slowly. "Bayonets at the front rank, no orders to use them, push them back off the square by noon. But sir — I'll say my piece once and then obey. Half those marines grew up in those dockhouse rows. Their mothers buy fish from those women. If anyone fires, I can't promise where the firing stops, and neither can you."

"That is why no one will fire," Crane said. "Because the moment a shot goes astray, this stops being a legal question and becomes a story, and stories, Colonel, are the only thing in this world I cannot forge. We move before the story sets. Dissolve the tribunal, suspend civil courts, garrison the square, and arrest the principals quietly — Vane, the girl, the printer's boy, the census clerk. No spectacle. By evening the square will be empty and by winter no one will agree on what happened in it. That is how power works when it is grown up."

"And if it doesn't work?"

Crane smiled the smile that had closed a thousand contracts. "Then I shall be very disappointed, and someone will be very sorry."

* * *

The proclamation was drafted by nine, printed by ten, and nailed to the tribunal doors at eleven minutes past — Crane had insisted on eleven minutes past, because a proclamation posted on the hour looked scheduled and one posted slightly late looked urgent, and urgency was contagious.

*By order of the Governor of the Province and Its Dependencies: the tribunal sitting in the harbor square is dissolved. Its proceedings are void. Civil courts are suspended until further notice. The city and harbor are placed under martial law. Assemblies of more than five persons are forbidden. This order takes effect immediately.*

He read it himself from the residence balcony an hour later — no, better: he sent Hargrove to read it. Hargrove had a voice like a filing drawer and a face nobody had ever trusted, but trust was not the point. Hargrove was the point. If the thing went ill, Hargrove had been the company magistrate who had pursued a fatherless girl, arrested a fishwife, and forged evidence against a sea captain; he was already so thoroughly painted that a little more red wouldn't show. Crane watched from behind the balcony curtains with the detachment of a man watching his own chess piece advance into exchange range. Sentiment, he reminded himself, was a tax paid by people who couldn't do arithmetic.

Below, the proclamation was read. The square went quiet in a way that Crane, listening through a cracked window, found almost worse than shouting.

Then a woman's voice — one of the fishwives, unhurried, pitched to carry over water the way fishwives' voices were — said clearly enough to reach the balcony:

"Five people, is it? Count again, love. You've never been good at counting us."

And the square laughed.

Not roared. Laughed — warm, brief, familiar, the laugh of people who knew each other, which was precisely the problem. Then they did not disperse. They simply stayed, four hundred and some souls arranged around the tribunal steps like a congregation around an altar, and Elias Vane, in irons, said something too low for the balcony to hear that made the whole square set its jaw, and Crane understood that his hourglass had been turned without his permission.

"Dissolve them," he told Prewitt. "Send Ashe. Bayonets. Now."

* * *

What followed took fifty-eight minutes, and Crane spent all of them at the window, and afterward he would remember the hour the way other men remembered shipwrecks — in perfect, useless detail.

He remembered the marines coming down the hill in column, blue coats bright at eleven in the morning, boots finding a rhythm on the cobbles that made the whole square's heartbeat seem to stumble.

He remembered the crowd not running. That was the part no manual covered. The manuals assumed crowds broke. This one compressed — dockworkers drifting backward a few steps, closing ranks, putting the oldest women at the center and the broadest men at the edges, the way a shoal turns. Someone began the harbor song again, low, and this time the marines' own boots seemed to falter against it, because half of them had been rocked to sleep by that song and knew it, and knew that they knew it.

He remembered Colonel Ashe at the head of the column, stopping twenty paces from the steps, and the long dumb tableau of a drawn saber pointed at nothing in particular while an old woman in the front row — Marta, it was Marta, the fishwife Hargrove had arrested and failed to break — looked at the nearest marine, a boy of perhaps nineteen, and said, not unkindly, "You'll want to put that away, love. Your mum's on the stall next to mine."

He remembered the boy lowering his eyes exactly the way, Crane realized with something like nausea, a tally clerk lowers her eyes. Busy hands. Lowered gaze. Invisible obedience. The entire machinery of his power built out of boys who did not want to be there.

And then he remembered the moment the hour cracked.

A lieutenant — young, freckled, name unknown, forever anonymous and therefore unforgettable — received some order from the column's rear and relayed it forward, and the front rank fixed bayonets, the metallic rattle of it running down the line like a shiver, and the song stopped, and for perhaps thirty seconds the square held the absolute silence of a held breath.

Crane's hand was on the window latch. He was aware, distantly, that he was leaning on it hard enough to whiten his knuckles, and that he had not leaned on anything in forty years.

One marine — the boy, the nineteen-year-old, Marta's neighbor's son — took one step sideways. Out of line. Not desertion, nothing so dramatic; just a half-pace of drift, the way a man moves toward his own people in a churchyard. Then another marine shifted. Then a sergeant, God help them all, cleared his throat and did not correct them.

"Hold," Ashe called, in a voice like a door closing. "Front rank — present nothing. Stand fast." And to the crowd, quieter: "Nobody's clearing anybody today. Go home respectful and let me do the same."

They did not go home. But they did not advance either, and the bayonets came down by degrees, and the black hour — Crane named it that later, privately, with a precision that amounted to self-flagellation — passed its worst minute and began, grudgingly, to drain.

* * *

At noon he received Ashe in the residence study and listened to a report delivered in the flat tone of a man reading his own resignation.

"No dispersal, sir. No violence either. The men will hold the perimeter but I won't order them into the square, and sir, I'd be misleading you if I pretended I could order it tomorrow either. Something's changed in them since morning. Or in the arithmetic." He paused at the door. "You should know the girl read the manifest again while we stood there. Names, dates, tonnage, signatures. She read it the way scripture gets read. My lieutenant wrote it all down, sir. My own lieutenant. Said somebody ought to keep a true record."

After he left, Crane sat for a long time with the ledger of his life open in his mind, running the columns.

He had always assumed force was the floor beneath everything — that when all else failed, the bayonets would hold, and therefore he could never truly lose, only postpone. Today he had watched the floor turn to water. It had cost nothing dramatic. No mutiny, no musket fired, no officer disgraced. Just a boy stepping half a pace out of line, and a song, and a grandmother with a chair carried down for her like visiting royalty. Power, he saw now with terrible clarity, was not a wall he owned. It was a tide, and it had gone out, and he had spent the morning standing on wet sand insisting he was dry.

There remained, of course, options. There always were. Crane poured brandy he did not taste and began, methodically, to count what was left: the patrons in the capital, whose letters had grown cooler with each report; the flagship *Resolute* riding at anchor with a loyal crew and coal for ninety days; Hargrove, now a liability wearing his own handwriting; and one final document — a general order of evacuation, prepared years ago against exactly this contingency, transferring the government to the *Resolute* "until the province is restored to order." From the deck of a warship, with the harbor guns beneath him, a governor could declare anything. Could bombard a city, if it came to that, and call it restoring order.

His pen hovered over the evacuation order.

It was at this moment that Prewitt knocked, entered, and stood holding a slate with the expression of a man bringing news of a death in the family.

"Out with it."

"The harbor, sir. You should come to the window. All of it."

Crane crossed to the north windows and looked down at his harbor, and felt, for the first time in forty years, the specific sensation of a ledger refusing to balance.

The fishing fleet was putting out. Not to fish — the whole harbor knew they weren't fishing. Dories, luggers, the little oyster boats with patched sails, dozens of them, spreading across the mouth of the harbor in a loose, deliberate, impossible-to-shell arc without looking like anything at all except boats at work. Behind them, from the seawall, came the dockworkers with hatch covers, dories' spars, cargo netting, their own bodies, stringing themselves along the mole in a picket line of grandmothers and stevedores. And beyond the point, hull-up in the afternoon light, moving with the unhurried grace of a ship that knows the water she was built for, came the *Fairwind* — impounded, seized, boarded at dawn by soldiers who were at this moment, according to the signal flags fluttering from her yardarm, no longer aboard.

Prewitt read the flags aloud, his voice failing halfway through. "She says, sir... 'Come and count us.'"

Governor Ambrose Crane set down his brandy, took up his pen, and signed the evacuation order with a steady hand.

"Signal the *Resolute*," he said. "We sail on the evening tide."
