## 35. Climax: The Manifest Read Aloud

The harbor square smelled of tar and fear.

I had known fear the way other people knew weather—something that arrived, did its business, and moved on. This was different. This was fear with architecture: the tribunal platform raised on barrels in front of the customs house, the gallows frame beside it still bare and patient, and three ranks of soldiers drawn up in a half-circle with their muskets held the way men hold things they hope they won't have to use. Above it all, the morning came up gray and ordinary over the water, as if the sky hadn't heard the news.

Elias Vane stood on the platform between two company guards. They had put him in a borrowed coat too small across the shoulders, and his wrists were bound in front of him, but he stood the way he had always stood at anchor—feet apart, chin level, a man who had decided where his center was and intended to keep it. He did not look at the magistrate's bench. He looked at us. At Silas, pressed against my side at the edge of the crowd. At Marta, who had insisted on coming even though her hands still shook when she thought no one was watching. At Finch, clutching his leather satchel of archives like a midwife's bag.

At me.

"Ask the girl what her father carried," he had said yesterday, and the words had been sitting in my chest ever since like a swallowed coal.

Hargrove presided from the magistrate's chair with the oily courtesy I remembered from his office, though today the oil had gone thin and I could see the machinery underneath. Governor Crane sat behind him and slightly above—a man arranged rather than seated—and surveyed the crowd with the expression of someone watching insects at a picnic.

"The prisoner stands accused of treason against the crown," Hargrove read, "of conspiracy with foreign powers, and of possession of seditious documents."

"I confess to the documents," Elias said pleasantly. "I've kept every letter anyone ever wrote me. Sentimental man. Ask anyone."

A ripple went through the crowd—not laughter, not yet, but the raw material of it. Hargrove's jaw tightened by a fraction.

I looked down at the oilcloth packet inside my coat, tied against my ribs where Papa's knife used to ride. The manifest. Forty hours old from the reef and still smelling of salt and old rope. Beside me, Silas's hand found my elbow.

"You don't have to do it from out here," he murmured. "There's a procedure. You could petition the—"

"There's no procedure for people like me, Silas. There never was. That's rather the point." I took a breath that hurt. "Hold Marta's hand. She'll want something to hold onto, and Finch has both of his full of paper."

Then I stepped out of the crowd.

It is one thing to spend eleven years learning to be invisible. It is another thing entirely to unlearn it in four steps across cobbles, with several hundred pairs of eyes turning toward you like compass needles. My legs wanted to take me back. My father's voice—or my memory of it, which amounts to the same thing—said, *Girl, you swim to the thing or you don't. There's no swimming halfway.*

"Magistrate," I said, and my voice cracked on the first word and steadied on the second, which seemed a fair division of labor. "Before you sentence Captain Vane for what my father carried, you might like to hear what my father found."

Hargrove's eyes narrowed. "This tribunal does not take testimony from—"

"From indentured tally clerks? Daughters of drowned divers? No. I know exactly what this tribunal takes testimony from. That's why I brought something that doesn't testify. It just sits there." I held up the oilcloth packet. "Being evidence."

The word *evidence* did something to the soldiers in the front rank. One of them, a boy with a sunburned neck, glanced at his sergeant. Sergeants, I have since learned, are the load-bearing wall of any army, and this one looked like a wall with doubts about its foundations.

"Seize that document," Hargrove said.

"If you seize it," said Finch, stepping forward with the unhurried air of a man who had rehearsed this moment in a garret for thirty years, "it will be noted in my records that the tribunal destroyed exculpatory material before reading it. I keep records, Magistrate. It is my profession. Places of birth, dates of arrival, tonnage of ships, names of witnesses. All counted. All retained." He adjusted his spectacles. "Counted twice, actually. I find counting once insufficiently reliable."

For one strange heartbeat, I loved him completely.

Crane leaned forward and said something too low for the square to hear. Hargrove swallowed and gestured. "Read your paper, then. Quickly."

* * *

I climbed the tribunal steps on legs made of someone else's wood, and the guards let me pass because nobody had given them an order to do otherwise and men with muskets are helpless without orders. Up close, Elias smelled of the hold of the Fairwind and the particular courage of a man who has already done all his fearing in advance.

"Steady, girl," he said quietly, the way he'd said it when I was nine and crying over a torn net. "You've got the current with you now. Look at them."

I looked. The square was packed shoulder to shoulder—dockworkers off the early shift, fishwives with their aprons still wet, crew of the Fairwind standing together in a knot near the front with Kerr among them, pale as new sailcloth. And everywhere, faces turned upward, waiting. Eleven years I had made myself nothing so that nothing could be taken from me. Now everything hung on being seen.

"My name is Oscar Sterling," I said, and saying it aloud felt like surfacing. "My father was Li Wei Sterling, pearl diver, of this harbor, drowned off the Serpent Reef fourteen years ago. The company called it an accident. Here is what he actually carried up from his last dive."

I unwrapped the oilcloth. My hands were shaking, so I did what Papa taught me—grip the work, not the worry. The manifest came out stiff and salt-stained, its ink faded to the brown of old blood but perfectly legible where it mattered.

"This is a cargo manifest. Dated the eleventh of March, fourteen years past. It lists forty crates shipped under Meridian Trading Company colors aboard the brig *Andromeda*, consigned to the customs house of this port. The official manifest filed for the *Andromeda* that voyage lists forty crates also—but declares them cotton goods." I looked up. "This manifest declares them otherwise. Rifles. Nine hundred rifles, forty cases of cartridge, and two field guns broken down into parts. Shipped through the blockade of a nation we were not yet at war with, to buyers whose names are written right here."

I read the names. I read them slowly, because slow was the only speed my mouth would agree to, and because I wanted every one of them to land. Some meant nothing to the crowd. Two or three meant a great deal—I knew this because a fishwife near the front said a word that would have gotten her mouth washed out by her mother, and a ripple of recognition ran through the dockworkers like wind through wheat.

"And at the bottom," I said, "the signature of the man who ordered the shipment, countersigned by the company factor who processed it, and initialed by the harbormaster who was paid to look away. Three signatures. Fourteen years. And a diver who saw the crates go into the water instead of onto the pier, because someone decided the guns should vanish until they were needed—and decided the man who watched them vanish should vanish with them."

The square had gone very quiet. Quiet squares are louder than loud ones; I could hear the harbor slapping against the pilings.

"They killed my father for this paper," I said, "and then they spent fourteen years making sure no one would ever ask why a pearl diver died in water shallow enough to stand up in. When the census taker wrote down where my father was born, they opened an investigation into *me*—not because my blood matters to anyone in this square, but because I am the last living link to a witness they thought they'd buried. They tried Captain Vane for treason because he refused to hand over his fleet to the same men who armed our enemies under our own flag. So before you hang him for treason—" I turned to face the bench. "Governor, perhaps you'd like to tell the square whose signature this is."

And I held the manifest up over my head, facing outward, Crane's signature catching the gray morning light.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, and afterward everyone in Harbor City would remember it differently, which is how history works, I expect.

First: silence, long enough that I could hear a gull arguing with itself on the customs house roof.

Second: Crane stood. He did not deny it. Denial requires a belief that the audience can still be persuaded, and Crane was a man who understood audiences precisely well enough to know when he'd lost them. "Arrest the girl," he said. "She's in possession of stolen company property. Arrest all of them."

Third: nobody arrested anybody. The sergeant with the doubtful foundation looked at his boy soldiers, and the boy soldiers looked at the crowd, and the crowd—which included every aunt, cousin, and drinking companion those boys had in the world—looked back. A musket is a very poor tool for shooting people who have handed you bread at their stall since you were tall enough to reach over it.

Fourth: Hargrove made his mistake. I will grant him this much—he was not a coward; he was worse, a believer in his own ledger, and when his accounts stopped balancing he reached for the only entry left. "Fire on the square," he said to the sergeant. "Clear it. That is a direct order. Fire."

"Firing on civilians in a tribunal square, sir," the sergeant said, in the careful voice of a man reading terms aloud before signing, "without a martial law proclamation in force—"

"I *said* fire!"

"No, Mr. Hargrove," said a young voice, "I don't believe he will."

Lieutenant Ashcombe—young, pink, recently transferred from the capital, and destined, I later learned, to be the only name from that day printed in the histories besides Crane's—stepped out of the second rank with his sword sheathed and his hands visible, which in that moment took more nerve than anything I did. "Mr. Hargrove, by what authority does a company magistrate order crown soldiers to fire on crown subjects? Because if the answer is Governor Crane, then I'll want that order in writing, signed, with the date. I'm told documentation matters greatly in this city lately."

Someone in the crowd laughed. Then more someones. Laughter, in a square like that, is a tide; once it starts you cannot recall it, and it came in wave after wave while Hargrove stood there with his mouth opening and closing like a landed fish, discovering in real time that power which exists only in a signature is worth exactly as much as the paper under the pen.

Ashcombe turned to two of his own men. "Place Mr. Hargrove under arrest pending inquiry. Gently. He's had a difficult morning."

And fifth—the piece I will admit I missed entirely, because I was watching Hargrove being led past me wearing the expression of a man whose entire fortune had just been repossessed—I did not see Crane leave. I only heard it: the crowd nearest the customs house rear door turning, surging, someone shouting "The governor!" and then the sight of Crane's carriage pulling away from the square at a pace unbecoming of his office, whipping its horses toward the waterfront, toward his flagship, toward the idea—so popular among great men—that the sea will keep your secrets if the land has stopped doing it.

"He's running," Silas said beside me, wonder in his voice.

"Yes," I said. My legs had chosen that moment to inform me they were done pretending to be structural, and I sat down abruptly on the tribunal steps, manifest still clutched to my chest. "They usually do. It's the ones who stay and apologize you have to watch out for."

Elias lowered himself carefully beside me, wrists still bound, and knocked his shoulder against mine. "Your papa," he said, "is somewhere right now being insufferable about this."

"Papa never insufferable," said Marta, arriving to plant herself on my other side with the finality of an anchor dropping. "Papa smug, maybe. Insufferable is your department, Captain."

"Guilty," Elias said, and closed his eyes, and for a moment he was just a tired man in a too-small coat, alive, on steps instead of a scaffold.

Finch appeared in front of us, breathless, satchel clutched to his chest, spectacles fogged. "Oscar," he said. "That was—statistically speaking—the most significant act of public record-keeping in the history of this port, and I would like it formally noted that I predicted you were capable of it. In my archive. Dated the day we met."

"You wrote a prediction about me?"

"I write predictions about everyone. You're the first one to come true ahead of schedule." He pushed his spectacles up. Behind them his eyes were wet, though he would deny this under oath forever. "Also, the lieutenant is asking for you. He says the manifest will need to be entered properly, witnessed, certified—"

"Of course it will."

"—and I volunteered to supervise. I hope that's acceptable. I have brought duplicate forms."

Silas laughed, and helped me up, and the crowd parted around us going somewhere—to the harbor, following the dust of Crane's carriage, a river of people flowing downhill toward the water to watch a governor learn what it feels like to be counted. Kerr was at the front of them. Whatever debt that man owed, I suppose he'd started paying it.

I looked out over the square, at the empty gallows frame that would never hold anyone, at the soldiers stacking arms because somebody sensible had finally suggested it, at the harbor beyond where the Fairwind rode at anchor with her crew's kin streaming toward her in boats.

Fourteen years ago my father swam to the bottom of the sea carrying the truth, and set it down in a reef, and drowned for the privilege. Today it crossed a square above my head and did what truth does, eventually, when someone finally holds it up high enough.

But Crane's flagship was already warping off the wall, canvas rising, standing out for open water—and there is such a thing, I thought, watching her go, as a man who runs fast enough to make justice buy a ticket.

Lieutenant Ashcombe was already shouting for a fast boat.
