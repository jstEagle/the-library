## 36. What Power Does Afterward

Aldous Finch had counted the population of Port Meridian for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once been invited anywhere. So it was with a peculiar sensation—not quite vertigo, not quite indigestion—that he found himself standing in the harbor square on a gray Tuesday morning, three days after the trial, holding a census ledger under one arm like a shield, watching men in fine coats dismantle a gallows.

They worked quickly, the carpenters. A gallows, Finch had learned from his archive of civic irregularities, took two days to raise and half a morning to forget. By noon there would be nothing but pale rectangles in the cobblestones where the posts had stood, and by the following week some vendor would set up a pie cart over the spot, and within a generation children would swear the square had always smelled of fried dough and never of rope.

*People are counted,* Finch thought, *and then they are uncounted, and nobody files the correction.*

He opened his ledger to a fresh page and wrote, in his small exact hand: *Gallows, harbor square, erected [date illegible—rain], dismantled Tuesday. Witness: self.* It was not strictly census business. Very little of what he did anymore was strictly census business.

"You'll want to move along, sir," said one of the carpenters, not unkindly. "Nothing to see."

"On the contrary," said Finch. "There is everything to see. That is rather my complaint."

The carpenter looked at him the way men look at a dog that has spoken. Finch tipped his hat and moved along anyway, because he had learned—late, but he had learned—that a man who insists on being right in public pays for it in company.

* * *

The news from the capital arrived on Thursday, carried in the morning paper that Marta's nephew sold off the fish carts, and it was read aloud, in various registers of disbelief, up and down the docks.

Governor Ambrose Crane would not face trial. He would not face a tribunal, a commission, or so much as a sternly worded hearing. He would be *retired*. The word sat in the sentence like a bone in a fish cake. Retired, with honors. Recalled to the capital "for consultations regarding his health." His patrons—the shipping magnates, the colonial office undersecretaries, the two lords whose names appeared in Finch's private archive forty-one times each under variations of *irregular disbursement*—had closed around him like water over a stone, and when the water cleared, Crane was simply gone, pensioned off to some damp estate where he would presumably spend his remaining years writing memoirs in which he was the hero.

Hargrove, at least, was in custody. Hargrove, whose ledger-book face had fooled everyone but a tally clerk and a fishwife network, would stand trial in the spring. Small, mean, prosecutable Hargrove, who had ordered soldiers to fire on civilians and found, to his astonishment, that even his own junior officers would rather arrest him than obey.

But Crane walked.

Finch read the announcement twice at his garret desk, then a third time by candlelight, checking figures as was his habit. And there in the column margins, beside the official notice, he wrote the thing that had been forming in him since the square:

*Sum of justice rendered: one magistrate, one fleet returned, one girl freed. Sum of justice withheld: one governor, forty-one lords' names, eleven years of drowned men. Balance outstanding.*

He underlined *balance outstanding* twice, which for Finch was the equivalent of weeping.

* * *

He found Oscar Sterling where he had expected to find her—at the harbor wall, in the late afternoon, looking at the water over the pearl beds with an expression Finch had catalogued years ago in a different context and only now understood. It was the expression of a person doing arithmetic that would not come out even.

She heard his footsteps—he made no particular effort to be quiet, having no talent for it—and did not turn.

"Let me guess," she said. "You've come to count me."

"Once daily, lately," Finch admitted. "It's become a habit I can't justify on official forms."

"And what number am I today?"

Finch considered this with the seriousness it deserved. Oscar had a way of asking idle questions that were not idle, and answering them carelessly felt, to him, like misfiling a soul. "One," he said finally. "You've been one person every day this week, which is more than can be said for Governor Crane, who has been at least four since Thursday depending on which newspaper one reads."

That earned him half a smile. She shifted on the wall, making room, and he sat, arranging his coat and ledger with ceremony, because sitting on a harbor wall was not something his body knew how to do gracefully and ritual was his only disguise.

"They say he's ill," Oscar said, to the water. "That's the word going round. *Fragile health.* Eleven years of taking bribes off missing ships and now his health fails him, conveniently, in a direction pointed away from the gallows."

"Gout," Finch supplied. "The papers specify gout. Gout is the traditional ailment of gentlemen caught in wrongdoing; I have counted nine instances of it in scandal coverage over my career. It is apparently an illness one catches from guilt, or from rich food, which may be the same thing."

Oscar laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly folded away—and then she was quiet again. Below them the tide moved over the pearl beds where her father had died, and where, a fortnight ago, she herself had gone down into the cold and come back up carrying the truth in a waterproof roll against her ribs. Finch had seen her afterward, changed, the way a coastline is changed by weather. He did not ask about the dive. Some entries one leaves blank out of respect.

"It should have been him," she said at last. "In the square. Not Elias on trial—*him*. I stood there and watched them take the ropes down and I thought, this is what winning looks like? A retired governor with gout, a pardoned captain who can't keep his crew fed through winter, and Marta flinching every time a uniform comes within ten feet of her stall?" She turned to look at Finch directly, and he endured it, because he was learning to endure things for her. "Tell me the numbers, Mr. Finch. You're the only honest mathematician I know. Did we actually win anything?"

Finch opened his ledger on his knees. This was theater, partly—he could have recited it from memory—but the ritual steadied him, and he suspected she knew that and allowed it.

"Item," he said. "The Fairwind is released, crew intact, minus one boatswain currently assisting the tribunal and hating himself productively. Item: your indenture, dissolved by your own signature, witnessed by myself, filed under S. Item: Meridian Trading Company, under interim administration, its registry open for the first time in thirty years—I have applied thrice for access and been refused twice, which is progress. Item: four hundred and six dockworkers struck from the irregular lists, restored to their wages and their names." He paused. "Item: one census taker, formerly regarded by this city as furniture, now invited to dinner on Sundays. That last item is, in my professional assessment, the most statistically improbable outcome of the entire affair."

"There it is again," Oscar said softly. "The list of everything we got. And underneath it, the thing we didn't."

"Yes," said Finch, because lying to her had proven impossible and he'd stopped trying in the summer. "Crane walks. And the forty-one names behind him walk further. I have spent the week trying to make the arithmetic agree with itself and it will not. But I will tell you what I have concluded, for what it is worth, which may be nothing."

She waited. Gulls wheeled over the masts. Somewhere behind them the pie cart was already open for business.

"Power does not fall the way men fall," Finch said. "I have counted its movements for years, in tax rolls and shipping manifests and the quiet gaps where ships disappear from record. Power retreats. It withdraws to the capital, consolidates, changes its coat, and returns under a new name with clean hands and excellent references. Crane is not the disease. Crane is the—clerk is too kind a word for him—the *symptom* that got written down. We convicted the symptom." He closed the ledger. "The balance remains outstanding. It will likely remain outstanding for years. Perhaps for my whole career, if I'm fortunate enough to have one, which is itself uncertain, as the new administration has already asked whether my archives might be 'consolidated,' and I have developed strong opinions about what happens to archives that get consolidated."

Oscar was silent a long moment. Then she said, "You're frightened."

"I am a man who has spent his life being invisible on purpose, and has recently become conspicuous," Finch said. "Fear is the appropriate response to my situation. I have entered it in the ledger."

"What do you do with it?"

"Count," he said simply. "When the world refuses to be just, I find the next true thing and write it down. It is a small weapon. It does not reach far. But it has not yet missed."

* * *

Sunday dinner, as it happened, was that evening, and Finch arrived—as he arrived at everything—with a folder of documents under his arm and no other contribution, having been informed by Silas that guests customarily brought wine and having decided that documents were better than wine because wine got drunk and disappeared whereas documents persisted.

Marta's stall had reopened in the fish market, though she still kept a knife within reach and still went still when anyone in a coat approached her too quickly. She had cooked, nonetheless—cooked enormously, furiously, as though feeding people were the argument she was making against everything that had been done to her—and the rented room above her stall held Elias at the head of the table looking uncomfortable in the role, Silas with ink permanently under his fingernails now, young Tam from the fish carts, and Oscar, who had laughed twice before the soup and seemed startled by it both times.

Finch ate in respectable silence until Elias set down his spoon and said, heavily, the way he said everything: "Read the paper this morning."

"We all did," Oscar said.

"Then you'll know my pardon came with a letter attached. Commission. Post-captain, coastal patrol, under the new admiralty." Elias turned his cup in place without drinking. "Same waters. Same ledgers. New flag painted on the same hull."

"What did you answer?" Silas asked.

"Hadn't, yet."

"You'll answer no," Oscar said.

"I expect I will." Elias almost smiled. "But it sits wrong with me, girl, refusing. There's a part of a man that wants to fix things from inside the room."

"The room fixes the man first," Marta said quietly, from the stove. "I was inside a room with Hargrove's men for nine days. I know how rooms work."

Nobody had an answer to that, so they ate, and Finch, watching them—the captain and the clerk's-daughter-turned-salvager, the printer's boy and the fishwife, himself, the counter of heads—understood suddenly what he was looking at, and opened his ledger right there at the table, which caused Tam to choke on his bread.

"What are you writing?" Oscar demanded.

"A household," Finch said. "Five persons at table, plus one absent boatswain who ought to be here and will be, I judge, within the month, once his shame finishes cooking. Occupations various. Relations: none by blood." He looked up. "All previous census forms list you all as unattached individuals. The form is wrong. I intend to correct it."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Marta laughed—the first full laugh out of her in weeks, rusty as an old winch—and Elias said, gruffly, "Put me down as 'patriarch, reluctant,'" and the room warmed the way rooms do when someone has finally said the true thing out loud.

* * *

He walked home late, along the harbor wall, past the pale rectangles in the square where the gallows had been, and stopped at the top of the seaward steps because there was a light moving in his garret window.

Finch did not carry a weapon. He had never seen the need; his weapons were duplicates, copies, and cross-references, and he kept them distributed among three locations precisely against this eventuality. He climbed the stairs calmly, noting as he went that the lock showed fresh scratches, and opened the door onto a man standing between his shelves with a candle, middle-aged, well-dressed, patient as a bill collector.

"Mr. Finch." The man inclined his head. "I represent parties in the capital with an interest in continuity. There's been talk of your archive. Certain pages concerning certain shipments. We hoped to acquire it privately. Generously."

"And if I decline?"

The man smiled the way Hargrove used to smile. "Then the new administration will find a regulatory pretext to consolidate it for you. Papers do go missing, Mr. Finch. Census takers do get reassigned. To the interior. The dry, distant interior."

Finch set down his candle, opened his ledger to the day's final page, and wrote, aloud as he wrote: *"Tuesday. One visitor, unnamed, representing forty-one names. Offered to purchase truth. Declined. Balance outstanding—"*

"That's not funny," the man said.

"No," Finch agreed, looking up. "It's arithmetic. You may tell your principals that my archive is not for sale, because it is not mine alone anymore. I have copies. In trustworthy hands. Hands that hold them for people who count." He blew out his own candle, leaving only the visitor's, and smiled with genuine mildness. "Do show yourselves out. I know exactly how many steps you take leaving, and I will check."

The man left. Finch stood in the dark among his shelves a long while, heart hammering in exact, countable rhythm, and then added one final line:

*They know about me now. Correction filed. Next entry pending.*

Outside, far below, a lamp burned late in the window above Marta's stall, and Finch thought it might be time to tell the household everything.
