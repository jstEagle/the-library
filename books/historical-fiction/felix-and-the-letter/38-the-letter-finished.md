## 38. The Letter, Finished

The letter had been in my possession for the better part of a year, and I had read every inch of it a hundred times—except for the part that mattered.

That is not entirely true. It is not even mostly true. But it is how it felt, standing at the window of my new rooms above Marta's stall on an October evening with the paper trembling in my hands.

Here is what I mean. The bill of lading—the evidence, the weapon, the thing that toppled a governor—I knew by heart. Every date, every signature, every crate of rifles logged as "cotton goods" and every shipment of "machinery" that was neither cotton nor machinery but powder and shot bound for a rebellion Governor Crane had been quietly funding with one hand while stamping it out with the other. I could recite it the way other people recite psalms. It had been read aloud on tribunal steps. It had been copied into newspapers from here to the capital. It belonged to everyone now.

But folded inside it, along creases so worn they'd gone soft as cloth, were two other pages. Pages written in my father's hand—not his ledger hand, which was cramped and careful, but his loose hand, the one he used for supply lists and jokes and the little drawings of fish he left tucked into my shoes on my birthday. Pages I had not been able to make myself read since the night Elias first unsealed the letter in the Fairwind's cabin.

I had told myself it was prudence. A girl who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being overlooked develops excellent reasons for not doing things. The private pages might contain something dangerous, I told myself. Names, dates, another secret. They should be kept intact for the record. Commissioner Oduya in the capital had asked for everything relating to the case; it would be irresponsible to—

It would be irresponsible. Eleven years of hiding behind that word like behind a cargo crate.

The truth was simpler and much heavier: those pages were from my father, written to me, and my father was dead, and as long as I did not read them, some part of me could go on pretending there was still one thing left to say between us. One conversation held in reserve. The moment I read them, the account closed forever.

I had wept precisely once since his drowning—that is to say, never—and I had no intention of starting over a letter.

* * *

Marta knocked at my door with the particular knock she used when she intended to come in regardless of my answer, which was all her knocks.

"You are sitting in the dark," she said, setting down a covered dish that smelled of fish stew and, beneath the fish stew, forgiveness. She had moved slowly back into the world after Hargrove's jailers released her—thin, quiet, flinching at boot-heels on cobblestones—but she cooked the way she had always cooked, as if feeding someone were the same as telling them they existed. "And you have been sitting in the dark for three days, Wren Sterling, because I can see your lamp oil hasn't moved."

"I've been working."

"On what? Your accounts?" She lifted the cover, sniffed it, made a face at her own cooking, and re-covered it. Marta judged her stews harshly; everyone else judged them the finest in the harbor district. "You don't work in the dark. You told me once you learned young that a person bent over papers in shadow looks like she's doing something wrong."

She knew me too well. That was the trouble with letting people in.

"Marta." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Did you know my mother?"

She went still, the way water goes still before a stone reaches bottom. Then she sat down across from me without asking, which answered more than any word could have.

"Your mother pulled me out of the surf when I was sixteen and stupid about a boy," she said. "I owed her my life and never paid it, because she drowned two years later, and then your father drowned, and then—" she waved a hand at the last year, at trials and tribunals and governors fleeing to flagships, "—then all of this. So." She looked at the letter in my lap as if she could see through the paper. "You finally going to open it?"

"It's open. It's been open since March."

"The whole thing?"

"The parts that matter."

"Wren." She said my name the way she salted fish—firmly, generously, without apology. "A man writes a letter knowing he's diving waters that killed better swimmers than him, and he puts something under the evidence for his daughter, and his daughter reads half and calls it done?" She shook her head. "In my experience, the part people hide underneath is always the part they meant most."

"I know."

"And you're afraid."

"I'm not afraid of a piece of paper, Marta."

"No," she agreed, standing, tucking the dish closer to my elbow so I couldn't pretend not to eat. "You're afraid of what you'll do after. That's different, and it's worse, and every woman in this harbor has stood where you're standing." At the door she paused. "He waited eleven years for you to read it. He'd wait eleven more. But you don't have to make him."

Then she was gone, boots on the stairs, the stall below rattling into its evening shut-down, and I was alone with the lamp and the letter and the long silence of the dead.

* * *

I want to record honestly what happened next, because someday—if Finch has his way and I let him put it in his absurd archive—someone may read this account and imagine I did something brave. I did not do anything brave. I sat for nearly an hour with my thumb under the fold of the second page, lifting it and putting it down, lifting and putting it down, like a girl checking whether the bathwater is ready. The lamp guttered. The stew went cold. Somewhere below, Marta dropped something heavy and swore with impressive range.

What finally moved my hand was not courage. It was arithmetic—which is fitting, since arithmetic is what saved me all those years at Meridian, tally by tally, invisible by invisible.

My father died on a Tuesday. He wrote this letter on the Monday before—I know because he dated everything, even birthday jokes, even the fish drawings, a habit from the ledgers. Which means he wrote these words in the last day of his life. And I suddenly understood, with the terrible clarity of numbers adding up, that whatever else that Tuesday took from me, it could not take this. These pages already existed. His last full day had already contained them. There was nothing in the sea or on land that could unwrite them.

All I could do by not reading was miss him twice.

I unfolded the page.

*My girl,*

*If you are reading this, then Elias kept his promise, which I never doubted, and you got yourself to him, which I also never doubted, though I expect the getting was harder than either of us would have liked. Your uncle Elias is a good man who worries too much and drinks just enough. Listen to him about tides. Ignore him about card games.*

*I am writing this the night before the Marlin dive, and I will tell you plainly why I'm going, because you'll hear ugly versions later and I want you to have the true one. There is a ship coming through the outer channel that isn't carrying what her manifest says she's carrying. I helped log her cargo last spring, thinking it was cotton, and I have since learned otherwise, and I have put what I know somewhere safe. Not in this letter. This letter is only the key, and keys are no good if the lock is found first.*

I stopped there. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Below the window a cat fought over fish heads, and the sound was so ordinary it felt like blasphemy.

*You will be angry with me,* the letter went on, and I laughed out loud, alone, in the dark, because he knew me exactly. *You will be angry because you will say I should have stayed in the boats and kept my mouth shut and my head down, the way I taught you. And you're right, and I'm sorry, and I would do it again anyway. Because here is the thing I taught you wrong, Oscar, or taught you right and lived wrong, which amounts to the same crime.*

*"Be unremarkable and you'll be safe." That's what I told you. It worked for me twenty years and it failed me the moment it mattered. A man who makes himself small enough to be safe becomes small enough to be used. They used me. They thought I'd stay quiet because staying quiet was my whole religion. I chose instead to be worth something, once, at the end. I don't recommend the timing. I recommend choosing sooner.*

I had to set the page down. Not because I couldn't go on, but because a sentence had gone off inside me like a signal flare, lighting up a landscape I'd spent eleven years walking in the dark.

*A man who makes himself small enough to be safe becomes small enough to be used.*

Eleven years of lowered eyes and busy hands. Eleven years of being furniture, of being weather, of being nobody—and I had called it survival, and it had been survival, and I would not let anyone, not even him, not even myself, take that away from the girl I'd been. She had done what she had to do. But God, to hear him say the other thing. To hear him say it out loud, on paper, in ink that had dried while he was still alive: *choose sooner.*

*Now the part that's only yours,* the second page began, in a looser hand, the hand of a man writing fast near the end of his candle. *The rest of this is evidence and belongs to the world. What follows belongs to my daughter.*

*First: I am proud of you, and I have been too proud to say it often, which is a fault of mine you should not inherit. You count faster than I ever did. You hold your temper better. You dive deeper—in every way, Oscar, in every way.*

*Second: whatever they tell you about me after—and they will tell you things, men like Crane keep their stories tidy—remember that I was not a hero and did not want to be one. I wanted to buy you a house above the tide line and grow old watching you argue with shopkeepers. Heroes die young. I would rather have been old.*

*Third: forgive yourself before you need to. Whatever happens because of this letter—and I pray it is less than I fear—you will be tempted to believe you carried it badly, or too late, or into the wrong hands. You won't have carried it badly. You'll have carried it the way it could be carried, by the person I trusted most in the world. Forgive yourself first and let the world catch up.*

*Fourth: live loudly.*

I stopped. The word sat on the page like a struck bell.

*I mean it,* he had written, as if hearing my hesitation across eleven years of water. *I know I taught you quiet. Take the quiet as a loan, not a gift. Sing in the morning where the neighbors can hear. Buy the good coat. Argue with shopkeepers, as practice. Love somebody inconveniently. When they offer you the restored name, the respectable name, the name that comes with conditions—ask yourself whose name it really is. Sterling blood runs loud, my girl. Your grandmother sang in three languages and got thrown out of two churches for it. I only muffled you because the times were bad. Unmuffle.*

*Last thing. If I'm wrong about all of this, if I surface Tuesday and laugh at my own fears and burn this letter—you'll never know, and that's fine. But if you're reading it, then know this above everything: there is nothing you could ever do, no mistake, no silence, no failure, that would make me anything but glad, every single morning of my working life, that I got up and you were mine.*

*Your papa,*
*who counted pearls and got rich anyway*

I read the signature four times. Then I read the fourth item again. Then I understood what he had hidden in plain sight, because my father loved a puzzle the way other men loved drink, and the last line of the evidence section—the dry, official close of the bill of lading copy, the line I must have read fifty times and skimmed past—was not official language at all:

*Counted: 214 crates. Counted truly. —J. Sterling, for my Pearl.*

For my Pearl.

Not the cargo. Me. He had signed the whole confession—the whole weapon that would eventually bring down a governor—with my pet name buried in it, the name he used when he tucked the fish-drawings into my shoes, the name no clerk or magistrate or census man would ever think to notice, because it looked like pearl-merchandise talk. He had hidden me inside the evidence the way he had hidden the evidence inside a letter of love. Everything he wrote was both things at once. Everything he wrote was both things at once.

And I wept.

I am not going to describe it prettily, because it was not pretty. It was eleven years late and it came up from somewhere below the lungs, and I slid off the chair onto the floorboards of my own rooms and cried the way children cry, without technique or dignity, for my father who dove on a Tuesday, for my mother before him, for the gray girl at the tally desk who had mistaken numbness for peace, for Marta's broken months and Elias's chained ones, for every crate of cotton that was never cotton. I cried until the lamp burned low and the cold stew sat untouched and the cat outside gave up on the fish heads and went home.

Grief, when it finally arrived, turned out not to be a wave. It was the tide itself—all of it at once, the whole sea, come home at last to a harbor that had barred its doors.

Somewhere in the middle of it I laughed too, which frightened the neighbors more, I think. Because of the card games. *Ignore him about card games.* Elias had lost four months' wages to Silas at cards during the blockade and sworn on his mother's grave that my father had taught him everything he knew. Papa had known. He had known from the beginning and put it in the letter, a joke with a nine-year fuse, timed to go off in a dark room years after his death, guaranteed to make his daughter laugh while crying. That was so exactly like him that I had to put my fist against my mouth to bear it.

When there was nothing left in me, I lay on the floorboards and listened to the harbor breathing—rigging ticking, water slapping pilings, the far bell of the point buoy—and I felt lighter in a way that had nothing to do with relief and everything to do with weight actually set down. The letter was finished now. Read entire. The account between us closed—not empty, but complete, the way a well-told story is complete.

He wanted me loud. Well. The tribunal steps had been loud. The trial, the manifest read aloud, Finch's testimony with his mad glorious archives—loud, loud, loud. I had thought I did all that for justice. It occurred to me now, lying in the dark, that I might also have been doing it, all along, for an audience of one, hoping somehow the news would reach wherever drowned divers go.

The news had reached me instead, by longer route, by slower boat. Better late.

I slept that night without dreaming, which had not happened since before March.

* * *

Morning brought Marta's stew reheated and eaten to the bottom, and with it, apparently, a new administration of myself. I bought the good coat. I am recording this so there is a witness: a coat of deep blue wool with brass buttons, from the tailor on Cooper's Lane, and when he asked if I was sure about the price I said yes, loudly, and argued him down ten percent anyway, as practice.

By noon I was at the harbor wall with my salvage license—my license, with my name printed on it, Wren Sterling, in government ink, no conditions attached—reviewing charts of the beds where I meant to start training the divers' children next month. Little Tomas Okonkwo first, whose father died in the Meridian collapse and who watches the water the way I used to watch ledgers: like it owed him something. I intend to teach him that it doesn't. That it gives, if you ask properly.

Elias found me there, cap in hand, squinting against the light like a man not yet convinced the sun had been fully pardoned along with him. He had that particular stiffness of a person approaching to say something kind and dreading it.

"You look different," he said. "Restless. Like the week before a storm."

"I read the rest of the letter."

"Ah." He looked out at the water for a long moment. "He mention the card games?"

"He told me never to trust you at cards."

"That," said Captain Elias Vane, with enormous dignity, "is slander from beyond the grave, and I will have it struck from the record." But his eyes were wet, and he put his hand briefly on my shoulder—heavy as an anchor, warm as a stove—and left it there while the gulls argued overhead and the harbor traffic shouted and the century gathered itself toward midnight.

"There's a thing," he said at last, awkwardly, staring hard at the horizon. "The crew. The ones who stood by me, and the dock folk, and your fishwife, and—God help us—the counting man. I've been thinking they should eat a meal together. Before winter. Something proper, with tables." He cleared his throat. "Your rooms above the stall are too small. Mine are too grim. So I've been looking at the old pilot house on Harrow Street, the one with the long room upstairs, and I thought perhaps—"

"Elias."

"—it's nothing, it's an idea, if it's foolish—"

"Ask them for Sunday," I said. "Ask all of them for Sunday."

He exhaled like a man surfacing. And that is how, with the letter finished and the good coat bought and the harbor turning gold around us, I came to spend the following week doing the loudest thing I have ever done in my life:

writing invitations.
