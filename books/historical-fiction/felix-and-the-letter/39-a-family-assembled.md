## 39. A Family Assembled

The release papers arrived on a Tuesday, which felt fitting. Tuesdays had always been the grayest day at Meridian House—the day the ledgers were audited, the day the porridge was thinnest—and it seemed right that the company that had owned eleven years of her life should give those years back on its most joyless morning.

Oscar read the document three times before she understood what she was looking at. *Be it known that Oscar Sterling, indentured tally clerk, is hereby released from all obligations to the Meridian Trading Company, its heirs and assigns...* The new administration's seal sat crooked in the corner, stamped by some clerk in a hurry, and beneath it waited a line for her signature and another for a witness.

She had imagined this moment so many times—in the dormitory dark, in the ledger room, in the water above her father's reef—that when it actually came, it felt almost ordinary. Just paper. Just ink. She dipped her pen and wrote her name with the same careful strokes she used for tallying cargo, then laughed at herself for it, and wrote it again, larger, in the margin, where no one could object.

"There," she said to the empty office, to the harbor light through the window, to her father wherever he was. "There."

She needed a witness. And there was really only one person she wanted holding the pen beside her.

* * *

Elias Vane came to the registry office at noon looking like a man who'd been dragged to church by his collar. He wore his good coat—the one with the brass buttons he only polished for funerals and tribunals—and he stood in the doorway of the little office as though he expected someone to arrest him for loitering.

"You're certain about this," he said. It was not quite a question.

"Elias. It's a signature."

"A signature is never just a signature." But he came in, and he took the pen, and he signed beneath her name with the flourish of a man who had signed articles of salvage and letters of marque and one very memorable surrender that had turned out not to be a surrender at all. "Oscar Sterling," he read aloud, squinting at her handwriting. "Freed woman of the port." He set down the pen and cleared his throat and looked out the window at the masts along the quay. "Your father would have—"

He stopped. He did that often lately, Elias did; started sentences that ended somewhere he couldn't follow them yet.

"He'd have signed it bigger," Oscar said.

"He'd have signed it drunk," Elias said, "and wept after, and pretended he hadn't." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "But yes. Bigger."

They stood a moment in the quiet, two people who had both loved the same drowned man and never quite learned how to say so.

"There's Sunday dinner," Oscar said, studying the crooked seal very hard. "Tomorrow. My lodgings. Marta's bringing fish, Silas claims he can make bread, and Finch is coming."

"Finch." Elias said the name the way other men said *reef* or *squall*. "The counting man."

"He saved my life. He saved yours."

"He counts his peas at table. I've seen him do it. Lines them up on the edge of the plate."

"That's why you're invited," Oscar said. "So somebody can watch him and tell me about it afterward."

Elias snorted—a great, unguarded sound, like a ship taking water—and for a moment he looked twenty years younger and thirty miles from any tribunal. Then he gathered himself back into captain's gravity, tugged his coat straight, and said, "I'll bring a cask of something. A man doesn't come to dinner empty-handed to a free woman's table."

"It's a lodging house, not a table."

"Then I'll bring enough for the whole house." He paused at the door, hand on the frame. "Eleven o'clock tomorrow, I'll be at your father's grave. If you wanted—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

"Ten-thirty," Oscar said. "I'll bring the good rum this time."

* * *

Silas arrived early on Sunday, carrying flour and terror in equal measure.

"I've made bread perhaps four times," he confessed from the doorway, ink still faintly staining the creases of his knuckles even now, months away from the forge-work of forgery, working honest presses these days for a newspaper that paid badly and printed bravely. "In the hold of a brig. Under fire conditions. The results were described as 'edible' by a man who'd been eating ship's biscuit for a year, so the bar was low."

"Then we're agreed," Oscar said, handing him her mother's apron, which was too short on him and made him look like a very tall, very worried child. "You'll make the bread and I'll lie about how good it is."

"That's the arrangement every baker's wife has had since bread was invented."

He worked with the same concentration she remembered from those nights in the hold—lamp low, sleeves rolled, hands steady over the delicate work. There was flour in his dark hair within ten minutes. There was flour everywhere within twenty. When he wasn't looking, Oscar found herself watching his hands and thinking things she wasn't ready to say out loud, and when he *was* looking, she found herself very interested in the fire.

"Your landlady's cat likes me," Silas observed, as the enormous orange brute installed itself across his shoulders like a stole.

"The cat has no taste. He also liked the debt collector who lived here in spring."

"Well, then we know where I stand." But he smiled down into the bowl, and the cat purred, and the flat filled up with the smell of yeast and warmth, and Oscar thought: *this*. This was what her father's letter had given her permission for. Not the evidence, not the fall of Crane—those were for history. This was for her.

Marta arrived next, in her stall-day best, carrying a whole baked fish and opinions about the government.

"They put a *new* harbormaster," she announced, setting the dish down with the authority of a woman laying siege. "Younger than the last one. Same face, though. They must grow them in a barrel somewhere, like eels." She kissed Oscar on both cheeks, held her at arm's length, and studied her the way she'd once studied fish for freshness. "You're sleeping better."

"I am."

"You're eating more."

"Marta—"

"Good. Sit. Both of you. The girl is free now and she'll be fed accordingly." Marta had come through her own imprisonment thinner and quieter, with a tremor in her hands on cold mornings that she never mentioned and everyone politely never failed to notice. But her voice was strong again, her stall had reopened to a line of customers who bought from her now as much out of loyalty as hunger, and if she counted the exits when she entered a room—which she did, and Oscar knew she did—she no longer flinched at the counting.

The knock that came at four o'clock was punctual to the point of aggression.

"Gentlemen," Oscar said, opening the door to Elias and Aldous Finch, who stood side by side on the landing radiating mutual discomfort like two cats in a sack. Elias carried a cask of rum under one arm. Finch carried a wrapped parcel and an expression suggesting he had catalogued seventeen structural flaws in the staircase on his way up.

"Captain," Oscar said warmly.

"Miss Sterling." Elias handed over the cask.

"Mr. Finch."

"Miss Sterling." Finch held out the parcel stiffly, as if presenting evidence. Inside was a book—an almanac of tides and tables, its margins dense with Finch's tiny, upright annotations. "I observed that you keep your own tide records. Yours are better organized than the harbor office's, which is not saying much, but these tables will improve them further. I have noted the errata. All of them."

"Thank you, Mr. Finch."

"I also brought butter," he added, producing it from his coat with the air of a man discharging a solemn duty. "Because one does."

Dinner itself was, by any objective measure, chaos.

Finch arranged his plate precisely as Elias had prophesied—peas in regimented rows, fish portioned into equal geometric segments—and when Elias, watching him with unconcealed fascination, moved his own potatoes a half-inch to the left purely to observe the reaction, Finch went pale and said, "That will trouble me through Thursday, Captain, thank you."

"Thursday? Why not tonight?"

"Tonight I have already accepted the potatoes as they are. Tomorrow the memory will reorganize itself incorrectly. By Thursday it will be intolerable." He said this as though explaining rain to a child.

Silas laughed so hard he had to leave the table, and returned having recovered, except that whenever he caught Oscar's eye he began again.

Marta told the story of the harbormaster's barrel-grown face until even Finch smiled, which Oscar suspected was a historic event witnessed by few living persons. Elias drank two glasses of rum and told a story about her father that Oscar had never heard—the time Josiah Sterling had won a diving bet against three mainlanders by staying down past sense, surfacing with a pearl the size of a thumbnail and immediately dropping it down the front of his shirt pretending he hadn't.

"He cheated," Elias said.

"He always said he didn't."

"He had an iron weight sewn in his boot cuff. Your mother found out and didn't speak to him for a week, and he let everyone believe he'd won fair rather than admit his wife was cleverer than him." Elias turned his glass slowly on the table. "He was the finest liar I ever sailed with, except about the things that mattered. About those, he couldn't lie at all. It got him drowned and it got us hanged—" he caught himself, "—it nearly got us hanged. But it's why the letter existed. He couldn't carry that secret and stay the man your mother married."

The table went quiet in the good way, the full way. Outside, gulls argued over the evening tide. The cat had claimed Finch's lap, to Finch's visible alarm and secret pride.

"I count people," Finch said abruptly, to no one, stroking the cat with one rigid finger as though it might be a form he had to fill out correctly. "It's my profession. Four at this table. One cat, though he resists classification. And it occurs to me—I have attended many dinners in this city, in thirty-one years of counting its inhabitants, and I have never once been invited to one." He looked up, briefly, almost fiercely. "This is statistically irregular. I intend to note it as such. In my private archive. Under 'corrections.'"

"Mr. Finch," Oscar said, "you're invited every Sunday. Indefinitely. Consider yourself a permanent irregularity."

"Every Sunday," Finch repeated, testing the weight of it. "I shall require the fourth chair. The third wobbles. I measured."

"The fourth chair is yours."

* * *

Later, when Marta had gone home and Finch had departed at precisely nine o'clock ("Sunday concludes; good night; the fish was satisfactory"), when Silas was washing dishes with his sleeves rolled up and Elias stood at the window watching the harbor lights come on one by one across the black water, Oscar took out the release papers and looked at her name again.

"You could sail," Elias said quietly, without turning. "Fairwind needs a tally master. Free papers, free berth. The sea doesn't ask where your father was born."

"I know."

"Or stay ashore. Salvage license, they say, is yours for the asking now—the new office owes you. You could work the beds. Your father's beds."

"I know that too." She set the papers down. "I'm going to do the second thing. And eventually, maybe, the first, when the beds play out. But not yet." She joined him at the window. Down on the quay, lamplighters moved along their rounds, small flames blooming in a row. "Papa spent his whole life underwater so I could stand on dry land. Seems ungrateful to spend mine the same way."

Elias nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed some calculation of his own. "Then Fairwind will run coastal routes out of this harbor. Home port." He said it gruffly, staring hard at the lights. "A captain ought to be able to see his family's windows from the quay. That's all. Practical matter."

"Enormously practical."

"Shut up, girl." But he put his arm around her shoulders, brief and fierce as a wave, and was embarrassed about it immediately, and she let him be.

At the sink, Silas had stopped washing and was holding her mother's chipped blue bowl up to the lamp, examining a hairline crack along its rim.

"This should be mended," he said. "Properly. Kintsugi-style—I read about it in the city. Gold in the seam. So the breaking becomes part of the pattern instead of something hidden."

"It's just a bowl," Oscar said.

"No," Silas said, looking at her over the rim of it, "it isn't, quite. Neither are you. Neither am I. Neither is anyone at this table." He set it down gently. "I'm staying ashore, you know. Took a room above the print shop. Thought you should hear it from me before you hear it from Finch's archive."

"I heard it from Finch's archive last Tuesday," Oscar said. "He's been keeping a file."

"Of course he has." Silas dried his hands and came to stand beside her, close enough that she could smell lamp oil and printer's ink and the ghost of flour. "So. A freed woman with a salvage license and a window over the harbor. And a printer with a garret two streets away." He hesitated, in the old hold-under-fire way of his, hands steady while everything behind his eyes negotiated terms. "I thought perhaps. Sundays. And possibly days that aren't Sunday."

"Days that aren't Sunday," Oscar agreed, and discovered she was smiling, and did not lower her eyes, and did not make herself small, and did not care who saw.

From the window, Elias made a noise like a man suppressing commentary, and wisely suppressed it.

* * *

They left within the hour—Elias walking Finch home because "someone has to make sure he doesn't stop to count lampposts," Silas lingering in the doorway with the cracked bowl tucked under his arm and a promise of gold leaf by spring.

Alone, Oscar banked the fire and sat at her little desk by the window, where her father's letter now lived framed under glass, its private pages worn soft from reading. On the desk lay the release papers, her name drying in careful ink, and beside them, the tide almanac with Finch's furious marginalia, already open to next week.

Through the glass, the harbor breathed—black water, riding lights, the far bell buoy marking the channel over her father's reef. Somewhere out there, the beds lay waiting, patient as they'd been for eleven years. Somewhere out there, the century was turning toward midnight, whether the world was ready or not.

Oscar picked up her pen, pulled a fresh sheet toward her, and wrote at the top of it, in letters large enough that no one could object:

*Oscar Sterling — Salvager.*

Below it, after a moment's thought, smaller:

*Est. the year everything changed.*

Down on the quay, a lamplighter reached the end of his round and paused, looking up at her lit window—one small flame among many, seen and seeing. Oscar lifted her hand in greeting, and the lamplighter, after a startled beat, lifted his.

She was still smiling when she blew out the lamp.
