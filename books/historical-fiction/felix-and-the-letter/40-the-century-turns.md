## 40. The Century Turns

The harbor wall smelled of salt and gunpowder and roasting chestnuts, and Oscar Sterling stood on it in the last hour of the old century with her father's diving knife at her belt and a paper cup of hot cider going cold in her hands.

Below her, the water lay black and patient, the same water that had taken Papa on an ordinary Tuesday nineteen years ago. Above her, the sky was holding its breath. Somewhere out past the point, the fireworks barge was anchored—the harbor master's idea, the new administration's money, everyone's festival now that there was no governor to skim the powder budget and no company to tax the light itself.

"Oscar!" Marta's voice carried over the crowd the way only a fishwife's voice can—designed by God and decades of practice to part through noise like a knife through mackerel. "Your cider's gone cold again. Honestly, girl, you'd lose your own head if it weren't attached."

"I nearly did once," Oscar called back, and Marta laughed the big unguarded laugh that had come back to her slowly over the past year, like spring coming to a cold garden—not all at once, but then all at once.

Marta pushed through the crowd with the ease of a ship through kelp, hauling two children behind her by their sleeves. They were not her grandchildren, strictly speaking—they belonged to the widow Perón from the fish stalls—but Marta had never let a technicality stand between herself and someone who needed feeding. The boy, Tomas, was seven and fearless; his sister Lila was nine and cautious, which in Oscar's professional opinion made her the better diver of the two.

"Are you nervous?" Lila asked, looking up at Oscar with the grave seriousness of a child who has been told she is being trained for something important.

"About what?"

"Tomorrow. First class. You said we could come watch you teach."

Oscar crouched down so they were eye to eye—a habit she'd learned from Elias, who believed that people deserved to be looked at straight on, especially small ones. "Terrified," she said. "Completely terrified. What if I've forgotten everything? What if I try to show them how to clear a mask underwater and I drown instead? What will the papers say? 'The celebrated salvage mistress Sterling perishes in four feet of water at the shallows.'"

Tomas giggled. Lila considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "You wouldn't drown," she decided at last. "You're too stubborn."

"There it is," Marta said. "The girl has your number, Oscaria."

*Oscaria.* Only Marta still used the name Mama had given her before Papa's sea-fearing practicality had worn it down to just *Oscar*, short and gray and easy to say without anyone asking questions. For years Oscar had corrected people who tried to use the long name. These days she let it stand. It fit better than it used to, the way an old coat does after you stop trying to be a different shape in it.

The first rocket went up without warning—a red starburst over the point that turned every upturned face in the crowd briefly pink—and Tomas shrieked with delight, and Lila grabbed Oscar's hand without thinking about it, and Oscar held on.

She had spent so many years learning not to be touched. It was strange, how quickly the body unlearned it when given half a chance.

* * *

The letter hung framed above her desk at the salvage office, and she passed it every morning on her way to the ledgers.

Elias had insisted on the frame. She had wanted to keep the pages loose, in the oilskin packet where they'd lived for twenty years, but Elias had marched into the stationer's on Cordwainer Lane and ordered walnut and glass with the same authority he'd once used to quell a near-mutiny, and paid for it before she could argue.

"A document that toppled a governor doesn't belong in a drawer," he'd said. "It belongs where you have to look at it. That's the whole point of surviving things, girl. Otherwise you might as well have drowned."

So there it was every morning: Papa's careful hand, the ink gone brown as old tea. The top third of the letter was the bill of lading—Crane's signature, the false manifest numbers, the arms shipments hidden under Meridian's flag—that had ended one empire and very nearly hanged three innocent people. But beneath the evidence, in the lower pages, were the words meant only for her. She had read them on a spring morning with the window open and Elias sitting awkwardly in her kitchen pretending not to watch, and she had wept for the first time since the day the sea gave her back nothing but his boots.

*My Oscaria,* the letter began. *If you are reading this, then either my blood has finally cost us something, or you went digging where I told you not to, which means you are mine past all doubt.*

*I watched you learn to hold your breath in the shallows when you were five and I thought: this one will not be able to make herself small, not really, not ever, however hard she tries. Water finds its level. So do Sterling girls.*

*Forgive me for teaching you to hide. I thought I was teaching you to survive. If the day comes that you must be seen, know that I always saw you, and I was proud the whole time.*

She read it most mornings. Some mornings she only touched the glass, the way sailors touch the masthead for luck, and got on with the day.

* * *

The salvage office occupied the ground floor of what had once been a Meridian counting house—which pleased her obscurely, the way turning a jail into a schoolhouse pleases people. There was a drying room in the back, a rack of dive helmets that Silas kept in better repair than some ships' engines, and a wall chart of the pearl beds that Finch had annotated, unprompted and unasked, with depth soundings he had extracted from forty-year-old tax records because, as he put it, "the data existed; leaving it uncorrelated would have been a dereliction."

Finch came to Sunday dinner now. He brought his own chair. This was not a metaphor—he literally brought a folding chair, having concluded after one dinner at Elias's table that the seating arrangements were "suboptimal for conversation flow," and no amount of teasing had persuaded him to stop. He would unfold it at the head of the table, sit with his spine perfectly vertical, eat exactly what was offered while silently cataloguing the nutritional adequacy of each dish, and then, over tea, produce from his coat some small terrible gift: a correction to the tide tables, or the true birthdate of the baker's wife, or—once, memorably—the news that the harbormaster had been quietly embezzling mooring fees, which Finch had noticed in passing and reported the following Monday with the mild satisfaction of a man wiping a smudge from a window.

"You're a menace," Elias told him regularly.

"I am accurate," Finch replied, which was, in his cosmology, the highest praise available to any creature.

He had been the first person in the city to count Oscar Sterling—to see her, truly, wet-hemmed and lying badly about who she was—and he had never once let her forget it, mostly by never once mentioning it. That was Finch's particular grace: he remembered everything and weaponized almost none of it.

* * *

Silas found her at the wall as the fireworks gathered steam, gold and green now, reflections shivering on the water. He came up beside her the way he did everything—without announcing himself—and stood shoulder to shoulder, watching.

Their shoulders touched. Neither of them moved away. This had been the arrangement for a year now, comfortable as an old rope bridge: closeness approached, tested, permitted. Silas had ink stains on his fingers still, even though the printing trade was years behind him; some habits live in the skin. He smelled of machine oil and lampblack, and Oscar found, in a distant and dignified way, that she liked it.

"Kerr sent a card," Silas said. "From upcoast. He's got a berth on a grain ship. Wants to know if he can write to the captain."

"What did Elias say?"

"Said Kerr could write, but that letters go both ways, and Kerr should think about whether he wants to hear what comes back." Silas turned a coin over his knuckles—an old deckhand habit. "That's the captain's whole philosophy, more or less. Everything goes both ways."

Oscar thought about that. Thought about Hargrove in his cell, waiting on a tribunal that would probably give him transportation instead of the rope, because Crane's patrons had needed someone to feed to public anger and Hargrove had been, by unlucky arithmetic, the cheaper meal. Thought about Crane himself, retired to a country estate with a pension and a gate, galled and alive. The century was ending and justice was still a partial thing, a ledger with entries missing. She had made her peace with it the way you make peace with weather: not gladly, but thoroughly.

"Do you want to hear what came back?" she asked. "From me. If Kerr writes."

Silas considered. "I'd want to know he was sorry," he said. "And I don't think I'd believe it unless he didn't ask for anything." He shrugged. "Which is probably why the captain said it that way."

Above them a rocket screamed upward, hesitated at the top of its arc like a diver on the edge of a board, and burst into white. The crowd gasped as one animal. Somewhere behind them, Marta was telling Tomas the story of the raid on the Fairwind with dramatic embellishments that grew less accurate and more magnificent with each retelling, and Elias was pretending to correct her, and both of them knew he was only there for the pleasure of the argument.

"First class tomorrow," Silas said. "Nervous?"

"Everyone keeps asking me that."

"Because everyone can see you are." He smiled sideways at her. "What's the worst that happens?"

"Someone drowns. Someone's child. Because I taught them wrong, or taught them carelessly, or taught them to love the water the way Papa loved it, which took him." The words came out low and even, and surprised her a little by existing at all. She hadn't known that fear was in her until she'd agreed to take students. It had been waiting, patient, under the floorboards.

Silas was quiet for a moment. Then: "My father was a printer. Set type his whole life. Died when a press collapsed on him—did you know that? Right in the shop. And when I signed on to the Fairwind at fourteen, everybody told Elias he was mad to take the son of a man killed by the trade." The coin stilled in his fingers. "He said, 'The sea doesn't inherit grudges. And the boy gets to choose what he loves.' So." He looked at her. "Let them choose. You'll teach them right. Better than right—you'll teach them scared and doing it anyway, which is the only kind of brave worth the name."

Oscar looked at the water. Nineteen years of looking at this water, and it had finally stopped being only the place her father died and become also the place her father lived—down in the pearl beds, in the green light, in the manifest tucked into the reef like a message to a future he wouldn't see but believed in anyway.

"When did you get wise?" she asked.

"Ship's cook taught me. Said wisdom's just fear that's been put to work."

The clock above the customs house began to strike midnight, and the harbor answered—every whistle, every bell, every foghorn in the anchorage, and then the great final battery of fireworks, gold and rose and a deep astonishing blue, pouring light down onto the black water until the harbor looked, for one impossible minute, like the inside of an abalone shell.

The crowd cheered. Marta wept openly and denied it. Finch checked his pocket watch against the customs house clock, found them in agreement to within two seconds, and was visibly comforted. Elias stood a little apart from all of them, hat in his hand, facing the sea, and Oscar went and stood beside him, and neither of them said anything, because between the two of them there had never been any need.

"He'd have liked this," Elias said at last. "All these fools making noise over his water."

"It's my water now," Oscar said.

"Yes," Elias said. "It is."

* * *

She stayed on the wall after the others drifted home—Marta herding children, Finch folding his chair with ceremony, Silas walking backward a few steps to make sure she was all right before the crowd took him. The smoke of the fireworks drifted seaward. The water settled back into its old darkness, its old patience.

Oscar took off her boots, the way she had done a thousand times as a girl, and sat with her feet dangling above the tide line, and let the cold bite her toes, and thought about the morning.

Tomorrow she would stand on the teaching platform at the shallows with six children in borrowed jerseys, and she would tell them her real name—Oscaria Sterling, salvager, daughter of a drowned man and a woman who named her loud—and she would teach them to breathe past the fear and drop below the surface of things. Some of them would be frightened. All of them would pretend not to be. And she would tell them what Papa's letter told her: that hiding is a skill, and seeing is a skill, and the second one is worth ten of the first.

For years she had believed invisibility was safety, and survival meant staying small. She had been wrong in the way that only costs everything once. Safety, it turned out, was a wall full of people who knew your name. Freedom was standing in plain sight on the last night of an old century, feet in the cold, name spoken aloud, letter hanging in a walnut frame above her desk where anyone who came into her office could read it—and reading it, understand exactly whose daughter she was.

She pulled her boots back on and stood, and the city lights wavered on the harbor, and somewhere below, in the green dark, the pearl beds waited with their slow, secret patience, keeping what they keep.

Oscar Sterling—Oscaria, salvager, teacher, seen—walked home along the wall with her face to the wind, visible to anyone who cared to look, and found she hoped, with her whole stubborn heart, that they would.
