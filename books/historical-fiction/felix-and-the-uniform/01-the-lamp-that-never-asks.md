## 1. The Lamp That Never Asks

Casper Nakamura had learned to tell the weather from the sound the tower made.

Most people thought a lighthouse was silent, or that its only voice was the lamp's great slow wink, but they were wrong. The tower spoke all night if you knew how to listen — a low hum in iron when the wind came off open water, a thinner note when it swung round to the northeast, and when a true storm gathered out beyond the shoals, a shudder in the masonry that traveled down through the stairs and into the soles of his boots like something alive turning over in its sleep.

On the last day of October, 1918, the tower was humming.

He noted it at four in the afternoon, standing in the gallery with his coat buttoned to the throat and the wind worrying the hem of it. The horizon had gone the color of old pewter, and the sea beneath it had begun to move in that long organized swell that meant weather still hundreds of miles out but coming on with purpose. He wrote in the log: *Wind shifting NE. Swell increasing. Barometer falling.* His handwriting came out small and even, because he made it come out small and even; there was no other way he permitted himself to write anymore.

Then he went down the ninety-three steps — he had counted them once, in another life, when counting things had been Maren's game rather than his discipline — and lit the stove, and made tea he would let go cold, and checked the stores list against what remained in the cellar.

The *Petrel* was due Thursday. It was now Saturday.

That was not yet alarming. The supply boat ran when it could and stayed in harbor when it couldn't, and no signal had been arranged for missed deliveries because none had ever been needed in eleven years. But Casper stood in front of the open cellar door doing arithmetic anyway: flour enough for three weeks if he stretched it. Lamp oil for six. Coal for five, maybe less if the winter meant to be cruel. Salt fish, potatoes, a jar of pickled onions Maren had put up the summer before she died that he had never had the heart to finish.

He did not count the days of anything else. There were no other days to count.

*History happens elsewhere,* he told himself, which was not quite a creed and not quite a prayer but had settled into the place where both usually lived. Wars happened in France, in Flanders, in the newspaper the post boat brought folded into quarters until the ink wore off at the creases. Kings fell and empires staggered and young men drowned in mud two thousand miles south of here, and the sea kept its own counsel, and Casper kept the lamp, and neither of them asked questions. That was the arrangement. The lamp asked nothing of him but oil and trimming at dusk and midnight and dawn. The sea asked nothing but that he watch it. In exchange they left him alone, which after Maren was the only currency he valued.

He had been a man who signed things once. Contracts, letters, the marriage register with his hand shaking too much to make the letters straight. Now the only thing he signed was the log, and even that he thought of less as writing than as weather reporting — impersonal, verifiable, belonging to no one.

At dusk he climbed the tower again. This was the part of the day he would have called the whole point of himself, if anyone had asked, which no one had in years. Weighting the clockwork, winding it until the spring resisted, trimming the wick so the flame sat exactly where the lens wanted it. The great light caught and turned and threw itself seaward, one flash each eight seconds, patient as a heartbeat. Somewhere past the shoals, in the dark coming on, boats would read it and steer their lives by it without ever knowing his name.

That suited him fine. He had spent eleven years becoming unreadable.

* * *

Maren had liked storms.

That was the thing about grief nobody warned you: it didn't keep the bad memories safe and release the good ones. It kept everything, indiscriminately, and dealt it back out at random hours. So it was the good memories that ambushed him — Maren on the gallery steps with her hair whipping loose, laughing at the thunder like it had told a joke; Maren cataloguing shells in a ledger she'd ruled herself, one column for the shell and one for the date found, as if the sea's small leavings deserved the same record-keeping as the weather; Maren saying, once, watching him trim the wick, *You know what I like about you, Casper? You're a man who keeps a light and stays out of the story.*

She'd meant it kindly. He had built a house on it.

He thought of it now because the storm was coming and she would have been at the window already, delighted. Instead the keeper's cottage held one man, one chair pulled up to the stove, one plate, one cup. He ate his stretched ration of salt fish and potatoes without tasting them, which was not a hardship anymore but simply how eating worked. Afterward he took down the book he was reading — a battered volume on tidal mechanics, chosen years ago precisely because nothing in it could ever happen to anyone — and got through four pages before he realized the tower's hum had changed pitch.

Higher. Tighter. The sound of something leaning into the coast.

He went out onto the shingle at ten o'clock, though there was nothing to see. The dark was total except when the lamp above swept its arm across the world and showed him the sea for an instant — black, confused, already white-capped close in — then took the world away again. The wind had teeth in it now. Off the water came a smell he knew and disliked: the green mineral stink of a sea being stirred from the bottom up. Not a gale yet. A promise of one, delivered early.

Storm season wasn't due for another fortnight. The barometer had told him that morning; the sea was confirming it now, rudely, ahead of schedule.

"Early this year," he said aloud, to no one, because eleven years of solitude had taught him that a voice in the dark was better than none, even his own.

Back inside, he wrote in the log: *Barometer falling fast. Sea rising. Supply boat overdue three days. Will hold lamp to standard watch rotation regardless.*

His hand did not shake. He looked at it a moment anyway, remembering that it used to.

* * *

Sunday the wind arrived in earnest.

It came in the night like an invading army, and Casper woke not to the sound but to the silence after a gust, the held breath between blows, which was always worse. By lamplight he could see sand and spray ticking against the kitchen window like fingers testing the glass. He dressed, went up, and spent the morning in the lamp room keeping the mechanism fed and the glass wiped clear of salt, because a storm's first act is to blind you and a keeper's first job is not to be blinded.

The sea was magnificent and terrible and entirely indifferent to him. That was the honest bargain of his life and he renewed it gladly, hour by hour, wiping glass while the world outside tried to tear itself loose from the coast. Waves came in off the shoals the height of the cottage roof and detonated on the rocks below with a concussion he felt in his chest. Spray went over the lantern itself, sixty feet up, and rattled down the dome like thrown gravel.

Somewhere out in that, he knew, were boats. Fishing boats that should have run for harbor already; maybe the *Petrel*, beating north late with her cargo, choosing between running before the storm and sheltering behind the headland. The lamp flashed its eight-second sentence out at them all: *Here. Here is the rock. Come no closer. Steer by me.*

He had done this through a hundred storms and would do it through a hundred more, and there was a comfort in that arithmetic that nothing else in his life offered anymore. The war had taken Anton and half the village's sons and any number of things Casper preferred not to itemize; the storm would take what it took; and the lamp would burn regardless, because he made it burn. Of all the transactions available to a man in 1918, this was the only one he still trusted.

In the afternoon the rain turned horizontal and the wind found its full voice, and the tower sang its storm note continuously now, a held organ tone through the stone. Casper went down at four to check the cellar against flooding, and that was when he heard the sound over the wind that stopped him on the stairs with one hand on the rail.

A gun. Or so his body insisted for one full second — the flat crack rolling in from the east, and his shoulders climbing toward his ears with a readiness he hadn't known he still carried. Then his mind caught up: not a gun. The fog signal on the headland, two miles north, the automatic chime buoy or the coastguard's cannon, sounding because visibility had gone to nothing. He stood on the stairs and waited for his heartbeat to accept the correction.

Eleven years, and the war still lived somewhere in him, sending out patrols at odd hours. He resented it the way you resent an unpaid debt — not with surprise, but with tiredness.

By evening he allowed himself the calculation he'd been avoiding: the *Petrel* was now four days overdue in worsening weather, and no boat that size rode out a storm like this in open water. Either she'd sheltered somewhere down the coast, which happened and cost nothing, or she had not, which also happened and did not bear thinking about. Either way, the coal was now a five-day supply in a storm that might last three, and the flour was a nineteen-day supply, and Casper Nakamura, who had arranged his entire existence around the principle that the world would leave him alone if he left it alone, sat at his table with the stores ledger open and understood, dimly and unpleasantly, that the world had begun renegotiating.

He blew out the lamp, went up the tower one more time into the screaming dark, confirmed the light burning steady, and came back down.

* * *

Monday the storm broke something loose besides the sea.

It blew itself out through the small hours — not ended, but eased from fury into the long grumbling chase of a gale departing, and Casper slept two hours in a chair with his boots on and woke at gray dawn feeling like a man who had been wrung out. The world outside the windows lay scoured and dripping. Driftweed hung in the gorse twenty feet above the high-water line. And the beach — the long shingle strand below the tower that eleven years had taught him to read like a page — had been written on all night.

He walked it out of habit, collar up, hands in pockets, cataloguing without meaning to: kelp in ropes, a broken dory plank with paint he didn't recognize, half a crate staved to kindling, the usual sea-tithe. The gulls were loud and pleased with themselves. The light lay flat and silver, washed clean, innocent, as the sea always pretended to be afterward.

He was halfway back, thinking of coffee he didn't have and the coal count, when he saw the shape.

It lay at the tide line where the shingle gave onto sand, forty yards off, dark against the pale stones. Driftwood, he told himself. Kelp wrapped round a spar. But his legs had already changed course, because eleven years on this beach had taught his eyes things his mind refused to hurry, and the shape had the specific wrongness of something that had been alive.

Twenty yards, and it resolved: a person. A woman, face-down at the edge of the surf, one arm flung forward across the stones as if she'd been reaching for the shore even after reaching stopped being possible. Her clothes were dark wool, soaked through, torn at one shoulder. Beside her, half-buried in shingle, lay a satchel — an ugly, serviceable thing of oiled canvas with a strap, the kind of bag designed to survive things.

Casper knelt. The sea sucked at the stones around his boots. He put two fingers under the woman's jaw, braced for cold flesh —

— and found a pulse. Slow, threadlike, stubborn.

For a moment he simply stayed there, kneeling on his own beach in the rinsed morning light, holding the wrist of a stranger the sea had decided to return. Eleven years of arrangements looked him in the face: the lamp asks nothing, the sea asks nothing, history happens elsewhere. The creed held perfectly right up until a body washed ashore at your feet, and then it held for exactly as long as you refused to turn your head.

Her lips were gray-blue. Her breathing came shallow and irregular, with a wet catch at the bottom of each breath that he recognized without wanting to. She needed fire, dry clothes, warmth, and she needed them soon. None of that was negotiable. All of it was entanglement.

He looked at the satchel. Even half-buried, it had the look of something guarded — strap cinched, buckles turned inward, as though its owner had gone into the water determined to keep it with her, and whatever was inside mattered enough to drown for.

Casper Nakamura had a rule about other people's business, which was that it stayed other people's. He picked the woman up — she weighed less than the coal sacks he hauled up ninety-three steps every week — and carried her toward the cottage. At the door he set her down just inside, went back out into the wind, and retrieved the satchel from the tideline, because leaving it for the next wave felt like theft.

He told himself he would put it untouched by the stove until she woke, and that whatever happened after that was the sea's affair and not his.

The tower hummed above them both, and the lamp went on asking nothing, and the storm season, weeks early and carrying passengers, had arrived.
