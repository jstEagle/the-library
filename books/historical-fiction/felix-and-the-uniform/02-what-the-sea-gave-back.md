## 2. What the Sea Gave Back

The sea had been generous all week, and Casper Nakamura did not trust it.

For three mornings running the storm had thrown gifts up onto the shingle below the lighthouse: a ship's door, blue paint still bright on its panels; a crate of oranges gone soft and blooming with mold; a coil of rope worth saving; and once — he'd told no one this, because there was no one to tell — a child's boot, small and empty, which he had buried above the tide line without letting himself think about whose child. The sea gave back what men lost. That was its oldest habit. It never asked whether you wanted the things back.

On the fourth morning the wind came around to the northeast and turned ugly, and Casper went down to the shingle anyway, because that was the ritual: coffee at five, lamp out at dawn, log written, then boots and oilskins and the long walk down the path to see what the night had left him. Forty-one winters of the same walk. His knees knew each stone.

He saw her from thirty yards and thought at first she was kelp.

Then the kelp moved — a slow, wrong movement, an arm dragging itself forward through the shallows — and Casper was running before his mind caught up with his legs, slipping on weed-slick rock, cold air knifing into his chest. The storm had not given him a door or a crate this time. The storm had given him a woman.

She was face-down in six inches of surf, one hand clawed into the shingle as if the island itself might be pulled away from her. Her coat was heavy with water, dark wool plastered to her back. Casper got his hands under her arms and hauled, and she was heavier than she looked, waterlogged, dead weight — except her weight wasn't dead, because the moment he touched her her eyes opened and she struck him.

Not wildly. Precisely. A short blow across the bridge of his nose with the heel of her hand, hard enough to make his eyes flood, and then she was scrambling away from him on hands and knees, coughing seawater onto the stones, watching him with an expression he would spend days trying to name. It was not fear exactly. It was arithmetic. She was counting him — his size, his age, the distance to the path, whether his hands were worker's hands or soldier's hands — and she was doing it while her body still shook with cold.

"I'm not going to hurt you," Casper said. Blood was running into his mustache. He wiped it with the back of his glove and held the glove up, empty. "I live here. The light. Up there." He pointed. She followed his finger to the tower, white-streaked against the gray sky, and something in her posture changed by a degree. A lighthouse was a known thing. Keepers were known things.

"Cold," she said. One word. Her voice was rough as the shingle.

"Yes. You're half-drowned. Can you walk?"

She could, barely, and she refused his shoulder twice before accepting it on the third offer — not because she softened but because her legs simply stopped negotiating. Halfway up the path she made him stop, twisted out of his grip, and went back for something he hadn't even seen: a satchel, waterproofed rubber, strapped tight, half-buried in the tidewrack where she must have crawled to it first thing before collapsing. She dragged it against her chest the way other people carried infants. When Casper reached to help her with it she turned her whole body away, sharp and sudden, and nearly went down again.

"All right," he said. "It's yours."

She looked at him over the top of it. Her eyes were gray-green, the color of the sea on a day like this, and red-rimmed from salt, and they stayed on him a moment too long, filing something away.

* * *

In the kitchen of the keeper's cottage he did what Maren would have done, and hated how naturally his hands remembered it. Strip the wet wool. Blankets. Heat. He built the stove up hot while she stood dripping in the corner holding the satchel and refusing, by silent degrees, every instruction he gave. She would not sit until he sat first. She would not turn her back to the window. She drank the broth he set in front of her in fast, mechanical swallows, watching him over the bowl's rim the entire time, and when she finished she held the empty bowl in both hands as if she might need to keep it.

"Your nose," she said, after an hour. It was the second thing she'd said. The first had been "not far," answering a question about where her boat was, and the answer had been so obviously false that neither of them had bothered to pursue it.

"It's fine," Casper said. "You've a good arm."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite an apology.

She was perhaps thirty, he judged, though it was hard to say — hunger and hard travel had sanded some of the youth off her face. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, hacked rather than styled, the way hair gets cut with a knife on a boat. Her hands were wrong for a fisherman's wife. They were ink-stained at the cuticles, and there was a callus on the middle finger of her right hand that Casper recognized because he had one himself, from decades of holding a pen on the log. A writer's callus. Or a clerk's.

Or a forger's, said a voice in his head that he did not listen to, because listening to it meant asking questions, and asking questions meant answers, and answers meant involvement, and Casper Nakamura had spent four years constructing a life with no room in it for any of those things.

"Name?" he asked.

She looked at the fire for a long moment, weighing it. Whatever she decided, she decided deliberately, the way she seemed to decide everything.

"Ilsa."

"Just Ilsa?"

"Just Ilsa."

He nodded as if that were a normal answer. Outside, the wind rose another note, and the tower hummed faintly, the sound it made in a northeast gale, like a bottle blown across. She tracked the sound immediately — head tilting, eyes moving to the ceiling, mapping the building.

"How tall is the light?"

"Thirty meters to the gallery. The lamp room's above that."

"Who lights it?"

"I do."

"Every night?"

"Every night. Two hundred and six nights a year, and all night during storm season, which is now." He stood to feed the stove. "Which means I'll be going up presently, and you'll have the cottage to yourself, and there's bread in the tin and a key to nothing that matters, if you take my meaning."

She took his meaning. He watched her take it — the small settling of her shoulders, the recalculation. He was telling her he would not search the satchel. He was telling her that his hospitality came with a blind spot attached, and that the blind spot was hers to use.

Whether that made him a fool or a decent man, he genuinely could not say.

* * *

The trouble started, such as it was, three nights later.

She had not left. He had half expected the cottage empty after the first evening, the satchel gone, maybe the bread too. But the storm settled in like a tenant, blowing hard from the northeast for four unbroken days, and no boat could come for her and no boat could leave, and so she stayed, occupying the cottage the way a cat occupies a barn: present, silent, always at the edge of vision. She mended things without being asked — the loose hinge on the cupboard, the latch that had needed oil since Maren died. She cooked once, badly, and ate standing at the window. She asked questions about the coast with the flat persistence of a surveyor: where the roads went, where the soldiers were quartered, how often the patrol boats ran, whether the village had a telegraph office.

Casper answered some questions and declined others, and she accepted both responses with equal grace, which unsettled him more than argument would have.

On the third night he came down from the lamp watch near midnight and found her asleep in the chair by the stove, upright, the satchel strap wrapped twice around her wrist and the buckle fisted in her hand. Even unconscious she didn't let go. He put a blanket over her and she woke instantly, fully, no grogginess at all — came off the chair with the satchel swinging like a weapon — and then stopped herself, breathing hard, ashamed of it in a way she tried to hide.

"You should sleep in the bed," Casper said. "I keep odd hours anyway."

"I sleep fine here."

"It's your back you'll ruin."

"It's my back." And that closed it.

But it was the fourth night that mattered. The fourth night the storm eased toward morning, and Casper came down to find her awake, sitting where the lamplight wouldn't reach her face, and a uniform coat spread open on his table.

Not hers. It was one of his — the dress coat he kept in the cedar chest, the one he hadn't worn since the summer of 1914, navy wool with brass buttons he had polished himself in another life. She had taken it from the chest to examine it, which was intrusion enough, but that wasn't what stopped him in the doorway.

What stopped him was her face.

She was looking at the coat the way a man looks at a dog that has bitten him. Her whole body had drawn inward, shoulders up, breath shallow, and her right hand hovered over the brass buttons without touching them, as though the metal might be hot. Casper had seen men flinch from shells and from rats and from the sound of a door slamming, and he knew what he was looking at now. Uniforms had done something to this woman. Uniforms were the shape the war wore when it came to her house.

"That's mine," he said quietly, and she startled anyway, spinning, and then the shame crossed her face like weather and was mastered. She folded the coat — precisely, along its seams, a careful folding — and laid it back in the open chest, and stepped away from it.

"The chest was shut," she said. "I looked. I'm sorry."

"Why look in a cedar chest?"

"Because everything in a house is either useful or hidden, and hidden things are usually more useful."

It was the longest sentence she'd offered him in four days, and it told him more than all the others combined. He hung the coat, closed the chest, and put his hand flat on the lid a moment.

"My wife bought me that coat," he heard himself say. Which surprised him. He almost never spoke of Maren aloud; speaking of her felt like opening a valve he'd welded shut. "She's dead. Three years in April."

Ilsa said nothing. But she looked at him then — really looked, the arithmetic returning, only different now, weighted differently — and he understood that she was revising his file. Widower. Keeper. Man who speaks of the dead only under pressure of someone else's fear. Whatever conclusion she reached, she kept it behind her teeth.

* * *

On the fifth day the storm blew itself out and the sky came up pale and rinsed, and Casper walked the shingle again, alone, while Ilsa stood at the cottage window with the field glasses he'd lent her, sweeping the horizon for a boat that might be hunting her. He found nothing new washed up. He found, instead, that he was relieved about the storm — relieved it had pinned her here, relieved the cottage had held two people's silence for five days without cracking — and that relief alarmed him more than anything else that week.

That afternoon he fixed the hinge on the coal shed, oiled the davit, wrote the log. Ordinary things. Through the window he watched her walk the path down to the tide line, satchel slung crosswise against her body, and stand looking north along the coast for a long time. Measuring distances. Counting days. Planning, whatever it was, alone — because whatever was in that satchel, she intended to carry it away from here herself, and soon.

At supper she pushed the potatoes around her plate and finally said, "How far is it to the herring station? The old cannery, north."

Casper's spoon stopped.

Twelve miles inland, the cannery had been shut since before the war — shuttered, stripped, forgotten, a rotting monument to a fishery that had died young. There was no reason on God's green earth for a woman who wouldn't tell him her second name to want directions to it.

"Twelve miles," he said. "Why?"

She looked at him across the table, and he watched her decide — watched the deliberation move through her face like light moving across water — and for one moment he thought she was going to tell him. Her mouth opened.

Then her eyes dropped to the table, to the corner where his log book lay, and specifically to the page visible beneath the cover: his own handwriting, the neat entries he made every day, and beneath them, pressed into the wood of the table itself, older marks — pencil ghosts of letters practiced and erased years ago, Maren's handwriting games, the traces of a woman who loved pens and paper and the shapes of names.

"Nothing," Ilsa said. "It doesn't matter."

But she was still looking at the table, at those ghost-letters, and her hand had gone to the satchel strap at her shoulder, gripping it, and Casper Nakamura — who had spent forty-one years learning to read weather and five days learning to read her — understood suddenly that the satchel was not full of money or medicine or any ordinary contraband at all.

It was full of paper. And the paper was writing to him from somewhere he had already buried.
