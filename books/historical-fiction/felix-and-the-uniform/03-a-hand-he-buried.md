## 3. A Hand He Buried

The satchel sat on the table between them like a third person who hadn't been invited but refused to leave.

Casper had made tea — the last of the good leaves, hoarded since September against a bad day he hadn't known was coming — and the woman, Ilsa, held her cup in both hands without drinking. She'd eaten half of what he offered and then stopped, as if fullness itself were a risk. Her clothes hung drying by the stove, steaming faintly. She watched him with the patience of someone used to waiting out weather, or interrogation.

"The boat," he said again. "There'll be one inside the week. Supply run, weather permitting."

She said nothing. Outside, the wind threw spray against the tower's north face hard enough to sound like gravel. The lamp turned above them, its beam sweeping the room in slow rotations — dark, pale, dark — and each time the light crossed her face she didn't blink.

He should have left the satchel where it lay on the shingle. That was the honest accounting of it, the version he would write in no log anywhere: he had seen it wash down with her, seen her clutch it even unconscious, her fingers white around the strap, and he had wanted to know what a drowning woman holds tighter than her own life. Curiosity was a thing he'd thought he'd buried with Maren. Apparently the grave was shallower than he'd believed.

"Your letters will be ruined," he said. "If they stay in that leather. Salt gets in, the ink goes to ghost. I've pulled bottles out of that water with the labels still readable, but paper—" He shrugged, a small lie of indifference. "Paper doesn't survive forgiveness."

Her eyes came up to his. For a long moment the only sound was the clockwork of the rotation mechanism and the sea working at the rocks below.

Then she set down the cup, crossed the room, and brought him the satchel herself.

* * *

She placed it in his hands and stepped back, arms folded, and he understood that this was not surrender but a transaction whose terms he didn't yet know. The buckles were brass, green with salt. His fingers were steadier than they had any right to be.

Inside: oilcloth wrapped twice around a bundle and tied with waxed cord. A pistol, small, with four rounds in a paper sleeve — she watched his face when he found it, and gave away nothing, and he set it on the table without comment, barrel toward the wall. Under the oilcloth, letters. Perhaps thirty of them, bundled in groups of five, each group sealed with a different stamp. Passports, some of them, blank pages showing faint watermark ghosts. And at the bottom, wrapped separately, a single envelope addressed in a hand that stopped Casper's heart the way cold water stops a swimmer.

He knew that hand.

He knew it the way you know a voice before the words resolve — the tall ascenders, ambitious and slightly crooked; the lowercase *a* with its flat little roof, like every *a* was wearing a hat; the way Anton Brandl crossed his *t*s low, almost lazily, as if the letter beneath didn't deserve the full ceremony. Casper had spent two years of their youth watching that hand work — school exercises done twice and sold once, concert tickets for girls they never worked up the courage to speak to, a forged permission slip the spring Anton's father died so Anton could take the ferry to the mainland and stand at a strangers' graveside because nobody else would.

Anton Brandl had gone to war in the second year. A telegram had come to this coast in the autumn of 1916 — *Regret to inform you* — and Casper had read it standing in the village post office while the stove ticked and the postmistress pretended to sort mail, and he had walked home along the shingle and not spoken for three days, and then he had gone on keeping the lamp, because that was what he did. History took people. You lit the light anyway.

He was looking at that dead man's handwriting now.

"Casper Nakamura," Ilsa said. It was the first thing she'd said since telling him her name. She pronounced it carefully, a word she'd rehearsed. "You knew him. The man who wrote these."

"You know that how?"

"He wrote your name on three of the envelopes." Her eyes didn't move from his face. "In case."

"In case of what?"

She didn't answer that. Instead: "Read one. The one on top."

His hands, when he broke the seal, were not steady at all.

* * *

The paper smelled faintly of the workshop it had come from — ink, yes, but under the ink something like machine oil and beeswax, a smell that unspooled twenty years in a single breath, back to the loft above the chandlery where Anton had kept his first press and printed pamphlets nobody read and tickets everyone bought.

The letter was dated the ninth of October, 1918. Three weeks old. Written by a man two years drowned.

*Casper — I have written this letter eleven times and burned ten of them, which you will recognize as my standard ratio. The truth is a document like any other: it needs the right weight of paper or no one believes it.*

*I am alive. I know what the telegram said. I wrote the telegram — not personally, but I chose the words, which amounts to the same crime. There are perhaps six men in Europe who could have told my false death from a true one, and I made sure all six were paid.*

*You will want to throw this in the stove. Do it after you've read the rest, because there is a job in it, and the job has a date attached, and the date does not care whether you forgive me.*

Casper looked up. The room seemed to tilt slightly, the way a boat tilts when a wave comes under it from an angle you didn't watch for. The beam swept through again — light, dark — and Ilsa stood unmoving at the edge of it.

"Keep reading," she said quietly.

*They tell me you kept the light through everything. Through Maren, especially through Maren. I am sorry I was not at the funeral. I was at a funeral, though — several — just none that concerned me. That sentence will make sense later or it won't; I have never yet found a way to explain what I do that doesn't sound like either madness or treason, and the problem is that it is both.*

*There is a workshop. You remember the old herring cannery at Skarvik, twelve miles up the coast road — where we stole the rowboat summer of '99 and you were sick over the side and I told everyone it was you who proposed the theft. The cannery is mine now, or mine in the way a man owns a thing he cannot sell and cannot leave. Inks. Stamps. Paper with the right ghosts in it. Uniforms, God help us. Everything a dead man needs to go on being useful.*

*I have been making papers for people the war wants to erase. Not soldiers — the war is finished with its soldiers soon, one way or another. Families. People whose names got written into ledgers by men who think a ledger is the same as fate. I have one document left to finish, the largest thing I've ever attempted, and I find — this is the part I have rewritten most often — that I cannot finish it myself.*

*Not can't. Won't say which, even now.*

*Here is the difficulty. Everything I make is a forgery, and everything I touch becomes suspect, including the things that must not be. There is a family — you don't need names, names are dangerous on paper, I of all people know that — who need to cross a border on documents that will hold under the worst kind of looking. And I have discovered, late, that every document I sign is signed by a dead man, and a dead man's papers invite exactly the wrong kind of looking. They need papers made by a living man. An honest one. Someone with no history of this trade, whose hand carries no signature anyone has learned to hunt.*

*You are going to say you are a lighthouse keeper. Good. Say it loudly. Then finish reading.*

*The deadline matters more than anything else in this letter. When the armistice comes — and it is coming, the rumors reach even me, weeks now perhaps — everything changes. Borders close properly. New stamps, new forms, new suspicion. Every document in Europe expires overnight and the forgers start again from nothing. Before the guns stop, a certain border post is still using the old regime's seals, still staffed by men whose authority is dying and who know it, and such men check less and take more. After, there will be no taking and no less checking. Finish it before the guns stop. After that, paper means nothing.*

*I have sent this with Ilsa, whom you may trust approximately as far as you can throw her, which is farther than it sounds; she is difficult to throw. If she has reached you, then the sea did what I asked of it, and I owe the sea a debt I will never settle.*

*Twelve miles of bad road, Casper. I know what I'm asking. I knew your telegram too, the one you never received, the one I intercepted — the one saying you'd been asked to serve. I burned it. Forgive me or don't, but understand that I have always known exactly what kind of man you are, and I have staked four lives on it.*

*Burn this after reading.*

*A.*

Casper read it twice. The second time his vision swam somewhere near the middle and he had to set the page flat on the table and stand with both hands braced on the wood until the room agreed to hold still.

The lamp turned. Dark, light, dark.

* * *

He put it in the stove.

Ilsa didn't stop him. She watched the paper catch, the ink flaring briefly green — Anton's inks, even his flames were forged somehow — and fold itself into black curls, and she said only, "He told you to," as if that settled something between them.

"He's alive." Casper's voice came out lower than he intended, scraped thin. "Two years. Two years I—" He stopped. What? Mourned? He had mourned, but it had been one mourning among many, a stone among stones, laid down in the same season he'd buried Maren, and there was a particular obscenity in that — that grief could be filed, stacked, made orderly. That the dead could be given drawers.

"He says alive," Ilsa said. "I'll tell you what I know, which is less than he thinks." She unfolded herself onto the bench across the table, and for the first time she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the sea. "I was his courier. Two years. Papers in, papers out. I never asked what was in them until the night I couldn't avoid knowing. There is a quartermaster — Brandt — who supplied Anton's materials through channels he shouldn't have, and who has decided recently that a crate went missing that he is owed for, and Brandt collects debts the way other men collect medals. If he finds the workshop before that document crosses the border, everyone in it dies, and then the families die, and then he writes it all down neatly in a ledger he keeps for the purpose."

"Why tell me this now?"

"Because you read his letter and put it in the stove in under a minute, without arguing, without preaching." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like the place a smile would be on a different face. "That told me what he said it would. That you're the kind of man who does the necessary thing quietly."

"I'm the kind of man who keeps a lamp lit and stays out of history."

"Yes," she said. "He mentioned you'd say that too."

The fire ticked in the stove. Above them the great lens turned its patient circles, throwing the beam out over water no boat should be on tonight, over the shingle where a crate might be landing or a man might be watching, over a coast that had kept its secrets so far and was clearly tired of the arrangement.

Casper looked at the satchel's remaining contents — the blank passports fanned across his table, the little pistol pointed dutifully at the wall, the bundles of letters he now understood he was not meant to read. Twelve miles of bad road. Four lives staked. A deadline measured in the slowing of artillery, of all the clocks in the world the least reliable.

"I'll need to think," he said.

"You have until the weather lifts," Ilsa said. "So do I. So does he, if he's still—"

She stopped. Casper heard the sentence complete itself in the silence: *if he's still alive, which neither of us knows, which is why the letter reads like a man saying goodbye.*

"If he wanted me at that cannery," Casper said slowly, "why not come himself? Why letters?"

"Because," Ilsa said, and now she did look away, out the dark window where the beam swung over the sea, "he says the document has to be finished by someone who never wanted power. He says a man who's spent two years forging authority can't be trusted to sign honestly anymore." Her voice flattened. "He's probably right. That's the worst part."

Outside, the storm changed its mind about something and came at the tower from a new quarter. Down on the shingle, the tide was climbing toward the high-water line, erasing the night's evidence one wave at a time.

Casper gathered the blank passports into a stack, squared their edges, and found his hands doing it with a precision that frightened him — the instinctive neatness of a man handling instruments. He set them down and wiped his palms on his trousers.

"One question," he said. "The envelope with my name on it. *In case.* In case of what, exactly?"

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment, the lamplight crossing her face one final time before the beam swung seaward again.

"In case the man who answered the door wasn't you," she said. "It's happened before. It nearly happened tonight."
