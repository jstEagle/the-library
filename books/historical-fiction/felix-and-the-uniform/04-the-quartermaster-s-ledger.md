## 4. The Quartermaster's Ledger

The keeper slept with his boots on.

Ilsa had noticed it the first night, when she'd woken on his cot at dawn and found him upright in the chair by the stove, head tipped back, hands loose on his knees, boots laced to the topmost eyelet. A man ready to run, or to fight, or simply to leave — she hadn't decided which yet. She had been deciding about people for three years now, sorting them the way a sorter sorts mail: useful, harmless, dangerous. Mostly they were dangerous in ordinary ways. The extraordinary ones you met only once, because afterward there was no one left to meet them again.

Casper Nakamura was an open question, and she disliked open questions. Open questions got couriers killed.

She stood at the tower window while he climbed the stairs above her, checking the lamp mechanism for the noon extinguishing, though there was nothing to extinguish at midday except habit. Below, the sea worked at the shingle like a woman washing stones, patient, indifferent. Somewhere under that grey water lay a boat with a hole in its hull that had not been made by any storm, and somewhere behind her, upstairs in a room where he thought she couldn't reach it, sat a satchel containing letters that would hang her if the wrong eyes read them.

* * *

She had told him nothing. That had been the discipline of three days: say little, watch everything, let the silence do the work. Silence made people fill it. Silence made them show you their shape. But this man did not fill silences; he seemed to live inside them the way a stone lives inside a wall, load-bearing and unremarkable, and it unsettled her more than questioning would have.

He fed her herring and black bread and asked only practical things. Could she walk. Did her ribs hurt. Did she take sugar. Once — only once — he had looked at her too long across the table and said, "You came ashore at my light. That's forty miles of open water in November."

"I'm a good swimmer," she said, and he had almost smiled, and gone back to trimming his lamp wicks as if the answer satisfied him completely, which meant it satisfied him not at all.

Now, while his footsteps creaked overhead, she crossed to the shelf where the satchel sat. He had not locked it away. That was either courtesy or a test, and Ilsa Brandt—

Ilsa Reinhardt, she corrected herself, the old name surfacing like vomit, swallowing it down — Ilsa Reinhardt did not fail tests.

The satchel's buckle opened without sound. She had oiled it herself, in another life, in a cellar in Danzig with Anton's hands guiding hers over the brass fittings. Inside: the oilskin packet, dry, intact, every letter accounted for. She counted them anyway, thumbing the edges like a bank teller. Seven. All seven.

And beneath them, wrapped in a page torn from a ledger, the thing she had carried against her body through forty miles of freezing sea because paper floats and bodies sink: the fragment of the quartermaster's ledger she had cut free with a penknife in a warehouse office in Kiel while a man twenty feet away counted crates and hummed a church hymn.

She didn't take the ledger fragment out. She knew what it said. She could recite it the way other women recited psalms.

*Crate 44 — contents per manifest falsified — destination Vesterhavn — collected by A.W. — payment deferred.*

A.W. Anton Wenzel. Dead two years, according to the telegram with the official stamp, dead at the front with a shell-hole through him, dead so thoroughly that Casper Nakamura had spoken of him once, briefly, with the flat finality men used for graves. Except the letters in this satchel — seven of them, all in that unmistakable leftward-slanting hand, all addressed to a courier whose name appeared nowhere — those letters said otherwise. Those letters said *the workshop still stands*, and *finish it before the guns stop*, and, in the last one, dated six weeks ago, *if you are reading this then the sea has done what I could not, and you must choose who finishes it. Not Brandt. Anyone but Brandt.*

She repacked the satchel, buckled it, set it back exactly two finger-widths from the shelf's edge where it had been, and became aware of the silence overhead.

He had stopped climbing.

She turned. Casper stood at the foot of the tower stairs, watching her, a rag in one hand and lamp-oil on the other, his face giving away precisely nothing — which told her he had learned long ago that faces were things other people read.

"You count them every morning," he said. "The letters. Through the canvas. I've watched you."

"Force of habit."

"Whose handwriting is it?"

And there it was, the question arriving at last, three days late, patient as the tide. She measured him. Forty miles of open water. Boots laced in sleep. A man who kept a light burning over rocks that would drown strangers without complaint if he failed at his work even one night.

If she told him nothing, he would go on feeding her herring until the supply boat came or didn't, and then he would ask her to leave, and she would leave, and carry the whole weight alone to Vesterhavn with Brandt's men already asking questions in every harbor town between here and the border. Alone she was fast. Alone she had been fast for three years, and fast was a way of saying *alive so far*.

But the letters named this coast. The last letter named *the keeper's light* by bearing and description, which meant Anton had trusted this man with knowledge that killed couriers, which meant Anton — who trusted no one, who taught her that trust was a currency spent only once — had spent it here.

"The letters are from Anton Wenzel," she said.

Something moved behind the keeper's face, brief as a fish under ice.

"Anton is dead," Casper said.

"Then a dead man has been writing to me since August." She held his gaze. "Or the telegram your government sent you was itself a piece of work. And before you decide which — you should know that the second possibility has a name, and the name is Brandt, and Brandt is looking for something that passed through my hands, and I am the only person alive who knows where it is. So we can go on being polite to each other, Herr Nakamura, or you can tell me what kind of friend Anton Wenzel actually was to you. Because I never met the man. I only carried his paper."

That was a lie, and she delivered it flatly, because the truth — that Anton had been the closest thing to a father she'd had since her own father was arrested — belonged to no one yet, least of all a stranger with lamp-oil on his hands.

* * *

They sat at the table with the letters spread between them like a dealt hand, and Casper read each one twice, and Ilsa watched him read.

She catalogued him the way she catalogued everyone: hands steady, breath even, pupils narrowing at the third letter — the one mentioning Vesterhavn — jaw tightening at the sixth, the one with the phrase *not Brandt*. He grieved quietly, in the way of men who had practiced grief alone until it became furniture. He did not ask who she was. He did not ask how the letters came to be in a satchel crossing forty miles of November sea. He asked only:

"What does Brandt want?"

"A crate. Number forty-four." She laid the torn ledger page beside the letters and saw him flinch — properly flinch, at the heading stamped in violet ink, the heading she had seen on documents that had sent men to firing posts. "Forged papers. Passports, transit permits, discharge certificates. Enough identity for a dozen people to stop existing in one country and start existing in another. Anton built it. It vanished from a warehouse in Kiel in October, and Quartermaster Brandt has been pulling this coastline apart ever since looking for it."

"Why does a quartermaster care about forged passports?"

"Because Quartermaster Brandt commissioned them," Ilsa said. "To sell. Deserters, escaped prisoners, families who need to become someone else — there's a market, and Brandt intends to own it. Anton took the crate instead of delivering it. Anton, who was supposed to be dead, stole a quartermaster's merchandise out from under his signature, and Brandt's ledger shows a debt of Crate 44 against the name A.W., and Brandt collects debts." She paused. "You understand what that makes me."

"A courier."

"A loose end. The last person who touched the crate." She folded her hands so he wouldn't see them tremble, and hated that they trembled, and hated more that she cared whether he saw. "Three of Brandt's people have already died carrying less than what I know. One fell downstairs. One drowned in a canal that had railings. One—" She stopped. "One stopped answering letters. With Brandt, that's the worst one. The ones who die quickly are the lucky transactions."

Casper looked at the window, at the grey light failing over the water, and when he spoke his voice was the voice of a man confirming a tide table.

"When does the boat come?"

"There is no boat. There's a contact in Vesterhavn, a mill on the border road, and a family in a cellar in Danzig who have eleven days to become four completely different human beings or be put on a train east." She leaned forward. "Eleven days, Herr Nakamura. And I can't do it alone anymore, because my shoulder is torn and my hands shake when I write, and a forger with shaking hands is a corpse with a delay on it."

The stove ticked. Overhead, in the lamp room, the great lens sat dark, waiting for night the way everything on this coast waited for night.

"Why tell me?" Casper said finally. "You don't trust me."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "I don't."

"Then why?"

She looked at him — at the widower's careful face, at the boots he slept in, at the man who had opted out of the century and kept a light lit anyway — and she said the truest thing she had said in three years.

"Because Anton's letters point here. Because whatever he was planning, he planned it around this lighthouse and around you, and I've learned not to argue with Anton's plans. They cost too much." She gathered the letters, squared their edges. "And because tomorrow I start walking south whether you come or not, and if Brandt takes me on this road, I'd rather someone on this coast knew where the crate is buried. Someone the sea hasn't finished with yet."

She stood, and the floor seemed to tilt with the exhaustion she'd been outrunning for six days, and she caught the table's edge, and his hand was there — not gripping, just present, a rail to steady against — and gone again before she could resent it.

"Vesterhavn is twelve miles inland," Casper said.

"The cannery road. I know."

"I know the road better than you do." He was already reaching for the oilskin coat on its hook, not looking at her, speaking in the tone of a man reading off a checklist of chores: lamp trimmed, wood stacked, twelve miles of bad road. "We leave at first light. If we're crossing the island, we cross it in daylight, where people can see us and no one can follow without being seen."

"That's your reasoning?"

"That's the reason I'll give you." He shrugged into the coat and went to the door, and paused there with his hand on the latch, half-turned, the lamplight catching the side of his face. "The real reason is that Anton pulled me out of a burning building in Bremen in 1912 and never once mentioned it afterward, and a debt like that doesn't care whether I trust you."

The door closed behind him. Ilsa sat alone at the table with seven letters and a torn ledger page and the sound of the sea coming up the shingle, and allowed herself, for exactly four seconds, to feel the weight lift — and then took it back, because relief was what got couriers killed.

Eleven days. Twelve miles. And somewhere south, moving up the coast with a ledger in his pocket and patience like a knife, a man who collected debts was already asking the postmistress in the village who had come ashore at the Nakamura light.

She blew out the lamp and lay down in the dark and did not sleep.
