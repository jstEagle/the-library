## 5. Twelve Miles of Bad Road

They left before dawn, while the tide was still thinking about coming in, because Ilsa said roads were safer than water and Casper knew the road she meant.

Twelve miles inland to Skjervold. Twelve miles he had not traveled in four years — not since Maren's funeral, when he'd ridden in the back of Halvard Olsen's fish truck with the coffin balanced on sawhorses and stared at his own hands the whole way as if they belonged to a stranger whose grief hadn't arrived yet and might never come. After that he had made an arrangement with the supply boat and then, when the war ate the boats, with the weather itself. Everything came to him or nothing did. That was the bargain. The world could keep its roads.

Now he walked one, and it felt like walking on a scar.

The cart track ran up out of the shingle, through the black pines, along the shoulder of the fell where the wind came at them sideways carrying the first hard smell of winter. He carried the satchel. She had let him carry it that morning without a fight, which frightened him more than if she'd fought. A woman who stops guarding a thing has either decided you're trustworthy or decided the thing no longer matters, and he did not know which was worse.

Ilsa walked ahead, half a step always, the way soldiers walk past a window. Her boots were Anton's — someone's — too big, stuffed at the heel with rag, and she moved in them without complaint the way people move in borrowed lives. Every quarter mile or so she stopped, tilted her head, listened, and went on. She never explained the stopping and he never asked. By the third mile he had started doing it too, and hated himself for how quickly the habit took.

"You've been this way before," he said, because the silence had begun to press.

"Once." She didn't turn. "In summer. It was easier in summer."

"Easier how?"

"Easier to see who was watching."

There was no one watching now. There was no one anywhere. The pines stood in their rows like men waiting for an order no one would give. Once, far behind them, the lighthouse showed its white flash above the trees, and Casper caught himself looking back at it the way a man checks that his house hasn't burned down simply because he left it. Then the road bent and took it away.

The first sign of the war was nailed to a barn door at the crossroads above the river: *FISH IS A PRIVILEGE. REPORT HOARDING.* Beneath the official text someone had penciled something in Norwegian that had been scrubbed at with a wet sleeve but not erased — the ghost of a joke, or a curse. Ilsa read it twice. Casper pretended not to see her reading it, which was its own kind of lie, and he was collecting those now. He seemed to be collecting a great many things lately: lies, habits, other people's letters. Four years of nothing, and in three days his pockets were full.

At the river they crossed on the stepping stones below the drowned bridge. The bridge had been blown in '15 by men from their own side, to slow an advance that never came, and no one had rebuilt it because no one came this way anymore except smugglers and the dead in the form of paper. The water ran fast and iron-colored. Ilsa crossed lightly, testing each stone with her toe before trusting it. Casper followed, slower, arms out, feeling sixty years old in his knees though he was fifty-one, feeling every one of the twelve miles already in his thighs.

On the far bank she waited for him. It was the first kindness she had shown him outright, and she made it look accidental.

"How much farther?"

"Two hours. We eat at the top." She glanced at the sky, gray on gray. "Rain by noon. We want walls before noon."

They ate at the top — dry bread, a scrap of salt fish, water from the burn in a tin cup they passed back and forth without ceremony. Sitting against a boulder with the whole valley spread out below them, Ilsa unwrapped her scarf from her throat for the first time since the beach, and he saw the bruise there, yellowing now, ringed like a collar.

"The boat," he said. "Someone did that."

"Yes."

"Brandt's man."

She wrapped the scarf again, unhurried, covering it like putting a photograph face-down. "Brandt does not like to dirty his hands. But things he wants have a way of arriving marked." She looked down the valley. "Eat, Keeper. The road is long and you are old."

"I'm not old," he said, and she almost smiled, and it was the closest thing to warmth either of them had traded yet.

* * *

Skjervold announced itself by smell before sight — herring oil and rust and wet creosote, the ghost of an industry rotting in its own clothes. Then the houses came, leaning together along one street like tired men holding each other up, and beyond them the cannery, long and low and iron-roofed, crouched at the tideline of the fjord with its conveyor gantry reaching out over the water like a broken arm.

Casper had known it would be bad. He had not known it would be *populated*.

There were people. That was what undid him — not ruin, ruin he could have loved, ruin asked nothing — but people, moving quietly between the houses, hauling water, splitting wood, living small and watchful. Children with the particular stillness of children who have learned that noise costs. An old woman beating a rug with a fury that had nothing to do with the rug. Smoke rising from chimneys in thin, disciplined threads.

A village. A real one, going on with it. And him walking into it with a dead man's letters against his ribs.

"Keep your eyes forward," Ilsa murmured. "Villages read faces. Yours has been unreadable for four years — don't start practicing expressions here."

It was meant, he thought, as a joke. It landed as an instruction, and he obeyed it, and obeying it felt like being conscripted all over again into a country he'd spent a lifetime refusing citizenship in.

The ration notices did the talking. They were everywhere, layered on the store wall like sediment: *BREAD FLOUR CUT TO TWO KILOS PER HEAD. POTATOES REQUISITIONED. ALL MEN 18–50 REMAIN SUBJECT TO RECALL. FAILURE TO REPORT IS TREASON.* Paper over paper over paper, each notice nailed through the corners of the last, so that peeling one loose would expose an older law beneath, an older hunger. At the bottom of the layers, half-hidden, a poster from the first autumn of the war, all trumpets and flags, promising it would be over by a Christmas that had come and gone four times since.

In front of the wall, a boy of perhaps nine stood on an upturned crate, reading. Not the top notices — the old ones underneath. Reading them the way Casper read tide tables: thoroughly, hungrily, alone.

Then Casper saw the photographs, and stopped understanding anything at all.

They filled the whole end wall of the meetinghouse, corner to corner, eave to foundation. Photographs of men — young men, mostly, some barely more than the boys who should have been reading ration laws instead of dying under foreign artillery — mounted in every size of frame, cheap ones, homemade ones, frames cut from crate wood and painted white. Dozens of them. Over a hundred. Faces in dress uniform, faces grinning out of group pictures with a hand on each other's shoulders, faces stiff and formal in studio light that made them look older than their mothers wanted them to look. Some had black ribbon across the corner. Some had two ribbons. A few had flowers tucked into the frame's edge, gone brown and papery, replaced and replaced and finally not replaced.

He counted without meaning to. He got to forty before the counting became obscene and he made himself stop.

"Maren's brother isn't there," he heard himself say. His voice came out strange, flat, informational. "Torjus. He was at Verdun sector, the papers said. He'd be — he'd have been on a wall somewhere further south. They didn't send him back either. Nobody comes back anymore, do they. They just stop needing to."

Ilsa looked at the wall the way she looked at everything — measuring it, cataloguing exits — but something in her face shifted when she reached the frames with double ribbons. "My brother is on a wall," she said. "Different country. Same wall."

"I didn't know you had—"

"There are many things you don't know, Keeper." But she said it without an edge. Almost gently. As if the wall had authorized a brief truce.

An old man came out of the store with a sack of meal and stood looking at them with the frank, unhurried appraisal of a man who has watched every stranger this village has produced since before the war and buried most of the locals. His eyes went to Ilsa's boots — wrong size, wrong walk. To the satchel. To Casper's hands, which had gone still at his sides.

"Lighthouse keeper," the old man said. Not a question. That was the trouble with being known; even your absences had names. "Nakamura. Thought you'd died."

"People keep saying that."

"Well." He shifted his sack. "You look it." He turned to go, then paused, and his voice dropped into a register meant only for the space between the three of them. "You'll want to be careful at the cannery. You're not the first to head out there this month, and the others didn't look like fishermen." His eyes flicked once toward Ilsa, once toward the road south, toward the coast, toward wherever such men came from. "Big fellow. Civil manners. Took tea with the postmistress an hour and asked about freight. Asked about crates." He heaved the sack onto his shoulder. "Asked whether anyone kept a lamp out at your rock, Keeper. Said he liked to know who was watching the water at night."

The cold that went through Casper then had nothing to do with the wind off the fjord.

"When," he said. "When was he here?"

"Day before yesterday, I'd reckon. Left on the morning boat." The old man shrugged, and the shrug contained the entire philosophy of Skjervold: whatever comes, comes; whatever leaves, leaves; the living get on with the potatoes. "Good day to you both."

He shuffled off. Down the street, the boy climbed down from his crate and drifted away along the wall of photographs, trailing one finger across the frames like a reader following a line of scripture.

* * *

The cannery rose out of the last of the afternoon fog like the hull of a ship that had run aground decades ago and been left to become geography. Up close it was worse and better than ruin: the windows boarded, the great doors chained but not locked — chains rusted through their own links, theater of security — and everywhere the smell of old fish worked so deep into the timber that the building itself had become a kind of fossil. Gulls argued along the ridge of the roof. Somewhere inside, water dripped with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world.

Ilsa stopped at the doors and put her hand flat against the wood. For a moment she stood there, eyes closed, and Casper understood that she was not praying and not listening but *arriving* — assembling herself into whoever she had to be on the other side of that door.

"Anton," he said. "Tell me about him. Before we go in. Tell me the version you knew."

She opened her eyes. "Why?"

"Because in three days I've learned my dead friend writes letters and my quiet guest carries bruises and my lighthouse is a question on a quartermaster's lips. I'm going to stand in that man's workshop in a minute. I'd like to know who built it."

Ilsa considered him for a long moment. Wind came off the fjord and flattened her scarf against the bruise at her throat.

"He was the funniest man I ever met," she said at last, "and the most frightened, and he had decided by the time I knew him that these were the same quality. He could copy any hand you put in front of him — ministers, generals, bishops — and he used to say that handwriting was just the shape a person's cowardice takes." She looked at the chained doors. "He believed someone existed who had no cowardice to copy. Someone whose hand would be honest because they'd never had power worth lying for. He talked about it the way other men talk about God." A pause. "He also said the person he described would never survive the war, because the war eats exactly that kind of person first."

"So why build all this for them?"

"Because he was funny," Ilsa said, "and frightened." She pulled the chain free. It came apart in her hands with a sound like an old man clearing his throat, and the doors swung inward on darkness and the smell of oil and ink and time.

Inside, the light fell in dusty blades through gaps in the boarding. Long benches stretched away into the dark. Machinery sat sheeted in tarpaulin like furniture in a house of the dead. And at the far end, past the rusted gutting lines, a lantern hung ready beside a workbench, set with tools laid out in neat rows — a room within the ruin, kept, tended, *waiting*.

Ilsa struck a match and lit the lantern, and the workshop leapt up out of the gloom all at once: trays of type, stamps in oiled cloth, bottles of ink ranked like an apothecary's shelf, blank passports stacked in a drawer that slid open on silent runners, maintained, oiled, *used*.

And pinned above the bench, square and centered, a single sheet of paper in a hand Casper had last seen on a letter he'd burned twenty-five years ago in a schoolyard in another country:

*Finish it before the guns stop. After that, paper means nothing.*

Casper stood very still. The gulls went on arguing overhead. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with its terrible patience.

"Keeper," Ilsa said softly. She was no longer looking at the note. She was looking at the workbench, at the document lying half-finished beneath a glass pane to keep off the damp — and her voice had changed, gone thin and careful, the voice of a person standing at the edge of something deep.

"Casper. Come look at the date field."

He crossed to the bench. The half-finished document lay there, elegant, meticulous, nearly complete — and in the corner, in fresh ink not yet a day old by its sheen, someone had written a name in the witness line.

Not Anton's hand. Not Ilsa's.

His own name. *Casper Nakamura* — copied perfectly, flawlessly, in a hand he almost recognized and then, with a cold turning in his stomach, did: it was his own current handwriting, aged four years, exactly as it would look after everything that had happened to it.

Below it, in Anton's hand, one line:

*I taught you everything except how to stay invisible. Finish what fits you.*

Far off down the fjord, muffled by fog and distance, a train whistled once — the first train whistle he had heard in four years, running on a line that was supposed to be dead — and Ilsa blew out the lantern.
