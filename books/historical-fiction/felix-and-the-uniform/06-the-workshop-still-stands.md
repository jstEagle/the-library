## 6. The Workshop Still Stands

The cannery announced itself by smell before it came into view — old brine and rust and the sweet rot of a hundred thousand herring that had been processed here and shipped away before the war swallowed the boats whole. Casper smelled it through the fog and felt something in his chest loosen and tighten at once, the way a knot does when you work at it.

"You've been here before," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question.

"Once. Twenty years ago, maybe more." He kept his eyes on the road. "Maren wanted to see the coast from somewhere higher than the water. There was a dance in the village that summer. The whole town smelled like fish oil and brass bands."

"And now?"

"Now it smells like nothing wants to live here."

The road climbed away from the sea and then bent back toward it, following the spine of the headland, and the village appeared below them all at once — a scatter of grey roofs around a grey harbor, smoke rising thin and straight from a dozen chimneys. It should have looked peaceful. Instead it looked held, the way a breath is held.

They had agreed to walk in separately. Ilsa went first, twenty paces ahead, her borrowed coat buttoned to the throat, her gait changed entirely — she had shown him that much on the road, how to walk like someone with nowhere to be and no reason to hurry. Casper followed with the satchel under his arm and tried to look like a man delivering fish oil samples, which was what he'd decided he was, and discovered that pretending was its own kind of labor, one he could feel in his shoulders after only a quarter mile.

The village had learned to grieve in public. That was the first thing he understood. A board had been nailed to the wall of the church hall, and on the board were photographs — dozens of them, then scores, arranged in rows like specimens. Young men in uniform, most of them; a few in sailor's collars; one boy who couldn't have been seventeen, photographed in his confirmation suit because no uniform photograph existed. Beneath each face, a name and a date. Some of the dates were clustered so close together they read like a single terrible week, which they probably were.

Casper stopped without meaning to. A woman was pinning a new photograph to the board, working with cold efficiency, the way you'd hang laundry. She stepped back, checked that it was straight, and walked away without looking at the other faces. She had done this enough times that the ritual had worn smooth.

Ilsa stood at the edge of his vision, not looking at the wall at all. Looking at him looking at it.

"Not your war," she said quietly, when the woman had gone.

"It's everyone's war. That's rather the problem with it."

"No." She shook her head once. "It isn't. That's the lie they tell so that everyone carries it. It belongs to the men who started it and the men who run it. Everyone else is just paying the freight." She turned toward the harbor. "Come on. We're losing light."

* * *

The cannery sat at the end of a shingle spit, half a mile beyond the last house, close enough to the water to have been fed by boats and far enough to be forgotten when the boats stopped coming. Its windows were boarded. Its great door hung open on one hinge, yawning onto darkness that smelled of salt and machine grease and, beneath it, faintly, something else — turpentine, maybe. Linseed. The smell of a workshop.

Ilsa went in first with the lamp unshuttered, moving along the wall, and Casper understood she was checking for tripwires or chalk marks or any of the small languages of people who lived hunted. He waited in the doorway with the wind at his back and watched her light travel across the interior.

The processing floor was vast and empty, the machinery sold or scrapped, but against the far wall, where the old sorting benches had been, someone had built a room inside the room. Timber partitions, floor to ceiling, with a door that had once been a hatch cover. From outside, in bad light, it would read as storage. Nothing more.

Ilsa knocked on the hatch — three, pause, two — and waited. Nothing answered except the wind worrying the roof sheets. She knocked again, the same pattern, and then pressed her palm flat against the wood as if listening through it.

"He's not here," she said. Casper couldn't tell if it was relief or grief in her voice. Perhaps both wore the same coat.

She found the key where Anton had always hidden keys, she said — taped inside the drainpipe bracket, wrapped in oilcloth — and opened the hatch, and the lamp light went into the little room ahead of them, and Casper Nakamura, who had spent four years believing the world contained nothing left to astonish him, stood in the doorway and forgot how to breathe.

* * *

It was not large. Ten feet by fourteen, perhaps, the height of a man with room to spare for shelves. But every inch of it was organized with a precision that made Casper think of the lighthouse — the same grammar of order, the same conviction that carelessness killed. Shelves of glass bottles, each labeled in a small exact hand: *iron gall, 1914 batch. Aniline violet. Sepia wash, aged. Sealing wax, Norwegian.* Trays of steel type sorted into compartments like an egg box. Rubber stamps in wooden cradles, their faces down, their handles worn shiny. Blotting paper in stacks. A press bolted to the bench. And paper — reams and reams of paper, some blank, some printed, hanging in folders like specimens in a naturalist's cabinet.

Ilsa lifted a folder and fanned it open under the lamp. Passports. Blank passports, a dozen of them, and not just theirs — Danish, Dutch, a Swedish document with the watermark so perfect that Casper reached out and touched it to confirm it wasn't real.

"It's better than real," Ilsa said, watching him touch it. "Real ones have mistakes. Anton's don't. That's how Brandt caught the last courier — not by finding a flaw, but by finding the absence of one. Too-perfect paper is its own confession." She set the folder down. "He taught me that. He taught me everything."

Casper moved along the bench slowly, hands behind his back now, afraid to disturb anything. There were pens racked like surgical instruments. A mortar and pestle stained black with ground ink. A small balance scale, weights nested beside it. A jar of tea leaves — actual tea, hoarded, worth more than the press — and beside it a tin of biscuits gone stale years ago, kept anyway, kept the way a man keeps a thing when the keeping matters more than the thing.

And pinned to the partition wall, at eye level, a photograph. Three young men on a dock, arms around each other's necks, squinting into sun. Casper knew two of the faces. One was his own, thirty years old and laughing at something off-camera. One was Anton's, younger than the telegram had ever let him imagine, whole-handed, unbent.

The third face he didn't know. When he looked at it too long, Ilsa turned the lamp slightly away.

"You," Casper said. His voice came out strange. "That's me. He kept — " He stopped. Started again. "Why would he keep my photograph?"

"Because you were the one person who never asked him what he did for a living." Ilsa said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Do you understand what that was worth to him? Everyone else wanted something. Papers, favors, silence. You just — drank his wine and argued about lighthouses and treated him like a man instead of a tool." She touched the edge of the photograph, very lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to check it still hurt. "When everything else got taken from him, this was proof there had been a time he was a person."

Casper looked at his own younger face and felt the vertigo of chapter three come back — the dead writing to the living, the past refusing to stay buried — but deeper now, because this wasn't a letter. This was evidence. Evidence that he had mattered to someone, and that the mattering had outlived him, and that it had been waiting here in a fish shed for two years for him to walk in and find it.

"I thought he died at Verdun," Casper said. "The telegram said — "

"The telegram was one of his." Ilsa almost smiled. "First-rate work. He was proud of it. Said it was the kindest forgery he ever made." The smile faded. "Then he stopped being proud of anything."

* * *

The masterpiece was in the flat drawer under the press, and Ilsa knelt to open it the way you'd kneel at a grave.

She drew out a folder of stiff grey card, and from the folder, a document half-finished — and even half-finished, even unlettered in half its fields, Casper could see it was different from everything else in the room. The other papers were tools. This was a cathedral.

It was a transit authorization, but layered — he could see that much even without understanding — a German military pass overlaid with a Red Cross corridor permit, keyed to a neutral embassy's seal, with countersignatures spaced so that no single office could void it without alerting the others. In the margins, in Anton's small exact hand, were notes in three languages, arrows connecting clauses, tiny calculations of dates and train times and border shift changes.

"What is it?" Casper asked, though he already knew it was the most dangerous object he had ever stood near.

"A way out," Ilsa said. "For the Rosen family. You won't know the name. Nobody was supposed to know it. Elias Rosen ran a shipping office in Hamburg until 'thirty-eight — " she caught herself, corrected, "— until the war took his business, and then his brother was arrested for something that wasn't a crime, and the family was listed for transport east. Transport east means what you think it means. Anton met the daughter at a border office where he was doing routine work, and something in her face — " Ilsa stopped. Pressed her lips together. "He came back and worked on this for eleven months. Eleven months, Casper. Every clause has to survive scrutiny by three separate authorities who hate each other, because the beauty of it is that they'll each assume another office verified it. It exploits the fact that empires don't talk to themselves. It's the best work he ever did, and it's why he can never stop, because as long as this exists unfinished, the Rosens are alive in the space between the lines."

"How many are there? The family?"

"Five. Two of them children."

Casper looked at the document. Half its fields were empty. The seals were drafted but unstamped. The final page — he turned to it — was blank except for the spaces where signatures would go, ruled and waiting, like a bed made up for guests who hadn't arrived.

Beneath the folder, a single sheet of paper, folded once. Ilsa unfolded it and read it silently first, and whatever crossed her face made Casper brace himself before she handed it over.

Anton's hand. Unmistakably. The same hand that had written the letters in the satchel, the same small exact letters that had built a cathedral out of lies:

*Ilsa — if you're reading this alone, then I'm delayed, or worse, and the choice is yours now, which I'm sorry for, because I never wanted it to be yours.*

*Finish it before the guns stop. After that, paper means nothing. Borders will harden overnight and every clerk will grow a conscience and a memory. While the war runs, they're too busy to be careful. Peace is the deadline. Not mine — theirs.*

*Don't trust anyone who offers help quickly. Trust anyone who hesitates and helps anyway.*

*And if Nakamura comes — and he will, because he's the only person alive stubborn enough to follow my handwriting into a fish shed — tell him the lamp was always the honest work. Tell him I envied it.*

Casper read it twice. Then a third time, standing in the lamplight in a dead friend's secret room, while the wind leaned on the cannery walls and the sea worked the shingle outside like a slow jaw.

"He envied the lamp," Casper said. It came out as accusation, though he hadn't meant it as one.

"Yes."

"I spent four years telling myself the lamp was enough. That keeping my head down and my light burning was a kind of virtue." He set the note down on the bench, carefully, aligning its edges with the blotter, because his hands needed something orderly to do. "And he was in a hole like this, forging hope for five strangers, envying *me*."

"He wasn't envying your lamp." Ilsa took the half-finished document from him and squared it, and began, with movements worn smooth by repetition, to lay out the tools: the pens, the inks, the stamps, the blotter. "He was envying that you never had to decide whether you were a good man. You got to just *be* one. Men like Anton don't get that. Every honest line we forge is bought with a dishonest one somewhere else." She uncapped the iron gall ink and stirred it, and the smell rose sharp and ancient, the smell of monasteries and contracts and confessions. "Now sit down. Because the guns haven't stopped yet, and five people in a cellar in Hamburg are alive exactly as long as this page gets finished, and I can't do it alone."

"Why not? You said he taught you everything."

"He taught me everything except the part that matters." For the first time since the shingle, since the satchel, since the storm, Ilsa looked at him without measuring him for threat — looked at him simply tired, and young, and asking. "The final countersignature has to be a name no one has ever connected to Anton, to me, or to the network. A clean hand. A real man with a real life who chose this — because if it's ever traced, the trail has to end at someone the authorities will believe acted alone, out of conscience, and then believed himself into silence." She slid the pen across the bench toward him. "It has to be someone history never bothered with. Someone invisible."

Casper looked at the pen. It was a good pen, walnut-barreled, worn to the shape of Anton's fingers.

"I'm a lighthouse keeper," he said.

"You're a man whose best friend forged his own death and left you the hardest job in Europe." Ilsa pulled the second stool up to the bench. "Sit down, Casper. First lesson: how to hold the pen like you own your own name."

Outside, the fog was thickening off the water, and twelve miles inland, in a coastal village where a postmistress was sorting the evening mail, a stranger in a well-cut coat was asking careful questions about supply boats and missing crates — but neither of them knew that yet, and the lamp on the headland burned on behind them, faithful and far away, keeping its vigil over a keeper who had finally, at forty-six years old, begun to learn how heavy a signature could be.
