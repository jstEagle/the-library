## 7. Uniforms Make the Man

The pen, Ilsa said, was the whole war in miniature.

"You'd think it was the gun," she told him, laying three pens on the workbench like surgical instruments. "Everyone thinks it's the gun. But a gun can only kill you. A pen can make you into someone who never existed at all."

Casper sat down on the second stool. The cannery workshop smelled of iron filings and lamp oil and something faintly sweet he couldn't place — shellac, maybe, or the resin Anton had used to seal his inks. Three weeks ago he had never heard of this room. Now he knew where the light switches were without looking.

"Left to right," Ilsa said. "Which of these would you use for a military discharge?"

He picked up the middle one. It had a nib finer than the others and a weight in the hand that reminded him of Maren's sewing scissors — balanced, purposeful, made by someone who understood that tools should be honest even when their users weren't.

"Why that one?"

"Because it's the lightest," Casper said. "Discharge papers get handled by tired clerks. They want a pen that won't fight them."

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment. Then she took the pen out of his fingers, set it down, and picked up the heaviest one instead.

"Wrong," she said. "But interesting. You chose by sympathy — you thought about the clerk. Anton would have chosen by authority. A discharge is an order dressed as a kindness. You write it with a heavy hand because the man signing it outranks the tired clerk, and everything about the document has to say so." She handed the heavy pen back. "You'll learn two ways, then. My way and his way. Try not to mix them."

"I don't have a way yet."

"Everyone has a way. Most people just haven't been asked." She pulled a sheet of scrap paper across the bench — cheap stock, the kind ration notices came printed on — and wrote a single line of text across the top: *The bearer is authorized to travel.* "Copy that. Ten times. Don't think about what it means. Think about how it sits on the page."

He wrote it ten times. His first attempt was stiff, a schoolboy's line, each letter standing at attention. By the fourth repetition his wrist remembered something — the years of lighthouse logs, thousands of entries in wind and bad light, weather notation written fast and legible with one eye on the glass. By the tenth, Ilsa had gone quiet beside him.

She took the sheet and held it under the lamp.

"Again," she said. "Same line. But this time, imagine you're tired. Not bored-tired. Frightened-tired. A man writing orders at the end of a fourteen-hour shift who wants to go home."

He wrote it again, and this time something strange happened in his forearm, a loosening, as if the words weren't coming from his hand but passing through it from somewhere behind his ribs. The letters slanted slightly. The *z* in *authorized* lost its formality and became a quick efficient slash.

Ilsa laid the new line beside the old one.

"Now look," she said softly, "at what you just did."

* * *

They worked until the lamp oil needed changing, and then they worked past that, and Casper lost track of hours the way he used to lose track during storms — time measured not by clocks but by the completion of tasks. Ilsa spread before him the grammar of her trade: weights of paper, the watermark that could be faked with a wire screen and patience, why official ink aged blue-black while amateur forgery aged gray, how a stamp was less a design than a pressure, and pressure was less force than confidence.

"You're hesitating on the descenders," she said, watching his third attempt at a captain's signature. "The tails on g and y. You slow down because you don't know whose hand you're forging, so your body doesn't trust the motion. Pick a man. Give him a life. Then the letters will come from somewhere."

"Whose hand am I copying?"

"This one." She slid a specimen card across the bench. A signature at the bottom: *H. Vogel, Hauptmann.* Above it, in the same hand, two lines of a requisition order.

Casper studied it. And here was the thing he would think about later, lying awake in the cannery loft with the sea audible even twelve miles inland — a low muttering like a conscience he couldn't quite silence. He studied Hauptmann Vogel's handwriting the way he had once studied tide tables, and within minutes he wasn't studying it anymore. He was *reading* it. He could feel the man: a career officer, precise but impatient, someone who dotted his i's hard because softness had been drilled out of him at thirteen. The letters leaned forward like a man perpetually about to stand.

"He's real," Casper said. "This isn't invented. This is a real officer's hand."

"Anton collected them," Ilsa said. "Years of them. Real hands, real lives. He said you can't forge a man honestly unless you respect the original." She paused. "Vogel died at Verdun. His widow writes us letters we answer with forged allotment payments, so she thinks the army still honors him. Somewhere there are three men wearing Vogel's rank and his signature, moving through checkpoints, and none of them ever met him."

Casper copied the signature. Once. Twice. On the fifth attempt Ilsa put her finger on the page beside it, not touching his letters, just indicating.

"There," she said. "That one. If I hadn't watched you write it, I couldn't have told you which was yours."

He looked at the two signatures side by side and felt nothing — and that was the frightening part. No guilt arrived. No vertigo. Just the quiet craftsman's satisfaction of a job true, the same feeling he got when the lamp mechanism clicked through its rotation clean as a heartbeat. He had spent four years keeping ships off the rocks, and now he had learned in one night how to steer men onto them, and his hands didn't know the difference between the work.

"Stop," Ilsa said, watching his face.

"I'm fine."

"You look like a man reading his own obituary in a foreign language. Stop anyway. We eat, then sleep, then uniforms."

"Uniforms?" he repeated.

She was already moving toward the crates stacked against the far wall. "Tomorrow you become Vogel. Tonight you become a man who sleeps."

* * *

The uniform came out of its wrapping paper the next morning smelling of camphor and another man's decade.

It was a captain's field tunic, tailored — not issued, tailored; the shoulders were cut for a broader frame than Casper's and Ilsa clucked at the fit, pinning the seam at his back with quick fingers. The wool was dense, expensive, the kind of cloth that announced itself. Brass buttons stamped with the crown and the number of a regiment Anton must have chosen deliberately, some unit whose officers rotated through coastal postings, plausible, unremarkable, forgettable.

"Hold still." Ilsa worked the last button at the collar. Her face was very close, expressionless, professional, and Casper realized he had stopped breathing. "There," she said, stepping back. "Stand up straighter. Vogel stands up straighter."

He stood up straighter, and the strangest thing happened, which was that standing up straighter felt *earned*. The tunic did something to his spine, settled weight across his shoulders like a yoke that had always been waiting for him. In the fogged window of the cannery office a man looked back at him whom he did not know: taller somehow, grayer at the temples in the right way, a face arranged into the mild permanent displeasure of authority.

"Say something," Ilsa said.

"What should I say?"

"Anything. Orders. Vogel gives orders the way other men give directions."

Casper cleared his throat. "The requisition will be processed by Thursday. See that it is."

The voice that came out of him was lower than his own. Calm. Bored, slightly. The voice of a man who had never in his life doubted that things would be processed by Thursday.

Ilsa laughed — short, startled, the first laugh he'd heard from her. "God. Do it again."

"No."

"Why not? It was perfect."

"Because it was perfect." He caught his own eye in the window, the stranger's eye, and made himself look away. "A minute ago I gave an order and some part of me expected to be obeyed. There's no Vogel here. There's just me in a dead man's coat, and my spine believed it. That's what frightens me — not that I could be caught. That I could get used to it."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. She gathered the pins back into their tin, one by one, taking longer than she needed.

"Anton used to say that," she finally said. "Almost word for word. He said the uniform isn't the danger. The danger is the hour when you take it off and feel relieved to be small again — because relief means part of you preferred the coat." She looked up at him. "He never took it off lightly. Neither should you."

"And you? Do you wear them?"

"When I have to." Something crossed her face, there and gone. "I'm better at women's hands than men's. Less to prove in the strokes."

Outside, the rain started again, drumming on the cannery roof in sheets, and beneath it Casper heard — or imagined he heard — the distant sound of wheels on the wet road below the embankment, an engine laboring uphill. He moved to the window. Through the streaming glass he saw nothing but gray, the road dissolving fifty yards out, but the sound persisted: slow, careful, stopping once as if the driver consulted something. A map. Directions.

"The mill road," Ilsa said behind him. She had heard it too. She was already at the crate, sliding the specimen cards back into their oilcloth sleeves. "Nobody uses the mill road anymore except—"

She stopped.

"Except quartermasters counting inventory," Casper finished.

She looked at him, and he saw the fear come into her face and watched her master it, fold it away like a document into its sleeve. Then she turned back to the workbench and began, with steady hands, putting away the pens in their exact order.

"Take the coat off," she said quietly. "Carefully. Fold it like Vogel would — he'd never let a tailor see wrinkles. We may need it sooner than Thursday."

Four years since her death, and his signature had been shrinking for four years, letter by letter, and he had never once noticed, because who reads their own name?

He covered it with his palm, the way you'd cover a wound, and went back to Vogel. Vogel was easier. Vogel had no wife buried under a rowan tree above the tide line. Vogel slept fine.

He climbed back to the loft ashamed, and could not have said exactly what the shame was attached to: the forging, or the relief of it.

"And if it doesn't stop?"

"Then we've been given tomorrow." She slung the satchel across her body and looked at the folded tunic in his arms. "Take Vogel with you. Learn his walk on the way.
