## 8. Brandt

The train did not go all the way to the coast. It stopped at a junction called Vesterhavn Halt, where the platform was a plank of rotting wood and the waiting room smelled of paraffin and wet wool, and from there Quartermaster Erich Brandt walked the last twelve miles because he had learned long ago that roads told you things trains never would.

He walked well, too. That was the first thing anyone noticed about him, if they noticed anything at all — that a man of his rank, a man with ledgers to keep and men to command, should arrive on foot like a pilgrim, coat brushed, boots polished to a dull military shine, carrying nothing but a leather case and an umbrella he did not open against the drizzle. Charm began with restraint. A man who arrived loudly had already spent his welcome. A man who arrived quietly could spend it wherever he liked.

The road ran through country that had been picked clean by four years of war. Fences missing pickets. A field ploughed and never sown, going soft with rushes. A boy of perhaps ten driving two skeletal cows, who looked at Brandt's uniform and stepped off the road into the ditch without being asked. Brandt nodded to him pleasantly. The boy did not nod back.

*Another debt*, Brandt thought, and made no note of it. Some debts were too small to be worth the ink. But he filed the boy away anyway, in that vast unlit room behind his eyes where he kept everything.

At the top of the last hill the sea appeared, grey on grey, and below it the village: forty houses, one church, one harbour, one street. And there, set back from the shore on its black spine of rock, the lighthouse — white tower, red lantern gallery, standing up into the weather like a finger raised in warning or accusation. The lamp was dark. It was afternoon; that meant nothing. He had read the shipping notices. In this season, on this coast, the lamp burned eighteen hours a day, storm or no storm.

Brandt had come eleven hundred kilometres on the strength of a number. That was how he preferred to explain his work to himself: not as hunting, not as vengeance, but as arithmetic. Somewhere between the front depot at Kowel and the rear echelon at Thorn, a crate had gone missing. Crate 114, manifested as *printing supplies, salvage*, weight six hundred kilos, consigned for destruction. Six hundred kilos of paper, ink, type, dies, stamps — the entire portable anatomy of a forgery shop — had entered the railway system and never exited it. Men had died for less. Men *would* die for less; that was simply a fact of the profession, like weather.

But numbers were only the beginning of a ledger. Ledgers recorded debts, and debts required names, and names required conversation. Brandt was very good at conversation.

* * *

The post office was also the general store, which was also, judging by the smell, partially somebody's kitchen. Brandt pushed open the door and a bell rang, thin and cheerful, absurdly domestic, and a woman came out from behind the counter wiping her hands on her apron.

She was perhaps fifty, built like the coastline itself — weathered, immovable — with quick grey eyes that took in his uniform in a single flick and settled on his face instead. Good. Most people looked at the uniform. The intelligent ones looked at the man wearing it, because the uniform only ever said the same thing.

"Fräulein—" he consulted his notebook, though he did not need to, "—Voss. Hedwig Voss."

"Postmistress," she said. "And grocer. And I mind the telephone when it works, which is never. You'll be wanting something, Captain."

"Quartermaster," he corrected gently, smiling. "Erich Brandt. And what I want, Fräulein Voss, is directions — though I find I've already got them. Yours is the last house before the headland, they told me at the junction. Which means you see everything that comes through this village before anyone else does. Which means—" he spread his hands, "—you're the most informed person on this coast, and I'd be a fool to ask anyone else first."

Flattery, but load-bearing flattery, the kind that held up actual architecture. Her chin lifted a centimetre. He watched her decide to enjoy being spoken to like a person of consequence, and he watched her simultaneously distrust enjoying it, which was better still. Suspicion and vanity pulling against each other kept a subject perfectly still, like a bird under the eye of a cat that has not yet decided to pounce.

"You'll be wanting tea anyway," she said. "It's a cold walk from the halt. Everyone comes off that halt looking half-drowned."

"That's kind of you."

"It's not kind. It's custom." She poured tea from a pot that lived permanently on the stove, in a cup she did not wash first. He noted that: either she was confident in her cleanliness or indifferent to his opinion of it. Both were useful signs.

He drank, complimented the tea, set down the cup, and did not open his case. This was the discipline. Never show the instrument until the patient is already on the table.

"I'm chasing paperwork," he said. "You'll have seen the war produces more paper than bullets. Requisitions, manifests, death notices." A small pause, measured to the millimetre. "I'm with the quartermaster corps auditing a discrepancy. A shipment that left the rail system near here some months ago. It's dull work. It involves asking everyone the same boring questions until somebody remembers something they didn't know they knew."

"And what do boring questions look like?" said Hedwig Voss. "So I know whether I've heard any lately."

He laughed, warmly, genuinely delighted. She was good. She was better than good. He made a mental note beside her name — *intelligent, hostile, alone?* — the way another man might note a fine vintage.

"They look like this," he said. "Has anyone new come to the village since autumn? Anyone washed up, walked in, bought a ticket out? Anyone writing letters under a name you didn't recognize?"

"No one new," she said. Too fast — not much too fast, a half-second too fast, the kind of too fast that only existed if you had spent years listening to answers the way a jeweller listens to glass. "We're not a village people come to. We're a village people are trying to leave."

"Mm," said Brandt. "Of course." He let the silence sit while he re-buttoned his coat, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world, because appearing to have all the time in the world was itself a form of pressure. "Then just this. If anything odd passes through your post office — letters without proper postage paid by someone who pays properly, mail collected rather than delivered, a name written in a hand that changes — you'll send word to me at the inn at Torshavn. Nothing official. Just neighbourly. I pay for neighbourliness, Fräulein Voss. Not much. But something."

"I don't need paying."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "People who need paying are the ones you have to watch." He stood, put on his cap, and paused at the door as if struck by an afterthought. "Oh — one more thing. Purely administrative. There was a courier attached to my department. A woman. Clever, quiet, quite remarkable with documents. She went missing in this direction in October. If she passed through here, even for an hour, even for a stamp—"

"October," said Hedwig Voss. "Storm month. Half the coast road was underwater. Nobody passed anywhere."

"Naturally," said Brandt, and smiled at her with real appreciation, because lying that smoothly deserved admiration even from its audience. "Then I've wasted your tea. Thank you for it."

The bell rang cheerfully again behind him.

* * *

He walked the harbour wall while the light failed, hands clasped behind his back, and let the village look at him looking at it. This too was method. A quartermaster who wandered openly gave everyone time to hide things — but hiding takes effort, and effort leaves traces, and by morning every hurried concealment in forty houses would be a small disturbance in the ordinary rhythm he was now calibrating. Let them hide. He was not here for what they hid. He was here for the shape their fear made.

The innkeeper at Torshavn gave him the best room without being asked, which told Brandt the telegraph line to the district office had been busy ahead of him. Good. Reputation preceded him everywhere now; it did half his work. He ate herring and potatoes, praised them beyond their merit, asked after the fishing, learned three boat names and two grudges, excused himself early, and sat by his window with the case open on his knees.

Inside the case, wrapped in oilcloth, was the ledger.

It was not an official document. It was bound in grey board, unmarked, and its columns were ruled in Brandt's own hand, and its contents would have interested a court-martial considerably more than Crate 114 ever could. Because Erich Brandt had understood something early, somewhere in the third year of the war, sitting in a requisitions depot surrounded by starving records: that paper was the true currency of the war, and he was its banker. Every forged transit pass he had sold to a frightened staff officer. Every desertion he had expunged from the rolls for a price. Every cargo he had diverted, every name he had struck and resold, every family that had paid him in silver, in favours, in silence, to make a son legally alive or legally dead. Four years of it. All of it recorded here, in pencil so fine it looked almost like regret.

And threaded through those pages, like red thread through grey cloth, one name recurring: *Ilsa*. His forger. His finest instrument. The woman who had made dead men's passports so beautiful they wept in the border offices. Who had sat across from him for two years with steady hands and steady eyes, forging the machinery of other people's escapes, until the day she walked out of the Thorn depot with a look on her face he had catalogued but not, at the time, understood.

He understood it now. It had been contempt. He had simply never been able to read contempt in someone who was smiling.

She had taken things when she went. Small things. A die. A specimen book. Enough to work with, enough to survive on. And then, months later, Crate 114 had vanished — vanished in exactly the manner that suggested professional help, vanished toward this coast, toward a workshop he had reason to believe existed here once and might exist still. Anton Weissler's workshop. The great Anton, officially dead these two years, mourned in three capitals by people who did not know they were mourning a fiction.

*Ilsa*, Brandt wrote in the margin of today's page, and beneath it: *coast confirmed. Village: forty houses, one church, one harbour, one lighthouse. Postmistress lying about October.*

He considered the postmistress a moment longer. Hedwig Voss, lying badly and knowing she was lying badly, lying anyway — for whom? For the woman, or for whoever sheltered the woman? A village like this had perhaps thirty souls who could hide a stranger: the fisherman with the spare bunk, the pastor with his cellar, the keeper with his tower. He would visit each of them in turn over the coming days, charmingly, boringly, asking the same questions until somebody remembered something they didn't know they knew.

And then there was the matter of the boy on the road with the two thin cows, and a hundred smaller entries, and the whole grey arithmetic of the ledger, which Brandt consulted nightly the way other men consulted scripture. People imagine cruelty requires passion. Brandt had found the opposite. Passion made errors. What he felt — reviewing accounts, balancing columns, planning collections — was closer to tidiness. The war had scattered debts across Europe like a dropped purse scatters coins, and he was simply the man walking along picking them up.

A debt, properly administered, was a kind of order. He believed that sincerely. He suspected, sometimes, in the hour before sleep, that this belief was the single most dangerous thing about him, and then he slept perfectly well.

* * *

In the morning it blew hard from the northwest, and the lighthouse lamp burned pale against the grey, sweeping its slow arm across the water. Brandt stood on the inn steps in the wind, coat buttoned, watching it.

Twelve miles of bad road, one crate of paper, one runaway forger, one dead man who refused to stay dead, and one lighthouse keeper whose file — he had pulled it in the district office — read like the biography of a ghost. Casper Nakamura. Widower. No party. No debts on record. No letters in or out but the supply chits. A man who had arranged his life so precisely that nothing stuck to it.

Brandt found that interesting. In his experience, invisibility was never free. Sooner or later every invisible man received an invoice.

He closed the ledger, tucked the case under his arm, and set off along the harbour wall toward the headland, whistling — softly, tunelessly, cheerfully — a little song the wind took and shredded and carried out to sea.
