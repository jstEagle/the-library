## 10. The Postmistress's Silence

The postmistress's name was Fru Vik, and she had hands like a woman who had spent thirty years licking envelopes and judging people by their handwriting. Ilsa had noticed the hands first, back in the village, when they'd stopped at the post office to ask about roads to the cannery. She'd noticed something else too — the flicker of recognition in Fru Vik's eyes, there and gone like a fish turning under ice.

Ilsa had walked out of that post office certain of two things: the woman knew her face, and the woman would say nothing. Not yet. People who said nothing right away wanted something, and they liked to decide how much it was worth.

Now, four days later, Fru Vik came up the path to the cannery with a market basket on her arm and the wind pulling her shawl sideways, and Ilsa watched her come through the salt-fogged window and thought: *there it is. The price.*

Casper was at the lighthouse. That was its own mercy. He would have made this harder by being kind.

* * *

Ilsa met her at the door of what had been the cannery's counting room, where they'd set up Anton's work table among the rusted scales. She didn't invite the woman in. Let her stand in the wind a moment. It told you more about people than an hour of talk.

"You're the Nakamura man's guest," Fru Vik said. Her eyes went past Ilsa's shoulder into the dim interior, cataloguing. "They say he keeps to himself."

"They say right."

"And you keep to yourself with him." The postmistress set the basket down on the step with deliberate care. Bread wrapped in wax paper, a jar of preserved plums, a twist of tea. Hospitality as invoice. "I lived in Bergen for six years before I married and came here. My sister ran a boarding house near the harbor quarter. I used to help her with the books."

Isla said nothing. The sea filled the silence, working at the rocks below the cannery with its patient, mindless industry.

"There was a girl at the boarding house," Fru Vik went on, unbothered by the quiet. "Came in the spring of '16. Kept to her room, paid in cash, spoke three languages when she thought no one was listening. The landlady asked me to look at a letter the girl wanted posted — to check the address was right for Copenhagen. It was right. It was *more* than right. The address was perfect, the hand was perfect, everything about it was perfect, and nothing is ever perfect, so I asked her where she'd learned to write like a notary." Fru Vik's mouth did something that was almost a smile and wasn't. "She said: from a man who could make a lie so true even the liar believed it."

The wind pushed between them. Ilsa kept her face still, kept her breathing slow. Somewhere behind her ribs, an old calculation turned over: distance to the road, weight of the woman, likelihood of anyone hearing anything out here. She put the calculation away, disgusted with herself and unsurprised. Old habits were patient things.

"That doesn't sound like me," Ilsa said. "I can barely write my own name."

"I know your name," Fru Vik said quietly. "Not the one you use now. The other one. The one that would interest a quartermaster who has been asking very careful questions in my post office these past three days."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree and settle. *Brandt.* Here already. Three days of careful questions while she and Casper worked ink and paper twenty miles inland like moles. "What kind of questions?"

"The kind a charming man asks while looking at your stamp drawer. Where do letters from the coast go, how are parcels weighed, who signs for military correspondence. He bought forty øre worth of stamps he clearly didn't need. He complimented my penmanship." Fru Vik's chin lifted. "He has a list, that man. I've seen him show it to himself, the way men do when they want to be reminded of their own importance. Your name is on it. Or the name I think is yours."

"Then why," Ilsa said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, "are you standing on this step telling me so?"

"Because I have a son," Fru Vik said, "in my cellar."

* * *

His name was Eirik. Nineteen years old. He had been at the front eleven months when, in his twelfth month, his company was ordered over the top a third time in a single week, and Eirik Vik walked into the trees during an artillery barrage and kept walking. Desertion in wartime. The penalty was a wall and a firing squad, if they caught him; a cell and slow ruin if they were feeling lawful.

"He came home in September," Fru Vik said. They were inside now, at the counting-room table, the basket's bread unwrapped between them like evidence. "Half the weight he was. His hands shake unless he's doing something with them. He mends nets now. There are no nets here. He mends them anyway and we burn them for kindling." She smoothed the front of her dress as if it had creased, though it hadn't. "I hid him in the cellar and I told the village his leave was extended, and I wrote to the army and lied in my own handwriting, which is the one sin I never thought I'd commit, and I have lain awake every night since listening for boots on my stairs."

"Why tell me?" Ilsa asked. "Why not just take what you know about me to Brandt and trade it? A deserter's mother with information about a forger on his list — he'd pay. Maybe he'd even deal. Papers for papers."

It was the obvious play. Ilsa would have made it herself, once. Fru Vik looked at her with those envelope-judging eyes and said, "Because Brandt doesn't deal. He collects. Ask anyone who has ever been in his ledger. There's no bottom line where he forgives the debt. He just moves it to a different page." She paused. "And because Anton trusted you. And Anton, whatever else he was, was never wrong about people. Only about himself."

The mention of Anton landed the way it always did now — a fist under the breastbone, brief and total. Dead Anton. Drowned-in-the-telegrams Anton. Anton whose handwriting she carried in a satchel against her body like contraband scripture. She hadn't told Casper everything yet. She was aware, with a kind of clinical detachment, that she was running out of reasons not to.

"What do you want?" Ilsa asked.

"A discharge," Fru Vik said. "A genuine-looking one. Medical. His father died of the lung sickness; the army knows our blood is weak. Give the lie some family to stand on." She reached into her basket again and produced a folded paper — Eirik's real call-up notice, his regiment, his number, his name in army type and his mother's address in a schoolteacher's careful hand. "You'll need his particulars. I brought them. I've thought about this every night since September. Every detail. I know what I'm asking, and I know what it makes me."

"What does it make you?" Ilsa asked, because she needed to hear it.

"An accomplice." Fru Vik said it without flinching, the way you'd state your age. "That's what I came to say, girl. Not just the favor. The confession. You should know that whatever you do for me puts your neck beside mine on the block. If Brandt finds Eirik, he'll find out how Eirik's papers were made, and then he finds you, and then he finds the keeper. I've counted all of it. Three months of lying awake gives a person time to count." She pushed the call-up notice across the table. "I decided I'd rather hang for something than waste away for nothing. Now. Can you do it or can't you?"

* * *

Could she do it.

Ilsa sat alone in the counting room an hour after Fru Vik left, the call-up notice in front of her, and let herself feel the full shape of the trap she'd just walked into willingly, almost gratefully, and examined why.

Complicity spread. That was the thing nobody warned you about when you took up this trade. Anton had understood it from the beginning; it was practically his theology. *Every kindness has a signature,* he used to say, tapping a finished passport with two fingers. *Somewhere down the line, someone vouched for this lie, and now their name is in it too, and that's the whole secret, girl — documents don't bind societies together. Debts do.* She'd thought it was wit, back then. A joke with ink on its hands.

One discharge paper. But a discharge needed a regimental stamp, and a stamp needed provenance, and a medical discharge needed a physician's signature, and somewhere a clerk would file it, and someday, if anyone ever looked — and Brandt looked at everything — the thread would run backward from Eirik Vik's clean new future through his mother's cellar to this table to Ilsa's hands to the name on Brandt's list.

She should refuse. She should tell Fru Vik the risk was too great, send her home with her plums and her tea, protect the operation, protect Casper, protect the unfinished document sleeping in its oilcloth upstairs that might, before the guns stopped, carry a whole condemned family across a border.

Instead she found she was already reaching for the good paper.

It wasn't sentiment, or not only. It was arithmetic. Fru Vik controlled the post. Every letter that reached the coast passed under her stamp; every inquiry Brandt made by mail would pass through her fingers first. A silent ally in the post office was worth more than any document Ilsa could forge, because silence couldn't be counterfeited. Either a person chose it or they didn't.

But that was the ledger's version, and Ilsa had learned, over four years of making lies true enough to believe, to distrust her own ledgers. Underneath the arithmetic sat something simpler and more dangerous: Fru Vik had stood in her doorway and confessed to being an accomplice before asking for anything, and Ilsa could not remember the last time anyone had handed her trust before payment. Trust freely given was the rarest currency in the war economy. You didn't refuse it because accepting it was expensive. You accepted it because refusing it meant you had already lost.

Besides. There was Eirik in the cellar, nineteen years old, mending nets that didn't exist. She knew about cellars. She knew about waiting for boots.

* * *

She started with the signature.

Medical discharges rose or fell on the physician's hand — a stamp could be copied, but a doctor's signature had a pulse to it, fatigue and hurry and small vanities baked into the loops. She'd studied hundreds. She closed her eyes and summoned the one she needed: Dr. Halvorsen of the coastal garrison, whose papers she'd handled in Bergen two years ago, a tired man who crossed his t's low, like someone bracing himself, and put a little tail on his final consonant as if signing off mid-sigh.

She practiced it nine times on scrap. On the tenth, she stopped feeling the imitation and started feeling the man — his sore eyes, his stack of hopeless cases, his private conviction that half his signatures were wasted on boys bound for the same wall Eirik had fled. Forging well required believing briefly in the person you stole from, and she had stolen from Halvorsen's compassion, which felt less like theft than like lending.

Then the particulars. Regimental number, correct. Company, correct. She added the father's death certificate reference — Fru Vik had thought of everything, even the registry number, copied in her schoolteacher's hand — and the diagnosis: chronic pulmonary illness, aggravated by service, unfit for further duty. The lie stood up on its own legs. It had family. Good lies, Anton taught, weren't invented; they were adopted.

She was sealing the last edge of the stamp impression when Casper's step sounded in the doorway — she heard it before she saw him, that soft lighthouse-keeper's tread, a man accustomed to not disturbing things — and she slid the discharge under a blotter with a speed that was itself a confession.

Casper looked at the blotter. Looked at her. He was getting better at reading her silences; she hadn't decided whether that pleased or alarmed her.

"Fru Vik came," he said. Not a question. News traveled strangely on the coast; he'd probably heard it from a fisherman, or seen the basket's contents already laid out in the kitchen.

"She came," Ilsa said. "She recognized me. In the village, that first day. She's known since."

"And?"

"And she wants papers. Discharge papers. Her son deserted in September. He's in her cellar." Ilsa pulled the blotter aside and let him see the document, because hiding it now would cost more than showing it. "I finished it. She'll have it tomorrow."

Casper came to the table slowly. He studied the discharge the way he studied everything — lamp-keeper's patience, checking the flame against the wind. "It's good," he said finally, and there was something reluctant in the admiration. "Whose hand is this?"

"A garrison doctor's. Dead tired, hates his job, secretly glad to sign a boy out of the war."

"No." Casper touched the edge of the paper, not the ink. "Yours. The hand. When you work, your whole face goes somewhere else. I've been watching." He straightened. "Brandt will trace it eventually. You said yourself — he looks at everything."

"He will. Months from now, maybe. After the guns stop, when paper means nothing anyway." She held his eyes. "But that's not what you came in here to say."

Casper took off his cap, which he did when something mattered — another thing she'd learned about him without meaning to. "The supply boat came today. First one since the storms began. It brought mail." He hesitated, and Ilsa felt the old alertness rise in her like water finding its level. "There was a letter at the post office. Addressed to the lighthouse. To me. The hand on the envelope—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "It's Anton's handwriting, Ilsa. I'd know it anywhere. He's writing to me. From an address outside Kristiansand." He set the envelope on the table between the forged stamp and the honest wax. "And either the dead have learned to write, or you've known more than you've been telling me."

She looked at the envelope. At that beloved, damning hand — the low crossings, the sighing tails — and understood exactly what Fru Vik had done, sliding it into the mail sack unsigned, a second favor, a nudge, a kindness with consequences attached.

Every kindness has a signature.

"Sit down, Casper," Ilsa said. "There's something about Anton I should have told you the night you pulled me out of the sea."
