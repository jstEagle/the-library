## 11. A Crate on the Night Tide

The letter had said *the night tide*, and the night tide did not care whether they were ready.

Casper stood at the edge of the shingle with the wind pushing at his back like a hand between his shoulder blades, and watched the water come in black and fast over the rocks he knew better than his own face. The moon was a smear behind cloud. Somewhere out there, if the letter could be believed — and it was in Anton's hand, so believing it felt like a kind of madness — a crate sat wedged in the sea cave below the old pilot's stairs, placed there two years ago by a dead man who apparently had not troubled himself to die.

"You're smiling," Ilsa said.

"I'm not."

"Your mouth is doing something. It hasn't done it since I met you." She pulled her collar up against the spray. "Don't do it yet. We haven't found anything but cold water."

They went down the pilot's stairs single file, Ilsa first because she insisted and because Casper had learned in the past week that insisting back cost more time than it saved. The steps were slick with weed, cut into the cliff by men whose names no one remembered, and at the bottom the cave opened its mouth to them: a low black arch that breathed salt and cold.

"The tide turns in forty minutes," Casper said. "After that the water reaches the roof line. Whatever we take, we take now."

Ilsa struck a match and cupped it. The flame showed him the inside of the cave — the ribbed stone, the tideline of kelp, and there, half-sheltered under an overhang where the spray couldn't reach, a shape with straight edges. Man-made edges. A crate perhaps a meter long, wrapped in tarred canvas, lashed down with rope that had been tied by someone who understood knots as a language.

Anton's knots. Casper recognized them the way you recognize a laugh through a wall.

*He taught me that hitch,* Casper thought, absurdly, standing in freezing water to his ankles. *Summer of 1909. He said a knot should tell you who tied it.* He had forgotten that for six years, and now it came back whole, and with it came the telegram — *Regret to inform you* — and the wooden cross with the painted name, and Maren holding his arm at the funeral while he stared at the cross and thought: *that's wrong, that's all wrong, they've spelled nothing right, not even the dates.*

He had thought it was grief talking. Grief always says the record is wrong.

"Casper." Ilsa's voice, low. "The rope."

She was already cutting it. He knelt beside her and together they dragged the crate up the shingle above the reach of the swell, and the weight of it told him before they opened it: paper. A great deal of paper. His heart was doing something he didn't authorize.

"We open it here or we don't open it," Ilsa said. "If Brandt's people are watching this coast, I'd rather they find us with empty hands than find the crate intact."

"And if it's nothing? If it's just paper?"

"Then we'll have carried nothing very far." But her hands were quick on the canvas ties, quicker than her voice wanted to admit, and Casper saw it — saw the same thing moving in her that was moving in him, that dangerous warm lift under the ribs, the thing he had spent two years teaching himself not to feel because wanting things was how the sea took them from you.

The canvas came away.

Inside: oilcloth bundles, packed tight as bricks. Ilsa lifted one out and unwrapped a corner, and the matchlight caught on the buff cover of a passport. Blank. Then another. Then a bundle of rubber stamps wrapped in wool, and a tin of ink cakes, and beneath those — she stopped.

Beneath those was a folder, and on the folder, in pencil, in that upright careful hand Casper would have known in the dark: *For whoever comes. Read the last page first.*

"He wrote to us," Casper said. His voice came out strange. "Two years ago. He sat somewhere dry and packed this and wrote to us."

"To whoever came. Don't flatter yourself yet." But she handed him the folder, and her hand stayed on it a moment longer than the handing required.

* * *

They read by matches, huddled against the cliff wall while the tide climbed the cave mouth inch by inch, and the wind overhead made a sound like the whole war being fought again at a distance.

The first pages were inventory. Casper skimmed them — stamps, plates, the locations of three caches, codes he didn't try to hold in his head — until Ilsa put her finger on a line and said, "Read this one aloud."

It was a list of names. Eleven names, each with a date and a place, written in columns neat as a hymnal. At the top of the list, in larger letters: *Moved out alive.* At the bottom, four names underlined twice: *Still waiting.*

"Families," Ilsa said quietly. "People he extracted. Papers, routes, money. This is what the workshop was *for*. Not the uniforms, not the passports — those were tools. This was the work." Her finger moved down the column. "The four at the bottom. The Brenners. That's the document in the cannery — the unfinished one. The family crossing papers. He never finished it."

"Because he died."

"Because he *stopped*," Ilsa said. "Read the last page."

Casper turned to it. The handwriting changed here — faster, pressed harder, the pen digging. As if the man writing it had run out of the patience that made his forgeries perfect.

*I am sorry for the lie. I know what a funeral costs the living; I have attended my own and watched the bill arrive. But a dead man can walk through checkpoints that stop the living, and there were eleven families, and I chose them. I would choose them again. If you are reading this, then either the war has ended or it is ending, and paper will soon mean nothing — which means everything I have must be used NOW, by someone the ledgers have never owned.*

*The crate you have found is the second key. The first is in the cannery. Take both. Finish the Brenner document. And do not look for me. Looking for me is how Brandt finds me, and Brandt finding me is how eleven names become four become none.*

*— A.*

Casper read it twice. The match burned down to his fingers and he let it, because the small pain seemed appropriate, seemed proportionate, and because if he moved he might do something undignified.

"He's alive," he said.

"It says not to—"

"He's *alive*, Ilsa." He heard his own voice crack on the word and didn't care. Two years. Two years of a wooden cross with misspelled dates, of Maren's arm under his hand, of a grief he had carried like a lamp he wasn't allowed to set down — and the whole time Anton had been walking around somewhere breathing, choosing, *choosing* to be mourned. Anger should have come first. He kept waiting for it. What came instead was worse and warmer: hope, arriving like blood back into a numb limb, painful and sweet and completely untrustworthy.

"He faked his own death," Casper said. "He let me stand at his grave. He let *Maren*—" Maren had liked Anton. Maren had cooked for him, argued with him about music, called him *that forger* with affection. She had been dead eight months when the telegram came, and Casper had grieved Anton alone in the lighthouse, and the grief had been one of the few things he still owned.

"Yes," Ilsa said. "He did. And it worked, which is why you're reading this instead of reading his file in Brandt's ledger." She took the letter from him gently. "Listen to me. I knew Anton for three years. I carried for him across two borders. He is the best man I have ever known and also the most ruthless, and those are the same fact, not a contradiction. He buried himself because it saved lives. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That I'm not allowed to be glad he's alive."

"That gladness is a door and Brandt is standing on the other side of it." She folded the letter away into her coat, next to the satchel she still wouldn't surrender even to him. "Every person who learns Anton lives becomes a thread leading to him. You've just become one. So have I. So has this crate."

Casper looked at the crate. Blank passports — a dozen doors out of the world. Stamps. Ink. And underneath the oilcloth, when Ilsa lifted the last bundle, a flat wooden case that opened on a half-plate photograph: Anton, younger than the war, grinning like a boy caught stealing apples, one arm around a woman Casper didn't recognize and the other holding up a forged diplomatic pass as if it were a fish he'd caught.

And on the back, in pencil: *Tell Casper the lamp trick works. Tell him I'm sorry about the cross. Tell him to spell his own name right for once.*

Casper laughed. It came out of him sideways, half sob, entirely involuntary, and he turned his face to the cliff wall so Ilsa wouldn't see what his mouth was doing.

"The lamp trick," he managed. "There was a — in '09, he hid a message in the timing of the light. I was sixteen. I thought I'd invented it." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "He remembers. Two years dead and he remembers."

The tide was at their heels now, white lace crawling up the shingle. They repacked the crate — Ilsa taking the passports and stamps into her satchel, Casper shouldering the folder and the photograph case — and climbed the pilot's stairs with the water chasing their heels, and stood at the top gasping in the wind like two thieves, which is what they were.

* * *

Back at the lighthouse, Casper lit the lamp first — habit, creed, the one order the world still gave him that he trusted — and then sat with Ilsa at the kitchen table while the beam swung its slow white arm over the water, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

He should have been afraid. He catalogued the reasons: Brandt hunting the crate, the postmistress's silence fraying, the deadline in Anton's note — *before the guns stop* — now measured in days. He laid the fears out on the table of his mind like tools, the way he'd laid out Maren's things after the funeral, orderly and untouchable.

But under the fear, the other thing kept rising, and he finally let himself look at it straight.

He wanted Anton alive. Not as an idea — as a man, sitting at this table, arguing, drinking tea too hot, complaining about the quality of northern ink. He wanted to hear him say *you spelled your own name wrong again, Casper, honestly, a keeper who can't keep his own letters.* He wanted two years back. He wanted the cross in the churchyard to be a prop, a stage set, a lie — because if it was a lie, then maybe death itself had been exaggerated, maybe the sea gave things back, maybe—

Maybe Maren.

The thought arrived without permission and stopped his breath.

No. He shut it. Firmly, the way you close a door on weather. Maren was in the churchyard under a stone he had chosen himself, and the difference between Anton and Maren was the difference between a man who chose to vanish and a woman who hadn't chosen anything, and confusing the two would be a cruelty to her memory that he would not commit. He would not. Hope was an intoxicant; Anton's own letter said as much in its way; Ilsa had said it outright on the cliff. A drunk man at a lamp was a danger to every boat on this coast.

But God, the warmth of it. Sitting there in the lamplight with the blank passports stacked between them like a deck of cards, Casper felt something he had not felt since before the telegram, since before Maren: the future opening like a road. Finish the document. Save the Brenners. Find Anton. Stand in front of the man who had let the world bury him and say *I looked for you* — no, say *I didn't look for you, and you were right not to let me, and I forgive you anyway, and you owe me a summer of '09.*

"I can hear you planning," Ilsa said.

"Can you."

"Your jaw does it." She turned one of the blank passports over with one finger, not looking at him. "Tomorrow we go back to the cannery. We finish the Brenner document. We do not go looking for Anton, and if he contacts us, fine, but we don't chase him. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Casper said, and meant it the way a man means *yes* while already halfway out the door in his head.

Because here was the thing he didn't say: the letter said *do not look for me.* It did not say *do not leave a sign.* And Casper Nakamura had spent twenty years keeping a light that asked nothing and answered nothing, and he knew — professionally, in his bones — the difference between a signal and a search. A search goes out after a man. A signal just stands where it stands, and burns, and says *here, here, here* to anyone with eyes.

He was a keeper. Signals were his trade.

Ilsa gathered the satchel and went up to the room he'd given her, and her footsteps faded, and the house settled into its sounds — wind, beam, sea, the tick of the cooling stove. Casper sat alone with the photograph case open in front of him, Anton grinning his apple-thief grin across two years of death.

"All right," Casper said quietly, to the photograph, to the dark window, to whatever was listening. "You wanted someone the ledgers never owned. You wanted someone who never wanted power." He touched the edge of the case, once, like touching a shoulder. "You might have mentioned you were alive. But I'll finish it. And I'll spell my name right."

Outside, far down the coast toward the village, a light moved that was not a boat and not a star — a lantern, swinging along the cliff road in the small hours, steady and deliberate, going where no honest man had business going at two in the morning.

Casper watched it until it passed out of sight behind the headland, and the warmth in his chest cooled by one degree, and he began, quietly, to count the hours until dawn.

* * *

He did not sleep. At first light he woke Ilsa, and she read his face before he got a sentence out, and by the time the sun cleared the water they were on the inland road with the satchel between them, walking fast toward the cannery and the unfinished document and the four names still waiting —

— and neither of them saw the figure detach from the tree line behind them and follow, at a distance, with a ledger in his pocket and all the time in the world.
