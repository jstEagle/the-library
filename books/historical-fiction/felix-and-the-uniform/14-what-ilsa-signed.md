## 14. What Ilsa Signed

The rain had been falling for three days, and by the third day Ilsa had run out of small tasks to hide behind.

She re-inked the stamps twice. She sorted the blank passports by country and then resorted them by date series, which was a nonsense exercise because none of them were dated yet. She cleaned Anton's nibs one at a time in a tin cup of turpentine until her fingertips came away gray, and then she sat in the lamplight of the old cannery workshop with nothing left in her hands, and Casper across the table doing his letters — his slow, careful, maddening letters — and she understood that the moment she had been postponing since the shingle beach had finally arrived, wearing work clothes and patience, waiting for her like a debt collector.

He looked up from his practice sheet. "You're staring."

"I'm deciding something."

"Decide faster," he said mildly, and went back to the letter *g*, which still leaned too far east, and that small ordinary kindness — that he did not ask what she was deciding, only that she hurry — undid her more than any interrogation could have.

Because here was the arithmetic she could no longer avoid: Brandt was somewhere on the roads behind them, methodical as weather. The crate they'd pulled off the night tide sat under canvas in the corner like a sleeping dog. And the man across the table had handed her a forged travel document made partly in his own hand and had never once asked her who she actually was. Not her cover name. Not the courier's name. The real one, underneath all of it, the name she hadn't said aloud in nineteen months.

He deserved better than arithmetic.

"Casper." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "Put down the pen."

* * *

He put it down. He was good at following instructions when they mattered; it was one of the first things she'd trusted about him, back when trust was a word she used about other people the way you use a language you don't speak anymore.

"There are two things I have to tell you," she said. "And after I tell you the second one, you may want me off this island, or whatever this coast is, and I wouldn't blame you. But I need you to hear both of them before you decide anything. Can you do that?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them the cannery roof ticked and settled under the rain, the whole derelict building breathing like an old animal.

"Yes," Casper said.

"The first thing is simple." She kept her hands flat on the table. She had learned, years ago, that hands told the truth before mouths did, so she trained hers to be furniture. "I worked for Brandt. Before. For nearly two years. Not against him — for him. I was one of the people who made his paper."

Casper didn't move. That was worse than if he had flinched.

"You've seen what Anton can do," she went on, because stopping now would kill her. "The passports, the discharge books, the way he builds a life out of ink. Brandt wanted that skill attached to his office instead of loose in the world. So the quartermaster's section ran what they called a documentation bureau, and it wasn't forges and rifles the way the novels would have it, it was a room with good light and four desks, and I sat at the second desk from the window for twenty-two months, and I made papers that sent men where Brandt wanted them sent."

"Papers," Casper said carefully. "Travel documents?"

"Requisitions. Transit passes. Discharge certificates for men who weren't discharged — men who were listed as fit and then routed where the army needed bodies, so their families would receive cheerful letters saying their son had been honorably released from duty while their son was being loaded onto a train to a sector the maps had stopped naming." She heard her own voice go flat and administrative, matching the work, and hated it, and kept going. "That was the genius of it. Nothing we made was illegal. Every stamp was genuine, or forged to be indistinguishable from genuine, and every line pointed a person somewhere they didn't consent to go. You could hold one of my documents in your hand and it would feel like mercy. It felt like mercy. Men kissed my hand once, in the transit yard at Havel, because I'd given them paper that said they were going home."

"Where were they going?"

"Does it matter which sector?"

"No," Casper said quietly. "I suppose it doesn't."

"They were going where Brandt's ledger needed them to go. That's the whole of it. He doesn't hate anyone, Casper. That's the thing nobody believes until they've sat across a desk from him. He isn't cruel the way wolves are cruel. He's cruel the way ledgers are cruel. Everything balances. Everything gets collected. A man is a figure that owes a column, and my job was to write the routing slip." She swallowed. "I was good at it. That's the part I can't sand off. I wasn't coerced, I wasn't starving, I wasn't duped. I was twenty-four years old and I was the best junior hand in the bureau, and Brandt told me so every quarter, with a raise."

The rain filled the silence for her.

"How did you leave?" Casper asked. Not gently. Gently would have been unbearable.

"A woman came through the transit yard with two children and papers I had signed. I recognized my own *R*. I have a tell — everyone does, Anton says you can't train it out entirely, you just learn to spend it in places no examiner will look. Mine is the leg of the *R*. I curve it when I'm rushing, and I had been rushed that week." Her finger drew the letter on the tabletop without her permission. "She had two children and one coat between them and my curved *R* in her pocket, and I watched a sergeant read my document and wave her toward the eastbound platform, and I understood in about four seconds that I had built the machine myself, plank by plank, and that it worked beautifully, and that it had eaten her."

"And then?"

"Then I did what cowardly people do. I didn't burn anything down. I stole." A breath. "I copied the bureau's specimen book — every official seal, every watermark recipe, every serial-number sequence — and I walked out of my lodging one morning and didn't come back, and I took the specimens to the one man in the country whose work was better than mine. Who'd been fired from the same bureau three years before I joined it, which is how Brandt knew such work existed at all." She looked up. "You know the rest. Anton hid me. Trained me properly — taught me to forge *against* the machine instead of feeding it. And I carried his letters and his crates and I told myself every month that the ledger was balancing, that I was paying down the column I owed. That's not repentance, Casper. That's bookkeeping. I know the difference. I've done both."

Casper sat back. His face, which she had learned to read like weather over water — the squall-line of his brow, the flat calm he wore over grief — showed her nothing she could steer by.

"That's the first thing," she said. "Here is the second."

* * *

She rose, crossed to her coat hanging by the door, and took from the lining the folded page she had been carrying against her body since Havel, since before the storm, since the boat went down. The paper was soft as cloth now from sweat and salt. She laid it in front of him and turned the lamp so the light fell across it.

A list. Typed, mostly, with handwritten annotations in a small, beautiful, terrible script — Brandt's own, she could pick it out of a crowd of a thousand hands. Names. Some struck through. Some annotated with figures, dates, ledger shorthand. A list of debts.

"Every person who ever took paper out of his bureau without authorization," she said. "Deserters' families. Smugglers who moved his crates and skimmed. Clerks who sold him blank forms. And every person who defected with knowledge of how the bureau works." Her finger found the bottom third of the page. "There. Third from the end."

Casper bent close. The lamplight caught his lashes, the deep seams beside his eyes, the gray coming in at his temples that he was too young for — grief does that, she'd noticed; it ages men in patches, like damp getting into a wall.

He read the name aloud, slowly, the way you sound out a word in a foreign alphabet. "Ilsa Voss."

"My real name," she said. "Not the courier's name. Not the name on any passport I've carried since I left him. My name. The one my mother gave me in a town you've never heard of, before I learned that names are tools." She sat down again. Her hands stayed flat on the table, furniture, furniture. "It's the only thing of mine he still holds. He can't find me with it yet — I've burned everything that connects it to my face. But it's on his list, in his handwriting, and that means that as long as he's alive and collecting, there is a version of me that exists in one place only: in the ledger of Quartermaster Brandt, filed as a debt."

"And debts get collected," Casper said.

"When the war ends, he'll need new work. A man like that always lands on his feet; peace is just a market correction to him. And the first thing a collector needs, when everything else burns, is his list." She met his eyes. "Which brings us to the ugly part, the part I said you might want me gone for. Because it isn't only that my name is on his list, Casper. It's that I know how he thinks. I know his tells the way I know my own curved *R*. And there is a part of me — a large, well-lit, second-desk-from-the-window part of me — that has spent nineteen months telling myself I'm running *from* him, when the truth is I chose this coast, I chose these letters, I chose this whole rotten beautiful errand, partly because finishing Anton's document is the one signature that could take my name off any ledger forever. Including his."

She made herself say the last of it plainly, no artistry, no craft. Craft was the problem.

"I used you," she said. "Or I let myself hope I was using you. The widower who wants no history, the keeper who signs nothing — of course I picked you. You were the safest pair of hands in the country precisely because you didn't want the work. Anton wrote it in his note: *finish it before the guns stop, and give it to someone who never wanted power.* I read that note and I thought: I know exactly that man. He's keeping a lamp twelve miles up the bad road, and he'll never ask me my real name." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it, because hiding it would have been one more forgery. "So. Now you know what I signed, and whose name I carry, and why I'm really here. Say what you're going to say."

* * *

Casper was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she catalogued exits — the north door, the loading stair, the tide window — out of pure habit, and despised herself for the habit even while performing it.

Then he did something she had not anticipated, though perhaps she should have, because he had done it once before, on the night he found the letters and the ground opened under him: he reached for the teapot, poured two cups with steady hands, and pushed one across the table to her. As if confession were weather, and you waited it out indoors, together.

"Maren died in March of 'sixteen," he said. "You know that."

"I know that."

"For eight months before she died, she wrote me letters I didn't open. She'd gone to her sister's in the south for her lungs, and I stayed here for the lamp, and she wrote — I knew she was writing, the postmistress told me every week, *another letter from Maren, Casper* — and I stacked them unopened in the drawer under the logbook. Do you know why?"

Ilsa shook her head.

"Because her handwriting was changing. The early ones, I could see through the envelope — I let myself see, which was its own kind of sin — the hand was getting weaker, the letters sprawling. And I thought: if I read them while she's alive, then I'll have watched her disappear in her own lines, page by page, and I'll have done nothing, because I chose the lamp. If I waited until—" He stopped. Restarted. "I told myself I'd read them when it couldn't hurt her to be seen failing. That's what I called it. Being seen failing. Like reading someone's handwriting was a kindness I could owe them late, with interest."

"What changed it? What made you finally open one?"

"The crate," he said simply. "Finding Anton alive, or possibly alive. Wanting something again. Chapter by chapter, wanting things again — I'd forgotten you're allowed." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. "But here's what I keep learning, Ilsa. You think shame disqualifies you. You carry it around like a verdict, sealed, and you figure the decent thing is to keep it sealed so it can't contaminate anyone else. I did. Eight months of unopened envelopes, and I'll tell you what that was. It wasn't decency. It was me protecting myself from being known, and calling it protection of her."

Ilsa's throat closed.

"So no," Casper said. "I don't want you off this coast. I want to be honest with you, since apparently we're doing that tonight and it turns out honesty is like the sea — you can't get a little bit in it." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and clear. "You forged papers for a machine that ate people. I read death notices for two years and thanked God each time the name wasn't one I knew, and called that innocence. We're both clerks of the same cowardice, you and I. Yours just had better penmanship."

A sound came out of her that she didn't recognize at first — a short, cracked laugh, half sob, absurd in the lamplit ruin of a herring cannery at the edge of the world. He laughed too, briefly, helplessly, and then neither of them spoke, and the rain went on, and something that had been clenched in her chest since the transit yard at Havel loosened by one degree. Not gone. One degree.

"Your name," he said at last. "Voss. Does anyone else living know it?"

"Anton. And now you."

"Then we keep it that way." He said it like a keeper stating a rule of the light: fixed, unromantic, absolute. "Not hidden — I'm done with hidden. Kept. There's a difference. Hidden means the name is a weapon someone could find and use. Kept means it's yours, and we decide who earns hearing it. When this is finished — the document, the border, all of it — you should get to choose whether Voss survives the war or dies with it. Either way, you choose. Not Brandt. Not his list."

"And until then?"

"Until then, you sign your work like Anton taught you. Curved *R* and all." The ghost of a smile. "Spend the tell where no examiner will look."

She reached across the table before she could talk herself out of it and covered his hand with hers. He didn't pull away. Under the lamp, two sets of calluses — hers from pens, his from rope and brass — rested on cheap wood in a dead man's workshop, and it felt less like comfort than like terms agreed upon.

"It will cost us," she said. "Him knowing I'm alive. He'll come, eventually, and he won't send soldiers first. He'll send paper. A summons. Something official-looking. He knows I can't resist checking a document — it's a professional compulsion. He'll bait the hook with typography."

"Then we'll read his typography," Casper said, "and throw it in the sea."

She wished she shared his certainty. She wished it with her whole traitor heart.

Outside, the storm had begun to change its mind — the steady three-day rain thinning, the wind swinging north with a rising, glassy note in it that she didn't recognize but that she watched Casper recognize, his head lifting toward the dark like a man hearing his name called.

"That wind," he said slowly, standing, going to the tide window, "shouldn't be coming from the north. Not this time of year. Not with the barometer doing—" He checked the instrument on the shelf and went very still.

"What?"

"Nothing," he said, in the voice of a man lying badly, which for a forger's student was almost encouraging. "Nothing. But I want the boat secured and I want us back at the lighthouse by tomorrow night at the latest. There's weather coming that isn't on any chart, and if the supply boat or anyone else is caught on this coast when it lands—"

He stopped. Through the black square of the window, far down the drowned road toward the village, a light was moving. Not a lantern's swing. Steadier. Vehicle-borne, climbing the twelve miles of bad road at an hour when no legitimate traffic moved, its beams sweeping the hedgerows like fingers counting.

Ilsa knew, before he turned back to her, before either of them said a word, whose headlights those were. You sit at the second desk from the window for twenty-two months, you learn how the collector travels.

"He's not sending paper," she said.

"No," Casper said, reaching for his coat. "He's coming himself."
