## 15. The Storm of the Century

The barometer began to fall on Tuesday morning, and by noon it had fallen like a man jumping.

Casper stood in the lamp room with his eye to the glass and watched the horizon assemble itself into something with intent. The swell came in long and green from the northwest, then shorter, then confused — three seas arguing with each other, which was never a good sign. Gulls had come inland in great wheeling clouds, sitting sullen on the turf behind the tower like refugees who knew better than to say what they'd seen coming.

"Barometer's dropped forty points since breakfast," he told Ilsa.

She looked up from the worktable below, where she'd been laying out the final document page by page under the weight of Anton's brass rule. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. There was ink on her left knuckle — Prussian blue, the expensive kind, the kind Anton had hoarded like communion wine. She'd been working for two days with the focus of a surgeon, and she'd begun letting Casper do the fine strokes himself: the guilloche borders first, then the watermark shading, then, at last, the names. The family of six from the interior. Father, mother, four children, one of them an infant whose birth no office had ever thought to record.

Three days, maybe four, and it would be done. Then the run north to the cove where the contact would take it across the water.

"How much does that mean?" she asked, nodding at the barometer.

"That means," Casper said, "that by tomorrow night there won't be a boat on this coast that can choose its own course."

He went down and did the arithmetic out loud because saying things made them true or untrue faster than thinking them. The document needed perhaps sixty more hours of careful work. The weather window — if there was one — closed tomorrow evening. The contact would come to the cove on the first calm tide after receiving the signal, and the signal could not go until the papers were finished, because a man caught carrying half-finished papers was not a smuggler, he was evidence.

"So we work through tonight," Ilsa said. "Finish by tomorrow midday. Signal tomorrow evening."

"Or—" Casper stopped.

He heard himself stop, and hated the small hesitation, because he already knew what lived inside it. He turned and looked out the kitchen window at the tower, white paint gone grey at the seams, the gallery rail he'd replaced twice, the lantern glinting dully in daylight like a sleeping animal's eye.

"Or what," Ilsa said. Not a question. She'd read him faster than he'd read himself, the way she read everything.

"The lamp," he said. "If this is what the glass says it is, boats will be out in it. Fishing boats caught wide. Coastal traffic running for harbor. Maybe naval traffic." He didn't say the rest, but they both heard it anyway: *maybe a small boat running dark up this coast on the first decent night after the storm, carrying a man who expects a light at the headland.* "The rocks off the point don't care about armistices."

"You're not suggesting—"

"I'm saying the lamp comes first. It always has. That's the whole of the job. Maren used to say I married a tower and got her as a bonus." His voice did something strange on Maren's name; it did that lately, more each day, as if grief were thawing rather than healing. "Sixty hours of work against a hundred tons of herring boat with eleven men aboard. There's no sum to do, Ilsa. The lamp wins."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in a voice stripped of all its courier's smoothness, "Anton said you were the steadiest man he ever met. He also said you were the best at hiding, which he considered a kind of illness." She began squaring the pages on the table, tapping their edges together, over and over, a small violence of tidiness. "We finish nothing if Brandt finds this place while you're up in that lamp playing Good Shepherd."

"Then we hide it well."

"And if the contact runs the coast during the storm because he thinks the window's closing? If he dies on your rocks because your light was lit but your signal never flew?"

Casper hadn't thought of that. He felt the thought go through him like cold water through a boot. He put his palm flat on the table to steady something that wasn't the table.

"Then the lamp is all I can give him," he said finally. "A fair chance at the rocks instead of none."

* * *

The storm arrived at dusk on Wednesday, and it did not arrive so much as take possession.

By the time Casper had the auxiliary generator running and the main lens turning, the wind was past anything in his nine years of logs. It came in gusts now, not a blow but a series of blows, as if something enormous were striking the coast again and again with the flat of its hand. Rain went horizontal. The sea beyond the point was not sea anymore but a churning pale waste, and the air was full of spray torn off the crests and flung a quarter mile inland, so that the whole world seemed made of smoke and salt.

At eight o'clock the electric supply from the village line failed entirely, as he'd known it would, and the tower ran on the oil-vapor lamp and the clockwork rotation, the way it had in his father-in-law's day. Old technology. Honest technology. You wound it, it turned; you trimmed the wick, it burned. There was comfort in that which Casper accepted without examining.

Ilsa worked below. That had been settled between them with only a look: he would keep the lamp, she would keep the deadline, and neither would say aloud that the arrangement suited both their natures too well.

But at midnight, when he came down for his hour's rest, soaked through three layers, hands too numb to unbutton his coat, he found her at the table with her brush poised and unmoving over the page, staring at nothing.

"It's bad out there," she said.

"It's bad."

"There's a boat." She pointed at the seaward window, where the black was absolute except when the lightning made it brief and grey. "I've been watching. Or listening. Something out there, low and slow, engine note wrong for fishing."

Casper crossed to the window and stood with her, dripping onto the boards. For a long minute there was nothing. Then the wind gapped — it did that, held its breath for ten seconds at a time like a man listening at a door — and in the gap he heard it: a small engine laboring, close enough that it might be inside the bay already, or might be nowhere, or might be his own blood in his ears.

"If that's him," Ilsa said, "he's early, he's desperate, or he's lost."

"All three are the same thing in this weather." Casper was already moving, pulling the storm shutter wider, checking the signal locker: flare gun, hand torches, the Aldis lamp he kept for signaling fishing fleets. "Get the oilskin on the third hook. You'll hold the door."

"Casper—"

"He can't see the signal lamp through this. But he can see the tower light, and if he's got sense he'll stand off and wait. And if he hasn't got sense—" He checked the flare gun with fingers that wouldn't quite obey. "Then I'll put a red flare over the water every twenty minutes, and red means danger, stand clear, rocks ahead. It's all I can tell him from here."

They climbed the ninety-one steps. At the top, in the shuddering lantern gallery, Ilsa braced herself against the frame and Casper opened the service door onto a night that tried to take the door off its hinges and take his arm with it. The flare went up with a sound like tearing cloth, arced out over the black water, and burned a hard crimson circle onto the underside of the storm cloud. For four seconds the whole sea was visible: grey ridges the size of houses, and no boat anywhere.

Twenty minutes later, another flare. Nothing.

Between flares, Casper checked the rotation, wiped spray from the storm panes — the salt built up on the outside faster than a man could wipe it, so he wiped anyway, because wiping was a form of prayer — and fed the wick. Ilsa stood inside the lantern with her back straight and her jaw set, and once he caught her with both hands pressed flat against the glass, looking down into the dark, lips moving. Whether she was praying or cursing he couldn't tell and didn't ask.

At the fourth flare, just as the red light guttered and died, the wind gapped again, and this time the engine was unmistakable, and closer, and then it stopped.

Not faded away. Stopped. As though someone had cut it to listen.

"He's in the lee of the point," Casper breathed. "God help him, he's actually in the bay."

"Is that safe?"

"Nowhere is safe. But the bay is a arithmetic problem and the open water is a certainty. He knows the ground. Or he doesn't, and we'll know in about a minute."

They waited. The tower turned its beam out over the chaos, patient, mechanical, indifferent to everything except geometry. Casper realized his hands were shaking and pressed them against his thighs.

Then, through the gap of a single enormous wave, he saw it — a smudge of darker darkness lifting on a crest, slewing sideways, and a figure at a tiller, and then it was gone behind the water and he was shouting without deciding to shout: "He's making for the old slip! He's seen the light — he's steering on us!"

The next three minutes lasted a season. Casper kept the beam steady on the headland marker rather than sweeping, breaking every rule of the service, because a fixed light was a bearing and a bearing was a course, and somewhere down there a man with a tiller was betting six lives' worth of cargo and his own on Casper's light meaning what lights are supposed to mean: *here. land. not yet. not here.*

The sea took the boat twice. Casper saw both times — saw it lift the hull and slam it down and swallow the figure at the tiller, saw it right the whole business like a hand flipping a toy, and each time the smudge reappeared, lower, closer, still moving toward the slip under the cliff path. On the third wave it simply wasn't there anymore, and the bay was empty black, and Ilsa made a sound Casper had never heard from her.

Then a torch blinked from the base of the cliffs. Twice. Deliberate. Alive.

"They're in," Ilsa said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "They're in, they're under the cliff — Casper, they're in."

Casper discovered he was leaning against the lantern frame with all his weight. Ninety-one steps below, the document lay unfinished on a table. Somewhere south, Brandt was moving — Ilsa had been certain of it, reading the pattern of the last week the way she read tides. And in the lee of his point, soaked, half-drowned probably, bruised by his own luck, sat a stranger with a boat who would need warmth, dry clothes, and a reason to trust two people he'd never met, before the next gust decided his mooring line's future.

"Well," Casper said. His heart was going like the clockwork drum. "It seems the lamp has opinions about the deadline after all."

* * *

Getting down the cliff path in that wind was the single most foolish thing Casper had done in nine years of keeping other people alive, and he did it anyway, with Ilsa behind him carrying the oilskin bundle of dry clothes and the storm lantern shielded in her coat. The path was a stream. The stream was a waterfall. Twice the wind picked Casper's shoulder as if choosing him up to inspect and finding him insufficient.

The slip was chaos and spray, and in the chaos a rowboat's hull — no, a gaff-rigged open boat, tarred canvas over frames, absurd, heroic — ground itself on the stone with each retreating wave, and men moved around it like shadows with ropes.

There were five of them. Not one courier. Five.

The tallest came up the slip toward the light of Casper's shielded lantern, and Casper saw that he was young, maybe twenty-five, with a beard full of salt and eyes that had the specific flatness Casper had learned to recognize in photographs from the front: a man who had stopped expecting anything good.

"You're the keeper," the young man said. Not a question. In Danish-tinged English, oddly formal. "The light held. My brother said it would. He said the keeper here never fails." A pause, wind screaming through it. "We have been standing off for two days waiting for your storm to end, and then my brother said the storm will not end, and we came anyway. That was stupid."

"It was," Casper agreed. "Come up to the tower."

The young man looked past him, up the black cliff, at the tower light swinging its steady arc above them all, and something in his flat eyes flickered, briefly, like the lamp of a lighthouse itself.

"We carry nothing," he said carefully. "We are fishermen. We came for fish."

"You came for fish," Casper said, "and I am only a keeper, and my sister is only my sister, and there is a fire and coffee at the top of those stairs. All true things are welcome here."

Behind him he heard Ilsa make a very small sound — almost a laugh, almost something else — and the young man's mouth twitched at one corner, the first crack in the flatness, and he gestured his people up the path.

It was only when they were all climbing, when Casper turned to take a last look at the boat, that he saw the fifth shadow pause at the waterline, kneel, and begin unlashing something long and rectangular wrapped in oilcloth from under the thwarts. Cargo that five fishermen did not declare. Cargo that had been worth two days standing off a coast in the worst storm in living memory.

The shadow looked up and met Casper's eyes across the spray, and even at that distance, even in that darkness, Casper knew the set of the shoulders, the tilt of the head, the particular way the man stood as though the world were a document he was considering forging.

The oilcloth came back from a face Casper had last seen in a photograph folded into a telegram that said *killed in action, regret to inform.*

"Well," said Anton, into the wind, in a voice rougher than death and twice as warm, "you'd better invite me up too, old friend. I've brought the rest of the ink."
