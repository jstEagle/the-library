## 16. Brandt at the Door

She heard the motorcar before she saw it — a sound the coast road almost never carried. Engines came to the village twice a month at most, and they belonged to inspectors, priests, or men whose arrival made other men stand straighter. This engine came slowly, deliberately, the way a man walks a property line he intends to own.

Ilsa was at the cannery's long workbench with Anton's half-finished document spread before her, checking the watermark against the sample in the crate, when the sound died somewhere on the road above. She counted. Ten seconds of silence. Then a car door, then another.

Casper had been gone since dawn. The storm coming down from the north had put a nervous urgency in him, and he'd gone back to the lighthouse to lay in oil and check the lamp before the weather closed the road. He would not return until evening, perhaps later if the rain arrived early. She was alone with a workshop full of treason and two exits she no longer trusted.

She moved the way Anton had taught her: nothing hurried, everything purposeful. A woman who runs is a woman with something to hide. A woman carrying a ledger is a woman on business. She slid the document into the false bottom of the ink chest, swept the loose blanks into the cabinet behind the boiler, pulled the canvas tarp over the printing frame, and stood for one breath in the middle of the room, listening to it become a dead space again. The cannery had spent ten years learning how to look abandoned. She asked it to remember.

The second door opened above her — the loading bay, rusted hinges screaming as someone forced them. Boots on iron stairs. Two sets. One heavy and even, one light and deferential.

Then Quartermaster Brandt stood in the doorway of what had been the cannery floor, and Ilsa understood why Anton had never once said his name without lowering his voice.

He was smaller than his reputation. That was the trick of him, she realized — men expected a wall of a man, some red-faced butcher, and instead they got this: neat, gray-templed, a civilian's softness to him, a man dressed well but not too well, holding his hat in one hand like a caller paying respects. His eyes did the work his body declined to do. They went around the room once, unhurried, and she felt them take inventory of every nail.

"Frau—" He consulted a small notebook, a gesture so casual and so terrible that her stomach turned. "Reiner. Yes?"

"Voss," she said. "Ilsa Voss."

His smile corrected itself by a millimeter. "Forgive me. Voss." He did not apologize. Men like Brandt did not apologize; they amended records. "You're the widow managing Herr Lindqvist's interests in the district. The herring concern."

"That's right." Casper's invented cousin in the city, his fictional letterhead, the paper trail they'd built in case exactly this happened. She heard how steady her voice sounded and marveled at it from a small distance, the way one marvels at a stranger's composure at a funeral.

"And you're here because?" Not accusatory. Almost gentle. A man asking after a neighbor's health.

"Herr Lindqvist asked me to assess the building. He's considering the lease." She gestured at the dereliction around them with a landlord's weary indifference. "Though I can't imagine why. The salt's eaten half the pilings."

"Mm." He walked. That was the worst of it — he walked the room, trailing two fingers along the workbench, pausing at the stove where the kettle sat still warm. He looked at the kettle. He looked at her. "Tea?"

"I had some earlier."

"In this cold?"

"I run warm."

The soldier behind him — young, wet-eyed, eager in the way of boys given authority they hadn't earned — smirked at that, and something in Brandt's face flickered, a schoolmaster's patience with an unruly class. "Leave us, Feldmann. Check the upper floor."

"The upper—"

"It's a cannery, Corporal. It has an upper floor. Go and be bored there."

The boy went, boots hammering the iron stairs, and Brandt set his hat on the workbench, precisely centered, and sat down on a packing crate as if he'd brought it himself. He crossed his ankles. He folded his hands.

"You'll forgive the intrusion, Frau Voss. I'm conducting an inventory. Somewhere between the front and this coast, a crate of mine went astray. Office supplies, mostly. Stamps, seals, certain papers of a bureaucratic nature." He smiled again, and up close the smile was worse than any expression could have been, because it was patient. "You'd be astonished what soldiers lose in wartime. Consciences. Uniforms. Entire identities. One develops a philosophical tolerance for loss."

"I wouldn't know. I've lost only a husband."

"Yes." He opened the notebook again. "I read the file. I read everyone's file — it's a habit, not a threat, though I notice people take it as one. Widowed at—" he turned a page "—hm. The dates are inconsistent. Your marriage record says one thing, your residency permit another. Paperwork is like that, isn't it? It drifts. It disagrees with itself. One learns not to trust it."

Every word was a scalpel laid neatly on a tray, none of them yet used. She kept her hands loose at her sides. Her heart was going hard enough that she could feel it in her throat, and she thought, with a strange clarity, *this is the moment. Not later. Right now, standing still, breathing slow.* Anton had told her once: the danger never comes at the moment of the lie. It comes in the three seconds before it, when the body wants to run and the voice must stay level. Those three seconds were where couriers died.

"War paper is all provisional," she said. "They reissued my permit twice. The registrar in Kiel mixed up two women named Voss. I stopped correcting it. It seemed arrogant, correcting clerks during a war."

Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion. Recognition. The pleasure of a craftsman seeing unfamiliar work done competently. "It did indeed," he said softly.

Above them, boots crossed the upper floor. A door scraped. Ilsa thought of the tarp over the printing frame, of the cabinet behind the boiler, of the ink chest with its false bottom sitting four meters from where Brandt sat warming his hands over nothing. She thought of the trapdoor under the herring presses that led to the tideline, and whether she could reach it before the corporal reached the top of the stairs, and knew the answer.

"Your Lindqvist," Brandt said. "A cousin, I understand. From the city."

"A cousin."

"Cousins are interesting. So often the load-bearing relation in a family fiction." He examined his fingernails. "You know what I've learned about crates, Frau Voss? They don't walk. Someone carries them. And whoever carried mine passed through this district — the railway clerk remembers a woman matching your description buying a through ticket she didn't use. Curious thing, an unused ticket. A ticket is a promise. Abandoning one suggests a change of plan. Or a fear of stations."

*Anton,* she thought. *You taught me the work but not this — being unwound slowly by a man who enjoys the thread.* Her mouth was dry. She made herself shrug, a small bored motion. "I changed at Havnø instead. There was a delay on the main line."

"There was no delay on the main line. I checked that too."

The silence afterward had weight. Rain began on the roof, a soft drumming far off, growing. She watched him watch her and understood that he already knew — not everything, perhaps, but enough that this whole visit was theater, courtesy paid before cruelty, a creditor observing the formalities before seizure. He wasn't here to ask questions. He was here to let her know the questions had answers.

And still she did not run. She heard herself say, in a voice dried and flattened by two years of grief and forged names: "Herr Brandt. You've traveled a long way to inspect a cannery. Either tell me what you want or charge me with something. I have a lease assessment to finish before dark."

The room changed. The corporal's boots had stopped overhead. Rain filled the pause. And Brandt laughed — a short, genuine, appalled laugh, as if she'd played a note he hadn't known the instrument contained.

"There it is," he said. "That's the voice. Do you know, I interviewed forty people in this district last week and not one of them produced that voice? They produce fear, or stupidity, which is fear wearing a coat." He rose, took his hat, brushed it with his sleeve. "Here is what I want, since you ask plainly. My crate. My forger. And the third party who has been signing documents in this district under a name I have reason to believe belongs to a dead man." He put the hat on. It suited him terribly. "I don't think you're the forger. Your hand is good but it's a copyist's hand — I saw the ticket requisition. Copies, not inventions. Which means someone taught you, and teachers are findable."

He paused at the foot of the iron stairs.

"One more thing, Frau Voss. There's a name I've carried a long while — a courier who worked for me and then worked against me. Clever girl. Cold hands. She vanished the same season a certain lighthouse keeper's friend died at the front." He turned the brim of the hat in his fingers. "If you should hear anything of such a person — a rumor, an initial, a discarded alias — the district office in Havnø keeps my hours. I am very reasonable, Frau Voss. Ask anyone I've ruined."

He was at the door when she broke the first rule she'd ever learned, the rule that had kept her alive across four borders: never give him anything free, never fill a silence, never—

"Your stamps," she said.

Brandt stopped.

"If your crate held office stamps and seals, you should know they're worthless now." She turned to face him fully, and her heart was a bird beating itself against a window, and her voice came out of somewhere older than fear. "Armistice terms are circulating. Everyone who matters has seen the drafts. When the guns stop, every document issued under the old registry becomes scrap inside a month. New authorities. New seals. You're collecting debts in a currency that's dying, Herr Brandt. Whoever holds your crate when the bells ring will be holding ash."

It was a gamble built on a rumor and a hope, and she watched it land — watched the first true uncertainty cross his careful face, a hairline crack across good porcelain. He had not come for the papers' power, she realized then. He'd come for the evidence. Ledgers, samples, signatures — proof of what he'd authorized, what he'd sold, whose names had passed through his office into enemy hands. In the coming peace, quartermasters who trafficked in identities would not be decorated. They would be questions asked in bright rooms.

"You've been reading things you shouldn't," he said quietly.

"I've been burying a husband and leasing a cannery. Reading is all I do."

For a long moment the rain spoke for both of them. Then Brandt inclined his head, almost a bow, the courtesy of one professional acknowledging another's move.

"Give my regards to Herr Lindqvist," he said. "Tell him I'll call on the lighthouse before the week is out. I find keepers honest company. All those logs. All those signatures." He smiled his patient smile. "Paperwork, after all, is like that. It disagrees with itself — until someone checks the original."

The boots came down the stairs. The corporal reported the upper floor empty — *empty, thank God, empty* — and the motorcar coughed, caught, dwindled down the coast road until the rain swallowed it entirely.

Ilsa stood alone in the cannery until her legs informed her they had opinions. Then she sat down hard on a packing crate, in the exact place Brandt had warmed, and pressed both hands flat against the workbench to stop their shaking, and laughed once — a thin, cracked sound with nothing of humor in it.

*Before the week is out.* He would go to the lighthouse. He would sit across from Casper with his hat and his notebook and his terrible gentleness, and Casper — honest Casper, invisible Casper, a man who had spent four years perfecting the art of being overlooked — would have to perform a fiction with no rehearsal, while the sea rose and the storm came down and the finished document waited in an ink chest four meters from where she was sitting.

She looked at the window. The light was failing early, the way it did before a northern blow. If she left now she might reach the lighthouse by dark. If she stayed, she protected the workshop and abandoned the man.

She was already pulling her coat on when she understood that the choice had never really been hers to make — that somewhere in the past weeks, without ceremony, without her permission, the question *what do I save?* had quietly become the question *who?*

Outside, the rain arrived in earnest, and the sea beyond the shingle was already white, and twelve miles of bad road lay between her and the lamp.
