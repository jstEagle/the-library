## 17. The Last Page

The storm had spent itself against the coast by the time Casper turned the lighthouse over to daylight, and he came down the spiral stairs with the lamp's oil smell in his clothes and his hands still shaking — not from the wind, not from the climb, but from what he had chosen. He had chosen the lamp. Ninety-one boats had passed the point in the black hours, and every one of them was alive because of him, and every one of them was a reason he could not be at the cannery, and he had made that choice alone and would carry it alone.

Now he rode Ilsa's borrowed bicycle along the flooded cart road with the finished storm behind him like a wall of grey glass, and the whole way he thought about one thing: dawn was coming, and after dawn there might not be another night like this one. The armistice rumor had reached even the lighthouse, carried on the wireless crackle of a passing navy cutter's signal lamp — *hostilities cease eleven hundred hours, eleventh day*. After that, paper meant nothing. Anton had written it himself: *Finish it before the guns stop.*

He left the bicycle in the nettles behind the cannery and let himself in through the loading door, and the smell hit him first — ink and lamp oil and old fish-bone rot, the smell of the workshop that had become, over these weeks, the closest thing to a home he had outside the tower.

Ilsa was awake. Of course she was awake. She sat at the long workbench with her sleeves rolled and her hair pinned up crooked, and before her, weighted down with brass letter-openers at the corners, lay the document they had been building for a fortnight: exit visas, transit permits, an internal passport, and the crown of it all — the extraction writ, the single sheet Anton had left half-made, the paper that could walk the Lindqvist family out of a detention camp and across a border and into lives that did not end with a number chalked on a barracks wall.

"You kept the lamp," she said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't an accusation. She had stopped making it an accusation somewhere around the third night.

"Ninety-one boats."

"I know. I counted them from the ridge." She pushed back from the bench and rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. "Come here. Come look at what we have and tell me what's wrong with it, because something is wrong with it, and I've been staring so long I can't see the page anymore."

Casper hung his coat on the nail — his hook, now; he had claimed it without thinking, weeks ago, the way you claim things in houses where you intend to stay — and came to stand beside her.

* * *

The document lay under the lamplight like a patient on a table. Four sheets, four hands' worth of work. Anton's hand was in the bones of it — the watermark he'd built by pressing wire forms into wet pulp, the guilloche patterns drawn with a ruling pen finer than a eyelash, the official seals pressed from molds carved into soapstone and cast in a wax-and-plaster compound Casper still didn't fully understand. Then Ilsa's hand, filling in the bureaucratic flesh: dates, stamp impressions layered to fake the accumulation of routine handling, the small authenticating scrawls that no forger should attempt but every good one does, because a document without human sloppiness is a document that screams.

And then Casper's hand. The Lindqvist names. Erik. Greta. The children, Astrid and Jonas, whose ages he had calculated from the photograph Ilsa carried, counting backward from the year the picture was taken to the present, subtracting war years the way you subtract anything precious.

"It's good," he said slowly. "It's better than good. The stamps are right, the sequence is right, the—" He stopped.

"What." Ilsa's voice went flat. "What did you see."

He touched the corner of the third sheet, where the signature block waited below Greta Lindqvist's particulars. Two signatures filled in. Erik's, which Ilsa had done from a sample in the satchel. Greta's, which Ilsa had invented, because there were no samples, because Greta Lindqvist had never in her life been asked by any government to sign anything.

"The countersignature," Casper said. "The camp adjutant's authorization. It has to be signed by the officer who releases them, and we don't have his hand, and if you invent it the way you invented Greta's—"

"Then it will pass," Ilsa said, too quickly. "I've invented forty officers' hands in three years. Brandt himself couldn't—"

"No." Casper heard his own voice come out steady and was surprised by it. "You said it yourself, the night we did the visa. A forged signature passes when nobody's looking for a lie. But this paper goes to a border desk where they're looking. Brandt will have told every checkpoint between here and the frontier to watch for this exact forgery — not because he knows what it looks like, but because he knows it exists. They'll scrutinize everything. And an invented hand is fine until someone compares it against another document signed the same week by the same officer, and then—"

"Then we're dead, yes, I know the arithmetic." She laughed, short and bitter, and dropped into her chair. "So what's your solution, keeper? We wait for the real adjutant to wander in and sign it?"

Casper looked at the half-finished sheet, and beneath it, where Ilsa had kept it flat and face-down all these weeks like something holy, the note in Anton's writing that had started everything:

*Finish it before the guns stop. After that, paper means nothing. And finish it honestly — the last line must be true, or none of the lines will hold.*

He had read that note a dozen times. Ilsa had read it more. Neither of them had ever understood the last instruction. Honest forgery was a contradiction, a joke, one of Anton's koans, the kind of thing he'd say over cards while losing cheerfully. *The best lie is built on a true foundation.* Fine. But how do you forge honestly?

Casper stood very still, and the answer arrived the way weather arrives off the sea — not gradually but all at once, a change in the pressure of the room.

"The last line," he said. "Anton didn't mean the adjutant's signature. He meant ours. Yours and mine. Look—" He turned the final sheet over. On the reverse, in the lower margin, in a space no inspector would ever check because inspectors only ever read the face of a page, Anton had ruled two faint lines and written above them, in pencil, in his looping careful script:

*Witnessed.*

Ilsa stared at it. "Nobody witnesses the back of a document."

"Somebody does," Casper said. "Somebody who knows documents the way we've come to know them, this month. If anyone ever turns this paper over — a clerk, an investigator, Anton himself, if he's alive — those two names are the message in the bottle. Proof that it was made by friends, not thieves. That the people who forged it believed in it." His throat tightened. "And if it's ever found in the wrong hands, those names damn us. That's why he wrote 'honestly.' Because signing it is the price. You can't put false names there. False names would make it just another piece of his stock. True names make it a testimony."

The room was quiet except for the rain finishing itself against the roof slates, running off in long diminishing threads.

"He wanted someone who never wanted power," Ilsa said softly, remembering the story she'd told him, the story of why Anton had chosen Casper — a lighthouse keeper who had spent five years making himself invisible. "That's why the letters came to you."

"And I threw the first one in the stove," Casper said. "Burned it in October and told myself it was none of my affair." He pulled out the work stool and sat, and his knee brushed hers under the bench, and neither of them moved away. "Ilsa. How much time do we have?"

She looked at the window, where the grey was not yet lightening but had begun to think about it.

"If the rumor's true — guns stop at eleven, today, the eleventh — then the border contact crosses at first light or not at all. After the armistice, the camps don't empty, they change management. The same men, new paperwork, worse moods. This family's chance dies at eleven o'clock this morning whether the shooting stops or not." She was already pulling the lamplight closer, dragging an inkwell near, shaking the bottle of the dark brown iron-gall mix that behaved, as far as Casper could tell, exactly as its color suggested it had opinions. "So we finish tonight. All of it. Every stamp, every seal, every signature, and the witnessing, and then you ride to the mill crossroads and put it into Hallberg's hands before the sky gets bright enough to shoot by."

"Hallberg can't ride to the border and back before eleven."

"Hallberg doesn't have to. There's a fishing boat, the *Solveig*, out of Havnø — I told you about her skipper. He runs the last leg. If the papers reach the crossroads by full light, the family walks out of the camp at nine under escort pretext, and they're across the water before the guns stop, and whatever happens after that happens to people who are already free." She set the pen down in front of him, parallel to the sheet's edge, the way she always did, precision even in surrender. "But it has to be perfect, Casper. There's no second copy. There's no tomorrow. There's this page, and this night, and then it's out of our hands forever."

Casper looked at the page. Under the lamp the guilloche work caught the light and gave it back in fine interference patterns, waves crossing waves, exactly like the sea he had watched for five years from a tower. Somewhere in those crossings, he knew, Anton had hidden things — micro-writing in the banding, tiny letters legible only under magnification, jokes and prayers and once, Ilsa had shown him, a complete recipe for plum brandy. The dead man's humor woven into the living man's salvation.

"All right," Casper said. "Wake up every stamp in the building. Let's do the whole night's work in one night."

* * *

They worked the way drowning swimmers work, if swimmers could be methodical.

The hours lost their names. Casper learned to stop consulting the clock because every consultation was a small wound. Instead he measured time in tasks: the transit permit needed the district registrar's oval stamp refreshed with a dry impression to simulate age — press, lift, breathe on it, press again at two degrees offset. The internal passport's photograph mount needed the paste applied in a hairline bead so no shine survived to catch a clerk's lamp. The exit visa's serial number had to match, digit for digit, the register-stub forgery they'd made separately, because the stub was what would be checked first, the way you check a book by its card before you read the book.

Ilsa worked beside him in a silence broken only by instructions delivered like surgical calls: *"Seal. Left hand heavier, you're pressing like a woman. Again."* And: *"Good. Don't improve it. Perfect is suspicious; almost-perfect is invisible."* Once, near what might have been midnight, her hand shook — fatigue or memory, Casper didn't ask — and she ruined a date entry, and she sat back and pressed both fists against her mouth, and Casper saw for the first time all night that she was crying without sound, the tears simply present, unattended, like rain on a window someone was too busy to notice.

He didn't touch her. He took the ruined sheet's twin from the spare stack, set it in front of her, and said, "Third column, second row, the registrar uses a figure seven with a continental bar, you showed me Tuesday," and that was the kindness available, and she took it, wiped her face with her sleeve like a child, and went back to work, and the tears dried up as abruptly as they'd come.

Around what the clock insisted was three in the morning, they ran out of the special drying powder and improvised with blotting sand and the warm iron, and the smell of hot paper filled the cannery like bread, absurdly domestic, and Casper thought of Maren ironing linens on the kitchen table at the keeper's cottage, and his chest did its familiar complicated thing, grief and love in one knot, but he kept his hand steady on the iron, because his hands had learned, this month, to hold grief and a ruling pen at the same time, and that, he suspected, was the entire education.

At four, the last mechanical element was done. The document lay complete on the bench: four sheets, correct in every falsifiable particular, ready to move a family of four across a line on a map that men had drawn and men would die defending.

Except the margins. Except the back of the final sheet, where Anton's pencil lines waited under the words *Witnessed*, empty.

* * *

Ilsa picked up the pen and held it, and did not write.

"I've been dreading this since October," she said. Her voice was stripped of all its craft, all its courier's smoothness. "Do you understand what you're asking? My name goes on this. My name is already on Brandt's list — I told you what that means, what he does to people whose names he collects. If this paper is ever found, if it fails, if the Lindqvists are taken and this is discovered on them—"

"Then Brandt learns your name anyway," Casper said quietly. "He'll learn it from their interrogation. That's the risk either way. The signature doesn't add danger, Ilsa. It adds truth. And truth is the only thing Anton asked us to contribute."

"You don't understand. My name isn't—Ilsa Bergström isn't—" She stopped, breathed, started again. "I haven't used my real name in three years. Do you know what that's like? To sign nothing, to own nothing, to be a series of borrowed identities stacked like plates? Every document I've ever made, I made anonymous. That was the discipline. That was how couriers survive. Anton taught me: *never sign your work, never sign your life.* And now his own last instruction breaks his own rule, and I—" She laughed, wet and short. "I'm frightened of ink, Casper. Three years of this and I'm frightened of a pen."

Casper took the pen from her hand. She let him.

"Then I'll go first," he said.

He drew the final sheet toward him and turned it face-down, and Anton's pencil lines appeared, faint as old scars. Above them, the word. Below them, emptiness, shaped exactly like a decision.

Casper thought of all the signatures of his life. The marriage register, where his hand had shaken with joy and he'd written *Casper Nakamura* in letters too large, like a boy proud of learning something. Maren's death certificate, where the clerk had handed him the pen and he had written his name so small and hard that the nib had nearly torn the paper, as if he could make the fact smaller by writing smaller. Five years of lighthouse logs in a careful neutral administrative hand, *C. Nakamura, Keeper,* the same every day, a man signing his own absence.

His hand had changed since Maren died — he'd discovered that in chapter after sleepless chapter of practice, watching his own letterforms drift, the downstrokes heavier now, the loops tighter, grief getting into the muscles the way salt gets into timber. For weeks that discovery had been a wound. Tonight it was simply a fact. His hand was his hand. It had walked through fire and come out still holding the pen.

He signed.

*Casper Nakamura.* Full size. Steady. Not the boy's triumphant sprawl, not the widow's crushed scrawl — something new, or rather something chosen: the letters of a man who had decided, this night, at this bench, in a burned-smelling cannery by a cold sea, that he existed, that he had acted, that history had happened through him and not merely around him, and that he would stand behind the fact of it in his own name.

He set the pen down. His heart was going hard, harder than it had gone during the storm, harder than it had gone at Brandt's checkpoint with a forged uniform on his back. That had been terror. This was something with no fear in it at all, only weight, the good kind, the kind you train for.

"There," he said, and his voice cracked on it, and he let it crack.

Isla looked at the signature for a long moment. Then she took his hand — briefly, firmly, her fingers cold from the night — and said nothing, which was the correct amount.

Then she took the pen.

She hovered over the second line for the length of one long breath, and Casper watched her close her eyes, and when she opened them there was something in her face he had never seen there: not the courier's watchfulness, not the survivor's flatness, but a kind of naked relief, as if a corset had been cut away.

She wrote four words in a hand Casper had never seen before — rounder, warmer, older than any of her working hands, the handwriting of whoever she had been before the war made her a stack of borrowed names.

*Ilsa Marie Vinter.* Her true name, given to a true witness line, under a true one. The first honest thing she had signed in three years.

"It's done," she whispered.

* * *

They blotted the signatures, squared the sheets, folded the document along Anton's pre-creased lines into the oilcloth wallet that would ride inside Hallberg's coat lining, and wrapped the wallet in waxed canvas against the wet. It was quarter past five. Outside the loading door, the eastern sky had turned the color of a healing bruise, and the rain had stopped, and the wind had dropped to that exhausted post-storm sigh that means the sea is thinking about forgiving everyone.

Casper buttoned his coat. Ilsa checked the fold-lines one last time, then a last-last time, then made herself stop with visible effort.

"The bicycle's front tire took a thorn yesterday," she said. "There's a repair kit in the basket. Ride the flint path past the mill instead of the cart road — the cart road floods and Brandt's patrol likes the high ground. You'll make the crossroads by seven if the tire holds, eight if it doesn't, and Hallberg waits until eight."

"And after?"

"After, I burn the practice sheets and the spare inks and pack what can be packed, and by the time you're back there'll be nothing here but fish bones and regret." She tried to smile. It mostly worked. "Ride fast, keeper."

He swung up onto the bicycle. One foot on the pedal, one on the ground, the parcel strapped flat against his chest under his coat, over his heart, which was still beating its strange unfrightened beat.

"Casper." Her voice caught him at the door. He turned. In the grey pre-light she stood behind the bench with the lamp guttering at her elbow, and she looked, for a moment, like a woman in a portrait — some future painting of this exact hour that hadn't been commissioned yet. "Whatever happens at eleven o'clock, when the guns stop — the world's going to act like everything that needed doing is finished. Don't let it make you believe that. Some of us will still need each other after peace arrives. Remember that I owe you a debt I haven't figured out how to name."

"You named it tonight," Casper said. "On the back of a document nobody will ever read."

He pedaled out into the bruised morning, and behind him the cannery stood dark against the dunes, and ahead the road ran twelve miles inland toward the crossroads and the mill and a stranger named Hallberg who would carry four folded lies, built on two true names, toward the water and freedom.

The tire held. The flint path held. At the top of the ridge he paused, one time, and looked back at the coast: the lighthouse white against the grey sea, the cannery a dark tooth in the dunes, and beyond both, far out, the first pale seam of actual daylight opening along the horizon like a page being turned.

Then he rode down into the day, and the day, the eleventh of November, began.
