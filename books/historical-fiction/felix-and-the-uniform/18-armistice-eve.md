## 18. Armistice Eve

The rumor arrived the way weather did on that coast — ahead of itself, half-heard, impossible to confirm until it was already on top of you.

It came up the road from the village in the mouth of a fish boy pedaling a rattling bicycle, and it passed from him to the postmistress, and from her to the woman who kept the tavern, and by late afternoon it had crossed the twelve miles of bad road to the cannery without ever once being written down. No telegram. No official notice. Just a word moving from mouth to mouth like smoke finding its way through a wall: *Monday.* The guns stop Monday morning. Eleven o'clock, they said. Dawn, others said, correcting nothing because no one knew anything, only that it was soon and it was certain and for the first time in four years certainty was not bad news.

Casper heard it from Ilsa while they stood over the worktable, their hands black to the knuckle with ink, the finished document lying between them like something newly born and still damp.

"Monday," she said again, as if repetition could make it true faster.

"Which is it — dawn or eleven?"

"Does it matter?"

He thought about it. Outside, the wind was rising out of the northwest, the kind of wind that meant the sea would be working all night, and somewhere beyond the point the lighthouse stood dark because he was here, because the last page of Anton's document had needed his hand and not his lamp. "I suppose not," he said. "After four years, a few hours."

But it mattered more than he let himself say, and he saw it matter to her too, in the way she went very still over the drying document, the way her eyes moved to the window where the light was failing. He had learned her stillness by now. It was what she did instead of screaming. Something was wrong in her silence, and it took him another hour — an hour spent eating cold herring and pretending calm — to understand what it was.

Peace was coming too slowly.

Not for them. For Brandt.

* * *

Quartermaster Elias Brandt heard the news at six o'clock in the evening, in the taproom of the tavern in the coastal village, from a corporal who delivered it with a grin he hadn't been authorized to wear.

"Eleven o'clock Monday, sir. Official. They're saying the delegation signed at five this morning."

Brandt thanked him pleasantly, bought him a beer, and sat with his own untouched while the room erupted around him — men singing, men crying, men already drunk on a peace they couldn't yet see. He watched them the way he watched everything, as an administrator watches inventory: noting quantities, conditions, expiry dates.

The singing meant nothing to him. The armistice meant one thing only, and he had understood it before the corporal finished speaking: at eleven o'clock Monday morning, every ledger he kept lost its authority.

Brandt's ledgers were not military documents, whatever the stamps on them said. They were private accounts, maintained in his own careful hand across three campaigns — records of favors granted and owed, of requisitions redirected, of names moved from one column to another column that decided whether men lived or died at the front. He had traded in discharge papers, transit permits, medical exemptions. He had sold passage out of condemned regiments and taken payment in cash, in contraband, in silence. The war had made him rich in the only currency that mattered: other people's desperation, denominated in paper.

And paper, after Monday, would be just paper. New governments. New authorities. Men with new stamps who would ask, politely at first and then less politely, how a quartermaster had come to own three houses and a shipping interest in Rostock. Every forged passport he had ever sanctioned, every deserter he had ever ransomed, every crate he had ever moved — it would all become evidence.

Unless it burned.

He finished his beer, wished the corporal joy of the peace, and walked out into the wet evening thinking about a crate on a night tide, a workshop in a dead cannery, and a courier named Voss whose real name was written in his book.

* * *

At the cannery, they counted what they had.

"One document," Ilsa said, laying it flat between sheets of blotting paper. "Finished. Signed." She looked at Casper when she said *signed*, and he felt the weight of it again — his own name, in his own hand, on the line where Anton had intended a stranger's. "One family's worth of passage. The border contact runs the boat Thursday if the weather holds."

"And everything else."

"Everything else burns. That was always the plan."

"Then why do I feel like we're being watched?" He didn't actually feel watched; he felt something cruder, an animal unease with no name attached. He went to the window — the tall salt-fogged windows of the old cannery floor, overlooking the road — and looked down the length of it into the dusk.

Empty. Rutted mud, black hedgerows, the first hard stars coming out over the water. But the wind was wrong. It came off the sea and up the valley, and it carried, faintly, the smell of coal smoke, and coal smoke meant an engine, and engines on that road at that hour meant one thing.

"Pack it," he said. "Now. Tonight. We don't wait for—"

Lantern light, far down the road. Not moving. Standing still, the way lanterns stand when the people holding them are waiting for full dark.

Ilsa was beside him without seeming to cross the room. She looked once, and he watched two years of discipline hold her face together while everything behind it came apart.

"That's not soldiers," she said. "That's too few. That's him."

"How many does he need? It's over. Whatever he thinks he's owed, there's no authority left to collect it with."

"You don't understand him yet." Her voice had gone quiet and flat, the voice she used for facts. "The armistice doesn't end his war. It starts it. Until eleven o'clock Monday, his stamps still work. His warrants still carry. He has — what — thirty hours left in which everything he's done is still legal, still official, still *his*. He isn't coming to arrest us, Casper. He's coming to close his accounts. The workshop, the crate, the ledger evidence, me." A pause. "In whichever order is fastest."

Through the dark came the sound Casper knew better than any sound on earth besides the sea: an engine laboring in low gear, climbing.

"They'll come to the front," Ilsa said, already moving. "The main doors. He'll want the formality. He'll want us to open it ourselves — it saves him trouble, and he likes things done through proper channels." She almost smiled. "So we won't use the doors."

"The loading chute."

"The loading chute."

She took the finished document, wrapped in oilcloth, and pushed it against his chest. "You carry it. Whatever happens, it leaves this building tonight, and it isn't in your hands when they find me, because I can talk longer than you can."

"I'm not leaving you."

"Yes," she said, "you are. Down the chute, along the streambed, over the wall to the road. Twelve miles to the coast. You've walked worse."

"I haven't. I've kept a lighthouse for four years. I haven't walked anywhere."

For a moment — the engine noise growing below, the lanterns beginning to move up the road in a slow deliberate procession — something young and unguarded broke through her face, and she gripped his ink-stained wrist hard enough to hurt.

"Casper. Thirty hours from now none of this will matter to anyone but historians. Don't let him take the last of it. Take the document. Run."

Below, fists on wood. A voice, pleasant and carrying, calling up through the dark:

"Herr Nakamura. Frau Voss. I know you're awake — the lamp in your window gave you away an hour ago. May I come in? It's a terrible night, and I have so little time left."

* * *

What happened next happened fast.

They doused the lantern. Through the rear of the floor, past the rows of gutted herring presses, to the loading chute where the barrels had once gone down to the carts — a wooden slope, dark, slick with forty years of fish oil soaked into the grain. Ilsa swung herself over the edge first to test it, then pulled back.

"You first. If it drops you, I'd rather find out now."

He went. The chute took him in a long grinding slide, spat him onto the cart landing among rotten sacking, and Ilsa landed beside him a moment later, rolling, up on her feet, one hand pressed flat to the small of her back where the years of courier work lived.

The streambed took them down behind the cannery, cold water over their boots, the building looming above them black against the stars. From inside — muffled, patient — came the sound of the front doors giving way, not broken but opened, unhurried, someone with a key or a crowbar and all the time in the world.

"He'll search for an hour," Ilsa breathed. "He'll be thorough. He wants the papers more than he wants us, and he'll assume we hid them." Then, quieter: "He'll be angry when he finds the workshop empty."

"What does Brandt do when he's angry?"

She didn't answer, which was an answer.

They gained the wall and dropped behind it onto the road, and turned north, toward the coast, and began to walk — fast, then faster, the document a hard rectangle against Casper's chest, the wind in their teeth, the first spit of the coming storm arriving sideways out of the dark.

Behind them, light bloomed in the cannery windows. Not lantern light. Firelight — orange, growing, reflected downward through the glass onto the road ahead of them, painting their shadows long and trembling on the mud.

"He's burning it," Ilsa said. "Now. Before the deadline. While it's still legal."

"No." Casper stopped walking. He turned and looked back at the glow, and something in him — the man who had spent four years believing history happened elsewhere, the man who had signed his name exactly once and meant it — refused the sight. "He's not burning the workshop. There's nothing left worth burning. He's making a signal. He wants us to look back. He wants us to know he's not finished."

The firelight flickered. And in it, briefly, at the top of the cannery steps, a figure stood watching the road — a single silhouette, unhurried, hands folded, a man taking the air on the eve of a great peace.

Then the wind shifted, and the rain came down in earnest, and the figure was gone, and there was only the storm, and the road, and eleven hours until dawn — and somewhere behind them in the burning dark, the sound of an engine starting, turning north, following.
