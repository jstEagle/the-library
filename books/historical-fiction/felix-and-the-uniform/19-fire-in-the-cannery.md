## 19. Fire in the Cannery

The smoke found them before the fire did.

Casper smelled it first — a thin, bitter thread woven into the tar-and-salt smell of the cannery — and his body understood before his mind did. He was on his feet with the finished document pressed against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth, before he had consciously decided to move.

"Ilsa."

She was already awake. She had been sleeping in her boots for three days, and she came up off the cot with the revolver in her hand as if she'd never put it down.

"Back stair," she said. "He'll have watched the front."

"He's one man."

"He brought four men from the village. I counted their cigarettes at the gate an hour ago." She was moving as she spoke, sweeping Anton's inks into a canvas bag with the economy of someone who had abandoned things before and knew exactly what could be carried and what could not. "He won't come to arrest us, Casper. Armistice is at dawn. Whatever happens in the next hour will never be written down by anyone who matters. He knows that. It's why he chose tonight."

Above them, floorboards creaked. Then another sound, softer and infinitely worse: the strike of a match.

* * *

Brandt had not come to search. He had come to erase.

They understood it when they reached the landing above the main floor and looked down through the grating. Two of Brandt's men were pouring lamp oil along the long benches where the blank passports lay in their neat, terrible rows. A third stood at the door with a rifle held with parade-ground correctness. And Brandt himself walked the aisles slowly, hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting stores, touching nothing, cataloguing everything.

"Start at the north wall," he said. His voice carried in the empty building without effort, unhurried, almost gentle. "The ledgers first. Then the plates. Paper burns fast; metal needs encouragement. We have until dawn, and I want nothing left but ash that could be read as anything else."

"You're burning your own records," Ilsa breathed beside Casper. "Every document he ever had me make. Every transaction. If the new authorities find—"

"He isn't destroying evidence," Casper said, and his mouth was dry. "He's destroying witnesses. Paper remembers what people are supposed to forget."

Below, a match flared.

The oil caught with a soft, obscene *whump*, and fire ran the length of the north bench like something released from a long leash. Heat rolled up through the grating and touched Casper's face. Forty years of Anton's work — the inks mixed to match three governments' seals, the stamps cut by hand from boot heels and doorknobs, the blank passports that had crossed borders inside coat linings while their bearers crossed inside new names — began to curl and bloom orange at its edges.

"Anton built all this to save people," Ilsa said.

"And Brandt builds ash so no one asks questions." Casper gripped the oilcloth bundle tighter. The document inside was warm from his own body. Eleven months of Anton's planning, four weeks of their finishing, every seal and signature correct, the family's names entered in a hand Casper had made honest by signing his own beneath them. If it burned here, the Voss family died in the east within the month. "The side window past the press room. You said it opens."

"It opens onto forty feet of mud and then the water." She looked at him, and in the light of the growing fire below her face was all planes and shadow. "He'll hear the sash."

"Then you go quiet and I go loud."

"No."

"Ilsa—"

"*No.*" It came out flat and final, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed goodbyes in mirrors. "You carry the document. That makes you more important than me, and for once in my life I'm going to insist that arithmetic is respected. Besides—" the ghost of something that was almost a smile — "I've been Brandt's before. I know how he thinks when he's cornered. You don't. Stay alive, keep it moving south, and don't you dare look back."

She kissed him once, hard, the way people kiss at stations, and was gone down the stair before he could answer.

* * *

What Casper did next, he did without deliberation, and would spend the rest of his life understanding.

He did not go to the window. He went up — to the old foreman's office on the third floor, where a fire bucket still hung on its nail out of some dead man's habit — and he filled it from the rain barrel at the broken skylight, and he waited until Brandt came up the stairs alone, drawn by the noise Ilsa was making two rooms away, patient as bookkeeping.

Brandt stepped through the office doorway with his pistol already level, and Casper threw four liters of seawater into his face.

It should not have worked. Later, telling it, Casper would say it worked because Brandt was a man who expected order — expected the confrontation to follow the form, demands and answers, the ritual of authority — and a bucket of the North Sea in the eyes violated every rule he lived by. The quartermaster staggered, blind, firing once into the ceiling, and Casper hit him with the empty bucket and went past him through the doorway at a dead run.

Behind him he heard Brandt's voice, wet and furious and still, horribly, composed: "*The ledger remembers, keeper.*"

Casper took the stairs three at a time into the smoke.

* * *

The main floor was a cathedral of fire now. The heat came off the benches in visible waves, and the passports were going up not in flames but in flakes — thousands of white fragments spiraling upward on the thermal like snow falling home to heaven. Blank pages that would have been lives. Seals that would have opened gates. All of it becoming weather.

Through it Casper saw Ilsa at the far end, backed against the press, Brandt's remaining men converging with rifles raised, and he understood with perfect clarity that loud-and-quiet had failed as a plan and that he had never actually intended to leave without her anyway.

He picked up the nearest thing — a brass stamp mount, two pounds of engraved steel — and threw it at the head of the man on the left.

After that it was not strategy. It was smoke and shouting and Ilsa's elbow finding a throat, and then they were through the press-room door with the fire roaring behind them like applause, out the low window into mud so cold it was a second kind of burning.

"South," Ilsa gasped, hauling him up. "The boat—"

"The boat is at the lighthouse. Twelve miles."

"Then we run."

They ran. Behind them the cannery windows lit gold one after another, and the whole structure groaned like a ship, and on the loading stage a figure stood watching them go — Brandt, singed and dripping, making no attempt to pursue, only lifting one hand in a small, courteous wave, a man marking a debt in a ledger no one would ever audit but him.

The document rode against Casper's chest, dry in its oilcloth, all the way down the dark beach.

At the tide line Casper stopped once, breathing like a bellows, and looked back. The cannery's roof fell inward in a gout of sparks, and forty years of Anton's dangerous mercy went up with it — and Casper felt no grief, only a fierce, unfamiliar cleanliness, as if he had finally set down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"He wanted to own the ending," Ilsa said beside him, watching the same fire. "Instead there's just... nothing left to argue over. Ash doesn't testify."

"Ash doesn't have to," Casper said. "We do."

She turned to look at him then — really look, the way she had that first morning on the shingle, measuring him for threat and finding, again, none — and above them the eastern sky had begun, very faintly, to grey.

"Bells," she said quietly.

Far off, from the village, the first stroke of dawn was ringing.
