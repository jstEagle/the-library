## 20. The Guns Stop

The guns did not stop all at once. That was the thing Casper had never imagined — that peace would arrive like weather, unevenly, in patches, the way a squall exhausts itself over one stretch of coast and rages on another. All through the night, from the gallery of the lighthouse, he had listened to the far-off thunder rolling up from the south, thinner now, gappy, like a fence with palings missing. Then, somewhere around four in the morning, there was a gap that didn't close.

He waited for it to close. His hand rested on the brass rail and his whole body leaned south, listening, the way Maren used to lean toward the window when she thought she'd heard the baby crying in a house where there was no baby.

The gap held. The horizon stayed dark and dumb.

He went down the stairs because there was nothing else to do at that hour but go down the stairs, and trimmed the wick, and logged the weather — wind southwest, moderating; sea abating; visibility poor improving to fair — and his hand did not shake once while writing it, which struck him as obscene. The world had changed its mind about continuing and his handwriting hadn't noticed.

At the bottom of the log page, below the last entry, the ink of last night still smelled faintly of smoke. He had not washed it off properly. Some of Anton's soot had gotten into him over these past weeks and he suspected it would never come out entirely. He found he didn't want it to.

Ilsa was asleep in the keeper's chair by the cold stove, upright, her boots on, her head tipped back against the wall, one hand still curled loosely around the satchel strap even in sleep. She had come up from the cannery sometime after two, soaked, gray-faced, saying only *it's done, it's done* before her legs quit under her. He had not asked what happened in the fire. There would be time. Or there wouldn't, and asking would make no difference either way.

Now, watching her sleep with the first flat gray light coming through the salt-hazed glass, he thought: *we burned a man's life's work twelve hours ago and I am looking at a woman asleep in a chair.* It felt less like triumph than like jet-lag of the soul, some dislocation of hours, the body arriving in a country the mind hadn't cleared for yet.

The bells started just after six.

Not the lighthouse bell — the village church, four miles inland, a sound he could usually only hear on still summer nights when the wind came off the land instead of the sea. This morning the wind carried it perfectly, swinging and swinging, no pattern to it, no service ringing it, just someone hauling on the rope with both arms like a man hauling a drowning brother out of water.

Ilsa's eyes opened instantly. That was training; Casper understood that now. Her hand closed on the satchel, found it empty except for papers that no longer mattered, and slowly her fingers unclenched.

"What is that?" she said, voice rusted.

"Bells."

"I know it's bells." She sat forward, listening the way you listen to a sound you don't trust. "What are they ringing for?"

And Casper, who had spent four years believing history happened elsewhere, heard himself say: "I think they're ringing for us."

* * *

They walked into the village because neither of them could bear the lighthouse another minute and because the road, for once, felt like the safest place in the world. The storm had spent itself in the night; the sky was rinsed and high and pale, and the light on the wet stones had that scrubbed quality of mornings after great violence, when the world looks newly made and slightly embarrassed about it.

The village was already in the street. That was the image Casper knew he would carry the rest of his life: not flags, not singing, but people standing in doorways in their nightclothes, holding cups they weren't drinking from, looking at each other as if checking whether everyone else was hearing the same impossible news. The postmistress stood on her step with her apron on backwards — she had dressed in the dark, in haste, or perhaps her hands had simply stopped being reliable — and when she saw Casper and Ilsa coming down the road she pressed that backwards apron flat against herself with both hands, and nodded to them, once, deeply. The favor in her cellar had papers now. Her son was a discharged soldier on paper, and this morning paper meant something again, or soon would, and she nodded at them the way people nod at midwives and gravediggers: witnesses to the private machinery of other people's lives.

Nobody cheered in the village square. That surprised him. He had expected cheering — he had seen photographs of crowds in cities, hats thrown, and he supposed the village had simply never learned how. Instead there was a strange, shuffling congregation forming around the war memorial, the fresh-cut granite one with the names already on it, nineteen names, and old Halvard Sørensen standing in front of it in his good coat with his hat in his hands as though waiting for a train that had been delayed four years.

A boy ran through the square shouting the official word — eleven o'clock, the eleventh hour of the eleventh day, signed in a railway carriage in a forest in France, *signed*, the boy kept saying, *it's signed* — and the word moved through the crowd like current through water, and still nobody cheered. A woman near the bakery put both hands over her mouth. Two old men who had hated each other since some quarrel no one remembered stood shoulder to shoulder and did not look at each other. The fish-crier sat down on the cobbles, right down, coat and all, and wept without noise, and his wife stood beside him with her hand on his shoulder looking straight ahead, letting him, guarding him, her face fierce and dry.

"Strangers," Ilsa said quietly.

Casper followed her gaze. Down by the harbor wall stood three men in merchant-navy coats he'd never seen, survivors of some boat or other, storm-wrecked or war-wrecked, and one of them had his forehead pressed against the stone of the wall like a man praying to it. Strangers weeping in their street. That was the part he couldn't have predicted — that grief would be the loudest instrument in the celebration. Four years of it, banked and unspent, and the armistice had given everyone permission all at once, and the permission itself was almost unbearable.

"They're crying," Casper said. "On the day it ends."

"What did you expect?"

"Hats," he said. "I expected hats being thrown."

Ilsa made a sound that was half laugh and half something else. She looked tired beyond the reach of sleep — hollowed, scraped-out — but her eyes were moving over the square the way they always did, cataloguing exits, uniforms, hands. Old habit. It would take years to wear off, if it ever wore off. But then her gaze snagged on something and stopped.

"Casper."

She didn't point. She never pointed. She turned her head a degree, and he learned to read the geometry of her attention long ago.

Across the square, outside the requisitioned customs house that had served Brandt as an office these past two weeks, two soldiers in field-gray were carrying out wooden crates and stacking them in a handcart. Not soldiers — clerks, he corrected himself, new men, armbanded, the vanguard of whatever authority was arriving to replace the authority that was dissolving. And between them, walking out of the customs house unbound, unhurried, adjusting his gloves, came Quartermaster Erich Brandt.

Casper's whole body went to iron. Beside him he felt Ilsa go still in the particular way that preceded motion.

But Brandt did not look at the square. Brandt looked at the crates. He counted them — Casper could see his lips move — then took a small black ledger from inside his coat, checked something against the count, and handed the ledger to the senior of the armbanded men. Handed it over. Like a man surrendering accounts at the end of an audit.

"He's giving them everything," Casper said. "Why would he—"

"Because it's worth more to him surrendered than seized." Ilsa's voice was flat and very fast. "Think about what's in those ledgers. Names. Debts. Every forged pass he sold, every officer he supplied, every man he let vanish for payment. If he runs, he's a hunted thief. If he stands here and hands his books to the new authorities like a cooperative official—" she exhaled through her teeth, "—he becomes a witness. Witnesses negotiate."

Brandt finished adjusting his glove, and then — Casper would swear to this on any document in the world, forged or true — Brandt looked across the square directly at them, and inclined his head. Barely. The acknowledgment one professional gives another across a room. Then he turned and walked with his escort toward the harbor road, unhurried, a man keeping an appointment with a future he had already arranged for himself.

Ilsa's hand had found Casper's sleeve, not to hold him back exactly, more to anchor herself. He could feel the fine tremor in her fingers, the residue of everything the night had cost.

"You could name him," Casper said. "To the new men. What he did. What he is."

"Yes." She watched Brandt's back until it vanished down the harbor road. "I could. And they would take my statement, and cross-reference it against his ledgers, and discover that my name appears in them too — in his hand, in his columns, dated and initialed. I worked for him, Casper. Whatever justice those books can deliver, they can deliver to me." She said it without drama, the way she said most true things. "He knows that. It's why he bowed."

"And that's it? He walks?"

"For now." Something moved behind her eyes, some long calculation closing like a ledger of its own. "But men like Brandt keep duplicates. Men like Brandt always keep duplicates. Sooner or later someone will find the second set of books, and then his witnesses will stop protecting him, and then—" she shrugged, and the shrug contained armies. "His fate isn't ours anymore. That's what the armistice means, partly. All the debts get called in by somebody else."

The bells were still going. Casper realized his jaw ached and made himself unclench it.

"Come on," he said. "If the world's ending or starting, I want coffee."

* * *

The postmistress made coffee for half the village. Her little parlor filled past capacity, then past decency, chairs brought from every house, strangers perched on sideboards and staircases, and nobody spoke above a murmur because the murmur itself felt like the appropriate register for the day — voices pitched as if in a sickroom, as if the whole world were a patient who had just, cautiously, been declared likely to live.

Casper sat near the stove with a tin cup going cold in his hands. Across the room, Halvard Sørensen was telling anyone who would listen that his boy would have been twenty-two next month, and people listened, because today was the day for listening to that. The wall of photographs from the cannery village — Casper couldn't stop thinking of it, that wall, dozens of young faces going brown behind their glass — well, there would be walls like it in every parish within fifty miles, and this morning every one of those faces was being spoken aloud somewhere by someone, and that was apparently what victory sounded like in a place like this. Not trumpets. Inventory.

Ilsa stood at the window with her cup untouched, watching the harbor. She had barely eaten. Casper crossed to her.

"You should sit."

"I've been sitting for four years." She smiled faintly to show it was a joke, then stopped bothering. "There's a boat in. Look."

A small coaster was working in past the breakwater, riding the moderated swell, flying some pennant of the new authority. At its masthead, unbelievably, laundry was drying — someone aboard had decided the war was over enough to wash socks. Casper found himself laughing, short and helpless, and after a moment Ilsa laughed too, and the laughter had an edge of hysteria in it that they both let run its course, because the alternative to laughing at drying socks over a war-dead harbor was weeping, and they'd each done enough of that in the dark to last a while.

"It feels wrong," Casper said, when he could speak normally. "That's what I keep circling. It should feel like something. Relief. Joy. Even grief, cleanly. Instead I feel like a man who's been holding his breath so long he's forgotten how to exhale — and someone tells him the fire's out, and he thinks: *good, yes,* and keeps holding it anyway."

"That's not wrong." Ilsa turned from the window. In the low light her face looked older than it had in autumn, and calmer, the calm of a woman who had finally put something enormous down. "Unearned feeling. Anton wrote about it once, in a margin, of all places. After Verdun he said the men who survived felt the same — the war ends and you wait for the trumpet and it never comes, and what arrives instead is breakfast. You'll exhale, Casper. Probably at something ridiculous. A tide table. A well-made stamp. That's how it works. Peace gets in sideways, through the cracks, wearing ordinary clothes."

As if summoned, the postmistress appeared at Casper's elbow with a fresh cup and, tucked beneath it, a folded newspaper of last week's vintage, and pressed both into his hands with a look that said she knew precisely who he was and who the woman at his side was and what had been in her cellar, and that all of it was now between them and God and no one else, forever, amen. Then she was gone, absorbed into the crowd, backwards apron and all.

Under the newspaper was not a newspaper at all. It was a railway timetable.

Casper stared at it. On the margin, in pencil, in the postmistress's neat hand, someone had circled the morning coastal steamer. Departing Thursday, 9:40, third-class tickets available at the office without documentation — *without documentation* underlined twice.

He looked up. Ilsa was reading over his shoulder. When he turned, her expression was unreadable, and then, deliberately, she made it readable: resigned, amused, already halfway gone.

"She doesn't waste time, your postmistress."

"Ilsa—"

"Don't." Gentle. Final. "Don't start the sentence you can't finish. We knew the shape of this from the beginning, you and I. The document goes to the family. The family crosses. And the courier—" she glanced at the circled departure, and something crossed her face too quickly to name, "—the courier travels light. It's the only way any of this stays true."

"But—"

"*Don't,*" she said again, softer, and this time it wasn't gentle at all, it was armor, and behind the armor he could see the cost of wearing it, plain as lamplight.

Outside, the bells finally, mercifully, stopped. And in the ringing silence that followed, the whole village seemed to hold one collective breath — the same breath Casper had been holding since dawn, since four in the morning, since the guns went quiet and stayed quiet — and then, from somewhere down by the fish market, an accordion began, badly, joyfully, playing something with no dignity whatsoever, and one by one, like lamps being lit along a dark coast, people began to dance.

Ilsa watched them over his shoulder. And Casper saw her exhale — long, shaking, four years' worth — right there in the postmistress's crowded parlor, at absolutely nothing, at everything, at bad music and drying socks and the unbearable ordinariness of a Thursday approaching, and he understood Anton's margin note completely, and loved her for it, and began, quietly, to grieve her in advance.

"Thursday," she said, eyes still on the dancers. "Nine-forty."

"Thursday," he said.

She reached into her coat and set something on the table between them: a blank passport, cream cover, Anton's workshop stamp crisp in the corner, every interior page empty. Every page a border. Every line an unwritten life.

"Anton left instructions," Ilsa said. "One blank passport for the keeper. He said you'd know why."

Casper picked it up. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

Somewhere out on the water, the coaster blew its whistle — Thursday's boat, arriving early, testing the harbor — and the sound rolled through the village like a question looking for its answer.
