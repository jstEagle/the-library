## 1. The Ledger of Small Names

The dead came to Jonah Vasquez in alphabetical disorder.

That was the first thing she had learned, in her third week at Saint-Aubin field hospital, when she was still naive enough to believe that dying followed rules. It did not. Men did not die in order of rank or age or goodness. They died in whatever order the shrapnel found them, and the officers' reports afterward arranged them like furniture—neat, sparse, mostly absent. *Casualties: moderate. Morale: satisfactory.* As if a man named Tomas Ilic, who had shown her a photograph of a goat he intended to buy when the war ended, could be folded into the word *moderate* and disappear.

So she kept the notebook.

It was nothing to look at. A cheap ledger, oilcloth-bound, the kind quartermasters handed out for tracking bandage inventory. She had stolen it honestly enough—from a crate of supplies marked for a dressing station that had been shelled into a memory two months before anyone thought to update the manifest. The dead did not need their bandages. She had reasoned it that way the first night, lying on her cot with her boots still on, and she had never managed to reason her way out of it since.

She wrote in pencil. Pencil could be erased; ink accused you. She wrote small, because small writing fit more names per page, and because small things were easy to hide against a body. She wrote at night, after lights-out, by the blue ghost-glow of the storm lamp turned down to its lowest wick, with her blanket over her head like a child reading forbidden stories. Which, in a sense, she was.

*Tomas Ilic, private, 3rd Battalion. Died of wounds, Tuesday. Liked goats.*

*Petra Ondrus, civilian, age unknown. Brought in from the crossroads village. No one came for her.*

*Anselm Voss, corporal. The report says "died en route." He died in my arms at 4:10 in the afternoon, and his last word was a woman's name, and it was not his wife's.*

That last one she had written with a hand that shook, not from fear but from the peculiar anger of being made a liar by omission. Somewhere in a staff office, a clerk with clean cuffs would copy *died en route* into a ledger of his own, and Anselm Voss would become a line item, and the woman's name—the one that mattered, the one he'd spent his final breath carrying—would be nowhere in the world except inside Jonah's head.

Well. Not nowhere. Inside the notebook too. She had recorded the name. Marta. She always recorded the names.

* * *

History happened elsewhere. That was the other thing she had learned, and it was the thing that let her sleep.

History happened in capital cities, in rooms with tall windows and men whose uniforms were tailored rather than issued. History happened to generals and ministers and the authors of doctrines, men whose names would be printed in schoolbooks with their portraits beside them, chins up, eyes fixed on some horizon of their own invention. History did not happen in a converted granary forty kilometers from anywhere, where the ceiling beams sweated in the rain and the smell of carbolic fought a losing war against the smell of everything else.

Jonah had understood this about herself early, and she had built her life around the understanding the way a mason builds around a load-bearing wall. She was twenty-nine years old, plain-faced in the way that made men look through her, competent in the way that made officers forget she was present, and quiet in the way that made everyone assume there was nothing worth overhearing. Invisibility, she had decided somewhere around her second year of nursing school, was not a curse. It was armor. The visible people got promoted, photographed, blamed, shot. The invisible people got to keep working.

And work was the whole of it. Work was triage at dawn when the ambulances came in a convoy of screaming engines. Work was holding a basin while a boy from the coast wept out the contents of his stomach and his courage together. Work was the long arithmetic of morphine tablets, deciding—not choosing, never choosing, only *calculating*—which screams could be answered tonight and which would have to wait for morning. Work was writing letters home in eleven different hands because half the ward couldn't hold a pen and the other half didn't know what to say, and Jonah had gotten very good at saying nothing beautifully.

The letters were another kind of ledger, though no one called them that. *He was not afraid. He spoke of you. He went quickly.* Three sentences, deployed like field dressings over the wound of a death. She told herself the lies were mercy, and most days she believed it, and on the days she didn't believe it she wrote the truth down in the notebook instead, so that somewhere in the world, in pencil, in small letters, the record existed.

Because here was the thing she had come to understand, the thing that had turned a habit into a conviction: the omissions were not accidents.

She had proof now. She had been keeping proof for fourteen months without quite admitting what it was. When a lieutenant died of a self-inflicted wound, the report said *killed in action*, and the pension his mother received was honorable, and the shame that killed him was scrubbed out of the historical record as efficiently as she scrubbed blood out of a floor—and less thoroughly than she did it, actually, because she always knew where the stains had been. When an entire consignment of winter coats was diverted to a rear depot and forty men froze at the forward posts, the reports mentioned frostbite as weather, not policy. When the retreating column passed through the crossroads villages and took what they needed and burned what they couldn't carry, the reports said *requisitioned* and *collateral damage*, and the names of the people under the rubble appeared nowhere at all.

Nowhere at all. Except.

Except in a cheap oilcloth notebook hidden inside the false bottom of her footlocker, beneath a layer of spare stockings that smelled of iodine, in a granary in the middle of a war that history would describe, someday, in terms of fronts and offensives and the wisdom of commanders.

Fourteen months. Two hundred and six entries. She had counted last Sunday, alone in the linen room, running her thumb down the pages like a rosary. Two hundred and six names that would otherwise have been nobody.

* * *

There was a ritual to it now, and rituals were dangerous, and she did it anyway.

Nights when the ward finally went quiet—when the moaning settled into the low tidal rhythm of the sedated and the sleeping, when Sister Marguerite had taken her turn at the stove and gone off duty—Jonah would sit on the edge of her cot and wait until she heard the sentry's boots pass on his round. Then she would count to sixty. Then she would open the footlocker.

The notebook came out wrapped in a flour sack. She kept a stub of pencil tied to the spine with surgical thread. And she would write the day's names while they were still fresh, because freshness mattered; grief decayed fast in a place like this, and if she waited even two days she would find herself unsure whether the man in bed nine had been called Aldric or Alaric, and an uncertain name was almost as bad as no name.

Tonight's harvest had been three.

*Emile Duret, private, 7th Company. Shell wound, abdomen. The morning report lists him as "wounded, recovering." He stopped breathing at 19:40. Nobody has corrected the report. Nobody will.*

*A boy, no papers, maybe sixteen, wearing a dead man's coat. Called himself Luc. Died on the table. Dr. Ferrand wrote "unknown soldier" and moved on. His hands were a farm boy's hands. There was straw under his fingernails.*

*Marta Kessler, civilian, brought in with the Luc boy. She held his hand while he died. She is not wounded. She simply would not leave him. She has nowhere to go. I gave her bread and pointed her toward the road south. God forgive me—I should have asked her name before today. I only got it because she signed the burial register for him, the way family does.*

Jonah sat with that last entry a long time, the pencil hovering. Then she added a line:

*Someone should have done more for her. I am someone. Note this.*

She did not know why she wrote that. She wrote it anyway. Some entries were letters to a future self, warnings mailed backward through time, and she suspected—without wanting to examine the suspicion—that this was one of them.

Outside, the rain had started again, that thin persistent autumn rain that made the canvas roofs drum like fingers on a table. Somewhere past the tree line the guns were doing their nightly argument, a low grumble more felt than heard, the war clearing its throat. Fourteen months ago that sound had made her teeth ache with dread. Now it was weather. That was the other price of invisibility: you adapted, and adapting felt too much like agreeing.

She closed the notebook. Wrapped it. Buried it in the footlocker among the stockings. Counted to sixty in case anyone was listening, though no one ever was, because no one ever looked at Jonah Vasquez twice.

Invisible. Armored. Safe.

She lay back on her cot and stared at the underside of the bunk above her, where someone—a nurse from another season, another army rotation—had scratched a small star into the wood, and she thought, as she did every night, about how strange it was that the safest place in a war was the exact spot where no one was watching.

Then she slept, and dreamed, as she often did, of a great official archive with marble floors and brass fittings, and herself walking down its endless corridor pulling open drawer after drawer after drawer, and every single drawer empty.

* * *

Morning came gray and busy, the way mornings did. Convoy day. You could tell convoy day by the sound of the road: the ordinary rumble of supply traffic replaced by something faster and more desperate, engines run hard, brakes used hard.

Jonah was at the triage table before the first ambulance cleared the gate, sleeves rolled, mask up, hair pinned back so tightly it pulled at her temples. Sister Marguerite worked opposite her, a Breton woman built like a dock crane, sixty years old and unkillable, who communicated primarily through glares and had taught Jonah everything worth knowing about tourniquets.

"Three litters," Marguerite said, reading the lead ambulance's chalk marks before the doors even opened. "Two walking. One bad."

One bad turned out to be an understatement delivered in the Breton fashion. The man on the third litter had taken shell fragments across the chest and belly, and the fact that he was breathing at all was a negotiation between his stubbornness and God, currently ongoing. They took him straight through to the theater tent, and Jonah assisted, and for four hours the world contracted to the width of a wound channel and the rhythm of Ferrand's clipped instructions—*suction, retractor, pack, again*—and there was no history anywhere, no archives, no drawers, only the work.

When it was over, the man lived, which felt like theft from the general order of things, and Jonah stepped out into the yard to breathe air that wasn't carbolic and blood.

The sky was doing its late-afternoon copper. The laundry lines sagged with bandages drying into flags of surrender. And at the far end of the yard, near the gate, near the ditch where the drivers dumped ash from the stoves—

There was a child.

Small. A girl, by the coat, though the coat was a man's, an enlisted man's greatcoat with the buttons changed, belted twice around her body so she looked like a bundle someone had dropped. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, the way hair gets cut in a hurry or by an amateur or both. She stood absolutely still at the edge of the ditch, facing the hospital door, and she was not crying, and she was not moving, and in a yard full of orderlies and stretcher-bearers and truck noise, somehow no one had seen her yet.

No. That wasn't right. People had seen her. Jonah watched an orderly glance at her and glance away, filing her under *refugee, not our problem*, the same category the war filed everyone in. A driver walked past within arm's reach and didn't turn his head. The girl stood in the open and was invisible, and Jonah felt something turn over in her chest, cold and slow, like ice shifting in a river.

Because Jonah knew exactly what it meant to stand in a crowded place and be unseen. She had spent years perfecting it. It was supposed to be armor.

On a child it just looked like abandonment.

The girl was clutching something against her chest with both arms, the way children hold things they've been told matter more than they do. At this distance it looked like a clock—a mantel clock, ornate, old-fashioned, the kind that sat on a shelf in a parlor in a house that had a parlor. Even from thirty paces Jonah could see it was broken, the case cracked, one hand hanging loose.

The girl saw her looking. Their eyes met across the yard.

And the child did not do any of the things a lost child does. She did not run forward. She did not cry out. She did not wave or mouth *help me*. She simply stood there, holding her broken clock, looking at Jonah with an expression far too old for her face—an expression of assessment, almost, of a person checking whether the next adult was worth the risk of existing at.

Then, slowly, deliberately, she reached into the pocket of the enormous coat and drew out a folded piece of paper, and held it up. Not waving it. Holding it. Presenting it, the way you'd present credentials at a checkpoint.

Jonah crossed the yard. She told herself it was procedure—unaccompanied minors were supposed to be reported to the provost's office, logged, processed, transferred to the rear with the other displaced persons—but procedure was a story she told herself the way she told families *he went quickly*, and she knew it, and she crossed the yard anyway.

Up close the girl was perhaps eight, maybe younger; war aged them unpredictably, malnutrition and terror scrambling the usual arithmetic. Her face was smudged with ash. Her feet, below the coat's hem, were wrapped in rags. And her eyes—dark, level, unblinking—tracked Jonah's approach with the patience of something that had been waiting a long time and could wait longer.

Jonah crouched, bringing herself down to the child's altitude, and held out her hand for the paper.

The girl placed it in her palm with strange formality, then immediately returned both arms to the clock, hugging it, chin resting on its cracked wooden crown.

The note was written in a hurried hand, in pencil, on the back of a requisition form. Five words.

*Forget the face. Keep the clock.*

Jonah read it twice. The guns muttered beyond the trees. Behind her, the hospital door swung open and shut, open and shut, admitting and discharging the day's business of wounds and paperwork. Somewhere in her footlocker, two hundred and six names lay wrapped in a flour sack, all of them people the world had agreed to forget.

She looked up. The girl was watching her read, and there was something in her face—not hope, exactly. Something before hope. The thing hope grows from, if it's given soil.

"What's your name?" Jonah asked.

The girl said nothing. Her lips parted slightly, and closed. She shook her head once, a small precise motion, and pressed the broken clock tighter to her chest, and looked at Jonah with those old dark eyes as the first drops of evening rain began to fall.

Behind them, at the gate, a staff car Jonah didn't recognize rolled through, flying command pennants, and an officer she had never seen before leaned out to survey the yard—the wards, the tents, the laundry lines, the nurses, the child—with the unhurried attention of a man taking inventory.

Jonah rose slowly to her feet, put her body between the girl and the car, and began, quietly, to be afraid.
