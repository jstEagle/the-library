## 2. A Child at the Door

The child came with the frost.

Jonah saw her first from the ward window, a small shape on the hospital's gravel apron where the ambulance road met the treeline. Dawn was doing its gray work over the valley, and the shape did not move the way the living moved. It stood too still. The wounded who wandered were restless; they drifted toward the latrines or the cook fire or the gate, always toward something. This stillness was different. It was the stillness of something set down.

She had been awake for thirty-one hours. There was a lieutenant in bed nine who would likely lose his left arm by noon, and a private in bed two whose fever chart looked like a mountain range, and Sister Aldous had already asked twice why Jonah kept writing in that little notebook when there were bandages to boil. The correct thing to do—the thing that kept a person invisible, unremarkable, safe—was to send for the provost, report an unidentified civilian on hospital grounds, and go back to bed nine before the surgeon called for her.

Jonah put down the notebook. She went out into the cold.

* * *

Up close, the girl was perhaps seven years old, maybe eight—war made age hard to read, since it took the flesh off children the same way it took it off old men. She wore a coat too big for her, wool, good quality once, now stiff with dried mud. Her hair was dark and cut badly, in the way hair gets cut by someone in a hurry with a knife. Her feet were bare inside shoes meant for someone else, and her lips had gone the blue-gray of things left out overnight.

In her arms she held a clock.

Not a wristwatch, not an alarm clock from a barracks shelf—a mantel clock, brass-cased, heavy enough that Jonah wondered how the girl had carried it any distance at all. The glass face was shattered, the hands stopped at some meaningless hour, and a crack ran through the case like a river on one of the survey maps Jonah had seen pinned in the officers' mess. The girl held it against her chest the way other children held dolls or dogs: entirely, with her whole body, as if it were the last warm thing in the world.

There was no one else on the road. No cart tracks fresh in the frost. No figure retreating toward the trees. Whoever had brought her had come in the dark and gone the same way, which meant they had known exactly what they were doing.

Pinned to the girl's coat was a note, written in a careful hand, the kind of hand taught in schools that no longer existed:

*Forget the face.*

Three words. No name, no village, no explanation. Just an instruction, as if the child were contraband to be smuggled and the note were the manifest.

"Hello," Jonah said.

The girl looked up at her. Her eyes were dry—not the dryness of a child past crying, but of a child who had learned already, somewhere, somehow, that crying cost more than it bought. She looked at Jonah the way soldiers looked at new nurses: assessing, deciding what she could afford to expect.

"You're frozen through," Jonah said. "Can you walk?"

The girl nodded. One small motion. Then she looked down at the clock in her arms, and Jonah understood: she could walk, but she could not be separated from what she carried. Fine. People came to field hospitals holding stranger things. Jonah had taken shrapnel fragments out of a man who refused to surrender his boot, because his brother had given him the boot.

"Bring it, then."

They walked together across the gravel, the girl's steps small and precise, the clock clutched tight, and Jonah felt the eyes of the ward window on her back—Sister Aldous, certainly, and probably young Pimm, who missed nothing and reported everything. Let them look. A nurse bringing in a half-frozen child was not a crime. Not yet.

* * *

By the rules—and the camp was nothing but rules now, rules stacked on rules like sandbags—a civilian child found on hospital grounds was to be documented and transferred to the civil affairs office within twenty-four hours. Jonah knew this because she had read the standing orders, all of them, the way she read everything: closely, quietly, keeping track of what the paper said versus what actually happened. Civil affairs was a desk in a requisitioned farmhouse forty kilometers east, staffed by a captain who processed displaced persons the way a mill processes grain. What came out the other end nobody asked about, and the ones who asked were reassigned.

Twenty-four hours. That was what the rules gave her. Jonah decided, standing at the stove in the supply room with the girl wrapped in two army blankets and thawing by degrees, that she would use every hour of it, and then decide again.

The girl would not let go of the clock even to eat. Jonah solved this by setting the bowl of barley soup on the girl's knees so both hands stayed free—one curled around the clock's cracked case, the other working the spoon. The girl ate the way she did everything: completely, silently, watching Jonah over the rim of the bowl.

"Do you have a name?" Jonah asked.

Nothing. Not a shake of the head, not a shrug. Just those steady eyes, and behind them something moving, calculating, the way a soldier calculates whether a position can be held.

"Can you speak?"

A pause. Then, slowly, deliberately, the girl shook her head.

It might have been true. It might not have been. Jonah had spent three years learning to read men's silences—the silence of a man too weak to talk, the silence of a man who didn't trust you, the silence of a man deciding how much to tell the officer who'd ask him later what the nurse said. This was none of those. This was the silence of a locked door in a house that had been searched before.

"All right," Jonah said. "You don't have to."

And then, because the girl's eyes had dropped to the notebook sticking out of Jonah's pocket, and because some instinct older than caution made her do it, Jonah took the notebook out and opened it to the last written page and turned it toward the child. Names. Columns of names, dates, places. Small names, names that appeared nowhere else.

The girl studied the page for a long time. Then she reached out with one finger—not touching the page, hovering above it—and traced a line down the column, the way you'd trace a road on a map. When she got to the bottom, she looked up at Jonah.

Something passed between them in that look that Jonah would spend years failing to describe. It was not gratitude. It was recognition—the specific, terrible recognition of one keeper of names meeting another. The girl knew what a list of names was for. She knew what it meant that someone had bothered.

Then she drew the clock closer and hid her face against its cold brass, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

* * *

Sister Aldous found them there within the hour, because Sister Aldous found everything within the hour.

"Aldous," Jonah said, before the woman could speak. "She was on the road at dawn. Half-frozen. Note pinned to her coat."

"I heard." Aldous was sixty, built like a field kitchen, and had been nursing since a war with a different number attached to it. She looked at the girl the way she looked at a wound she hadn't yet decided how to treat. "Provost knows?"

"Not yet."

"And you're telling me this why?"

"Because you'll hear it anyway, and I'd rather you heard it from me."

That earned her a grunt, which from Aldous was practically a treaty. The sister crouched, joints cracking, and examined the girl with the impersonal thoroughness she applied to everything—turning her chin with one finger, checking her scalp, peeling back a blanket edge to look at her feet. The girl endured it without flinching, the clock pressed to her chest.

"No marks," Aldous said finally. "No lice, no sores. Someone fed her, recently. Someone walked her to our gate in the dark and left her where the morning shift would find her." She straightened up. "You know what that means."

"It means someone wanted her found."

"It means someone wanted her *here*, Vasquez, which is a different animal altogether." Aldous's voice dropped. "Commandant Reiss's adjutant does a registration sweep Thursday. Unregistered persons get transferred. You've seen the transfers."

Jonah had seen the transfers. Trucks at dawn, the same direction as the civil affairs desk, and nothing ever came back.

"She stays in my section," Jonah said. "I'll list her as a convalescent under my care. Pending identification."

"With what authority?"

"With whatever authority you decide to lend me."

Aldous stared at her for a long moment, and Jonah watched the calculation move behind the older woman's eyes—the same calculation every nurse in this camp performed daily, weighing risk against conscience on scales worn smooth from use. Then Aldous snorted, pulled a second blanket off the supply shelf, and dropped it over the girl's shoulders.

"Convalescent," she said. "Pending identification. And Vasquez—" she pointed one thick finger, "—if Reiss asks me, I never saw her before today either. But you keep that notebook of yours out of sight. Whatever else you are, you're not stupid."

She left. The girl watched the door swing shut, then looked up at Jonah and pointed, very precisely, at the pocket where the notebook lived.

"Yes," Jonah said. "That's exactly the trouble."

* * *

She should have slept. She had been on her feet for most of two days, and there was an arm to come off at noon, and exhaustion made mistakes and mistakes in this place had weight, real weight, the kind that ended careers or worse. The correct thing was to hand the girl to Pimm, crawl into the nurses' bunkroom, and sleep until the world required her again.

Instead Jonah sat on an overturned crate beside the stove and watched the child sleep, and thought about the word *forget*.

*Forget the face.* Whoever wrote it had not said *protect her* or *keep her safe*. They had given an instruction aimed at the future—at everyone who would ever look at this child and be tempted to remember her. Which meant the danger wasn't finished. Which meant the people who needed to forget her face were still alive, possibly nearby, possibly wearing rank. And whoever had carried her through the dark to a hospital full of witnesses had gambled that among all those witnesses, someone would refuse to forget.

Jonah had built three years of safety on a simple architecture: history happened elsewhere, to other people, to the named and the ranked and the dead. She moved through it unseen, recording what the record omitted, and invisibility was her armor. As long as no one looked at her, no one could aim at her. The ledger stayed hidden. The ledger stayed safe. She stayed safe.

But the girl had looked at her. Had traced one finger down a column of names with the tenderness of someone reading scripture, and looked up, and Jonah had felt the armor take its first hit—not a breach yet, just a crack, a fine hairline fracture running through the conviction that she could stand outside of anything.

Because here was the truth she had been avoiding since dawn: the girl was now a name only Jonah knew. An unnamed child, unregistered, undocumented, existing nowhere on paper except in the margin of a hidden notebook where Jonah had already, without quite deciding to, penciled a single line:

*One girl, arrived at dawn. Carrying time.*

It was a stupid phrase. It was sentimental. It was exactly the kind of entry that could hang her if the notebook were ever found, and she had written it anyway, because some part of her had understood, watching that finger hover over the columns, that this was what the ledger was actually for. Not protest. Not defiance, even. Witness. The refusal, one name at a time, to do what the note pinned to a child's coat had instructed the whole world to do.

Outside, the frost was burning off the ambulance road. Somewhere beyond the treeline, the guns had started their morning argument, distant and rhythmic, the sound the whole valley had learned to sleep through. Noon was coming, and bed nine, and an arm. The world required her.

The girl stirred against the clock and did not wake.

Jonah rose, tucked the blanket higher around the small shoulders, and went to answer it—but at the door of the supply room she stopped, because down the corridor, past the ward, she could see an unfamiliar figure being escorted through the camp gate: a tall man in a civilian coat, carrying a leather map case, talking with the adjutant who handled Thursday's sweeps.

She didn't know his name. She didn't know yet that he would change everything.

She only noticed, with the reflexive attention of someone who catalogued details for a living, that he walked like a man who had been sent away from somewhere, and that he glanced—at the ward, at the windows, at the gravel apron where the frost still held the small prints of bare feet in oversized shoes—with the eye of someone measuring ground.

Then the surgeon's bell rang for bed nine, and Jonah went.
