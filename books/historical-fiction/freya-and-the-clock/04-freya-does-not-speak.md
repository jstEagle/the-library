## 4. Freya Does Not Speak

The girl did not cry at night, and that frightened Jonah more than crying would have.

Crying was a language she knew how to answer. She had spent three years learning it in the ward — the different pitches of pain, the flat gray whine of a man who had given up, the sudden shrieks of morphine dreams. She could translate all of it. But this child lay on her cot in the corner of the linen room with her eyes open and her hands folded over the broken clock as if someone might come to take it, and she made no sound at all.

Jonah had tried everything in a week's time. She tried questions gentle and blunt, in three languages and fragments of a fourth. She tried food first and food later, warmth, cold water, a clean bandage on a scrape the girl didn't seem to notice having. She tried leaving her alone, which was supposed to be the trick with wild things, though the nurses who advised it kept glancing at the girl as if they half expected her to vanish like smoke.

Nothing worked. The girl looked at Jonah with dark, patient eyes and did not speak.

She wasn't deaf. Jonah tested that early, dropping a tray behind her once, guiltily, and watching the girl flinch before the crash even finished. She wasn't simple, either; you could see the machinery working behind her face, quick and orderly. When Jonah held up two fingers, then two biscuits, the girl understood the exchange without being taught it.

She simply would not — or could not — speak.

*Forget the face*, the note had said, in a woman's careful hand. Whoever left her at dawn that morning had wanted no trail, no questions, no connection drawn between them and the child standing at the door of a field hospital holding a clock with its guts spilled out. And so far Jonah was complying beautifully, because she had learned precisely nothing about where the girl had come from.

Until Thursday, when the girl began to answer after all.

* * *

It happened at the end of a long shift, during the dead hour after evening rations when the ward went quiet and the lamps burned low. Jonah came into the linen room to fetch a blanket and found the child sitting cross-legged on the floor, surrounded by an arrangement of objects so deliberate it stopped her in the doorway.

The girl had gathered things from wherever children gather things — a button off the floor, a splintered wooden spoon, a scrap of blue cloth torn from somewhere, a bent nail, a strip of tin, and the gears of the clock, which she'd laid out in a row by size, smallest to largest, gleaming like little brass teeth.

And around them, arranged with unmistakable intention: the spoon lay pointing north toward the window. The button sat beside the nail. The blue cloth had been folded into a rough square, a house-shape or a flag-shape, and the strip of tin curved around it like a wall.

The girl looked up at Jonah. Then back at her arrangement. Then up again. *Do you understand?* said the look.

"No," Jonah admitted, kneeling down slowly, the way you kneel to anything you don't want to startle. "But I want to."

She pointed at the blue cloth. "Is this home?"

The girl's face did something complicated — not quite a nod, not quite a flinch. She picked up the cloth square and pressed it flat between her palms, hard, as if trying to make it hold still.

Then she picked up the largest gear, the one from the clock's mainspring, and set it down on top of the cloth house.

And then, with terrible patience, she took each of the other gears away, one by one, and put them behind her back, until only the big one remained sitting where the home had been.

Jonah felt the cold move through her the way it moved through the ward at night, under the doors, along the floors.

"You're telling me what happened," she said quietly. "People gone. Taken away."

The girl watched her. The lamp flickered between them.

"Who took them?" Jonah asked, and immediately hated herself for it, because the child's face closed like a shutter, and she reached for the clock casing and drew it against her chest, and the conversation was over.

But it was not over. It had only begun in a new language, one Jonah now had to learn fast enough to keep up.

* * *

Over the next days the arrangements came steadily, whenever the ward slept and the linen room filled with lamplight. The girl — Freya, because she had answered to nothing else and Jonah needed something to call her besides *child*, and because the single word written on the tag inside her collar had been *Freya* in the same careful hand as the note — built her answers out of whatever the hospital offered.

A row of pebbles meant soldiers. A row of pebbles with one turned sideways meant soldiers coming from a direction nobody expected. A matchstick laid across a cup meant a road blocked. Two matchsticks crossed over a folded rag meant — Jonah never learned what, because the first time Freya made that shape she also covered it with her hand, and looked away, and would not be drawn back to it.

Some messages Jonah understood instantly, and wished she didn't. The day Freya lined up six buttons and then swept four of them off the table with a flat stroke of her small hand, Jonah sat very still afterward and thought about the ledgers hidden under her own mattress, the columns of names she recorded every night while the camp slept, and understood that she and this seven-year-old were doing the same arithmetic in different currencies. Counting what was missing. Refusing to let the missing be nothing.

Other messages defeated her completely. Freya once spent an entire evening arranging dried beans into spirals that grew tighter and tighter toward a center, frowning at them, adding and subtracting, until finally she scattered them in frustration. Jonah guessed at mathematics, at maps, at grief. She guessed wrong every time, and Freya forgave her every time, which was its own kind of unbearable.

What undid Jonah was not the messages. It was the smaller things.

That Freya saved half of every meal without being asked, wrapping bread in her sleeve, and Jonah realized with a lurch that the girl was rationing — that she had done this before, somewhere, for someone. That she flinched from boots but not from bare feet. That she woke at the sound of engines and slept through shelling, which told Jonah everything about which dangers had lived near her, and none of it good.

That she had begun, sometime in the second week, to leave a gear on Jonah's pillow at night. Always a different one. Never explained. But Jonah, lying awake with her hand resting on the small brass weight of it, understood it for exactly what it was, because she had invented the same currency herself: *I am keeping count of you too. You are entered in my book.*

She was in trouble. She knew it with the calm certainty of a nurse reading symptoms. You did not survive three years of war by letting a mute orphan rearrange your heart into patterns you couldn't read. Invisibility was armor; attachment was a target painted on your back. Every rule she had lived by said take the girl to the chaplain's column, hand her to the relief convoys, forget the face as instructed.

Instead she found herself mending the hem of the girl's dress with thread stolen from her own kit, and humming, God help her, actually humming, some half-remembered market song from before the war, and catching the child watching her with an expression that was almost — almost — a smile.

That was the evening Commandant Reiss chose to visit the ward.

* * *

She heard him before she saw him, the way everyone did. Reiss did not enter rooms; he arrived in them, preceded by a particular quality of silence, the sound of thirty people deciding at once not to be talking. He came down the ward with two aides trailing him and his hands clasped behind his back, pausing at bedsides to say something pleasant to the wounded men, and every word he said was pleasant, and every word of it landed like a hand patting your pockets.

"Sister Vasquez." His voice was warm gravel. "They tell me you run the quietest ward in the sector."

"They're tired men, Commandant. Tired men sleep."

"Tired men sleep." He smiled as if she'd said something clever rather than something obvious, and Jonah understood that this was part of the technique, that he made you feel you'd performed well while he decided what you were. "And yet I hear you've taken on an additional patient. Unregistered."

The word sat in the air between them like a scalpel on a tray.

"A child," Reiss continued, drifting down the aisle, touching the foot of a bed here, straightening a chart there. "Found at your door, my staff informs me. No papers. No unit. No name beyond whatever was pinned to her collar." He stopped at the doorway of the linen room, where Freya sat on her cot with the clock in her lap, and looked at her the way a man looks at inventory. "Curious thing, isn't it, Sister. Everyone in this war has papers. Even the dead have papers, eventually. And yet here is a child with none. As if someone went to considerable effort to make sure she couldn't be traced."

"Someone left her at our door in the freezing dark," Jonah said. Her voice came out steadier than her hands felt. "If they went to effort, Commandant, it was the effort of walking away."

"Mm." He turned the look on her. It was a good look, unhurried, faintly amused, the look of a man who had already opened the drawer and found it empty but enjoyed watching you wonder whether he'd found what he expected. "Compassion is a virtue, Sister Vasquez. I admire it. Truly. But compassion without registration becomes — complication. An unregistered person cannot be accounted for. And what cannot be accounted for..." He let the sentence finish itself in the silence.

"Is a child eating soup in my ward," Jonah said, "or is it something worse?"

"It is whatever the armistice finds it to be," Reiss said pleasantly, and Jonah felt the words go through her like cold water through cloth, because he had said *armistice* — casually, carelessly, the way men say things they are not yet authorized to know — and then he smiled again, wider, and she understood he had said it on purpose, to watch her face. "I'll want a report on her. Origins, circumstances, any information whatsoever regarding her family or place of origin. Within the week, please." He clasped his hands. "You've been asking around the camps about her, I'm told. Asking questions is your habit, I think. A thorough woman. Thoroughness will serve."

He gave Freya a small nod, the courtesy one gives furniture, and moved on down the aisle, and the ward exhaled behind him.

Freya had not moved during any of it. She sat with the clock against her chest, and her eyes were fixed on Jonah, and only when the commandant's footsteps faded did she do the smallest thing: she lifted her hand and touched her own collar, where the name-tag had been, and looked a question at Jonah that required no translation.

*I'm registered now,* Jonah wanted to tell her. *In my book, in my hand, in my keeping.* But aloud all she said was, "You're safe tonight," and hated how little of the truth fit inside it.

* * *

That night she wrote in the ledger by candlelight, the flame shielded inside an ammunition crate with a blanket over the gap, and her entries came faster than usual because fear always loosened her pen. A gunner's mate from the river crossing whose name appeared on no casualty list. Two brothers from the supply column, buried under a number. A woman from the refugee stream, no surname ever known, whom the records called *female, approx. 30.*

Then she paused, pen lifted, and looked across the room at the sleeping shape on the cot, the blanket risen and fallen with small steady breaths, the clock held even in sleep.

Slowly, on the last line of the page, Jonah wrote:

*Freya. Age uncertain. Origin unknown. Village unknown. Family unknown. Present witness: J. Vasquez.*

Below it, after a moment:

*Status: unregistered. Which means, in the language of this war: deniable. Which means: they can lose her the way they lost the others, and call the loss a clerical matter.*

She read the entry back. It was the first name in the ledger belonging to someone still alive, and somehow that made it heavier than all the rest together, because a dead man's name asks only to be remembered and a living child's name asks to be protected, and Jonah had promised herself three years ago that she would never again be responsible for anyone but herself.

Reiss wanted origins within a week. Origins were exactly what she didn't have — except.

Except the clock.

She crossed the room on silent feet and knelt beside the cot and eased the battered casing from under the girl's fingers, and Freya stirred but did not wake, and Jonah carried it to the candle and turned it in her hands. Brass gears, hairline cracks, a scorch mark along one side. And there, on the inner face of the case, half-hidden under a century of polish and grime and the violence of whatever had broken it: an engraving. Tiny letters, a date, and beneath them what looked like the beginning of a name or a place — worn nearly smooth, illegible in candlelight.

Somewhere in that scratched metal was a village, a family, an origin. Somewhere was the answer she owed Reiss, and the answer she owed the small breathing shape on the cot.

She needed someone who could read it. Someone who knew metal and engraving and the geography of erased places. Someone whose business was maps.

There was exactly one man in this camp who fit, and he had arrived last month in disgrace, assigned to redraw retreat routes nobody expected him to draw well — a cartographer named Elias Brandt, whom the officers ignored and the nurses fed extra, the way the invisible feed their own.

Jonah wrapped the clock in cloth and hid the ledger in the ammunition crate and blew out the candle, and in the dark she heard Freya turn over in her sleep and murmur — the first sound Jonah had ever heard her make, small and unfinished, half a word, swallowed again by dreams.

She stood listening in the black until the ward's ordinary noises closed back over it, and she thought: *a week*. One week to find a village in a broken clock, before the commandant's courtesy ran out and the girl became another omission in somebody's report.

She did not yet know that the man she was going to ask had his own omissions, his own erased things, his own brother-shaped hole in the world — or that the clock in her hands had already begun ticking toward all of it.
