## 5. The Commandant's Courtesy

The news came down the ward at midmorning the way bad news always did in a field hospital: not announced, but felt. A shift in the posture of the orderlies. A sudden industry among the men who usually leaned on their brooms. Sister Anselm moving down the rows straightening blankets that were already straight.

"Commandant's coming through," she said without stopping at my cot-side station. "Ward Three first. You've twenty minutes."

Twenty minutes was generous by Reiss's standards. I had seen him walk a supply tent in nine and leave three men reassigned for the crime of standing where he'd already looked.

I finished bandaging the forearm of a boy from the artillery who had learned to sleep through anything, including my hands, and then I did what every nurse in that camp had learned to do when the commandant's shadow lengthened: I made the ward beautiful. Beauty, for Reiss, meant order, and order meant nothing hidden — or so he liked to believe.

I tucked sheets to knife-edge tautness. I aligned the water pitchers, handles all facing out like soldiers at attention. I swept the floor twice, though it needed it once. And all the while my mind ran its old arithmetic, the arithmetic I had been doing since the first night I opened the ledger: *what is here that should not be.*

The ledger itself was not here. It never was, during daylight hours. It lived in the flour tin behind the cookhouse, wrapped in oilcloth, beneath four inches of flour that Marta the baker believed — because I had told her so, with a straight face and a lie about damp — was reserved for the officers' pudding. At night I brought it back to my cot and wrote by candle-shield, and at dawn it went to the tin again. Two hundred yards, twice a day, every day. A ritual as fixed as prayer.

But knowing where a thing is does not stop you from seeing it everywhere. As I moved down the ward I saw the ledger in my mind's eye — the small names, the careful columns, the dates in my own cramped hand — superimposed over everything Reiss might touch. Over the flour tin. Over my cot. Over the girl.

Freya sat on her pallet near the stove-end of the ward, where we had put her because she gravitated to warmth the way cats do, and arranged her objects. She had been doing this for six days now. She owned almost nothing — a wooden soldier missing one arm, a button of horn, a scrap of blue ribbon someone had given her, and the clock. The clock she did not arrange. The clock she kept apart from the others, always within reach of her hand, its broken face turned toward her as if it were a person whose attention she didn't want to lose.

She looked up when I passed and held out the horn button. This was our language. The button meant *stay*, or possibly *look*, or possibly nothing at all except *here is a thing I have*. I took it, closed it in my palm, and gave it back, which meant *I see you*. It was a poor language but it was ours, and I had come to dread how fluent I was becoming in it, because fluency meant attachment, and attachment meant exposure.

"You," I said softly, crouching by her pallet, "are going to be very quiet this morning. Can you do that? Quiet as a mouse."

She considered this with the gravity of a judge, then picked up the headless wooden soldier and laid him flat, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

Sister Anselm reappeared at the ward door. "He's early."

They were always early. That was the whole point of being commandant.

* * *

Reiss entered the way he always did, which was to say he entered before you registered that the door had opened. He was not a large man. He was a neat man, and neatness, in a camp full of mud and blood, read as a kind of violence. His boots made no sound on the swept boards. His uniform fit him the way a verdict fits a sentence.

Behind him came his adjutant, Lieutenant Voss, carrying a leather folio against his chest like a shield, and behind Voss, as always, came the silence. Reiss traveled with silence the way other officers traveled with aides-de-camp.

"Sister Vasquez." He said my name correctly, which was worse than getting it wrong. A man who mispronounces your name doesn't know you. Reiss knew everyone. "You've done wonders with the light in here."

"The windows were already south-facing, Commandant."

"And yet most men wouldn't notice." He smiled. He had a good smile; I will grant him that, because granting your enemy his strengths is the only honest form of fear. "May I?"

He gestured at the nearest cot, and though the question was addressed to me, he was already moving past the answer, the way he always did. He stopped at the artillery boy's bedside. The boy woke, saw the collar tabs, and tried to sit up. Reiss pressed him back down with two fingers, gently, a father's gesture performed by a man who had no children and wanted none.

"Gunner Hessler. Third battery." He read the chart without picking it up. "You were at Kessel Ridge."

"Yes, sir."

"How many guns did you lose?"

"Four, sir."

"And how many men?" A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Officially?"

The boy's eyes flicked to me and back. "I — don't know the number officially, sir."

"No," Reiss said pleasantly. "Very few do. That's rather the point of official numbers." He patted the blanket over the boy's knee and moved on, and I understood that the entire exchange had not been about the boy at all. It had been a tuning fork struck against the ward, and now Reiss would listen for what rang.

He walked the rows. He asked after bowels and fevers and morale, and he asked each question with such genuine-seeming warmth that the men answered him honestly, which was precisely the danger. He complimented Sister Anselm's stores ledger. He admired the new screens around the septic cases. He paused at the window and remarked that the view of the ridge was "almost pastoral, if one ignored the smoke," and laughed at his own joke, and Voss laughed too, on schedule.

And then he turned, unhurried, and looked at Freya.

I had known this moment was coming since the day I carried her inside. Six days of dreading it, six days of telling myself that a child among two hundred wounded men was furniture, invisible, beneath an officer's notice. But Reiss noticed everything. Noticing was his profession the way nursing was mine.

"Ah," he said. "And who is this?"

"A patient, Commandant."

"She looks remarkably recovered for a patient." He approached her pallet the way he approached everything: directly, without hurry, giving the object of his attention no room to be anywhere else. He crouched — actually crouched, boots and all, bringing his face level with hers — and smiled his good smile. "Hello. What's your name?"

Freya looked at him. She did not shrink, which surprised me; most children shrank from Reiss the way plants shrink from frost. She simply looked, with those gray eyes that seemed to hold more years than her body did, and said nothing.

"She's mute," I said. "Found wandering the road east of the crossroads. No papers, no identification. Deaf-mute, we believe, or at least she hasn't spoken since—"

"I didn't ask if she spoke, Sister. I asked her name." Still smiling. Still watching her, not me. "Perhaps she can write it."

"We've tried. She draws." I kept my voice level, bored even, a woman discussing bedpans. "Mostly circles. Wheels, we think. She's fond of that clock."

"Mm." Reiss rose, brushing nothing from his trousers, because there was never anything on his trousers. "A child. No papers. Found on a road during a withdrawal, in a sector where three villages were cleared last month." He said it lightly, conversationally, a man discussing weather. "You know, Sister, there's a regulation about unregistered persons. I wrote half of it myself, so I'm rather fond of it. Persons of unknown provenance are to be reported to camp administration within twenty-four hours."

"She was reported."

"To whom?"

"To you, Commandant. Verbally. By me. The morning after she arrived." This was true. I had stood in his office doorway and told him, and he had waved one hand without looking up from his maps and said, *the hospital may keep strays, Sister, God knows someone must.* I had counted on his indifference. I had staked a child's life on a busy man's carelessness.

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger; disappointment, perhaps, that I had closed a door he'd left open on purpose. "Did I acknowledge that report in writing?"

"You acknowledged it verbally, Commandant. As did Lieutenant Voss, who was present."

Voss's ears went pink. Reiss laughed — a real laugh, warm, delighted, terrifying. "Well. You have me there. Verbal reports are binding in this army, aren't they, Voss? We're terribly informal." He turned back to Freya, who had gathered the clock into her lap while the adults talked over her head, both arms around it now, chin resting on its broken face. "That's quite an attachment. Where did she get it?"

"I don't know. It was with her."

"Broken, I see." He extended a hand toward it, casually, the way a man reaches to pet a dog.

Freya pulled the clock against her chest and turned her shoulder.

For a moment — half a breath, no more — the good smile stayed exactly where it was, and something underneath it did not. Then Reiss straightened, spread his hands in cheerful surrender, and stepped back. "Quite right. Private property is sacred. Even broken property." He looked at me over the child's head. "Especially broken property, some would say. There's a great deal of it about."

"War makes wreckage, Commandant."

"It does. And wreckage, Sister, has a way of accumulating in corners until someone comes along with a broom." He buttoned his coat, though it wasn't cold. "Keep her well. Children are the future, and all that. But do have her papers drawn up properly, won't you? Registration forms, provenance statement, the signature of a responsible officer. I'll send Voss back tomorrow with the forms. He's very thorough." A pause, perfectly weighted. "We all should be, in times like these."

He thanked Sister Anselm for her hospitality. He wished Gunner Hessler a swift recovery. At the door he paused and said, without turning around, "South-facing windows. Clever. One sees so much more with good light."

Then he was gone, and the silence he traveled with went with him, and the ward exhaled.

* * *

I did not go to the flour tin that evening.

I had planned to. All afternoon the plan sat in me like a swallowed stone: wait for dark, take the ledger from the tin, bring it back, write the day's entries — two more names, a gunner from the second battery whom the morning report listed as *returned to duty* when he had in fact died at four o'clock with his hand in mine — and then decide. Because Reiss had changed the arithmetic. *Forms tomorrow. Provenance statements. The signature of a responsible officer.* A paper trail pointed at Freya like a rifle barrel, and a paper trail, once begun, could be followed backward — to the road, to the crossroads, to whatever had happened in the villages that were *cleared*, and forward, after the armistice everyone whispered about, to wherever unregistered children were transferred to.

Transferred. The word had a clean administrative sound, like *laundered*.

So I went to the cookhouse after supper, under the pretext of checking the officers' pudding stores, and I knelt in the flour dust and lifted the tin — and stopped.

Because the tin was where they would look. Not tonight, maybe not tomorrow. But Voss was thorough, and thoroughness is patience wearing a uniform, and eventually there would be an inspection of stores, a routine audit, a bored clerk with a list. The ledger could not live in the tin anymore. The ledger could not live anywhere that logic could find.

I carried the empty-feeling tin back across the yard in the dark, heart hammering at nothing, at everything, and let myself into the ward through the side door where the night nurse owed me a favor and asked none.

Freya was awake. Of course she was. She slept the way soldiers on watch sleep, in shallow sips, surfacing at every sound. She watched me cross the moonlit aisle to the shelf where her few possessions lived, and she watched me pick up the clock, and her whole body went rigid.

"Hush," I whispered. "Hush, little one. I'm not taking it. I'm going to give it something to carry."

She slid off her pallet and came to me, barefoot on cold boards, and stood close enough that I could feel her breath, and together — because I could not have done it otherwise, and because some part of her understood before I explained — we opened the clock.

Elias Brandt had loosened the casing's back plate days ago, when he first examined it, hunting the maker's mark. He'd left it sitting slightly proud of the housing, muttering about a stripped screw, and I had thought nothing of it then. Now it came away in my hands like a held breath released. Inside: the movement, brass gears furred with dust, the mainspring coiled in its barrel like a sleeping animal. And below the movement, in the base of the case, a cavity. Hollow. Waiting. Clockmakers hid weights there; I would hide something heavier.

I had brought the ledger from the tin after all — I realized this only as I knelt there, that my feet had made the decision before my mind caught up, that I had gone to the cookhouse not to fetch it but to confirm what I already knew: nowhere was safe, so safety had to be made, built, engineered, like a bridge under fire.

I wrapped the ledger in the same oilcloth, pressed it flat — it fit, it *fit*, as if the case had been waiting years for exactly this cargo — and lowered it into the cavity beneath the gears. Then the plate. Then the screw, tightened with the tip of a scalpel until the metal bit and held.

The clock weighed almost exactly what it had weighed before. That was the miracle of it. Two hundred names, two hundred lives the reports had erased, and the scale registered nothing.

Freya touched the case once, flat-palmed, the way you'd check a fevered forehead. Then she looked up at me, and in the moonlight her gray eyes were steady, and she did the thing she had never done before: she took my hand. Not for the button game. Not to ask or show. Just took it, and held it, and looked at the clock, and then at me, as if to say: *now it carries us both.*

I sat down on the floor beside her pallet, because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me, and I held that small warm hand and understood that the armor was gone. Invisibility, distance, the long discipline of not mattering to anyone — Reiss had walked through all of it in eleven minutes with a smile and a question about paperwork. There was no longer any version of the future in which I was a woman who kept records and stayed safe. There was only a woman who kept records and was found, sooner or later, holding them.

Fear should have hollowed me out. Instead it did something else. It sharpened. Every nerve I owned came to a point, and at the center of the point was a single clear thought, cold and bright as a probe: *he will send the forms tomorrow, and Voss is thorough, and I have one night, and then another, and I will use every one of them.*

Across the ward, a man moaned in his sleep and called for his mother. The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere beyond the wire, on the ridge, a flare went up and drifted down, red as a wound, lighting the smoke from the burning sectors for three seconds before the dark took it back.

Freya fell asleep sitting up, her hand still in mine, the clock on her knees.

In the morning, Lieutenant Voss arrived with the forms — and with him, unannounced, carrying a battered leather map case and a requisition chit signed in Reiss's own hand, came a stranger: a thin, stooped man with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who had once looked at the world closely enough to be ruined by it.

"Cartographer," Voss said, by way of introduction and dismissal in one word. "Brandt. He's to redraw the retreat routes. He'll need workspace."

Sister Anselm assigned him the corner table by the dispensary.

He set down his case, unrolled his instruments with the tenderness of a priest handling relics, and the first thing his eyes went to — before the maps, before the window, before anything — was the clock on the shelf above Freya's empty pallet.

He went very still.

Then he took out his pens, and began to draw, and did not look at it again.

But I had seen his face. And a face like that, I knew, was a door — and doors, in my experience, opened in both directions.
