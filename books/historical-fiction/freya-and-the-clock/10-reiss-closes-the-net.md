## 10. Reiss Closes the Net

The notices went up on a Tuesday, nailed to the mess hall door and the latrine partition and the post where the flag hung at half-mast more often than not. They were typed, which was itself an omen—Reiss's orders were always typed, never handwritten, as though ink could be denied and print could not.

BY ORDER OF COMMANDANT REISS, they read. COMMENCING THURSDAY, ALL PERSONS RESIDING WITHIN THE HOSPITAL COMPOUND WILL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR INSPECTION OF CREDENTIALS AND QUARTERS. UNREGISTERED PERSONS DISCOVERED AFTER THE ARMISTICE WILL BE TRANSFERRED TO PROVINCIAL AUTHORITIES FOR PROCESSING. THIS IS NOT A PUNISHMENT. THIS IS ORDER.

Jonah read it twice, then folded her hands behind her back the way she did when she wanted to look like a woman with nothing in them but patience.

*Transferred for processing.* She had been a nurse long enough to know what that phrase meant. It meant a truck. It meant a road. It meant a child who did not exist being sent to a place that did not appear on maps—and she knew now, thanks to Elias Brandt and his careful fingers turning gears in lamplight, exactly how it looked when a place stopped appearing on maps. Halden had not been destroyed. Halden had been *processed.*

Freya stood beside her at the notice board, small hands in her coat pockets, reading the words with the flat attention of a child who had learned to read danger before she'd learned to read letters. She couldn't have understood every word. But she understood the shape of it. Children always understood the shape of a thing aimed at them.

"Go wash your hands," Jonah said gently. "Matron will want the water changed before rounds."

Freya didn't move. Her eyes stayed on the paper, on the word TRANSFERRED, and then they came up to Jonah's face, and the question in them was so plain, so unguarded, that Jonah felt it go through her like cold water down a sleeve.

"No one is transferring you," she said. "Not while I'm standing."

It was the wrong thing to say, or the right thing said too loudly; a corporal at the woodpile turned his head. Jonah kept her voice level after that, kept her instructions practical—water, linens, the boil of instruments—and Freya went inside, but she looked back once from the doorway, and Jonah pretended not to see, because pretending not to see had kept her alive through four years of war and might, if she was careless about it, stop working altogether.

* * *

The inspections began Thursday morning at seven, which told Jonah something about Reiss: he believed in starting early, in catching people before they had arranged their faces. He came through the officers' ward first with two clerks and a sergeant carrying a ledger of his own—a thick, ruled thing, bound in government leather, everything his opposite in everything Jonah kept folded against her ribs.

She watched him work. That was the education of that morning: watching Reiss work.

He was charming. Everyone said so, even the nurses who flinched at his name. He remembered names—remembered that Nurse Okafor's mother had died in the spring fever, remembered that Nurse Lindqvist sang, remembered that Jonah had once pulled shrapnel fragments out of a lieutenant everyone had expected to die. He complimented the wards. He asked after supplies in a voice full of sympathy, as though the shortages wounded him personally. And all the while his clerks moved through the rooms behind him like men counting sheep they intended to buy, opening footlockers, sliding hands between mattress and frame, lifting the lids of instrument cases and running fingers along the undersides of shelves.

By ten they had cleared the officers' wing and the pharmacy stores. By eleven they stood at the door of the nurses' quarters, and Jonah felt the ledger against her body like a coal sewn into her coat.

It wasn't there, of course. She had moved it weeks ago, the night after Reiss's first visit, when she'd sat up with a screwdriver pried from the maintenance kit and taken the back off Freya's broken clock. The casing was brass and heavy, built to house a movement larger than the one it held; there was room inside for the clock's guts and grief both. She had wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, flattened it, fitted it against the inner wall of the case beneath the gear train, and screwed the plate back down with hands steadier than she had any right to expect. The clock sat on the shelf above Freya's cot, hands frozen at twenty past four, looking for all the world like a broken clock.

Now Reiss paused in front of that shelf, and Jonah discovered how long a single second could be made to last.

"You've taken in a child," he said. Not a question. He consulted the clerk's ledger anyway, drawing out the moment. "Unregistered. No papers, no province of origin, no name but the one you've given her." He pronounced *Freya* carefully, as though tasting a foreign dish to determine whether it was safe. "Where did she come from, Nurse Vasquez?"

"The road," Jonah said. "Someone left her at the gate."

"People leave things at gates," Reiss agreed pleasantly. "Rubbish. Ordnance. Sometimes children." He picked up the clock, weighed it in his hand, and Jonah's heart performed a maneuver she would not have believed possible without fainting. "Heavy for its size."

"It's brass," Jonah said. "Good brass. The girl won't let us near it. She'll want it when—" She stopped herself from saying *when she's ready*, because ready implied a future, and futures were precisely what this man was auditing.

But Reiss had already set the clock down. He was watching her instead, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had spent years learning that people told you where they hid things by the way they stood near them. Jonah kept her posture bored. She thought about ledgers. She thought about linen inventories. She let her face go soft and slightly stupid, the face of a tired nurse at the end of a double shift, and after a moment Reiss smiled his charming smile and moved on.

In the doorway he paused. "After the armistice," he said, to the room in general and to no one, "there will be order. Everything in its proper column. You'll find I'm reasonable, Nurse Vasquez. I only ask that people be *countable*."

When his boots had faded down the corridor, Jonah sat down on Freya's cot and found her hands were shaking so badly she had to press them between her knees.

*Everything in its proper column.* She had spent four years writing names into columns of her own—the dead, the displaced, the ones the reports skipped—and she had told herself those columns were invisible. Now she understood that Reiss had columns too, and that his columns were not passive records but instruments, that to be entered in his ledger was to be scheduled, routed, processed. Two books, two truths, and a child sitting in the space between them like a coin balanced on its edge.

That evening she checked the clock's casing by lamplight, unscrewing the plate with Freya asleep three feet away, breathing in the small even rhythm of a child who trusted the room. The oilcloth bundle sat snug against the brass, dry, undisturbed. Two hundred and six names. Two hundred and seven, if she counted the entry she'd made just that morning—a private, a boy of seventeen, omitted from the casualty report of a sector whose name she'd had to write twice to get right.

She screwed the plate back down. She wound nothing, because the clock had no spring left to wind. It simply sat there holding its dead, keeping no time at all, and Jonah lay in the dark listening to Freya breathe and understood, with the clarity that comes only at three in the morning, that the armistice everyone prayed for would be the most dangerous day of all of their lives.

Peace, for men like Reiss, was not an ending. It was a filing deadline.

* * *

She found Elias at the survey tent at dusk, hunched over drafting paper with his bad pen and his worse coffee. Since the commandant's first visit he had redrawn retreat routes until they were works of fiction—roads that had never existed, bridges that could bear weight they could not, a whole geography of convenient lies rendered in beautiful, deniable ink. His hands were stained black to the second knuckle. He looked up when she came in, read her face in half a second the way he read contour lines, and put the pen down.

"They posted the notices," he said.

"You've seen them."

"I've seen them." He pushed the coffee aside. "Tell me."

So she told him—the clerks, the mattress-slitting, the pause at the shelf, the clock lifted in Reiss's hand like a piece of evidence that hadn't yet decided what it was evidence of. She told him about the word *processing*. Elias listened without moving except for one muscle in his jaw that worked and worked.

"He held it," Jonah finished. "He said it was heavy for its size."

"Brass is heavy," Elias said. "Guilt is heavier." He stood, crossed to the tent flap, and looked out at the darkening compound—at the sentries walking their posts, at the new wire Reiss had ordered strung around the motor pool, at the flag coming down for the night. "There's something you should know. A week ago I was summoned to headquarters to draw supply routes for the occupation authority. Provisional maps. Post-armistice administration." He kept his back to her. "On the desk beside my paper was a folder stamped for the historical section. Official record compilation. They're already assembling it, Jonah. Before the guns stop. The victors' historians are traveling with the advance staffs, and they are compiling the war as it will be remembered—and they are compiling it from Reiss's files."

The tent seemed to shrink. "His files."

"Casualty returns. Displacement schedules. Transfer orders." He turned around. "Whatever isn't in his files will not be in the history. Whatever he burned will never have happened. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your book isn't just a protest anymore. In three weeks' time it may be the only copy of the truth that exists anywhere on this earth."

Jonah had known this, in the way you know the load-bearing wall of your own life. Hearing it said aloud was different. Hearing it said aloud was standing in a room and watching someone point, calmly, at the wall, and say *that is all that holds up the roof.*

"Then we move it," she said. "Out of the camp. To someone outside."

"To whom? The roads are closed to unregistered traffic. Every courier is searched. Every church cellar and farm loft within forty kilometers has been emptied twice." He came back to the table, spread his hands on the drafting paper, and looked at what he'd drawn there—not a route map but a small, precise diagram of the hospital compound, done from memory, every building labeled. "And now there's a girl to consider. The notice isn't aimed at you, Jonah. You're registered; you're countable. It's aimed at her. After the armistice, Reiss doesn't need a reason to take her. He needs only a column, and she's already been written into it. *Transfer for processing.*" His finger rested on the word. "I've drawn erased things before. I know how the erasure is done. First the village goes from the map. Then the road to the village. Then the name of the road. Then the language of the name. And the children—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Children get processed last, because children remember, and memory is the only part of an erased place that can walk out on its own feet."

Jonah thought of Freya asleep with her breath going in and out, in and out, the metronome Jonah had begun to need the way other people needed prayer.

"So what do we do?" she asked. "Elias. What do we do?"

For a long moment he studied the compound diagram, and she watched him do what he always did—reduce a problem to terrain, reduce terrain to lines, reduce lines to decisions. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and very certain.

"We make her countable," he said. "Properly. Under our protection, under our names, before Reiss's clerks can file her under theirs. There are forms. Emergency wardship provisions—it's a loophole wide enough to drive an ambulance through, and Reiss knows it exists, which means he's left it open on purpose, which means it's a trap, which means we walk through it anyway, but carefully, and soon. Because Jonah—" He looked up at her, and the cartographer's calm cracked just enough to show the man underneath. "The armistice rumor is real. I've seen the fuel requisitions for a victory parade in the provincial capital. Ten days. Maybe less. When the shooting stops, the net closes, and everything Reiss has been waiting to do quietly, he will do quickly, under cover of celebration."

Ten days. Jonah stood in the lamplight of the survey tent and counted backward from ten days the way she had once counted pulses: what could be done, what must be done, what would kill them if attempted.

Behind her, outside, a sentry called the hour, and somewhere in the nurses' quarters a broken clock sat on a shelf above a sleeping child, holding two hundred and seven names against its brass heart, keeping perfect silence, keeping no time at all.

Jonah turned toward the door.

"First thing tomorrow," she said, "we fill out the forms."
