## 14. Reiss Finds the Clock

The notice went up on the ward door at noon, pinned by a corporal who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes: *Inspection of premises and persons, per order of Commandant Reiss. All effects to be laid open for examination. Compliance is not optional.*

Jonah read it twice, then folded her hands behind her back so no one could see them shake.

Three days. That was how long she'd had the ledger inside the clock casing—three days of carrying the thing from her cot to the ward and back like a woman transporting a bomb, which in every way that mattered she was. Two hundred and eleven names. Two hundred and eleven people the army had decided did not exist, written in pencil on paper thin enough to read through, sealed behind the brass face of a dead girl's clock. And tomorrow morning Reiss would walk through this ward with his charming smile and his gloved hands, and everything would be laid open.

She found Elias in the map room, hunched over a survey table with dividers in his fist, pretending to work. He'd been pretending to work for two days; Reiss had him redrawing supply routes he had almost certainly already memorized, keeping him close the way a man keeps a dog he suspects of chewing slippers.

"We need to move it tonight," Jonah said, without greeting.

Elias set down the dividers. "Move it where? They're inspecting cots. They'll inspect the latrine trench. I watched a sergeant go through a dead man's boots yesterday."

"I know."

"Then where?"

Jonah had spent the afternoon turning the question over and finding nothing on the other side of it. Bury the ledger and the damp would take the pencil in a week. Give it to one of the nurses and you multiplied the risk without reducing it. The tribunal convoy was rumor and hope, days away at best, and Reiss's inspections were tomorrow at first light.

"The clock stays where it is," she said finally. "On Freya's cot. It's always been there. It's been there since before any of us knew what was inside it. The best hiding place is the one nobody thinks to look in because it's been looking at them for weeks."

Elias was quiet. Through the map room's cracked window they could hear the evening gun, and beneath it, fainter, the sound of documents burning somewhere behind the commandant's office—a smell that had become as ordinary as woodsmoke.

"He's not searching for maps," Elias said. "You understand that. Maps are the excuse. He's searching for the thing that made Corwin talk to you before he died. Someone told him Corwin confessed something. He's hunting a ledger he can't prove exists, and he's going to turn over every sock in this camp to find it."

"Then we give him socks."

"And if he opens the clock?"

Jonah looked at the table, at the half-drawn route lines running toward towns whose names she'd recorded beside the dates they stopped appearing in reports.

"Then a mute child's toy is broken," she said. "That's all he'll see. A broken clock. He won't know how to read what's inside it even if he sees it. Paper means nothing without context, Elias. I've counted on that from the beginning."

"That's a gamble."

"Everything left in this camp is a gamble. The only choice is what we bet."

He looked at her for a long moment, and something moved across his face—not argument but recognition, the expression of a man watching someone walk to the edge of a map and step off it.

"All right," he said. "But I want to do one thing first." He pulled a sheet of drafting paper toward him and began, quickly, in his precise cartographer's hand, to copy out a fragment of the retreat routes—the false ones, the official ones, the ones with Halden scraped off them like a scab. "If they take me, or take you, I want the shape of the lie documented too. The omissions are evidence. You taught me that."

"You never said I taught you anything."

"You never asked," he said, and kept writing.

* * *

They worked through the night, by the gray light of a shielded lamp, reassembling the clock with the ledger folded flat against the inside of its back panel, gears replaced around it in their correct positions so that only a man who knew how the mechanism should sit—and Reiss did not, and none of his sergeants would—could tell anything was missing. Freya sat up on her cot and watched them work, cross-legged, unblinking. She had not slept; Jonah suspected the child slept the way soldiers on watch slept, in shallow sips, waking at every bootfall.

Near dawn Jonah held the clock's face in both hands, ready to press it home, and paused. Inside, visible in the lamplight, the top page showed through the gear-work: a column of names, small and close-written, like seeds.

Freya reached out and touched the paper. Gently. The way she touched everything—deliberate, economical, as though language had been taken from her and replaced with attention.

"It's heavy, isn't it," Jonah said. "Even paper gets heavy."

Freya looked up at her. In the shielded light her eyes were very dark and very old, and Jonah thought, not for the first time, that whatever silence the girl carried was not emptiness but a room with the door shut, behind which something enormous was being carefully kept.

Jonah pressed the face into place. The clock sat on the blanket, hands frozen at nine minutes past four, whole and harmless and ordinary.

By six the ward smelled of carbolic soap and fear.

* * *

Reiss arrived at seven, and he arrived the way he did everything: beautifully.

He came with four men and a clerk, and he came smiling, shaking Sister Brenn's hand, complimenting the cleanliness of the floors, asking after the health of patients by name—he knew their names, that was the terrible thing, he had memorized the roster the way other men memorized scripture—and moving down the aisle of cots at a pace calculated to seem unhurried while missing nothing. His men followed him like shadow thrown by a strong lamp, opening footlockers, lifting mattresses, leafing through Bibles to check for papers between the pages.

Jonah stood at her station with her hands folded and her pulse tapping in her throat and watched him come.

He worked the far end of the ward first. She heard him pause at Private Anselm's cot—Anselm, who had lost both eyes and could hear everything—and say, warmly, "Corporal. Good morning. We haven't met formally, but I feel I know you; your name comes up in dispatches." It did not come up in dispatches. Jonah had checked. It was in *her* dispatches, the ones in pencil. Anselm turned his bandaged face toward the voice and said nothing, and Reiss laughed pleasantly at some joke of his own and moved on.

At the third cot he picked up a letter, read it, put it down. At the fifth he had the clerk record a pack of cards—"unauthorized currency"—with an apology to the card's owner that somehow made the confiscation feel like a gift. He was very good. He had been doing this his whole life, Jonah realized: making power feel like courtesy until the moment it didn't.

Then he reached Freya's cot.

The girl sat on it exactly as instructed, hands in her lap, the broken clock on the blanket before her. Jonah had rehearsed this with her in gestures: *sit still, don't look at him, don't look at the clock.* Freya had watched the instructions with that shuttered attention and given no sign of understanding, which meant either that she understood completely or not at all, and there was no way to know which.

"Well," said Reiss. "The famous guest."

The ward went quiet in the way a field goes quiet when hawks circle. Nurses bent to their work and listened with their whole spines.

He crouched by the cot—not sitting on it, never touching, Reiss touched nothing he might be accused of wanting—and studied Freya with frank, professional interest, the way he might study a bridge he intended to blow.

"They tell me you don't speak," he said. "That must be very restful. Words are so often misused, don't you find? People say things, and then the things get written down, and fifty years later the writing matters more than the truth." He smiled. "You're too young to have learned that. Somebody taught it to you early."

Freya looked at him.

Jonah kept her eyes on the dressing tray in front of her and counted her own heartbeats and thought, with a clarity that felt almost calm: *he knows. He doesn't know what, but he knows there's something, and he's going to stand here opening drawers until it falls out.*

Reiss straightened. "May I?" he said—to Freya, courteously, gesturing at the clock—and did not wait for permission, because children could not grant it.

He lifted the clock and turned it over in his gloved hands.

Jonah watched his fingers find the seam of the back panel. Watched him tilt it toward the window light. Watched him notice, as anyone would within three seconds, that the case was heavier than a clock that size should be—that something extra lived in it, something flat and dense, something that shifted faintly when he tilted it, the way paper shifts.

His eyebrows rose, a small performance of curiosity. He took out a pocketknife.

Time did not slow. That was the horror of it; time ran exactly as fast as it always ran, and Jonah stood rooted to the clean floor with two hundred and eleven names balanced in a stranger's hand, and the war outside went on shelling its distant hills, and a nurse named Marta dropped a basin somewhere behind her and did not pick it up.

Reiss slid the knife blade under the panel's edge.

"No."

The word came from the cot. Small, clear, cracked with disuse, like a hinge opening for the first time in a year.

Reiss stopped. Everyone in the ward stopped. Even the clerk's pen hung above the page.

Freya was looking at the clock, not at Reiss, and she said it again, louder, her voice rough and rusty and absolutely steady:

"*Broken.*"

One word. One lie. Delivered by a child whom three armies' worth of paperwork described as speechless, staring down the commandant of the camp as though he were a boy who had picked up something that wasn't his.

"It doesn't go," she added—two more words, extravagant now, reckless, spending her hoarded voice like a beggar spending a fortune—"it's *broken.*"

For a moment Reiss said nothing. Then he laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him—and set the clock down on the blanket with exaggerated care, and spread his empty hands.

"So it does talk," he said. "Well. There's a lesson in everything." He patted the clock's head as if it were a dog, and the gesture, more than anything else he had done, made Jonah's stomach turn, because it said he had noticed the weight, filed it away, and chosen—for now—to let it lie. He was not finished. He was simply patient. "Broken clocks," he announced to the ward at large, rising, buttoning his coat, "are to be catalogued as salvage. See that it goes in the requisition bin by week's end." His eyes crossed Jonah's on the way to the door, mild as milk. "Sister Vasquez. A pleasure, as ever."

And he was gone, taking his shadows with him, leaving the ward in a silence so complete Jonah could hear the clock's gears ticking faintly against the hidden paper, though she knew—she *knew*—that the mechanism was dead and had been for years.

* * *

Afterward she sat down hard on the edge of the next cot and discovered that her legs had simply stopped consulting her.

Marta brought water. Sister Brenn brought brandy she claimed not to know the provenance of. Neither of them spoke. Around them the inspection continued in the yard—she could hear lockers slamming, a soldier protesting, the clerk's flat voice reading numbers—but inside the ward the nurses arranged themselves around Jonah and Freya in a loose, unspoken guard formation, and no one asked any questions, because everyone had understood what they had just seen, and understanding was enough.

The requisition bin. He had ordered the clock into the requisition bin. By week's end. Which meant he hadn't believed the girl, and hadn't decided yet whether the clock was worth the trouble of admitting he hadn't, and had found the perfect bureaucratic solution: let the army confiscate it, and open it later, in private, with witnesses of his own choosing.

Four days. Maybe five.

Elias appeared in the doorway, drawn by some instinct, still holding his drafting pen. He looked at Freya, then at Jonah, and asked with his eyebrows what his mouth couldn't in a monitored room.

*Later,* Jonah mouthed.

Freya had picked up the clock and was holding it against her chest with both arms, the way she had held it the dawn she arrived—except that now Jonah could see the difference, and the difference undid her. A year ago the child had clutched the clock because it was all she had left. Today she was guarding it. She knew what was inside. She had known—of course she had known; she watched everything, she missed nothing—and she had spent her first words in a year not on grief, not on her village, not on her own name, but on a lie built to keep a nurse out of a cell.

Jonah had believed, for eleven chapters of her life, that invisibility was armor. That if you stayed small and quiet and useful, history would pass you by, happening elsewhere, to other people, to names in other people's ledgers. She had told herself that recording the dead without signing the record was prudence. She had told herself that taking in a mute child against orders was charity, contained, costless.

There was nothing contained about it anymore. The child had spoken for her. The child had lied—had *chosen*, deliberately, at terrible risk, knowing what speaking cost her and what silence had already cost her—to protect a woman who had done nothing yet to deserve it except write down names.

Jonah knelt in front of the cot, so their eyes were level.

"Thank you," she said.

Freya shrugged, small and terrible, a gesture learned from soldiers.

"No," Jonah said. "Listen to me. What you did—you don't have to do it again. Do you hear me? Not for me. Never again for me. The next time that man touches that clock, I will already be gone, and so will what's inside it, and he can catalogue an empty case until the end of the war."

Freya studied her face with that ferocious attention, and then, slowly, she reached out and pressed two fingers against Jonah's breastbone—an instruction, unmistakable, the way all her language was instruction.

*You carry it,* the gesture said. *In here. Where it belongs.*

Jonah covered the small hand with her own and found she could not speak at all, and understood, finally and completely, that neutrality was a country she had left months ago and simply hadn't noticed the border crossing. She was not a bystander who happened to keep records. She was a courier. The ledger was not her diary; it was a dispatch, addressed to whoever came after, and she was now its armed escort, and Reiss knew it, and the clock had four days.

She stood. Her legs held. Across the ward, Elias was watching her with an expression she was beginning to be able to read, and she crossed to him and said, quietly, under the cover of the returning noise of the camp:

"He wants the clock in the requisition bin by week's end. So it leaves this ward before then—out of the camp entirely." She glanced back once at the small figure on the cot, arms wrapped around brass and paper and two hundred and eleven souls. "And Elias—I'm not sending it with anyone else. Whatever has to happen, I carry it myself."

Elias nodded slowly. Then he leaned closer, and his voice dropped to almost nothing.

"Then you should know," he said, "before you decide anything. There's a wagon leaving for the crossroads the day after tomorrow. And Jonah—Reiss is riding escort."
