## 16. Tribunal at the Crossroads

The wheels did not come to the camp.

Jonah stood in the doorway of the ward with Freya's cold hand buried in hers and watched the convoy pass on the crossroads road, half a mile off, a long grey ribbon of lorries and mounted officers and a field kitchen that clattered like a dropped drawer. Painted on the lead lorry's canvas, white over mud: a scale, a sword, an open book. The tribunal insignia. She had seen it once stamped on a summons that had come for a colonel who never returned.

They were not stopping. They were going somewhere else.

"They'll take the main road north," Elias said at her shoulder. He had come out without his coat, and the dawn had a knife's edge to it. "There's a depot at Verren. They'll collect documents there—what's left of them—and then on to the capital. They won't come here. Why would they? This place is being emptied as we speak."

He was right. Reiss's evacuation orders had gone up on the ward door before sunrise, typed this time, which somehow made them worse. *All personnel and transportable wounded to be moved to rear facilities within forty-eight hours. All records to be surrendered to camp administration for disposition.*

*Disposition.* Jonah turned the word over. She had watched men dispose of things her whole war. It meant a pit behind the cookhouse and a tin of lamp oil.

Freya tugged her hand. Jonah looked down. The girl held up the clock—not the works, not today; the whole thing, both hands wrapped around its ruined face, and she pushed it against Jonah's hip the way another child might push a coat at you when you were cold. As if to say: *take it, wear it, carry it.*

Inside the casing, wrapped in oilcloth and wedged between the strike-spring and the frame, were two hundred and six names. Two hundred and seven now, if she counted Major Corwin, and she did, every time she breathed.

"He'll inspect before they move us," Jonah said. Her voice came out flat, practical, and she was grateful to it. "Reiss. He'll count heads, count beds, count crates. He's been counting since autumn."

"Then don't be here when he does."

She looked at him.

Elias kept his eyes on the road, on the last of the convoy shrinking into the morning haze. "There's a cart going out with the laundry detail at noon. Marta drives it; she owes me nothing but she'll do it anyway, because that is what your nurses do, though none of you will admit it's a network." A ghost of a smile crossed him and vanished. "The cart goes south first, then west along the mill track. Nobody watches the mill track. From the ford it's six hours' ride to where the convoy turned off—you could reach Verren by tomorrow night. By the time anyone notices a nurse missing from a moving column, you'll be a rumor."

"And Freya?"

"Freya goes with you, obviously."

"Obviously," Jonah repeated, and heard how the word cracked down the middle. Because he had said *you*, and he had said it the way a man sets a table he will not sit at.

"Elias."

"There's a version," he said, still watching the road, "where we all three go. And there's a version where Reiss stops the cart because a disgraced cartographer with falsified maps already drawn against him is worth searching twice, and he finds what you're carrying, and then there is no ledger anywhere in the world except in an incinerator, or in his safe, which amounts to the same thing." He turned to her then, and his face was very calm, the calm of a man who has already done his arguing alone, at night, and lost. "I'm the risk, Jonah. Not you. Me. I'm the one he's already halfway to hanging."

"Then I should be the risk," she said. "It was mine first. The ledger. You only found it."

"Yes," he said simply. "It was yours first. That's why it has to be you that carries it through. Not because you're less likely to be searched. Because you're the one who wrote the names, and a record means something different coming from the hand that made it. The historians will want provenance. They'll want *her*—" he nodded at Freya— "and they'll want you, and they can have you, and I will be exactly where I've wanted to be all along, which is useless to everyone except as a wall between you and Reiss's attention."

"I don't accept walls."

"I know," Elias said. "That's the whole trouble with you."

* * *

The morning went by in the particular hell of ordinary tasks. Jonah changed dressings, signed a transfer list with hands that wrote someone else's signature neatly, counted morphine ampoules into a crate that would be *disposed of* by noon. Around her the camp tore itself apart in an orderly fashion, and everywhere, under everything, ran the low continuous murmur of the armistice—*signed, they're saying signed, papers at eleven, guns quiet at noon*—passed mouth to mouth like water down a line of thirsty men.

Some of the orderlies had already gotten hold of a bottle. Two of them sang in the yard, badly, arms around each other, and no one told them to stop. An old sergeant sat on an overturned bucket staring at nothing, and when Jonah passed he said, without looking up, "Eleven years I've been soldiering, Sister. What does a man do on a Tuesday that isn't war?" She had no answer. Neither did he. He went back to staring.

In the linen room, Marta took the news in silence, folding sheets with the violence of a woman who would rather be folding something breakable. "Noon," she said finally. "Be under the east fence at noon with whatever you're carrying and don't carry it like it matters. Carry it like it's dirty laundry. Things that matter get searched. Things that are embarrassing get waved through."

"Marta—"

"I know what's in it." She didn't look up. "My brother's in it. Page nine. You wrote him small, like you were ashamed to be writing him at all." Now she looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "You get it there, Jonah Vasquez. You get it there and you make them read page nine out loud."

Jonah promised. She did not say how. There was no *how* yet. There was only noon, coming at her like the far end of a fall.

* * *

At ten o'clock Commandant Reiss walked the ward.

He did it beautifully, of course. He always did everything beautifully. He came down the aisle between beds with his gloves on and his cap at the correct angle, pausing here to compliment a convalescent's color, there to inquire whether the pillows were sufficient, and all the while his gaze moved over the room the way a customs man's hands move over luggage—not resting, not hurrying, taking inventory of everything by seeming to look at nothing.

"Nineteen wounded fit for transport," he said, consulting a card. "Fourteen unfit. Twenty-six staff, minus four already reassigned." His eyes came up. "I show twenty-seven, Sister Vasquez. Someone is miscounted."

"The child," Jonah said. "She's on no roster. She never has been."

"No," Reiss agreed pleasantly. "She never has been." He drifted a step closer. Outside, distantly, a church bell somewhere in the ruined country began striking eleven, and kept on striking, and men stopped talking to listen. "You know what I find about armistices, Sister? Everyone believes the ending settles things. It doesn't. Endings are merely the beginning of the accounting. And accountings—" he smiled— "require records. I have spent eleven years making sure the records say what they ought to say. I would hate, in the final week, to meet a record that says otherwise."

"We keep excellent records here, Commandant. You've seen them."

"I've seen the ones you keep for me." He said it lightly, almost fondly, and turned to go, and then paused, as she had known he would pause, beside the shelf where the broken clock sat among the bedpans and the borrowed toys, because Freya had left it there and Freya left it nowhere else.

"What a curious thing to keep," Reiss said.

"It's the girl's. It's broken."

"Most precious things are." He touched the casing once, two fingers, almost a caress, and Jonah felt the two hundred and seven names press against the inside of her ribs as though the ledger itself were breathing. "Afternoon inspection, Sister. Full inventory. Everything opened. Everything accounted." He settled his gloves. "Do give some thought to what accounts for what."

Then he was gone, and the bell finished striking, and the guns stayed silent, and Jonah stood in the aisle of her ward and understood that there was no longer any version of the day in which the ledger stayed hidden. Noon was the cart. Afternoon was Reiss. The arithmetic allowed exactly one solution and it was monstrous.

She found Elias in the map room, bent over his false routes, and told him the shape of it in five sentences. He listened without interrupting. Then he said, "Then you leave at noon, and I stay, and when he opens the clock at three o'clock he finds me standing next to it with a story about why a broken clock belongs in a hospital, and my arrest buys you until dark, and by dark you're at the ford."

"That's not a plan, that's a funeral with extra steps."

"It's arithmetic," Elias said. "You taught me arithmetic, remember? Every name costs. The question is only who pays." He straightened, and for a moment the lamplight caught the scar at his temple, the one from the border survey, the one he'd never explained. "Jonah. Listen to me once. I have redrawn the maps of a hundred places I've never dared to stand in. I have put rivers where rivers weren't and erased roads that existed. My whole life has been paper truth and paper lies, and I have never once carried anything true through anything dangerous. Let me do this one real thing."

But it was Freya who ended the argument, the way Freya ended most arguments: without a word.

She had come into the map room unheard, as she always did, and while they spoke she had climbed onto the stool at the big table and begun to arrange things. Jonah watched her do it with the particular dread and wonder she'd never lost. The girl placed Elias's compass at the table's center. Around it she set three pins from the disorganized pin-tray, points inward. Then, deliberately, she took the broken clock from under her arm, opened the back with her thumbnail—the way Jonah had taught her, weeks ago, as cover—and drew out the oilcloth packet, and laid it flat beside the compass, inside the ring of pins.

A compass. Guarded. Surrounded by what it must steer by.

Then she pointed at the packet, and at herself, and at Jonah, and then—slowly, gravely—at the west wall, the way out, the road, the ford, the convoy, the historians, the future. And she pointed at Elias, and at the door he'd be dragged through, and shook her head once, hard, *no*, and pointed after Jonah again: *both of us go, and neither of us stays.*

"She's right," Jonah said, and her voice shook, and she let it. "We all go or none of us does. You don't get to be a wall, Elias. You get to be a man walking beside us on a laundry cart, thinking fast, because that's what you're good at—thinking fast about terrain. So think. How do three people and a packet of paper get past an afternoon inspection that starts at three o'clock, on a road where a tribunal convoy passed this morning?"

For a long moment Elias looked at the child, and then at Jonah, and something in his face came unclenched, the way ice lets go of a stone in spring.

"Mill track," he said slowly. "But not the ford. Too obvious once the search starts. There's an old drover's crossing two miles downstream—marshy, ugly, nobody uses it. And the timing—" he was already pulling the mill-track map toward him, hands moving, and Jonah saw it happen, the transformation, the disgrace becoming usefulness: "Inspection at three. Evacuation column rolls at four, horns and confusion, everyone counting everyone else's heads. If the cart goes out with the *four o'clock* movement instead of noon—part of a column, not apart from it—then Reiss isn't inspecting a suspicious laundry cart, he's inspecting a column, and columns get waved through because stopping one delays the whole retreat and Reiss would sooner burn the records than delay the retreat."

"You just invented that."

"I invent things for a living," Elias said. "This one's true. That's new." He looked up at Freya. "Can she ride six hours in a laundry cart?"

Freya picked up the packet of names, folded the oilcloth with great care, and put it back inside the clock, and closed the casing, and set the clock in Jonah's lap.

Then she climbed down off the stool, went to the corner where her few things lived, and brought back her shoes. She sat on the floor and put them on. Ready. It was the loudest thing Jonah had ever heard.

* * *

They went out under the east fence at four minutes past four, in the middle of a retreating army.

It was worse than any plan and better than any plan. The evacuation column was a mile of mud and complaint—wounded men on lurching wagons, mules with their ears pinned, orderlies running alongside holding IV bottles aloft like lanterns. Marta's cart slotted into the stream of traffic between a water bowser and a stack of crated records destined for disposal, and Jonah crouched in the laundry with Freya pressed against her and the clock between their bodies, warm from being held, and Elias rode up on the bench beside Marta wearing an orderly's borrowed armband and the expression of a man doing sums.

Twice in the first hour the column halted, and boots came along the line, and each time Jonah arranged her face into laundry-room blankness and each time the boots went past. Once, close, a voice she knew—Reiss's voice, further up the column, giving instructions about the crated records, *"all of it, to the pits, before Verren, I want nothing readable leaving this province"*—and Jonah held Freya's hand tighter and Freya held the clock and neither of them breathed, and then the wheels rolled again.

The crossroads came at dusk.

Marta slowed the cart at the junction, as ordered, to let the tail of the column sort itself, and from between the laundry sacks Jonah could see it: the north road, and on the north road, camped in a stubble field with lanterns lit, the tribunal's rear party—tents, a guard mount, a staff car with the scales-and-book painted on its door, and officers moving among the crates they had collected from other camps, other pits, other burnings interrupted too late.

Half a mile. Half a mile of open ground between the column and the lanterns, with Reiss's rearguard strung along the east road not much farther than that, and the light dying, and the clock ticking silently against her heart the way it had ticked for eleven years of other people's silences.

"Now or never," Marta said, not turning around. "There's a gap in the hedge fifty yards on, where the ditch crosses. After that it's wire and open field all the way to their picket."

Elias dropped down from the bench, came around the cart, and lifted the flap as if checking laundry, and for a moment the three of them were a small huddle of faces in the failing light.

"I'll draw the rearguard," he said. "East road, on foot, asking directions—lost orderly, wrong column, perfectly ordinary fool. When they turn to deal with me, you cross." He was already unbuckling his watch to leave it, absurdly, with Jonah, so that someone would know his hours. "Sixty seconds. Maybe ninety. Then walk, don't run, and when the historians ask who kept the record, you tell them the truth: a nurse kept it, and a mute child carried it, and a liar bought them the road."

"Elias—"

"Ninety seconds," he said, and smiled his paper-map smile, and was gone into the dark toward the east road, and within a minute Jonah heard him raising his voice, cheerfully, idiotically, calling *hello, hello, is this the Verren column, I've lost my unit*—and she heard the answering shouts, and the boots, several pairs, converging away from the hedge.

"Now," Marta said.

Jonah lifted Freya down. The girl grabbed the clock. They went through the gap in the hedge side by side, into the stubble, walking as fast as walking could be made to look like nothing, across the open ground with the lanterns growing ahead and the shouting behind, and at thirty yards from the picket a sentry called out and a lantern swung toward them and a voice demanded who goes there, and Jonah opened her mouth to say *nurse, with testimony for the tribunal*—

—and Freya stepped forward, ahead of her, into the lantern light, lifted the broken clock high above her head in both hands so the whole camp could see it, and spoke into the armed silence, clear as a bell across the stubble:

"My name is Freya of Halden, and I have brought you the record of the dead."
