## 17. The Burning Ward

The order came at four in the morning, delivered by a corporal who would not look at her.

"Commandant Reiss wants the ward cleared by noon. All records to the yard for burning. Ambulances will take the wounded east." He shifted his weight, and ash from the corridor outside drifted past his shoulder like gray snow. "You're to ride with the last transport."

"And the girl?" Jonah asked.

"I wasn't given instructions regarding the girl."

Which meant he had been instructed not to give instructions about her. She understood then that there was no evacuation plan for Freya at all — only a gap in the paperwork where a child could fall through and be gone. The same trick they'd played on Halden, she thought. You don't need an order to make someone disappear. You just leave them off the list.

"Thank you, Corporal," she said, and watched him go, his boots kicking up small ghosts of paper ash. They had already started on the files. She could hear it through the wall: the low roar of the yard fire, the wet slap of ledgers being thrown on.

She went back inside and put her hands on either side of the clock's casing, where two hundred names slept in folded pages against the brass gears, and she thought, *not today.*

* * *

Reiss had chosen his moment well, she had to admit that. With the armistice signed but not yet announced, the roads clogged with retreating columns and the sky thick with the last spite of artillery, no one was counting nurses. A hospital burning its records was almost respectable — hygiene, order, the tidying of history before the victors arrived to do it for you. Reiss had even made a speech about it the night before, standing in the doorway of the ward with his cap in his hands like a man at a funeral, saying that some things were kinder left unrecorded. The wounded had stared at him. The nurses had looked at their shoes. And Jonah had felt the ledger's weight against her ribs where she'd worn it all evening under her apron, warm as a second heart.

Now she moved through the pre-dawn ward doing arithmetic with lives. Forty-one patients fit to be moved without dying en route. Nine who would die if moved. Freya, who was neither registered nor wounded nor officially present at all.

"You're making lists again."

Elias stood at the foot of the nearest cot, a canvas satchel over his shoulder, his coat still smelling of the map room. Three days since they'd taken him. Three days since he'd walked back out of Reiss's office untouched, blinking in the daylight, refusing to say what had been said to him — only that the charge of falsification had been "suspended," which both of them knew meant *postponed until useful*. He hadn't drawn a map since. He'd spent three days instead watching the camp the way he once watched terrain, reading its slopes and dead ground.

"I'm deciding who rides first," Jonah said.

"No. You're deciding how to move nine men the army has decided are already dead." He came closer and lowered his voice. "I've seen the transport manifest. It goes east through Kessel Ford. There is no bridge at Kessel Ford anymore. I drew the retreat maps myself."

The floor seemed to tilt slightly. "Then where—"

"The northern road. Longer by six hours, but it exists. I can get us a wagon and a driver who owes me." His eyes went to the clock sitting innocently on the supply shelf among the bandage tins. "And you'll need to be on it."

"*We* will need to be on it."

He didn't answer that. Instead he said, "The yard fire isn't just files, Jonah. I heard the corporals talking. After the transports leave, the ward itself burns. Everything. Beds, stores, records." A pause. "People who aren't on manifests don't slow fires down."

* * *

By seven the ambulances were loading, and Jonah worked with the deliberate calm that twenty years of wards had taught her, lifting the stretcher cases first, murmuring the small lies nurses tell — *almost there, nearly home, just over the next rise.* Her hands were steady. Her mind was running two maps at once: the official one, east to Kessel Ford and the missing bridge, and Elias's secret one, north through country she'd never seen, toward a crossroads where — if his information held, if the rumor was true, if the war's last chaos allowed it — a tribunal convoy might pass within the day.

Two hundred and thirty-one names. That was what the clock weighed now. Two hundred and thirty-one people whom the official histories would file under *enemy casualties: negligible*, or *civilian losses: unconfirmed*, or nothing at all. Major Corwin's confession was in there too, his own signature recorded beside the order that erased Halden, because he had begged her for it with his last breath and she had hated every letter she wrote and written them anyway.

If the clock stayed here, it burned. If it burned, those two hundred and thirty-one people had lived and died for nothing, and the men who erased them would grow old writing memoirs.

"All right," she told the ward quietly, when the last official ambulance pulled away. "All right."

* * *

The disguise was Sister Amelie's idea, and it was so simple Jonah was angry she hadn't thought of it herself.

"They're expecting nurses to flee," the older woman said, pinning Jonah's hair under a wounded soldier's cap. "So don't be a nurse. Be cargo. Shell-shocked men travel in blankets and nobody asks a blanket questions. Especially not shell-shocked ones." She looked at Jonah with eyes that had seen three wars' worth of triage. "The whole staff knows, by the way. About your notebook. We've known for months."

Jonah went cold. "You—"

"What did you think 'restock the laudanum' meant, twice as much as any doctor ordered, receipts that never balanced? What did you think the extra bread was?" Amelie tucked the last pin. "Invisible isn't the same as alone, girl. It took you long enough to notice."

Nine men who could not survive the eastern route were loaded onto a farm wagon with a lame horse and a driver named Ost, who owed Elias Brandt a debt he described only as "the kind you don't ask twice about." The nine men lay layered like cordwood, blanketed, their faces turned up to a sky the color of old zinc. Jonah lay among them, a blanket to the chin, the clock strapped flat against her stomach beneath her coat, its gears pressing into her flesh like a fist.

Freya sat at the wagon's front beside Ost, wrapped in a Red Cross blanket that made her officially, fictionally, a casualty of something. She held the clock's key in her fist. She had refused to surrender it. Jonah hadn't pushed; there are possessions that are souls, and you don't argue with souls.

They rolled out the north gate at nine minutes past noon, while behind them the yard fire climbed the hospital's eastern wall, and the records of four years of war went up in smoke that could be seen, Jonah knew, from ten miles off — Reiss's farewell address to the truth, written in black ink across the whole sky.

* * *

The shelling began at the second mile marker.

It was not aimed at them — artillery rarely aims at anything as small as a wagon — but that was the obscenity of it, that destruction this vast didn't even bother to notice you. Shells walked across the ridgeline to their east in long thundering strides, and each impact sent a pressure wave down into the valley that shoved the wagon sideways like a hand on its shoulder. The lame horse screamed and Ost swore and held the reins with both fists and kept going, because there was nowhere else to go, the road being the road.

One of the blanket-wounded began to weep. Another began to pray, in a language Jonah didn't know, and she found herself mouthing along anyway, matching rhythm if not words, her body remembering what her mind had decided long ago about prayer: that she didn't believe in it, except that she did, except that in moments like this belief was less a conviction than a reflex, the soul grabbing for anything bolted down while the world shook itself apart.

A shell struck the road fifty yards ahead. Earth geysered up, and when it fell it fell as stones, and one of them cracked across the wagon bed a hand's width from a private's skull. The man didn't flinch. He was beyond flinching; he was somewhere else entirely, and Jonah reached through the jumble of limbs and found his wrist and held it, and counted his pulse against the guns, and thought with a clarity that frightened her:

*I am visible.*

That was the thing. Four years of invisibility, of moving through wards like smoke, of believing that safety was the same as being unseen — and now she lay in the open under a soldier's cap with a contraband archive strapped to her body, in a wagon full of men the army had amputated from its records, on a road that officially did not exist, fleeing a fire that officially was not happening, and every gun in the world seemed to be aiming at her personally. She had never felt so exposed in her life. She had never felt so *alive* in it either, and the two sensations, terror and purpose, had fused somewhere back at the second mile marker into a single burning wire that ran from her chest to her hands.

*This is what it costs,* she thought, as another shell bloomed black against the ridge. *Fine. Pay it.*

The clock's gears dug into her stomach. She imagined she could feel each name separately, two hundred and thirty-one heartbeats stacked against her own.

* * *

They made the tree line as the light failed.

Ost brought the wagon off the road and into the birches, and the shelling fell behind them like weather, and the silence afterward was so complete that Jonah could hear the blood in her ears. Men stirred under blankets. Someone asked for water. Someone asked whether they'd won, and nobody answered, because the honest answer — *won what, exactly, and who is we* — was more than anyone could carry.

Freya slid down from the seat and came to Jonah's side of the wagon. In the blue half-dark her face was pale and unreadable, and she held up the clock's key, and pointed north, and then — with the terrible practicality of children — pointed at Jonah's stomach, where the clock lay, and made a winding motion.

*Keep going. Keep it wound. Keep time.*

"I know," Jonah whispered. "I know, little one."

Elias appeared out of the trees with the wagon's spare lantern, and the light caught his face, and she saw that he'd been running. "Kessel Ford is confirmed gone," he said. "The eastern transports were turned back at midnight. Every man on them is alive because of your lists." He said it lightly, but his hands weren't light; they gripped the wagon rail hard enough to whiten. "The crossroads is six hours ahead. The tribunal convoy passes tomorrow morning if it passes at all. Reiss's riders left an hour ago — I saw them taking the same road."

"He knows."

"He suspects. Which is worse, because suspicion doesn't tire." Elias looked back the way they'd come, toward the distant orange smear of the burning hospital against the night. Then he looked at her, and for a moment the cartographer and the disgraced man and the quiet stubborn friend all collapsed into one expression she couldn't name. "There's a checkpoint at the mill stream. Reiss's men, not army — I recognized the procedure. They'll stop everyone going north after dark."

"Then we go around."

"There is no around. I've seen the ground." He paused. "There's only through."

Jonah pulled the blanket higher against the cold and felt the clock shift against her, solid, patient, heavier than any object she had ever carried. Two hundred and thirty-one names. One mute child. One man who had already given everything once and was preparing, she realized with a lurch, to give it again at a mill-stream checkpoint before morning.

"Then we go through," she said.

Far ahead, at the bottom of the valley, a lantern swung. Once. Twice. Deliberate. A signal, or a summons, or a trap closing its bright jaw.

Freya took Jonah's hand. Her grip was very small and very strong, and when Jonah looked down at her, the girl was staring at the distant light with an expression no child should own — the fixed, ancient attention of someone assembling words she had buried for years, and choosing, at last, the place to dig.
