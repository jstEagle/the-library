## 18. Freya Speaks

The cart had been moving for two days, and Freya had counted everything there was to count.

Forty-one wheel rotations between the ruts in the road. Nine crows on the burned fence line outside Marren. Six soldiers at the checkpoint ahead, though she could see only three from where she lay folded among the wounded men under the canvas, because the other three were only boots, visible beneath the cart's side rail. She knew the difference between a man and his boots now. She had learned it the way you learn anything that keeps you alive: thoroughly, and too young.

Jonah lay beside her, breathing the slow, deliberate breath of a woman pretending to be asleep. The clock was strapped against Freya's stomach under her coat, its weight no longer strange. It had been part of her body for so long now—the gears inside the casing, and inside the gears, the pages—that when she moved she moved carefully, as if carrying a second heartbeat.

*They will ask for papers,* Jonah had said before dawn. *If they find them, we are finished. If they find the clock, we are finished. So we are nobody. We are the wounded and the women who tend them.*

Freya had nodded. Nodding was easy. It was the one language everyone agreed on.

But here was the thing no one knew, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal: she was not mute. She had not been mute since the night the soldiers came to Halden, when she was seven years old, when Lieutenant Voss put his finger to his lips and said, *No voices from this village. No names. A place that makes no sound cannot be accused.*

She had obeyed him for three years. Not because she couldn't speak—because speaking felt like handing them the last thing they hadn't taken. Her voice was the only property of Halden that still existed, unburned, unlisted, unmapped. She kept it hidden the way Jonah hid her ledger. The way Elias hid his true maps. Everyone she loved was a keeper of contraband; hers simply lived behind her teeth.

But the checkpoint was close now. She could hear the sergeant's voice, flat as slate, asking the driver questions he had no good answers for. And she understood, with the terrible clarity of children who have already lost everything else, that if they searched this cart they would find the clock, and if they found the clock they would find the ledger, and if they found the ledger then two hundred names would die twice.

There was a third option. There had always been a third option. It just cost more than any of the others.

Freya reached up and touched Jonah's wrist.

* * *

Jonah woke the way soldiers wake—all at once, hands first—and found the girl's eyes an inch from her own. In three months Freya had never been this close voluntarily. Something in Jonah's chest went cold and bright at once.

"What is it?" she whispered.

Freya didn't sign. Didn't point. Instead she did something Jonah had never seen her do: she opened her mouth, and what came out was rough, rusted, a hinge unused for years—but it came.

"Take off my coat," she said. "Not yours. Mine."

Jonah stared. Then she did it, hands shaking, and together they unbuckled the clock's straps, and Freya held the casing against her chest and looked up the road toward the checkpoint where the sergeant was now walking toward the cart with his lantern raised.

"They're going to search," Jonah breathed. "We have minutes. Maybe less."

"I know." Freya's voice was low and even, a child's voice with an old woman's cadence underneath. "You told me once that a record isn't paper. You said it's whoever can stand up and say *I saw*. Do you remember?"

"I remember."

"Then stay beside me," Freya said, "and don't interrupt."

* * *

The sergeant reached the tailboard and hauled the canvas back with one hand, lantern swinging, and the light fell across eleven faces arranged in the geography of wounds—one man without an arm, a woman with a splinted jaw, old Tomas whose bandages had gone amber at the edges. And at the center, sitting upright, a small girl holding a broken clock like a shield or a plate.

"Papers," the sergeant said to the driver. "All of it. Registration, requisition, the wounded men's units."

"We're evacuation transport out of Marren—" the driver began.

"Papers." He stepped closer, and his boot found something on the cart floor—a gear, small and brass, that had worked loose from the casing during the long jolt of the road. He picked it up. Turned it over. His face did something complicated and then went very simple. "Well," he said. "A clockmaker's daughter."

Freya stood up in the cart.

It was not a dramatic motion. That was what made it land—she rose the way a witness rises in a courtroom, unhurried, certain of her ground. Jonah's hand flew toward her sleeve and stopped. Behind the sergeant, the other five soldiers turned. Down the road, the checkpoint fires threw their shadows long across the mud.

"That's not mine to keep," Freya said, indicating the gear in his fist. "Nothing I have is mine to keep. It belongs to a village called Halden. Which your army says does not exist."

The sergeant's mouth curved. "Never heard of it."

"Yes," Freya said. "That was the order."

The word dropped into the checkpoint like a stone into a well, and everyone heard it strike bottom. Even the wounded men went still.

"There is a written order," Freya said, and her voice grew stronger with each sentence, as though three years of silence had been water backing up behind a gate and now the gate was open and nothing in the world would close it again. "Signed by Major Corwin, countersigned by Commandant Reiss. It says Halden is to be removed from maps, records, and reportage. It says the inhabitants are to be registered as displaced from Marren so that no inquiry would ever find a discrepancy in the numbers. It was read aloud by Lieutenant Voss in the square on the fourteenth of October, two years ago, before the granary burned. I was seven. I stood third row from the front, between my mother and the well."

"Girl," the sergeant said slowly, "who taught you to say all this?"

"No one," said Freya. "I was there. That's the whole lesson. Being there is the qualification. Everything else you people invented."

* * *

Later, Jonah would try to reconstruct how the next hour unfolded, and fail, because it did not unfold—it detonated, in fragments, each one burning independently.

Freya named the officers. Voss, who gave the order for the granary. Sergeant Halem, who kept the list of households and crossed them off. Captain Durre, who chose the road out of Halden that the refugees were made to take, and who knew the bridge at Kessel Ford would not hold carts. She named them with dates. She named them with weather—*it was raining, which matters, because rain means the fire needed pitch, which means the pitch was brought by wagon, which means someone signed for the wagon.* She built, in twenty minutes, standing in the bed of a wounded-cart under a lantern, the architecture of an erasure, joint by joint, and every soldier at the checkpoint felt the floor tilt beneath them, because this was not an accusation shouted in grief. It was testimony. It was organized. It sounded, God help them all, like evidence.

"You're lying," the sergeant said, but his voice had climbed half an octave, and his hand had left his rifle and gone instead to his collar, an unconscious gesture, a man feeling for the rope already around his neck.

"I have never spoken before," Freya said. "Ask anyone here. Ask her." She pointed at Jonah. "Three months. Not one word. Why do you think a child would choose silence for three years?" She let the question sit. "Because Voss said no voices from this village. Because the last time I spoke, my mother was alive to hear it. I was keeping what was left. Now I'm spending it. There's a convoy of historians at the crossroads, one day east. Take me to them. Or shoot me here—but write down, in your own report, in your own hand, that you shot a ten-year-old girl from a village that officially never existed, and see how that reads after the armistice."

The silence afterward was total. Even the wind seemed to stop to listen.

And then, from the front of the cart, the one-armed corporal—whom Freya had never once heard speak above a murmur—said clearly: "She's telling the truth. My unit escorted the pitch wagons. I'll testify to it."

Old Tomas sat up. "I carried grain from Halden in '43. There's a millstone with the name carved on it. I know where they buried it."

The woman with the splinted jaw could not speak at all, so she pulled back her blanket and showed the sergeant the brand on her forearm—the requisition mark, the wrong date inked over the right one. Evidence was rising out of the canvas everywhere, like water finding every crack.

The sergeant looked at his five men. None of them moved. Then he looked at Freya for a long moment, and something in his face collapsed—not into mercy, exactly. Into arithmetic. He was a man who had finally seen which side of the ledger his own name would fall on.

"Get the canvas down," he said hoarsely. "Corporal—drive. Crossroads. Move."

* * *

They rolled east through the black hours, and Freya did not sleep, and neither did Jonah, but they held hands under the blanket like two conspirators of the same small nation.

"You lied to me," Jonah said at last, softly, and there was wonder in it rather than hurt. "For months."

"I protected you," Freya said. "It's what people do with things they love. You taught me that too. You hid your book in a clock."

"How long have you known about the ledger?"

"Since the night Reiss visited the ward. You thought I was asleep." Freya looked out at the dark road. "I've known everything for a long time. Knowing was all I could do. Now it isn't."

Jonah turned the girl's small hand over in her own. "What you did tonight—there's no taking it back. Reiss will know within a day. He'll come for you."

"I know." Freya said it the way she said everything now: plainly, without flinching, a witness to her own life. "Elias is already in his cell for less. So we go fast, and we go all the way, and we make it cost them more than it costs us." She paused. "Is that how you'd say it? In your book?"

Jonah laughed—a cracked, wet sound, half sob. "That's better than I'd say it."

Ahead, past the last ridge, lights: the crossroads, the tribunal convoy, tents pitched in the mud of history. Freya stood up in the moving cart, hair whipping, the clock hugged to her chest, and Jonah saw her not as a child anymore but as what she had chosen to become—the one voice a whole silenced village had left.

The sentries saw them coming and raised their lamps.

"Halt! Who goes there?"

Freya drew one breath, the deepest of her life.

"Freya of Halden," she called into the dark, "and I've brought you two hundred names."
