## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The fog came in off the water before dawn, as it always did, and Xanthe Thackeray learned to tell the hour by it the way other men told time by church bells. When the mist lay thick enough to swallow the masts along Meridian Wharf, it was not yet five. When it thinned to a grey gauze through which the harbor lights bled like wounds, the first carts were already rolling over cobbles slick with brine. And when the sun finally burned through—on perhaps one morning in four, and never in winter—it was time to stop being whoever he was before eight o'clock and become instead the clerk at the third desk in the long room of the Meridian Trading Company, pen behind his ear, ledger open, face arranged into the blankness that kept him alive.

He had been that clerk for nine years. He had been invisible for longer.

Greywater Bay woke the way a workhorse wakes: without appetite, without complaint, because stopping was not among its choices. The town existed for one reason only, and the reason sat at the mouth of the harbor like a patient animal—the deep-water anchorage, rare on this stretch of coast, where ships of any tonnage could take on cargo when the ports to north and south silted shallow. Everything else followed from that fact. The fish houses with their reeking salt pans. The cooperages that turned out barrels by the thousand. The ropewalk where women worked twelve-hour shifts spinning hemp until their fingerprints wore smooth. The chandlers, the shipwrights, the taverns that opened at six and did not close so much as briefly dim. And above all of it, built from the money that flowed through all of it: the counting house of the Meridian Trading Company, three storeys of grey granite with a clock tower that told the town when it was permitted to begin.

Xanthe walked to work in the half-light, past the fishwives spreading their glistening silver on boards still dark from yesterday's catch, past the dive crews coiling their lines and checking the glass masks his mother's generation had taught this coast to use. The divers were mostly gone now. A few old ones remained—Maren Kess among them, straight-backed as a mast even at sixty, hauling her gear down to the skiff with hands like knotted rope—and the young men who might have taken up the trade had gone elsewhere, to the Company's freight decks or away entirely. Nine years ago there had been forty pearl boats working the oyster beds beyond the headland. Now there were eleven, and everyone in Greywater Bay could recite the reasons as if reciting scripture: bad beds, bad weather, bad luck.

Xanthe wrote those reasons into the ledgers himself, sometimes. *Loss due to storm. Loss due to accident. Vessel unsound, written off.* His hand was considered the finest in the counting house—even Hale's secretary remarked on it—and so it fell to him to make the losses legible, to give each drowned boat and each drowned diver a tidy line of ink and a sum at the bottom. He did it without trembling. That was the trick he had learned early: the body could be trained past its own terror, the way a diver trained past the burning in his lungs. You simply did not permit yourself to feel it while anyone was watching.

At seven minutes past eight he hung his coat on the peg marked *T* and took up his pen.

* *

The long room smelled of tallow, ink, cold tea, and beneath it all the faint salt rot that no amount of scrubbing ever lifted from a building this close to the water. Eleven clerks sat at desks ranged in two rows, and at the far end, behind a partition of frosted glass, sat Mr. Quill—the chief accountant, a narrow man whose name suited him so perfectly that Xanthe had privately suspected Providence of a sense of humor.

"Thackeray." Quill's voice came around the partition without warning, as it always did. "The *Gannet*'s manifest. Have you posted it?"

"Posted yesterday, sir."

"The insurance schedule?"

"Entered. Fourteen pounds, six shillings against hull and cargo."

A pause, the sound of paper being turned. Then, mild as milk: "And you found nothing irregular in the valuation?"

It was a question Xanthe had answered a hundred times in nine years, in a hundred variations, and every time it arrived with the same quality of a hand resting lightly on the back of his neck. He knew what was irregular. The *Gannet* had been valued at nearly double her worth, insured through three separate offices under slightly different names, and her master—a Company man named Dree, who drank—had been heard to say she'd be going to the bottom within the quarter regardless of what the weather chose. Xanthe knew all of it the way he knew the taste of salt: completely, uselessly, silently.

"Nothing irregular, sir," he said. "The figures reconcile."

"Good lad." The papers rustled again, and Quill moved on to torment someone else about a misdated bill of lading.

That was the whole architecture of Xanthe's survival, laid out in miniature: they asked him if he saw anything, he said he saw nothing, and both parties understood the exchange perfectly. The Meridian Trading Company did not fear clerks who knew too much. It feared clerks who knew too much and wrote it down somewhere. So Xanthe kept his knowledge where he kept everything else that mattered—in the locked room behind his ribs—and paid out his indenture penny by penny, week by week, year upon year, toward a freedom date that crept toward him slower than the tide came in.

The arithmetic lived in his head with terrible precision. Indentured at fourteen, after his mother drowned and the Company bought the debt her funeral had left, plus the fee for the privilege of teaching him letters—which they had done grudgingly, having discovered the orphan could already read better than most of them and deciding it was cheaper to exploit that than to wonder about it. Nine hundred and forty days served. Two hundred and ninety-one remaining, at the current rate of deduction, which meant roughly ten more months of Quill's voice through the frosted glass, ten more months of posting losses he knew were lies, ten more months of walking home in the dark with his collar up and his eyes down. Unless he found another shilling here or there. He was always finding another shilling here or there. Small debts were like small fires: you starved them steadily or they consumed you.

At noon he ate bread and pickled herring at his desk, as the rules required meals be taken on premises, and copied manifests until his wrist ached. At half past two, Director Hale passed through the long room on his way to the harbor gate.

Everyone felt it when Ambrose Hale walked through a room, though no one would have said the man raised his voice or hurried his step. He moved the way money moved—quietly, inevitably, rearranging everything in his path without appearing to touch it. He was fifty or thereabouts, silver at the temples, dressed in charcoal broadcloth cut in the southern fashion, and he had a habit of pausing beside whichever clerk least expected it and asking some small, precise question that revealed how precisely he watched them all.

He paused beside Xanthe.

"Thackeray." Hale's smile was famous in Greywater Bay. Mothers pointed it out to children at the quay as one points out a well-dressed dog. "How is your mother's slate?"

The question landed like ice dropped down Xanthe's collar. His hands stayed flat on the ledger. His pulse, trained these nine years, did not so much as stumble.

"I wouldn't know, sir. I haven't a slate."

"No?" Hale tilted his head, and for a moment something moved behind the pleasant grey eyes—not cruelty exactly; cruelty implied passion. Curiosity, perhaps. The curiosity of a man taking inventory. "I thought I remembered. A diving family, weren't they? Well." He straightened his cuffs. "Reconciliation on the *Merrow* accounts by Friday. I want the loss columns clean. We are audited from overseas this year, and I'll have no slop in my books."

"No slop, sir."

Hale moved on. Behind the frosted glass, Quill coughed once, softly, like a man clearing his throat of a laugh.

Xanthe looked down at the *Merrow*'s account page. In the loss column, in his own immaculate hand, stood a figure representing a brig that had sunk in calm water eleven miles offshore with a crew of six, four of whom had been divers the Company held insurance policies on. He drew a fine rule beneath the column total and thought, with the particular patience of a man who has practiced patience for almost a decade: *Friday.*

* *

His rooms were above a net-mender's shop on Trawl Street, one room and a lean-to kitchen that leaned further every year, rented from a widow who charged fair and asked nothing, which in Greywater Bay made her practically a saint. He climbed the outside stair at half past six, in the last grey light, and locked the door behind him with both bolts.

Then, and only then, he became himself again.

There were not many hours of the day that belonged to Xanthe Thackeray. Perhaps two, between full dark and the fog-warning bell. But in those hours he read—anything, everything, books borrowed from the Wesleyan lending shelf and smuggled merchant gazelles of newsprint that came as packing paper around crates of china—and he counted his escape fund, and he tended the slate.

The slate hung on the back of the door, wrapped in oilcloth, and he unwrapped it now as he did every night, though there was nothing new to see. It was a diver's slate, bone-framed, wax-surfaced, the kind divers carried to write messages underwater when a line-signal wasn't enough: *found bed, coming up, send second man.* His mother had carried it for twenty years. It should have gone down with her. Instead it had washed ashore at Wreckers' Beach three days after the *Pearl of Greywater* went under in flat calm water, and a beachcomber had sold it back to the fourteen-year-old Xanthe for sixpence—an act of either kindness or extortion, he had never decided which.

Her writing was still on it, ghosted into the wax where she had pressed hard and never wiped the surface clean. He had long ago memorized every stroke, but he traced them anyway with his fingertip, nightly, the way other men said prayers.

*Maren — beds thinning N side. Tell E. come home. Tell E. I love.*

She had been writing to Maren Kess about the oyster beds. She had been writing to Elias Vane, captain of the coastal packet *Freya*, telling him to come home from his run down-coast. And the last words, unfinished because there had been no time, no air, no mercy in whatever those final minutes held—*tell E. I love*—cut off mid-word, mid-breath, as if the sea itself had lifted her hand from the wax.

Elias Vane had come home to a drowned harbor and a drowned woman and a boy sitting on the quay with a slate in his lap, staring at nothing. It was Vane who had arranged the indenture papers so the Company couldn't bind Xanthe to the workhouse. It was Vane who had taught him, in the six months before the *Pearl* sank, that reading was a kind of weapon and that a quiet man with a weapon was more dangerous than a loud one. And it was Vane who had knelt down on this same floor, one night in peacetime, gripped the boy's shoulders with those rope-hard hands, and said words Xanthe had spent nine years trying not to remember.

He did remember them now, as he did every night, and as every night he set them aside. Not yet. There was a sequence to things. First the debt. First the invisibility. First the two hundred and ninety-one days. Then, when he was free, when no contract and no watchman and no man in Greywater Bay had power over his movements—

He wrapped the slate in its oilcloth and hung it back on the door.

Outside, the fog bell began its slow bronze tolling, and somewhere below, the widow's cat complained about the weather. Xanthe banked his fire, took out his private counting book—a cheap thing, half the pages torn—and entered the day's economy with the same care he gave the Company's lies. *Wages, minus board, minus indenture payment: one and eightpence toward freedom.* He looked at the figure for a long moment. One and eightpence. At that rate, the sea itself might drown before he did.

Then he added a second entry, in a cipher of his own devising that no clerk in the Meridian Trading Company could read: a note of what the *Merrow* had truly been worth against what she had been insured for, and the names of her crew, and the word *again*—because the *Merrow* was not the first, and both of them knew it.

He blew out the candle. In the dark, the fog bell tolled on, and beneath it he could hear the harbor working even at night—the slap of water against pilings, the groan of moored hulls, the distant clang of the chain being winched across the harbor gate at curfew, closing Greywater Bay like a purse.

Two hundred and ninety-one days. He had endured nine years by never once looking past the number. But tonight, sleep was slow in coming, and when it finally took him it brought the dream it always brought: his mother's hand moving across the wax, writing faster and faster, and the words refusing to finish, and somewhere beyond the surface of the water, boats turning away.

He woke at five to the fog swallowing the masts, and went down to open the counting house as he had nine hundred and forty-two mornings before.

Except this morning, there was a man lying against the door.

* * *

He was sprawled in the recessed doorway of the Meridian Trading Company like something the tide had given up—a big man ruined thin, clothes crusted with dried spray, one arm bent wrong across his chest. The fog curled around him and made the scene unreal, a painting of disaster rather than the thing itself. Xanthe stopped three steps away, keys in hand, heart suddenly loud.

The man's face was turned toward the wall, but what the wall didn't hide needed no turning. A burn had taken the whole left side of it—from hairline to jaw, the flesh seamed and shining, an ear reduced to ridged scar. It was a face that ended arguments before they started. It was a face that had no business being alive, and yet the chest beneath the salt-stiff coat rose and fell, rose and fell.

As Xanthe watched, the man stirred. His good eye opened—a dark, exhausted, terribly awake eye—and fixed on Xanthe with an effort that seemed to cost him everything he had.

"Thackeray," the stranger rasped. Fog-wet lips cracked into something that had once been a smile. "Knew you'd be... punctual."

And then his hand emerged from the folds of his coat, holding out a letter sealed in wax, addressed in a bold, slanting hand that Xanthe had seen only once before—kneeling on this very harbor's quay, nine years ago, on the deed of indenture that had saved his life.

He knew that handwriting the way he knew his own heartbeat. The problem was that its owner had been dead for three years, drowned with all hands when the *Freya* went down in the southern straits, mourned in two taverns and cursed in a third, his grave a rumor and his name an epitaph.

The letter lay in the stranger's palm between them, seal uppermost, patient as a lit fuse.

Behind Xanthe, up the hill, the clock in the Meridian tower began to strike eight.
