## 2. Letters from a Drowned Man

The storm came in off the Greywater on a Tuesday, which Xanthe Thackeray would remember afterward because Tuesdays were the days he balanced the coal entries, and the coal ledger was open before him when the world he had spent nine years sealing shut came knocking at the countinghouse door.

He heard the weather before he saw it. The Meridian Trading Company's countinghouse stood close enough to the quay that the harbor spoke through its walls at all hours—the knock of hulls against pilings, the complaint of rope, the long sigh of water finding its level among the stones. On ordinary days these sounds were so familiar they ceased to be sounds at all, becoming instead a kind of silence, the way a man ceases to hear his own heartbeat. But that morning the harbor's voice changed register. It rose. It deepened. Somewhere beyond the fogged windows, the sea stood up on end and began throwing itself at the town.

"Barometer's dropped like a stone," said Mr. Pellow, the second clerk, from his desk by the far window. Pellow was sixty and had survived four decades at Meridian by noticing nothing that was not entered in a ledger. He said it in the tone of a man remarking on the price of tallow. "There'll be damage."

"There'll be claims," Xanthe said, and bent again over the coal figures, because that was what an indentured clerk did: he bent over figures while the sky outside turned the color of a drowned man's lips.

Nine years. He had been fourteen when old Meridian's son had put the contract before him—a boy with no father, no boat, and no name anyone respected—and explained, with the gentle cruelty of a man describing arithmetic to a child, that seven years' labor would settle the debt left by his mother's burial. The figure had been wrong. Xanthe had known even then, even at fourteen, that the figure was wrong; the fees compounded, the charges bred small charges of their own like mice in a granary wall. But he had signed. He had signed because the alternative was the workhouse, and because signing meant paper, and paper could be read, and reading—his mother's secret gift to him, taught by lamplight on a diving slate—was the one wealth no one could take from a clerk's apprentice.

So he had learned to live two lives. By day he was Thackeray, the quiet one, the reliable one, the clerk whose columns never wavered and whose opinions never surfaced, invisible as dust on a high shelf. He fetched, carried, copied, summed, kept his eyes down and his hands ink-stained and his mouth shut about everything he saw in the Company's books—which was a great deal, more than any clerk his station should have seen, because Hale liked to use him for the sensitive freight entries, precisely because Xanthe never asked questions. Ambrose Hale, director of the Greywater Bay office, had once said in Xanthe's hearing that a silent man was worth three talkative ones. He'd said it smiling. Hale always smiled.

But by night, in the rented room above the ropewalk where he had lived since his indenture began, Xanthe became someone else. Not boldly—never boldly, boldness was death in Greywater Bay—but privately, secretly, in the hour before sleep. From beneath a loose floorboard he would take the slate: his mother's diving slate, its surface ghosted with the pale ghosts of her letters, *M-A-R-K-E-T S-Q-U-A-R-E B-O-Y-S W-A-I-T*, the shopping list she'd written on her last living morning, or perhaps some earlier morning—it didn't matter which—the wax pencil long gone but the words pressed into slate forever, the only handwriting of hers the sea had left him. He would hold it and read it and let himself, for five minutes only, be the son of Freya Thackeray, pearl diver, who had gone down into Greywater Bay one bright October day nine years ago and not come up.

*Accidental drowning,* the magistrate had ruled. *Weight belt fouled.* Xanthe had been fourteen and voiceless and it had been ruled, and rulings in Greywater Bay were like the tide: they came in over everything and receded leaving the same ground behind.

He put the slate away now, mentally, the way he did every morning, and gave his attention to coal.

The rain arrived first, in sheets that made the window glass weep sideways. Then wind, a long rising howl out of the northeast that set the shutters clattering along Rope Street and sent a chimney pot crashing into the lane somewhere near the cooperage. Pellow moved his chair away from the window without comment, as though the glass might be taking notes. By noon the harbor was white water from the mole to the fish market stairs, and the fishing fleet came running home under bare poles, and the whole town pulled its doors tight as a fist.

Xanthe stayed late. Storm days meant damaged cargo, and damaged cargo meant entries, and Hale wanted the *Cormorant*'s spoiled hemp valued before the insurance men's representative arrived from the capital on Thursday. He worked by lamplight with the rain hammering the roof like a fist demanding entry, and he was alone—Pellow gone home at four, the junior boys dismissed, even the night watchman sheltering in the gatehouse—when he heard the knock.

Not a knock, exactly. A sound at the door. A heavy, dragging sound, as of something being propped against it from outside, followed by three blows struck low on the wood with what sounded like the flat of a hand.

Xanthe sat still. It was past seven. No legitimate caller came to the Meridian countinghouse after dark; the Company's business kept Company hours, and everyone in Greywater Bay knew the penalty for disturbing the director's premises out of them. He told himself it was driftwood. Storm-tossed timber striking the door in the wind. He almost believed it.

Then the sound came again, and with it a voice—roughened, half-drowned-sounding, barely human: "Thackeray."

Just the name. Nothing else. Not *open in the Company's name*, not *I seek the director*, not any of the forms a petitioner used. Just *Thackeray*, spoken through six inches of oak and iron-bound storm, spoken as if the name itself were a key that must fit some lock.

His own name. In this building, after dark, in this storm. Nine years of careful invisibility, and his name had come looking for him wearing the weather.

He should not have opened the door. He knew it even as he lifted the bar—knew it the way one knows not to touch a hook that's baited—and he opened it anyway, because the voice had said his name, and in nine years almost no one had.

The man fell across the threshold.

He would have gone down entirely if Xanthe had not caught him—an armful of sodden wool and salt-stiff leather, heavier than he looked, reeking of seawater and tar and something burnt beneath both. Xanthe got him to the bench by the cold hearth and lowered him onto it, and in the lamplight got his first true look at the stranger's face, and felt his stomach drop through the floorboards of the world.

Half the man's face was simply not there. Fire had taken it—the left cheek, the left eyelid, the outer ear, all melted and healed and melted again into ridged scar tissue the color of candle wax, pulling the mouth into a permanent lopsided grimace. The right side was younger, weathered brown, the eye dark and startlingly calm. One side of him looked at you; the other side of him looked at something further off, something that had happened years ago and never stopped happening.

"Water," the man rasped.

Xanthe brought the jug from the cooling cabinet. The stranger drank as if water were a debt being collected, then sat breathing, dripping onto the flagstones, studying Xanthe with that single calm eye while the other side of his face did its ruined, distant looking.

"You're him," the man said at last. "Thackeray. Xanthe Thackeray." He said the Christian name as though he'd carried it a long way and it had gained weight in the carrying. "You don't remember me. That's fine. Nobody remembers crew."

"I don't know you," Xanthe said. His voice came out steadier than he expected. Nine years of practice.

"No." The man worked at something inside his coat—coat of good oiled canvas, now ruined, cut in a style no Greywater tailor had ever made—and drew out an oilskin packet, wrapped and tied, which he held out in a hand knuckled with old rope-scars. "I know you, though. Knowed your mother too, by reputation. Finest diver on this coast, they said. Before." He did not say *before what*. He did not need to. "My name's Corvo. And I've got here letters what been waiting on you three year."

The packet sat in Corvo's palm between them, black oilskin gleaming, tied with waxed cord, sealed at the knot with a blob of green wax stamped with a device Xanthe could not make out in the dim light and did not need to. Because he already knew. Some animal part of him, older than his caution, had known from the moment the voice came through the door.

"Letters," he repeated.

"Three of 'em." Corvo's voice was gravel over deep water. "Wrote three year back, give to me with instructions: find Thackeray, the clerk, Meridian countinghouse, Greywater Bay. Only I got delayed. Delayed three year." Something moved in the ruined half of his face, an expression that could not fully form. "Delayed bad."

"Who wrote them?"

Corvo looked at him a long moment with the living eye. Rain thundered on the roof. Somewhere out in the dark the storm drove something large against the pier with a boom like a signal gun.

"You know who," Corvo said quietly. "You knowed it the second you see that seal. I can see it in your face, boy. You're white as the foam."

And he was. Because the seal was green wax, and the device was an anchor crossed by a gull in flight, and there was exactly one man on God's grey ocean who signed that way—one man who had carved the device into his pipe, his knife-handle, the rail of his ship, laughing that a captain should stamp his mark on everything he loved—and that man was Captain Elias Vane, master of the brig *Pearl of Greywater*, who had taken a fourteen-year-old count-house boy out on calm-water runs and taught him knots and letters and the names of stars; who had promised Xanthe's mother, laughing, that no diver of his would ever dive unsound bell; who had gone down with his ship three years ago in the Southern Reach with forty souls aboard, officially lost at sea, mourned in this very harbor by widows who still laid flowers on the breakwater each anniversary—

Officially lost. Officially dead. Drowned, buried, closed, finished, sealed—like everything else Xanthe had ever loved.

"No," Xanthe said aloud.

"Aye," said Corvo.

"He's dead. The *Pearl* went down with all hands. There was a memorial. The Company—" His voice snagged. "The Company paid the widows' claims."

Something passed across Corvo's face then, both halves of it at once, and it was not grief exactly, nor anger, but a terrible kind of patience, the look of a man watching someone drink poison slowly and knowing the cup is not yet empty. "Did it, now," he said softly. "Paid the claims. Well. That's a thing to think on, ain't it. That's a thing worth opening a letter over."

He held out the packet again. The green seal caught the lamplight.

Every instinct Xanthe owned, every survival instinct honed through nine years of being small and quiet and employed by dangerous men, screamed at him in one voice: *Do not touch it.* To touch those letters was to touch the drowned past; to read them was to know things; to know things in Greywater Bay was to hang. He thought of his indenture, four years yet unworked. He thought of Hale upstairs in the director's rooms above the warehouse, awake late as always, meticulous, smiling. He thought of the slate beneath his floorboard, and of his mother going down into green water with a fouled belt, and of the ruling that had closed over her like the sea closing over her, smooth and final and unquestioned.

If Vane lived—if the word *lived* could attach itself to anything connected with the *Pearl of Greywater*—then the drowning had not been the ending. It had been the beginning of something, and that something had just come through the storm to his door, wearing a burned face and carrying his name.

"Take them back," Xanthe said.

Corvo did not move. "Boy—"

"Whatever this is, whatever game, whatever scheme—I'm an indentured clerk. I have four years left on my contract. I copy figures. I say nothing. That is the whole of my life and I chose it and it keeps me alive. Take them back and take yourself out of this harbor before the watch finds you, because a man with your face asking after clerks by name will be noticed, and the notice will go upstairs, and upstairs there sits a man who pays for notice." He heard himself, heard the fear singing through the words, dressed up in clerkish order, and hated it, and could not stop it.

Corvo rose slowly from the bench. Standing, he was taller than he'd seemed, and the fire-scarred side of his face in the lamplight made him look like something off a warning broadside. But when he spoke, his voice was almost gentle.

"Captain said you'd say that. Said, *if Thackeray refuses, tell him to burn the first one unread. Tell him burning it changes nothing, because he'll already have seen my hand, and a man can't unsee a hand.*" Corvo set the packet on the bench between them, deliberate as a man laying a card face-up. "There's your instruction, delivered. First letter's marked for burning. Second and third is yours to do with as your conscience allows." He pulled his ruined collar up against the light of the lamp and moved toward the door, pausing on the threshold with the storm roaring at his back like a congregation. "I'm at the wreckers' beach, the old smokehouse, when you're ready to stop being alive-safe and start being honest-living. Either way, boy—them letters found you. That part's done."

And he stepped out into the rain and was swallowed.

* * *

Xanthe sat alone for a long time.

The lamp burned. The rain lessened by degrees, from hammering to drumming to a steady grey whisper against the glass. Out in the harbor, the storm-wrecked night settled into wreckage and mooring-lines creaking. He did not move. He sat with the oilskin packet on the bench beside him the way one sits beside a sleeping animal that may not be tame.

A man can't unsee a hand.

That was the truth of it, and it was already done. Even wrapped, even sealed, even unopened, the letters had done their work, because he had seen the green wax and the anchor-and-gull, and the seeing had undone nine years in a single breath. Elias Vane was alive—or someone wished him believed alive, which in Greywater Bay amounted to the same danger—or, worse, truer, heavier: Elias Vane was alive *and had thought of him*, had sat somewhere in the wide wet world three years ago with pen and ink and written *To Thackeray*, had trusted a count-house boy with his last words, and Xanthe had let those words sit unclaimed while the man was declared dead and the widows laid their flowers and the Company paid its claims.

*The Company paid the claims.*

Corvo had said it softly, but it had gone in like a fish-gaff. Why would the Meridian Trading Company pay death-claims promptly and generously for a ship it did not own? Except that it did own the *Pearl*; everyone knew the *Pearl* sailed under Meridian papers, whatever flags she showed abroad. Everyone knew the Company had insured her. Everyone knew—no. Knowing was the one thing Xanthe had spent nine years refusing to do. He had seen figures in the sensitive ledgers that a boy should never have seen: loss entries too neat, claims too prompt, ships written off with tidy precision. He had looked at them the way one looks at something in the water one does not wish to see, and had copied them faithfully into fair hand, and had said nothing, because saying nothing was the whole of his life and it kept him alive.

He looked at the packet.

He took it up. The oilskin was cold and smelled of tar and of long storage in some ship's hold. The cord was waxed and knotted in a way he recognized—Vane had taught him that knot, the third winter, laughing, calling it the *honestman's hitch* because it could be tied and untied one-handed in the dark. His fingers, remembering without him, found the knot and loosed it.

Inside lay three letters. The topmost bore his name in a hand he would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea: the strong forward-slanting script, the crossbar of the *T* struck like a sail needle, the peculiar doubled loop on the *y*. Elias Vane's hand. Unmistakable. Alive on the envelope as its writer was supposedly dead beneath the waves.

Xanthe stood. He walked to the hearth, where the previous tenant of the night-watch had left banked coals, and fed them shavings until a tongue of flame stood up. Then he knelt there, the first letter in his fingers, and fought the longest battle of his life in the space of ten heartbeats.

*Burn it unread.* That was the instruction. That was mercy, maybe—Vane offering him a clean refusal, a way to keep his sealed life intact. Burn this one, refuse the rest, remain Thackeray the invisible, alive-safe, four years from freedom and free thereafter to forget.

But the flame was already bending toward the paper, and he understood suddenly, kneeling there, that there was no unread anymore. That knowledge was like water: it found every crack. That he could burn all three letters and the knowing would remain, growing in him like rot in a hull, until it sank him anyway.

He burned the first letter unread, as instructed. He watched Elias Vane's hand curl and blacken and go to ash among the coals, and felt nothing he could name, and everything he could not.

Then he stood, shaking, and carried the remaining two letters back to the bench, and sat down heavily among the settling dark, holding them the way a man holds live shells with the pins drawn.

Outside, the storm was dying over Greywater Bay. Inside, in the ash-smelling silence, Xanthe Thackeray sat with his sealed past cracking open along its wax line, and knew—with the terrible clarity of a man who has just heard ice begin to groan beneath his whole weight—that the burning had changed nothing at all.

The second letter lay in his lap. His name stared up at him.

Above the countinghouse, in the director's rooms, a light was still burning.
