## 5. The Tide That Owes Nothing

The wreckers' beach lay a mile north of Greywater Bay, past the last of the fish-drying racks and the leaning fence where the town's tidy world simply gave up and let the cliffs speak for themselves. Xanthe had not walked that road since he was eleven years old, the year before his mother drowned, and his feet remembered it better than his head wanted them to. The path dropped through gorse and broken slate in switchbacks worn smooth by generations of men who preferred not to be seen carrying things home from the sea.

He came down in the blue hour after sunset, when the fog was thickening off the water and every rock above the tideline wore a gray hood. The Meridian coat hung behind his countinghouse door; tonight he wore his dead father's oilskin, cracked at the shoulders and smelling of tar and old rain, and in the pocket of it his hand kept closing around the diving slate he had brought without quite deciding to. His mother's slate. He could not have said why. Perhaps because Maren Kess would be there, and there were debts between them that predated words.

Corvo was already on the beach.

The man sat on an overturned dory, scarred face turned toward the water, so still that the fog seemed to have agreed to keep him. He looked like something the sea had tried to digest and failed. When Xanthe's boots crunched on shingle, Corvo spoke without turning.

"You counted your steps coming down. I heard you stop twice."

"I stopped to listen," Xanthe said. "If I'd been followed, listening is what would save me."

A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Vane always said you were the careful one." He rose, and the dory rocked behind him as if relieved of weight. "Careful men are slow, Thackeray. Slow men drown in shallow water."

"Then it's fortunate I've spent nine years learning to breathe in shallow water." Xanthe came closer, keeping a boat's length between them out of habit he did not examine. Below the tideline the sea worked at the wreck of some old hull, the *Providence* or the *Charity*, no one living remembered which—the timbers groaning with each swell like an animal too tired to cry out properly. "Where is she? Vane's ship?"

"Anchored beyond the shelf, darkened. Six miles out, maybe seven in this murk." Corvo nodded at the horizon, which had dissolved entirely into sea. "She'll stand off until signaled. Elias won't bring her in close while Hale keeps friends on the harbor guns."

"And the ledger?"

"Aboard. Under my bunk, under a false floor, wrapped in oilcloth." Corvo's ruined mouth twisted—Xanthe could not read whether the expression held pride or contempt. "Elias calls it the second book. Every diver's name. Every ship Hale scuttled and claimed as weather. Every payment the Company made to the insurance houses in London, dated before and after each sinking. It's all there. Nine years of drowning written down in a captain's neat hand."

Nine years. The number landed in Xanthe's chest like a stone into a well. Nine years since his mother went down off the Kettle Rocks; nine years since the Company clerk—a younger version of himself, ink-stained and obedient—had entered her death in the loss book as *drowned, misadventure, no salvage*. He had been the one to copy the fair copy. He had been fourteen.

"The spring tide," he said, forcing his voice level. "When?"

"Four nights hence. Moonless, and the tide runs hard enough to carry a loaded longboat over the bar if you know the channel." Corvo crouched and began dragging lines in the shingle with one finger: the bay, the headland, the teeth of rocks below the old signal station. "Here's what we need, and here's what we don't have. We need a fire on the headland—three flames in a triangle, high and steady, visible past the shelf. We need a boat that doesn't answer to the harbor master, launched from a beach the watch doesn't walk. And we need eyes inside the countinghouse when it happens, because Hale will know within the hour if anything moves on this coast, and the harbor chain goes up faster than gossip."

"That's three needs and no plan."

"It's three needs and four nights," Corvo said. "Which is nearly the same thing, if everyone does their part." He straightened, and his eyes—pale, unscarred, startlingly gentle in that wrecked face—found Xanthe's and held them. "Your part is the countinghouse. You told me you can touch the freight schedules without questions. I need the watch rotations for the next five nights, the tide tables the Company uses, and the name of whoever holds the key to the harbor chain house."

"You're asking me to steal from Ambrose Hale."

"I'm asking you to take back what he stole first," Corvo said. "There's a difference. Most men can feel it, once they try."

* * *

Maren Kess lived at the top of the wreckers' road, in a cottage whose chimney leaned like a question mark against the sky. She had been expecting them; there was a lamp burning in the window and the smell of smoked haddock and strong tea rolling out when she pulled the door open before Corvo had finished knocking.

"Inside, both of you, before the fog gets in and wets my floor worse than your boots will."

She was seventy if she was a day, and she moved through her own kitchen the way a helmsman moves through weather—sure-footed, unbothered, taking up exactly the space she required. Her hands were the thing Xanthe always remembered about her: broad as dinner plates, knuckles swollen to walnuts, the nails cut square and short from decades of prying shells and hauling lines. Those hands had taught his mother to hold her breath and read the water. They had pulled his mother, once, from a rip current off the Kettle Rocks, when Xanthe was six and screaming on the shingle. They had failed, eight years later, to pull her from anything at all, because they had been forty miles away, and the boats that were near had turned away.

Maren poured tea without asking and set the cups down hard enough to slosh.

"Well," she said, sitting, folding those hands on the table. "Corvo says Elias lives. Says he wants his book published and Hale hanged by newspapers instead of knives, which is a novelty. And now he says you need me, Thackeray." Her eyes were the flat gray of the sea in January. "So say it. What do you need an old woman for?"

"We don't need an old woman," Xanthe said. "We need the best diver between here and the Orkneys, and the only person alive whom the widows of Greywater Bay will follow anywhere. The spring tide, four nights out. A fire signal from the headland. A boat from the wreckers' beach to meet Vane's ship past the shelf—and witnesses aboard her when the ledger comes ashore. If it's only Corvo and me, Hale can make us disappear and call it smuggling. But if half the harbor sees that ledger come off a Company-framed captain's ship—"

"Then it stops being a secret and starts being a story," Maren finished. She looked at the table for a long moment. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, the surf on the wreckers' beach kept its patient, indifferent time.

"There are eleven widows in this town," she said at last, "from the *Pearl of Greywater* alone. Add the *Speranza* and the *Kestrel* and the ones Hale lost on the northern run, and you've got thirty households that eat bitter bread every day because that man signed their men underwater. I've had tea with every one of those widows. I've taught their daughters to dive and their sons to mend net. Some of them think Elias Vane sold his crew to the devil and ran. Some of them think worse." She lifted her cup and drank, deliberate as a ritual. "But none of them—not one—will lift a finger unless they believe the man asking them has already given everything he has to give. So before I say yes, boy, I want to know what you're risking. Not what you hope for. What you lose if this goes wrong."

Everything, Xanthe thought. The word arrived whole and unbidden. My indenture, my wages, my clean hands, my mother's good name, my neck.

What he said was: "I copied a page out of the loss book two weeks ago. Hale's forged insurance claims. It's hidden where no one will look for it, but if this fails—if Vane's ship is taken or the ledger never comes ashore—that page is the only proof outside the ledger itself, and if they find it, it's the workhouse or the gallows. And I'm the last person alive who saw the *Pearl*'s manifest the morning she sailed overloaded. I've known for nine years that the figures didn't add up, and I said nothing, because saying something would have cost me my position." He laid his mother's slate on the table between the teacups. The letters carved into its face had gone pale with handling but were still legible: *BREATHE BEFORE YOU ARE AFRAID. THE WATER IS OLDER THAN YOUR FEAR.* "I owe her more than silence. That's what I'm risking. Everything silence bought me."

Maren looked at the slate. Something moved behind the gray of her eyes—an undertow, far down.

"She wrote that the week before she died," Xanthe said quietly. "You remember. You gave her the chalk."

"I remember everything about that week." Maren's voice did not change, but her hands tightened around her cup until the knuckles whitened further. "That's the trouble with divers. We don't get to forget. The water remembers through us." She set the cup down and looked up at him, and then at Corvo, and the full weight of her attention was like a change of current—you felt the whole room move with it. "All right. Here is my answer, and you'll take it with the warning that comes attached, because I don't sell anything without the warning. Yes. I'll light your fire on the headland—I've lit signal fires on that rock since before your father could shave, and I can put three flames in a triangle that God Himself could read from the Faroes. Yes, the widows will help, the ones I choose, and only the ones I choose. Brigid Coulter has a boat that draws two feet and answers to no harbor master. The Danner brothers know the channel over the bar better than the fish know the tide."

"But?" Xanthe said, because the word was sitting in her mouth.

"But hear this." Maren leaned forward, and her voice dropped into the register she must have used thirty years ago, talking divers down before a deep run. "The sea keeps no promises, boy. Men keep fewer. I've watched better plans than yours go to the bottom with everyone aboard, smiling at how clever they'd been right up until the water came in. Elias Vane owes me for the night the *Pearl* went down and every Company boat on that water turned away—and he knows he owes me, and I'll collect by seeing this done. But I'm not doing it for him, and I'm not doing it for you. I'm doing it for thirty households that deserve to see the book that killed their men. If you're along for vengeance or glory or your own guilty conscience, leave now, because the tide doesn't care why you're in the water. It only cares whether you can swim."

The fire ticked in the grate. On the table, his mother's slate caught the lamplight along the grooves of her letters.

"I can swim," Xanthe said. "My mother made sure of it. I just haven't been in the water in nine years."

"Then it's time," Maren said. "Drowning men float longest when they stop fighting. Remember that Thursday night. All of you." She took up the slate, turned it in her broad hands as though weighing it, and handed it back to him with a gentleness that startled him more than any threat could have. "Keep her letters where you can see them. We'll want her at the fire with us, whether she's breathing or not."

* * *

Xanthe walked back along the cliff road alone, Corvo having melted into the fog toward whatever hiding place held him between meetings. The town showed itself below in scattered lamps—harbor bells, a dog, the great blind mass of the Meridian countinghouse with its single lit window where the night clerk bent over freight entries. In four days that window might be looking down on ruin. In four days his own handwriting might hang him.

And yet, walking the wet road with the fog beading in his eyebrows and the sea talking to itself below, Xanthe found something in his chest he had not felt since he was fifteen years old and first understood the shape of the cage he lived in. It was small and strange and he hardly recognized it.

Hope. Not the passive kind—the kind that waits for luck—but the kind with a date on it. Four days. Three flames in a triangle. A boat that answered to no harbor master. Thirty households of bitter bread, waiting to be handed a book.

His mother had written *breathe before you are afraid*, and he had read that line ten thousand times as advice about diving. Tonight, climbing the hill toward the sleeping town, he understood she had meant it about everything else too. Fear was coming—Hale's fear, patient and elegant, already circling. The trick was not to avoid it. The trick was to draw breath first, and hold it, and go under anyway.

At the top of the road he stopped once and looked back. Out past the shelf, invisible beneath the fog and the dark, a ship rode at anchor, carrying a dead man's living hand and nine years of names.

Then, far out where no light should be—a single flicker. Gone before he could be sure.

Someone else, it seemed, was already watching the water.

And when Xanthe reached the countinghouse door a quarter hour later, letting himself in with his clerk's key through the side entrance, he found the night clerk gone from his desk, the lamp still burning, and on the blotter, squared neatly at its center, a folded note in the director's unmistakable hand:

*Thackeray — My office. First thing tomorrow. Nothing urgent. — A.H.*

Nothing had ever been less urgent in its life.
