## 6. A Debt Paid in Salt

Maren Kess had not slept a whole night through in eleven years, and she had stopped apologizing for it. Sleep was a country she had emigrated from; she visited sometimes, briefly, the way a woman visits a house she once lived in and no longer recognizes. What woke her tonight was not memory exactly. It was weather. The wind had come around to the northeast, dragging fog up from the deep water in long grey ropes, and somewhere below her window the harbor chains clanked against their moorings like a patient counting out coins.

She rose, lit the stub of candle on the sill, and listened.

In this town, the wind off the northeast carried voices if you knew how to hold your ear to it. Tonight it carried something else: news, moving door to door the way it always moved among the people the Company never bothered to inform—a stranger at the countinghouse, letters from a dead man, Thackeray's boy seen walking the quay with his face gone white as gull bone. Maren stood at the window with the candle guttering and let it all arrive, and when it finished arriving, she dressed, took down her mother's slate from behind the loose brick above the hearth, and put it in her coat pocket.

Then she went down into Greywater Bay to find Xanthe Thackeray before the tide could change its mind about anything.

* * *

Her cottage sat at the top of the wreckers' path, where the cliff dropped toward the shingle beach the maps called Widow's Reach because the chartmakers, in their mercy, had never thought of a better name. The walk down took ten minutes in daylight and twenty-five at night, and she made it in nineteen, which told her something about how fast her blood wanted to move.

The boy was already there. Of course he was. He stood at the tideline where the sand went dark and firm, hatless, coat collar up, staring out at the fog bank as though he could read a ship's name written inside it. He heard her boots on the shingle—she did not soften her step; soft steps were for thieves—and turned.

"Maren," he said. "I didn't wake you."

"Nobody wakes me anymore, boy. I keep the watch now. Somebody has to." She came and stood beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, the way divers stand together, and looked where he was looking. "Corvo's boat is beached past the point under oilcloth. I counted the men watching it. Two. Amateurs. Hale's getting cheap or careless."

"He shouldn't know to look yet."

"Everyone knows something by now. Fog carries gossip faster than the mail boat." She studied his profile—the jaw set too tight, the eyes doing that thing they did where they watched everything and confessed nothing. Nine years she'd watched him grow into that habit, and every year it looked more like his mother and less like a boy learning to survive a countinghouse. "You've come to ask me something."

He didn't deny it. That was one thing about Xanthe Thackeray: he lied beautifully with his mouth and terribly with everything else. "The ledger names them," he said quietly. "The divers Vane says were left in the water. I haven't read it—I couldn't—but Corvo said there are testimonies. Statements. And I thought—" His voice caught on the hook of itself. "I thought you would have to be one."

Maren looked out at the fog for a long moment. The candle of the moon burned somewhere behind it, a smeared yellow coin.

"There's a rock out there," she said, "we used to call the Deacon. Black, sharp-topped, sits a half-mile past the channel marker. On a clear night you could see it from my mother's kitchen. Your mother learned her bottom-work in the cold water around that rock, and so did I, and so did fourteen other women and men whose names are carved nowhere except inside the people who loved them." She turned to face him fully. "You want my testimony, Xanthe Thackeray, you'll get it. But testimony isn't a thing you carry in your pocket like a coin. It's a thing that carries *you*. So first you'll sit down and hear the whole of it, because I've kept it eleven years in the only place Hale's clerks couldn't audit, and I am tired of being its strongbox."

* * *

They walked to the old net-shed at the base of the cliff, abandoned since the Company bought out the last independent fishing crews and let the building rot as a kind of monument to what cooperation cost. Inside it smelled of tar, salt-rot, and old hemp, and the wind played the broken planks like a slow organ. Maren lit her lantern, hung it on a nail, and sat on an overturned dory with her hands folded, and Xanthe sat across from her on a coil of rope like a schoolboy called before a mistress—which, in a way, he was.

"Eleven years ago this autumn," she began, "the *Pearl of Greywater* went down off the Deacon with nine divers working the beds below. The Company's report called it an accident of weather. Sudden squall, shifted cargo, hull failed. Signed, witnessed, filed." She smiled without any warmth in it whatsoever. "I was on that boat."

Xanthe's breath left him slowly. "You never said."

"You never asked. There's a difference between keeping a secret and having nobody come for it." She let the lantern flame steady herself. "Your mother was aboard too, but not below that night. She'd twisted her wrist two days prior and I forbade her the water—she argued, but your mother arguing was like weather arguing; loud, and then it passes. So she was topside, tending lines, and I was below with the others, walking the bottom in forty feet of green dark, filling our bags."

The shed creaked. Somewhere outside, the fog pressed against the world.

"We felt it before we heard it," Maren went on, and her voice did not shake, because she had practiced this telling in the silence of eleven thousand sleepless hours and practice makes even horror smooth as sea glass. "A shudder through the water, like the whole sea clearing its throat. Then the sound—wood giving up, a long groan, and after it that terrible quiet. The line above us went slack. Do you understand what that means to a diver, boy? Your lifeline goes slack. Your air-hose sags. Everything that connects you to the living world suddenly weighs nothing, because the thing it was tied to has stopped existing."

Xanthe had gone very still. Divers' children knew what slack line meant in their marrow.

"We came up fast, and coming up fast can kill you, but staying down kills you quicker, so we chose our poison. I broke surface into rain. Rain, and wreckage, and nine of us treading water among floating crates of Company pearls—loose pearls, boy, scattered on the waves like hail, because the *Pearl* hadn't been carrying cargo at all. She'd been carrying *insurance*." She spat the word into the lantern light. "Overloaded, badly ballasted, sent out in weather the oldest skipper in the harbor wouldn't have crossed a millpond in. And why? Because a drowned boat with papers pays better than a delivered cargo. Because Ambrose Hale had discovered that the sea will sign any receipt you ask of it."

"The boats," Xanthe said. His voice was barely there. "My mother always said—she wrote it, on her slate, the night she—"

"I know what she wrote." Maren's hands tightened on each other. "There were three Company boats standing off the wreck that night. Three. Not passing vessels, not unlucky witnesses—they'd escorted us out, same as always, riding herd on their investment. We came up screaming, and those boats turned away."

The lantern hissed. The wind found a gap in the planks and moaned through it low and long.

"Not sailed away," she said. "Turned away. Tacked off, slow and deliberate, like men declining an invitation. We were forty yards off, then sixty, then a hundred, and I swam until my arms were lead and my lungs were full of broken glass, screaming until my voice tore, and I watched Brigid Oduya go under not thirty feet from me with her bag of pearls still knotted to her wrist because even drowning, she wouldn't let go of what she'd bled for. I watched Tam Weller call out for his wife in a voice that got smaller and smaller like a lamp going out. Seven of us died that night within sight of three dry decks and forty pairs of working eyes. And your mother—"

Maren stopped. Eleven years of practice, and still this part always snagged.

"Your mother refused to turn away," she said finally. "She was on the wreck of the foredeck, half of it above water, and she got a line and she went in—wrist and all—and she pulled two men to the debris. Two of them lived to be grandfathers. Then she went back for a third and the sea took the deck out from under her and she went down, and I was too far away, and I saw her light go out too." She lifted her eyes to Xanthe's face. "So when they tell you your mother drowned in an accident, you believe what you need to believe to get through their countinghouses. But know this: Elias Vane dove after her himself. Captain of the escort, a Company man that night, and he stripped his coat on a burning-deck and went down three times looking for her until his own crew hauled him back half-drowned. He held her slate when it washed up two days after. He read what she'd scratched on it in the dark while she waited to die. And he never forgot it. Whatever else that man became, boy, that's the man who taught him the difference."

Xanthe was crying without any of the machinery of crying—no sound, no collapse, just the tears tracking down in the lantern light, the way water finds its level. Maren reached over and gripped his shoulder hard, diver's grip, the grip that says *I have you, look alive.*

"She wrote four words," he said at last, hoarse. "On the slate. I've kept it nine years and I've never shown anyone all four."

"*They watched us drown.*" Maren said it evenly, without flinching. "She showed it to me once, years before, about a different wreck, a smaller sin. Your mother believed in writing things down, boy. She believed ink was a promise to the future. That's why I'm giving you what I'm giving you."

She drew it from her coat pocket then: not Xanthe's mother's slate—that one stayed with him, and she nodded at his breast pocket where he carried it as he had carried it for nine years—but her own mother's. Old, cracked at the corner, framed in wood worn glassy by two generations of hands. Her grandmother had used it to teach fish prices; her mother to tally dive shares; Maren herself had scratched her first letters on it before she ever touched bottom-water.

"My mother's slate," she said. "It's not evidence. It's not a document Hale's lawyers will tremble at. It's older than this whole rotten company, and every woman in my line used it to write down what was owed. You take it to the widows—to Brigid's daughter in Millhaven, to Tam Weller's sister, to every household the Company made hollow that night. You show it, and you say Maren Kess stands witness, and they'll give you their words for your ledger. Because a token is a key, boy. It opens doors that signatures can't."

Xanthe took it with both hands, the way you take a child, or a coal of fire.

"Why now?" he asked. "You've held this eleven years. Why hand it to a clerk who can't leave his own shadow?"

Maren almost laughed. Almost.

"Because for eleven years I've been waiting for the story to grow legs," she said, "and tonight the fog tells me it finally has. A dead captain writing letters. A scarred stranger sleeping on my beach. And you—" she tapped the slate in his hands— "walking around with your spine straight for the first time since you signed yourself away. Hale thinks this harbor is his because he owns the paper. But paper burns, boy, and paper floats, and paper—" her smile showed teeth now— "paper can be copied by anyone who knows their letters. Which is more than Hale ever learned: he can read figures, but he cannot read *people*, and that's the ledger that's going to ruin him."

* * *

They walked back up the wreckers' path together as the fog thickened toward dawn, and at the fork where the paths divided—hers to the cottage, his down toward the town and the countinghouse and the cage he wore like a coat—Maren stopped him with a hand on his arm.

"One thing more, and you'll hear it once," she said. "Whatever Corvo has told you, whatever Vane wants—there are two ways to sink a man like Ambrose Hale. You can put a hole in his hull, and the sea takes him, and in a year nobody remembers which wave did it. Or you can make him surface, in daylight, before witnesses, and let the whole world watch him drown in his own paperwork. The first way is faster. The second way is forever." She searched his face in the greying light. "Vane is a hole-in-the-hull man. I've known him since he was younger than you, and grief has been sharpening him on both sides these three years. So you mind which way you're steering, Xanthe Thackeray, because your mother didn't die pulling men out of the water so her son could learn to push them in."

"I know," he said. "I think that's the only thing I do know."

"Well then." She patted his cheek, rough and brief, the only tenderness she allowed herself before weather. "Go to your numbers. Smile at your masters. And get me word when you want the widows assembled, because when this tide turns, boy, I intend to have every soul this harbor ever hollowed standing on the quay to watch."

She watched him descend toward the town until the fog took him, then turned up the cliff path. Behind her, far out past the Deacon Rock, the fog lay on the water like a sheet over furniture in a closed house—and beneath it, unseen, unheard, the dark shape of things inbound was already moving with the flood tide toward Greywater Bay.

By nightfall, Maren knew, there would be red coats at the harbor gates and questions with knives in them. By morning, someone would pay for all this hope.

She climbed faster.
