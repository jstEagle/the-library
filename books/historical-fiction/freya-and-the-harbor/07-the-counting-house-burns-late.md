## 7. The Counting House Burns Late

The order came on a Wednesday, folded inside a routine freight manifest, as if Ambrose Hale could not even summon the decency to ruin a man's sleep in person.

*Mr. Thackeray will attend the counting house each evening at seven o'clock for the duration of the autumn audit. He will work until relieved. He will speak to no one of what he sees.*

No signature beyond the Company's seal, pressed so hard into the wax it had split it—Xanthe had learned long ago to read Hale's habits even in his omissions. The director never signed orders he might later deny. He merely stamped them, the way a boot stamps mud into a floor.

Nine years ago such a summons would have frightened him. It still frightened him, but the fear had grown calluses, the way his hands had from hauling crates he was too educated to be carrying. That was the arithmetic of indenture: everything you were became something the Company owned, and everything you felt became something you carried quietly, in a place no ledger could audit.

He read the note twice at his desk in the clerks' hall, then folded it small and put it in his waistcoat, over his heart, where he kept all the things that might one day hang him.

Around him the day shift packed up—Pellam arguing about his supper, young Ostler whistling through the gap in his teeth, the whole ordinary machinery of men who would go home to wives and stew and lamplight and sleep. Xanthe watched them go with something that was not quite envy and not quite contempt. He had been the invisible one so long that invisibility fit him like his own coat. But coats wear thin.

"Working nights now?" Pellam paused at the door, hat already on, his breath fogging in the cold that came off the harbor through every seam of the building. "Hale's got you auditing freight? That's hangman's work, Thackeray. Nobody audits autumn freight and stays clean."

"It's overtime," Xanthe said, which was what obedient men said.

"Overtime." Pellam laughed without any laughter in it. "Sure. Mind the third floor, then. And mind—" he stopped himself, some instinct of survival overriding gossip, touched his hat brim, and was gone into the blue evening.

Xanthe sat alone a while in the emptied hall. Through the tall windows Greywater Bay was turning its colors: gulls going gray against gray water, the masts of the fleet swaying like a congregation at prayer, fog assembling itself out at the point with the patience of something that knew it always won. Somewhere out there, past the harbor gates, past the sandbar they called the Widow's Teeth, Corvo was waiting in whatever hole the wreckers kept for him, holding letters from a drowned man, and Xanthe had not yet decided whether knowing Elias Vane lived was a gift or a sentence.

He lit no lamp in the clerks' hall. He climbed the stairs to the third floor in the dark, because a man who worked nights learned to save his light for where it mattered.

* * *

The third floor of the Meridian Trading Company's counting house was where the real books lived—not the ledgers shown to shareholders in London, with their handsome columns and confident totals, but the working records, the raw intake of nine years of ships and cargo and men. Xanthe had been up here exactly four times in his indenture, and each time by accident, and each time he had memorized more than he meant to. That was his secret shame and his secret craft both: he could not help remembering numbers. His mother had been the same with tides. *The water doesn't lie, Xan,* she used to say, waxing her slate by lantern light before dawn dives. *It only waits.*

The night clerk assigned to supervise him was named Grole, an old Company man with a face like a dropped pie and hands that shook when he poured the brandy he wasn't supposed to drink on duty. Grole gave Xanthe a stack of freight manifests as thick as a Bible, pointed at a desk under the one lit lamp, said "Loss records first, Hale wants the winter claims drafted by Friday," and then retreated to a chair by the cold fireplace, where within the hour he snored with the deep, wet regularity of a man who had made his peace with his own conscience or lost it entirely.

Xanthe opened the first manifest and began, as instructed, to audit.

For two hours it was honest tedium: barrels of pitch, bolts of sailcloth, crates of tea gone damp in the crossing. He wrote his neat clerk's figures in the margins, initialed discrepancies, did the small faithful arithmetic of a small faithful life. The rain started around ten, soft against the tall windows, and the harbor lights below blurred into smears, and Grole's snoring kept time like a metronome, and Xanthe almost—almost—let the work make him stupid again. Numbers could do that. They could lull you into believing the world was balanced, that every column summed, that loss and gain were only entries.

Then he opened the file marked *Losses — Vessels, 1895–1897* and the world stopped balancing.

The first thing wrong was the handwriting. Loss reports were supposed to be sworn statements from masters or mates, their signatures witnessed at the harbor office. These bore a single hand throughout—elegant, unhurried, unmistakable. Xanthe knew it the way you know a voice through a wall. He had copied out freight orders in this hand during his second year, when Hale still trusted his best clerk's pen for anything. It was Hale's own writing, filling in the names of dead ships as calmly as a man filling in a dinner invitation.

The second thing wrong was the dates.

*Xanthe's mother went into the water in the spring of '95.* Nine years of careful silence had built the date into him like a cornerstone: the twelfth of April, the fog, the *Pearl of Greywater* sitting low and wrong at her moorings, the Company boats that stood off and watched, the divers who went down to inspect her hull and did not come up. Officially she had died in an accident of weather. Officially the *Pearl* had been lost with all papers, which was convenient, because a ship without papers cannot be audited.

Here, in Hale's beautiful hand, was the *Pearl*'s loss report—filed eight months after her sinking, backdated to April, claiming hull, cargo, and *fourteen lives insured at forty pounds per diver*, with the premiums paid by the Meridian Trading Company and the payout collected by the Meridian Trading Company, and the sworn statement of the master attesting that all safety protocols—

But the *Pearl*'s master had drowned too. Xanthe knew that. Everyone in Greywater Bay knew that; they had buried what the sea gave back of him in a coffin so small the sexton had wept. So whose statement was this? Xanthe turned the page. At the bottom, in a different ink, slightly newer, was the signature of the harbor master who had witnessed the document—a man who had held that office for six years and could not have witnessed anything in 1895.

Someone had signed a dead captain's oath three years after the captain died, and a witness who wasn't there, and the insurance house in London had paid out fourteen hundred pounds for fourteen drowned men, and the file was filed here, on the third floor, under a lock whose key hung on Grole's belt while Grole drooled into his collar ten feet away.

Xanthe sat very still. The rain ticked. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a watchman's boots crossed the yard and faded.

He thought, absurdly, of his mother's slate, of the letters she had scratched into it before her last dive—her name, the date, the depth, and one word he had never understood until this moment: *low*. She had written *sits low*. She had known the ship was wrong. She had gone down anyway, because the Company's contract fined divers who refused inspection, and fines extended indentures, and her son was four years into his own indenture by then, and she was buying his freedom penny by penny with the only coin she had.

They had not even needed to push her. They had only needed to make refusing cost more than drowning.

Xanthe turned more pages with hands that did not shake, which surprised him; he had imagined his hands would shake at this hour ever since he was eleven years old and pulled his mother's slate from a fisherman's net. Ship after ship. The *Cormorant*, scuttled on the bar, insured twice—once in London, once through the Dutch house at Rotterdam, which should have been impossible unless someone had forged the survey. The *Kindly Ann*, listed as lost with a cargo of silver that no manifest ever showed her loading. The *Merry Weather*, whose crew had survived, thank God, and whose mate had tried to swear out the truth and been found floating under the quay a fortnight later, officially drunk.

And in every file, the same elegant hand. The same patient forgery of oaths. The same arithmetic, repeated nine times across two years: *premium paid, vessel lost, claim collected.* Over and over, the Company drowning its own ships the way it had drowned its own divers, and collecting on both, and the difference between the two crimes being only that ships left no widows who could count.

Except they had. Maren Kess could count. The widow Brannock could count. Half the crooked houses along the wreckers' beach could count, and had been counting silently for years, the way he had.

Xanthe looked over at Grole. Asleep. Rain on the glass. Lamp steady.

He thought: *You are a witness. You have always been a witness. Witnesses survive by seeing and saying nothing.* Nine years of that doctrine had kept his body fed and his secrets intact and his mother unavenged, and Corvo's arrival had already cracked it, and now here was the crack widened into a door, and through the door was a page, and the page was evidence, and evidence in the hands of a nobody clerk was worth nothing, but evidence in the hands of a newspaper abroad, or a magistrate not yet bought, or a branded pirate with a ship—

Evidence in Xanthe Thackeray's coat, tonight, walking out this door.

And if it walked out with him and he was searched—if Hale's watchmen patted down a night clerk on a whim—the workhouse for theft of Company property, or the gallows for conspiracy, and Hale would not even need to forge anything. There were cells under this building. There were men who owed Hale money. A clerk could drown in Greywater Bay as easily as a diver, and the report would be written afterward, in a fine hand, witnessed properly.

His mother's voice, or his memory of it, very calm: *The water doesn't lie, Xan. It only waits.*

He was done waiting.

* * *

He did not take the original. That was the first discipline—he surprised himself with how clearly he thought it, as though nine years of copying other men's figures had trained him for exactly this theft. An original missing from a locked file would be noticed by Friday, when the winter claims came due. A copy, made carefully, in a different notebook than the one initialed on his audit sheets, might never be noticed at all. Hale's whole system rested on the assumption that no one downstairs dared look up. Xanthe had spent nine years teaching everyone, Hale included, that he was furniture. Furniture is not searched.

He chose his page the way his mother chose a dive: one breath, one target, no waste. Not the *Pearl* itself—that would be the first file anyone checked if suspicion fell on him, the emotional choice, and Hale would think of it instantly because Hale knew about his mother, had perhaps always known. Instead he took the *Cormorant*: double-insured, forged survey, Hale's hand unmistakable, and—this was the beauty of it—witnessed by the same falsely-dated harbor master's signature as the *Pearl*. One signature, two dead ships. Whoever saw the copy would understand it was not an error. It was a system.

He waited for the watchman's round. He counted the boots across the yard, twelve steps, pause, twelve steps back, a rhythm as reliable as tide. On the pause he lifted the *Cormorant*'s loss report from its folder, laid it flat beneath the lamp, and set his own notebook beside it open to a blank page.

Copying was slow work. Slower than he'd guessed, because his hand wanted to hurry and hurry was how errors crept in, and an error in a forgery of a forgery would destroy the whole document's worth. He copied Hale's words in his own plain clerk's script—there was no point imitating the hand; the point was the content—and he copied the false signature, and he copied the false date, and he noted in the margin the premium figures from the London insurance house, cross-referenced against the payout recorded in the general accounts, which he had seen once, years ago, in a summary Hale had burned after the meeting but which Xanthe, who could not help remembering numbers, had never forgotten.

Fourteen hundred pounds for the *Pearl*'s divers. Two thousand six hundred for the *Cormorant*, claimed twice. He wrote the sums small and true.

Grole snorted awake mid-copy. Xanthe did not freeze—freezing was for guilty men, and he had decided, somewhere in the last quarter hour, that guilt was a luxury for later—but turned a manifest page and said, mildly, "Mr. Grole, the *Kindly Ann*'s tonnage doesn't agree between the intake sheet and the loss report."

"Mm." Grole squinted at the clock, at the rain, at the clerk who had worked for the Company longer than most wives put up with their husbands. "Initial it and move on. Hale wants volume, not virtue." He poured brandy with his shaking hand and slept again, and Xanthe understood that Grole had seen these files before, many times, over many years, and had solved the problem of them the way old men solve everything: by drinking until the numbers blurred.

There were perhaps four men in Greywater Bay who had refused to blur. One was in jail soon, maybe. One was a pirate. One taught gutting on the wrecker's beach. And one sat under a borrowed lamp committing treason in pencil.

He finished the *Cormorant*. Then—because courage, once drawn on, ran like a tapped cask and he might as well drink it dry—he took the *Merry Weather* as well, the ship with the murdered mate, since a drowned honest man was a harder thing for a magistrate to explain away than a drowned dishonest one. Three pages total, folded once, wrapped in oilcloth from his lunch, and sewn—three quick stitches he'd learned from watching sailmakers—into the lining of his coat's inner breast pocket, over his heart, where the summons already lived. His coat now carried two documents that together weighed less than a letter and more than the harbor.

He refiled the originals. He squared the folders. He erased, with a gum square and great patience, every trace that the folder edges had moved, and he initialed his audit sheets honestly, because the audit itself had been genuine and a genuine audit was his alibi: *I was here all night doing exactly what I was told.* Obedience was the deepest water he had, and he had learned to hold his breath in it a very long time.

At half past two the rain eased. Grole woke properly at three, unlocked the files, counted them by rote—twelve folders, twelve folders present—signed Xanthe's time sheet without reading it, and said, with something almost like kindness, "Go home, Thackeray. You've got the look of a man auditing ghosts."

"Only freight," Xanthe said.

"Sure." Grole looked at him one beat too long, eyes red-rimmed and old and knowing nothing, or knowing everything, there was no way to tell and it didn't matter. "Only freight."

Outside, the town was asleep under a sky the color of wet slate. Fog had come all the way in from the point and taken the harbor, so that the masts appeared and vanished like the masts of sunken ships, and the gas lamps burned halos around themselves, and the tide, far out at the Widow's Teeth, was beginning its long slow turn toward the spring flood. Four days, maybe five, and the water would be high enough over the bar for a dark ship to come in quiet—or be signaled to stay away, depending on what got decided on the wreckers' beach.

Xanthe walked home through the fog with his coat buttoned to his chin. At the corner of Fishergate he stopped, took out his watch, and pretended to consult it, and in the pretending saw the reflection of the shop window across the way: a man in an oilskin some twenty feet behind him, keeping pace, unhurried, one of the new watchers Hale had hired onto the harbor gates. Not following him specifically. Following everyone. That was almost worse.

*He knows something has changed,* Xanthe thought. *He gave me the night work because he wants me where he can weigh me. He is baiting a trap and I have just walked into the room where the trap is being built.*

Or—and this came to him with a cold clarity that felt almost like his mother's voice—*he is baiting a trap, and you have just stolen the cheese, and the only question left is whose hand snaps shut.*

He reached his rooms, barred the door, lit no lamp. In the dark he unpicked one stitch of the coat lining just to touch the oilcloth, to confirm it was real, that the pages existed, that the elegant hand had really confessed itself in duplicate and triplicate under his pencil. Real. Warm from his body already, as if the paper had a pulse.

Tomorrow he would find Corvo. Tomorrow he would put these pages in the scarred man's ruined hands and let the whole dangerous machinery start turning—Vane, the ship, the spring tide, Maren Kess and the widows, all of it. He understood, standing in his own dark with fog pressing at the shutters, that there was no version of the future now in which he went back to being furniture. He had copied the page. He had crossed from the shore where witnesses stood into the water where conspirators swam, and the water, his mother would have told him, does not care whether you meant to dive.

He was almost asleep when the knocking started—soft, deliberate, three taps and a pause and three more, from the alley side of the building, where no honest visitor would stand at four in the morning.

Xanthe lay still. The knock came again. And then a voice, low and roughened, through the shutter slats:

"Thackeray. It's begun already. They've taken Maren Kess."
