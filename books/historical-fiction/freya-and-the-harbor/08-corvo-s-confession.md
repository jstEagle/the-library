## 8. Corvo's Confession

The wreckers' beach smelled of tar and old losses.

Corvo had been ashore three days now, and the smell had worked itself into his clothes, his hair, the creases of his ruined face, so that he could no longer tell whether it came from Greywater Bay or from him. He crouched at the tideline where the wrack piled in black ridges—kelp, split timber, a child's boot with no pair—and he watched Xanthe Thackeray walk toward him across the shingle with the careful, measured steps of a man who had spent nine years learning not to be noticed.

That was the thing about the clerk that gnawed at him. Corvo had known brave men and broken men and men who were both at once, and he knew how to read them the way a sailing master reads weather. Thackeray was not a coward. But he moved through the world as if it might notice him and punish him for existing, and Corvo had buried too many men who moved that way to trust it. Caution kept you alive right up until the moment it killed you. He had seen that arithmetic play out before, written in fire.

"You came," Xanthe said. Not an accusation. Worse—an observation, delivered flat, as though Corvo's presence were merely another item to be entered in a ledger.

"I said I would."

"Men say many things." Xanthe stopped an arm's length away, hands clasped behind his back like a schoolmaster or a prisoner at attention. His eyes went to the water, the cliffs, the dark mouth of the tunnel behind them—checking, always checking. "Hale's men walked the quay twice tonight. New faces. They don't drink local and they don't talk to anyone. That costs money."

"It does." Corvo turned a stone under his boot. Beneath it a crab the size of his thumbnail clattered sideways into the weed. Everything here hid; everything here survived by hiding. "You count well. That's what they made you for."

Something flickered in the clerk's face—hurt, quickly shuttered. Corvo felt the barb land and did not take it back. Better the boy was stung now than dead later.

"Maren Kess says the tide will serve on Thursday," Xanthe said. "Spring tide, just past full dark. She says her nephew can hold a light in the old signal tower without the watch seeing it, if the fog sits low. And she says—" he hesitated, and for a moment he looked young, very young, a boy who had lost his mother in cold water nine years ago and never once cried about it where anyone could see, "—she says we should decide what we're asking Vane to do when he comes. Because once that light burns, there's no taking it back."

"We're asking him to come get his ledger and take us out of this harbor alive. What else would we ask?"

"That's what I thought you'd say." Xanthe looked out at the water. The fog was coming in off the sound in slow grey ropes, coiling around the headland light like something patient. "I keep thinking there ought to be more to it than that. A plan. A way to make sure Hale doesn't simply follow you out and buy another magistrate somewhere else. Maren thinks so too. She says a man like Hale doesn't drown. He floats."

Corvo laughed—a short, ugly sound, like a hatch cover slamming. "Maren Kess is smarter than half the captains I've sailed under."

"And what about you?" The question came quietly, but it came like a knife laid flat on a table between them. "What did you sail under, Mr. Corvo? You know the weight of these ledgers. You knew Vane's hand on sight. You knew which door of the countinghouse to fall against so the night clerk would find you and no one else. A stranger doesn't know those things. So either Vane told you everything—and men like Vane don't—or you already knew Greywater Bay before you ever washed up on it."

The fog closed a little. Somewhere out on the water, a bell buoy tolled its slow, half-drowned count.

*Here it is,* Corvo thought. *He wants it. He's earned it.* And underneath that, in the older voice, the voice that lived in the scar tissue: *If you tell him, he will look at you the way everyone looks at you. Like something that crawled out of a fire that should have finished the job.*

"Sit down," Corvo said. "This is a longer story than standing allows."

* * *

They settled in the lee of an overturned dory, its hull furred with barnacle, its name scraped away long ago. Corvo pulled a flask from his coat—not rum, but the harsh herbal spirits smugglers favored—and drank, and passed it, and Xanthe took a swallow without flinching, which surprised him a little and pleased him more.

"My name isn't Corvo," he said. "It was. Once. Before the fire it was just Silas Brand, second mate out of Port Sorrow, and before that nothing worth telling. Twelve years ago I signed articles with the Meridian Trading Company. Freight run, coastal trade. Honest work, or honest enough—the Company has always understood that honesty is a matter of which page you're looking at."

Xanthe said nothing. His stillness was total. It occurred to Corvo that the boy had probably sat through hundreds of interviews exactly like this one, on the other side of a desk, letting men talk themselves into his record books.

"Hale wasn't director then. He was cargo master. Rising. The kind of man who remembers your wife's name and your daughter's birthday and the exact sum you owe on your house, and offers to help with all three at once, and somehow you end up owning him more than you started with and thanking him for it." Corvo turned the flask in his scarred hands. The fire had taken two fingers' worth of feeling from his left; the flask wobbled, and he hated it. "I was good at my work and careless with my opinions, which is a bad combination. When the Pearl of Greywater went down—"

Xanthe's breath stopped. Corvo heard it, the small catch, the way a rigging line sounds just before it parts.

"—when the Pearl went down," he went on, deliberately, because the boy deserved to hear it spoken plainly by someone who knew, "I was already gone from Meridian's rolls. Transferred. Conveniently transferred, I understand now, so that nobody who might have asked questions about the insurance papers was anywhere near the harbor that month. But I had friends aboard her. Divers I'd drunk with. And when the news came in, when we heard that the Company boats stood off and watched men and women drown within shouting distance, I said so. Loudly. In a room where Hale's friends could hear me."

The buoy bell tolled. A gull complained somewhere in the fog, thin and bitter.

"They didn't kill me," Corvo said. "Killing is expensive and leaves questions. They did worse. They ruined me slowly, by arithmetic. Debts I didn't owe. Wages withheld. A black mark that followed me from port to port so no reputable ship would sign me. In eighteen months I went from second mate to ballast. In two years I was drinking in ports I'm ashamed to name, and Hale—" he spat the name like a fishbone— "Hale sent a man to find me. Not to hurt me. To *hire* me. He wanted a man with nothing left to lose, and he'd made one, and he considered that good management."

"He put you on his own ship."

"He put me on the *Meridian Star*, the night she burned. The night Elias Vane died." Corvo let the silence stretch, let the boy absorb it, because the next part would not survive being rushed. "Only Vane didn't die. And neither did I."

Xanthe's voice came out very carefully controlled. "The fire. The official record says the powder room ignited. Eleven dead."

"The official record was written by Hale's clerks, and I've since read enough of Hale's clerks' work to know what that's worth." Corvo touched his own face without meaning to—the melted topography of it, the cheek that had fused into something smooth and wrong, the eye that no longer fully closed. "There was no accident. There was an order. Vane had found the loss records—he'd been carrying freight for Meridian for years and he'd started noticing that ships full of insured cargo kept meeting misfortune, and divers' wages kept being paid to widows instead of living men, and the numbers only balanced one way. He told Hale he was going to the Company board overseas. He was fool enough to say it out loud. So Hale decided Elias Vane would die spectacularly, with witnesses, in a way that would let the Company collect on both the ship and the captain's life policy, and would teach every other captain in the fleet what curiosity cost."

"But the fire—"

"I set part of it." Corvo said it flatly, because there was no other way to say it. "That's the confession you're owed, clerk, and you can carry it however you like. Hale ordered a small fire, forward, in the lazarette—enough to scuttle the insurance claim, nothing more, and Vane and the officers would be ashore safe while it burned. That's what I was promised. What I didn't know was that Vane had changed his plans and slept aboard, and what I didn't *guess*—because I was a fool and a drunk and grateful for the coin—was that Hale never intended a small anything. The powder room went up forty minutes after I lit the lazarette. Someone else's hand did that. Hale's own bosun, a man named Grell, whom I have dreamed about most nights since."

The flask was empty. Corvo set it down in the shingle with exaggerated care, because his hands wanted to throw it into the sea.

"I woke in the water. I don't remember getting there. I remember the fire standing over the bay like a second lighthouse, and I remember finding Vane clinging to a spar, and both of us so far gone with burns that we couldn't even hold on to our hatred of each other. Because he knew. He looked at my face in the light of his own burning ship and he *knew* whose hand had lit it, and he hauled me onto that spar anyway. Held me on it. Kept my head above the swell for four hours until a fishing boat out of the islands picked us up. Four hours. With what I'd done."

Xanthe was looking at him. Not with disgust—that was the strange thing, the thing Corvo had braced for and hadn't got. With something more terrible: recognition. As if he were reading a ledger entry and finding his own name in it.

"Why didn't he kill you?"

"He asked himself the same question, I think, for about a year." Corvo almost smiled; the scars made smiling an imprecise business. "Then he said something I'll repeat until I die. He said: *A debt you can't repay is the only chain that never rusts.* And he put me to work. Three years, clerk. Three years of running messages, moving coin, building a new name in ports where nobody asks what happened to your face because half of them are hiding their own. Finding the widows. Collecting testimony. Getting the second ledger copied and hidden aboard his ship, page by page, in six different hands so no single scribe could be traced. And waiting. Because you cannot fight a company, boy. You can only make it answer for itself, in front of the whole world, in ink it cannot buy back."

* * *

The fog had thickened. The headland light was a smear now, a wound of yellow in the grey. Down the beach, the tide was turning, and the sound of the shingle shifting under the retreating water was like slow, continuous applause—or slow, continuous escape.

"So you came here," Xanthe said at last. "To finish it."

"To finish it." Corvo nodded slowly. "But hear the rest, because you've earned that too, and I'd rather you hear it from me than guess it at a bad moment. I don't trust you."

Xanthe blinked. Whatever he had expected—pity, apology, brotherhood—it wasn't that. "You don't—"

"No. And I want you to understand why, because understanding it may be the only thing that keeps us both breathing through Thursday." Corvo shifted to face him fully. In the dying light his ruined face must have been hard to look at, and he let it be looked at anyway. "Everything you've done since I arrived, you've done carefully. You burn letters unread. You check the street twice. You weigh every word before you spend it, the way your Company taught you, and you think that caution is armor. But I watched a whole crew of careful men die on the *Star*, and I'll tell you what killed them. It wasn't carelessness. It was the belief that if they moved slowly enough, quietly enough, politely enough, the fire would pass them by. Hale doesn't reward caution. He harvests it. Every year you stayed silent in that countinghouse, he built another deck on top of you. Nine years, Thackeray. Nine years you knew—or half-knew—what those books meant, and you filed them neat and straight and told yourself you had no choice."

"That's not—" Xanthe's voice cracked, and Corvo saw, finally, the fury underneath all that stillness, the thing that had been banked down so long it had nearly gone out. "You don't know what it was. You weren't indentured at fourteen. You didn't watch them write your mother's death into a column marked *operational loss* and initial it. You didn't—"

"No," Corvo said quietly. "I did something worse. I lit the fire myself, and told myself I was only following orders. So don't think I'm judging you from some high place, boy. I'm judging you from the bottom of the same pit. The difference is I've learned what happens down there when you wait."

The waves worked the beach. Far off, the buoy bell counted on, indifferent.

"Thursday," Xanthe said finally. His jaw was tight, his eyes bright and dry the way eyes get when crying has been forbidden too long to be possible. "Say what you actually mean. All of it. Then I'll decide whether I trust you."

"What I mean is this." Corvo leaned close, and the smell of burnt skin and tar and salt went with him. "When that light goes up on Thursday night, Hale will know within the hour. He has bought the magistrate, the harbor master, and half a dozen knives, and the moment Vane's hull touches Greywater water, he will have law, guns, and opportunity all pointed at it. Your plan—light, signal, row out, sail away—is a plan for survivors. It is not a plan for victory. Vane will want Hale's blood, and he'll be right, and you'll argue with him, because you're a clerk and clerks believe paper can hang a man like Ambrose Hale. Maybe you're right too. I don't know anymore. But I do know that between your paper and his pistol, somebody in this harbor is going to bleed, and I have owed Elias Vane a debt for three years that I can only pay by finishing what the *Star* started. So I am telling you now, in the last quiet hour either of us will get: whatever happens Thursday, I am done being careful. And if your caution gets in the way of that debt, clerk, you and I are going to have a problem that no ledger solves."

For a long moment Xanthe said nothing. Then he reached inside his coat and drew out a folded paper—not the copied freight page, Corvo saw at a glance, but something older, soft at the creases, handled often. He held it out.

"What's this?"

"The manifest of the *Pearl of Greywater*. The real one. Names, wages, next of kin. I reconstructed it over four years, from memory, from parish records, from what the widows told me." Xanthe's voice was steady now, and it was a different voice, lower, with iron in the floor of it. "Thirty-one souls. Twenty-six recovered. Five listed as lost at sea who were, per the Company's own boatswain's log, alive in the water when the boats stood off. My mother is the fourth name. You talk about your debt to Vane. Fine. But you don't carry mine, and you don't get to call me careful again. I've been careful the way a man holding his breath is careful. Thursday, I stop holding it. Just—God help us both—let it be for something more than blood."

Corvo took the manifest. His damaged fingers fumbled it, and Xanthe steadied his wrist without thinking, without flinching at the touch of scarred flesh, and that small unthinking kindness undid Corvo more than any argument could have.

"Ambrose Hale floats," Corvo said at last, echoing Maren Kess. "So we'll need more than a signal fire to sink him."

"Then it's fortunate," said Xanthe, rising, shaking the fog from his coat, "that Maren Kess has been organizing the widows of this harbor for nine years, and tonight I'm finally going to tell her everything."

He turned toward the cliffs, toward the tunnels, toward the town where the lamps were coming on one by one like a jury filing in—and Corvo, gathering the empty flask and the weight of three years' guilt, followed him up the wreckers' road, and for the first time since the fire, the following felt less like penance and more like company.
