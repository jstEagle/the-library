## 10. Bait Taken

The page was gone.

Xanthe knew it before he touched the desk—knew it the way a diver knows a current has shifted before the water shows him anything, some animal ledger in the blood tallying what should be there and is not. He stood in the doorway of the counting house with his satchel still on his shoulder and the morning fog pressing at the windows, and he looked at the blotter where he had left the copied sheet beneath his logbook, weighted with an inkwell as he had done every night for a week, and the desk was only a desk. Leather, wood, a ring of dried ink like a tide mark. Nothing underneath but the blotter's soft grey face looking up at him.

He crossed the room slowly, because running would be a confession, and set down his satchel, and lifted the logbook, and turned the inkwell aside, and slid his fingers across the blotter as though the paper might have sunk into it, might have soaked through into some lower stratum of the desk where lost things went to wait. His fingertips came away clean.

* * *

He had copied the page over three nights, a few lines at a time, in the intervals between legitimate entries, using the same iron-gall ink and the same nib so that any difference in hand would come down to pressure alone—and he had practiced the pressure, practicing like a boy at his letters, until his forgery of Hale's own clerkly script could pass his own eye by lamplight. That had been the arrogance of it. He had thought the danger was in the copying. He had thought that once the copy existed, folded into the false bottom of Maren Kess's market basket, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, the dangerous part was finished. He had even begun to allow himself something adjacent to hope—the spring tide was four days out; Corvo had the route through the wreckers' channels; Vane's ship would answer the signal fire—and hope, Xanthe understood now, standing in the cold counting house with his heart going thin and fast, was precisely the kind of carelessness that got people drowned.

The forged page itself—the bait Hale had planted, the fabricated loss record showing the *Meridian Gull* insured twice over and scuttled once—he had not intended to steal at all. He had intended only to confirm it, to hold it under his glass long enough to read the watermark, and leave it exactly where it lay, untouched, so that no one could ever say a hand had been near it. But on the third night, exhausted, half-dreaming of green water and his mother's slate, he had copied it too. Copied it and returned it. He was almost certain he had returned it. He remembered smoothing it flat. He remembered the inkwell coming down on its corner.

The corner. He turned the blotter over with hands that did not feel like his hands. There, pressed faintly into the felt, was a rectangular ghost—a pale rectangle where the page's dye had transferred a corner's worth of itself to the fabric. And the ghost was whole. Four corners. The entire page had lain here, flat and complete, after he placed it back.

So it had been taken since. Taken deliberately, and recently, and by someone who knew what they were looking for.

Xanthe straightened. Around him the counting house performed its morning arithmetic—ledgers opening, pens scratching, Tredgold two desks over complaining about a mislabeled crate of indigo as though indigo were the world's great scandal. No one looked at him. No one ever looked at him; that had been the whole architecture of his nine years here, invisibility laid up course by course like ballast stone. He hung his coat on its peg. He sat at his desk. He opened yesterday's freight manifest and ran his finger down columns of tallow and salt cod, and inside his skull a voice was screaming with a clarity he had not permitted himself since childhood.

*They know. They have known since the night you took it. Every hour you sit here they are watching you sit here.*

* * *

He lasted four hours before he broke his own rules.

There was a delivery he could legitimately make—the quarterly abstract to the harbor master's office, a leather folio, a signature required—and he took it, and walked out through the yard where stevedores were winching crates past the customs shed, and felt the weight of eyes between his shoulder blades without once turning to find them. Beyond the Company wall the town smelled of low tide and woodsmoke. Gulls argued over the fish tables. Ordinary life, proceeding as though nothing had changed, and he moved through it like a man carrying a lit lamp through a powder store.

Maren Kess sold winkles from a barrow by the fish gate on Thursdays. That much was ordinary too; she had sold there since before he was born. He did not slow as he passed. He did not look at her. He said, in the flat voice of a man reciting an invoice, "Fair morning for it," which was their signal for *nothing yet*, and she grunted, which meant she'd heard, and he walked on eleven paces to the end of the arcade where he could see both the quay and the mouth of Fishers' Row.

He was counting to sixty when the bells began.

Not the harbor bells—the church bell, and then the watchman's rattle, and then a sound he had heard only twice in nine years: the Meridian whistle, three short blasts, the Company summoning its own authority into the street. Down Fishers' Row came six men at a pace just short of running, four of them constables in bad coats and two of them not constables at all but the quiet, broad-shouldered men who drank at the Anchor and were paid from accounts Xanthe had balanced himself. At their center, unmistakable even at distance by her height and her white plait of hair, went Maren Kess, her wrists bound before her with cord, walking steadily, refusing the elbow that tried to steer her, her chin up as though she were being escorted to a dinner held in her honor.

A constable nailed a paper to the notice board beside the fish gate. Xanthe did not need to cross the square to read it; he had written enough warrants-adjacent documents in this town to know the shape. *Harboring pirates. Enemies of trade. Committed for examination.* The words assembled themselves behind his eyes in Hale's elegant clerical hand.

The barrow sat abandoned, winkles spilling from its tipped basket onto the cobbles. A gull landed in the middle of them and began to eat with the unconcern of a creature that had watched empires fall.

*Maren.* The word was not a thought so much as a place his mind kept returning to and finding on fire. They had taken her within six hours of taking the page. Which meant the page was never the point. Which meant the page had been laid on his desk like a lure dragged before a cod, and he—careful, invisible, penny-by-penny careful Xanthe Thackeray—had risen to it with his mouth open.

And she had been arrested not for anything she did but for what she was to him: the oldest diver in Greywater Bay, the woman who taught his mother the reefs, the one name that connected every thread of the case Hale was quietly sewing shut. *Harboring pirates*—which meant Corvo was known too, which meant the wreckers' beach might already be watched, which meant everything they had built in three weeks of whispers was built on ground Hale owned.

* * *

Corvo found him at the agreed place—an alley behind the cooperage where rain from the eaves had worn a dark seam down the wall—within the hour, which meant Corvo had heard the bells too, which meant Corvo had come anyway, into a town whose exits were closing one by one.

"The beach is watched," Corvo said without preamble. He stood well back in the shadow, hat low; even so, half his ruined face caught the grey light, the burn scar pulling his mouth into a permanent grimace that had nothing to do with how he felt. "Two men at the cliff path and a boat cruising off the point, riding to a Company pennant. Your director has spent money."

"He can afford it." Xanthe's voice sounded far away to him. "He insures ships he sinks. We've seen the arithmetic."

"And now he has your old diver." Corvo's good eye fixed on him. "Which means by tonight he'll have whatever she knows, and she knows the beach, the channels, and your face, boy. She's stubborn, I'll grant—salt gets into them—but everyone talks eventually. It's only a question of what the asking costs."

"She won't talk." Xanthe heard the words come out of him with a certainty that surprised them both.

"Everyone talks," Corvo repeated, but gently, and Xanthe understood that he was not describing Maren's weakness; he was describing the world's.

They stood in the dripping alley. Somewhere beyond the roofs, the Meridian whistle blew again, and again, calling the harbor master, the magistrate's clerk, all the small gears of the machine Hale had oiled for years.

"Then we go now," Corvo said. "Tonight's tide, or the next one if the watchers turn in. There's a boat at the north slip I can have for the price of a coat. We light nothing, we signal nobody—we row out past the point in the dark and let Vane's lookout find us, because his lookout is better than Hale's. You, me, the testimony we carry, the pages sewn in my jacket. In a month the ledger is in a printer's shop in Lisbon or Boston and Greywater Bay is a story other people tell." He paused. "It's a clean ending, boy. Clean endings are rare. Take one when it's offered."

"And Maren hangs for it."

Corvo was quiet a moment. Rain ticked in the cooper's gutter. "Yes," he said finally. "Probably. Or transportation, if the magistrate is in a mild mood, and Hale pays him extra to be mild, because a hanging makes noise and noise makes newspapers." He said it flatly, a man reading a chart of depths he had already sounded. "I've left people behind before. On decks, in ports, in burning water. You do the arithmetic of the many against the one and you row, and you don't sleep well, but you row. That's what surviving is."

"I made her a promise," Xanthe said. "Not in words. She gave me my mother's slate, Corvo. She put it in my hands and told me to learn what the dead write. If I row away and let them examine her in a cell—" He stopped. The word *examination* sat in his stomach like swallowed ballast. He knew what examination meant in a town where the magistrate signed what Hale paid him to sign. It meant questions asked in rooms with no windows, asked patiently, over days, by men who were paid by results.

"Nine years," Corvo said. "You were invisible for nine years. You told me. You survived by making yourself nothing." The burned side of his face twitched, the way it did when he was choosing words carefully among all the sharp ones available. "If you stay for her, you stop being nothing. You become the man who was in that counting house when the page went missing. You become the leak Hale is hunting. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Staying doesn't save her. Staying may drown you both, and then the ledger drowns with you, and then your mother drowned for nothing, and the divers of the *Pearl* drowned for nothing, and I wore this face for nothing."

Every word was true. Xanthe stood with his back against the wet wall and let the truth of it break over him the way cold water breaks over a wading man—wave after wave, each one undeniable, none of them the whole sea. Because there was another arithmetic underneath Corvo's, an older one, the first sum he had ever learned, scratched in chalk on a diving slate by a woman who could not read print: *what you owe is not measured by what you can afford.*

His mother had gone down for the Company's insurance money. Maren Kess had taught her the reefs that let her work at all, had pulled her half-frozen from the water twice, had given a silent boy a slate and told him his silence was not the same as consent. If he fled, Hale would not merely punish Maren. Hale would *use* her—parade her, break her, hang her piracy from the fish-gate post as a warning nailed to the whole harbor: *this is what remembering costs.* And every widow on Widow's Row, every boy who mended nets, would learn the lesson and go back to being nothing, and Greywater Bay would belong to Ambrose Hale forever, tidied, insured, sunk at his convenience.

"They'll move her tonight," Xanthe said. "Before the assizes. To the county jail, where no one watches. Once she's out of this town she's dead; you said it yourself—noise makes newspapers, and Hale will want her quiet."

"So?"

"So we don't row tonight." Xanthe pushed off the wall. His hands had stopped shaking; that frightened him more than the shaking had. "We row tomorrow, or we row after. Tonight we follow the prisoner wagon."

Corvo stared at him, and for the first time since the alley, the ruined face showed something unguarded—not a grimace but astonishment, plain and almost young. "Boy—"

"You said everyone talks eventually." Xanthe picked up his satchel, the clerk's satchel, the invisible man's satchel. "Hale talked tonight. Six men and a wagon and a pennant boat—he showed me his whole hand because he thinks I'm still hiding under my desk. Nine years I taught this town not to see me. Let's see what it's worth when I decide to be seen."

He turned toward the mouth of the alley, toward the bells and the gray water and the cell where an old diver sat refusing, even now, to give up his name.

Behind him, boots splashing through the puddle-seam, Corvo followed.
