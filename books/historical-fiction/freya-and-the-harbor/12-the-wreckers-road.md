## 12. The Wreckers' Road

The jail in Greywater Bay was built to hold fishermen who brawled and smugglers who were slow, and it had never once been asked to hold a man the Director of the Meridian Trading Company wanted kept until morning. Corvo had spent two days learning that, and one night deciding what to do about it.

He learned it first from a boy of perhaps eleven who sold whelks from a basket and watched everything with eyes like wet slate. The boy—Pike, the other dock children called him, though Corvo doubted he had another name—took Corvo's coin on the second evening and told him, in the flat voice of a child who has already understood the world's arithmetic: *They moved him. Not to the cells by the water. To the strong room above the magistrate's stable. Two men on the door, and they're not Company men, they're Hale's own. The kind that don't wear livery so nobody can say later who gave the orders.*

Maren Kess stayed in the water-side cells, charged with nothing yet, held the way you hold a note of hand. Leverage. Corvo turned the word over in his mouth and found it tasted like the tar he'd chewed through three years of hard sailing. He knew leverage. He had been leverage once, a face ruined and a name erased, passed between men who signed papers about him without ever looking at him.

He would not be that again, and he would not watch Xanthe Thackeray become it either.

The trouble was that Corvo owed Vane a debt he could not count, and everything he did now was struck against that account. Breaking a clerk out of the Company's grasp before Hale chose to make his disappearance formal—that was not caution. That was fire carried in an open hand through a timber yard. Vane would have planned it for a month. Vane would have had three ways out and a fourth in reserve.

Corvo had a boy who sold whelks, a tide that was turning, and a promise he'd made to himself in a cell of his own once, in a port he did not name even in his own head.

That would have to be enough.

* * *

The magistrate's stable stood behind a wall that had been built to keep horses in, not armed men out, and its rear abutted the fish-curing sheds whose reek had defeated every attempt at gentility Greywater Bay had ever entertained. Corvo came at the change of the watch, somewhere between the third bell and the fourth, when honest men are drowsiest and dishonest men are at their most comfortable. He wore oilskins against the drizzle, which was common enough on the streets that night to be invisible, and under them a short boarding hatchet, which was less common but more useful.

There were two guards at the door, as Pike had said. They sat under the lean-to out of the rain, a lantern between them, a jug going back and forth. Company men off-duty drink like Company men on-duty: competitively, and with grievance. By the time Corvo circled the curing sheds and came up through the reek of brine and smoke, one of them was asleep sitting up and the other was telling the sleeping one a story about a woman in Saltmarsh, gesturing with the jug.

Corvo considered, briefly, the man he might have been if he still settled things quietly. Then he stepped into the lantern light, let the guard see him—one heartbeat, two, long enough for the man's mouth to open around the beginning of a shout—and put the hatchet's flat against the side of his head with just enough of his shoulder behind it. The jug hit the cobbles. The sleeping man slept on, which was the only mercy the night offered anyone, and Corvo dragged both of them inside the lean-to, bound them with their own belts, and took the ring of keys off the first one's neck.

The stable loft smelled of hay and horse and, beneath it, the tallow-and-ink smell of the Company's paper, because the strong room had been an office before it became a cage. There was a new hasp on the door, bright iron among old wood, and Corvo's hands remembered a hundred locked holds and how they complained before they gave.

Xanthe Thackeray was sitting upright on the cot when the door came open, dressed, boots on, as though he had been waiting for exactly this and had rehearsed his posture. Only his eyes betrayed him—they went from the hatchet in Corvo's belt to Corvo's scarred face and something in them steadied, which was not the reaction Corvo expected from a clerk meeting a pirate's man in the dark.

"You took your time," Xanthe said.

"There wasn't a fast way." Corvo scanned the little room. "They haven't charged you."

"They haven't *finished* with me." Xanthe rose. His wrists were ringed red; someone had taken his belt and bootlaces and been thorough about it. "Hale came himself. Yesterday evening. He was very courteous." A pause, and then, flatly: "He knew my mother's name. He's known all along, Corvo. Nine years I sat at that desk and he knew what I was and let me keep counting his freight because a secret is cheaper than wages."

"We can stand here and hate him when there's sea between us and his magistrates." Corvo tossed him the boots' laces, recovered from a guard's pocket. "Can you run?"

"I can walk very quickly for a long time. My mother was a diver. She'd have laughed at the question."

Outside, the drizzle had thickened toward rain, and the town lay under it like a coin under cloth. They went over the stable wall where a stack of cure-racks made a stair, dropped into the reeking alley, and ran—not straight, because straight roads have eyes, but the crooked way, down behind the cooperage, along the tidal sluice where the water talked loud enough to drown footfalls. Once a constable's lantern swung at an intersection ahead, and Corvo pulled Xanthe into a doorway that smelled of fish guts, and they stood chest to chest in the dark while the light passed, and Xanthe's breathing never once quickened.

"You've done this before," Corvo muttered as they moved on.

"No," Xanthe said. "But I've imagined it every day for nine years. It's nearly the same thing."

They reached the cliff paths above the wreckers' beach an hour before dawn, and that was where the night stopped being theft and became something else—because there were men waiting on the path, and Corvo's hand went to the hatchet, and a woman's voice out of the fog said, "If you draw that on Dunny Tam you'll regret it, he's got six brothers and all of them uglier."

* * *

Her name was Sorrel Fitch and she had been running goods through the cliffs under Greywater Bay since before the Meridian Company had finished building its counting house, and she made this known within four sentences, as credentials. She was sixty if she was forty-five, rope-thin, with a face like a drift log—smooth at a distance, full of holes close up—and she looked at Corvo the way an old horse looks at a new rider, measuring weight and temper together.

"Corvo," she said. "That's the name. The one that sailed with Vane. Half the harbor thinks you're a story."

"Half the harbor's right most of the time."

"And the other half says Vane's alive and coming back with fire in his fist." Her eyes were quick and humorless and missed nothing—the hatchet, the clerk, the direction they'd come from. "Which half do you belong to?"

"The half that needs the tunnels before the gate watch changes."

She considered him for a long moment, rain dripping off her hood. Then: "Tam. Take them down by the low way. And mind the third gallery—it flooded at spring tide and it'll flood again tonight, the way the moon's sitting."

"Why?" Corvo asked. Not why the tunnel. Why any of it. Why risk the noose for a clerk and a stranger.

Sorrel Fitch wiped rain from her face with the back of her wrist and looked at him with something that was almost pity.

"Because in 'ninety-one the Company watch took my son off the quay for lifting a sack of damaged rice—damaged, rotten, bound for the pit—and gave him to the press gang to square the books. Because Elias Vane fished two of my sister's boys out of the water after the *Pearl* went down, when the Company boats wouldn't put back. Because everything Hale built, he built out of us, and we remember the shape of every stone." She turned away, then added, over her shoulder: "Also because Maren Kess sent word down the beach an hour ago. If you came for the clerk, bring him safe. If you didn't come at all, tell him she said: *don't be noble twice.*"

Dunny Tam was a huge, silent man with a lantern shuttered to a slot of light, and he led them down into the cliffs through a crack in the rock that Corvo would have sworn, passing it at noon, was nothing but a shadow.

* * *

The tunnels under Greywater Bay were older than the wreckers who used them, cut when the cliffs were quarried for the harbor's first stones, and the smugglers had widened and shored them across three generations until the whole headland was honeycombed—a road with no map except memory. Tam went surely in the dark, calling hazards in a low voice: *step down, low arch, mind the sump.* The sea came through the stone everywhere, a pressure in the ears, a breath in the dark galleries, sometimes a sudden gleam where a shaft opened on black water far below.

Corvo walked with the hatchet loose in his hand and his nerves stretched wire-tight, and it took him a mile of darkness to understand what was wrong with the picture: nothing was wrong. No pursuit behind. No ambush ahead. Men at the tunnel mouths above—Tam's brothers, presumably ugly and loyal—turning them onward with a lifted shuttered lamp and a nod. Word traveling faster than footsteps, down through the fog-bound town, from the jail to the beach to the cliffs: *they're moving the clerk tonight, clear the road.*

Nobody had ordered this. There was no pay in it. The forgotten people of Greywater Bay were simply, quietly, opening their own doors.

"It's the same under the whole point," Tam said at last, when Corvo asked. "Vane knew it, before. Ran cargo through here two winters. Never cheated the beach crews, never left a man ashore with the watch on his heels. There's them in this town would sell you for a shilling." He shrugged, a landslide in the dark. "There's more wouldn't. You just don't see us till you need us."

Behind them, faint through stone, a bell began to ring—the harbor alarm, thin and urgent. Xanthe stopped. "That's the gate watch. They've found the guards."

"Found them an hour ago by now," Corvo said. "Hale doesn't shout early. He'll seal the town gates, put men on the beach, send a rider to the magistrate. He'll want you back quiet, before word gets out that his clerk ran. A trial makes noise. A body in the tide doesn't."

Xanthe was quiet a moment. In the shuttered lantern's slot of light his face looked carved and older than his years. "Then Maren—"

"Stays where she is, and hates us for it, and stays alive because a live hostage is worth more than a dead witness." Corvo said it plainly because kindness would have been worse. "That's the arithmetic. I don't like it either."

"Don't say 'either,'" Xanthe said. "You didn't sit across a desk from her testimony for an hour yesterday while Hale smiled. Every word she said about the *Pearl*, he wrote down. Not for the court—for himself. He was deciding what she was worth." His voice didn't rise; that was the terrible thing about Xanthe Thackeray, Corvo thought. Everything came out level, like ledger lines. "We owe her more than arithmetic."

"We owe her a ship out and a story printed on three continents. That's the only payment she'll ever take." Corvo put a hand on the stone wall, steadying himself as the passage tilted. "Move. The tide doesn't wait and neither does Hale."

They came up out of the cliffs near midnight's end, through a gorse-choked sinkhole onto the wreckers' beach, where the bones of old ships lay silvered under the last of the ebb. The fog was lifting off the water in rags, and beyond the point, far out where the deep water began, Corvo saw what he had been watching the horizon for since dusk: a dark hull, no lights, riding too high and too fine for a fishing boat.

Vane's ship. Answering the signal they'd burned on the headland two nights past—or simply patient, the way Vane was patient, holding offshore and waiting for his man to earn his rescue.

And below it, small and fast and wrong, a second shape had cleared the point: the cutter, black-hulled, lateen-rigged, pulling hard for the same water. Hale had bought his warrant and his speed, and the magistrate's signature was worth more than any fog.

Tam saw it too. He swore softly, and then he did something Corvo did not expect: he unshuttered his lantern fully and swung it three times toward the dark ship—an old wrecker's signal, warning, *danger on your course*—and far down the beach, answering lights bloomed one by one among the beached fishing fleet, widows' boats and widows' sons lighting lamps in their windows and on their masts, until the whole shore was speaking to the sea in a language the Company had never bothered to learn.

"They'll jam the chain," Tam said grimly. "Sorrel's people. Slow the cutter down. It won't stop it."

"It doesn't need stopping," Corvo said. "It needs delaying until we're aboard and gone." He turned to Xanthe, and the words came out of him before he could weigh them, heavy as ballast: "There's a boat cached under the far shelf. Two oars, room for three." He looked at the cutter, then at the dark ship, then at the jail somewhere behind the fog-bound town where an old woman sat counting hours. "Room for three, Thackeray. Not four. Whatever we do next, we do knowing that."

Xanthe followed his gaze to the horizon, to the ship and the cutter converging on the same dark water, and when he answered, his voice was the flattest Corvo had ever heard it.

"Then we'd better decide fast. Because I don't think Vane is planning to sail away at all."
