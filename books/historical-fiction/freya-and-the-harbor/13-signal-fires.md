## 13. Signal Fires

The headland above Greywater Bay was called the Widow's Tooth, though no one Xanthe had ever asked could say why. Perhaps it was the way the cliff leaned out over the water like a woman craning toward a horizon that never gave back what it took. He had climbed its goat path once as a child, with his mother's hand rough around his wrist, and she had stood him on the grass at the summit and told him: *From up here you can see every lie the harbor tells.* He hadn't understood her then. He was nine years old and the harbor was only boats.

He understood her now. The wind at the top of the Tooth came off the sea raw and salt-heavy, tearing at his coat, and below him Greywater Bay lay spread out like something dissected for study—the crooked quay, the countinghouse roof with its green copper cresting, the Company warehouses squatting along the waterline like fattened ticks, the harbor chain's great iron links glinting where they broke the surface at the narrows. And beyond the chain, beyond the mole, the open water of the Roads, gray under a sky the color of old pewter, running in long slow swells toward a horizon that held, somewhere out in all that emptiness, a ship that should have been dead three years.

Corvo crouched beside the pile of driftwood they had hauled up the path through most of the night, feeding oilcloth and tar-soaked rag into its heart. His ruined face—half of it melted into scar tissue that pulled his mouth into a permanent grimace—was turned toward the sea, and the wind made him squint his one whole eye.

"Dark soon," he said. "We light it when there's just enough light left to see by and not enough to be seen from the quay." He glanced up at Xanthe. "You're certain of the signal? Three fires in a line, doused together, relit twice."

"I'm certain." Nine years old again, standing in this same spot while Captain Elias Vane drew the pattern in the air with two fingers: three points of fire, dark, dark again, then burning steady. *If I'm out past the shoals and I see that, boy, I'll know it's you and no one else. No one else would know it. Not Hale, not the harbor watch, not God himself.* Vane had laughed when he said it, that big careless laugh of his, before the war came and the Company came after, and everything careless in the world was spent.

Xanthe knelt and struck the first match. The wind killed it. The second caught, and he touched it to the tarred rag, and flame ran up the driftwood pile with a sound like a drawn breath.

* * *

They waited through the last hour of daylight with their backs against the wind, watching the three fires take hold. Corvo ate dried fish from a paper twist without seeming to notice he was doing it. Xanthe couldn't eat. The ledger's weight was gone from his life now—he had passed it to Corvo to carry down to the cove, to hold aboard whatever boat came for them—and yet he felt it still, strapped invisibly across his shoulders. Nine years of dead men's arithmetic. His mother's name written in a stranger's careful column: *asset lost at sea, value recovered.*

"Look," Corvo said.

Far out, at the very edge of sight where the swells turned silver, a light appeared. It burned for three heartbeats—a lantern hoisted, shown, doused—and then darkness, and then it showed itself again, twice more.

Three times. Dark. Dark. Steady.

Xanthe's breath went out of him as if he'd been struck. He had imagined this moment so many times in the past week—in the countinghouse, in Maren Kess's smoky kitchen, lying sleepless in his narrow room—that he had assumed he was finished with being surprised by it. He had been wrong. Elias Vane was alive, was answering, was coming, and grief and hope rose together in Xanthe's chest so tangled he could not have said which one was which.

"He sees us," Corvo said quietly. There was something in his voice Xanthe had never heard there before—not the hard flat caution the man wore like armor, but something younger, unguarded. "Three years I've carried those letters, Thackeray. Three years thinking I'd die before I—" He stopped himself. The grimace of his scars deepened, or perhaps it softened; it was impossible to tell. "He's coming."

"He's coming," Xanthe agreed, and the words tasted like spring.

That was when they heard the bell.

* * *

It came up faintly across the water from the harbor—the Company bell at the gate tower, ringing fast and urgent, the pattern that meant *all hands, close the chain.* Both men were on their feet before the second peal. Below them, Greywater Bay was waking in the wrong way: lamps flaring behind windows, figures spilling onto the quay, and at the mouth of the harbor the great capstan house beginning, unmistakably, to turn.

"They're raising the chain," Xanthe said. His voice sounded far away to his own ears. "At night? They never raise it at night unless—"

"Unless someone told them to expect company." Corvo's single eye had gone flat and bright. He pointed, and Xanthe followed the line of his finger to the Company wharf, where a sleek black shape was already breathing smoke—a steam cutter, low and fast, the *Meridian's Pride*, Hale's private toy, built for speed the way knives are built for cutting. Her boilers must have been lit for an hour. She had not begun getting underway tonight. She had been waiting.

And then Xanthe understood, all at once and completely, the way one understands a wave is going to break over a boat a moment too late to do anything about it. The magistrate. Hale owned the magistrate the way he owned the harbor gates, and magistrates talked, and warrants moved, and somewhere in the last two days—perhaps the moment Maren Kess was taken, perhaps before—someone had whispered to Ambrose Hale that a dead captain meant to come home.

"We have to warn him off," Xanthe said.

"With what?" Corvo's laugh had no humor in it at all. "We've no second signal he'd know. We can't show a lantern from here without telling the cutter exactly where we are. The ship's already answered, Thackeray. Vane will make for the smugglers' cove on the midnight tide because that's what we agreed, and the cutter will be sitting between the narrows and the cove with the chain raised behind her, and there will be nowhere for him to run but out to sea—or into the guns of the harbor battery." He spat, deliberately, into the wind. "Hale didn't need to catch him. He just needed to know when he'd come."

The fires crackled beside them, absurdly cheerful, still announcing to the whole dark coast exactly where the signal had come from. Xanthe looked at them and thought, with a strange cold clarity: *I did this. I lit them.* Hope arriving hand in hand with catastrophe, and no way to send one back without sending both.

"The cove," Xanthe said slowly. "He'll stand in toward the cove first, won't he? To take us off. That's the plan—we're supposed to be at the wreckers' beach by midnight with Maren's testimony and the widows' tokens."

"Maren's in the jail," Corvo said. "There is no testimony coming off that beach except ours."

"Then we don't go to the beach." Xanthe was already moving toward the goat path, his mind running ahead of him faster than his feet, the way it did in the countinghouse when the columns finally revealed their pattern. "We go around. Down the wreckers' road to the point below the battery—if we put a light on the wrong headland, in the wrong place, Vane will know it isn't the agreed signal. He taught me that much: any fire that doesn't answer true is a trap. But he has to *see* it, and to see it he has to come close enough that the cutter sees him too, and—" He stopped. Turned. "There's a boat below the cliff. Old Tomas keeps it in the cave under the Tooth. He owes me for nine years of looking the other way on his landing fees."

Corvo stared at him. "You want to row out to meet a pirate ship past a closed harbor chain and a steam cutter, in the dark, to shout a warning over open water."

"Yes."

For a moment the scarred man said nothing. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled—the half of his face that could—and it transformed him. "Vane always said you were the bravest clerk in the Company," he said. "He said it like an insult. Come on, then. And pray the old man hasn't gone stubborn enough to ignore a fire in the wrong place, because if he has, we're both going to hang by morning."

* * *

The cave under the Widow's Tooth breathed with the tide, a hollow groaning throat of wet stone, and Tomas's boat knocked against its inner rocks like a patient animal. Xanthe had rowed these waters since before he could write; his mother had put an oar in his hands at six years old and told him the sea was a creditor who never forgave a debt. They launched into the swell with the last light dying behind them and the three signal fires burning above like accusation, and Corvo took the second oar without being asked, pulling with a rhythm that spoke of years before his face was ruined.

The cutter's smoke hung against the darkening sky like a bruise over the harbor mouth. She was moving now, easing out past the raised chain with her running lights hooded, feeling her way toward the smugglers' cove with the patience of something that knew exactly where its prey would arrive. Behind her the town lamps glittered, indifferent. Somewhere in the jail down there, Maren Kess sat in a cell because of Xanthe's carelessness, and the thought of her rode in his chest alongside the thought of the ship coming over the horizon, two debts pulling him in opposite directions like tides.

"She'll make the cove in under an hour," Corvo said between strokes. "If Vane stands straight in, she'll be between him and open water before he knows she's there."

"Then we'd better be between them first."

They rowed. The swells came in long and orderly, lifting the little boat and setting it down, and the cold worked through Xanthe's coat and into his bones, and he rowed until his shoulders screamed, and kept rowing. Once, far off to seaward, he saw the dark shape he was looking for—a shadow among shadows, taller than the swells, making inshore under reduced canvas, ghost-quiet. Coming home. Coming into the trap they had lit for him with their own hands.

"There!" Corvo stood, rocking the boat dangerously, and fumbled beneath the thwart for the shuttered lantern they'd brought. "Get ready to kill the oars. If he has lookouts—and he has lookouts—he'll see us before he sees anything else. Pray he sends a boat and doesn't simply run us down."

"Or shoots," Xanthe said, and Corvo's grimace might have been agreement.

The shadow grew. The sea-sound of it reached them next, canvas and hull and the hiss of a bow-wave, enormous suddenly, and Xanthe stood up in the tossing boat with his heart hammering against his ribs and lifted the lantern, opening the shutter—once, twice, three times, the old wreckers' code, the *peace* sign, the sign every diver's child in Greywater Bay learned before they learned their letters.

The great shadow changed. Canvas rattled, and the ship came up into the wind perhaps two hundred yards away, a dark wall of hull and rigging blotting out the stars, and a voice came down across the water, thin and carrying and impossibly familiar, a voice Xanthe had last heard promising him a future in a warmer time:

"Who calls the *Pearl's* sister? Name yourself or eat iron!"

Corvo dragged in a breath beside him. Xanthe cupped his hands and shouted with everything nine years of silence had stored up in him:

"Elias Vane! It's Xanthe Thackeray, and you're sailing into a noose! The cutter's in the narrows and the chain is raised—turn out to sea or you'll be sunk inside your own harbor!"

Silence, but for the water. Then the sound of hurried movement aboard, orders snapped low, and the ship began—slowly, majestically, agonizingly—to fall off the wind, her bow swinging toward the open dark.

But behind Xanthe, out of the south, came a new sound: the quickening slap-slap-slap of a steam engine pushed hard, and a hooded light winking open, showing a low black hull and the gleam of a gun mount forward.

The cutter had seen them too.

"Hold that light up," Corvo said quietly, taking the lantern from Xanthe's shaking hands and holding it high, making themselves the brightest thing on the water. "Whatever happens now, boy—keep it burning."
