## 16. Two Kinds of Justice

The smugglers' cove smelled of tar and cold iron, and the fire they had built against the cliff threw shadows that made the men around it look like a jury of the drowned.

Xanthe sat with the ledger on his knees, wrapped in oilcloth, and he did not let go of it. Not when Corvo paced. Not when the tide came up the shingle and licked at their boots. Not even when Vane came out of the darkness beyond the firelight like a man rising from his own funeral, gaunt and salt-burned, and stood over him with eyes that had gone the color of old pewter.

The reunion was three hours behind them now. The ledger's pages had been turned once, in silence, by that ruined hand—Vane reading the names of his dead crew the way a man reads a bill he already knew was coming. Then he had closed it, handed it back, and said only: *Now Hale.*

That word had been sitting between them ever since, growing teeth.

"Now Hale," Vane said again, into the fire. "You've had your night to rest, Thackeray. So have I. Tomorrow we go back across the harbor, and tomorrow night Ambrose Hale learns what the sea does with men who spend other men's lives."

"With what?" Xanthe asked, though he knew.

Vane drew something from his belt—not a pistol but a knife, short and ugly, its handle wrapped in waxed cord worn smooth by use. He laid it on a flat stone between them as if setting a price.

"Corvo tells me you stopped me at the jail door last night," he said. "Tell me why I shouldn't be angry about that."

"Because Maren Kess is still in Hale's cells," Xanthe said, "and because killing him makes everything in this book—" he put his palm flat on the oilcloth "—worth nothing."

* * *

They argued for most of an hour, there in the cove, while the smugglers watched from the edges of the firelight like men watching two currents meet.

Xanthe laid out his case the way he had learned to lay out columns of figures—carefully, without flourish, each entry where a reader could find it. The ledger named ships. It named dates. It named the insurance houses in London and Amsterdam that had paid out on hulls Hale scuttled and divers he had simply let drown, because a drowned diver cost nothing and a paid-out loss cost the Company nothing at all. It named the clerks who had copied the false entries, including—on one page he had not been able to look at twice—a clerk's initials that were his own father's.

"It proves nothing if I'm dead," Vane said. "A hanged pirate makes poor testimony. But a dead director—that, the world understands. Men read obituaries, boy. They don't read ledgers."

"They do," Xanthe said. "In Amsterdam they do. In London, if the price of shipping shares falls far enough, they will." He heard his own voice steadier than he felt. Nine years of keeping his mouth shut had taught him exactly how much weight words could carry, and how little. He was spending all of it now. "Hale is not afraid of your knife, Captain. He has bought magistrates to stand between himself and knives. He has bought the harbor master and half the town council and probably the coroner. What he cannot buy is paper. Print travels faster than rumor and it can't be bribed. You take his blood and in ten years Greywater Bay has a new director, new books, new drowned divers, and a story about the mad pirate who shot him. You give those newspapers this ledger, and in ten years every insurance house in Europe checks Meridian's hulls twice before paying, and no Company can do again what was done here."

"And my crew?" Vane's voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about it; it dropped instead, lower and flatter, until it sounded like water finding the crack in a hull. "Brennan. Okafor. The Sorensen brothers. Eleven men on the *Pearl of Greywater*'s last manifest, and every one of them signed away to the bottom so a director could collect on their deaths. My mother is in the churchyard at Porthaven with a slate marker because there was no body. You want me to hand that grief to a newspaper office and wait for the post?"

"I want you to hand it to the whole world," Xanthe said, "which is the only witness Hale can't buy or bury."

"Cowardice," Vane said quietly. "Dressed up in principle. I expected better from the man who kept a promise nine years."

The words went in under his ribs the way a boarding axe goes through canvas, and Xanthe understood, sitting there, that Vane had aimed them precisely, because a man three years branded a pirate had learned that cruelty was cheaper than argument. He let the hurt sit in him for a moment, examined it, and found—to his own surprise—that underneath it there was something solid.

"Yes," he said. "Maybe it is fear. I've been afraid since I was eleven years old; it's the only inheritance my mother left me besides her slate. But listen to me, Captain, because I have thought about almost nothing else for nine years, in the dark, after the countinghouse lamps went out." He leaned forward. "I used to imagine Hale dead. Every way there is. Fire, rope, the sea, my own hands. And every time, the next morning, in my imagination, the Company was still there. The ledgers were still there. The next director read the same books and learned the same lesson—that drowning men is profitable and nobody comes to collect. Your knife kills a man. This—" he unwrapped one corner of the oilcloth, let the firelight fall yellow on the first page of fine, damning columns "—kills the arithmetic. Kill the man and the arithmetic lives. Kill the arithmetic and every man like him is poorer forever."

Silence, except the fire and the sea.

Then Corvo, from the shadows at the cliff's foot, spoke for the first time in an hour. "He's right, Elias."

"You would say so." Vane didn't turn. "You owe me a debt. Debts make men agreeable."

"I owe you the truth, which is heavier." Corvo came forward into the light, and the fire painted the ruin of his face in orange and black. "I sailed under Hale. I know what happens to the men who shoot directors. They hang, and the Company writes the pamphlet, and the pamphlet says pirates got what pirates deserve, and everyone sleeps well. I burned once already for this man's profit. I won't burn again for nothing."

One by one, the smugglers shifted. Xanthe watched it happen—the small movements, shoulders turning, men finding reasons to look at the ledger rather than the knife on the stone. These were men whose grandfathers had run brandy past the revenue cutters, men who remembered Vane fondly, and still: they looked at the book. Paper was something they could hide behind. A pistol was something that brought the magistrate down on all their heads.

Vane saw it too. Xanthe watched him see it—the flicker in the pewter eyes, the captain taking the count of his own crew and finding it changed. For a long moment he stood absolutely still, and Xanthe thought he could see the whole shape of the man's exile passing through him: three years of holding himself together with hatred because hatred was portable, hatred fit in a boat, hatred needed no harbor.

"You were his clerk," Vane said at last. "Nine years in that countinghouse. You know how these men think better than any of us. So answer me this, Thackeray, and answer it honestly." He crouched, eye level, close enough that Xanthe could smell tar and old smoke on him. "If the papers print your ledger—if Hale is ruined, stripped, run out of every decent port from here to Batavia—and he walks free, untried, unbloodied, onto some ship bound for nowhere—will that be enough for you? Will your mother's ghost accept the arrangement?"

Xanthe held his gaze. It took everything he had.

"No," he said. "It won't be enough. Nothing will be enough. My mother is at the bottom of the harbor and no sentence printed in Amsterdam changes that. But enough isn't the thing we're owed, Captain. Enough doesn't exist. There's only better and worse. Killing him in the dark is worse. Making the whole world read his name beside the names of the drowned is better. That's the entire choice, and pretending there's a third option with more satisfaction in it—that's how he wins."

Vane looked at him for a long time. Then he picked up the knife from the stone, weighed it in his palm, and slid it back into his belt—not sheathed, Xanthe noticed. Just returned. A debt deferred, not forgiven.

"Four days," Vane said. "Give me four days to think on it. If your newspapers are slow, or your proof is thin, or the wind changes—" he smiled, and there was nothing in it "—the wind changes, Thackeray, and men change with it. Remember that."

He walked off up the beach toward the caves, and the firelight let him go, and Xanthe realized his hands were shaking and pressed them flat against the ledger until they stopped.

* * *

The camp split along the argument's seam before morning.

It wasn't loud. Smugglers did not quarrel loudly; they drifted. By the gray hour before dawn, Xanthe counted the fires and found three where there had been one, and men sitting at each who no longer looked at the others. Old Pell, who had run the cove since before Vane was born, sat with Vane's faction and sharpened a boathook with slow, deliberate strokes. Two of the younger men—the Danner cousins, whose father had drowned off the *Mermaid's Debt* in a loss the Company had called weather—sat near Xanthe and Corvo, and the younger one, Tam, said quietly: "Our mam got six pounds compensation. Six pounds for our dad. If the papers print that, print it in London where they'll have to look at it—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

"We need to move the ledger tonight," Corvo said, low, crouching beside Xanthe at the tideline where the shingle clicked and sucked. "Split it. Half stays with the widows' women in town—they can hide pages in flour barrels, in bibles, anywhere. Half goes out to a neutral hull. If Vane changes his mind, or if the soldiers come, no single search finds the whole of it."

"And if Vane decides to take all of it by force?" Xanthe asked.

Corvo was quiet a moment. Out on the black water, the dark shape of Vane's ship rode at anchor beyond the point, showing no light.

"Then I'll be standing between you," Corvo said. "I told you I owe him a debt I can't finish. Turns out finishing it might mean refusing him. That's a bitter kind of payment, but it's the only honest coin left to either of us."

* * *

Word came up from the town an hour after sunrise, carried at a dead run by a fisherman's daughter with straw in her hair and terror all over her face.

Xanthe was awake—he had not truly slept—when she came down the wreckers' road, and he met her at the first turn above the cove. She grabbed his sleeve with a hand hard as a cleat.

"They posted it on the market cross not an hour past," she said, breathless. "Maren Kess. Condemned. Piracy, harboring, conspiracy against the Crown's trade—all of it written out proper, seals and ribbons, like a fair trial happened somewhere none of us saw." Her voice cracked. "Four days, mister. She hangs in four days. The magistrate set it fast on purpose, everyone says so—fast, before anyone can appeal, before the assizes sit."

Four days. Xanthe stood very still and let the arithmetic assemble itself, the way it always did, unbidden, in the clerk's part of his mind. Four days until the law killed the woman who had taught his mother to dive and taught him, years later, how to hold grief without letting it hold him. And beneath that figure, another: four days was exactly long enough for Hale's cutter to repair its rudder damage from the chain, take on marines from the fort, and seal the harbor mouth entirely. Four days was not a deadline. It was a door closing slowly, with a woman on the wrong side of it.

And beneath that, darker still, the third sum, the one Vane would do first: four days was long enough for a knife to do its work and call itself rescue.

"Who else knows?" he asked.

"The whole town knows. The widows are meeting at the net-loft this morning. Mrs. Danner's sent runners to every creek between here and Salt Head." The girl's eyes went to the bundle under his arm, then quickly away, as if the ledger might burn her. "They're saying—some of them are saying the only way to open the harbor is to give Hale what he wants. Turn over the pirate and his ship. Trade him for her."

Of course they were. Xanthe closed his eyes and saw Hale at his desk, pen moving in that beautiful patient hand, writing the warrant, the sentence, the trap, all in one continuous line of ink. *Let them choose,* that hand would say. *Their loyalty or their elder. Their justice or their friend. Either way, someone betrays someone, and betrayal is the cheapest rope in my yard.*

"Thank you," he said to the girl. "Run back. Tell Mrs. Danner: no one trades anyone. Tell them to hold the net-loft and keep the harbor chain jammed, whatever the soldiers offer. Can you remember it?"

She nodded and ran.

Corvo had come up the path behind her, silent as ever, and stood now looking down at the town below—the smoke, the masts, the gray line of the chain across the harbor mouth, and beyond it the open sea where a dark ship waited and a faster one prepared.

"Four days," Corvo said.

"He'll go tonight," Xanthe said. "Or I will, ahead of him. One of us crosses to the jail, and one of us carries the ledger to the coast road and the mail packet south." He looked at Corvo, and found, strangely, that the fear was still there but it had stopped being in charge. "You told me once you'd stand between me and him. I'm asking you to stand somewhere harder. Stand between him and Hale. Because if Maren hangs, there will be nothing left in Captain Vane but the knife, and I cannot watch two men I promised to save destroy each other on that quay."

"And what will you be doing while I play wall?" Corvo asked.

"What I've always done," Xanthe said. "Paperwork. The mail packet leaves Thursday. The *Amsterdammer* takes shipping news on Fridays, and a shipping correspondent named Verhoeven owes Meridian a grudge older than I am—I've read his letters in Hale's files. Four days is enough, if I write through the nights and the wind holds." He looked out at the harbor, at the chain, at the gallows timber he could not yet see but knew was being raised somewhere behind the courthouse wall. "Hale thinks he's given us a choice between his rope and Vane's knife. He's never once considered that the choice might be neither."

Below them, the tide was turning, and far out past the point, the dark ship showed a single brief light—one flash, gone—as if something out there had seen them standing on the headland and was asking a question.

Corvo followed his gaze, and his ruined face went grim.

"That," he said quietly, "is not Vane's signal. That's his ship asking *us* one."
