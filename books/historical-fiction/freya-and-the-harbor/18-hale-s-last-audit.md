## 18. Hale's Last Audit

The fire in Ambrose Hale's study was the only honest thing left in Greywater Bay, and he intended to keep it that way until dawn.

He fed it a ledger page and watched the paper curl. The Company's seal—the twin anchors, the rising meridian—blackened first, then bloomed into orange, then collapsed into ash that settled on the grate like grey snow. He had always found burning satisfying. Not for the destruction; destruction was vulgar, the resort of men without imagination. He found it satisfying because it was tidy. Every account, however complicated, could be brought to zero.

Somewhere below, in the counting house, his clerks were still at their desks—he had ordered the lamps kept lit, the shutters open, business conducted as usual. Appearances were the last collateral he possessed, and he meant to spend them slowly.

The letter from the overseas board had arrived three days ago, carried by a fast packet out of Lisbon, and it had been very brief. *Rumors have reached us regarding certain loss records.* Rumors. As if the word itself were not an accusation dressed in politeness. He had read it twice, standing at the window of his office with the harbor spread below him like a ledger of its own—masts, moorings, the great chain across the mouth of the bay that he had paid for out of his own pocket when the town council balked—and he had felt something he had not felt in thirty years of trade: the cold, precise sensation of arithmetic turning against him.

Someone had talked. Someone had copied. Somewhere in the fat body of his enterprise there was a wound, and it was leaking truth into the world.

Hale did not waste anger on the question of who. He would find the leak eventually, or he would not; either way the answer changed nothing now. What mattered was what he held in his hands as he knelt at the hearth like a man saying his prayers: the second set of records. The true ones. Fifteen years of ships bought cheap and sunk dear, losses inflated and insured twice over, premiums collected on hulls he had already sold for scrap in Amsterdam under other names. And beneath all of it, in the columns where the arithmetic required them, the names of men who had gone into the water and not come out. Divers. Crews. A woman named Maren's girl-friend—no, that wasn't right either; a woman whose boy still sat at a desk in his counting house with ink on his fingers and silence in his mouth.

Thackeray. Hale turned the name over as he might turn a coin to check for clipping. He had known about the mother for years—had approved the file himself once upon a time, one line among hundreds: *loss accepted, claim filed, no inquiry recommended*. He had never thought of her again, which was not cruelty but efficiency. No man can hold ten thousand accounts in his mind and grieve each of them. That was precisely why the Company existed: so that grief could be divided by a number large enough to make it negligible.

And yet here he was, kneeling at his own fire, feeding it the negligible.

* * *

He worked through the night with the methodical pleasure he brought to everything. First the insurance correspondence, bundled by year. Then the scuttling orders—the signed instructions to masters like Elias Vane, who had once been the best of them and had therefore been the most dangerous when his conscience grew teeth. Hale remembered the fire aboard the *Meridian Star* with something close to nostalgia. It had been elegant. A ship reported lost with all hands in foreign waters; a captain believed dead who had in fact been smuggled out half-burned by men loyal to him rather than to their pay; an insurance settlement of eleven thousand pounds collected before the smoke cleared. He had even written the condolence letter to Vane's sister himself. He had been genuinely sorry about the sister. She had been useful.

The papers went into the fire in bundles, and the bundles went in order, oldest first, because Hale distrusted improvisation the way other men distrusted debt.

His partners—the board in London, the silent shareholders in Rotterdam, old Van der Meer with his gout and his moralizing—believed they owned him. They believed the arrangement ran upward: that Hale was their instrument, their factor in a fog-bound colonial backwater, rewarded handsomely for obedience. They had never understood. He had built this. The harbor chain was his idea. The magistrate was his purchase. The scheme of insuring the same hull twice through two brokerages under two names—that had come to him one night over the accounts, fully formed, the way a diver finds a pearl by knowing where the oysters bed. Men like Van der Meer signed what he put in front of them and cashed what he sent home and called themselves the Company. But the Company, in any sense worth the name, was Ambrose Hale. Remove him and you removed nothing but the keel from a ship the rest of them had already boarded.

Which was why the letter from Lisbon, polite as it was, amounted to a verdict. Boards did not investigate their own golden geese unless the goose had begun to lay rotten eggs in public. The rumors had reached newspapers. Perhaps the man himself—Vane—had reached newspapers. And if a board smelled exposure, it did not defend its factor. It amputated. Hale had done the same often enough to recognize the blade coming toward him by its particular courtesy.

Very well. Amputation required a body to cut from, and by morning his body would be elsewhere.

* * *

He paused over one bundle longer than the rest: the divers' fund. For nine years the Company had paid a small quarterly sum—pennies, truly—to the widows of the Pearl of Greywater, distributed through the harbor mission with a card reading *In Memoriam, from grateful friends of the departed*. Hale had drafted the card himself. It had cost almost nothing and purchased an incalculable quantity of goodwill; the mission's director prayed for him by name every Sunday. There were men in this town who would take a bullet for him because of those pennies, never knowing they were fired from his own purse back into their own hearts.

He had never considered it charity. Charity implied sacrifice. It had been maintenance—the upkeep of a narrative, the way one maintained a seawall. You paid a little each season so the sea stayed where you put it.

He threw the bundle on the fire and watched the widows' gratitude go up in smoke, and felt nothing he would call regret. Regret was a tax paid by men who had made mistakes. He had made none. He had made decisions, and every decision had been correct within its terms, and the terms had been his to set. If the terms were cruel, well—so was the sea, and nobody called the sea wicked. They called it the sea. He had simply had the clarity to conduct himself according to its actual laws rather than the sentimental ones men pretended governed them. Everything floats or sinks. Everything pays or is paid for. Fuel burns; the engine runs. Vane had been fuel. The divers had been fuel. Even Thackeray, poor quiet Thackeray with his careful ledgers and his drowned mother, was fuel—and had been good fuel too, better than most, nine years of obedient service drawn from a single spark of fear.

The trouble with fuel, Hale reflected, dropping in the last bundle and wiping his hands on his handkerchief, was only that a man occasionally mistook himself for something that did not burn.

* * *

At four in the morning, with the chimney drawing hard and the grate heaped white, a knock came at the study door—two soft strikes, the signal agreed upon weeks ago, back when the signals had been precautions rather than necessities.

Brann. His man, his knife, his shadow for eleven years.

"The sloop's victualed, sir. Water, salt meat, powder. Captain says tide turns at six and he'd rather not wait for light."

"He may stop preferring things he cannot have," Hale said mildly. "We wait for light. A boat leaving Greywater Bay in darkness reads as flight. A boat leaving at dawn reads as an inspection tour." He rose, joints stiff from kneeling at the hearth, and crossed to the window. Below, the town was black except for the counting-house windows he had ordered kept lit, and beyond the quay the water lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky just beginning to bruise grey at its eastern hem. The chain hung slack between its towers, dark against the paler water. "Is the magistrate's warrant ready?"

"Signed at midnight. Hostile vessel, name of the *Corvus*, master unknown. Harbor master has orders to raise the chain on your word."

"Then we have a net in the water and bait on the hook, and all of it perfectly legal." He smiled at his own reflection in the dark glass—an old man's face now, lined and thin-lipped, the face of someone who had spent a lifetime being mistaken for respectable. "If Vane comes for his ledger, the law will meet him at the mouth of the harbor. If he does not come, the law will hang the Kess woman anyway, and the town will remember who tried to protect it from pirates while others stood wringing their hands."

"And the ledger, sir? The one they say exists?"

"Ledgers do not exist, Brann. Paper exists. Ink exists. A ledger is a story men agree to believe, and I have been telling stories to this town for thirty years." He turned from the window. "But yes, the paper should be found. Search the counting house again after we're gone. Quietly. If a copy surfaces later, better it surface incomplete."

Brann hesitated, which he almost never did. "There's talk on the docks. Widows walking in pairs past the chain towers. Fishermen mending nets that don't need mending, right where the capstan housing sits. The harbor master doesn't like the look of it."

"The harbor master is a coward who was bought cheap and knows it." Hale said it without heat. He had assessed the man correctly fifteen years ago and saw no reason to revise. "What are widows going to do against a warship's chain? Weep at it?" But he filed the observation away, because he filed everything away, because a man who stopped noticing small things deserved whatever large thing finally hit him.

Still. The docks had felt wrong for days. Quieter than fear made them. Fear chattered; this hush did not. He had heard a hush like it once before, years ago, in the hour before the Pearl of Greywater went down—when the divers' families had gathered on the beach and neither wept nor shouted, only stood in a long silent line watching the Company boats stand off from the drowning men. He had watched that line from the deck of the *Resolute* and thought: *they understand. They know who holds the rope.* It had been, in its grim way, the most respectful moment of his career.

He wondered, briefly, whether he had misread it.

He decided he had not. One did not revise thirty years of sound accounting on the evidence of a feeling before dawn.

* * *

By five the study smelled of ash and cold tea. Hale dressed with care—not his best coat, which would read as ceremony, but his second-best, brushed, with the seal ring turned inward. He took one valise. In it: letters of credit drawn on banks in Antwerp and Hamburg, under names that belonged to no one in particular; a passport in the name of Mr. Ambrose Merle, merchant, of Bristol; and a miniature portrait of himself at forty, painted in the year the Pearl sank, because he was not a sentimental man but he was a thorough one, and thoroughness sometimes wore the clothes of sentiment.

Before leaving the room he stood a moment before the empty grate. Fifteen years of paper, reduced to a grey drift he hadn't the time to scatter. Let the servants find it. Let them gossip. Gossip would be the least of what the town had soon enough.

He looked around the study once—the mahogany, the charts, the brass barometer he had tapped every morning for twenty years, the shelf of accounts that had made him, in the only currency that had ever mattered to him, indispensable—and permitted himself a moment of honest inventory, as he would for any concern about to change hands.

He had built all of it. The chain, the warehouses, the magistrate's conscience, the mission's prayers, the very rhythm of the tides of money that let this town eat. Without him, Greywater Bay was a fishing village with delusions. With him it had been an empire's smallest, neatest province. Men like Vane had come to him wanting wages and a compass course, and he had given both, and asked in return only what the sea asks: that they serve the current or be taken by it. That was not villainy. Villainy was a word small men used for large weather.

He picked up the valise.

On the desk, squared to the blotter's edge, he left three items: the day's freight manifest, initialed; a note to the head clerk instructing that wages be paid a day early—"to maintain morale during the present excitement"; and, weighted beneath the note where a searching hand would find it, a single loose ledger page. He had composed it himself overnight, in his own hand aged by a slight tremor he could counterfeit at will. A page of loss records. Plausible, incomplete, damning in exactly the wrong direction—it implicated the Rotterdam brokerage, pointed east, gave any future investigator a trail that ended in a dead man's filing cabinet in a city Hale had no intention of visiting.

Bait for whoever had leaked him. A parting gift. An audit conducted from beyond the balance sheet.

"You're smiling, sir," Brann observed from the doorway.

"I frequently am," Hale said. "It disassembles people. Come along. The tide owes me one more favor, and I intend to collect it personally."

* * *

They came down the private stair into the yard behind the warehouse, where the trap waited in a drizzle fine as sifted flour. The town was stirring now—a fish cart at the corner, a lamplighter making his rounds in reverse, dousing the flames he'd lit twelve hours earlier. Ordinary. Beautiful, in its way. Hale looked down the length of the quay at the shuttered stalls and tar-black pilings and felt the peculiar vertigo of a man seeing a completed work for the last time. Thirty years. He had arrived with a sample case and a borrowed fifty pounds, and he was leaving—provisionally, merely provisionally—with the proceeds of an era.

At the foot of the steps a figure detached itself from beside the fish cart and stood waiting in the drizzle. Small, upright, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate. Old Maren Kess, who should have been in a cell awaiting trial, and whose presence there—free, deliberate, positioned directly in his path like a channel marker—told him more in one instant than a week of dockside rumor.

She did not speak. Neither did she move. Behind her, unhurried, other figures appeared at intervals along the quay: a netmender here, a cooper there, a boy too young for any of it standing on a bollard with his arms folded. Not a crowd. A line. The same silent line he had seen on the beach the night the Pearl went down, reassembled now on dry land, arranged across his road like a row of figures in an unfinished column.

Hale calculated, because calculation was what he did instead of fearing. The trap was at the door. Brann's hand was inside his coat. Sixty yards to the sloop's boat at the far stairs. He had faced creditors, storms, and once, memorably, a mutinous mate with a boarding axe, and he had never yet lost his footing on the deck of his own affairs.

"Mistress Kess," he said pleasantly, raising his hat a fraction. "You've taken the air early. I'd have thought the magistrates had arranged quieter lodgings for you."

Maren looked at him with eyes like old sea glass, and said only, in a voice pitched not loud but carrying the way a bell carries through fog:

"The tide's turning, Mr. Hale."

Behind her, down the length of the quay, the hush broke all at once into motion—and from beyond the harbor mouth, thin and strange through the drizzle, came the low groan of the great chain beginning, against all orders, to move.
