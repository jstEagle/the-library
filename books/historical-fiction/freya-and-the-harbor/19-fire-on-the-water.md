## 19. Fire on the Water

The fog came off the sea that night like something poured, thick enough to drink, and Xanthe thought it fitting that Ambrose Hale should flee through it—dissolving into grey as he had dissolved men's lives into columns of figures for twenty years.

They rowed in silence. Corvo pulled the oars with the steady, economical stroke of a man who had done nothing else his whole life, and Xanthe sat in the stern with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, holding it the way he had once held his mother's slate as a boy: too tightly, as if grief could be squeezed into safety. The smugglers' boat made no sound beyond the soft knock of water on wood. Somewhere ahead, muffled by fog, a ship's bell rang twice—the signal Corvo had waited for, passed hand to hand down the cliff paths by fishermen's boys paid in coin and grievance.

Hale was running. They had known it before midnight, when old Pieter Voss had come stumbling up from the private quay with the news: lights aboard the *Meridian Star*, crates going out under guard, Hale himself on deck in his good coat like a man attending his own funeral an hour early. The overseas board had heard whispers. The ledger had moved faster than any knife.

"Vane's already aboard," Corvo said quietly, between strokes. "Went over the side at the anchorage an hour past. He'll have found the hold by now."

"He said he'd wait."

"Vane doesn't wait. Vane arrives." Corvo's ruined face turned toward him, half shadow, half scar. "You knew that when you promised him."

Xanthe had promised a dying-seeming man in a peacetime harbor nine years ago that he would find a ledger if anything happened. He had not promised to watch that man put a pistol ball into Ambrose Hale on the deck of a fleeing ship while the sea kept score. But promises, he was learning, did not come itemized. You agreed to the whole of a man or none of him.

The *Meridian Star* rose out of the fog all at once—a black wall streaked with lantern light, her canvas being let fall in slow, reluctant folds. She was a fast ship, built for the opium runs and the tea trades where speed meant everything, and she was making ready to take her director and his secrets out of Greywater Bay forever. Her anchor was already hove short.

"There," Corvo breathed.

A rope hung down the forechannel, looped and waiting—not waiting, Xanthe corrected himself; abandoned. Vane had left it. A message, or a courtesy, or simply the habit of a man who had climbed too many sides of ships to bother with niceties now.

Corvo shipped the oars and caught the rope, and they went up the *Star*'s flank hand over hand, the ledger swinging heavy against Xanthe's ribs, the fog swallowing them whole at the rail.

* * *

The deck was chaos of the quiet kind: a skeleton crew forward, busy with the last of the cargo, their voices pitched low as conspirators'. Nobody marked two more shadows amid so many. Corvo led them aft along the shadowed side, past the great cabin where lamplight spilled through the quarter-gallery windows, and there—through the glass—Xanthe saw him.

Ambrose Hale stood at the cabin's center table, not packing, not counting, but reading. Even now. Even fleeing. He held a single sheet in his clean white hands and studied it the way he had once studied freight manifests, and Xanthe felt something twist in his chest that was not quite hatred and not quite pity but some third thing that had no name and no mercy in it either.

Then the lamplight shifted, and behind Hale, emerging from the shadows of the doorway like something rising from deep water, stood Elias Vane.

He looked worse than the cove had let him look—gaunt as a winter mast, his coat hanging off him, three years of exile carved into every line of his face. But his eyes were the same. Cold blue, patient as tide, fixed on the back of Ambrose Hale's head with the fixed attention of a man who had rehearsed this moment every day of a long, bitter exile.

"No," Xanthe whispered.

But Corvo's hand was already on his arm, pulling him down the companionway, away from the gallery windows, into the belly of the ship—and Xanthe understood, with the sick clarity of a man arriving late, that they had not come aboard together by accident. Corvo had known what Vane intended. Perhaps Corvo had always known. His own purpose tonight was the ledger; Corvo's was to make sure no one stopped what happened next.

"I have to go up," Xanthe said. "He'll kill him, Corvo. He'll kill him in front of the whole—"

"And you'd stop him." It wasn't a question. "With what? Your clerk's hands? He's got a pistol and twenty years of reasons."

"With the truth." Xanthe pressed the oilcloth bundle hard against his chest. "This goes to the papers, not to a grave at the bottom of the sea with both of them in it. That was the whole point, Corvo. That was the only point."

Corvo looked at him for a long moment, and in the dimness of the passage, with the ship beginning to stir beneath their feet—canvas filling above them, the hull leaning into the ebb tide—something moved behind his scarred features that might have been envy, or respect, or the memory of a man he'd been before fire took his face and gave him nothing but debts instead.

"The powder room," Corvo said finally. "Stern, below the cabin. If it comes to it—if you need to stop him—that's where the light will show you what kind of ship you're standing on."

And then the world turned orange.

It began as a smell—sulfur, sharp and wrong—and then a thud from somewhere below, felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard, followed by a second, louder, and then the corridor aft of them filled with light that came from nowhere and everywhere at once. Smoke rolled up the passage like flood water finding its level. Somewhere below, a man began to scream.

"The powder room." Corvo was already moving. "God in heaven—it's the powder room."

* * *

They ran aft through smoke that tasted of tar and burnt lamp oil, and Xanthe understood even as he ran: this was no accident. Lamp oil spread deliberately, a fuse of flame laid neat as bookkeeping. Someone had fired the powder room—whether to destroy evidence, or destroy the ship, or destroy everyone aboard her, there was no way yet to know. The fire did not care about intentions. Fire never had.

The heat grew with every step, and the smoke thickened until Xanthe could see only feet ahead, and still he ran, because the ledger was against his chest and Vane was above him and somewhere in this burning iron throat of a ship, men were drowning in smoke the way divers drowned in green water while Company boats turned away.

The powder room door was already hot. Through the seams around it, orange light pulsed like breathing.

"Back!" Corvo roared at two sailors who came blundering toward them through the smoke. "She'll blow! Get topside!"

The sailors fled. Corvo kicked the door once, twice, and it burst inward on a scene from the worst kind of dream: flames climbing the bulkheads in bright sheets, powder kegs stacked and blackening, and against the far wall—slumped, one leg bent wrong beneath him, blood on his temple where something had struck him in the confusion—Elias Vane.

Not dead. Not yet. His eyes opened at the sound of his own name, and for a moment, through the smoke and the roar, captain and clerk looked at each other across nine years of silence and debt.

"Ledger," Vane rasped.

"It's safe." Xanthe was already moving into the heat, Corvo beside him, both of them dragging Vane's arms over their shoulders. The captain cried out once as his broken leg took weight, then bit the sound back behind his teeth like a man used to swallowing pain whole. "Hold on. Hold on to me."

"The fire—" Vane coughed, sagging between them. "Set... deliberate..."

"I know. Move."

They hauled him up the passage, through smoke that had begun to eat the light itself, the ship groaning around them like a living thing in torment. At the foot of the companionway Xanthe looked back once—only once—at the orange heart of the burning powder room, and saw, in the second before Corvo shoved him upward, that the fire had found the edge of a powder keg and was crawling toward it with the patience of a slow tide.

Then they were on deck, in blessed cold air, with the fog swirling around them and the crew already lowering boats in orderly panic. The quarterdeck lay empty. The great cabin's door hung open on lamplight and scattered papers, and Ambrose Hale was gone.

* * *

They found him at the rail aft, alone.

Not fleeing, Xanthe realized. Waiting. He stood with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the fire glow beneath decks like sunset under skin, and he did not turn at their approach. The fog drifted past him in slow grey rivers. Beyond the rail, the launch that should have carried him to safety bobbed empty at its painter, abandoned by the crew who had once scrambled to do his bidding.

"Director Hale." Xanthe's voice sounded strange to his own ears—young, breathless, ordinary. "The ship is burning."

"So I observe." Hale turned then, and his face was perfectly composed, perfectly pale, the face of a man doing sums. "Thackeray. Of course it's you. It was always going to be you, wasn't it. The little clerk who reads everything."

Vane stirred between Corvo and Xanthe, and his hand moved toward his belt—and stopped, because his leg had folded beneath him and Corvo was easing him down against the rail, and because Xanthe stepped forward, just slightly, placing himself in the line between the captain and the director.

"You're under arrest," Xanthe said, and heard how thin the words sounded on a burning ship in open fog. "By the new commission. There are warrants. There will be a trial."

"A trial." Hale smiled faintly, the smile he had worn at a hundred dinners. "In Greywater Bay? Where I bought the magistrate and hanged the witnesses?" He shook his head slowly, almost gently. "Mr. Thackeray. Do you know what I learned in thirty years of trade? That justice is a commodity like any other. It has a price. Some men can afford it. Most cannot. You could not. Your mother could not. Captain Vane could not." He glanced at the slumped, bleeding man against the rail. "Though he intends to pay me in another currency, I think. I've seen his eyes."

Vane said nothing. His hand had not left his belt.

"The ledger is published," Xanthe said. "Or it will be by month's end. In London, in Amsterdam. Your partners have read copies. Your board has read copies. There is no price you can pay to unwrite it."

"No." Hale considered this with what appeared to be genuine intellectual interest. "No, there isn't. Which makes this the last audit of my career, does it not?" He looked past them all, at the fire climbing toward the deck, at the fog waiting beyond the rail like an open page. "I built all of this. Every quay, every warehouse, every dividend that fed this town for twenty years. Men like him—" a nod at Vane—"were fuel. That is all history has ever asked of men like him. And when the histories are written, gentlemen, they will call me harsh, perhaps. But they will not call me wrong."

"The histories will call you a murderer," Xanthe said quietly, "because the ledger says so. In your own hand."

For the first time, something moved behind Ambrose Hale's composure—something small and cold and quick, like a fish turning in dark water. And in that moment of movement, Vane lunged.

It should have worked. Would have, against a slower man. But Xanthe was faster—he had spent nine years learning to move before thought, to catch falling ledgers, to step between things. He got his body between the pistol Vane had half-drawn and the man it was meant for, and he put both hands flat against the captain's chest and shoved—not hard, not cruelly, but with all the weight of everything he had ever refused to become.

"No."

Vane stared at him. The firelight painted half his ruined face orange. Behind Xanthe, he heard Hale move—heard the scrape of a boot, the splash and pull of a body going over the rail into the launch—and did not turn around.

"He killed your mother," Vane said. Low. Terrible. "He drowned her for an insurance settlement, boy, and you're standing in my way."

"Yes."

"She was worth more than that."

"She was worth more than this, too." Xanthe's voice cracked, and he let it. There was no one left to hide from; the careful clerk was done forever. "You kill him here, in the fog, on a burning ship, and in fifty years nobody will know the difference between you and him except the name on the monument. The ledger survives. He answers to the world, or he runs from it the rest of his life—but he answers, Elias. He answers in daylight, or none of this was worth anything. None of them. Not my mother. Not Maren's sons. Not your three years."

The ship groaned beneath them. Flame had reached the quarterdeck rail astern; the fog glowed all around them now like the inside of a lantern. From the water below came the rhythmic pull of oars, growing fainter—Hale, rowing into the grey, escaping into the one witness that would never testify.

"He's getting away," Corvo said softly. Not accusing. Simply stating, the way a man states the weather.

"Yes," Xanthe said. He did not move his hands from Vane's chest. "He is. And I am letting him. Because if I let you do what you want to do, then my mother drowned for nothing at all—she drowned just to teach her son how to burn a man alive and call it justice. I won't learn that lesson. Not from him. Not from you. Not from anyone."

For a long moment, the only sounds were the fire and the sea, and the bell of the *Meridian Star* ringing itself, unmanned, somewhere forward in the smoke.

Then Elias Vane closed his eyes. And when he opened them again, they were not defeated, exactly. They were tired—tired the way a man is tired who has carried something heavy for three years and has just been told, finally, that he may set it down whether he wishes to or not.

"The ledger," he said hoarsely.

"Will reach London inside the month," Xanthe said. "I swear it. On her slate."

Vane looked at him a long time. Then he nodded, once, the smallest motion, and let his head fall back against the rail, and Xanthe turned at last to help Corvo lift him—for below, cutting fast through the fog with lanterns swinging and the widows' fleet already answering the alarm, came the boats of Greywater Bay, come to save whom they could and witness what they must, while the *Meridian Star* burned down to the waterline behind them, and Ambrose Hale vanished into the fog like a column of figures that had finally, at the last, failed to add up.
