## 20. What the Papers Say

The papers came to Greywater Bay the way everything came—late, salt-stained, and weeks behind the world. They arrived folded into canvas mail sacks on the fortnightly packet from the north, and Xanthe watched the harbor master's boy carry them up the quay under one arm, swinging them like a dead gull, with no notion at all of what they weighed.

He bought three copies off Mr. Pell at the chandlery, paying with coins that still smelled faintly of ink and lamp oil, and he did not open them on the street. He carried them home to the room above the cooper's shed where he had lived for nine years among other men's crates, and he sat a long while at the window with them unopened on his knees, watching the fog come in off the water in long gray ribbons.

Outside, Greywater Bay was going about its morning as though nothing had changed. Gulls screamed over the fish tables. A woman argued with the eel-man about the freshness of his catch. Two boys chased a barrel hoop down the planks. And yet something had changed—the town felt it even if it could not name it. The Meridian Trading Company's flag no longer flew over the counting house. Someone had hauled it down in the night after the news arrived, and no one had put anything in its place, and somehow that bare pole, stripped and ordinary against the gray sky, said more than any flag could have.

Xanthe broke the seal on the first paper—a *Londinium Gazette*, three weeks old—and read the headline he had already read twice in proof sheets that Corvo had smuggled him, and which still did not feel real: **SCANDAL IN THE NORTH ATLANTIC TRADE — MERIDIAN CHARTER SUSPENDED BY ORDER OF THE BOARD — LEDGER NAMES DIRECTORS IN DROWNINGS OF DIVERS.**

The ledger had crossed the ocean in Corvo's care, sewn into oilcloth and packed in a barrel of pickled herring, because Corvo insisted that no customs man alive would search herring thoroughly. From Londinium it had gone to a newspaperman Vane trusted, a man with ink-stained cuffs and a memory for grudges, and from there it had spread like spilled bilge water—into the foreign papers, the shipping registries, the parlors of investors who had never seen Greywater Bay and never wanted to. The divers' names ran in columns. Maren Kess's testimony, taken down by Xanthe's own hand in the jail visitor's room, ran in italics beneath them. His mother's name was there too—*Sefa Thackeray, diver, lost 1889, wages unpaid at time of death*—printed in a typeface small enough that most readers would pass over it, but printed. Set down in the permanent record of the world. Not a rumor. Not a private grief. A fact, with numbers beside it.

He had thought, in the nights when he allowed himself to imagine this, that vindication would taste like something. Sweet, or hot, or clean. It tasted mostly like paper.

The articles told it all in the flat, tidy language papers used for disasters: how Ambrose Hale, director of the Meridian Trading Company, had for more than a decade insured vessels against loss and then arranged their losses; how divers sent down onto wrecks and pearl beds under Company contract had been left in the water when rescue cost more than insurance paid; how the sinking of the Pearl of Greywater in '89, ruled accidental by a magistrate whose signature Hale had purchased along with his dinners, was now understood as murder done slowly, by arithmetic. The Company's charter was suspended pending inquiry. Three directors had resigned within a week of publication. Two others were reported fled to the continent. Shareholders were suing shareholders. There was talk of an imperial commission, of warrants, of testimony to be taken.

And there was this, in the final paragraph of the longest article, set almost as an afterthought: *The principal figure named in the ledger, Ambrose Hale, has not been located. Reports place him variously at Port Royal, at Cape Sorrow, and aboard a private vessel believed bound for southern waters. Authorities decline to speculate.*

Vanished. Into the fog, the way he had done everything else. Xanthe read the paragraph four times, and each time he heard again the creak of that ship's rigging in the dark, and saw the burning deck fall away behind him, and felt Vane's blood soaking through his shirt as he held the man back from following—felt his own voice, hoarse with smoke, saying what he had said: *Not this way. Never this way.* He had chosen the ledger over the knife, and the ledger had done its work across an ocean, and Hale had walked out of the wreckage of his own empire untouched, unhanged, untried, probably already drinking good brandy in some warm port and telling himself, as he always had, that he had built everything worth having and that men like Vane were simply fuel.

Justice, it turned out, was not a thing you caught. It was a thing you released into the world and then watched to see where it landed—and it landed where it landed, on the guilty and the ruined alike, unevenly, imperfectly, late.

Xanthe folded the papers and set them on the sill, and went down to the quay, because there was work now—real work, paid work, work with no indenture behind it—and because standing still felt dangerous in a way he could not have explained.

* * *

The harbor commission had taken over the old counting house, which should have been impossible and yet had happened anyway, the way impossible things happen all at once once they start. The widows' fleet had done what the law would not: jammed the chain, grounded the cutter, made the town ungovernable for two days until the magistrate—who had signed his warrants and taken his dinners—resigned quietly and left on the same packet that brought the newspapers. The new commission was made of people who had never held office before: a fisherman, a rope-maker's daughter, Maren Kess, who accepted the appointment the way she accepted weather, as something to be endured and worked around.

They hired Xanthe within the hour of asking. Someone had to rebuild the books, and everyone in the room knew that the only clerk in Greywater Bay who had spent nine years reading Meridian's ledgers without flinching was sitting right there. He kept the ledgers now in honest ink, in a hand that was entirely his own, entered under his own name—which still gave him a small, strange pleasure every time he wrote it, *X. Thackeray, clerk,* belonging to himself and no one else.

Maren was at her table in the corner of the former counting house, going through salvage claims with a pencil stub and the patience of tide. She had walked out of the jail eleven days after Xanthe's arrest, freed when the warrant collapsed along with the magistrate's authority, and she had emerged thinner and harder and entirely unbowed, blinking in the light like a woman who had been asked to wait somewhere inconvenient.

"They're saying," she said now, without looking up, "that they'll hang him in effigy at the harvest fair. Hale. A straw man with a fat belly."

"Will you go?"

"I'll go to judge the craftsmanship." She looked up then, and her eyes, pale as winter water, held his. "You read the papers?"

"I read the papers."

"And?"

Xanthe turned his chair toward the window. The bare flagpole stood against the sky. "It's not enough," he said. "And I don't know what would be."

"That's because there isn't anything that would be." Maren went back to her claims. "Your mother taught me that, though she never said it. The sea takes what it takes, and afterward you don't get even. You get up in the morning. That's the whole of it." She made a mark with her pencil stub. "You got up in the morning, boy. Every morning for nine years. Don't let anyone tell you that wasn't the brave part."

He worked through the afternoon with those words sitting in his chest like a coal banked in ash, and when the light failed he banked the lamp and walked home along the quay, past the spot where—new since the fair, new since the papers—a small crowd of children had chalked names on the stones. Names of divers. Someone had started it and no one had washed it off, and the tide came twice a day and the names came back with the chalk every evening, renewed by small hands. His mother's name was there, in a child's uncertain letters, with a little boat drawn beside it.

He stood over it a long time in the dusk.

* * *

Vane found him there, or perhaps Vane had been waiting for him to be there; with Vane it was never clear which. The captain leaned against the bollard where the fishing boats tied up, and in the low light the burn scars along his jaw looked like tide lines on a rock. He was thinner than the sea had made him, and moved carefully on the leg that had been pinned under the falling spar in the fire, but he was dressed to sail—Xanthe could see it in the way he stood, the way his eyes kept going out past the mole to where the fog opened and closed over the water.

"You've read them, then," Vane said.

"I've read them."

"Hale's gone."

"Hale's gone."

Vane nodded slowly, as if confirming a sum. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the gulls filled the silence, and somewhere out in the fog a bell buoy clanked its patient, idiot rhythm.

"I spent three years wanting this," Vane said at last. "Wanting him ruined. Pictured it every night in ports where a man with my face can't show himself indoors. And now it's done and it doesn't—" He stopped. Started again. "It doesn't fit the shape of the wanting. You understand? The hole's the same size. The thing I poured into it just runs out the bottom."

"I know," Xanthe said, and meant it, and saw Vane hear it—that small flicker, the captain finally understanding that the clerk had paid for this too, in a currency Vane had never bothered to count.

"The *Kestrel* sails on Thursday's tide," Vane said. "South, first, then wherever the wind is honest. Corvo has the watch list half-drawn already. He asks after you." A pause. "I ask after you."

Xanthe said nothing.

"There's a berth," Vane went on, and his voice had the careful flatness of a man reciting terms he had rehearsed. "Second mate's pay to start, though you'd be my supercargo and my bookkeeper and probably the only literate soul aboard. New papers, new name—you'd never be Thackeray the debtor again, never be the Meridian boy, never be the man who was in that counting house when it mattered. Any horizon you want. Pick one."

The offer hung in the fog between them. It was a generous offer. It was freedom, offered by a man who understood precisely what its absence weighed, because he had lost his own name and gotten a pirate's instead.

"It's a good berth," Xanthe said quietly.

"It's the best one you'll ever be offered in this town."

"That might be true."

Vane studied him, and something in the captain's face shifted—not anger, not quite, but the recognition of a thing he had perhaps known was coming. "You're not taking it."

"I'm not taking it."

"Why?" And there was the old edge in it, three years of exile sharpened into the question: *why would anyone stay in the place that broke them.*

Xanthe looked down the quay, at the chalked names, at the bare flagpole, at the lit window of the counting house where Maren was no doubt working late over salvage claims because that was what Maren did with grief and always had. He looked at the mooring rings worn smooth by decades of lines, at the spot where his mother's boat used to sit, empty water now, holding its shape in his memory the way a footprint holds rain.

"Because leaving would be another kind of drowning," he said. "And I'm done with drowning."

Vane was quiet for a long moment. Then he did something Xanthe had never seen him do: he laughed, briefly and roughly, a sound like a hatch slamming in wind, and he pushed himself off the bollard and gripped Xanthe's shoulder hard enough to feel in the bone.

"Stubborn," Vane said. "Like your mother. She argued me out of running a cargo I shouldn't have run, the night before the Pearl went down. Only person in this harbor who ever did." His grip tightened. "Thursday's tide. If you change your mind, the berth holds. If you don't—" He glanced down the quay at the chalked names, and something moved behind his eyes that he did not translate into words. "Then build it well, Thackeray. Build it better than they did."

He turned and limped away into the fog, toward the smugglers' road and the cove where the *Kestrel* lay at anchor, and Xanthe stood alone on the quay with the offer dissolving behind him like wake.

He was not sorry. That was the strangest part—he had expected to feel the pull of the horizon like a hook, and instead he felt only the solid stone under his boots, and the cold clean air, and the faint, stubborn warmth of lamplight spilling from windows that belonged, now, to people who had never expected to own anything.

He bent down, took a nub of chalk from his pocket, and added one small thing beneath the names: the first letter of the alphabet his mother had cut into her diving slate, the one she had taught him first, worn nearly smooth by water and years.

Then he straightened, and looked out at the fog rolling in off the harbor, and did not see—could not have seen—the tall figure at the far end of the quay, standing motionless where the lamplight failed, watching the *Kestrel*'s captain disappear toward the cove with the patience of a man who had crossed an ocean and was in no hurry at all.

The figure turned, and was gone into the fog, and the bell buoy clanked on.
