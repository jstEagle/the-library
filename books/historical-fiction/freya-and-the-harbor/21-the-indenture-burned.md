## 21. The Indenture Burned

The paper had been waiting for him for nine years, and in the end it took eleven minutes to die.

Xanthe stood on the quay in the gray light of a March morning, the contract unrolled across a plank table that the harbor commission had dragged out from the customs shed for the occasion. The ink had faded to the color of old bruises. His own signature—signed at fourteen, in a hand too careful for a boy who hadn't yet learned that carefulness could be a kind of drowning—sat at the bottom like a small animal frozen mid-flight. Above it, Ambrose Hale's signature, crisp and black and confident as a knife laid on a table.

Chairman Ostrander of the new harbor commission read the terms aloud, though half the town knew them by heart. Nine years' service to the Meridian Trading Company in consideration of debts incurred by the estate of the deceased. Room, board, and wages of one shilling six per fortnight, less deductions for candles, coal, mended boots, broken ledger pages, and time lost to illness. Xanthe had once calculated, in the privacy of his own skull, that the deductions had cost him more than the original debt. He had never said so aloud. Saying things aloud had not been part of his survival.

"—and being found void under the Commission's ordinance of the fourteenth," Ostrander finished, "the contract is null, discharged, and of no further force or effect."

He said it the way men say anything when they have been appointed rather than elected—with a certain self-conscious gravity, as if the words might crack under their own weight. But the crowd did not care about ordinances. The quay was packed to the fish-market stairs: dockworkers with their caps in their hands, fishermen's wives in their good shawls, the widows—and there were more widows than anyone liked to count, standing together in a loose cluster near the crane, as if they had arranged it without arranging anything. Maren Kess stood at their center, her burned hand wrapped in clean linen, her face like something carved by weather.

"Burn it, then," someone called from the back. It sounded like Tam Weller, the chain-hand who had spent a night in the harbor water loosening links for the widow's fleet. "We didn't come to hear you read."

There was laughter at that, and even Ostrander permitted himself a thin smile before he nodded to the brazier.

Xanthe had imagined this moment for nine years. In the counting house, on the long winter evenings when the candles were counted against him, he had imagined it with the specificity of a starving man imagining bread: the paper curling, the wax seal splitting like a seedpod, the smoke rising off the quay and out over the water where nothing could ever gather it back. He had imagined feeling lighter. He had imagined feeling free.

What he felt, as he took the contract in both hands and held it to the coals, was his mother's hands closing over his own—the memory of them, cold and salt-roughed, guiding his fingers through the letters on her diving slate. *Water remembers what men forget,* she used to say, which was either wisdom or just something divers tell their children so the sea seems less like an enemy. The corner of the contract caught. Flame walked up the page with terrible patience, taking Hale's name first, then the clauses, then the deductions, then at last the boy's careful signature, and Xanthe watched it go and understood that the thing burning was only paper, and that what it had bound was still standing here, whole, holding its breath.

The crowd cheered when it caught. Maren Kess did not cheer. She caught his eye across the heads of the crowd and gave him a single nod, the way one diver nods to another before going down, and that meant more to him than all the cheering on the quay.

* * *

Vane sailed at dawn.

Or almost dawn—the fog came in thick that morning, and the sun never properly rose so much as the sky stopped insisting on darkness. Xanthe walked out to the smugglers' cove alone, because Vane had asked him to come alone, and because some partings cannot be witnessed without becoming performances.

The ship lay at anchor in the roadstead like a rumor given rigging. A launch waited at the water's edge, and Corvo stood beside it, his scarred face turned toward the gray horizon, and for once he was not watching the land behind them but the sea ahead, as if he had already left.

"He'll want to say goodbye," Corvo said. "He won't say it well."

"He never says anything well."

"No." Something moved under the scars—amusement, maybe, or grief wearing amusement's coat. "But he means everything he says badly. There's a difference."

Vane came down the shingle path with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth under his arm—though the ledger itself was already gone, three weeks gone, carried aboard a Danish freighter under a bill of lading that named it as stationery, bound for a newspaper office in Amsterdam that had printed worse truths for less proof. What Vane carried now were copies, and his own notes, and whatever other cargo a man takes when he has decided that revenge is a country he can no longer reach by the roads he knows.

They stood facing each other on the wet stones, captain and clerk, pirate and free man, and neither of them spoke for longer than was comfortable.

"You could come," Vane said finally. It was not an apology. Xanthe understood it would never be an apology; Vane had told him as much on the night after the fire, when the burns on his arms were still bandaged and Hale's ship was a smudge of smoke on the horizon. *You stopped me,* Vane had said, with a bitterness that was almost respect. *You saved me from getting what I wanted. I don't know how to thank a man for that.* "There's berths enough. New name, new flag. Amsterdam first, then anywhere."

"I know."

"That's not a yes."

"It's not a no, either."

Vane looked at him for a long moment, and Xanthe saw the years in his face—not the legend, not the drowned man come back, but Elias Vane, fifty-some years old, tired in a way the sea had not made him and could not fix. "Your mother dove the Pearl wreck twice before the Company ever leased it," he said. "Did you know that? Before you were born. She found the captain's strongbox and brought up every piece of silver in it, and gave it back to the underwriters, because that was the law and she believed in law." He shook his head slowly. "She'd have wanted the papers, not the pistol. She'd have wanted what you did."

It was the closest thing to peace the man would ever offer, and Xanthe took it as it was offered—unfinished, unburnished, real.

"The widows will meet Thursdays," Xanthe said. "At Maren's. If your copies find more families owed money, send the names. We'll find them."

"And if Hale turns up?"

Xanthe thought of the fog rolling in off the water, patient and total, swallowing whatever it pleased. "Then the sea keeps its own books," he said. "I don't have to be the one who reads them."

Vane almost smiled. Then he gripped Xanthe's shoulder—one hard squeeze, a captain's grip, testing whether the man would hold under load—and went down to the launch without looking back. Corvo followed, pausing only long enough to say, quietly, "Keep writing, clerk. Somebody has to remember it right."

The launch pulled away into the fog. The ship's anchor came up without sound, or with the sound swallowed, and by the time the gulls started their morning argument the roadstead was empty water.

* * *

He did not go home afterward. He walked the length of the quay instead, past the fish stalls opening for the day, past the customs shed with its new sign—*Greywater Harbor Commission, Est. MDCCCXCIX*, the paint still tacky—and on to the western end where the moorings gave way to rotting pilings and the tide ran brown-green over the kelp.

His mother's boat had moored here. The *Freya*, named for the figurehead his father had never gotten around to carving and his mother had done herself one winter, a small woman with closed eyes at the prow, her face worn smooth by nine years of weather before the boat was sold to pay the first installment of a debt Xanthe had inherited along with his father's funeral costs and his own hunger. He had been fourteen. He had signed what they put in front of him because the alternative had been the workhouse, and because Hale himself had sat across the table and smiled and said, *The Company looks after its people, Thackeray. You'll find us generous.*

Nine years of generous. One shilling six a fortnight. Candles deducted. Coal deducted. Grief, uncharged only because grief could not be entered in a ledger, though Xanthe sometimes suspected Hale had simply not yet found the column for it.

He stood at the empty mooring now, his indenture papers gone to ash, his freedom signed and sealed and read aloud by a chairman nobody had voted for, and he tried to feel what he was supposed to feel.

The tide came in around the pilings. Somewhere behind him the market was waking, women arguing over herring, a cart horse stamping, the whole ragged orchestra of a harbor town starting its day. Out past the point, the fog was lifting off the water in long slow rags, and beneath that water lay the Pearl of Greywater, and beneath the sand around her lay whatever the Company had paid to have forgotten, and above it all the gulls wheeled and screamed their appetite at the morning.

*I could leave,* he thought. That was the thing. That was the enormous, terrifying thing that freedom actually was, once you held it in your hands. Amsterdam first, then anywhere. Vane's berth. A new name. He had watched ships leave this harbor all his life from behind a counting-house window, and he had always assumed the ache he felt was longing. Now, standing at the mooring with nothing between him and any horizon in the world, he understood it had never been longing at all. It had been the ache of a man watching other people drown themselves politely, at a distance, in foreign water, and calling it living.

Leaving would be another kind of drowning. Slower, warmer, easier—but the same ending. A man who left Greywater Bay with this much unforgotten in him would carry the bay inside him everywhere he went, and it would fill his lungs a little more each year until there was no room left for anything else. He had seen it happen. Half the taverns on the front were full of men who had left and come back hollow, who spent their evenings telling the same three stories about Lisbon or Hull or the Baltic run, men for whom leaving had been not an escape but a sentence—exile pronounced by their own mouths, commuted only by return.

Freedom was not the open sea. Freedom was this: standing where his mother's boat used to moor, and choosing.

"Thought I'd find you here."

Maren Kess came down the quay with the unhurried gait of a woman who had spent forty years letting the sea set the tempo. She stopped beside him at the pilings and looked out at the water the way she always did—as if reading it, and finding the reading satisfactory.

"They're saying you'll go," she said. "With the captain."

"People say a lot of things."

"Mm." She worked something in the pocket of her coat—a slate, he realized. Her mother's slate, the one she had pressed into his hands in the jail yard three weeks ago, token for the widows. She had given it back to him yesterday, wrapped in flannel, without explanation. Now she took it out and held it between them. On its face, in chalk that had somehow survived thirty years of sea chests and jail cells, were letters in a diver's careful hand. *Water remembers what men forget.* Below that, smaller: a list of names. Divers. Dates. The arithmetic of the drowned, kept by a woman who had no other way to keep it.

"My mother couldn't read when she married," Maren said. "Learned it off this slate when she was forty, so she could write down who came up and who didn't. Said the Company kept records of cargo and none of people, so somebody bloody well had to." She turned the slate over in her scarred hand. "Hale's gone. The commission's sitting in his chairs. But commissions change, girl-becomes-magistrate, magistrate-becomes-director, and in twenty years there'll be a man in that office again who thinks a ledger is a place where people stop existing." She looked up at Xanthe, and her eyes were the flat gray of deep water. "Unless there's somebody here who writes it down. Here. Not in Amsterdam. Here."

The tide turned. He felt it before he saw it, the way you always did—the subtle reversal in the pull against the pilings, the kelp swinging all at once the other way, the whole harbor exhaling and beginning to breathe in again.

"I've been asked to teach the commission's clerk," Xanthe said. "The new one. A boy of sixteen, indentured—under the new ordinance, shorter terms, wages that can't be deducted. He can't read figures yet." He heard his own voice steady in a way it had never been steady in the counting house. "I thought I'd start him on letters. The ones on this slate, first."

Maren studied him for a moment. Then she put the slate back in his hands and closed his fingers over it, hers over his, cold and salt-roughed, exactly as another pair of hands had closed over his once upon a night he could not remember and had never once forgotten.

"Aye," she said. "That'll do."

* * *

That evening he walked back down to the western moorings with a lantern, though he did not need it for seeing. He needed it because his mother had always hung a lantern on the *Freya*'s stern post when she worked late at the mooring, and because a man is allowed his rituals, and because light on dark water is the oldest prayer there is.

The figurehead was long gone with the boat—sold north, he'd learned, to a coaster's master who refitted her without ceremony. But he had seen her once, two years ago, from the deck of the ferry: a worn wooden face at the prow of a stranger's vessel, eyes closed, enduring. She was somewhere out on the water still, or she was at the bottom of it, or she had been broken up for firewood in a yard in Dunmore. It did not matter as much as he had once believed it would. Things endure or they do not, and the endurance that matters is rarely made of wood.

He set the lantern on the old mooring post and stood a while, listening to the harbor.

Behind him, lamplight was coming on in the windows along the front. The commission offices still glowed—there was a year of undone work ahead of them, contracts to void, wrecks to survey, widows to pay from whatever could be clawed back from Meridian's scattered assets, and everyone involved already knew that most of the guilty would never see a courtroom, that Hale himself was a rumor by now in ports he could not name, and that the law, having arrived late, would settle for what it could reach. Tomorrow the commission would sit again, and Thursday the widows would meet at Maren's, and next week the boy-clerk would come for his first lesson, nervous, careful, hiding how much he already knew. Xanthe recognized the shape of that boy. He intended, this once, to reach him in time.

Out on the water, the lantern threw its small circle against the incoming tide, and the tide took the light and carried it, wavering, out toward the channel, toward the open sea, toward every horizon he was now free to choose among.

He chose the one that ended at the top of the quay, at the foot of the stairs, at the door of a room with a table in it where a slate and a stack of paper waited.

Water remembers what men forget. He meant to give the water some competition.
