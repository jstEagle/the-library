## 22. Freya and the Harbor

The morning came up pale and clean over Greywater Bay, the fog burned off early by a wind out of the southwest that smelled of open water and warm rope. Xanthe Thackeray stood at the door of the old net-house—he had signed for it in March with his own name, ink on paper he had read twice before signing, a habit nine years of indenture had carved into him too deep to ever lose—and looked down the length of the quay.

It was his quay now. Not legally, not in any way a magistrate's seal would recognize, but in the way that mattered: the fishermen nodded to him as they came up the ramp with their baskets, the dockhands made room for him without being asked, and the children—God help him, the children were already waiting by the memorial, sitting in a row along its low stone base like sparrows on a rail, swinging their boots and calling his name before he had taken three steps.

"Mister Thackeray! Pip says a g is just an s wearing a hat!"

"Pip," said Xanthe gravely, "will find when he is older that half of everything true is something else wearing a hat."

They laughed. There were eleven of them this morning—the harbor children, the sons and daughters of fishermen and coopers and the widows of men whose names were carved into stone now instead of crossed off ledgers. He counted them the way he still counted everything, out of habit: eleven children, one slate, one piece of chalk that Maren Kess had bought him from the stationer's in Port Ellery and which he used sparingly because chalk, like everything else, cost money, and money was a language he had learned to speak slowly.

He taught them at the memorial because there was nowhere else. The net-house was for nets again, as it should be; the countinghouse stood empty and shuttered since the charter suspension, its windows reflecting the harbor like blind eyes. But the memorial—Freya—faced the water, and the water faced back, and it seemed right to Xanthe that children should learn their letters with the sea looking over their shoulders. Their parents' world was the sea. Their letters should be learned where the sea could see them learning.

The figurehead had been raised from the mudflats two months after everything ended, by men who would not say who paid them, though everyone knew it was Vane's prize money and everyone pretended otherwise, because that was how things were done in Greywater Bay—generosity disguised as accident, kindness smuggled ashore like contraband. The carvers at Port Ellery had mounted her on a plinth of grey granite, and she stood perhaps eight feet high at the head of the quay: a woman with her arms folded across her breast, her hair swept back from her brow as if by wind, her face lifted toward the horizon with an expression that was neither sorrow nor hope but something older than either. Attention. Readiness. The look of someone who has seen weather coming all her life and intends to meet it standing.

His mother had carved her. That fact was on the brass plate now, small enough that most visitors missed it:

FREYA — carved by Sereia Thackeray, diver, 1889
For the ship Pearl of Greywater, lost with her people, 1894
"They go down but they are not gone."

Xanthe had chosen the inscription himself, over the objections of the commission, who wanted something dignified and vague. He had insisted on the dates and the words, and when they asked what the last line meant, he said only: *it means we remember.* It was not until later, chalking letters on the slate for the children, that he understood what he had done—that he had put his mother's voice onto a public stone, in letters anyone could read, after nine years of keeping that voice locked behind his teeth. Some mornings the understanding arrived all at once, like cold water, and he had to stand very still beside her figurehead and breathe.

This morning was not one of those mornings. This morning was work.

* * *

"Letters first," he said, and sat down on the plinth's edge, and eleven children arranged themselves with the practiced chaos of any school anywhere.

He had no books. Books cost more than chalk. So he wrote in the air with his finger, big strokes, and they traced it in the space before them, and then he set the slate against Freya's granite base and wrote in chalk where all could see. He had started, in autumn, with the practical alphabet—the letters that appeared on cargo manifests and fish prices and the notices nailed to the harbor office door. A clerk's alphabet. An alphabet for surviving.

But somewhere in February, without quite deciding to, he had changed.

"Now," he said this morning, wetting the chalk with his thumb. "Who remembers yesterday's word?"

"*Mother!*" they shouted, and Pip shouted *MOTHER* a half-beat behind everyone else and slightly wrong, which was somehow more endearing.

"And who can tell me why I chose that word?"

Silence, and then little Ada Corrigan, six years old, freckled, ferocious, raised her hand the way she did everything—as if defending it. "Because it's on the lady."

"It's on the lady," Xanthe agreed. "Come read it to us."

Ada marched to the figurehead, stood on her toes, and sounded out the carved letters on the brass plate with her finger: "*They... go... down... but... they... are... not... gone.*" She turned around, satisfied. "That doesn't make sense. If you go down you're drowned."

"Not always," Xanthe said.

"Mr. Kess says the drowned come up with the spring tides."

"Mr. Kess," said Xanthe, "is seventy-one years old and believes the moon owes him money." The children laughed. "But he's not wrong about tides. Things rise. That's what the sentence means. You write your mother's name, or your father's, or your own—and so long as someone living can read it, you're not gone. Writing is how the drowned breathe."

Ada considered this with the total seriousness of the young. Then: "Did you know the lady?"

"I did," Xanthe said. "She made me learn my letters too. On a slate smaller than this one. She held my hand around the chalk and told me that a man who could write could carry his own name anywhere in the world, and no one could take it from him."

"Is that true?"

"No," Xanthe said. "People took mine anyway. But I got it back. And that's also worth knowing: names can be stolen, and names can be won back, and both of those facts take longer than you'd think."

He was aware, as he said it, of Maren Kess coming up the quay behind the children, slow, leaning on the gaff he refused to surrender even though he hadn't hauled anything heavier than a beer mug in two years. Maren had become Mr. Kess to the children through sheer accumulation of years and stubbornness; to Xanthe he remained simply Maren, the man who had taught Sereia Thackeray to dive, who had watched Company boats turn away from drowning swimmers, and who had testified—dry-eyed, merciless, four hours—in front of the foreign commissioners when they finally came to Greywater Bay to take depositions. It was Maren's testimony, more than the ledger, more than anything Xanthe had copied in the burning hours of that last summer, that had suspended the charter. Ledgers proved numbers. Maren proved people.

"Am I interrupting lessons?" Maren asked.

"You're improving them. Sit."

"I'll stand. Sitting's for funerals and magistrates, and I've no use for either." He lowered himself onto the plinth anyway, groaning theatrically. "I brought you something, boy."

"You bring me something every week. Last week it was a dead eel."

"That eel was a gift of distinction. And this—" he produced, from inside his coat, wrapped in oilcloth, a small book, "—is better than an eel."

Xanthe unwrapped it carefully. It was thin, cheaply bound, printed in small type on paper that had crossed an ocean: *The Greywater Papers: Being a True Account of the Loss of the Pearl of Greywater and Other Frauds of the Meridian Trading Company, As Proven Before Commissioners.* Published in Amsterdam, translated into three languages, sold in bookshops in cities whose names Xanthe knew only as ports on charts. His own deposition was in it—pages forty-one through fifty-three. He had never been able to bring himself to read those pages. He touched the cover now and found his hands were not steady.

"The stationer had it," Maren said. "Ordered special. Cost me eight shillings, so you owe me, but seeing your face just now, I'll call it square."

"There's a printing of the ledger in the appendix," Xanthe said quietly. "The second ledger. Vane's ledger."

"Aye. With your copying noted in the introduction. Your hand's famous now, boy, in a small way, among people who care about such things. Somewhere in London there's a fat man reading your handwriting and cursing."

The children, sensing an adult matter, had begun teaching each other to whistle. Xanthe closed the book. "Hale?"

Maren's face did what it always did at that name—a stillness, a shutter drawn. "No news is no news. They say Lisbon. They say the Cape. Some say he's dead of fever in a place with no honest name." He shrugged. "A man like that doesn't drown. Men like that float."

"He'll float somewhere," Xanthe agreed. "But he'll float alone. That's not justice. I stopped expecting justice some time ago. But it's something."

"It's exile," Maren said. "Worst thing you can do to a man who thought he owned the world is let him keep breathing in a world that won't have him."

Behind them, out on the water, the fleet was coming in from the night grounds—twelve boats now, where there had been five after the Company's collapse, because freed hands and fair prices called men back to a harbor the way spring called fish. The *Pearl's Daughter*, new-built, captained by a woman named Ilsa Brand who had rowed supplies through the blockade during the chain affair, led them in with her sails set full and careless. On her prow, painted rather than carved, was a small woman with her arms folded, facing forward.

Xanthe watched her come in and felt the particular ache he had learned to live with—not grief exactly anymore, grief's quieter cousin, the one that moved in permanently and paid its rent in small moments. His mother had never seen this harbor. This harbor, this free one, this one with children on the quay and a fleet that answered to itself. She had seen only the other kind: the harbor that weighed her, priced her, spent her, and called it accounting.

"Well," said Maren, watching him watch the boats. "You could be on any of them, you know. Still. Brand would take you tomorrow. Or Vane—he was in port in April, asking after you in that way of his, like he's doing you a favor by being alive."

"What did you tell him?"

"Told him you were teaching children their letters on a dead woman's statue and seemed in no danger of leaving." Maren grinned, gap-toothed. "He said, 'Then he's a fool.' And I said, 'Aye, Captain, like most of us,' and he laughed, which for Vane is practically weeping."

* * *

Lessons ran until noon. Afterward, while the children scattered toward bread and mischief, Xanthe stayed on the quay, mending the chalk line he kept in a tin, and let himself do the thing he permitted himself once a day now: remembering without flinching.

Nine years as a clerk. Nine years of double-entry silence, of knowing the books and saying nothing, of paying his indenture down penny by penny while the numbers in the margin of his mind spelled out murder. For a long time after the trial commissioners left—after Hale vanished, after the charter fell, after Maren walked out of the jail squinting at daylight like a cat—he had hated himself for those years. Had lain awake cataloguing every page he copied and didn't copy, every irregularity he noticed and filed away under *not yet, not me.*

It was Corvo, of all people, who had taken that from him—or most of it. Corvo had come back through the bay the previous winter, his ruined face half-hidden under a seaman's collar, drinking tea in Xanthe's rooms above the sailmaker because it was the only place in town where nobody stared. And Xanthe, in a black hour past midnight, had confessed the whole weight of it: the years of watching, the years of swallowing it.

And Corvo had said—slowly, in that rusted voice of his—"Boy, I carried fire for Hale for six years before Vane. Six years I told myself I was only following orders, and orders were only weather, and weather wasn't anybody's fault. You watched. Watching isn't the sin. The sin belongs to the ones who wrote the numbers, not the ones who read them. You were a hostage, not a guard. Took me losing half a face to learn the difference. You learned it younger. Count yourself lucky and shut up about it."

It was not tenderness. Corvo did not do tenderness. But it had done what tenderness could not.

Corvo sailed with Vane still, mostly. They took contracts now—recovery work, salvage, occasionally the kind of errand that kept its details below decks. Once a season a letter came to the net-house in that unmistakable hand, sharp as rigging knives, and always the same closing: *Ledger safe. Debt stands. — E.V.* Xanthe had written back once, carefully, that the debt was discharged, that the promise was kept, that Vane owed him nothing and he owed Vane nothing, and that this mutual emptiness was, in his experience, the soundest foundation for whatever came next. Vane's reply had been a single line: *We will disagree about that until one of us drowns.* It was, from Vane, almost affection.

In April, in port, they had stood together on the quay at dawn—Vane gaunt as ever, salt-bitten, older than his years—and Vane had looked at the figurehead a long time without speaking. Then he had said, "Your mother carved better than she knew. That face isn't looking at the horizon. It's looking at whoever comes next." And he had put out his hand, and Xanthe had shaken it, and neither of them had said anything else, because between men like them everything essential had already been said in arguments and knife-light and fire on dark water, and words would only have been the hat worn by something else.

* * *

The afternoon lesson was the oldest students—Ada and Pip and five others, the ones nearly ready to write whole sentences. Xanthe saved the hardest material for them. Today it was the diving slate.

He kept it in the net-house now, in a box, wrapped in the same oilcloth Maren had once wrapped a token for the widows. He brought it out only for these lessons. He unwrapped it and set it on his knee and let them crowd close.

The slate was small, scarred, its frame of whalebone cracked and repaired with wire. On its face, in a diver's crude and beautiful capitals, cut deep enough to be read by feel in the dark of the water, were the words Sereia Thackeray had used to speak when speaking was impossible:

AIR LOW. BOAT HOLDS. GO HOME SAFE OR NOT AT ALL.

"Her last message," Xanthe said. "Not the last one she ever sent. But the one she kept. Divers wrote on slates because down there you can't talk. So if you loved somebody, you wrote it where they'd see it when it mattered." He traced the letters with his finger, and the children traced after him. "Now. Copy it. All of it. Ugly is fine. Deep is what matters—cut your letters like you mean them."

They worked in silence on scraps of board with their chalk, tongues out, and Xanthe watched them and felt the thing he had no word for yet, though he was working on it, though lately he suspected the word might simply be *happy*, plain and undramatic as a nail.

Pip finished first, as Pip always did, and held up his board. AIR LOW. BOAT HOLDS. GO HOME SAFE OR NOT AT ALL. Crooked, over-pressed, chalk dust to his elbows.

"Good," Xanthe said. "Why do you think she wrote *or not at all?*"

Pip frowned. "Because drowning?"

"Partly. But think harder. She's telling her crew: don't take risks to save me if saving me sinks us all. She's saying the boat matters more than any one person. Even her."

Ada, chewing her chalk, spoke around it: "That's stupid. People aren't cargo."

"She was a diver," Xanthe said. "Cargo was her business. She knew the difference better than the Company ever did." He looked down at the slate, at his mother's capitals, and added, more to himself than to them: "The Company thought people were numbers. Hale thought they were fuel. My mother thought they were the boat. Everything that happened here—all of it—is just the argument between those three ideas, fought out over twenty years. And the third idea won. Barely. Because enough people stayed to argue for it."

"Where is your mother now?" Ada asked, with the directness of the very young, and the other children froze the way children do when someone breaks the surface rule.

"Down," Xanthe said, "and not gone." He tapped the slate. "Which is why we learn to read. Somebody has to say her words out loud, or they're only scratches."

"I'll say them," Ada said.

"I know you will."

* * *

Evening came soft. The fleet was in, the market stalls struck, lamps going up along the quay one by one—the harbor commission had installed proper lamp-posts in the spring, and every dusk they bloomed like slow flowers, and every dusk Xanthe found reasons to still be outdoors to watch it. Light on the water where the water used to keep secrets.

He walked the length of the quay, as he did most evenings, and ended where he always ended, at the foot of Freya.

Up close, in lamplight, you could see tool marks in the wood—fine ones, patient ones, the marks of someone working with bad light and borrowed time. His mother had carved this figurehead in a shed, at night, after fourteen-hour dives, with three coins to her name and a son asleep on her coat in the corner. She had never seen it mounted. Never seen it admired. It had sailed once, been sunk deliberately once, lain in the mud for years, and risen with the tide like everything true in Greywater Bay eventually rose.

"Evening," Xanthe said to her, as he always did. "Quiet day. Eleven pupils. Pip thinks letters wear hats. Maren owes me an eel apology. Vane writes twice a year and pretends it's business. The harbor's ours. I don't know when exactly that happened—I think it happened slowly, and then all at once, the way you taught me tides turn." He paused. "I could leave, you know. Any morning. Berths everywhere, now. New names on new horizons, Vane says, like it's a gift he's offering."

The figurehead looked past him at the water, at the lights, at the boats riding easy on their moorings.

"But you knew," he said. "Didn't you. Carved it into yourself before I was born. Belonging isn't given by law—they gave me my papers and it took a year before I believed I was free. And it isn't won by vengeance—ask Vane, sailing his horizon with his grudge polished smooth as ballast, going nowhere beautiful forever. It's this. It's chalk and lamplight and eleven children who can spell their mothers' names. Built daily, by people who stay. Somebody has to stay. Somebody has to be here when the tide brings everything back up."

Far down the quay, a door opened and spilled warmth and noise—the commission had converted the old countinghouse into a hall, of all things, a place for harbor meetings and weddings and the winter dances that Maren complained about and attended religiously. Someone was laughing inside. Out on the dark water, the *Pearl's Daughter* rode her anchor, and her painted figurehead faced the same direction as the wooden one, facing the horizon together like two women keeping watch over the same house.

Xanthe stood a moment longer. Then he went home to light his own lamp, and set out his chalk for the morning, and slept without dreaming of ledgers—only, sometimes, of a small boat on bright water, and a woman singing while she worked, and a boy on her coat in the corner of a shed, learning the shape of belonging before he could possibly have known its name.
