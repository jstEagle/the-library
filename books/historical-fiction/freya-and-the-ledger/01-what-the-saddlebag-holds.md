## 1. What the Saddlebag Holds

The road into Haldenmere had drowned twice that spring, and the third flood had simply never bothered to leave. Noor Eastwick came down out of the border hills at first light and found the causeway lying under a hand's depth of brown water, the surface so still it might have been glass laid over the faces of the drowned. Mist stood on the fields in columns, like witnesses. Somewhere beyond it, a bell was ringing in a town Noor could not yet see—slow, uneven strokes, the sound of a bell with no one obliged to answer it.

Noor stopped at the edge of the water and did what had become ritual: opened the saddlebag and counted.

One oilskin packet, sealed twice, the wax stamped with the sigil of a chandler's guild that did not exist—a guild invented three years ago by a clerk in the Chancellery whose name Noor had never learned and would never be told. That packet rode against the bag's spine, wrapped in a shirt that smelled of another country's soap. One second packet, smaller, sealed in plain wax with no mark at all, which was its own kind of signature; nothing, in the Republic of Vessany, went unmarked by accident. One merchant's pass bearing the name *Maren Voss*, grain factor, of a town upriver that had burned in the first year of the war and therefore could not contradict anyone. A purse of eleven silver pieces and some copper. A knife with a cracked handle. A change of stockings.

And that was all. That was everything. Noor had learned, over four years and perhaps sixty crossings, to measure each possession against a single question: *if I have to drop it in a ditch, will I hesitate?* The stockings: no hesitation. The knife: none—the blade was more sentimental than sharp now, carried because a person named Rell had given it, and Rell was dead, and dead people did not mind being abandoned in ditches. The purse: painful but survivable. The pass: the pass was the difference between Maren Voss, respected dealer in grain, and an unnamed courier found carrying sealed dispatches through a flooded border town in wartime, which was the difference between a fine and a cellar with a drain in its floor. The packets were not possessions at all. They were the reason for the body that carried them.

Noor weighed the saddlebag in one hand, felt the small hard heart of the oilskin packet through the canvas, and stepped down into the water.

It rose only to mid-calf on the causeway, cold enough to make the bones of the ankles ache, and the walking was slow—the stones beneath were slick with something finer than mud, a silt like flour, and twice one foot skidded toward the drop into the drowned fields. To either side the water stretched away among the tops of buried fence posts, and in one place a pear tree stood to its shoulders in the flood, its fruit small and green and utterly unreachable, ripening for fish. Noor did not look at it long. There was a kind of country, Noor had found, that you learned not to look at: the drowned orchards, the burnt farms, the dogs gone feral and shy along the hedgerows. Looking cost time. Time was the one cargo that could not be replaced once dropped in a ditch.

The mist began to thin as the causeway climbed toward higher ground, and Haldenmere announced itself in pieces: first the bell again, patient as rain; then a smell of wet charcoal and river weed and, underneath both, improbably, baking bread; then roofs—red tile gone black with damp, ranked down a slope toward a river broad and sluggish as a sleeping animal; then a crane at the water's edge, tall and skeletal, its arm folded like a heron's neck; and last, resolving all at once out of the last of the mist, the gate.

The gate was new. That was the first thing wrong with Haldenmere. It was a timber frame thrown across the road where the old toll arch used to stand, and it was manned—or about to be, for two soldiers in the grey-green of the Republic's Fourth Army were dragging a table out of a hut and arguing about it, and a third was hammering a notice to a post with the unhurried blows of a man who has done it nine times this month. The notice was too far away to read, but Noor had read its brothers in five towns already that spring. *By order of the Provisional Command.* Requisition. Registration. Curfew. The grammar of collapse.

Noor stopped on the causeway where a stand of alders threw shade across the road, and became someone else.

This was the part no training ever made easy—not the fear, which was familiar and almost comfortable, but the act itself: standing in a public road assembling a stranger out of one's own body. Shoulders down. The walk shortening, a merchant's walk, a person with a bad hip and a good ledger. The face arranging itself into mild, aggrieved patience, the face of a woman who had walked a flooded road since dawn and wanted breakfast and a bed and thought about nothing in the world but the price of grain. Noor practiced it twice, silently—*Maren Voss, grain factor, papers in the left breast pocket where they can be reached without reaching*—and noticed, with the detached irritation of long habit, that the hands were shaking.

Not badly. Enough. Four years of this and the body still insisted on its opinion.

There was a trick an old courier named Sorrel had taught, before Sorrel's luck ran out outside Vessany city: press the thumbnail hard into the meat of the forefinger, behind the knuckle, where no one can see. Pain is honest, Sorrel said; it takes the shaking's place. It does not stop the fear. It gives the fear a job.

Noor pressed thumb to finger until the world narrowed to a clean bright point, picked up the saddlebag—heavier now, or the arm was heavier, or the morning was—and walked the last quarter mile of causeway toward the gate of Haldenmere.

* * *

The soldiers at the table were younger than they pretended. The one who took the pass—a corporal, cheeks still carrying the softness of boyhood, helmet a size large—looked at it right side up, which was the first mercy, and for a long moment said nothing at all. Behind him the third soldier had finished his nailing and come to stand at the table's end, older, with the flat patient eyes of a man who had been told to watch his comrade work and intended to do exactly that.

"Grain," the corporal said, reading from the pass with the careful diction of someone sounding out unfamiliar words. "Factor. You've business in Haldenmere."

"I've business everywhere the barges go," Noor said, in Maren Voss's voice, which was lower than Noor's own and had gravel in it from years of pretending indifference. "If there are barges. I saw your crane."

"There are barges." He turned the pass over. Plain side. Turned it back. Noor watched his eyes move and knew precisely what he was looking for, because he was looking for it the way Noor would have looked: the watermark, the stitch-holes of a forgery, the slight thickness of a page pasted over another page. The pass was genuine in every particular except its existence. It had been made by the same Chancellery clerk who had invented the chandler's guild, on stolen stock, with stolen stamps, and it would bear any inspection that did not include a lamp held behind it.

"Lamp," said the older soldier quietly, without looking up from the notice he was re-reading. Not an order to Noor. An order to the corporal.

The boy hesitated. And here was the moment—Noor had stood in this moment eleven times in four years, in eleven towns, and had learned that the moment was never actually about the paper. It was about whether the man holding the paper wanted, this morning, to be thorough. Thoroughness was dangerous to couriers the way deep water was dangerous to non-swimmers, and it could not be predicted, only survived.

The corporal looked back at the table. At the lamp, unlit, sitting beside a stack of blank requisition forms and a chipped mug of tea going cold.

"She's soaked to the knee," he said, "and there's nobody coming through behind her. Wave her on, we'll get the lamp working for the cart traffic."

A silence, in which the older soldier finally raised his eyes from his notice and considered Noor—not the pass, but Noor, the whole animal, the way a butcher considers a beast not yet bought. Noor returned the look with Maren Voss's flat impatience and let one hand drift to rest on the saddlebag's flap in the gesture of a woman guarding her samples, and the older soldier's gaze touched the bag, weighed it, and moved on.

"Grain prices in Haldenmere," he said. It was not quite a question.

"Foolish," Noor said. "Whatever they're paying, it's foolish. There'll be a convoy up from the south inside the month and the price will halve."

"That's what everyone says." He stepped aside—one step, no more, but the step was the width of a road. "Welcome to Haldenmere, mistress Voss. Mind the curfew bells. Eight strokes and the streets close."

Noor nodded the nod of merchants everywhere, collected the pass with fingers that did not shake because the thumbnail was still buried in the forefinger, and walked through the gate of Haldenmere without once looking back, because people with honest business never look back, and because the shoulders between the shoulder blades had begun to itch in the old way, the way they always did when a soldier's eyes stayed on them longer than the paperwork required.

The street beyond the gate bent downhill and hid the gate within ten paces. Only then did Noor permit the thumbnail to lift, and let the breath out, slowly, through the nose, in the shadow of a shuttered dye-shop, where no one saw a grain factor lean against a wall for the length of six heartbeats with her eyes closed.

* * *

Haldenmere in the early light was beautiful in the specific way that towns are beautiful just before they are taken, a beauty that seemed, afterward, in memory, to have been a kind of warning. The river ran wide and brown-gold along the bottom of the town, and the houses leaned over it in tiers, painted shutters faded to the colors of dried flowers—madder-red, weld-yellow, a blue that might once have been woad. Laundry hung between upper windows like signal flags. On the water, fishing punts drifted among the moored barges, and the great crane swung slowly with no one commanding it, loading sacks of something pale onto a barge whose skipper stood watching with his arms crossed and his mouth set in the expression of a man doing arithmetic he does not like.

Noor walked down through the town slowly, as a merchant would, and read it as Noor had been trained to read towns: the baker's chimney smoking, good sign; the smithy silent, bad sign; the taverns shuttered but not boarded, ambiguous; the granary—there, behind the customs house, squat and blind-windowed—with a new lock the size of a fist, very bad sign. Soldiers in ones and twos at the corners, more than a garrison, fewer than an occupation: the leading edge of something. A town being measured for seizure the way a tailor measures a man for a coat he will wear to a funeral.

And everywhere, ink. That was the thing the briefing had not prepared Noor for, though the briefing had tried—*Haldenmere, the ledger workshop, you know the place, everyone knows the place*—and had failed, because knowing a fact and smelling it were different things. Ink was in the air of Haldenmere, iron-and-oak-gall, rising off the river like a second weather. In the window of a stationer's shop, rows of bound volumes stood in ranks. On the customs house wall, pasted beside the army's notices, hung hand-lettered sheets in a fine round secretary hand—curfew hours, ferry tariffs, a reward poster for a deserter whose printed face had faded to a ghost. Even the graffiti, when Noor passed it—an alley wall scrawled with the word *STAY?* above a newer, angrier *GO*—had been written in good ink by someone with a trained nib.

Everyone in this town, Noor thought, wrote. Everyone in this town was therefore, in some sense, a record. And Noor had spent four years learning what armies did to records.

The workshop found Noor before Noor quite decided to find it—which was itself worth noting, worth logging in the mental ledger where couriers kept such things. It stood halfway down the hill from the market square to the water, wider than its neighbors, its ground floor all deep windows glazed with the small thick panes of an earlier century, and over the door, in letters carved deep and worn shallow by two hundred years of weather: *VENN & SONS, LEDGER-MAKERS.* Below, added later in paint rather than stone, in the round secretary hand from the customs house wall: *& DAUGHTER.*

Through the windows Noor could see lamplight, though the sun was well up now, and figures moving among racks of drying paper. The smell from the open side-door was overwhelming this close—ink and size and leather and the sweet dust of rags beaten to pulp—and beneath it all, faintly, bread.

Noor stood across the lane with the saddlebag against one hip and performed the final count of the day, the count that mattered. The oilskin packet, sealed with a chandler's imaginary sigil, addressed in a code of dates and tide-times that resolved, when read correctly, into a single instruction: *Deliver to A. Venn, workshop of, Haldenmere, by hand, before month's end.* The second packet beneath it, sealed in plain wax, whose instructions Noor had not been given permission to read and had not read, and whose weight, small as it was, had been pulling at the bottom of the bag for three hundred miles like a stone with opinions.

Eleven silver pieces. A knife. A name that belonged to a burned town.

And across the lane, coming down the steps from the upper floor with her sleeves rolled and a streak of ink drying on one forearm, hair pinned up with what appeared, even at this distance, to be an actual goose feather—a young woman who moved through the workshop's doorway as if the doorway were hers by ancient right, and who stopped on the threshold, and looked directly across the lane at Noor, and did not look away.

People did not look at couriers. That was the entire architecture of the trade; invisibility was not a talent but a contract, honored by every soldier, farmer, and innkeeper from the hills to the sea, and Noor had crossed a flooded border and survived a lamp that was never lit on the strength of it. But this woman looked—at the saddlebag first, with a professional's eye for bags, then up at Noor's face, with a different eye entirely—and something passed over her expression that Noor could not name and did not like, because it resembled recognition, and no one alive had anything to recognize.

Then she turned back inside, and the doorway stood empty, breathing out its smell of ink and warm bread.

Noor tightened the strap of the saddlebag, shifted it to the shoulder that ached least, and crossed the lane.
