## 3. Freya of the Second Floor

The storeroom below had a new sound in it, and Freya Venn heard it before she was fully awake: the particular silence of a stranger trying not to exist.

She had grown up fluent in silences. The workshop taught you that early—her father's house breathed in paper, and paper made its own weather. There was the dry whisper of vellum being squared against the blade. The soft concussion of a press coming down on a signature. And beneath all of it, the human noises that paper provoked: the held breath of a man waiting to see whether his name would be written into the good book or the bad one, the small exhale when the wax sealed warm and red over his future. For six years of war, Freya had listened to those sounds through the floorboards of her own bedroom, and she had learned to sort them the way other girls sorted buttons—by color, by weight, by what they were for.

So when she woke on the third morning after the courier arrived and heard nothing from the storeroom—not a cough, not the scrape of a boot, not even the honest restlessness of a sleeping body—she knew someone down there was awake and pretending otherwise.

She dressed in the dark, by feel, the way she did everything now. The stove in her room had gone cold at midnight because coal cost more than medicine this winter, and the window glass wore a skin of frost on the inside. Haldenmere lay quiet under it, the river town folded into itself like a hand closed around a coin. Somewhere out on the water a barge horn sounded once, low and mournful, and did not repeat.

Freya went downstairs with her shoes in one hand.

* * *

Her father was already at the binding table, which did not surprise her. Aldous Venn slept the way the town's clocks ran—badly, and only when no one needed him. He sat hunched under the great lamp, its green shade throwing his face into the yellow topography of an old map, and he was working through a stack of trade ledgers that did not need working through. He did that lately. Filled the hours with harmless arithmetic so his hands would not learn to shake.

"The courier," Freya said, barely above a whisper.

"Storeroom." Her father didn't look up. "I gave him the pallet behind the paper racks. He asked for somewhere without a window."

"Did he say why?"

"He said windows let light out." Aldous turned a page with exaggerated care. "Which is either a very clever way of saying he doesn't want to be seen, or a very strange way of saying nothing at all."

"Which do you think?"

Her father finally lifted his head. In the lamplight his eyes looked older than the rest of him, older than the workshop, older than the republic that had commissioned half its registries from his bench. "I think I stopped thinking things years ago, little bird. It got too expensive." He nodded toward the corridor. "Go see if our guest eats breakfast. If they don't, we're feeding them anyway. That's not thinking. That's just bread."

It was, Freya thought, as she took up the loaf and the knife, the closest thing to a philosophy their family had ever held.

* * *

The storeroom door was closed but not latched—a courtesy or a carelessness, she couldn't decide which, though she suspected the courier never left anything truly to chance except, perhaps, the things that revealed them. She pushed it open with her knuckles because her hands were full, and stepped into air that smelled of size and glue and old linen rag-paper, the smell she had loved since before she could name it, the smell that meant *safe* as surely as her mother's voice had once meant it.

The lamp was out. Grey light came through the high transom over the racking, enough to make shapes of things: the great rolls of paper standing like shrouded ghosts, the press with its screw arm raised, the long table where apprentices had once trimmed folios six at a time—and on the pallet behind the racks, the shape that was the courier.

Noor Eastwick was sitting up. Had been sitting up, Freya judged, for some time. The blanket was folded with a soldier's precision across the thin hips, the boots stood side by side beneath the pallet with their laces tucked in, and the satchel—that wretched, famous satchel the whole town would be talking about within the week—was strapped across the courier's chest like a child carried against the heart. Even asleep, apparently, Noor did not let go of it.

"You fold your blanket," Freya said, by way of greeting.

"I was taught it might be my last comfort." The voice was low, roughened with sleep, and careful. Everything about Noor was careful. "Your father offered me a room above. I told him the storeroom suited me."

"It suits no one. It's cold enough down here to keep meat."

"Cold is fine." A pause. "Cold keeps a person honest about where they are."

Freya set the loaf on the trimming table and began, deliberately slowly, to cut slices. She wanted to watch. Watching was a skill like any other; her father claimed she'd been born with the sharpened version of it, but she knew better. She had learned it at the kitchen table in the second year of the war, when the first neighbor came to the back door with a frightened story and a photograph that wasn't quite his photograph anymore, and her mother—who had died believing the town would hold—had shown Freya how to look at a person's hands while they lied to you, because hands were worse liars than faces. Faces practiced. Hands only ever told you how afraid someone was.

She watched Noor's hands now.

They were resting flat on the folded blanket, palms down, and they were shaking. Not violently—it was a fine tremor, the kind you'd miss if you weren't trained in fear the way Freya had been trained in it—but continuous, a vibration like a struck string that would not stop singing. The fingers were ink-stained at the edges, callused in the pattern of someone who wrote a great deal and wrote fast. Courier's hands. But there was something else in them, some history of finer work, and Freya filed that away with the hundred other facts she collected about strangers and kept in the locked drawer at the back of her mind, next to the names of every person in Haldenmere whose papers were wrong.

"Eat," she said, sliding the bread across the table along with a cup of chicory that had never in its life been near coffee. "There's butter if you can find it. I won't tell you where, because then you'd owe me a secret, and secrets are dearer than butter these days."

Something moved at the corner of Noor's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, come to pay respects. "You always talk like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like everything costs."

Freya considered the honesty of the question, which was itself a kind of currency. "In Haldenmere we measure in ink," she said. "Everything gets written down eventually. My father writes down what the republic wants remembered. I write down"—she cut a second slice, unhurried—"what the republic would prefer forgotten. Between us, the town stays balanced, like a ledger."

"And which column am I in?"

"That depends entirely on your hands."

The tremor stopped. Instantly, completely, the way a river stops when it freezes—and that, more than anything, told Freya what kind of person lay on her father's pallet. Ordinary fear trembled and kept trembling. Trained fear could put itself away like a tool.

"They've shaken since the border road," Noor said quietly, looking at those hands as though they belonged to a hired stranger. "Three days of flooded crossings and checkpoints, and they haven't stopped. I keep waiting for them to settle."

"Do you know why they're shaking?"

"I have theories."

"Theories are for people with time." Freya leaned back against the paper racks and crossed her arms, and let a long silence stretch between them—the kind of silence she used on the neighbors who came to the back door, the kind that said *I have nowhere else to be and neither do you, so we may as well be true.* "My mother used to say the body knows before the mind agrees to. You're carrying orders for the army, courier. Your hands have read them already, even if you haven't."

Noor looked up sharply, and in the grey transom-light Freya got her first real look at the face that went with the careful voice—younger than she'd guessed, worn thinner than the years accounted for, with eyes the exact brown of iron-gall ink left too long in the well. There was a moment where something vast and wordless passed across that face, the way a fish's shadow passes under a boat: felt in the hull, never seen whole.

Then it was gone, and Noor laughed—one short breath, no humor in it. "You should have been the courier."

"No thank you. I don't like roads." Freya pushed off from the racks and gathered the knife and the crumbs onto the board. At the door she paused, because there was one more thing, and the habit of protecting strangers had rules, and rule the first was *never leave them alone with their own thoughts longer than necessary.*

"There's a bathhouse two streets over that still has hot water Tuesdays," she said, not turning around. "The woman who runs it owes me. Tell her the Venn girl sent you and she'll ask no questions, which in Haldenmere is the most luxurious thing anyone can give you. And courier—" now she did turn, and met the ink-dark eyes directly, and let the courier see that she saw— "the shaking stops when the thing causing it stops. Not before. Whatever you're carrying, either deliver it or drop it in the river. Carrying is the worst of the three."

* * *

Upstairs, her father asked nothing, which was his way. Freya worked the morning at her own bench in the corner of the second floor, the one with the good north light, doing the work she did not speak of even inside her own house unless the shutters were closed.

On the bench before her lay the week's harvest: a birth certificate for the Kessler baby, who had been born in a root cellar in March and deserved better paperwork than that; a travel pass for old Marta Ilves, whose lungs were drowning and who needed to reach the spa town at Veilholm before winter closed the passes; and the hardest one, half-finished, the widow Ansel's late husband's discharge record, which required Freya to imitate a dead man's handwriting convincingly enough to satisfy a garrison clerk, and which meant she spent her mornings being haunted on purpose—copying the dead man's cramped, flour-dusted script from forty years of grocery chits until she could think in his hand.

She was good at it. That was the part nobody warned you about forgery: you got good, and goodness felt like guilt wearing competence's clothes.

Through the floorboards she could hear the workshop below going about its official morning—the apprentice Tomas arriving late again, the muffled argument about a miscounted ream of foolscap, her father's voice rising and falling in the cadence of dictation. And beneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the sound of the courier moving around the storeroom: pacing, stopping, pacing again. Three steps, turn. Three steps, turn. A creature in a cage of its own constructing.

At noon Freya took the finished Marta Ilves pass down to the back door herself—she always delivered her own work; trust no intermediary, rule the second—and found the courtyard bright and cold and smelling of the river, and found Noor Eastwick standing in it, utterly still, watching the light move on the water in the millrace channel.

The satchel was still strapped across that chest. Of course it was.

"Your bread's gone," Freya observed.

"I ate it." Noor didn't look away from the water. "All of it. I'm sorry. I'll replace—"

"Don't apologize for eating. Rule the third." Freya came to stand beside her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to remain deniable. Together they watched the millrace carry last night's frost-melt seaward, toward the lowlands, toward the war. "Do you know what I did before I looked at your hands this morning? I checked the storeroom floor."

Now Noor looked at her.

"You slept on top of that satchel and you folded your blanket over it like a mother tucking in a feverish child," Freya said. "But you also slept beside the door. Anyone coming in would wake you first. Except—" she pointed, mildly, at the transom window high in the storeroom wall, visible from the courtyard, wide enough for a slim person, reachable from the paper racks. "Except you positioned yourself so that if anyone came through the window, you'd hear them cross the boards. Which means you weren't worried about the army kicking the door. You were worried about someone coming quietly. Someone who knew you were here."

The silence that followed lasted four breaths. Freya counted them, the way she counted everything now—days until the passes closed, pages left to forge, coins in the tin.

"Your town is observant," Noor said at last.

"My town is *surviving*," Freya corrected. "Observance is just survival with its eyes open." She held out the wrapped pass. "Deliver this to Marta Ilves at the tannery lane, if you want to earn your bread honestly. Knock twice, wait, knock once. Tell her nothing. She'll know."

And Noor took it—their fingers brushing for the length of a heartbeat, and Freya noting, filing, locking away: the courier's fingers were cold as the storeroom and steady as a stone, and both of those things were lies the body was telling—and for one strange suspended moment, in the cold bright courtyard, with the millrace running and the town holding its breath around them, Freya Venn felt the peculiar vertigo of recognizing something in a stranger that she recognized in herself: the loneliness of a person made entirely of what they carried.

Then the bell in the temple tower began to ring the hour, and beneath it—out of rhythm, out of season—came another sound, hooves on the quay stones, many of them, and the high clear voice of an officer calling for the town's magistrate by name.

Freya stood very still in the courtyard and listened to the republic arrive at her father's door.

"Well," she said softly. "That was faster than usual."
