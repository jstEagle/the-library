## 4. Requisition Orders

The morning the army came to Haldenmere, Noor learned that dread has a sound, and it is not drums.

It is paper.

They heard it from the storeroom where they had slept badly on sacks of sizing and curled vellum—the rattle of a bridle, then voices in the yard below, then a crisp unfolding, like a heron taking wing, and Aldous Venn's voice saying, with the careful flatness of a man reading his own sentence, "Yes. I see the seal. Come inside."

Noor dressed in the dark, fingers finding buttons by memory. The habit of three years held them: boots laced loose enough to kick off and run in, satchel packed with everything essential and nothing identifiable, the false merchant's pass tucked into the lining where a knife would sit if Noor had carried knives. They had woken this way in nine towns. They knew how to be ready to leave before understanding why.

But this time there was Freya, two floors up, and the ledger below, and the strange new weight of having somewhere worth leaving slowly.

Through the gap between shutter slats, Noor watched the soldiers come into the yard of the Venn workshop. Not many—eight, perhaps nine, in the gray-green of the Fourth Requisition Column, their uniforms faded to the color of river fog. They did not smash anything. That was the first terrible thing. They were orderly. They stood in the drizzle with their hands folded over their belts while an officer stepped forward out of their ranks, shaking rain from the visor of his cap, and Noor's stomach dropped through the floorboards, because Noor knew him.

Captain Ilsen. The same captain whose aide had accepted the first dispatch at the checkpoint outside Corvess. The same seal. The same wax, the deep republican crimson that looked, in certain lights, like something drying.

*The dispatches,* Noor thought, standing very still among the smells of glue and cold ash from last night's candles. *The dispatches I carried.*

There had been two. The first had gone to Corvess, signed for, sealed again, gone. The second Noor still carried—orders within orders, the republic's habit of sending its truths in installments so that no single courier ever knew enough to be dangerous. Noor had been told only: deliver to the Venn workshop, Haldenmere, into the keeper's own hand, timing critical. And beneath that instruction, unstamped but real, the kind of message that lives in the space between words: *this one matters.*

Now a requisition column stood in the yard of the workshop it was addressed to, and Captain Ilsen was wiping his boots at the door like a guest.

* * *

Noor could have stayed in the storeroom. It would have been wise. Three years of wisdom said: be furniture, be dust, be no one until the road clears. But the door to the workshop proper stood ajar, and through it came Aldous Venn's voice rising—not loud, but strained taut as wire—and Noor found they had crossed the room without deciding to.

The workshop in daylight was a different country than the workshop by candlelight. Rain-light fell through the high windows in long gray panes and lay across the great binding tables where forty apprentices' benches stood empty, the town summoned to hear whatever was about to be read to them. The presses waited under their leather covers like sleeping animals. And there, open on the master's table, guarded by nothing but habit, lay the registry itself.

Noor had seen it only once, briefly, two nights ago—but even briefly had been enough to rearrange something in the chest. Pages upon pages of names in Aldous Venn's small exact hand, thousands of them, each entered with a date and a district and a promise-code in the margin. Noor had lingered too long over it. Someone had noticed. Noor had told themselves it was only the beauty of it—the discipline, the faith of a thing so carefully kept—and had not let themselves think what it might mean that the army had come for it.

Aldous Venn stood at the head of his tables now, a narrow man made narrower by age, his ink-stained fingers pressed flat to the wood as though he could hold the whole building down by touch. Beside him, arms crossed, chin lifted in that way Noor was beginning to know—defiance worn so long it had become posture rather than decision—stood Freya.

She did not look at Noor when Noor slipped along the wall to stand near the doorway. But something in her shoulders eased a half-inch, and Noor felt that easing land in them like a stone in still water. Two days in this house and already her steadiness had become a thing Noor oriented by, the way sailors orient by a light they are not supposed to need.

Captain Ilsen stood at the center of the floor with a document case open on the table before him, and he read aloud.

"By authority of the Provisional Council of the Republic of Vessany, sitting extraordinary at Corvess, and pursuant to Article Eleven of the Internal Security Act:" — here his voice took on the cadence of a man who had read this particular liturgy many times and had long since stopped hearing it — "the premises known as the Venn Ledger Workshop, together with all contents, stock, instruments, and records; the grain reserves held in common at the Haldenmere granary; and all vessels moored at the town quay exceeding six meters in length, are hereby declared property of the state, requisitioned for the duration of the emergency."

He paused. Turned a page. The paper sounded like ice settling.

"The registry maintained on these premises is designated a document of strategic record and shall pass immediately into military custody. Its removal, concealment, mutilation, or copying—" and here, Noor would swear later, the captain's eyes lifted for exactly one breath toward the shadowed doorway where Noor stood— "constitutes sabotage against the republic, punishable under statute by imprisonment or such lesser penalty as the commanding officer shall determine."

*A lesser penalty,* Noor thought. *Determined by him.*

Aldous's voice came thin and level. "The registry is a protection record, Captain. Those names were entered under evacuation guarantee. Under oath. Families surrendered their places in the resettlement queues on the strength of those pages. If you take it—"

"I take nothing, Master Venn." Ilsen closed the document case with a soft click that Noor felt in their teeth. "The state takes. I merely carry."

"You can tell the state—" Aldous began, and then stopped himself, because Freya's hand had moved, barely, toward her father's sleeve, and old men who have survived wars learn to read their daughters' hands.

Ilsen watched the small drama of it with an expression Noor could not name—something patient, something almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty because it left nowhere to put your anger.

"You may file objections," the captain said. "At Corvess. Through the office of the Provisional Council. I am instructed to tell you that petitions will be heard in the order received." He paused, and then added, in a different voice entirely, quieter, almost private: "You will be approximately eleven thousandth in that order."

Nobody in the room moved. The rain ticked against the windows. Somewhere in the yard a horse shifted its weight and the gravel answered.

"You will want inventory taken," Aldous said at last, and his voice had gone somewhere past anger into the flat country beyond it. "You'll want my marks and seals catalogued. You'll want me to sign a transfer of custody."

"Yes."

"Then you will need me for some days yet."

"Yes," Ilsen said again, and Noor heard what lived under the word, the way a courier learns to hear what lives under official language: *yes, and then we will see whether we still need you.*

* * *

Afterward—the soldiers posted at the doors, the granary seized, the quay already crawling with men measuring barges with knotted cords—Noor stood at the second-floor window of the storeroom and watched the town absorb the news the way ground absorbs rain, unevenly, some of it running off in angry rivulets, some of it sinking in slow and silent.

Below, in the yard, Captain Ilsen conferred with his sergeant. He held the requisition papers in one hand, and with the other he touched his breast pocket—once, briefly, unconsciously, the gesture of a man confirming the presence of something else he carried. A second document. Orders within orders.

Noor knew the shape of that pocket. Noor had one just like it against their own ribs.

They drew out the second dispatch then, alone, in the gray light, and turned it over without opening it. Republican crimson wax, unbroken. The courier's oath was simple and absolute: deliver, do not read, do not understand. For three years the oath had been easy, because Noor had understood nothing. Ignorance had been a kind of armor; they had crossed flooded roads and checkpoints and burned districts carrying other people's secrets and had slept well enough, telling themselves the same story every night: *I am the road, not the destination. What happens at the end of me is not mine.*

But now the destination had a face. It had flour on its hands and bread left silently outside a door. It had a father with ink-stained fingers pressed flat against a table he was watching get measured for confiscation. And the timing—*timing critical*—no longer meant anything innocent. Dispatches that arrive with requisition columns are not messages of rescue.

Noor thought of the margin codes in the registry. Thought of Aldous saying, *families surrendered their places on the strength of those pages.* Thought of the word *strategic*, which in the republic's vocabulary had never once meant *precious* and always meant *useful*, and of the word *custody*, which always, eventually, meant fire or a locked drawer, and rarely the drawer.

Downstairs, faintly, came the sound of Freya arguing with a soldier at the workshop door—not loudly; she would never give him loudly. Just her voice, level as a spirit gauge, insisting on some small refusal, and the soldier's confusion, and Noor pressed the dispatch back against their chest beneath their shirt where it sat like a swallowed coal.

Three years of carrying. And for the first time, Noor wanted to open something they had sworn not to open.

That evening, as the garrison's lanterns came up one by one along the quay, Freya found Noor at the top of the stairs. She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. She studied Noor's face for a long moment with those clear, appraising eyes that missed less than was comfortable.

"They're posting a schedule," she said. "For the inventory. My father is to present the registry seals tomorrow morning." Her jaw tightened. "Eleven thousandth in line, he says. Did you hear him say that? He counted us, Courier. He knows exactly what we're worth."

Noor's mouth was dry. "Freya—"

"The dispatches you brought." She kept her voice low, and her eyes did not accuse, which somehow made it worse; she asked the way a surgeon asks, to know the true shape of the wound. "The ones still sealed. Do they have anything to do with this?"

And Noor, who had lied to captains and clerks and border guards with a steady voice and a merchant's smile, stood on the workshop stairs with the coal burning through their shirt and heard themselves say:

"I don't know."

It was not the truth. It was also, Noor realized with a cold turning in the gut, no longer entirely a lie—because the not-knowing had become unbearable, and unbearable things do not survive the week.

Freya held their gaze a moment longer, then nodded slowly and went down into the lamplight, and Noor stayed on the dark stair, listening to the rain, feeling the seal against their heart like a wound that had not yet decided whether to bleed.
