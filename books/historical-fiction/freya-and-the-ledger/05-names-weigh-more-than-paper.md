## 5. Names Weigh More Than Paper

Freya had learned to read the workshop by sound before she learned to read it by light. Her father's rule was simple and old as the building itself: the ledgers slept when the lamps went out. By day the second floor hummed with apprentices' pens, the rasp of burnishers on calf vellum, the creak of the great binding press that groaned like a ship in weather. But at night the workshop kept a different life—a settling of timber, the river breathing against the pilings below, the paper itself shifting in its shelves with changes in damp, a dry whisper like someone turning over in sleep.

She was awake past all of it, mending a folio by candle stub, when she heard the courier's door open.

Not the storeroom door—the outer one, the one that should have been barred. She set down her bone folder and listened to footsteps cross the workshop below, soft, deliberate, stopping where they should not stop. Where the registry lay open on its reading stand.

Freya did not go down at once. That was the first lesson of the war, learned early in Haldenmere: watch before you are seen. She pinched out her candle and stood at the top of the stairs with the darkness pooling around her hem, and she looked down through the balusters at the shape of Noor Eastwick standing before the great book with a lamp turned low, hands hovering above the pages without touching them, the way people hovered before things they did not believe were real.

The courier had been in the town four days. Long enough for the bread in the storeroom to go stale, long enough for Freya to learn the particular quality of Noor's silence—not emptiness but compression, a silence held under pressure, like water behind a lock gate. Long enough to notice how Noor counted exits without seeming to: the window over the cutting table, the stair to the loading dock, the alley door with its hinge that needed oiling. Long enough to notice the way Noor ate, quickly, apologetically, as though meals were interruptions to be apologized for.

And long enough to notice that every night, the courier checked the seal on the second dispatch case strapped beneath the coat, the one with the wax so dark it looked like dried blood.

Now Noor stood before the registry and did not touch it, and something in the stillness of those hovering hands undid Freya's caution. It was not the posture of a spy casing a room. It was the posture of a person at a graveside, or a font.

She went down the stairs without hurrying. The fourth step complained; everyone in Haldenmere knew the fourth step of the Venn workshop complained; there was no point pretending otherwise.

"You're letting the draft at it," Freya said.

Noor started—actually started, shoulders jumping, and for one unguarded moment Freya saw the whole architecture of the courier's composure show its scaffolding, the way a house under construction shows its bones. Then it was rebuilt between one breath and the next, and Noor Eastwick turned with an expression of mild, merchantly curiosity that would have convinced anyone who hadn't watched it assemble itself.

"The lamp," Freya said, nodding at it. "You've got it too close. The heat curls the leaves. My father can tell which pages have been read near a bad flame—he says they hold their warmth like guilt."

"That's superstition."

"It's vellum." Freya came forward into the lamplight, barefoot, sleeves rolled from the mending. "It remembers everything done to it. That's rather the entire profession."

Noor looked at her then—really looked, the way no one in Haldenmere had looked at Freya in years, because everyone in Haldenmere had already sorted her long ago into the daughter of Aldous Venn, keeper of the town's accounts, a quiet girl with ink-stained knuckles who could make your dead husband's trade papers say he'd died a free man instead of a debtor. Noor's look was newer than that. Hungrier and more careful at once, the look of someone reading a page written in a hand they didn't recognize.

"I only wanted to see it again," Noor said. "The book. I couldn't sleep."

"Again?"

"This afternoon. When I delivered the dispatches. I lingered."

"I know," Freya said. "I was watching you do it."

Something moved across Noor's face—was it alarm? shame?—and settled into a wry, tired almost-smile. "Of course you were."

* * *

Freya banked the lamp properly, trimmed the wick, and moved it back from the stand, and then, because she had already decided something in the dark of the staircase and had not yet admitted it to herself, she did not send the courier back to bed.

Instead she asked, "What did you think it was?"

"A tax ledger." Noor said it easily, but the ease had a seam in it. "That's what they say in the capital. The famous ledger of Haldenmere. The republic's great account of grain and tolls and tithes. They teach it in the schools. *The Ledger That Fed a Nation.*"

"And what do you think now?"

Noor was quiet a moment. The river filled the quiet, slapping softly at the pilings, and somewhere down the quay a dog gave one desultory bark and thought better of it.

"I think," Noor said slowly, "that a tax ledger doesn't need this many names. I counted. Well—I tried. There must be ten thousand entries, and not one column of sums. Names, parishes, dates. A tax ledger wants numbers. This wants—" The courier stopped, and Freya watched the understanding arrive like cold water reaching a warm place. "People. It wants people."

"My father calls them the promised," Freya said.

She heard herself say it and felt the old vertigo, the one that came whenever she stood too close to the truth of the thing. Four years of war and Haldenmere had kept its secret the way the river kept its drowned: quietly, completely, out of sight until you went looking. The world believed this workshop produced the republic's accounting. Only the town—and now, apparently, one sleepless courier—knew what else it held.

"Promised," Noor repeated.

Freya lifted the great book's edge, careful of the spine, and turned back to the first quire. The paper made its dry whisper. "When the border provinces seceded, the republic couldn't hold the territory, so it did what republics do. It made promises. Every family displaced by the fighting, every dissident marked for arrest by the new governments, every debtor-soldier, every orphan of the burned villages—the republic swore to evacuate them. To bring them across the lines before the enemy armies arrived. It wrote the pledge into law. And then it discovered that laws are cheap and barges are expensive, and the war went longer than anyone planned, and the promises became—" She paused. "Paper."

"So the names here—"

"Are the people the republic swore to take out. Ten thousand, four hundred and eleven. Each name entered the day the oath was sworn, witnessed by two officials, sealed with the republic's mark." Freya touched the margin of the page, where a small stamped emblem sat faded to the color of weak tea—a bridge over water, the old seal of the evacuation commission. "This isn't a record of what the republic owns, courier. It's a record of what the republic owes."

The lamp flame leaned and righted itself. In its light Noor's face had gone very still, and Freya, who had spent the war learning to read faces because reading documents was only half the trade, saw that the stillness was not calm. It was the stillness of a person holding a door shut against something.

"They don't know," Noor said at last. "In the capital. They can't know what this is, or they'd never—"

"Or they know exactly what it is," Freya said, "and that's why your captain is coming."

The word was out before she could weigh it. *Captain.* She had heard it from Tomas three days ago, dropped carelessly among the ink pots—that an army column under a Captain Ilsen had taken the crossing at Marrow Ford and would reach Haldenmere within the fortnight, requisitioning as they came. Requisitioning grain, boats, livestock. And workshops. Especially workshops.

Noor's head came up sharply. "Who told you about a captain?"

"Tomas. The apprentice. He hears things at the ferry office." Freya studied the courier's face and found what she feared finding: not surprise. Confirmation. "You knew."

"I carry dispatches," Noor said. "I don't read them."

"That's a strange sentence," Freya said, "for someone whose hands shake when they say it."

* * *

They ended up at the binding table because neither of them wanted to stand anymore, and because the table sat far from the windows and its underside was shadowed, an old habit of the household. Freya brought up the last of the plum brandy from her father's shelf—two fingers in two mismatched cups—and Noor wrapped both hands around the cup as if it were January instead of late summer.

"Tell me about the shaking," Freya said.

"Is it always this way with you? Interrogation by lamplight?"

"In this workshop we keep minutes," Freya said. "Everything entered, dated, witnessed." She meant it as a joke. It landed as something else, and she saw it land, and neither of them laughed.

Noor drank. For a long moment the courier looked at the cup, turning it, watching the brandy move.

"When I was eleven," Noor said, "my family was on a list like yours. Not this ledger—a different one, a provincial roll, the kind they kept before the commission standardized everything. Our village was inside the secession line. The republic swore it would come for us before winter." A small, terrible pause. "It came in spring. After the enemy did."

Freya did not say anything. She had learned, in the forging room, that the most important documents required silence around them.

"There were nine hundred names on our roll," Noor went on, voice flat and careful, a courier's voice, carrying cargo. "Sixty-one were evacuated. My mother kept our entry memorized—number four hundred and six, the date, the witnesses' names—as if reciting it might make it true. She died still believing the republic had simply misplaced us. A clerical error, she called it. She forgave it. Can you understand that? She forgave the republic its error the way you'd forgive a cousin." Noor looked up. "I became a courier because someone told me once that if you carry the paper, you're closer to the truth of things. Closer to where the errors happen."

"And are you?"

"No," Noor said. "You're just further from being allowed to grieve them."

The candle fluttered. Below them the river went on with its patient work against the pilings, and somewhere across the sleeping town a night-watchman's bell counted the hour—two, thin and silver—and Freya felt the strangest sensation, of a door she had kept barred for four years opening onto another person standing outside it, holding exactly the same key.

She reached across the table and turned the registry toward the light, back to the beginning, and began to turn pages. "Look at the format. Name, parish of origin, date of oath, class of protection. Class one means immediate evacuation—children, the ill. Class three means 'when transport permits.'" Her finger moved down a column. "My father fought for months to get a class added. Look at the bottom of the page. Class five."

Noor leaned in. The entries at the bottom were in a different hand—her father's hand, unmistakable to Freya even across a decade, younger then, angrier—and beside each name, where the other classes had their neat bureaucratic annotations, these bore a single word in small capitals: **SHELTERED**.

"People the republic couldn't evacuate," Freya said, "but refused to surrender. It agreed to hide them instead—to enter them here so that any future commission would find them, would owe them. My father invented that class himself. He argued before a committee in the capital for eleven days. He used to say it was the only good thing he ever made with his life, and it wasn't a book or a binding. It was a category."

"SHELTERED," Noor read aloud, barely a breath.

"There are nine hundred and forty of them. People living under other names in cellars and attics from here to the coast, alive only because a clerk once refused to let them be forgotten." Freya closed the book gently, both hands flat on the cover, feeling its weight—thirty pounds of vellum and oath. "So now you know what the ledger is. And now you understand why, when your captain arrives, this is the first door he'll nail shut."

Noor was silent. And Freya watched—and would remember watching, afterward, in all the years that came—the exact moment when the courier decided not to speak. It happened in the face, a flicker like a shutter closing against wind: the mouth opening slightly, the eyes going to the second dispatch case's outline beneath the coat, then away, then back to Freya, and then the whole countenance smoothing over into the merchant's mild mask, competent and empty, the face of a person carrying nothing heavier than brandy.

"There will be copies," Noor said instead. "Surely your father has made safeguards. Duplicates."

"Duplicates of a promise aren't the promise," Freya said. "A copy of an oath, filed in a cellar, obliges no one. The ledger's power is that it's *here*, official, entered, sealed—that the republic's own mark sits on every page. Burn this and the debt burns with it. Paper the copies however you like; no one hunts down a debt they've been told is cancelled."

"You sound like someone who has thought about this a great deal."

"I'm my father's daughter. Arithmetic is in the blood." Freya stood, gathered the cups, and made her voice lighter, because the heaviness in the room had become a weight bearing down on the joists, and because some instinct older than sense told her to give the courier an exit from the moment. "Finish your drink. The storeroom's drafty and I won't have it said that Haldenmere killed the republic's errand girl in her sleep."

"Errand *person*," Noor said, and there it was again, the almost-smile, quick and crooked and gone. Then, as Freya turned toward the stairs: "Freya."

She stopped. It was the first time the courier had used her name.

"Why did you show me?" Noor asked. "You've kept this secret through four years of war. Your whole town has. And tonight you open the book to a stranger who arrived four days ago with sealed cases and a false pass sewn into a merchant's coat"—a beat, the faintest acknowledgment that each of them had catalogued the other's concealments—"and you show me everything. Why?"

Freya stood with the cups in her hands and considered lying, and found she had somehow lost the habit in this room, tonight, at this table.

"Because you looked at it like a person at a graveside," she said. "And I thought—someone who mourns names that were never theirs might help me save the ones that are."

She climbed the stairs with the fourth step complaining beneath her, and did not look back, because she knew that if she looked back she would ask the question pressing at her teeth—the question about the second seal, the dark-wax case, the thing Noor carried that made the courier's hands shake and the shutter come down—and she was not yet ready, she told herself, to know whether trust was a thing one extended or a thing one risked.

Behind her she heard Noor stay a long while at the binding table. Much later, well past three, the lamp finally went out. But the courier did not return to the storeroom for a long time after that, and in the morning, when Freya came down to mix ink, she found the registry closed and re-shelved correctly, its clasps turned, and beside it on the binding table a single sheet of scrap paper laid square and precise.

On it, in a small, disciplined hand she did not recognize, was written a number:

**10,411 — 1 = ?**

And beneath the arithmetic, unsigned, as though the courier could not decide whether it was a promise or a confession:

*I counted too.*

Freya stood holding the note as the first grey light came up over the river, and downstairs, faint and far off, she could hear the ferry bell ringing wrong—ringing fast, ringing alarmed—the sound Haldenmere had been dreading for four years, the sound that meant soldiers on the water.

The captain had come early.
