## 6. The Captain's Courtesy

The invitation came the way danger usually came to Noor Eastwick: folded in half, sealed with wax, and carried by someone too young to know what he was delivering.

A garrison boy of perhaps fifteen stood in the doorway of Venn's workshop that fourth evening, rain still beading on his shoulders, holding out a card as though it might bite him. It was good cardstock, cream-colored, the kind used for weddings and funerals in wealthier towns. The seal was the republic's double star pressed in gray.

*Captain D. Ilsen requests the company of the courier at supper. Seventh hour. Quarters at the old customs house.*

Noor read it twice by the light of the workshop's front lamp, aware of Tomas watching from behind the binding press, and of Freya somewhere above on the second floor, her footsteps moving across the boards overhead—there and then gone, like everything else Noor had learned to track without looking at it directly.

"Does he invite every merchant to dinner?" Noor asked the boy.

The boy shrugged with the whole economy of his shoulders. "He invites who he wants."

Which was its own kind of answer.

Noor turned the card over. On the back, in pencil, small and neat: *Come alone. Come fed-up. I pay better than your employers.*

The handwriting was unhurried. That was the thing Noor noticed most, standing there with rain hammering the shutters and Aldous Venn's registry lying closed under lock in the back room like a sleeping animal. Unhurried hands belonged to men who already knew how the evening would go. Either Ilsen was very confident or very bored, and Noor had seen enough officers in enough collapsing towns to know that boredom was the more dangerous condition. Confident men could be flattered. Bored ones needed theater.

"Tell him yes," Noor said.

* * *

Noor spent an hour before the seventh hour preparing, and most of that preparation consisted of deciding which self to wear.

The courier's trade was a wardrobe of selves kept folded and ready: the dull merchant with grievances about tariffs; the nervous functionary eager to please; the blank-eyed drifter who remembered nothing because nothing had been worth remembering. Each one was stitched together from small true details—a limp borrowed from a man met on a bridge outside Sorrel Ferry, a stutter acquired from a clerk whose name Noor had never known—and each one could be abandoned in a ditch like any other possession too heavy to carry.

Tonight required the merchant. Merchants were greedy, and greed was a language Ilsen would respect, because it was his own.

Noor dressed accordingly: the good coat with the worn cuffs hidden under turned sleeves, boots brushed but not too well, the false pass of Marten Hale, grain factor, copied into memory so thoroughly that even the ink smudge at the bottom felt lived-in. In the storeroom mirror—a tarnished rectangle propped against sacks of sizing glue—Noor practiced the face. A little hungry. A little aggrieved. A man who had come to Haldenmere for profit and found only requisition papers.

From the second floor came the sound of Freya's pen moving through paper. She had not spoken to Noor all day beyond the necessary words of the workshop's business, but she had left bread again that morning, cut thick, with honey this time, set on the binding table where Noor would be sure to find it. Neither of them had ever mentioned the bread. It existed between them the way certain entries existed in ledgers: recorded, unspoken, accruing interest.

Noor picked up a crumb of it now, absently, and realized only after swallowing that it was the first food eaten all day. That was what the girl upstairs did to the careful architecture of hunger and habit. She made a person forget their own systems.

*Don't,* Noor told the reflection in the mirror. *Not tonight. Tonight you are nobody's.*

The rain had thinned to a fine mist by the time Noor crossed the market square toward the customs house. The town at dusk was beautiful in the way of things about to be taken—the river running copper between the quays, lamps coming on one by one along the Shambles, the smell of woodsmoke and wet stone and, faintly, from the bakery on Cooper Lane, sugar burning sweet. Somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse. Somewhere else a woman laughed, high and brief. All the ordinary music of a place that did not yet know its own future.

Two soldiers stood at the customs house door. One took Noor's measure with a single bored glance; the other held the door open with a flourish that managed to be both courteous and mocking.

Inside, the old customs house smelled of cold pipe smoke and lemon polish. The requisitioned furniture had been arranged into something like a dining room: a long table meant for weighing manifests now laid with a white cloth, silver candlesticks that belonged to some family who would never see them again, and a meal that was obscene in its abundance—roast fowl, brown bread, butter molded into a curl, wine dark as river water at night.

Captain Ilsen rose from the head of the table.

Noor had seen him twice before, always at a distance, always in uniform and motion. Up close, seated, in plain dress grays with only his insignia to mark him, he was smaller than expected—not short, but diminished somehow, as though the man inside the officer were slowly being used up by the office. He was maybe fifty, with a scholar's stoop and a soldier's cropped hair gone iron at the temples. His hands, when he gestured Noor into a chair, were clean and precise and terribly gentle, the hands of a man who had learned that gentleness frightened people more than shouting.

"Marten Hale," Ilsen said, pronouncing the false name with perfect faith. "Grain. Sit, please. You'll forgive the setting—I've requisitioned the room, the table, and apparently the dead family's candlesticks. The war makes thieves of us all; the only question is whether we steal politely."

"I hadn't planned to complain about the service," Noor said, settling into the merchant's voice like a coat. It fit loosely tonight. It had begun fitting loosely everywhere.

"Good. Complaining ruins appetite." Ilsen poured the wine himself, refusing help from the silent soldier at the sideboard. "You've been in my town four days, Master Hale. Four days is a long time for a man whose barges supposedly left without him."

"The barges left. My money didn't." Noor lifted the wine, sniffed it as a merchant would—performed the whole vulgar theater of appraisal. "Requisitions freeze credit. Every factor in Vessany knows it."

"And yet here you are, lodging above a ledger workshop." Ilsen smiled. He had a good smile, which was the worst discovery of the evening. It was the smile of a favorite uncle, of teachers who kept canes in their desks. "Expensive tastes for a frozen market."

"A man has to sleep somewhere."

"He does." Ilsen let that sit. He carved the fowl himself, sleeves rolled to the forearm, and Noor watched those precise hands move the knife and thought, absurdly, of Freya's father at his binding bench, and pushed the thought away before it could reach the face. "You know what I admire about merchants, Master Hale? Honesty. Oh, they lie about prices, provenance, their mother's health—but they're honest about wanting. A merchant wants money. Simple. Clean. I can work with that."

"Flattered."

"Don't be. It means I'm about to insult you." Ilsen set down the knife and folded his hands. "Somewhere north of here, in the last three weeks, eleven couriers have passed through lines carrying sealed dispatches. Eleven. I have intercepted six. The others got through—or didn't, which amounts to the same thing now. Do you know why? Because nobody trusts anybody anymore. The republic is a body that has stopped recognizing its own limbs. And into that—" he gestured with two fingers, a small circular motion encompassing the wine, the war, the world "—walks a grain factor who stays four days over a frozen account, sleeping above the single most valuable archive between here and the capital. So. Let us not insult each other. What do you carry?"

For a moment Noor considered the truth—not all of it, but some of it, the way one throws a fish back to keep the fishing grounds. But Ilsen's eyes were patient and gray and missed nothing, and the merchant's voice, when it came, stayed level.

"Papers. Grain contracts, mostly. Copies."

"Contracts." Ilsen tasted the word. "For a workshop that binds ledgers, not trades them."

"I'm told the owner does beautiful work. I wanted to commission a piece." This was almost true, and Noor delivered it with the flat sincerity that made lies travel well. "A gift. For a colleague."

Ilsen studied him for a long moment. The candles guttered and steadied. Outside, a patrol went by, boots ringing on wet cobbles, and neither man looked toward the sound.

"A gift," Ilsen said at last, softly, and reached beneath his chair.

What he brought up was a leather folder, and what he laid on the cloth beside the roast was not a document but a map—or rather, a plan. Noor recognized drafting conventions: elevations, annotations, a floor diagram of a building Noor knew intimately, having slept three nights in its storeroom. Venn's workshop, drawn in careful ink. The second floor. The archive room. The cellar stairs.

"You see the problem," Ilsen said, conversational now, a man among colleagues. "An archive like that is a weapon. Names, dates, obligations—every promise this republic ever made to every displaced soul it shoved across a border. If the enemy takes Haldenmere, they take the registry, and suddenly they hold a list of every person in these provinces with a grievance and a claim. Recruitment rolls, essentially. Ready-made." He tapped the drawing of the archive room with one clean finger. "So it comes out of their reach. Standard procedure. We've done it at Marren, at Ost Ford. The archives burn, the towns stay quiet, everyone goes home."

Noor looked at the map instead of at him. Looked at the little annotated rectangle where thousands of names lay sleeping under lock. *Within the month*, said a notation in Ilsen's own hand, dated nine days hence. Nine days. Not a month at all—the month was what he told civilians; the nine days was what he told his quartermaster.

"When?" Noor asked. The merchant's voice had gone thin. Noor rebuilt it around the word like scaffolding. "When does it happen?"

"Why?" And then Ilsen laughed—not cruelly, which was worse. "There it is. There's the wanting. Good. I was right about you, Master Hale. You want something, and it isn't a commissioned bookbinding." He refilled both glasses, unhurried, sovereign in his ruined courtesy. "Here is what I offer. You are a courier—I won't insult either of us further by pretending otherwise. Your employers are finished; whatever they promised you died somewhere south of the border with the rest of the republic's promises. But I command the roads out of this town until the operation concludes. Safe passage, any direction, papers signed in my own hand. Plus payment—real payment, in hard script, the kind that spends east of the river when nothing else will."

"In exchange for?"

"Cooperation." Ilsen pronounced it gently, the way a doctor pronounces a diagnosis. "When the time comes, I will need to know that the workshop is empty. No accidents. No heroics from the daughter. No lost pages turning up in flour sacks or ferry holds years from now, causing questions. You are inside that house, Master Hale. You hear things. See things. I am asking you to do what sensible people everywhere are doing right now—which is to survive, and to help other sensible people survive, and to let the paper burn."

He raised his glass slightly. A toast to sensible people.

Noor raised the glass too, because the hand knew the choreography even when the chest had gone hollow, and drank, and set the wine down untouched thereafter.

*The workshop is empty.* Meaning Freya. Meaning Aldous Venn, who protested quietly and was threatened quietly. Meaning the girl who left bread and honey on the binding table and never once asked for anything in return except, perhaps, to not be alone in the arithmetic of the war.

And underneath that, colder: *nine days*. The notation on the map had been written five days ago. The clock was already running, had been running since before Noor ever crossed the flooded road into Haldenmere with a saddlebag full of sealed lies.

"You're very quiet," Ilsen observed.

"I'm calculating," Noor said. It was even true.

"Then calculate this." Ilsen leaned forward, and for a moment the uncle's mask slipped and something older and wearier showed through, something that had burned archives at Marren and Ost Ford and had not slept properly since. "I am offering you the only decent thing left on this table. Most officers wouldn't offer at all. They'd simply list you among the persons to be detained when the operation starts—couriers, factors, anyone with loose ties and working legs. That list exists, Master Hale. Yours could be on it, or not, depending entirely on the next week. Think of it as weather. You can stand in it, or you can come indoors."

"I've always liked the rain," Noor said.

Something flickered across Ilsen's face—approval, possibly, or disappointment, or the recognition of a fellow creature refusing a kindness it couldn't afford to take. He sat back. He resumed eating, methodically, and the mask reassembled itself piece by piece until only the favorite uncle remained, wiping his fingers on the white cloth, discussing river levels and the price of tallow as though no other subject had ever passed between them.

At the door, cloak on, the mist coming down silver through the lamplight, Noor paused.

"The gift," Noor said. "The commission. For the colleague."

"What about it?"

"Consider it withdrawn."

Ilsen smiled his good smile and closed his own door on his own requisitioned candlesticks. "Master Hale," he said, "everyone withdraws eventually."

* * *

The walk back through Haldenmere took twelve minutes and lasted approximately a year.

Noor took the long way, along the quay, past the moored barges sleeping black on the black water, ropes creaking, the river muttering its one syllable against the pilings. The town's windows glowed warm ahead and behind. In one of them—second floor, front, above a workshop full of names—a lamp was still burning.

Noor stopped at the quay rail and stood in the mist and did the arithmetic that couriers do, the cold inventory: what could be abandoned in a ditch, and what could not. The safe passage, abandoned. The hard script, abandoned. The self called Marten Hale, grain factor, useful for perhaps another day, abandoned gladly.

The lamp in the window. The bread with honey. The nine days.

Nine days to copy a registry of thousands of names by hand.

Noor stood at the rail until the cold climbed through the coat, and then walked back through the wet streets, up the workshop's stair, past Aldous Venn's closed door, into the storeroom where the mirror leaned against the sacking and the merchant's face finally fell off like something unlaced.

Above, the pen had stopped scratching. Footsteps crossed the second floor. The stair creaked, once, and Freya's voice came down through the dark, low, not quite a question:

"You're back."

Noor looked at the ceiling, at the faint square of lamplight beneath her door, and understood with terrible clarity that the next words spoken in this building would decide what kind of person the war got to keep.

"I need to show you something," Noor said, and reached for the lantern.
