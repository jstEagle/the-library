## 7. Copying by Candlelight

**Noor**

The storeroom had a window the width of a hand, and through it Noor could measure the days now not by the dispatch case at their hip but by the light that fell across the floor in a thin gold ribbon, like something spilled and never mopped up. It touched the flour sacks first, then the legs of the binding table Aldous Venn had let them drag in from the workshop proper, then—by late afternoon—the edge of the ledger itself, closed under its leather lid as if it were sleeping.

Three nights after Freya showed them what the book truly was, Noor still woke with the names behind their eyes. Not any name in particular. All of them. The sheer mass of them, column after column, the small careful letters of clerks who had believed, apparently, that writing a thing down was the same as making it true.

Noor had been a courier long enough to know better. Paper promised nothing. Paper was only paper until someone did something with it—and most of the time, what people did with paper was burn it.

The door opened without a knock. That was Freya's way now; they had passed some invisible border between stranger and guest, and she crossed it the way she crossed every threshold in her father's shop, without ceremony, carrying things.

Tonight she carried two candles, a stoppered flask of ink, and a bundle of quills wrapped in flannel like bones of small birds.

"Your father's asleep?"

"My father sleeps like a man listening for bells." She set the candles on the table, lit them with a taper from the lamp downstairs, and the little room turned amber. "He'll wake if we drop anything heavier than a pen. So don't drop anything heavier than a pen."

"And if I do?"

"Then you'll have the pleasure of explaining requisition orders to him at three in the morning." She pulled the second stool close, so close their knees nearly touched under the table, and Noor felt that small proximity like heat off a stove. "Sit."

They sat.

The ledger opened to where they'd left it, and Noor looked down at the columns and felt the old vertigo again—the sense of standing at the top of a stair in the dark. Thousands of names. And somewhere behind them, sealed in oilcloth in Noor's saddlebag, the second dispatch, unopened, patient as a stone at the bottom of a river.

*You don't have to open it,* Noor told themselves, the same lie as last night and the night before. *Not yet. Not while there's work.*

Freya slid a sheet of good rag paper across the table—a blank page cut to match the registry's dimensions almost exactly, though not quite; Noor could see the half-finger difference and knew it would matter later, knew everything would matter later—and dipped the first quill.

"We start with the northern parishes," she said. "The river villages. If it comes to running, those people can run. They have boats. The inland farms—" Her voice caught on something and she smoothed it flat. "We do the ones who can still be saved first."

"You've been thinking about order of operations."

"I've been thinking about it since I was twelve years old," Freya said. "I just didn't know what for." She set the quill down and looked at Noor across the candlelight, and her face did the thing it did when she decided to say something true: a small settling, like a house taking its weight off one wall and onto another. "My father thinks I'm sleeping. He thinks you're a merchant waiting out the floods. Let's keep both of those fictions alive, shall we? At least until the ink dries on the last page."

"He'd forbid it."

"He'd do it himself, and get caught doing it. He writes like himself. Everyone in Vessany who's seen the registry knows his hand." She tapped the page. "Ours has to look like nothing. Like tax copies. Like boredom itself."

Noor picked up the quill. It sat strange in their fingers—too light after years of carrying weight, of sacks and satchels and sealed cases whose importance was measured in ounces and consequences. A pen weighed almost nothing. Noor had forgotten you could hold something so light and feel it pull.

"Copy exactly," Freya said. "Even the errors. Especially the errors. An error corrected looks like a copy. An error preserved looks like an original written badly, and no clerk burns bad handwriting."

"That's either brilliant or insane."

"It's both. Most survival is." She turned the ledger toward the light and began to read aloud, low, barely more than breath: *"Marta Ilves, of Ostrava Landing, ferryman's widow, four children."* Then she stopped reading aloud, and only her lips moved, and Noor wrote.

It was slow. God, it was slow. Each name took shape letter by letter, and the candle burned down in increments that felt like insults, and Noor's hand—which could carry forty pounds over flooded roads all day without complaint—began to ache within the hour, a fine trembling in the tendons that was somehow worse than any pack-strain because it came from precision rather than load.

But something else happened too, something Noor had not anticipated. Somewhere around the fortieth name, the copying stopped being labor and became rhythm, and the rhythm became a kind of shelter. The world outside the window went soft. The checkpoint near-misses, the ditches, the false pass with its forged merchant's seal—all of it receded, and there was only this: Freya's voice naming people, and Noor's hand making them permanent somewhere else.

*Marta Ilves, ferryman's widow.* Somewhere north, a woman Noor would never meet was pulling nets or mending hulls or sleeping, unaware that her whole existence fit in eleven words and that eleven words were currently being carried, letter by letter, out of the path of a fire.

"This is what couriers are supposed to feel like," Noor said, before they could stop themselves.

Freya glanced up. "What do you mean?"

"I've carried orders my whole adult life. Sealed things. Important things. And I never once knew what was in them, and I told myself that was fine—that the not-knowing was a kind of innocence." Noor watched the ink gloss on the fresh letters. "This is the first time I've ever carried something and known. Known the faces. Known what happens if I fail."

"Doesn't it frighten you?"

"Yes," Noor said simply. "But it's a better fear than the other one. The other fear was that none of it mattered. This fear is that it does." 

Freya looked at them for a long moment, candlelight moving in her eyes, and then she bent back over the ledger and read the next name, and the next, and neither of them spoke again except in names.

Near midnight, when the first candle was a puddle and the second guttered, Freya rose, took six finished pages, and crossed to the shelf of bound ledgers along the far wall—old stock, unsold, tax abstracts from years no one audited. She opened one at its back hinge with a binding-knife, slipped the fresh pages into the hollow between cover and sewn signatures, and pasted the endpaper down over them with a brush no wider than a fingernail.

When she was done, the book looked exactly like what it was: dull, fat, forgettable. She pressed it shut and put it back spine-inward between two others.

"No one rebinds a tax abstract," she said. "And no one reads one twice."

"And if they search?"

"Then they'll find forty books about grain tariffs and die of it before they find ours." She blew out the candle, and the dark came in close and warm, smelling of paste and tallow and the river beyond the wall. In the darkness Noor heard her hesitate, then cross the small room, then the brush of her fingers finding Noor's shoulder—light, deliberate, gone again. "Same time tomorrow. Bring steady hands."

"They're never steady."

"Good," Freya said, and Noor could hear the smile in it. "Steady hands belong to people who haven't noticed what's at stake. Goodnight, courier."

The door closed. Noor sat alone in the dark with the smell of ink and paste, and for the first time since crossing the flooded border road, they did not inventory the room by what could be abandoned in a ditch.

For the first time, everything in it seemed worth carrying.

**Freya**

Her father kept the good tallow downstairs and the cheap tallow upstairs, which told Freya everything about where he expected the household's dignity to be displayed, and so she climbed to the second floor each morning with wax on her fingers and resentment in her throat that was really just love wearing a disguise.

On the fourth morning of the copying, she found the work had followed her upstairs. Not literally—she checked the hiding places twice a day now, a compulsion she recognized and declined to treat—but in her hands. She was setting stitches into a client's passport forgery, the third this month, a young cooper from Millbrook whose brother had been conscripted and whose mother had come to her weeping with a purse of coins that couldn't possibly cover what Freya was about to risk, when she noticed her fingers were moving differently.

Slower. More exact. The copying was teaching her patience inside her patience, a stillness under the stillness, and the passports were getting better for it. Which was absurd, and also true, and also—she suspected—the entire secret of her father's craft, which he had never articulated but performed daily for thirty years: that care is transferable. That a person who has copied four hundred names of the displaced cannot afterward forge a lazy document.

She finished the cooper's passport, sanded it, held it to the window light, and thought: *somewhere in that ledger is his name too.* She'd seen it. Row after row of Millbrook. She had copied it herself two nights ago, and her hand had not shaken once, and she had been quietly proud of that until she understood why it hadn't shaken—because by now, names came to her pen the way bread dough came, with the body knowing the work before the mind engaged. Grief, done often enough, became technique.

Downstairs, the bell over the workshop door rang, and Freya's whole body went rigid before she remembered that legitimate customers also rang bells.

She descended into the front room and found not a customer but Tomas, the apprentice, standing awkwardly among the presses with his cap in his hands and his eyes anywhere but hers. Seventeen, all elbows, a boy who swept well and inked poorly and had grown up two streets over with dirt under his nails from his family's failing garden plots.

"Mistress Venn. I—" He swallowed. "I cleaned the binding room yesterday. After hours. I know I shouldn't have, but the master wanted the glue scrapings cleared and I—" His ears had gone red. "There were candle stubs missing. Two nights running. And the good rag paper, a sheet or two. I wasn't spying. I just notice things. It's my job to notice things."

The words landed in Freya's chest like stones dropped into still water, one after another, each ripple widening.

*I wasn't spying. I just notice things.*

She kept her face pleasant and empty, the face she wore for garrison clerks and customs men, and said, "Paper goes missing in a workshop, Tomas. It's the nature of paper. My father blames the moths."

"Yes'm." But he didn't leave. He stood there turning his cap, and Freya watched the war happen in his face—the same war she'd watched happen in a hundred faces across six years of forging papers, the war between the thing a person owed and the thing a person was.

"Was there something else?" she asked gently.

He looked up then, and his eyes were wet and furious with himself, and he said, "The captain's clerk pays me two silvers a week to note who comes and goes. I never write anything worth reading. I swear it. I never—" His voice cracked. "I just needed you to know that whatever I saw or didn't see, I haven't written. I won't write. The debts, you understand, it's not—it was never about—"

"Tomas." She crossed the room and took the cap out of his hands and made him look at her. "Breathe."

He breathed.

"I don't know what candles you mean," she said, very quietly, "and neither do you. But if a person were working late in this shop—if such a person existed—I'd want them to know that the clerk's ledger isn't the only ledger in Haldenmere, and that debts can be settled in more currencies than silver." She let that sit. "Do you follow me?"

He nodded, slow, understanding arriving in him like dawn—not all at once but impossible to stop.

"Good boy. Now sweep something, loudly, and tell your captain's clerk that Mistress Venn quarrels with her father about money and that the courier snores. Both true. Boring things make the best reports."

A ghost of a grin crossed his face and vanished. He swept. He left. Freya stood in the empty front room with her heart going hard against her ribs and understood, with terrible clarity, that the conspiracy had just acquired its first liability—and its first witness in their favor, and that these were the same thing.

That night, back in the storeroom, she said none of this to Noor. Instead she read the names, and Noor wrote them, and the candles burned, and somewhere past midnight Noor paused mid-stroke, flexing their cramping hand, and said, "Tell me about the first one. The first paper you ever forged."

Freya smiled at the ledger. "A market permit. For Widow Sascha. She sold fish without one and they fined her twice, and the second time she cried in our kitchen and my father went upstairs and didn't come down for six hours, and in the morning there was a permit on the table with a stamp so perfect even he couldn't tell me how he'd done it." She drew the next name out of the columns. "I was nine. He let me hold the stamp. That was the whole apprenticeship, at first—just holding things. Holding them carefully."

"And now you hold everything."

"Now I hold everything," she agreed, and reached over without looking away from the page, and found Noor's injured hand resting beside the inkwell, and covered it with her own, ink-stained fingers laced over ink-stained fingers, and held it there while her other hand kept writing, because the work did not stop, the work could not stop, and tenderness, she was learning, was simply another kind of labor you learned to do one-handed.

They worked until the third candle drowned in its own wax. Forty-one names. It would take months at this pace. Captain Ilsen's requisition gave them weeks at best, and Freya had spent enough evenings near the garrison kitchens, delivering forged papers, to hear the way the soldiers talked—like men unpacking for a long stay, which meant the burning, whenever it came, would come with bureaucratic thoroughness, with lists and witnesses and sealed doors.

Forty-one names hidden in a book about grain tariffs. Out of thousands.

She pasted the pages into their binding, pressed the cover shut, and stood in the amber ruin of the candlelight looking at the ledger, still thick with the uncopied dead-weight of the unprotected, and felt the arithmetic settle over her like cold water: the pace was a lie, the pace was death dressed up as diligence, the pace had to change.

Across the room, Noor was watching her. "You've thought of something."

"I've thought of several somethings." Freya pinched out the flame. In the dark, her voice came steady, but underneath it ran the first thread of real fear. "I've thought that two hands aren't enough. And I've thought that the hands we might ask for are attached to people who owe debts—to this shop, to this town, to each other. And I've thought that one of those debtors sweeps our floor and carries two silvers a week in his pocket from a man named Ilsen."

In the darkness, she heard Noor go very still.
