## 8. The Informant's Debt

Freya found the first thread of it on a Tuesday, in the way she found most things—by keeping her hands busy and her eyes open.

She was re-gluing the spine of a parish register that had come in water-damaged from the farms upriver, the kind of small repair work her father took to keep the workshop's legitimate face turned toward the town. The glue pot sat over its little flame, smelling of hide and vinegar, and outside the window the autumn light came down thin as shaved wood. Tomas was at the cutting table across the room, ruling lines on fresh folios for the grain ledgers the garrison had commissioned. His knife moved. His lines were straight. And every few minutes—he probably believed himself unobserved, or perhaps he had simply stopped believing anyone observed him at all—he glanced at the storeroom door.

At the door behind which Noor slept.

It was not the glance itself that troubled her. Freya had watched men look at Noor since the courier arrived: with suspicion, with curiosity, with the particular hunger people had for anything new in a town that had begun to eat itself. A glance could mean nothing. Glances were the town's common currency; everyone spent them freely because they cost nothing.

But Tomas's glances had a ledger quality to them. He counted. She knew counting when she saw it, because she had been raised inside it—raised among quires and columns, among the small disciplines of record-keeping where nothing was written down without purpose. Tomas looked at the storeroom door the way a clerk looks at a figure that does not match his sum. He was entering it somewhere. Somewhere outside this room.

Freya drew her brush along the spine and thought about the winter before last, when the debts had come for the Hessel family. Tomas's father had borrowed against the tannery—everyone borrowed against something in those years—and then the tannery's trade had dried up with the river, and the lender had turned out to be not a lender at all but a factor for the garrison's supply officers, and by spring the Hessels owed a sum that had no honest shape. Freya's father had spoken of it once, quietly, over supper: *Old Hessel came to me about selling his labor contract. I couldn't help him. Nobody can.* And Tomas, who had been apprenticed to Aldous Venn since he was eleven, who had ink under his nails in the permanent way that marked all of them, had gone from being a boy who whistled at his work to a boy who worked silently, and then to a boy who worked with his eyes down, and then—somewhere she had failed to mark the date—to a boy who flinched when boots rang on the cobbles outside.

She should have seen it sooner. That was the thought she kept returning to as the glue cooled and the register's spine set firm. She, of all people, should have known what fear looked like when it learned to be punctual.

Because there was another thing, too. Three days ago, coming back late from the Kessler woman's with a bundle of forged travel papers sewn into her coat lining, Freya had seen Tomas at the corner of Binder's Row and the Garrison Lane, standing in the lee of the shuttered chandlery, talking to Corporal Vey. Just talking. Standing at the relaxed distance of two people who have spoken before, many times, whose meetings have worn a groove in the pavement of habit. Vey had said something and Tomas had nodded, and then Tomas had reached into his coat and handed over a folded paper, and the corporal had taken it the way a man takes a coin that is owed to him.

Freya had stood in the shadow of the chandlery with other people's false names pressed against her ribs and watched, and done nothing, because doing nothing was also a skill and she had practiced it for years.

But now the glances at the storeroom door had acquired arithmetic, and arithmetic meant Noor, and Noor—

Noor, who was upstairs at that moment with her father, going over the registry's collation marks as if they were tax codes, as if that were all they were. Noor, whose hands shook when she thought no one watched. Noor, whom Freya had begun—foolishly, unforgivably, in the small hours between copying sessions—to imagine as someone the war had not yet gotten its fingers around.

Tomas finished his ruled sheet, blotted it, and set it in the drying rack with the others. Then he pulled out his watch—a tin watch, a dead man's watch, he'd told them once it had been his grandfather's—and checked the time, and Freya saw it plainly: he was measuring his shift against an appointment. He had somewhere to be at a certain hour, and the somewhere was not home.

She closed the parish register. Set a weight on it. Smiled at Tomas, who did not quite smile back, and went upstairs to tell her father she was walking to the apothecary for gum arabic, which was a lie so old it practically walked itself.

* * *

She followed him at the distance the market crowd allowed, wearing the particular invisibility of a woman carrying a basket. This was a thing she had learned early: no one suspects a basket. Baskets are permission. She drifted behind Tomas through the fish stalls and the cloth sellers, past the fountain where the soldier boys filled their canteens, and out the mouth of the market square into the streets where the town kept its shame—the pawnshops, the debt-factors' offices with their brass plates polished bright, the lending house that everyone called the Counting Room though its sign said something respectable instead.

Tomas did not go to the Counting Room. He turned up Garrison Lane, and Freya's stomach tightened, because Garrison Lane ended at the requisitioned customs house, and the customs house was Captain Ilsen's headquarters, and everything that went in through its green door went in twice: once as a fact and once as a file.

There was a side entrance for clerks and messengers. Freya knew it because she had spent one long, sweating afternoon last spring copying its schedule of use for a smuggling route her father never asked about directly. The messenger's door had a bench beside it, and beside the bench grew an elderberry bush gone leggy and half-bare with the season, and behind the elderberry bush was a gap between the customs house wall and the neighboring chandler's storehouse—a gap full of crates and shadow, wide enough for a thin woman with a basket and a grievance.

She stood in that gap for forty minutes, until her feet went cold and the light began its long amber slide toward evening, and then the messenger's door opened and Tomas came out of the garrison's headquarters pulling his gloves on, and Freya watched his shoulders as he did it. That was where the truth showed, in shoulders. A boy leaving an errand he's proud of walks loose. A boy leaving an errand that is eating him alive walks like his coat has been nailed shut at the shoulders. Tomas walked like a nailed coat. He got four steps down the lane and stopped, and stood very still, and Freya watched him press the heel of his hand against his eyes, once, hard, the way you swat a fly you cannot admit is there. Then he walked on toward home, and she let him go, because following further would teach her nothing she didn't already know, and what she knew already was heavy enough.

Tomas was informing. Tomas was informing on the workshop. On her father. On her. On Noor.

She stayed in the gap a moment longer, breathing the smell of tarred rope and cold stone, and made herself look at it without flinching, the way her father had taught her to read a damaged page: all of it, even the parts that burn.

Here was the thing about Haldenmere, the thing the republic's maps would never show: everyone in it was trading. The baker traded bread for silence about his son in the northern regiments. The ferryman traded passage for news. Her father traded official ledgers to keep unofficial ones safe. Freya herself traded in forged identities, sold clean papers at prices scaled to desperation, and told herself every night that the telling of the lie made it true. And Tomas—Tomas, who inked ledger lines straighter than any apprentice she'd ever taught—Tomas traded the only currency he had left: what he saw. What he witnessed in a workshop where dangerous things happened daily, where strangers slept in storerooms, where the keeper's daughter disappeared on errands she never explained.

He was trading them. And the buyer was the army. And the price was his family's debt, paid down in installments of betrayal, and Freya stood in the darkening gap between buildings and understood—with a clarity that felt like a slap—that she could not even hate him properly. Because if her father had been the one drowning, if it had been Aldous Venn's name on the Counting Room's books, she did not know—not truly, not honestly—what she would have refused to do.

Everyone was trading something. The whole town was a ledger, and every column balanced, and the currency was whatever a person could not afford to lose.

The question was only ever: what would you spend?

* * *

She caught him two days later, at the lunch hour, alone in the workshop because her father had gone to the grain office and Noor had gone to the river with measurements Freya had invented for her—an errand about barge drafts that would take an hour and involve, coincidentally, walking past every patrol point between here and the quay.

Tomas was eating bread and dripping at the workbench, hunched over it like a boy much younger than seventeen. Freya came down the stairs slowly, so he heard each step, and sat down across from him without asking, which in that workshop was a declaration in itself. You did not sit at the binding table unless you belonged there.

"Your lines have been crooked," she said. "This week. All week."

Tomas stopped chewing. "I've been tired."

"Yes," Freya said. "That's one word for it." She reached across and took his wrist—not gently, but not cruelly either—and turned his hand over, palm up, the way you'd inspect a tool. The ink stains were there as always. So was the tremor. "My father hasn't noticed. He notices paper, not people; it's why we're both still alive. But I notice people. It's my part of the trade."

"Freya—"

"Tuesday you ruled eleven folios and miscounted the gutter margin on three of them. Wednesday you burned the glue twice. Yesterday you looked at the storeroom door nineteen times while I counted. Don't ask me how I know it was nineteen. I know things like that the way you know your letters." She released his wrist. "And on Tuesday evening you went through the green door on Garrison Lane and came out lighter than you went in. Lighter and sicker. I watched your shoulders."

The color left his face in the order she'd seen it leave other faces: jaw first, then forehead, then lips. He looked at the door. He looked at the stairs. There was nowhere to go that she hadn't already mapped, and some part of him must have known it, because he put down his bread and folded his hands like a schoolboy awaiting a sum's correction.

"You don't understand what it's like," he said finally, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, humiliating him further. "They hold the paper, Freya. My father's contract—it's not even a loan anymore, they bought it, they own the debt outright, and the man from the Counting Room comes every month and sits in our kitchen and eats our food and writes numbers, and my mother just sits there. She doesn't even look at him anymore. She just sits. And Vey came to me in September, and he didn't threaten me, that's the thing nobody understands, he was *kind*, he said a boy in a workshop hears things, sees things, and every report shaves a little off the principal, and I thought—I thought I'd do it once, just once, just enough for the winter—" His hands came apart and pressed flat on the table as if steadying the wood against a storm. "I never gave them anything real. Names of grain shipments. How many cords of wood came in. That the courier sleeps in the storeroom. Nothing real."

"That's real," Freya said quietly. "You gave them her existence. In a town where they're hunting people, existence is the whole cargo."

Tomas's eyes were bright and terrible. "If I stop, they call the debt. The whole of it, tomorrow morning. They'll take the tannery and the house and my father goes to the labor levy, at his age, with his lungs, and you know what the levy roads are. You know." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "So yes. I looked at the storeroom door nineteen times. Count my lines however you want, Freya, but don't stand there and pretend you wouldn't have done the same. You, of all people. You sell lies for coin and call it rescue."

The words landed, and she let them, because they were true and she had promised herself she would hear the true version. That was the discipline of the ledger: enter the figures honestly or the whole book rots.

"I would have," she said. "Almost certainly. Which is why I'm not here to condemn you."

His head came up slightly. Hope is fast, even in the ruined.

"I'm here to buy you," Freya said.

For a moment the workshop was perfectly silent. Down in the street a cart passed, iron-rimmed wheels on stone, and the sound went through the floor and was gone.

"The debt," she said. "What is it now? The true figure. Not the one Vey recites—the real number the Counting Room carries, because they always carry a real number underneath the one they use as a whip."

Tomas blinked at her. "Four hundred and ten crowns. Near enough. Why—"

"And what does a discharge paper sell for these days? A genuine one. Medical, stamped, a labor exemption that lets a family walk away from a contract debt because the contracted party is unfit for levied service. Vey himself would have to honor it, because it would come through channels he can't question." She watched him understanding arrive piece by piece, like cargo unloaded. "I can make one. I've made six this year, and the last three would have survived an audit. It won't erase four hundred and ten crowns, but it voids the *basis* of the debt—if the contracted party is discharged, the remainder transfers to a schedule the garrison office has to process, and processing takes months, and by then—" By then, she did not say, because neither of them knew what Haldenmere would be by then. "I will make it tonight. Your father's name, a physician's signature I've used twice successfully, the right stamps. And in exchange, you stop. Everything you've given them, you bury. If Vey asks questions, you give him weather and grain weights until the paper clears. And if he presses—you tell me, immediately, before you tell him anything else. Before you decide anything else. Do you understand what I'm saying? From tonight, when you're frightened, you come to me first. That's the trade. Your eyes for your father's freedom. Silence for a signature."

Tomas was crying now, without drama, the way boys cried when they'd been carrying something alone too long—just water running out of him while he stared at the table. "Why?" he managed. "Why would you—after what I—"

"Because you're going to help me," Freya said, and heard her own voice go soft and strange. "Because a boy who can count nineteen glances back at him is a boy who counts everything, and I need someone in this workshop who counts. Because everyone in this town is trading something, Tomas, and I'd rather you traded with me than with them." She rose, took his cold plate, and set beside him instead the one thing in the room that was genuinely hers to give: the truth, in careful measure. "And because in ten days or a hundred days, whatever's coming for this workshop arrives, and when it does, I want to look around this room and know that every soul in it owes me and I owe them, all of us tangled past any unpicking. That's the only kind of safety there is left. Not money. Tangle."

She was halfway to the stairs when his voice stopped her.

"There's something else." He said it to the table, to his own hands. "Vey asked me—Tuesday, after. He asked me specifically about the courier. Whether she'd been to the registry at night. Whether she'd handled the second dispatch. He wrote the questions down himself and made me read them back." Tomas swallowed. "Freya—they're not watching the workshop. They're watching *her*. The captain has a list, and her description is on it, and Vey wanted me to confirm which room she sleeps in."

The stairwell seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

"They've moved the burning schedule up," Tomas said. "I heard Vey tell the other corporal it wasn't a month anymore. He said *before the month is out*, and he said it like a man reading a change in orders." He finally looked up at her, red-eyed, seventeen years old, already an old man in the ways that mattered. "You'd better make that discharge paper good, Freya. You'd better make it very, very good."

She stood on the stairs with her heart hammering out the arithmetic she suddenly could not balance: days remaining, pages unfinished, a name on a list, and a boy across the room whose silence she had just purchased at the price of trusting him with everything.

Upstairs, in the storeroom, Noor was sleeping. Or pretending to sleep. Freya no longer knew which possibility frightened her more.
