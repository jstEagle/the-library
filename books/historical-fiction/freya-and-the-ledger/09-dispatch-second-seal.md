## 9. Dispatch, Second Seal

The second dispatch had been with Noor so long it had become a part of their body—a weight beneath the breastbone, like a swallowed stone that the years of travel had worn smooth. They had carried it across the flooded border road, through the checkpoint at Marren's Ford where a soldier's fingers had hovered a hand's width from the false bottom of the saddlebag, down into Haldenmere itself, through streets that smelled of wet wool and river mud and the tallow-stink of the chandler's shop on the corner. Everywhere they had gone, it had gone. When they slept in the workshop storeroom, it slept too, folded into the lining of their coat, patient as a tumor.

Noor had been trained never to open what was not addressed to them to open. That was the first law of the courier's trade, older than the republic itself: you are the road, not the letter. A courier who reads becomes a witness, and a witness can be made to speak, and a speaking courier is a broken seal walking around on two legs. Old Sefa, who had taught Noor the trade in the depot at Ilveshade—Sefa with her cracked knuckles and her habit of tapping the table twice before she lied—had said it plainly the first week: *You carry other people's words the way a mule carries ore. The mule doesn't smelt.*

But Noor had never been a mule. That was the trouble. Sefa had seen it early—the way Noor's eyes moved over sealed packets as if reading could be done through paper, through wax, through twine—and had shaken her head with something close to pity. *You'll either be the best I ever trained or you'll hang,* she'd said. *Possibly both.*

Nine days now in Haldenmere. Nine days of transcribing names by candlelight with Freya Venn while the town outside counted its grain and whispered about requisition orders. Nine days of ink under the fingernails and bread left silently at the storeroom door and the slow, terrifying discovery that invisibility—the condition Noor had lived inside for six years—was dissolving. Someone looked at Noor now. Someone expected things.

And all the while, the stone sat where they had swallowed it.

* * *

It was the third watch of the night when Noor finally took the coat down from its nail.

Not decision. Decision would come later; Noor understood that already, the way one understands the shape of a wave before it breaks. What happened first was simpler and worse: sleep refused to come. The storeroom was cold enough to see breath, and the smell of leather bindings and paste hung thick as weather, and somewhere above, Freya's floorboards creaked once, twice, then went quiet. Noor lay listening to the building settle and thought of nothing—which is to say thought of everything, all of it compressed into a single gray pressure behind the eyes.

Then they were standing. Then the coat was in their hands.

The storeroom held one shuttered lamp, and Noor did not light it. There were reasons for caution—Ilsen's patrols passed the alley below on a schedule that had grown shorter every night since the warehouse fire—but caution was not the true reason, and Noor knew that too. Light would have made this an operation, deliberate and defensible. Darkness kept it what it actually was: a person alone with a thing they had refused to know.

They worked by touch and moonlight. The seam of the coat's lining opened under their thumbnail—it had been opened and sewn shut so many times across six years that the thread holes had worn into a soft channel, a scar in the fabric that mapped every mission like a ledger of its own. Out came the oilcloth packet. Out came the first dispatch, the one already delivered to Aldous Venn, the one that had announced the army's arrival and the requisition of the workshop. And beneath it, wrapped separately, the second.

The packet was small. Smaller than it should have been, for how heavy it had felt. Oilcloth, dry now with age and the heat of Noor's body, gone stiff at the folds. Around it, three turns of black cord, knotted in the depot fashion. And beneath the cord, the seal.

Noor turned it in the moonlight from the high window. The wax was dark, almost black, stamped with the sigil of the Second Chancery—not the broad republic seal used for public business but the narrow one, the one reserved for instruments that were never meant to be read aloud. Noor had seen that sigil twice before in six years. Both times, someone afterward had disappeared.

Their hands were shaking. They noticed this with a strange detachment, as if observing a stranger's affliction: look, the courier's hands shake, the instrument has become unreliable, note it in the margin. Freya had noticed days ago—Noor had caught her watching them across the binding table, her eyes dropping to their fingers on the pen, saying nothing, leaving bread without words. She knew something was wrong. She had perhaps guessed wrong about what. Or guessed right. Noor no longer knew which answer was kinder.

*The mule doesn't smelt.* But the mule had watched families load their children onto barges because a ledger said they were promised passage. The mule had spent nine nights copying those names out in its own careful hand, page after page, while a girl with ink-stained fingers explained what each column meant—*this is the mother's name, we list the mother first, my father says mothers are harder to lose track of*—and the mule had felt each name go down like a stitch in its own chest.

A courier who reads becomes a witness. Noor had been becoming a witness for nine days already. The only question left was whether the witnessing would happen honestly or through a lie of omission delivered back up the chain like a knife returned handle-first.

They broke the cord. They broke the seal.

* * *

The wax parted with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Noor unfolded the paper—thin stock, the cheap kind the Chancery used precisely because it signaled contempt for ceremony—and angled it toward the window. The moon gave barely enough. But Noor had read documents in worse light, in ditches, in moving carts, in a hayloft during the shelling at Corve with artillery flashing through the slats like a madman's lantern. The eye learns. The eye will betray you into understanding even when you beg it not to.

The hand was clerical, precise, unornamented. It began, as all such instruments began, with recitation:

*By authority of the Second Chancery of the Republic of Vessany, acting under emergency provision fourteen, the evacuation commitments recorded in the registry of the workshop of Aldous Venn, Haldenmere, are hereby rescinded in full.*

Rescinded. In full.

Noor read it again, though there was no need. The words did not change. Words never changed; that was their cruelty and their only mercy.

*Be it further directed,* the document continued, *that the said registry constitutes no obligation of the republic, financial or otherwise, and that its continued existence presents a security risk in the event of occupation or inspection. The bearer of this instrument is empowered and instructed to certify the destruction of the registry and all partial records pertaining thereto, and to obtain the signature of the workshop's proprietor upon the attached certification as acknowledgment of same.*

Attached. Noor's fingers found the second sheet folded within, smaller, bearing a form in boxes and ruled lines. Certification of destruction. A place for Aldous Venn's name. A place for the date. And at the bottom, a place for the courier's own countersignature—the attestation of a neutral carrier, confirming that the act described had been performed.

That was why they had been sent. Not to deliver orders for the seizure—that had been incidental, a matter of logistics riding along with the real cargo. They had been sent to be the republic's witness at a burning. To stand in the workshop with their steady courier's hands and watch the registry go into the fire and then write down, in official language, that it had gone, and carry that paper back to Ilveshade like ore in a mule's pack.

Six years. Six years of carrying other people's words, and every word had led here: *the names are expendable. The promises were provisional. Provision expired.*

Noor became aware, distantly, that they had lowered themselves onto the floor—that their legs had simply stopped participating—and that they were sitting against the wall beneath the high window with the moonlight laid across their knees and the document trembling in a wind that came from nowhere, from their own shaking, probably, from the small earthquake running through them. The storeroom smelled of glue and old leather and, faintly, of the river. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped. The world was proceeding. That seemed obscene.

They thought of specific names. That was the error—they had promised themselves they would read it as a courier, abstractly, and instead they read it as a copyist. The Halvorsen family, five entries, listed mother-first per Aldous's system, evacuated from the eastern marshes two winters ago and promised return passage when the border settled. The widow Brandt, whose entry included a note in Aldous's hand: *eyesight failing, requires escort.* The boy Danya—just *Danya*, no surname, entered alone because he had arrived alone, age estimated at eleven by the ferryman who found him.

None of them were obligations anymore. None of them were anything. The republic had performed, with a clerk's pen and cheap paper, the trick of making people stop existing without requiring them to die first.

And Noor had carried this. Had slept beside it, guarded it through checkpoints, fed it before themselves at inns because the packet must not be damaged—had loved it, in the dumb faithful way a courier loves cargo, because keeping it whole had been the whole of their purpose. Every step of the flooded road. Every held breath at Marren's Ford while the soldier's fingers hovered. They had protected the order of abandonment with their life and called it duty.

And Freya—

Freya, who copied names until her candle drowned itself in its own wax. Freya, who believed—with what patience, what ferocity—that the ledger meant what it said, that the republic's promise was a load-bearing wall in the architecture of the world, something worth forging papers for, worth bleeding for, worth staying in a doomed town for. Who had shown Noor the registry by lamplight with something like reverence and said, quietly, *this is the best thing my father ever did, and he doesn't even think of it that way—he just writes the names.*

What was Noor supposed to do with the truth? Set it on the binding table between them like a corpse?

Or carry it still—carry it the way they had carried everything, silently, professionally, all the way to its destination, which was now apparently the moment of Aldous Venn's signature and the strike of a match?

* * *

Time passed. Noor could not have said how much. The moon moved the width of two shutters. The dog barked again, farther off. At some point Noor realized they had read the rescission four more times, hunting for a loophole with the desperation of a drowning person hunting a knot in a rope—and there was none, of course. There was never any loophole. The Chancery wrote clean. Clean and final and empty, like a grave marker with no body under it.

But there was the date. That detail surfaced slowly, the way details do when the mind has given up on the large catastrophe and begun cataloguing small ones. The instrument was dated eleven days ago—before Noor had reached Haldenmere, before the requisition, before any of it. Which meant the decision had been made before Noor ever saw the ledger. Before Freya had left bread at the storeroom door. The lie of omission had been built into the mission from the first mile of the flooded road. They had sent Noor here already abandoned, already decided, and let the courier discover it at leisure—or not discover it at all. Some couriers wouldn't have opened it. Most couriers wouldn't have. The system worked because most couriers were mules.

*Ilsen knows.* The thought arrived cold and complete. The captain had invited Noor to dinner, had probed with such courtesy, had offered safe passage out in exchange for cooperation—and had mentioned, delicately, the archives, the burning, the timeline. Ilsen had known the shape of the second dispatch before Noor did. Perhaps he had wondered why the courier hadn't produced it yet. Perhaps he was wondering still, tonight, in his warm room at the garrison house, whether the republic's own courier had gone soft.

And Tomas—Noor thought of the apprentice's quick guilty eyes, the debts, the reports flowing nightly to the captain. If Tomas reported that the courier lingered over the registry, that the courier copied pages by candlelight alongside the keeper's daughter—

Noor folded the document along its original creases. Their hands had steadied. That was the strangest part: the shaking had stopped the moment the catastrophe became a problem rather than a revelation. Problems could be worked. Sefa had taught them that too, though she'd never said it aloud—you could always tell the ones who would last in the trade, because their fear converted to method instead of paralysis.

Fold the paper. Return it to the oilcloth. But not into the coat lining—no. Into the waistband, flat against the small of the back, where a courier carried the things that had become part of the body proper. Then thread a needle by moonlight—slow work, three attempts—and sew the lining seam closed again anyway, out of habit, out of some superstitious sense that the packet should appear to be exactly where anyone searching would expect it, undisturbed, unread, innocent.

Then sit back against the wall and do the arithmetic.

Three copies existed of roughly half the registry, hidden in book bindings and flour sacks and one hollowed-out psalter. Ten days of transcription had covered perhaps forty percent of the names. The certification required Aldous Venn's signature—could be delayed, forged, refused. The burning, Ilsen had implied, waited on paperwork and garrison scheduling. Weeks, possibly. Possibly less.

And Noor's own name sat in neither ledger nor registry—Noor Eastwick, courier, invisible, a person who was only their cargo and had just learned the cargo was a death warrant signed against four thousand people, several of whom were sleeping upstairs and across the street and down by the quay right now, breathing softly, believing in a promise that had been rescinded before half of them had even fled to Haldenmere seeking it.

There was one more line on the certification form Noor hadn't examined closely. They drew it out again, tilted it to the moonlight, and read the countersignature block in full for the first time: *Signed and attested by the undersigned carrier, who affirms personal witness of the complete destruction of the registry and all partial records, including copies.*

Including copies.

The Chancery had thought of everything. Of course it had. It had written the instrument knowing what couriers were likely to do—knowing that some soft-hearted fool in some river town would try to save the names, and requiring that fool to sign, personally, an affidavit that they had failed.

Noor set the form down on the floor as if it might ignite from contact.

Above, a floorboard creaked—Freya, turning in her sleep, or rising, or lying awake the way Noor was lying awake, the whole building stacked with unsleeping people. Tomorrow there would be the binding table, and the bread, and the candle, and her voice low over the columns: *this is the mother's name, we list the mother first.* Tomorrow she would look at Noor's hands and draw her conclusions.

Tomorrow Noor would have to decide which truth to tell her—the one that would break her heart or the silence that would break it slower.

The moon slid off the page. In the dark, Noor sat with the rescission pressed flat against their spine and understood, at last, what they had been carrying across the flooded road all along: not orders. An ending. And the ending was waiting for their signature.

They did not sleep. When the first gray light came through the shutters, they took out the needle and unpicked the stitches they had sewn, and put the certification form somewhere else entirely—somewhere Freya would find it, if it came to that, or somewhere no one would. They hadn't decided. Even the hiding of it had become a lie of omission, and Noor sat in the cold storeroom among the smell of paste and leather, holding the smallest, sharpest knowledge of all:

Every hour they didn't tell her, the lie grew another ring, like a tree.
