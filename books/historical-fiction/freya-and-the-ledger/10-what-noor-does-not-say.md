## 10. What Noor Does Not Say

Freya knew ink the way other people knew weather. She could read a room by its smells—the sharp bite of gall-apple, the sweetness of gum arabic, the tallow-fat heaviness of cheap copying ink—and she could read a person by their hands. Her father's hands moved in slow, deliberate arcs when he was certain and hesitated at the downstrokes when he was not. Tomas's hands had begun to twitch toward his pockets in the weeks since his debts had found their purchase on him. And Noor's hands, which had shaken so badly that first night in the storeroom that they couldn't hold a spoon steady, had grown sure over the long candlelit weeks of copying—sure on the pen, sure on the page, sure in the small geography of Freya's own fingers.

Which was why it unsettled her so completely when, on the ninth evening of their conspiracy, Noor's hands went still.

Not trembling. Still. The stillness of water above something drowned.

They were working at the binding table, the one with the worn leather skirt that Aldous Venn had bought before Freya was born, back when the workshop made ledgers for grain merchants and not for promises. Forty-three pages lay finished between them, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting to disappear into the false spines of hymnals and almanacs. Outside, the river moved in its sleep, and somewhere beyond it the army's watchfires burned orange against the low clouds, close enough now that on still nights the smell of their woodsmoke drifted through the shutters like a rumor.

Freya dipped her pen. Noor did not.

"Your ink is drying," Freya said.

Noor blinked as if surfacing. "Sorry." The pen touched paper. Three names later, it lifted again.

By the fourth time it happened, Freya set her own pen down deliberately, aligning it with the table's edge the way her father did when he wanted a room to notice.

"You've copied the same name twice," she said. "Marta Ilev, of the Weir Road. Twice."

Noor looked down. Struck the second Marta Ilev through with a single careful line—not scratched out, but cancelled, corrected, the way an official would do it—and Freya felt something cold slide down between her shoulder blades.

"That's how they strike names," Freya said quietly. "In requisition orders. In detention lists. Not how we strike errors here."

"I know how they strike names," Noor said. "I was a courier for six years."

"Yes. You were." Freya kept her voice level, kept it pleasant even, because the workshop walls were thin and Tomas slept below and sound traveled up staircases like smoke. "You were also the one who told me that the republic keeps its promises in this book. That every name here is a debt the state has sworn to pay. That's why we're doing this. Isn't it?"

"Of course it is."

"Then copy Marta Ilev once, and stop writing like a clerk who's crossing people off."

Noor flinched. It was small—the kind of flinch a person learns to make invisible after years of checkpoints—but Freya had spent three weeks learning every visible inch of this stranger, and she saw it land.

* * *

She should have let it go. She knew that even then, some part of her standing slightly outside the moment with her arms crossed, disapproving. But the fear had been growing in her all evening, all day really, since breakfast, since she'd watched Noor stand at the window longer than the water barrel needed watching, since she'd noticed that Noor had stopped humming—the tuneless, half-swallowed humming that had become, without Freya's permission, part of the architecture of these nights.

People who carry secrets hum. People who carry verdicts don't.

"You haven't asked me anything in two days," Freya said. She began gathering the finished pages, squaring their edges harder than necessary. "Usually you ask me everything. Which families came from where. Whether the ferryman can be trusted. Whether my father sleeps well. Two days of nothing."

"I've been tired."

"You've been somewhere else." She tied the oilcloth with more force than the knot required. "I've spent this whole war learning to read what people carry, Noor. My father taught me ledgers; the occupation taught me faces. And you—" she turned, and the words came out before she could weigh them, which was the trouble with words, they never waited for weighing— "you look like someone carrying a dispatch they haven't opened."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like the felt pads they used under the presses.

Noor stood very slowly. "That's absurd."

"Is it? Where is it, then? The second dispatch. The sealed one you brought into this house the night you arrived, the one you didn't deliver with the rest. I've watched you check its hiding place eleven times—I stopped counting after eleven. Whatever it says, you already know. You've known since before we started copying. Maybe since before you got here."

"Noor—"

"Don't." Noor's voice cracked on their own name, and something in Freya broke along with it, because she realized she'd never heard them say it aloud before, their own name, as if it belonged to someone else. "Don't say it like that. Like I'm hiding treasure."

"What else would a courier hide?"

* * *

It went badly after that, the way fires go badly—one spark finding a draft.

Noor said, "You think because I carry republic paper I'm the republic," and Freya heard herself answer, "I think the republic sent you here for a reason, and I think you know what it is, and I think you've decided I'm not strong enough to hear it"—and then they were both speaking too fast, too low, the furious whisper-fight of people who cannot afford volume, which is the worst kind, because whispering forces you closer, and closeness makes everything truer.

"The army burns archives," Freya said. "The army seizes grain. The army requisitions workshops. And ten days after the requisition order, a courier arrives carrying sealed dispatches to my father's workshop. Do you know what my neighbors call that timing? They don't call it coincidence. They call it the errand."

"I am not—" Noor stopped. Pressed both hands flat on the binding table, and Freya watched the knuckles whiten, watched the stillness come back, that terrible drowned-water stillness. "You don't know what you're asking me to tell you."

"Then I'm asking nothing. I'm telling you what I see." Freya's throat ached. "I see a courier who copies our names by night and dines with Captain Ilsen by invitation. Who knows the patrols' schedules better than Tomas does. Who flinches every time a garrison boot hits cobblestones—flinches, Noor, like recognition, not like fear. And I see someone who looks at me lately the way my father looked at the registry last month, when he thought I wasn't watching. Like something already lost."

"That isn't fair."

"No. It isn't. Nothing about this war is fair, that's rather the point of it." She heard her own voice turn cruel, the precise, ledgered cruelty her mother had used, dead now nine years, whose sharpness Freya had sworn never to inherit. "So run your errand, courier. Deliver whatever it is you were paid to deliver. But don't sit at my father's table copying names you've already crossed out in your head."

The words left her and immediately she wanted them back—all of them, but especially that one, the last one, the truest and most unforgivable one, because Noor's face did something then that Freya had never seen a face do: it agreed. It went pale and still and agreed, as if Freya had merely read aloud from a document Noor had been carrying for weeks.

"I should go down to the storeroom," Noor said quietly.

"Noor—"

"It's late. Your father needs his table tomorrow."

And they gathered their coat with those steady, terrible hands, and took the candle, and paused at the top of the stairs—a pause Freya would take out and examine for years afterward, looking for what was written in it—and said, without turning around:

"For whatever it's worth, Marta Ilev of the Weir Road is safe. She was always going to be safe. There are things in that ledger no one is coming to collect, and I would burn before I let you learn which ones those are by watching them happen."

Then they were gone down the stairs, each step creaking its old familiar complaint, and Freya stood alone in the workroom with forty-three finished pages and the smell of ink and her heart hammering out a rhythm that felt less like anger than like dread.

* * *

She did not sleep.

She sat at the window instead, in the cold, watching the watchfires across the river multiply like a rash spreading over the far bank, and turned the quarrel over and over the way her father turned a page under magnification—looking for the forgery in it, the flaw, the thing that didn't match.

Because there was a flaw. She'd known it even while saying it. Every accusation she'd thrown at Noor had landed, yes—but landed wrong, the way keys land in locks that aren't theirs. A courier loyal to the army wouldn't have wept over the names, and Noor had wept, the third night, over a child's name entered in a round unpracticed hand, a name Freya had recognized and taken away and buried in the false spine of a psalter. A courier running the army's errand wouldn't have mapped escape routes on the backs of hymnal endpapers, wouldn't have memorized the ferryman's daughter's face so she could be found again, wouldn't have held Freya in the dark of the storeroom like something that could be requisitioned away at any moment.

Noor was not betraying them.

Noor was carrying something.

And Freya, who had built her whole survival on knowing the difference—who forged papers for strangers because she could tell, almost always, who was lying and who was only afraid—had just accused the person she loved of being the lie itself.

She pressed her forehead against the cold shutter slats. Below her, faintly, through the floorboards, she could hear the storeroom: movement, pacing, the scrape of a crate. Then nothing. Then, very softly, unmistakably, the sound of paper being unfolded. Unfolded, held, refolded. Again. Again. As if it could be read differently each time. As if enough readings might change what it said.

Her father's voice came back to her from years ago, teaching her the first rule of the workshop: *A document doesn't tell you the truth, girl. It tells you what somebody needed written down.*

Whatever Noor carried in that seal, it wasn't orders anymore. Orders get delivered. This had been opened, and reread, and sat with for weeks—and still Noor hadn't acted on it, hadn't fled, hadn't signed, hadn't run. Which meant it was something Noor was refusing. Or something Noor was protecting them all from. Or something so heavy that even now, with the watchfires multiplying across the river, with the burning surely coming closer, they could not yet put it down or pick it up.

Freya closed her eyes.

Tomorrow she would apologize. Tomorrow she would find a gentler way to ask—or perhaps not ask at all, perhaps simply stand beside Noor and say, *whatever it is, put it on the table; we've hidden enough things in this house.* Tomorrow there would be time, because the forgery papers had bought them days yet, because Tomas swore the schedule held, because the river was high and the barge was arranged and forty-three pages were done.

Below her, the storeroom door opened. Quiet feet crossed the yard. Quiet feet went out the gate, north, toward the garrison quarter—toward Captain Ilsen's lamp, still burning yellow in the command house window.

Freya watched the small dark figure until the fog took it, and her dread, which had spent all evening dressed as anger, finally showed its true face.

Something was about to break open. And Noor was walking straight toward it, alone, carrying whatever it was, rather than bring it home.
