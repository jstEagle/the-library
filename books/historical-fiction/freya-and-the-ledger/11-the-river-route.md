## 11. The River Route

The Halden was a grey-green ribbon in the dawn mist, and Noor stood at the reed bank south of the ferry landing with a stolen survey map folded against their chest and counted barges like a miser counting coins.

Three, moored at the grain wharf with their holds tarped. One more upstream at the tannery slip, riding low, half-loaded with hides nobody had come to collect since the requisition notices went up. A fifth sat sunk to its gunwales in the shallows past the eel-weirs, good for nothing now but a roost for gulls. Noor had watched it settle over three mornings, the way you might watch a neighbor sicken.

Five barges. Two usable, maybe three if the tannery's master could be bribed or persuaded, and Noor had learned in six years of carrying other people's sealed words that persuasion came cheaper when it wore a uniform's shadow. The courier's coat still hung in the storeroom at the workshop, brushed and buttoned and waiting. It had opened every checkpoint from Vessany's spine down to this drowning river town. It would open one more thing before Noor was finished: a route out.

*Against orders.* The phrase arrived without heat, the way the worst thoughts do. They had no orders left that mattered except the second dispatch, sleeping in its oilskin sleeve beneath the third floorboard of the storeroom, and Noor had stopped pretending they intended to obey it. That pretense had died somewhere between chapter and candle, between the first page Freya copied and the first name Noor memorized against forgetting. What remained was not defiance. Defiance was loud. This was quieter and worse: it was arithmetic.

Two hundred and forty-one names transcribed so far. Four hundred and eleven still waiting. Eleven days by Ilsen's own stamped calendar—Noor had read the requisition schedule upside down across the clerk's desk while pretending to admire the penmanship—and perhaps nine of those days were real, because schedules like that always moved earlier, never later. Nine days to finish copying. And then the copies themselves had to *go* somewhere, which meant water, which meant a barge, which meant a courier standing on a cold riverbank doing sums with other people's lives as the mist burned off and the gulls screamed over the drowned boat.

Noor unfolded the survey map against their knee and began, in soft pencil, in a hand that belonged to no one, to draw the town as it would be walked.

* * *

Haldenmere was built like a cupped hand around its water, streets sloping down to the quay in long parallel ribs. That geography was a mercy and a threat at once. A warning shouted from the workshop's door would carry down all five lanes to the river—but so would boots, so would shouting soldiers, so would fire, moving downhill through timber-framed houses the way flame moves through a letter held to a lamp.

Noor marked each lane and then marked the households along it, working from memory and from the registry itself, which they now knew the way some men know scripture. Not every name could be reached. That truth sat in Noor's stomach like a swallowed stone and refused to dissolve. There were farms up the mill road, a mile and more from the quay, whose occupants would need an hour's notice just to harness a cart. There was the widow Ostrand in Cooper's Lane who had no cart, no horse, and legs that had not carried her past the chapel steps in two years. There were the Halven brothers at the net-sheds who would refuse to go—Noor had delivered enough dispatches in enough dying towns to know the type, men who had decided that their grandfather's eel-rights were worth more than their grandsons' breath—and no amount of warning would move them, and they would have to be warned anyway, because the promise in the ledger did not distinguish between the grateful and the stubborn.

*Promised.* Noor pressed the pencil hard enough to snap a fleck of lead. The republic had promised them. The republic had signed them into Aldous Venn's careful columns and then, in a room Noor would never see, in language Noor carried in oilskin against their own skin, un-promised them all. Every line Noor drew on this map was drawn against the state's own intention. Every household circled was a circle around a wound the republic had decided to let fester.

And yet the hands kept working. That was the strange mercy of the body, Noor thought: it would do the right thing even while the mind stood apart, wringing itself, asking *who am I to decide who is warned, who is saved, who is left?* The body simply drew the next lane. The body simply remembered that the baker's ovens started before dawn and that flour sacks going downstream needed no manifest under four hundred weight. The body had already chosen. Only Noor remained to be convinced.

By full light the map held eleven circles and two routes: the barge, and the old drover's track over the fells that sheep and smugglers used, passable on foot in a night if the fog held, useless for families with children and carts. The fells route was for pages only. For names without bodies. Noor looked at it a long time and understood what it was for—it was the route *they* would take if everything else failed, the courier and the ledger-keeper's daughter, running light, carrying paper instead of people—and hated it, and kept it anyway, because hope required a fallback the way a lock required a key.

* * *

The tannery master's name was Sorrel, and he took Noor's coin with the unhurried greed of a man who had already sold everything else worth selling. The half-loaded barge, he said, could be had for three crowns and silence; he'd heard what happened to boats the army requisitioned, which was that they came back empty or didn't come back. He asked no questions about cargo, which told Noor he guessed there was some, and named his price for guessing quietly, which was the same three crowns.

"Water's high this week," Sorrel said, spitting a brown ribbon of juice into the reeds. "Melting up-country. You could run the weir after dark if your pilot knows the channel markers."

"I don't have a pilot."

Sorrel looked at Noor the way men look at a horse offered without saddle. "Then you have a problem. The bends below the ferry will hole you in the dark unless someone aboard has run them ten years." He wiped his hands on his apron, considering. "Ask at the net-sheds. Ask for Maren Halven. Her father ran grain to Vessany through two wars and she learned the river before she learned letters." He grinned, gap-toothed. "If her brothers let her off the roof long enough."

Noor wrote the name on the inside of their wrist in pencil, the courier's old habit, ink where ink could be licked away. *Maren Halven.* Sister to the stubborn men at the sheds. Another name added to the ledger Noor was building inside their own skull—the ledger of everyone complicit now, everyone whose survival was braided into Noor's choices whether they knew it or not. The list grew daily and it frightened Noor more than any patrol, because it meant the fall, when it came, would not be theirs alone.

* * *

The warehouse fire started at dusk.

Noor saw it from the workshop's second floor, where they had gone to check the hiding-copies sewn into the false backs of unsold account books, and for one whole breath believed the end had come early—that Ilsen's men had found the pages and this was the burning arriving ahead of schedule. Then reason caught up: the blaze stood east of the quay, in the customs row, and the flames were orange and greedy but the bells rang the ordinary alarm, not the garrison's triple stroke, and soldiers' boots in the street below ran *toward* it, not toward the workshop.

An empty warehouse, it proved, by morning. The old salt-storage shed on Customs Row, condemned since spring, torched in the dark with no one inside and nothing to lose—which should have made it meaningless, and did not, because of what Ilsen did afterward.

He doubled the patrols.

Noor counted them the next day from behind a fishmonger's awning: foot patrols now walking pairs along both quays at all hours, a mounted pair making the circuit of the lanes twice a day where before there had been one ride at dusk, and—this was the new cruelty—a checkpoint at the ferry landing itself, a plank table set sideways across the gangway, a corporal and two men turning back anyone leaving town with more than they could carry in their arms. A family's worth of luggage became evidence. A sack became suspicion. The river, which had been Haldenmere's door, was being nailed shut one plank at a time, and the pretext for it all was a burned building that had held nothing but pigeons.

Unless it hadn't been empty. Noor turned the question over all afternoon while pretending to price eels. Fires were tools; Noor had seen them used as tools in three collapsing provinces. A fire gave an officer pretext, and pretext was currency, and Ilsen struck Noor as a man short of coin—or a man being pressed from above to produce results, arson conveniently proving that Haldenmere harbored saboteurs and therefore justified any severity. Or—darker, and Noor made themselves consider it—someone in town had burned the warehouse to force exactly this tightening, someone who profited when the town's exits closed. Tomas's face rose unbidden, pale and earnest over the binding table, and Noor put it away again. Accusing the boy without proof was its own kind of requisition.

Either way, the lesson was written in patrol-boots: whatever route left Haldenmere would leave soon, and quickly, and possibly at night through reeds with the pages wrapped in oilcloth against the spray.

* * *

Freya found the map that evening.

Noor had spread it on the binding table between the stacks of blank endpapers, the pencil circles plain as bruises, and had stepped away to fetch the second lamp, and came back to find her bent over it with her sleeves pushed up and ink to her second knuckles, reading the town as fluently as she read her father's columns.

"You've circled the Ostrand house," she said. Her voice was carefully level, which Noor had learned meant it wasn't.

"She can't be moved by cart. I'm working on that."

"And the Halvens?"

"Her brothers won't go. But Sorrel says Maren can pilot the weir in the dark, so I'll ask her anyway. If she comes, we warn the brothers through her and let them choose." Noor hesitated. "There are eleven houses I'm certain we can reach on foot in under an hour. Beyond that—" They touched the edge of the map, the unmarked fells, the far farms. "Beyond that is prayer."

Freya straightened. In the lamplight her face had the same stillness her father's had during the requisition reading, the stillness of a person holding something breakable very carefully inside themselves. "You're drawing escape routes for people you've never met," she said, "while carrying orders that say none of them matter. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"A traitor," Noor said. It came out easily, almost lightly, and surprised them both.

"No." Freya came around the table. She smelled of soot-ink and wine and the tallow of a hundred candles burned past midnight. "It makes you someone choosing. Which is harder than treason. Traitors get to stop caring." She laid her hand flat on the map, over the circles, over the eleven houses. "I watched you count patrols today from the fishmonger's. I know what you're doing out there. And I know—" her voice caught, steadied— "I know you haven't told me everything. Whatever the dispatches say. Whatever it is you carry besides paper."

The floor of the world tilted, gently, the way a barge tilts when a passenger shifts their weight. Noor looked at the oilskin sleeve's imagined shape beneath the floorboard upstairs and felt the whole architecture of silence tremble.

"Tomorrow I talk to Maren Halven," Noor said. "The night after, if the fog holds, we test the weir with a dry run—empty barrels, nothing incriminating. Three nights after that, the pages go." They paused. "And Freya. When they go—"

"When they go, I'm on the barge," she said. "That was settled the first night you drew a circle on a map. You just haven't admitted it to yourself yet."

She went back to her copying. Noor stood a long moment with their hand hovering over the fell-route, the escape drawn for two and labeled for none, and understood, with the mild wonder of a person watching ice finally give way underfoot, that she was right—that the choice had been made weeks ago in candlelight and shared labor, and all that remained was for duty to finish dying quietly somewhere in Noor's chest, unmourned.

Upstairs, in the street, a patrol passed. Boots in pairs. Slower than yesterday, and closer to the door.
