## 12. Tomas Pays His Debt Differently

Freya found the first sign of Tomas's guilt in the ink itself.

She had been grinding pigment for the copying work—lampblack stretched with linseed, a poor cousin to the workshop's proper gall-ink but good enough for names—and she had gone to the supply cabinet on the ground floor for the last cake of true carbon black. It was gone. In its place, a scrap of paper with Tomas's cramped apology: *Borrowed for a commission. Will return it. T.*

That was three days ago. The cake had not returned, and neither had Tomas's habit of meeting her eyes. He worked at his binding bench in the corner of the second floor with his shoulders drawn up around his ears like a man braced against weather that would not come, and when she passed behind him he flinched at the sound of her footsteps, which was a strange thing for an apprentice to do in the shop where he had been raised. Strange enough that Freya had begun to keep her own count of it. Six flinches. Seven. She had grown up reading small tells; forgery taught you that. The tremor in a signature. The hesitation before a seal. A boy who could not look at the woman whose bread he ate every evening was telling her something as plainly as if he had written it in the ledger itself.

She waited until Aldous went down to the cellar to inventory what could not be saved, and until Noor left for the river district with a sample page sewn into the lining of a coat, and then she crossed the workroom and stood at the edge of Tomas's bench and said nothing at all.

He was stitching a folio he had no orders to stitch. His hands moved with the automatic grace of ten years' training, awl and thread and bone folder, and they were steady—it was only his face that shook.

"You've been to see the captain," Freya said.

The needle stopped. Outside, a cart rattled over the bridge stones, and somewhere down the lane a fishwife was crying her prices in the flat, exhausted voice of someone who no longer believed anyone would buy.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Tomas." She kept her voice low, almost gentle. That was the trick she had learned from her father—that gentleness opened more doors than fury ever had. "You took the ink cake because you needed something to bring him. A gift. Proof you were still useful. And now you can't meet my eyes because you've given him other things too, and you know I know it."

His hands lay flat on the bench as if pinned there. For a long moment the only sound was the fishwife's voice fading down the lane, and then Tomas made a noise that was half laugh and half something breaking.

"My mother owes the garrison commissary forty-one marks," he said. "Since spring. My father's pension stopped coming in the third month of the occupation—they said the records were lost. Forty-one marks, Freya. It isn't even a large debt. It's a *mean* one. Small enough that they could forgive it in an afternoon and choose not to."

"And Ilsen knows."

"Ilsen knows everything in this town. That's his whole talent. He doesn't need informants—he needs debts, and this town is made of them now." Tomas finally looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terrible with the particular misery of a young man who has discovered that his conscience arrived too late to be convenient. "I told him about the grain shipments being rerouted. I told him about the ferryman's night schedule. Small things. Things he already knew, mostly. I thought—I told myself—if I gave him small truths, he'd never ask for large ones."

"But he asked."

"He asked about you." The word came out of Tomas like a stone he had been holding under water for weeks. "About your father. About whether papers were moving through the shop that weren't on the requisition manifests."

* * *

Freya walked to the window and stood with her back to him, looking down into the lane where the fishwife had been. The street was empty now except for a dog turning over refuse with methodical hopelessness. She made herself breathe slowly. Panic was a luxury with a price she could not pay; she had learned that in her fifteenth year, forging her first false passport with her heart hammering so hard she had smeared the date, and learned it again every season since. Fear was permitted. Panic was not.

"How much does he know?" she asked the glass.

"Nothing certain. He suspects. There's a difference, and the difference is time—he has to prove things before he can act on them, and proof takes him days. But Freya—" Tomas's voice cracked, and she heard him rise from the bench behind her, heard the desperation in it. "I came tonight to tell you because I can't—I can't carry it anymore. What I did before was survivable. This isn't. Listen to me. The burning has been moved up."

She turned.

"The archives. The registry. All of it. The original order said thirty days—I saw the requisition schedule, it was posted in the clerk's office, thirty days for cataloguing and then destruction. Ilsen tore it up four nights ago. New order. Ten days from yesterday. Nine now." His voice dropped as though the walls themselves might inform. "They're not even going to catalogue properly. They're going to burn it all in the yard behind the garrison, in iron braziers, a section at a time, so nothing blows away. The clerk complained—the clerk, of all people—that there wasn't time to record what was being destroyed, and Ilsen said—" Tomas swallowed. "He said the recording *was* the destruction. That the ledger's whole purpose ended when the republic fell, and ash doesn't need an index."

Nine days. Freya counted backward without wanting to, the arithmetic running itself: nine days, and perhaps two hundred pages still untranscribed, working by candlelight three hours a night in stolen intervals, two hands and sometimes a third when her father could hold a pen. Even at their best pace they would finish six days short. The gap yawned open in front of her like the flooded border road Noor had described, all water and no bottom.

"Does anyone else know the date changed?"

"No one outside the garrison. Not yet. The bakers don't know, the ferryman doesn't know. You have nine days where everyone else thinks you have twenty-nine." He hesitated. "There's more."

"There always is, lately."

Tomas reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper—not official stock, she noticed, just cheap wrapping paper, the kind of thing a frightened man copies onto because he cannot bring himself to steal stationery from the people he is betraying. He unfolded it on the binding bench and smoothed it with both hands, an apprentice's gesture, reverent and doomed.

"I copy the duty roster every morning for the sergeant who signs my mother's debt deferrals. It's part of the arrangement. Last week there was a new list appended—a detention list. Persons to be held when the archive action begins. Hostages, effectively. Anyone who might make trouble while the burning happens." His finger trembled above the paper, not quite touching it. "I wasn't going to take it. I copied it instead, which is worse somehow, isn't it? Stealing would have been simple. Copying meant I chose to know."

Freya looked down at the list.

It was short. Eleven names in a clerk's tidy hand. The ferryman Brandt. The baker Ostermann and his eldest son. Two aldermen. Her father's name, which she had expected the way you expect a wound you have already dressed in your mind—Aldous Venn, keeper of the registry, to be detained at his place of business upon commencement of operations.

And beneath her father's name, added later, the ink slightly different, slightly fresher:

*The courier known as Merchant Vessin — description attached — to be identified and held. Believed lodging at or near the Venn premises.*

Noor. Their false merchant's pass, the careful alias, all of it already fraying at the edges of Ilsen's suspicion—and now there was a paper with a line drawn under the name, waiting. Not suspected anymore. *Scheduled.*

"They're not certain," Tomas said quickly, watching her face. "The entry says 'believed.' Ilsen wants you watched, that's all—he told the corporal on the bridge detail to note everyone who enters the shop after dark. But once the burning starts, 'believed' becomes easy. Detain first, sort afterward. Nobody gets released quickly from a list that exists to frighten a town."

Freya read the name again. *Merchant Vessin.* Underneath it, in smaller characters, the clerk had written the identifying details Noor had invented at the border: height, coloring, the small scar at the left eyebrow that Noor had actually earned falling out of a pear tree at fourteen and wore now like a forged mark of authenticity. Someone in the garrison knew that scar. Someone had looked at Noor's face closely enough to transcribe it.

"When was this list posted?" she asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm, clerical, her father's voice.

"Six days ago."

Six days. Before the dinner with Ilsen. Before the second course and the offered pen. Which meant the dinner had never been an interrogation at all—or rather, it had been worse than that: it had been a courtesy extended to someone already filed. Ilsen had fed Noor wine and safe-passage offers with the detention paper sitting in his office drawer, already signed. The seduction of being seen, she thought, and felt sick. Noor had indeed been seen. Completely.

"Why?" she asked, and it came out harder than she intended. "Why are you telling me this? Your mother's debt doesn't vanish because your conscience woke up. If Ilsen learns you warned us—"

"He'll forgive the debt," Tomas said simply. "Or he'll double it. Either way my mother eats the same bread." He began folding the list again, then stopped, holding it out to her. "Take it. You'll need it more than I will."

"That's evidence. If they search the shop—"

"If they search the shop we're all finished regardless of what's in my pockets." He pressed it into her hand. His fingers were cold; the workroom had been cold for weeks; the whole town seemed to be running out of warmth the way it was running out of ink. "Freya, listen. I owe this shop everything. Master Venn took me off the street when I was eleven. He taught me that a book is a promise—you bind it so it lasts, you make it strong enough to survive its reader. And I've spent this autumn feeding promises to a man who burns them." His jaw worked. "My mother's debt bought silence. I'm paying the rest of it differently. That's all. That's the whole accounting."

She wanted to say something to him—forgiveness, or warning, or both—but what came out was practical, because the clock in her chest had started ticking and would not stop. "You can't come here again after tonight. If you're seen leaving, the watching starts today instead of at the burning."

"I know."

"And your mother. If this goes wrong—"

"I've written her a letter explaining the debt. It's honest, which will confuse her terribly." The ghost of the old Tomas surfaced for one second—a boy who had once laughed constantly, who had inked noses on the portraits in the sample folios—and then it sank again. "Nine days, Freya. Maybe less. Ilsen moves dates the way other men move chess pieces. Don't build anything on nine."

* * *

After he left—out the garden door, past the dying espalier pears, keeping to the wall the way apprentices learn before soldiers teach them better ways—Freya sat down at the binding table and laid the copied list flat in front of her and allowed herself exactly sixty seconds of stillness. She timed it by the pendulum clock in the hall, an old habit from her forging years: one minute to feel the fear fully, to let it fill her like floodwater, and then the minute ends and the water goes to work turning wheels instead.

At fifty-eight seconds, Noor's key turned in the street door below.

She heard the careful footsteps, the pause at the ground floor to check the shutter latch—Noor checked everything twice, always, the discipline of a person who measured every possession by what could be abandoned—and then the stairs, and then Noor was in the doorway with river mud to the knees and a loaf under one arm and that expression, that terrible open expression, asking the room how it fared before asking anyone in it.

Freya looked at the list on the table. Looked at the name on it. Thought: *nine days,* and *they know your face,* and *I cannot put this paper down and I cannot pick it up.*

"You're back early," she said. "The tide must have been kind."

"The tide was indifferent. I paid it." Noor set the loaf down, and then, because Noor read rooms the way Freya read signatures, went still. "What happened?"

Freya picked up the paper. Held it out.

"Your name," she said, "is on a list. And the burning isn't a month away anymore. Tomas says nine days." She watched the color leave Noor's face the way dye leaves cloth—quickly, thoroughly, at the first touch of the right solvent. "Perhaps less. Perhaps much less."

Noor took the list and read it, and Freya stood there in the cold workroom surrounded by two hundred unfinished pages, counting the seconds of the next minute herself, because nobody else in the room was going to.
