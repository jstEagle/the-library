## 13. Confession at the Binding Table

The binding room smelled of glue and old leather and, faintly, of the tallow candles they had burned down to stubs over the past week. Noor sat at the long binding table with a copy of page four hundred and eleven under their hands, and for the first time since crossing the flooded border road into Haldenmere, their hands were not shaking.

That was how they knew the moment had come. A person's hands only went still when everything in them had already decided.

Freya was across the table, bent over her own page, her hair tied back with a strip of blue ribbon that had once, Noor guessed, bound a sheaf of invoices. She wrote the names in a hand that was not hers—she had learned, over the war years, to write in a dozen hands, none of them her own—and the candlelight moved along her jaw and the small ink stain at the corner of her mouth where she touched it absently when she thought.

"Kellam," she said, reading from the original propped on the ledger stand between them. "Kellam, Ondrey, wife Maret, three children, last registered at the Weir district. Do you know the Weir?"

"No."

"Flooded twice since the registry was opened. The republic kept promising to move them upriver." She dipped her pen. "They're still there. My father says the ferryman knows them by name. He carries their grain tax every autumn like clockwork, and every spring the water comes into their house, and every summer the republic writes them another letter."

Noor watched her write. The letters were small and even and entirely someone else's.

*Tell her.*

The words had been sitting in Noor's chest for six days now, since the night Freya had shown them what the ledger truly was, since the sealed second dispatch had gone from being an unknown weight to a known one—a weight Noor carried alone, through shared meals, shared work, shared sleepless hours, through the slow and terrifying discovery that invisibility was not, after all, the thing they wanted most in the world.

*Tell her, and lose this table, this light, this person. Or don't tell her, and lose yourself.*

"I can't do this one," Freya said suddenly, sitting back. Her pen hovered. "Ondrey Kellam. I've been writing his name for ten minutes and my hand keeps stopping. It feels like—" She frowned at the page. "Like signing something on his behalf. Like promising him something."

"You are," Noor said. "We are."

"Yes." She looked up. "Well. Better to promise than to pretend we couldn't."

*She keeps saying things like that,* Noor thought, *and every one of them lands on me like a sentence read aloud.*

"Freya."

"Mm." She had bent back over the page.

"I have to tell you something, and when I'm done you're going to want me to leave this workshop, and you'll be right to want it, but I need you to let me finish before you decide."

Freya set down her pen with great care, aligning it parallel to the page edge, the way she did when she was steadying herself. Noor had learned to read all her small rituals: the pen aligned meant fear; the ribbon retied meant anger; bread left without comment meant tenderness too large for words.

"The dispatches I carried into Haldenmere," Noor said. "There were two. You know about the first—the routing orders, the requisition schedule, the things Captain Ilsen already knew when he arrived. Everyone assumed that was all I carried. Including me, at first. The second seal was marked for opening only upon certification of receipt—that is, only after I'd confirmed delivery of the first, confirmed the army held the town and the workshop. That's standard. Couriers aren't trusted with whole truths. We're trusted with weights and distances, and the truth is delivered to us in installments, like rations."

Freya said nothing. In the next room, Aldous Venn coughed in his sleep—the broken, rattling cough of a man who had been quietly threatened by a captain and had never fully breathed right again.

"I opened the second dispatch six nights ago."

Freya's eyes went, involuntarily, to the storeroom where Noor slept, then back. "You've been carrying it for six days."

"In my coat. Under the floorboard. I couldn't burn it—I told myself it was evidence, that we might need proof, but that was a lie too. I couldn't burn it because burning it felt like making it true, and not burning it felt like keeping open the possibility that I had misread it." Noor laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, hollow. "As if the words would rearrange themselves if I left them folded long enough."

"What does it say?"

Noor took a breath, and then another, and found that the breaths were not enough, so they simply began.

"It says that the Republic of Vessany, by order of the Provisional Council for Continuity of State, rescinds all prior commitments of evacuation and protection enumerated in the civil registries of the eastern provinces. That's the language. *Rescinds.* As though the promises were subscriptions. It says the registry is reclassified from protective instrument to administrative surplus. It says—" Noor's voice caught, and they pushed through. "It says that the courier bearing this dispatch is instructed to certify, before a garrison officer, the orderly destruction of the aforementioned records, and to sign such certification under seal, thereby confirming the republic has no further obligation, legal or moral, to any person named therein."

The candle flame stood perfectly still. Somewhere outside, far off, a patrol boot struck loose cobblestone and was silent again.

"Any person named therein," Freya repeated.

"Fourteen thousand names. Eleven thousand, two hundred and six, as of the last amendment. Your father's count."

"And your orders are to help destroy them."

"My orders are to *certify* the destruction. To make it official. The republic didn't send an officer to do it because an officer's signature might be contested someday, by some surviving tribunal. A courier's signature means nothing. That's the design. I'm disposable paper certifying disposable people." Noor spread their hands flat on the table, on either side of Ondrey Kellam's half-copied name. "I have known for six days. Six days while we copied pages together. While you taught me which bindings hide best. While—" They stopped. The rest was too large to say into this room with its smell of glue and its one candle.

Freya sat very straight. When she finally spoke, her voice was level in the way that ice is level—smooth, load-bearing, and thin.

"You watched me bleed ink into flour sacks for the baker. You watched my father, who cannot climb stairs without resting twice now, sort the names into matters-most and matters-less. You ate our bread."

"Yes."

"You slept in our storeroom."

"Yes."

"Were you going to sign it?"

And here was the question Noor had spent six nights failing to answer honestly even alone in the dark, and now it came across the binding table in Freya's quiet voice and could not be dodged, because she was watching with those eyes that had caught Noor lingering over the open ledger the very first day, that noticed everything, that had noticed the shaking hands and answered them with bread instead of questions.

"No," Noor said. "I don't think I was ever going to sign it. But I didn't know that until tonight, and I wasn't sure until an hour ago, and a person who isn't sure for six days while other people do the dangerous work—that person doesn't deserve the distinction between *wasn't going to* and *couldn't decide to.*"

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have. If you'd asked me on the fourth day, I might have signed it. I would have told myself the copies made it survivable, that signing was the way to stay near Ilsen, near the machinery, so I could keep stealing time for the transcription. And maybe some of that would even have been true. But underneath it, Freya, underneath everything, there was a part of me that wanted the mission to be survivable for *me.* That's what they build into us, the courier service. Not loyalty—arithmetic. Every decision weighed against what can be abandoned in a ditch. I came to your town measuring everything I owned by whether I could walk away from it, and I never stopped doing it. I just added new things to the scale." Noor looked down at the page. "I weighed you. I weighed your father. I weighed fourteen thousand strangers whose names I copied in a stranger's handwriting. And I told myself that as long as I hadn't signed, the scale was still balanced."

The silence that followed was long enough for the candle to gutter and recover.

Freya rose. For one terrible moment Noor thought she was leaving the room, and braced for it—but she only walked to the window and stood looking out at the dark shape of the river warehouses, arms crossed, the blue ribbon catching what little light remained.

"Do you know what I did when my mother died?" she said, not turning. "I was thirteen. The fever year. And the republic sent a form. One form. Notification of Death, Civil Registry, with a line for the recorder's signature confirming the event had been entered in the provincial ledger. My father was too broken to hold a pen, so I forged his hand. I wrote his signature on the document that recorded my mother's death, and I did it well, because I was good at it, because I'd already been forging papers for neighbors for a year by then. And afterward I understood something that I have never told anyone." She turned around. Her face was composed; only her hands, gripping her own elbows, betrayed her. "I understood that ink is never neutral. Every signature serves somebody. There is no neutral hand. When you write a name down, you are either keeping it or erasing it, and sometimes the same penstroke does both, and you do not get to choose which, except by choosing very carefully, always, in advance, who you serve."

"I know," Noor said. "That's why I'm telling you. Because I chose badly, slowly, by inches, and then—"

"And then Tomas." Her voice sharpened. "You knew about Tomas? That he reports to Ilsen? You must have suspected—you dined with the man twice."

"I suspected the dinner was a test. I didn't know about Tomas. But if I'd been caught, everyone in this house would have paid for it, and I knew that too, and I stayed anyway, because—" Noor stopped. "Because of you. Which is not an excuse. It's just the truth, and I'm apparently spending all my truth tonight at once, so I may as well say it plainly: I stayed because of you, and my staying endangered you, and I would make the same choice again, and that is the most selfish thing I have ever done and I am not sorry for it."

Freya came back to the table. She did not sit. She stood over the ledger stand, over the original registry with its thousands of patient names, and pressed her palms flat against the wood as if she could feel the book breathing.

"When you knocked on our door with your merchant's pass and your sealed case," she said, "my father asked me what I thought of you. I said: *a person who is only their cargo.* That's what you seemed like. Someone hollowed out and filled with errands. And I have watched you fill back in, these weeks, with work and bread and—" she faltered, just slightly, the first crack in her composure, "—and me. I believed it. I believed the filling-in was real."

"It is real."

"Then why does tonight feel like finding out the hollow was there all along?" She closed her eyes briefly. "No. That's unfair. I'm doing what frightened people do—reaching for the cruelest available sentence. Forgive me."

"There's nothing to forgive."

"There is. I'm choosing not to use it, but it exists." She opened her eyes and looked at Noor directly, and whatever Noor had expected—rage, dismissal, the door—it was not this: grief and calculation braided together, the look of a woman already three moves ahead. "Here is what I believe. I believe you did not sign. I believe you could have left at any point in six days—Ilsen offered you safe passage, you told me that much, whatever else you softened—and you did not take it. I believe the courier service built you for arithmetic, and somewhere between the border road and my father's binding table, you became bad at it. People become bad at arithmetic for exactly one reason." She did not say the reason. Neither of them needed it spoken; it lay on the table between the ink pots like a third candle.

"Then—"

"Don't," she said, raising a hand. "Don't ask me to say it's all right. It isn't. You carried a death warrant for my neighbors under our roof and told me nothing while I bled my fingers copying the names it condemned. That is not a wound that closes because the cause was love or fear or cowardice or whatever we decide to call it. What it is, is finished. The secret is out. So we don't have to spend any more of ourselves hiding it." She pulled out her chair and sat, and picked up her pen, and for a moment Noor thought the conversation was over, that forgiveness had been declined and work resumed, and the loneliness of the border road rose up in them like floodwater.

Then Freya set the pen down again and folded her hands, and looked at Noor across the binding table the way her father must have looked at ledgers—unflinching, itemizing, ready to balance whatever was brought to her.

"So here is my only question," she said. "Not *why*, not *how long*, not even *do you love me*—that one can wait, we have a deadline. Here is the question: the certification exists. Ilsen expects your signature within days, perhaps sooner. If you refuse outright, he detains you and finds another courier, and the destruction proceeds on schedule with you in a cell. If you flee, same result minus the cell. If you sign, the republic is legally finished with these people forever, and some future court will wave your signature like a torch. So." Her voice did not rise at all; it dropped, gently, to almost nothing. "What will you do now?"

And Noor, who had rehearsed confessions for six days—who had imagined the anger, imagined the door, imagined packing the saddlebag in a ditch and measuring it one final time for abandonable weight—found that they had prepared for everything except being asked, calmly, by the woman they loved, to stop confessing and start deciding.

"I don't know," Noor said. It was the second-hardest thing they had ever said aloud. "I genuinely don't. Every plan I've made has a hole in it shaped like my signature."

"Good," Freya said.

Noor stared. "*Good?*"

"Good. Because a person who knows the answer is a person who decided it alone, and we are done with that—both of us, do you understand? No more single sealed dispatches in this house, no more decisions under floorboards. Whatever you do, we will find it together, at this table, the way everything true in this town gets found." She drew the original ledger toward her and opened it to the amendments section, and her voice, when it came again, had the particular steel Noor had heard in it only once before—the night she had confronted Tomas. "Now. Tell me exactly what the certification document requires. Word for word. Every clause, every blank, every place a seal or date or countersignature goes. Because if your signature is the problem—" and here something flickered at the corner of her mouth, not quite a smile, more like a blade turning toward the light, "—then you should know you're sitting at the table of the finest forger in three provinces, and my father taught me that no document is stronger than its weakest requirement."

Noor leaned forward, heart pounding with something that was no longer dread, and began, word for word, to recite the terms of their own condemnation.

Outside, the river ran dark toward the sea, and somewhere along it, in a warehouse that smelled of tar, a barge waited with a hold full of hidden pages—and neither of them yet knew that by morning, the forgery would need to be perfect, and the time for perfection had already begun to run.
