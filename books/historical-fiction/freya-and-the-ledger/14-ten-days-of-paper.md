## 14. Ten Days of Paper

### I.

Noor had never believed a town could keep a secret. Secrets were cargo; they leaked the way grain leaked from a torn sack, a few grains at a time, then a trail, then a confession. Every courier learned this in their first year. You did not tell the person beside you on the ferry what you carried. You did not tell the innkeeper. You did not tell anyone, because everyone had a price and the republic's enemies had deep purses.

Haldenmere was dismantling that belief one hour at a time.

It began with the baker, Maret Sorl, a broad woman with flour worked so deep into the creases of her knuckles that she looked permanently dusted with snow. Freya had approached her on the third morning of the ten, before dawn, when the ovens were hot enough to make the whole back room shimmer. Noor had waited outside in the alley, heart hammering against the false bottom of the bread basket they carried, listening for the sound of refusal — a raised voice, a door slammed, boots running for the garrison.

Instead there was laughter. Low, brief, almost incredulous — and then the scrape of a chair being dragged to the table, and Maret's voice saying, *well, sit down then, girl, before you let the cold in.*

Noor went in and found Freya spreading a single copied page across a surface dusted white as a winter field, and Maret reading it with her lips moving, her thick fingers hovering over the names without touching them, the way one hovers over something holy or breakable.

"I know this family," she said at last. "The Ostrandis. The boy used to steal raisins from my cooling racks when he was small enough that stealing was still charming." Her jaw worked. "They're supposed to be evacuated. That's what the notice said. Two years ago."

"That's what the ledger says," Freya answered quietly.

"That's not the same thing anymore, is it." It wasn't a question. Maret looked up at Noor — looked *through* would have been more accurate, the way she looked through dough to judge its rise — and whatever she saw there, in the courier's face or hands or the particular stillness of someone who has decided to stop running, made her nod once, sharp as a knife coming down. "Flour sacks," she said. "You want to know where you can hide paper that soldiers won't search. Flour sacks. They never search the flour. Half the garrison's bread comes out of my ovens and if they poison their own supply with ink, well." A smile crossed her face without warming it. "That would be a kind of justice too."

By noon the system existed. Maret baked early, as she always did, filling sacks for delivery to the river settlements downstream — legitimate orders, legitimate routes, stamped with her mark which no clerk had ever thought to question. Between the inner lining and the sacking, folded flat and wrapped in waxed cloth against the damp, the pages rode out of Haldenmere like seeds in a furrow. Each sack held six sheets. Each sheet held forty names. Maret began humming while she worked, an old planting song, and did not explain it and was not asked.

Noor ran the deliveries themselves for the first days, using the merchant's pass that had gotten them across the border — the pass that now felt like an artifact from another life, belonging to a person who measured every possession by what could be abandoned in a ditch. But the checkpoints multiplied. Ilsen's patrols tightened after the warehouse fire, standing at the corners with their collars up against the river damp, stopping carts, unfolding papers by lantern light. On the fifth morning a sergeant waved Noor's cart down at the mill bridge and made them open the basket, and stood very close while Noor lifted out loaves one by one, and the page folded inside the second loaf — Noor had argued against loaves, but Maret had overruled, saying paper rode better in bread than anywhere — sat there under the sesame crust like a heart inside a chest.

"Bit thin, this one," the sergeant said, weighing a loaf in his hand.

"My nephew's wedding batch," Noor said. "The bride's family likes them delicate." A lie with flour on its hands. The sergeant grunted, took the loaf — *took the loaf* — bit into it right there on the bridge, chewed, swallowed, and waved them through, and Noor drove the cart the length of the mill road with their pulse roaring in their ears and grief for the Ostrandis' names dissolving in a soldier's stomach.

They told Freya that night. She laughed until she had to sit down, and then the laugh turned into something else, and she pressed her forehead against Noor's shoulder and stayed there a long moment, and neither of them said anything about how much time remained, because the number was written everywhere already — in the candle stubs shortening on the worktable, in the ink pots going light, in the way Aldous Venn now checked the street through the shutters each morning before unbolting the door, a habit he had sworn he'd never take up.

Ten days. Seven left, after the bridge. The arithmetic sat in the room with them like a third person who never slept.

### II.

Freya's father changed on the fourth day, and the change frightened her more than the burning would have.

For weeks since the requisition notice he had been folding inward, a man crumpling like paper held too near a flame — going through the motions at the binding table, answering questions a day late, eating because she put food in front of him. She had watched the registry break him by degrees. Thirty years of his handwriting lived in those volumes, thirty years of *received, entered, witnessed*, and the state had declared all of it kindling.

But something happened to Aldous Venn the evening Maret Sorl came to the workshop with flour in her hair and a list of questions about which families could read and which could not, and which roads out of the river settlements were passable in autumn — and Freya watched it happen. Her father straightened at his table. Something lit behind his eyes that she hadn't seen since before the war's worst winters: not hope exactly, but *usefulness*, which for a man like him was better.

"You're copying everything," he said, turning the nearest volume toward the lamplight, "and copying everything is how you save nothing. Listen to me." His voice had the old lecture-room authority in it, and Tomas, who had been sanding bindings by the window and pretending very hard not to exist, stopped pretending. Even Noor set down their pen.

"A ledger is not a list," Aldous said. "A ledger is an argument. Every entry is a claim the republic made and must be held to. But some claims are stronger than others, and when there is no time—" his hand moved over the open page, and Freya saw it tremble only once before it steadied— "when there is no time, you must copy like a lawyer, not like a priest."

He showed them. The entries with witness signatures and garrison stamps — those proved the promise was official, undeniable, worth more than paper in any reckoning that might come after the war. Copy those first, in full, seals and all. Then the entries with dates of promised passage attached — those told families *when*, which mattered more than anything now that *whether* had been answered in secret in Noor's dispatch case. Then the family groupings, kept together, because a mother's name saved alone was a grief, not a rescue.

"And these?" Freya asked, indicating the loose leaves at the back, older entries in a different ink, uncounted.

Her father was quiet a moment. "Families who paid bribes to be listed twice," he said finally. "Or once, having never been listed honestly at all. I kept them anyway." He met her eyes. "Every keeper keeps things off the books. It is the only corruption I ever permitted myself, and I have never regretted it. But we cannot carry them. Choose the stamped ones. The republic's own hand is the only weapon we have that they cannot call forged."

After that the workshop reorganized itself around him like iron filings around a magnet. Aldous sat at the head of the binding table with the master registry open before him and called the order of work the way he had once called it for apprentices: *Ostrandi, volume four, stamped, full entry — Kellermere ferry families, volume seven, passage dates — the Halven widows, volume two, witnesses.* Freya copied. Noor copied, in a small disguised hand they had practiced until it looked like nothing at all. Tomas copied, badly and fast, until Aldous put a hand on his shoulder and said, gently, "Slower, boy. A name copied wrong is a name lost twice," and Tomas bent over the page again with his eyes wet and did not look up for an hour.

Freya found she could not hate him anymore. There had been a night — she kept it in a closed drawer inside herself — when she had held a forged discharge paper in front of Tomas and watched him understand what it cost and what it bought, and she had felt nothing but contempt with fear underneath it. Now she watched him laboring over each letter as though penmanship were penance, and understood that everyone in Haldenmere was trading something — debts, silences, safety, sleep — and that the only question worth asking was what a person traded it *for*. Tomas was trading his guilt for names. She could drink from that cup.

On the sixth night her father worked until his fingers would not close properly around the pen, and she pried it away from him and walked him to the stairs, and at the bottom step he stopped and gripped the rail and said, without turning around:

"I spent the war believing the ledger would matter someday. Some tribunal, some accounting. I told myself that was why the work was worth doing." He was silent a moment. "It turns out it matters now, tonight, in flour sacks and ferry memorization, in the hands of a courier whose government sent them here to erase it all. It needed to matter *before the fire*, not after the war. I had the timing wrong by years." He climbed two steps, paused. "Don't tell your mother I said the work was worth doing. She never believed in it. She'll be insufferable, wherever she is."

Freya stood on the stairs until his footsteps faded above her, and then she went back to the table and copied until the candles gave out, and by then it was the seventh day, and three remained.

### III.

Noor's POV.

The ferryman was named Corin Haste, and he had agreed to help for reasons he declined to give, which Noor respected deeply. Professional courtesy among people whose professions depended on not explaining themselves.

They found him at the water stair before dusk on the seventh day, mending net by touch more than sight, the river behind him running the color of old pewter. Freya laid out the plan: barge downstream on the last night, pages distributed at the three landings, families warned by knock and whisper. What they needed was not the ferryman's boat. They needed his memory.

"The routes," Noor said. "Which landings have patrols, which are dark, which families on the registry live within a walk of the water. You've run this river thirty years. We have three days. You know things it would take us three weeks to learn."

Corin wound a net-mending needle slowly around a strand of twine, unhurried as the current. "Army watches the mill landing since the warehouse fire," he said. "Two men, sometimes three, but they change at moonrise and moonrise is when they eat, so there's your gap, though I'd not swear my life on it." Another loop of twine. "Church landing's dark — the vicar douses his lamp early, claims economy, but I think he just likes the dark. The stone stair below the tannery floods this time of year, so nobody uses it, which means nobody guards it, which means everybody should." He looked up, and his eyes were pale and quick and missed nothing. "The Beckel family's a mile inland from the church landing, past the orchard, you'll hear the dog before you see the house. The Sorls' cousins run the tide mill, they'll take papers. Old Wenna at the eel sheds can't walk far but her memory's a ledger all by itself — tell her the names and she'll find the people."

He recited it all without pausing, thirty years of the river unspooling, and Freya copied it onto slate because paper was too precious, and Noor committed it to the other archive they trusted — the one behind their eyes.

"Why?" Freya asked at the end, the question Noor had been too proud to ask. "You live here. If this goes wrong, they'll hang you from your own beam."

Corin considered the river. Downstream the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing, gold at the edges, and the first lamp of the far shore came alight and wavered on the water.

"When I was young," he said, "there was a flood. Before the wall was raised. Water came up to the second floor and everything floated away that wasn't tied down — furniture, livestock, the church records, a man's whole life in an afternoon." He bit the twine and knotted it. "My mother grabbed me and the family bible, and we stood on the roof, and I remember being angry at her. Eight years old, freezing, and all she'd saved was a book." He coiled the net. "She said: *the house can be rebuilt, boy. The names can't.*" He stood, joints cracking like rigging. "I didn't understand it then. I've had thirty years to work on it. Come back tomorrow and I'll draw you the tannery stair in charcoal, and we'll say nothing to anybody, and that's the last we'll speak of why."

Walking back up from the water in the blue dark, Noor felt something in their chest that they could not immediately identify, having been a courier too long to recognize it at first. It took the length of the mill road to name it.

Belonging. The specific, terrible weight of being counted among people instead of passing through them. For years Noor had survived by being nobody — a false pass, a false name, possessions measured by what could be abandoned in a ditch. Now there was a baker who knew their face, a ferryman who had drawn them a map in charcoal, a woman at a binding table waiting up with ink-stained fingers and a plate kept warm. Every one of them a liability. Every one of them a reason.

The lamp above the workshop door was lit, which meant Freya was still working, which meant Noor would not be alone with the silence tonight. They quickened their steps.

Then they saw the figure leaning against the doorpost beneath the lamp, arms crossed, uniform buttoned to the throat despite the mildness of the evening, and stopped dead in the lane.

Captain Ilsen removed his hat, courteously, the way he did everything.

"Courier," he said pleasantly. "I hoped I'd find you in. May I come in? I have a document that requires your signature — and I'm told," his eyes moved to the lit window, to the shadow that passed across it, "you've become fond of this town. It would be a shame to make the paperwork difficult."

Noor stood in the lane between the river at their back and the lamp ahead, and understood that the ten days had just become fewer.
