## 15. Ilsen's Dinner, Second Course

The invitation arrived the way everything official arrived in Haldenmere now: on stamped paper, carried by a boy too young to understand what he was delivering.

*Captain Ilsen requests the pleasure of the courier's company at supper. Quarters at the old customs house. Eighth hour.*

Noor read it twice by the light of Freya's shuttered lamp, then folded it into quarters and held the fold against their knee until the crease set like a small bone. *Requests the pleasure.* The republic had always been fluent in that particular language—the grammar of courtesy stretched over the skeleton of threat. Noor had carried enough dispatches to know that when an officer said *pleasure*, what he meant was *presence*, and when he said *supper*, he meant *interrogation with knives laid beside the plates instead of upon them.*

"You don't have to go," Freya said. She was sitting on the floor with her back against the binding table, ink to her elbows, a half-finished page of names drying on her lap. In the candlelight her hands looked stained almost black past the wrist, as though she had reached into the ledger itself and pulled something out. "Men like Ilsen don't invite people twice. If you don't come to his table, he comes to your door."

"If I don't go, he wonders harder about why I wouldn't." Noor turned the invitation over. Blank on the back. Even the army's paper was economical now; there was no longer any margin in which to write things that weren't orders. "A courier who avoids the captain's table is a courier with something to hide. I'm supposed to be a merchant waiting out the flood roads."

"You're not a very good merchant."

"No," Noor agreed. "I'm not."

Freya looked up at them then, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because they had both learned in the past weeks that some silences were more honest than anything either could put into words. The page on her lap had begun to dry; the names sat in the paper now, permanent, patient. *Mira Ostrow and child. The Brandt brothers, millers. Ansel Kray, ferryman's son, age eleven.* Noor knew without looking that if they read down far enough the page would end where all the pages ended—in nothing, in a promise no one upstairs intended to keep.

"Eat what he gives you," Freya said finally. "Drink less than he pours. And come back."

It was the last part she said more quietly than the rest, as though it were not an instruction but a request she wasn't certain she had the standing to make. Noor wanted to answer it properly—wanted to say something about how coming back had stopped being a matter of routes and schedules and had become, somewhere along the river road, the whole of the plan—but the words were still under construction when the church bell struck seven, and there was only time to take her hand, ink-stained and warm, and hold it a moment longer than caution allowed.

Then the door, the dark street, the smell of the river coming in off the flats, and the customs house waiting at the end of it with its windows lit yellow against the night like eyes kept open on purpose.

* * *

The old customs house had belonged, before the war, to the office that collected duties on the grain barges. Now it belonged to Captain Ilsen the way everything in Haldenmere was beginning to belong to Captain Ilsen—by right of the man standing in it with a list in his hand. A soldier took Noor's coat at the door and another stood at the foot of the stairs, and between these two small courtesies Noor understood exactly what kind of dinner this was: one where the exits were already counted.

Ilsen received them in the second-floor room that had once been the assessor's office. Someone had moved a table to the window, and the window looked west, out over the rooftops of the town toward the workshop district, and Noor noted this—noticed it the way they noticed everything now, the courier's habit of measuring every room by sightlines—and understood that the seating was not accidental. From this chair, a man could sit at his supper and look at the thing he intended to burn.

"Courier." Ilsen rose. Out of uniform—he wore only a grey shirt open at the collar, sleeves rolled—he looked less like a soldier and more like what Noor suspected he had been before the war: a provincial clerk, or a schoolmaster, someone whose authority had once lived entirely in penmanship. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the deep-tired face of a man who had slept badly for years and had stopped pretending otherwise. "You found the address easily, I hope. The town's streets confuse strangers after the floods changed them."

"The floods changed mine too, Captain. Once." Noor took the offered chair. "Before Vessany."

"Where before Vessany?"

"South of the Sarrow marshes. A town that isn't on maps anymore." It was true, which made it safe. Noor had learned long ago that lies were work and truth, carefully rationed, cost nothing. "There's very little left to say about it."

Something moved across Ilsen's face—acknowledgment, or its cousin—and he nodded and poured the wine himself. Two glasses only. The room held no servants; whatever Ilsen wanted from tonight, he did not want witnesses to it.

The food came up from somewhere below on covered dishes brought by a boy who didn't look higher than the tray: fish from the mere, bread, a stew of roots with very little meat in it, because requisition had taken the cattle weeks ago. Army rations dressed in civilian china. Noor ate what was given, as Freya had told them to, and drank less than was poured, also as instructed, and between bites the two of them spoke of nothing—the weather, the state of the east road, the price of lamp oil—which is to say they spoke fluently in the language of two people circling the same fire.

It was Ilsen who ended the circling. He set down his glass, turned it once on the tablecloth so that the ring of moisture it left looked like the mark of some small official seal, and said:

"How long have you been carrying dispatches?"

Noor let the pause last just long enough to be honest. "Nine years."

"Nine years." Ilsen looked out the window, at the rooftops between him and the workshop district. "Long enough to remember when the republic deserved them."

"That's not a question they trained us to answer, Captain."

"No." He smiled, and the smile was brief and cost him something. "They never do. But you've been in my town three weeks now, courier, and I find I'm curious about you in a way I haven't permitted myself to be curious about anyone since the armistice failed. Curiosity is a luxury. I've had none to spare." He turned the glass again. "So let me ask plainly, and you may answer as plainly or not at all. You carried papers to Aldous Venn. You have stayed past any reasonable delivery schedule. You dine with the ledger-keeper's daughter. You walk the quay at dusk counting barges—I know, because my sergeant counts you doing it. And tomorrow—" here he paused, and Noor watched his hand flatten on the table as though holding something down, "—tomorrow I am required to ask you a question I would rather not ask anyone. So tonight, we skip ahead. Tonight I will simply show you."

He rose, went to the writing desk beneath the window, and came back with a folder—grey, government stock, the kind Noor had carried sealed for nine years without once being given permission to open—and laid it on the table between them.

And on top of the folder, deliberately, like a stone placed on a grave, he set a pen.

* * *

The certification document was short. That was the first thing Noor noticed—the brevity of it, the terrible economy. Nine years of carrying dispatches had taught Noor that the weight of an order bore no relation to the length of its paper. Some orders ran to pages of cipher. This one needed fewer than two hundred words.

*I, the undersigned courier, bearing full authority of the Provisional Office of Records, do hereby witness and certify the destruction, by order of the Committee of Public Continuance, of the registry known as the Venn Ledger of Haldenmere, comprising [number] entries, said registry having been determined redundant to the needs of the state...*

Redundant. Noor read the word four times and each time it landed in a different place in the chest. Redundant, as though the names were copies of something. As though the people themselves existed elsewhere, in some other record, in some safer keeping, and this book were merely a duplicate that could be discarded without loss. Noor knew better. Noor had seen the true ledger by candlelight, had copied pages of it until dawn with ink on their knuckles and Freya asleep against their shoulder. There was no other copy. There never had been. The republic had written these promises down exactly once, and had now decided, in the language of committees, that once was too many times to keep a promise.

"You'll note," Ilsen said quietly, watching Noor read, "that the certification requires your signature, your courier's seal, and the date of destruction. The date has been left blank. You are invited to fill it in yourself." He refilled Noor's glass, though Noor hadn't touched it. "In exchange—the offer stands as before. Safe passage south on the next military barge. Your name cleared of the irregularities in your file. Passage for one."

"For one," Noor repeated.

"For one." Ilsen did not look away, and Noor respected him slightly for that; most men offering a coward's bargain studied the tabletop while they made it. "The daughter is not included. I want to be precise with you, courier, because precision is the last form of honesty available to men like me. I can save you. I cannot save her, and I cannot save the town, and I cannot save the book. Those decisions were made above my rank, in rooms I have never entered, by signatures I will never see. What remains to me is this table, this bottle, and the question of whether you leave Haldenmere alive or stay in it and die with the rest of us when the order is executed."

"And if I refuse to sign?"

"Then I must report a courier obstructing a lawful requisition, and you become a detainee instead of a guest, and someone else signs the paper anyway." Ilsen leaned back. Outside, far off, a patrol boat coughed along the river, its lantern dragging a pale seam across the black water. "Do you understand what I am telling you? Your refusal changes nothing except the manner of your leaving. The book burns regardless. The signature is a formality. It is the cheapest thing you will ever be asked to give up."

Noor set down the fork that was no longer in use and folded their hands, because the hands wanted very much to do something else—to reach for the pen, or reach across the table, or reach for the window latch—and folded hands were the safest disguise for all three desires.

"May I ask you a question, Captain?"

"You may ask. Whether I answer depends on the question."

"Why did you invite me to dinner?" Noor lifted their eyes. "You could have summoned me to the garrison office. Clerk present, soldiers at the door, signature extracted in ten minutes. Instead you feed me fish and pour me wine and show me your tired face. Why?"

For a long moment Ilsen didn't answer. He looked at the window again—at the dark shape of the town between here and the workshop chimneys—and Noor saw something happen in his face that Noor had seen before only in mirrors, in border-town inns, at three in the morning, in the nine-year habit of examining their own reflection for evidence of what they had become. It was the look of a man taking inventory of himself and finding the ledger unbalanced.

"Because," Ilsen said finally, "twenty-six years ago I was a junior clerk in the Office of Records myself. Did you know that? Before the commission, before the uniform. I helped compile the third supplement to the evacuation rolls—the early ones, the war's first winter. I wrote names in ledgers with my own hand. Eleven thousand names that winter, and every one of them a promise, and for twenty-six years I believed the promises were the point." His voice stayed level; only his thumb pressed whiter against the table's edge. "Last month I was ordered to prepare this town's requisition schedule, and I recognized my own handwriting in the annexes. Not literally. But the format. The columns. The way the numbers sit where the names used to be." He picked up the pen, weighed it, set it back down on the folder with exaggerated care, as though disarming it. "So I invited you to dinner, courier, because I wanted to see the face of the person the committee chose to close the account. I wanted to know if they sent someone who would enjoy it. And I find—"

He stopped. Drank, this time, actually drank, a long swallow.

"—I find you look like me," he finished. "Twenty-six years ago. Which is either a compliment to you or an indictment of everyone who ever gave us a stamp."

The candle guttered in a draft from the window seam. Neither of them moved to shield it. In the unsteady light the captain's face seemed to flicker between two ages, and Noor understood, with the strange clarity that arrives only at the worst possible times, that this was the truth of the enemy: not cruelty, but captivity. Ilsen was a man chained to a desk inside his own obedience, signing papers he despised because the alternative was to stop being the sort of man who signed papers at all, and after twenty-six years he no longer knew how to stop. He was offering Noor the exit he had never taken, and calling it mercy, and perhaps in the only currency left to him it was.

"I need until morning," Noor said. It was not agreement. It was not refusal. It was the oldest courier's answer in the world: *the answer travels separately.* "The document requires the courier's seal as well as the signature, and my seal kit is at the workshop. I'll come at the tenth hour."

Something eased in Ilsen's shoulders—so slightly that anyone who wasn't watching for it would have missed it—and Noor realized that the captain had wanted the delay too. Wanted one more night in which the paper lay unsigned between them, one more evening in which the burning remained theoretical. He was not testing Noor. He was buying time for both of them, the way a drowning man clings to another drowning man and calls it swimming.

"The tenth hour," Ilsen agreed. He stood, and the dinner was over as formally as it had begun. At the door, while the soldier fetched Noor's coat, the captain spoke once more, quietly, without looking at them.

"There's a warehouse fire listed in the incident log. Down by the ropewalk, eleven days ago. Arson, unsolved." A pause. "My patrols have doubled since. The quays especially. I thought you should know what my nights look like now, so you can judge what yours should look like."

"I don't know anything about a fire, Captain."

"No," Ilsen said. "Of course you don't. Nobody ever knows anything about fires." He opened the door himself, an odd courtesy, and as Noor stepped past him into the stairwell's cold air, the captain added the last thing, almost inaudibly, almost as if to the empty room rather than to the courier:

"Whatever you decide by morning—decide it as a person, not as a carrier. One of those is still possible. I checked recently."

* * *

The streets were empty and silvered with the first thin frost of the season, and Noor walked them slowly, taking the long way to the workshop because the long way passed the quay.

The barges lay dark along the pilings. On the nearest, a tarpaulin bulged over cargo that Noor knew was not cargo—crates of copied pages, sewn into false bottoms, waiting on a tide and a tide-man's silence. Farther down, the garrison patrol moved between lamps, two soldiers and a lantern, checking mooring lines that needed no checking. Doubled patrols. Ilsen had not been warning Noor idly; he had been telling them, in the only language an officer could safely use, that the window was closing.

Noor stood in the shadow of the fish-market awning and felt the two halves of the night pull against each other like river ice. On one side: the grey folder, the blank date line, the pen lying on top of it like a small black bone. Sign, and live, and carry forever the knowledge of what the signature certified. Refuse, and be detained, and watch someone else sign anyway. Either way the ledger burned; Ilsen had been precise about that, brutally precise, because precision was his last honesty.

But Ilsen had assumed the choice was between signing and refusing.

He did not know—could not know—that the ledger already burned in a hundred smaller fires, that its pages were folded into flour sacks and hymnals and the false bottoms of barges, that the names were already traveling by water and memory toward the families they had been written for. He did not know that the certification he needed was already worthless, or that the courier he had dined with had spent three weeks becoming something no dispatch had ordered.

Noor looked once more at the dark barges, at the lantern swinging along the quay, and then turned toward the workshop, where a single candle still showed in a second-floor window, kept lit by someone who had asked them to come back.

Tenth hour tomorrow. One night left to decide how a person, and not a carrier, refuses a signature.

Inside the workshop, the candle waited. And beneath the binding table, wrapped in oilcloth where no requisition officer had yet thought to look, the last unopened crate of pages sat ready for the river—and beside it, in Freya's careful hand, a fresh list of names that had not been in any ledger at all: the families on the quay who had asked, these past two days, in whispers, whether there might be room on the barge for one more.

The list was longer than the barge.

Noor stood in the doorway with the frost melting off their shoulders, read that simple arithmetic by candlelight, and understood that whatever they told Ilsen at the tenth hour, the real answer had already begun moving downriver without them.
