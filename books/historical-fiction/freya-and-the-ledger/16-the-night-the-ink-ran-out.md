## 16. The Night the Ink Ran Out

The ink ran out on a Tuesday, though Freya would remember it afterward as the night the whole world thinned to the color of soot and water.

It happened mid-word, the nib scraping dry against the page with a sound like a small animal dying. She lifted the pen, held it to the candlelight, and watched the last bead of black gather at the tip and refuse to fall. The name she had been writing — *Marta Ostell, her daughter Ede, her daughter's daughters* — ended in a ghost stroke, a scratch that trailed off into nothing, as if the republic's promise itself had simply given out.

She set the pen down carefully, the way her father had taught her to set down any instrument that could not be replaced.

Then she took inventory of the storeroom, because inventory was what she knew how to do, what she had been doing since she was nine years old and first allowed to grind pigments at the binding table. Two candles left, both guttering low in their jars. A quarter loaf of bread gone hard as a shingle. Six finished copies of registry pages, folded flat and sewn into the false spines of hymnals nobody in Haldenmere had opened in years. And ink — ink for perhaps four more pages if she thinned it, eight if she cheated, and cheating meant names that would fade before winter was out.

Four hundred pages remained. Perhaps five hundred. She had stopped counting precisely because the number kept growing worse each time she counted, like a wound measured daily to confirm it had not healed.

She sat very still in the dark of the workshop's second floor and listened to the building breathe. Below her, the great presses stood silent under their dust sheets, useless now; requisition had claimed the type metal weeks ago. Her father slept — or lay awake pretending to sleep — in the room behind the office, his breathing a thin whistling through ribs that had grown too prominent since the army took the grain. And somewhere down by the river, Noor was doing whatever couriers did when they bargained with barge-men in the dark, and Freya had let them go without saying half the things she wanted to say, because some part of her still did not trust that saying things aloud would not break them.

The candle flame leaned. Outside, the wind off the mere carried the smell of wet ash from the warehouse Ilsen's men had burned — a punishment fire, empty of purpose, lit only to remind the town what fire could do.

Freya looked at the ledger open before her, at all those columns of careful hands going back forty years, her father's hand among them, her grandfather's above his, and she thought: *this is only ink. It was always only ink. Which means it can be made again.*

* * *

She found the soot in the kitchen chimney of the bakery two streets over, where Frau Kessler kept a fire burning night and day through fear more than cold, and scraped the flue with a trowel while the old woman stood watch at the door in her nightdress, complaining in a whisper about the state of her chimney and the state of the world and which of those she minded less.

"Vinegar," Frau Kessler said. "My mother used vinegar with lamp-black when the ink sellers stopped coming through. And gum, if you can get it. You can't get gum."

"I have wine."

"Wine." The old woman sniffed. "Wasting good wine on bad news."

"It isn't good wine."

"No," Frau Kessler admitted. "I suppose none of it is, anymore."

Back at the workshop, Freya worked by feel and memory, the recipe assembling itself out of childhood — the soot ground fine against a slab of slate until it stopped gritting, the wine warmed and added drop by drop, a thread of honey scraped from the bottom of the crock, a pinch of powdered oak gall hoarded from better days. The mixture came together wrong twice and then, on the third attempt, came together *almost right*: a grayish black, thin, smelling faintly of vinegar and spoiled fruit, but black. Black enough. Names written in it would hold for a year, maybe two. Long enough to matter. Long enough to be read by someone standing in a doorway somewhere across the border, holding a scrap of paper up to the light and finding, impossibly, their own name on it.

She filled the little horn pot and dipped the pen and went back to work.

The hours after that blurred into a rhythm she would carry in her wrists forever: dip, write, blot, turn the page. The new ink dragged differently than the shop ink, caught in the fibers of the cheap paper she'd resorted to, demanded a slower hand, and the slowness was its own kind of torture, every letter an argument with the clock. Ten days, Tomas had said. Nine now. Eight by morning, maybe. She wrote *Bren Anselm, wheelwright* and thought: does he know yet? Has anyone told Bren he is a name in a book, and that the book is scheduled for fire?

Her father came up once, near midnight, and stood in the doorway watching her write. He didn't speak for a long time. Then: "Your mother had a phrase for this kind of night. She said you could measure a person by what they do when no one is keeping score."

"She never said that."

"No," Aldous Venn agreed. "I invented it just now. But it's true anyway." He came closer and looked at the page, at the grayish letters drying matte and slightly rough. "It will fade."

"Not before it's read."

He put his hand on the back of her neck, briefly, the way he had when she was small, and went back downstairs, and she heard him moving around the binding table below, and understood that he was working too, that neither of them had said anything about sleep because sleep had become a country neither could afford to visit.

By the third hour of the new ink her right hand had begun to complain. By the fourth it had progressed from complaint to protest — a hot wire of pain running from the first knuckle to the wrist — and she switched hands, the way Noor had shown her, laughing at first and then not laughing, demonstrating how a courier learns to write with either fist because on the road you take the light and the surface and the hand you're given. Her left-handed script was ugly, a child's crawl, but legible, and legible was the only standard left that mattered.

She blotted a smear with her sleeve and saw that her cuff was spotted through with old ink and new, layered like sediment, like tree rings, like something geological. She had been becoming a record of herself.

Dip, write, blot. The candle burned down to its final thumb-width. Somewhere outside, a patrol passed, boots ringing on wet cobbles, and she did not stop writing, only wrote smaller, hunched over the page like a woman sheltering a match from rain.

*Ostrell. Haddem. Pike. The Varrow family, seven souls. Old Sela who cannot walk. The miller's boys.*

When the first true grey came through the shutters, her left hand had split along the second knuckle — she never felt it happen, only saw the bright drop fall past the pen and land at the edge of a name and had to decide, with her heart hammering, whether a blood-spotted page was a dangerous page or simply a true one. She decided true. She blotted the edge of it away with her thumb and kept writing.

And then the latch below clicked, softly, in the particular way it clicked when someone who knew the door opened it, and she was moving before she'd finished deciding to move, down the stairs with her bleeding hand wrapped in her apron, and there in the grey doorway stood Noor, soaked to the shoulders, mud to the knees, eyes rimmed red with river wind and exhaustion, carrying nothing but the satchel and an expression Freya had never seen on that careful face before.

* * *

"There's a barge," Noor said, before even removing their coat. Water ran off the hem onto the floorboards. "The Verlaine woman's grain barge, the one that runs salt upriver. I paid half in advance. The other half in the pages themselves — she understands what they are. She'll run them to Tessel's Landing, and from there the border road is three days' walking for anyone who wants to walk it."

"That's—" Freya's voice cracked on the second word; she hadn't spoken since sundown. "That's everything. That's the whole—"

"No." Noor set the satchel on the binding table with a care that frightened her more than any urgency could have. They unbuckled it slowly. "There's a weight limit. Draft on the barge, the weir at Corvey Bend, the patrol skiffs. Maren Verlaine can take the pages and one adult to mind them. That is the arithmetic of the boat. Pages, and one person."

The words took a moment to arrange themselves into meaning, the way a blow takes a moment to find its full depth.

"The people," Freya said.

"The people are ours to move." Noor's voice stayed level, courier-flat, and Freya recognized the flatness now for what it was — the sound of someone holding a door shut against what was behind it. "The barge takes the copies. We take the town. On foot, in ones and twos, down the towpath, over the ford at Corvey if the patrols don't—"

"In eight days? In ones and twos?" Freya heard her own voice rise and did nothing to stop it. "Noor. The old ones alone. Sela can't walk to the well. The Varrows have a child in a cradle. You've mapped routes for *paper*. Paper travels light."

"I know." And then the level voice failed, just once, one syllable fracturing, and Noor pressed the heels of both hands against the table edge and bowed their head, and Freya saw the whole cost of the river trip laid bare in the slope of those shoulders: every mile walked, every coin spent, every argument lost in a smoky boathouse, all of it bent toward saving exactly what could be saved and no more, and Noor had known the shape of that failure since before dawn, had carried it twenty miles upstream alone, and had come back anyway to say it out loud.

"I'm sorry," Noor said. "I tried for another boat. There isn't one. I tried for the ferry route — Ilsen has it chained. I sat with Maren until her tea went cold and we counted the draft board twice hoping the numbers would change. They didn't change."

Freya looked down at her own hands. The left knuckle had begun seeping again, a slow red thread tracing the crease of her palm, and beside it the right hand ached in sympathy, both fists stained — one grayish black, one reddish brown — so that between them she held, literally, the colors of everything this night had cost.

"We knew," she said finally, and was surprised at how quiet her voice came out. "We always knew it would come to choosing. Papa used to say a ledger teaches you the one lesson God won't: that everything fits somewhere except everything at once."

Noor laughed — a broken, grateful sound — and crossed the room, and took Freya's ruined hands in their own chapped ones, and looked at them the way scholars look at manuscripts.

"You're bleeding."

"It's authentic," Freya said. "Adds provenance."

"Don't." But Noor was smiling, wetly, terribly, and pulled a clean rag from the satchel — the rag itself an extravagance, cloth being currency now — and began winding it around her left hand with the same unhurried precision they gave to everything, as if the wrapping of a wound were a dispatch that must be delivered correctly.

"How many pages did you finish?" Noor asked.

"Nineteen. Twenty with the one I bled on."

"Then nineteen families exist outside this building now." Noor tied off the bandage. "Twenty. That's not nothing. That's twenty more than yesterday."

"And four hundred and eighty still on the shelf."

"Yes." Noor met her eyes. "So we keep going. Tonight. Both hands. I'll grind soot."

* * *

They worked through the grey morning and into the next afternoon without stopping, and the strangeness of it was that it became almost gentle — the two of them shoulder to shoulder at the binding table, Noor grinding and straining and refilling the horn pot, reading names aloud in a low steady voice like a litany while Freya wrote, the ugly left-hand script improving page by page until even Noor admitted it had character. Her father sorted and collated, deciding with each sheet which names were priority — households with children first, then the elderly, then single adults young enough to run. It occurred to Freya, somewhere in the eighth hour, that they had become, all three of them, a kind of anti-requisition office: weighing and measuring and assigning value, but in reverse, counting not what the state could take but what the state could be denied.

Tomas came at dusk with bread stolen soft from his mother's oven and news stolen harder from the garrison kitchen: the certification still unsigned, Ilsen still waiting, the burn date holding at ten days minus two. He set the loaves down and looked at Freya's bandaged hand and said nothing, and left money — actual coin — on the table, and Freya understood it was the last of his debt-money or close to it, and let him leave it.

When full dark came again, Noor stood, stretched until their spine crackled, and took up the satchel.

"Where—"

"Maren loads at midnight. The pages go aboard tonight, before the patrols change shift." Noor buckled the straps. "And I need to tell her the count changed. We found room."

Freya looked up. "Room? For—"

"One more person. The Verlaines are taking their nephew's widow; she was supposed to go overland with the others, but her lungs—" Noor stopped. Started again, plainly, the way they had learned to say hard things. "One berth opened. One seat. Someone who cannot make the ford. Someone the town would grieve beyond function."

The room went very quiet. Freya's father set down his pen across the open page, deliberately, like a man laying down a card he has been holding all night.

"Aldous," Noor said. "You built this registry. Forty years of your hand is in it. If it's ever reconstructed, it gets rebuilt from memory, and the memory that matters most is yours. You go tonight. You go with the pages."

For a long moment her father did not move. Freya watched him look at the binding table where he had worked since before she was born, at the presses under their sheets, at the staircase she had climbed as a girl, and she saw him perform the whole arithmetic in silence — everything fitting somewhere except everything at once.

Then he shook his head, slowly, and picked the pen back up.

"The registry's author doesn't abandon the copyists," he said. "Besides — my hand shakes now. Look." He drew a wobbly line in the air with the pen. "Whoever rebuilds this needs a steady hand and a younger memory, and I know exactly where both of those are sitting." He nodded at Freya, and then, with something flickering at the corner of his mouth that in another life might have been humor, at Noor. "You'll both go when it's done. Not before. I'll hear no arguments from couriers."

Noor opened their mouth. Closed it. Freya saw the argument die there, unspoken — saw Noor understand, in one long breath, what it was to be refused a sacrifice, and how much it cost to accept being spared.

"The pages load at midnight regardless," Noor said at last, quietly. "I'll see them aboard."

At the door, pausing with the satchel over one shoulder, Noor turned back. The candlelight caught the river-damp still in their hair.

"There's something else," they said. "Maren told me at the dock. A rider came through from the capital two days ago — military post, sealed orders, went straight to Ilsen. Nobody's seen the garrison clerk since." A beat. "Whatever was in those orders, Ilsen hasn't signed anything in forty-eight hours. Tomas says the men are whispering."

Freya felt the night tilt, very slightly, the way a floor tilts beneath a sleeper who realizes the ship has changed heading.

"So it's moved again," she said. "The date."

"Or cancelled. Or made worse. Nobody knows." Noor's hand rested on the latch. "Which means from tonight, every hour is borrowed. Write fast."
