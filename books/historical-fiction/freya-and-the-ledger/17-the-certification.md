## 17. The Certification

The summons came at dawn, folded into the hands of a garrison boy too young to shave, who would not meet my eyes when he passed it over. *Courier Eastwick will present himself at the requisition office at the ninth hour.* Beneath it, in smaller script, the word that had been following me for days like a dog I couldn't shake: *certification.*

I had known this moment was coming since the night of Ilsen's dinner, since the second course of pheasant and probing questions, since he'd leaned back in his chair with his wine untouched and said, almost gently, *You carried two dispatches, didn't you? I've seen your manifest.* He had not asked what the second contained. He hadn't needed to. Men like Ilsen do not ask questions they already hold the answers to; they lay them on the table like cards and wait to see which way you flinch.

For three days I had flinched nowhere, and every hour of that stillness had cost me something I could not name. Freya knew—of course she knew; she read me now the way she read her father's ledgers, line by line, hunting for the entry that didn't balance. But knowing and naming were different things, and we had agreed, without ever saying so aloud, that some things would stay unspoken until after the ninth hour.

Now the ninth hour came walking up the cobbled lane toward me, wearing my own boots.

The requisition office had been a wool merchant's house before the army took it. I remembered it from my first arrival in Haldenmere, when it still had bolts of grey cloth stacked in the windows and a sign painted with a fat sheep grinning over a pair of shears. Now the windows were papered over from inside, and the sheep was gone, and two soldiers stood at the door with the bored menace of men who had stood too long in one place. They checked my papers with fingers made clumsy by cold. The morning had come in hard off the river, a white wind that smelled of ice about to arrive, and my hands—I noticed this with a distant, clinical sort of horror—were steady.

That was the strange thing. For days my hands had shaken: carrying bread at Freya's binding table, holding a pen over copied names, lying awake in the storeroom listening to the river mutter under the ice. But now, walking toward the thing I had dreaded most, they were quiet. As if some part of me had already signed whatever needed signing and was simply waiting for the rest to catch up.

* * *

Ilsen's office occupied what had been the merchant's parlor. The tapestries were gone but their pale rectangles remained on the walls, ghosts of better rooms. A fire burned low in the grate. At a side table sat the garrison clerk, a narrow man named Pell with ink-stained cuffs and the patient expression of someone whose entire war consisted of making other people's decisions legible. Before him, squared to the table's edge with ceremonial precision, lay a single sheet of paper.

And there it was. I had imagined it a hundred times in a hundred forms—a proclamation, an order, a list—but seeing it was different. It was small. It was one page, covered in the republic's cramped official hand, with blanks left where blanks were meant to be filled. My blanks.

"Courier Eastwick." Ilsen rose behind his desk. He looked tired in a way he had not let show at dinner—the kind of tired that lives behind the eyes and is rationed carefully to subordinates and strangers. "Thank you for coming promptly."

"I wasn't given the option," I said.

A flicker at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. "No. You weren't." He gestured to the chair across from Pell's table, and I sat, and for a long moment the only sound was the fire chewing its wood and the clerk's pen scratching as he noted, presumably, the time and date of my presentation.

"You'll understand," Ilsen said, settling back into his chair, "that this is a formality."

"Yes," I said. "I understand about formalities."

"Do you?" He studied me, and I understood that everything hinged on how I answered—that the whole architecture of the next five minutes was being built right now, out of tone and posture and the steadiness of my hands. "Because formalities are how wars stay civilized. A man burns a village, it's an atrocity. A man signs a document and the village burns itself—it's administration. I prefer administration. I suspect you do too."

"The dispatches I carried," I said, keeping my voice flat as river ice, "authorized certification of the registry's disposition. I'm here to complete my commission."

"Your commission." He repeated it as though tasting it. Then he nodded to Pell. The clerk slid the page across the table toward me and turned it to face my hands.

*Certification of Destruction and Disposition of Sequestered Records.* That was the header. Below it, paragraphs of dense legal prose that I did not need to read because I had read them already, alone, by candlelight, the night I broke the second seal—the night the floor dropped out from under my life. The registry of Haldenmere, comprising four thousand one hundred and twelve names of persons promised evacuation and protection by the Provisional Republic of Vessany, having been determined *infeasible of retention*, was to be destroyed by fire under supervised conditions, and the destruction certified by the courier bearing the sealed orders, signature and date affixed, witnessed by the garrison commander and his clerk, *whereupon all obligations of the Republic toward the named persons shall be considered discharged.*

Discharged. As if the names were soldiers mustered out with pay. As if four thousand people were a debt being crossed off a ledger rather than a promise being fed to a fire.

Pell set the pen down beside the page. It was a good pen, black lacquer, goose-quill, the kind used for documents meant to last. Someone had thought about that. Someone had thought about permanence while drafting a paper whose entire purpose was erasure.

"If you'll sign at the mark," Pell said mildly, "and enter today's date."

I looked at the page. I looked at the blank lines. And somewhere beneath my ribs, in the place where fear usually lived, there was instead a terrible calm, the calm of a river the moment before it goes over the weir.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about betrayal: it looks exactly like duty. Same posture, same steady hand, same lowered eyes. I had spent six years learning to be unreadable—to carry secrets across borders in my face the way I carried them in my saddlebag, packed tight, padded against inspection. Six years of practice were now going to be spent on the single largest lie of my life, and the beauty of it, the obscene elegance, was that no one in this room would be able to tell the difference. To sign honestly and to sign falsely require the same motion of the wrist.

I picked up the pen.

"Before you do," Ilsen said quietly.

I stopped. Every muscle in my arm locked, and I prayed—with whatever part of me still prayed, to whatever was left listening—that my face showed nothing but courteous attention.

"There is a version of this," Ilsen said, "where you sign, and take the safe passage I offered you at dinner, and are on a barge south by tomorrow night. There is another version. I won't describe the second one. I'd like to believe you're a sensible person who has chosen the first."

"And if I'm not sensible?"

His eyes held mine for a moment longer than comfortable. Then he did something unexpected: he sighed, and some of the commander went out of him, and for an instant I saw the man underneath—not the officer administering a town's erasure, but someone else, someone who had also once been handed a page with blanks in it and told that filling them in was the same as being innocent.

"Then you're a person who has decided what his name is worth," he said. "Sign or don't. But sign it clean, Courier. Pell hates corrections."

It was almost kindness. It was, I would understand later, exactly the kind of courtesy a man extends when he suspects he is watching something happen that he will later have to pretend he did not see.

I bent over the page.

*Noor Eastwick, courier, first class,* I wrote, and my hand did not shake. The letters came out even and unhurried, each one placed with the care I'd learned in the courier academy, where they taught us that a signature was a small act of architecture—you built it to bear weight. I felt Pell's eyes on the nib, and Ilsen's on me, and I kept my breathing slow and my wrist level, and I wrote the name I had worn like a uniform for six years onto the paper that would erase four thousand others.

Then the date.

This was the whole game, and it rested on something so small it seemed almost comic: the garrison's calendar. Two nights ago, Freya and I had gone through Pell's posted requisition notices in the workshop's storeroom, comparing them against the almanac Aldous Venn kept above his binding table, and we had found the seam. The garrison office ran its clerical calendar a day ahead through the end of the month—a transcription error made weeks ago during the changeover of quartermasters, never corrected, because no one corrects anything anymore except people trying to survive. Officially, in this room, in Pell's ledger, today was the twenty-sixth of Frostmonth. In fact, in the world outside these papered windows, it was the twenty-third.

If I signed the twenty-sixth, the certification would stand until the actual twenty-sixth—three days purchased, three days of grace, bought with nothing more than a clerk's uncorrected mistake.

But a wrong date alone might be excused. Corrected. Re-signed under supervision. What I needed was something that voided the document entirely—not suspiciously, not provably, just *wrong*, in the way that makes bureaucracies shudder: internally inconsistent. So I had done what Freya had taught me. Three nights at her binding table, copying the registry by candlelight, she had corrected my hand—*looser here, close your loops, couriers write like they're afraid of the paper*—until I could produce, on demand, a fair imitation of the round, careless script of a provincial scrivener, the kind of hand found on market receipts and letters home. That was the hand I wrote the date in. Round loops. Slightly crowded numerals. The twenty-*sixth* rendered in a fist that belonged to no courier, dated into a day that had not yet happened, on a document whose witness clause required the certifying signature and the dating to be executed *in the same hand and at the same sitting*.

Signature: mine, true, architectural. Date: a stranger's, false, impossible. Together on one page, they cancelled each other like poison and antidote.

I set down the pen. The scratch of it ending sounded very loud.

Pell drew the page back, angled it to the light, and began his examination—and here I discovered what it costs to commit a forgery: you have to watch. You cannot look away. You must sit in perfect boredom while a man reads your treason aloud in the language of margins and alignment. Pell checked the signature against my courier credentials, which matched, of course. He checked the date against his desk calendar, which matched, of course—the twenty-sixth, faithfully transcribed from a calendar that lied. His brow furrowed faintly at the date's hand, and my heart performed a slow, catastrophic roll—but he only tilted the page further, sniffed—ink, always sniffing, ink was his whole kingdom—and muttered something about *provincial pens* and made a small notation in his own book.

"Witnessed and filed," Pell said.

"Thank you, Clerk." Ilsen rose. The audience was over. I stood, knees performing their duties through sheer administrative discipline, and gave him the shallow bow one gives an equal one does not respect, and turned for the door.

"Eastwick."

I stopped. Did not turn around immediately—turned slowly, as a man with nothing on his conscience would.

Ilsen stood by the fire, one hand resting on the mantel, watching me with those tired, rationing eyes. For a moment I thought—*he knows. He's seen it. He's going to tear the page up and put me in the cellar next to Aldous Venn and everything ends here in a wool merchant's parlor.*

"You carried our orders well," he said instead. "Whatever you do after this—wherever you go—remember that the republic asked you to do this, and you did it. That's more than most men can say. Most men never get asked anything. They just get burned."

"I'll remember," I said, because it was true in a way he could not possibly have meant.

He waved me out with two fingers, already turning back to his fire, and I walked through the doorway into the papered dimness of the hall, past the soldiers, out into the white wind off the river, and I did not allow myself to breathe properly until I had rounded two corners and put the requisition office behind me.

* * *

Then I leaned against a wall between a shuttered chandler's and a house with all its windows blown in, and I laughed—one short, cracked sound that was half sob—and pressed the back of my hand against my mouth, and my hands finally shook, both of them, violently, now that it was done, now that steadiness was no longer needed. Three days. I had stolen three days out of the republic's pocket with a borrowed hand and a clerk's arithmetic, and the theft was invisible, and it was the finest thing I had ever done.

I pushed off the wall and walked. Fast. Toward the workshop, toward the binding table, toward Freya, who would see it in my face before I got a word out—she always did—but this time what she'd see would be different. This time the thing I was carrying wasn't a secret. It was news. *Three days. We have three days, and then they burn it, and Tomas must reach the ferryman tonight, and the baker's sacks go out tomorrow morning whether they're full or not—*

I was half a street from the workshop when I saw the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Chimney smoke rises thin and domestic from a dozen stacks across Haldenmere every morning, and I had learned its grammar. This was different: a single column, fat and black, climbing from beyond the grain quarter, leaning east on the wind. Even as I watched, a bell began to ring—not the chapel bell, but the shorter, harder clang of the garrison alarm.

And then I saw Tomas running. Down the middle of the lane, coat flying, hatless, past the shuttered stalls, and when he saw me he changed direction and crashed into my arms, gasping, and the words came out of him broken and terrible:

"The warehouse—they know about the warehouse—the copies in the flour—Noor, they found the pages in the sacks, and Ilsen's men are burning what they found, and—" he grabbed my sleeve, pulled himself upright, and finished in a whisper: "He's moved it up. The workshop. Tomorrow night."

The smoke rose. The bell rang. Three days, I had bought three days—

And somewhere behind me, in a parlor full of pale rectangles, a clerk was filing a page that would unravel by morning, and a captain was watching a fire he had lit for reasons of his own, and the days I had stolen were already being taken back.
