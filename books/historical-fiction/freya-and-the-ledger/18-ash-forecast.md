## 18. Ash Forecast

The morning the forgery was discovered, Freya knew it before anyone told her. She knew it the way sailors know a storm—not by seeing it, but by the sudden, wrong-shaped silence of the gulls, by the way the light went thin and metallic over the rooftops of Haldenmere, by the dogs beginning to howl in the lanes near the garrison long before the drums could have reached them.

She was at the binding table, her fingers gray to the second knuckle with soot-ink, finishing the last of the copies—a family named Ostrand, seven names, the youngest written in a careful child's hand because the boy had insisted on adding his own name beneath his mother's, as if signing himself into safety. She was blowing on the page when the bells began.

Not the market bells. Not the chapel bell that rang for weddings and drownings. This was the garrison bell, struck hard and fast, the alarm rhythm she had heard only twice before in her life—once for the flood year, once for the fire at the tannery—and both times it had meant: *something is coming for all of you at once.*

Tomas burst through the workshop door without knocking, which Tomas never did. His face had gone the color of the river in February.

"They know," he said. "Freya. They *know.*"

She set down the page with a steadiness she did not feel. The Ostrand copy was still wet. Everything was still wet; they had been working against the ink's own drying time for ten days, and now the clock had stopped pretending to be kind.

"Tell me."

"Ilsen's clerk found the certification this morning—the date, the hand. He compared it against the dispatch register from the border post. Noor signed it wrong on purpose, Freya. Wrong hand, wrong date. It voids the whole order. Ilsen has been in his office since dawn and no one has seen him smile." Tomas's voice cracked on the last word, and Freya understood that the boy who had sold them out once was now standing in front of her paying everything back at interest. "He's ordered the workshop burned tonight. Tonight, Freya. And he's taken your father."

The room did not spin, though she would remember afterward wishing it had, because spinning would at least have explained the way the floor seemed to tilt under her feet. Her father. Aldous Venn, who had spent thirty years ruling his ledgers like a small gray kingdom, whose hands had taught hers to hold a pen straight, who had gone quiet and bent since the requisition but never broken—taken.

"Where."

"The cellar. Garrison cellar. They walked him out through the market an hour ago, past the fish stalls, so everyone would see. It wasn't an arrest, Freya. It was an announcement."

*So everyone would see.* Yes, that was Ilsen's grammar. He wrote his threats in public, in the language of processions and requisition papers stamped twice at the corner. Freya looked down at her hands—soot to the knuckle, a fresh split along the heel of her palm where the copying had worn the skin through—and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that the time for hiding things inside book bindings was over.

"How long until dark?"

"Six hours. Maybe five. They're stacking pitch barrels behind the chandlery already. I saw them. Soldiers, not laborers—they want to be seen doing it too."

Freya stood. Around her the workshop rose in its familiar dusk-smelling rows: the presses, the drying racks, the great flat stones where paper was smoothed, and everywhere, sewn into spines and slipped beneath endpapers, hidden in the hollows of false backs, the copied names of four hundred families. Four hundred promises, distributed through the town like seeds in winter wheat.

"Tomas," she said. "Listen to me very carefully, because we will only say this once and then we will do it."

He nodded. His eyes were wet but his chin held, and she thought—not for the first time, but for the first time without anger—that her father had chosen his apprentice well after all, or perhaps the boy had simply grown into the shape of the choice.

"You take the barge route. All the finished copies—all of them, every binding, every loose signature—down to the quay and onto Noor's barge. Tell the ferryman it's lamp oil if anyone asks, tell him it's your grandmother's bones, tell him anything, but those pages go on that boat. Noor knows the manifest. Go now, go the back lanes, and do not come back here. Whatever happens here tonight, you do not come back."

"And you?"

"I'm going to get my father."

* * *

The town of Haldenmere spent its last ordinary afternoon performing its own forgetting.

That was how Freya experienced it, moving through the streets with a laundry basket on her hip—a prop, a disguise, the oldest trick in a town that had survived three occupations by knowing how to look busy and harmless. The baker was still selling bread. Two old men still argued over dice outside the cooperage. A woman beat a rug from an upstairs window with slow, resigned thwacks, as if even the dust were tired. And threaded through all of it, thin as wire: soldiers. At the corners. At the well. Walking the quay in pairs with their collars up against the wind that came off the water carrying, faintly, the smell of pitch.

They were going to burn the smell into the town forever, Freya thought. Every year afterward, when someone lit a tallow candle wrong, some neighbor would flinch.

She kept her eyes down and her pace dull and let the basket do its work, and inside the folded linen at the bottom of it rode the one thing she could not send ahead with Tomas: her father's traveling coat, the good one, and beneath it the small packet she had made at dawn without letting herself think about what it meant—his spectacles, his spare nibs, the little tin of his own black ink he had mixed the year Freya was born and guarded like relics ever since. If he was going to be taken out of Haldenmere, he would go out as a ledger-keeper, not a prisoner. Some dignities you could smuggle.

The garrison occupied the old customs house at the head of the quay, a fat gray building with windows too tall for its walls, and it was there, in the shadow of the rope-walk opposite, that Freya allowed herself to stop and look, really look, for the first time since the bells.

Two sentries at the door. One more around the side, smoking. A wagon backed up to the loading step, half-loaded with document crates—her father's crates, she realized; the registry's supporting records, the correspondence, the indexes, everything that proved the names had ever been written—and a clerk she half-recognized checking items off a list with the serene brutality of a man inventorying a drowned ship.

And through a ground-floor window, bars thick as a wrist: her father, sitting on a bench, hands folded in his lap, perfectly still.

He was not pacing. He was not pleading. He sat the way he sat at the binding table when a difficult signature required three hours of steady breath—gathered, patient, entirely present. Even at this distance Freya could see that he had arranged himself deliberately, that sitting still was the last work available to him and he was doing it well. Only his eyes moved, tracking the soldiers, the wagon, the light. Counting, always counting. Measuring the afternoon in whatever currency was left.

Something in Freya's chest went hot and clean and terribly simple.

She had spent the whole war being careful. Forging papers in the second-floor room with the shutters drawn, learning which neighbors could keep secrets and which needed to be lied to gently, holding her face smooth through inspections and interrogations and the long gray years of her father's silences. Carefulness had saved forty families and cost her every easy night's sleep she'd ever owned. But careful was a strategy for weather you expected, and the sky above Haldenmere had gone the color of iron shavings, and the forecast was ash, and her father was behind bars with six hours of daylight left.

Careful was over. What came next would have to be true.

* * *

She found Noor at the barge.

Or rather, she found the aftermath of Noor at the barge—Tomas white-faced on the deck, the ferryman swearing in two languages, crates repositioned twice and repositioned again, and Noor standing at the gangplank with the manifest crushed in one fist, looking like a person who has just been informed the floor was never load-bearing.

"Noor."

Noor turned, and Freya watched the whole argument happen in that face in the space of a breath—relief first, fierce and unguarded, then fear, then the terrible arithmetic of a courier measuring whether love was a thing that could be abandoned in a ditch. Freya had learned to read that arithmetic weeks ago, on the night they had first shared the secret of the registry, and she had learned something else too: that Noor always lost that calculation. That every time, without meaning to, Noor chose the heavier cargo.

"You've heard," Noor said.

"Burning tonight. Father in the cellar." Freya climbed the plank, took the manifest from Noor's hand, and smoothed it flat against a crate. "How many pages are aboard?"

"Three hundred and twelve families. The rest are still in the bindings—we couldn't finish, the last forty are drying, they need another day—" Noor stopped. Both of them heard the absurdity of *another day* land between them like a dropped tool.

"Then the last forty stay in the books, and the books go on the boat, and we cut them out later on the water." Freya heard her own voice come out level, clipped, a stranger's voice, and realized with distant surprise that it was her father's voice, his ordering voice, the one he used when the presses ran hot and panic wanted the room. Inheritance arrives strangely. "Tomas. You're staying with the boat. Ferryman, low tide is at moonrise—be ready at moonrise, not after, and if the patrol comes to the quay you are loading lamp oil for the garrison and you are bored."

"And you?" Noor said again, softer this time, and this time it was not a question about logistics.

"I'm going into the customs house."

"Freya—"

"He sat still for me," she said. "Did you see him? Through the window? He sat the way he sits when he's waiting for a difficult page to dry. He's waiting for me, Noor. He has been waiting for me since they walked him through the market, and he will sit there until the smoke comes down, because Aldous Venn does not leave a ledger unfinished, and I am the unfinished part." Her voice caught on the last word, finally, one hairline crack across the level surface, and Noor crossed the deck and took her hands—the soot-blackened, split-skinned, shaking hands—and held them the way you hold a compass.

"Then we go together," Noor said. "I know the cellar layout. There's a coal chute off the rope-walk side; I mapped it the first week, in case. In case of exactly this." A ghost of the old courier's grim humor. "I was always planning to steal something from that building. It might as well be your father."

For one moment—one breath's length, no longer—Freya let herself lean into those hands and be twenty-four years old and terrified. Then she straightened, and the moment closed over like water, and the resolve underneath it held.

"Moonrise," she said. "Both of us, at this boat, at moonrise. With him. Whatever it costs."

* * *

The last hour of daylight, Freya spent at her father's bench.

It made no practical sense. The copies were packed; the tools that mattered were wrapped in oilcloth in her basket; the workshop was already dead, its presses cold, its racks stripped. But she went through it anyway, room by room, touching things—the smoothing stone still bearing the shallow wear of thirty years of palms, the wall where her height had been marked each birthday in pencil until she outgrew the ritual but never the marks, the narrow stair to the second floor where a stranger had once slept with shaking hands and where Freya had left bread without a word, because that was the first language she had ever learned.

She had thought she would feel grief. What she felt instead was inventory. Every surface said: *I was useful. I held what mattered.* And beneath that, quieter, in her father's hand: *What is kept is not what matters. What matters is carried.*

At the door she paused, took the pencil from her apron—the stub she had used for the height-marks, for marginalia, for a thousand small unsanctioned notes—and on the back of the requisition notice nailed there, in quick sure strokes, she wrote the only epitaph the workshop would get:

*Names weigh more than paper.*

Then she pulled her father's coat tighter around her shoulders, settled the basket on her hip like any woman walking home through a town on the edge of its own burning, and stepped out into the smoky gold of the last light.

Across the roofs, the garrison bell rang the hour. Five hours to moonrise. Somewhere behind the chandlery, the pitch barrels waited in their neat official rows, and somewhere behind barred glass, her father sat with his hands folded, counting.

Freya turned toward the rope-walk, toward the coal chute, toward Noor already waiting in the shadow with a crowbar and a face like flint, and began to walk—not fast, not slow, but at exactly the pace of a woman with nothing in the world left to hide.

The first barrel was rolled out while she was still crossing the square.
