## 19. The Burning of the Workshop

The fire announced itself the way storms do on the mere—not with light first but with a change in the pressure of the air, a tightening behind the ears. Noor was three streets from the workshop when the sky over the eastern quarter turned the color of a healing bruise, and by the time they reached the tanner's lane the glow had climbed above the rooftops and begun to breathe.

They had run from the barge with their lungs full of river cold, and now the heat came to meet them, rolling down the lane in waves that smelled of pitch, of scorched paper, of the peculiar sweetness of glue and leather surrendering all at once. The workshop's great sign—*VENN & SON, LEDGERS, FINE BINDING*—had already fallen into the flames. Noor heard it go: a groan of iron brackets letting go, then a bright crackling rush as the painted board became light.

"Freya—" Noor caught her arm at the corner, because Freya had gone rigid, watching her father's life's work eat itself.

"The cellar," Freya said. Her voice was flat with purpose, which Noor had learned in ten days of paper meant she was more frightened than she would ever say. "He's held in the garrison cellar. Under the guardhouse. If the fire spreads down Windrow Street it takes the guardhouse with it, and no one will open the doors for a man who's already written off."

Noor did the arithmetic they had been doing since the border road, since the flooded fields, since the checkpoint where a soldier's lantern had swung within a hand's width of the dispatch case: how much can be carried, what must be left in the ditch. Aldous Venn weighed nothing next to the registry. Aldous Venn was worth every copied page.

* * *

The garrison guardhouse stood at the end of Windrow like a dark tooth, and around it the town was dissolving into its own panic. Carts jammed the mouth of the street; a woman screamed a child's name into the smoke; two militiamen were hauling buckets toward the granary with the grim economy of men saving what they'd been ordered to save. Nobody looked at a courier and a ledger-keeper's daughter running against the current. Fire made everyone anonymous.

The cellar door was barred from without—a beam set in iron brackets—and the single guard posted there was not a soldier at all but an old town constable, helmetless, his face lit orange by the spreading glow. He had his keys out already. He was standing there looking at the door the way a man looks at a thing he cannot decide whether to do.

"Constable." Noor slowed, raised both hands slightly, palms out—the old courier's gesture, harmless, official-ish. "Who ordered the prisoner released?"

"No one ordered it." The constable's voice was hoarse. "That's just it. No order either way. Captain Ilsen marched the garrison to the fire lines an hour ago. Left me with one instruction: keep Venn secure until the officer of the day returns. But the officer of the day is fighting sparks on the mill road, and the wind's shifted, and this whole street is going to go up before he comes back." He looked at the beam, then at them, then back at the beam, as if the wood might advise him. "If I open it, I've freed a state prisoner."

"If you don't," Freya said quietly, "you've burned one."

The arithmetic hung in the smoke between them. Noor thought, absurdly, of Tomas, of forged discharge papers, of the baker sliding pages into flour sacks—everyone in Haldenmere these past weeks doing sums with their consciences as counters. The constable was doing his sum now. Noor could see it move behind his eyes like water finding a level.

"My wife's name," the constable said abruptly, "is in that registry. Third page of the fourth volume. Marta Kessel, née Brenner." He spat on his palms and took up the beam. "I never saw you. Go."

The beam came free with a shriek of iron. The door opened onto darkness and the smell of wet stone, and then Aldous Venn was climbing the steps out of it—thinner than Noor remembered, one eye swollen half-shut, hands trembling in a way that had nothing to do with age—but alive, alive, blinking at the orange sky like a man surfacing from deep water.

"Papa—"

"They took my glasses," Aldous said, in the tone of a man cataloguing grievances because the alternative was weeping. Then he saw the fire over the rooftops and stopped cataloguing. His face went through something Noor would carry forever: grief arriving whole, unannounced, in a single breath.

"The copies are safe," Freya told him, gripping his arm. "Tomas has them. Every volume. They're on the barge or going to it."

"The originals—"

"Burning," Noor said. There was no kinder word.

Aldous Venn nodded slowly, as though confirming a calculation. "Then the ink has done its work," he said. "Come. Away from here. This street will be embers by midnight."

But Noor was already turning back toward the fire.

* * *

It made no sense. Noor knew it made no sense even while walking toward it, and later—in another country, in another life—they would try to explain it to Freya and fail, and finally settle on the truth, which was simply: *I could not let the last thing I ever did for the republic be nothing.*

The workshop's front wall still stood, a black lattice against the furnace of the interior, and through its empty window frames Noor could see the registry hall. The long tables had gone to charcoal. The binding press had toppled, its screw warped. And along the far wall, where the great shelving had sagged and split, the final volumes lay in a drift of flame-edged paper—thousands of names curling at their edges like leaves in autumn, each line of ink lifting off the page as the page itself went gold, then grey, then gone.

Noor went in through the side door, low, where the storeroom had once held their sleeping pallet and Freya's hidden bread. The heat hit like a hand pressed to the chest. Smoke lay in layers, and Noor moved beneath the worst of it, crouched, one sleeve over their mouth, and reached the edge of the shelving where the nearest volumes had not yet caught.

One volume. That was all a person could carry out of a fire like this. Volume four, third page, Marta Kessel née Brenner—no, that name was safe, copied, delivered. Noor's hands moved anyway, pulling the book free of the shelf with a sound like tearing cloth, and it was heavier than any dispatch case they had ever carried across any border, because it was not paper now but everything the paper had promised and the republic had rescinded, and Noor held it against their chest and ran.

The ceiling groaned. Somewhere behind, the great window frames folded inward with a sigh, admitting the night air in a rush that fed the fire to a new pitch. Noor felt rather than heard the first timber let go, and then they were through the side door, out into blessed cold, the volume hugged tight, their eyebrows crisped, their coat smoking at the shoulder, and Freya was there—Freya was always there—pulling them by the sleeve into the alley, both of them stumbling, laughing the terrible laughter of people who have done something stupid and lived.

"You went back for *paper*," Freya gasped.

"I went back for names."

Aldous took the volume from them with shaking hands, opened it against his knee despite the dark, and read a few lines by firelight alone. Then he closed it gently, the way he closed ledgers at the end of a working day, and handed it back. "Keep it," he said. "You're the better courier."

* * *

The quay was chaos braided with grace. Boats were putting out from the water-stairs, loaded with whatever Haldenmere had decided was worth saving—children, hens, photograph frames, a sewing machine rowed out into the dark by two determined sisters. The barge waited at the far mooring, riding low, Tomas a small frantic silhouette aboard it, counting crates of copied pages the way other men counted coins.

And between the water-stairs and the mooring, alone, unhurried, stood Captain Ilsen.

He had lost his cap. His coat was ash-grey down one side, and there was a cut above his brow that he had not bothered to have seen to, and he held a pistol loosely at his thigh in the manner of a man holding it because regulations said so and not because his heart was in it. Noor saw him from twenty paces and felt the floor of the world drop exactly as it had dropped the night the second seal broke: the certainty of the ending, arriving early.

"Go to the barge," Noor said.

"He'll shoot you."

"He won't." Noor did not know where the certainty came from—perhaps from the dinner, months ago it seemed, the wine and the careful questions and the moment Ilsen had looked at the candle flame and said, very quietly, *In the last war I burned a village that was already empty. It took an hour. Nothing about it was necessary.* Perhaps from that. "Take your father. Take the volume. Get them aboard."

Freya's hand tightened on their sleeve—one heartbeat, two—and then she pressed their knuckles briefly to her lips, a whole argument compressed into a kiss, and went.

Noor walked the last twenty paces alone, empty-handed, palms open, the firelight making long shadows of them both across the planking of the quay.

"Courier," Ilsen said.

"Captain."

"You're carrying nothing."

"That's right."

"I have orders." Ilsen's voice was level, almost gentle, the voice of a man reciting a lesson learned long ago. "Persons to detain. A courier's name on a list. A certification signed in the wrong hand—I'm not a fool, Eastwick, I've been reading ledgers longer than you've been carrying them. Three days bought with a false date and a clerk's slant on the capital letter." He looked past Noor, at the burning quarter, at the sky gone the color of an open wound. "You voided my paperwork. You emptied my cellar. You're stealing state records off a barge in my harbor."

"Yes," Noor said. There was nothing else to say, and it seemed suddenly, magnificently irrelevant whether it was enough.

Ilsen looked at them for a long moment. The fire roared somewhere behind; a roof fell; sparks rose like a reversal of snow. And Noor watched the captain do the sum—watched it move behind his eyes, water finding its level, the same sum the constable had done, the same sum Tomas had done, the whole town of Haldenmere in these last weeks nothing but conscience and arithmetic—and then Ilsen holstered the pistol.

"There will be an inquiry," he said. "Someone will have to account for tonight. For the delayed certification. For the guardhouse door. For why a garrison captain was occupied at the fire lines while prisoners escaped and records walked out of his jurisdiction." He drew himself straighter, and Noor understood, with a feeling close to vertigo, what was being assembled in front of them. "Ilsen, R., captain of garrison, accepts responsibility for the failure of the requisition at Haldenmere. That will be my report. Written tonight. In my own hand."

"You don't have to—"

"It's the only signature left to me that means anything." Something crossed his face that was not quite a smile—tiredness perhaps, or relief wearing tiredness's clothes. "You taught me that, courier, though you didn't mean to. Everything else I've signed this year was lies. Let this one be true." He stepped aside—an ordinary step, two feet of weathered plank, the smallest movement in the world—and the way to the barge lay open. "The tide turns within the hour. After that, patrols on the river road. Go."

Noor stood a moment longer. Words offered themselves—thanks, absolution, argument—and none of them fit. At last Noor said only: "Marta Kessel. Fourth volume, third page. She's on the barge's list too."

Something moved in the captain's ruined face. "I know," he said. "My sister-in-law. Ninth page." He turned back toward the fire. "Go, Eastwick. You're making my report late."

* * *

The barge pulled away from the mooring into a river full of light, the whole burning town reflected upside down beneath them, so that they seemed to glide over a second Haldenmere made entirely of flame and slowly drowning. Freya sat amidships with the salvaged volume in her lap and her father's head heavy against her shoulder, already asleep or pretending to be, and Tomas stood at the tiller with tears cutting clean tracks through the soot on his face and did not wipe them.

Noor took the steering oar beside him and put the current under them.

Behind, the workshop went at last—came down all at once in a bloom of sparks, the roof folding, the walls exhaling a column of embers that rose and rose until it mingled with the actual stars. Freya watched it without flinching, one hand resting on the cover of the book, and Noor watched her watch it, and understood that what rose from that fire was not destruction at all but release: the republic's promise, burned at last to its truth. Not the paper. Never the paper. The names, which had already gone out into the world by flour sack and ferry and memory and hand.

"Where first?" Freya asked softly, when the sparks had thinned to drifting motes on the water. "Tomorrow. When we land. Whose door do we knock on first?"

Noor thought of the lists. The routes mapped against orders, the families within reach, the ones who would flee and the ones who would refuse. Thought of the pages pressed into hands, the warnings whispered, the work finished imperfectly, in time.

"Marta Kessel," they said, and put the oar over, and the barge turned its bow toward the far shore, toward the doors, toward everything that remained to be delivered.
