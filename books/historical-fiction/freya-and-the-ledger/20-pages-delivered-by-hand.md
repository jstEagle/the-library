## 20. Pages Delivered by Hand

*Noor.*

The town was burning the way paper burns—not all at once, but in decisions. A shutter caught here. An eave there. The workshop was a column of orange against the river-dark sky, and the wind off the water carried its ashes north over the rooftops, so that it snowed char in Haldenmere, small black flakes settling on doorsteps like the opposite of a blessing.

Noor ran through it with a satchel against their chest and the satchel's strap crossed twice, the way they'd crossed it on every road of the war, and for the first time the weight of it felt holy instead of cursed.

The pages inside were Freya's work and Noor's work and the baker's hiding and the ferryman's memory and Aldous Venn's ruined eyes going over each name one last time before his hands were taken—pages copied by candlelight until the ink ran out and then copied in wine and soot, pages that smelled faintly of the workshop's glue and now, increasingly, of smoke.

Three days. The forged certification had bought three days, and two of them were gone, and the third was spending itself fast.

The first door Noor knocked on was the Maret family's, the tallow-chandlers, and when Maren Maret opened it with a candle stub in her hand and ash already in her hair, Noor did what they had rehearsed on the quay: pressed the folded page into her palm, closed her fingers over it with both of their own, and said the words they had agreed on.

"Your names are in the registry. The republic promised you passage and has broken the promise. The army is coming for the lists tonight, and after the lists, the people on them. Go to the ferry. Grete is running it until the light fails."

Maren stared at the page. Her thumb found her husband's name, her daughters', moved along the column like a woman reading scripture she had never expected to be addressed to her.

"This is the ledger," she said. "This is *the* ledger."

"It's yours now. That's the whole point of it. It was always supposed to be yours."

Inside the house a child was crying—not from fear, from being awake past bedtime—and Maren turned and shouted something up the stairs, and the sound that came back down was the sound of a family beginning to move, drawers opening, the thump of a sack hitting a floor.

Noor was already at the next door.

That was the arithmetic of the night: no lingering. Linger and the pages stopped. Linger and the questions started, and there was no time to answer why a courier of the republic was handing out its secrets like alms, why the daughter of the ledger-keeper was going door to door with smoke in her braids, why anyone should believe two strangers in a burning town.

They didn't linger. They knocked, they pressed, they whispered, they ran.

The Widow Aske would not open her door. She spoke through it, her voice thin and level as a plane drawn across wood.

"I heard about your pages. Tomas told me this morning, gods keep him. I'm not going."

"Mother Aske, your son's name is on the list. Your granddaughters' names are—"

"My son is buried behind the chapel and my granddaughters are in Vessany's capital living under other people's papers, which is exactly what I intend to go on doing until I die in my own bed." A pause. The bolt did not slide, but neither did it shoot home. "Leave the page. I'll wrap fish in it if nothing else."

Noor slid it under the door. Their hand stayed flat on the wood a moment longer than efficiency allowed.

"There's room on the barge," they said to the door. "If you change your mind. Until dawn."

The door said nothing. Doors never do.

By the third street Noor had stopped counting refusals separately from acceptances. They were both deliveries. That was the thing nobody told you about carrying names—you didn't get to decide what happened after you set them down. You only got to decide not to be the kind of courier who never sets anything down at all.

The chapel bell began to ring—not the hour, not alarm, but the old evacuation pattern, three and two and three, which meant the priest had been warned, which meant Grete or the baker or one of the dozen others had done their part of the night's conspiracy. The sound rolled over the rooftops and under the smoke, and doors began to open ahead of Noor before Noor reached them, faces appearing, hands reaching.

Word travels faster than fire when the word is *run*.

At the corner by the cooperage, where the whole western sky was the color of a forge, Noor stopped to catch breath and saw, halfway down the lane, another figure doing exactly what Noor was doing—a woman moving door to door, quick and certain, pressing paper into hands, and even at this distance, even in this light, there was no mistaking the set of her shoulders, the economy of her reach.

Freya. Working her side of the street. And between them, the whole map of Haldenmere coming apart into families in motion.

Noor's chest did something complicated and unhelpful, and they made themselves turn away and knock on the next door, because love could wait its turn; the list could not.

* * *

*Freya.*

Her father had taught her that a name written down was a promise, and her mother—who she barely remembered—had taught her, in whatever way mothers teach things without meaning to, that promises kept by other people were still your responsibility while they lasted. So Freya had spent the war learning both sides of ink: the official ledgers downstairs, and upstairs, in the good light, the forged papers that let neighbors become someone the republic hadn't decided to hurt yet.

Tonight was both trades at once. Tonight the whole town held a page in its hands.

She had given away forty-one copies. She knew because she'd made sixty-two, and she counted everything, always had—it was how you survived a forgery bench, counting—and the number ticked in her head with every doorstep like a second heartbeat.

The Ferro family took theirs and left immediately, the sons already loaded, the mother weeping without sound, which Freya understood to be the most efficient kind. Old Pell took his, read it by torchlight in the doorway, and laughed—a cracked, astonished laugh—and said, "I thought they'd forgotten us years ago. Turns out they remembered just long enough to break their word properly. At least it's in writing." Then he went inside to wake his sister, and Freya moved on, because you moved on, because grief could be braided with purpose but purpose had to hold the weight or the whole rope gave.

The Hallorans refused. All of them. Patriarch, wife, three grown sons standing in their doorway like a wall with opinions.

"We farm that land since before the republic," Halloran said. "Where does it say on your page that anyone feeds us wherever we run to?"

"It doesn't say that anywhere," Freya admitted. "It says the republic promised to protect you. It doesn't say the republic will. That's why I'm here instead of the republic."

He looked at the page in his hand for a long moment, and Freya watched him do the arithmetic she hated—the same arithmetic Tomas had drowned in, the same everyone traded, everyone weighed—and then he folded the page very carefully, put it inside his coat, next to his heart, and shook his head.

"We'll keep the names," he said. "We'll keep the land too. If soldiers come for us, they'll find out which was heavier."

There was nothing to say to that, so Freya said, "There's a page for each of you. Don't lose them," and walked on, and did not look back, because looking back was how couriers became mourners, and she wasn't finished working.

At the end of Tanner's Row she found the house she had been saving for last, the way you save the hard letter in the stack. The Doss house. Sela Doss answered the door herself, ninety if she was a day, straight-backed, one lamp.

"Child," Sela said, "you're covered in ash."

"I have something for you." Freya's voice did something she didn't authorize. She cleared her throat. "Your name's in the registry. It's been in the registry eleven years. Since the eastern evacuations."

Sela Doss stood very still.

"Eleven years," she repeated.

"The republic promised you resettlement. Full compensation. There's an entry number and everything. My father wrote it himself—he remembers writing it. He said—" Freya stopped, because her father had said, once, drunk on one glass of plum brandy, *the ones I can't stop thinking about are the ones whose entries will never be honored, I write them as beautifully as I can, it's the only thing I can give them,* and none of that belonged in this doorway tonight. "He said you mattered enough to write carefully."

Sela took the page in both hands. She read her own name the way you read a letter from someone dead.

"I packed a bag eleven years ago," she said. "It's still under my bed. I've repacked it every spring." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and enormous. "Do you know what it does to a person, keeping a bag packed for eleven years?"

"No," Freya said honestly. "But I think I'm finding out what it does to take it out."

Sela Doss went inside and came back with a canvas bag, faded, mended at the strap, packed tight and neat. She locked her front door behind her with the ceremony of a woman closing a very long book.

"Walk me to the ferry," she said. "And tell me about your young man. Or young woman. I don't care which, but I care very much about the details."

* * *

*Noor.*

The east quarter was where the night turned cruel.

Not because of the fire—the fire stayed west, hungry for the workshop district and the grain stores, roaring like a patron saint gone wrong—but because of the garrison patrol that swept Meridian Street with lanterns and a list of its own, turning families back from the riverside lanes, directing them to the "assembly point" in the market square, and everyone in Haldenmere who had read a requisition order in the last month knew what assembly points were for.

Noor ducked into a doorway and watched a lantern swing closer, and did the courier's oldest calculation: routes, timing, what can be abandoned in a ditch.

Then the calculation broke, because a boy darted across the patrol's path with a bundle of pages under his shirt, and it was Tomas—Tomas who had sold them out, Tomas who had paid his debt differently, Tomas who should have been on the barge by now—distracting the lanterns with the world's least convincing drunk act while a family slipped down an alley toward the water.

A soldier grabbed his collar. Noor was moving before deciding to move, sliding out of the doorway with their merchant's pass already in hand, wearing the face they wore at checkpoints.

"Evening," Noor said pleasantly, loudly, stumbling slightly into the lantern light. "Evening, sirs—is he with you? He's with me. Apprentice. Can't hold his drink." They got an arm under Tomas's elbow, felt him shaking, kept the pass high and steady. "Paperwork, if you need it. Merchant's pass, river trade. We're hauling the last of Venn's stock out tonight, lawful requisition salvage, all stamped."

The sergeant looked at the pass. Looked at Noor's face. Something moved behind his eyes—the particular tiredness of a man doing ugly work late—and he handed the pass back.

"Get to the quay and stay there," he said. "Curfew's real tonight."

It took two streets before Tomas could speak. "You came back for me."

"You came back for us first," Noor said. "We're even. Now go—barge, Grete, dawn. Don't argue with me, I outrank you in guilt."

Tomas laughed, wetly, and ran toward the water, and Noor stood a moment in the middle of the empty street with ash falling on their shoulders, and understood, with a strange calm, that this was what the whole war had been preparing them for: not the carrying, but the setting down. Not the escape, but the delivery.

The last house on their route was the midwife Yola's, and Yola took her page, kissed Noor on both cheeks, said, "I delivered half the babies whose names are on that thing, I'll spread the word myself, I know where everyone sleeps," and vanished into the dark like the professional she was.

Which left the quay. Which left the barge. Which left—

"Please tell me you're nearly done."

Freya, materializing out of the smoke-shadow with Sela Doss on one arm and her satchel hanging empty and flat against her hip. Empty. Every page delivered. Noor's own satchel had four left, and they handed them, wordlessly, to a passing neighbor of Yola's who swore she'd see them placed.

"All done," Noor said. "Imperfectly."

"There's no other way to finish anything." Freya's face was streaked black and pale where tears had cut channels through the ash, though Noor had seen her cry at nothing all night and suspected she didn't know it either. Behind her the workshop burned on, her father's life's work ascending in sparks, and she did not look at it, the way you don't look at a wound you've already bandaged. "The barge?"

"Grete's loading. Dawn tide. Room for pages"—they both heard it, the terrible phrase, the price made literal—"and for whoever fits."

Sela Doss, between them, snorted. "Room will be found. Boats always find room. It's towns that don't."

Down at the water, the bell rang again—three and two and three—and figures converged on the quay out of every alley: families with sacks, the baker dusted white and gray, old Pell and his sister, Tomas waiting with his head up like a man newly discharged from something. Grete stood at the gunwale counting souls aloud, refusing to hurry, because some kinds of counting are a form of care.

Freya's hand found Noor's in the dark, ash-slick, ink-stained, gripping hard.

"It's not everyone," she said quietly. "Aske wouldn't come. The Hallorans won't. Half the east quarter went to the square instead."

"I know."

"So it's finished, and it isn't finished."

"That's what finishing ever looks like," Noor said. "Come on. We deliver ourselves next."

They started down the slope toward the quay—and then Freya stopped so suddenly that Sela staggered, and her grip on Noor's hand went from holding to crushing.

"The cellar," she said. "My father isn't on the barge. He was detained—I went back for him and the garrison cellar was locked and I thought—there's no time, Noor, they took him before the fire, and if the fire reaches the granary the whole quay district—"

She was already turning, already running back toward the burning heart of the town, and Noor ran after her, because there had never really been a choice, not on any road, not since the first flooded mile—and above the rooftops the sparks rose thick as a blizzard, drifting toward the granary roofs.
