## 21. The Last Page

The river at night did not look like water. It looked like poured pewter, like something molten and slow, carrying the burned-orange smear of Haldenmere on its back the way a beast carries a wound. From the deck of the barge *Mercy of Saints*—a name painted on her hull by some long-dead optimist, since repainted twice over in cheaper white—the town was a line of light that should not have existed. Fires were not supposed to be beautiful. Noor had learned that years ago, on the flooded border road, watching a granary go up outside Vessany with the whole horizon lit like a festival. Beauty was the cruelest thing fire did.

Noor stood at the stern rail and counted the bargemen's lanterns as they moved along the quay: one, two, three, four. Four lanterns meant four loads still to come. Five would mean the baker's family had made it. Six, and the ferryman's sister with her bad hip and her three children. The arithmetic of lanterns. It was what Noor's life had become—not names now, not yet, because names were too heavy to hold while they were still being carried, but lights, distances, the number of trips left before dawn made the river unsafe and Ilsen's patrols resumed their circuits of the eastern bank.

Behind, in the low cabin under the cargo deck, Freya was sorting pages.

Noor could hear it through the open hatch: the soft shush of paper against paper, the small sounds she made when she worked, half-hummed, half-breathed, a habit Noor had first noticed across the binding table in the workshop's storeroom and had been helpless to ever unhear again. She sorted the way her father taught her, by district and then by family name, each stack squared against her knee, each stack wrapped in oilcloth against the river damp. Three hundred and eleven pages. Noor knew the number the way other people knew prayers.

The air smelled of smoke and tar and cold iron. Somewhere upstream a night bird called, three notes, wrong for the season. The current pulled at the hull with a patient hand.

*We are doing nothing*, Noor thought, and the thought was so foreign that it almost hurt. For twenty days—since the checkpoint on the border road, since the false merchant's pass with its forged seal and the saddlebag weighed every morning against what could be abandoned in a ditch—there had never once been nothing to do. There had been dispatches, seals, lies, candles burned to stubs, ink made from wine and lamp-soot, hands bleeding, pages hidden in flour sacks and hymnals and the false bottoms of cradles. There had been Tomas's face in the doorway saying *ten days, not a month*. There had been the confession at the binding table, and the terrible relief of it, and the question Freya asked that was better than forgiveness: *what will you do now?*

Now there was only the river, and the waiting, and the counting of lanterns.

Noor's hands would not accept the idleness. They hung at the rail wanting a pen, wanting paper, wanting the small steady labor that had saved a life Noor could name for every hour of work done. So instead they did the other thing hands do at rails: they remembered. The weight of the second dispatch, sealed in red wax, carried for six days without opening because opening it would make it real. The garrison clerk's ink-stained thumb. The certification document, and the forgery performed in a borrowed hand with a borrowed date, the courier's final betrayal of duty, and how steady that hand had been—steadier than it had ever been signing true things.

And beneath all of it, quieter than the current, the memory of the last page copied.

It had been two nights ago, in the workshop, with the smell of the army's kerosene already seeping up through the floorboards from the barrels stored in the cellar below. The transcription was nearly finished; only the final district remained, the western marsh families, the ones the republic had promised to lift out by ferry and had never once mentioned again after the third winter. Noor had copied their names in the registry's own careful format—name, district, date of entry, date of promised passage—in the hand that had grown fluent in the ledger's grammar over weeks of theft-by-candlelight. And when the last marsh name was done, when the page was full except for the empty ruled lines at its foot, Noor had sat very still for a long moment, pen lifted, listening to Freya breathe in the next room where she was melting soot into wine over the spirit lamp.

Then Noor had written two more entries.

Not hidden. Not coded. In the republic's own format, in the registry's own hand-style, entered as plainly as any marsh family: two names, side by side, dated the night of the confession at the binding table. Date of entry filled in. Date of promised passage left blank—and that blank had been the whole point, the entire argument, made in the only language the ledger spoke. Every other name in that book had been promised a passage and abandoned. These two names promised nothing except that they had existed, together, and that someone had chosen to write them down.

Noor had folded the page into the middle of the western district stack and told no one.

* * *

The hatch creaked, and Freya came up into the night with an oilcloth bundle under her arm and ink still shadowing the edge of her right hand, a crescent of it along the heel of her palm that no amount of river water would take off quickly. Her hair was down, which it almost never was during working hours, and the wind took it sideways across her face so that she had to keep pushing it back with the clean hand, an unconscious gesture that Noor had watched perhaps nine thousand times now and expected to watch nine thousand more.

"Three hundred and eleven," she said, sitting herself on a coil of rope beside the rail. "I've checked twice. The baker's stacks are complete, the ferryman's route list is separate like he asked, and your handwriting is getting worse, which is impressive, because I didn't think it could."

"It got worse on purpose. The worse it looks, the less it looks like mine."

"That is either clever or deranged."

"Both. Usually both." Noor shifted to make room, and she settled against the rail's other post, close enough that their shoulders touched through the coat. Neither of them commented on it. That language had stopped needing commentary weeks ago.

Below them the lanterns moved. Three now. Two loads left.

Freya unwrapped the nearest bundle to check the oilcloth's fold one more time—it was compulsive with her now, the checking, and Noor understood it completely, because Noor had spent a career checking things—and then paused. Her thumb stopped on the topmost page.

"This isn't the marsh district order," she said slowly. "These aren't squared. Someone shuffled this stack."

"I carried it down the ladder. It'll resquare."

"No." She was frowning now, the ledger-keeper's daughter, the woman who had spent a war forging clean papers while keeping her own loyalties folded so flat nobody in Haldenmere ever found a crease. Disorder offended her at a level below thought. "This is wrong. Look—here's Harrow and his household, but they should be with the ferry families, and this one—" She drew out a single sheet and turned it toward the stern lantern, and whatever she was going to say next died somewhere between her mouth and the river air.

Noor watched her read it. Watched her lips move slightly, forming the registry's column headers the way she always did when reading her father's formats, the inherited liturgy: *Name. District. Date of entry. Date of promised passage.* Watched her eyes reach the names themselves and stop, and stop again, the way a person stops on ice when they feel the crack travel out ahead of them.

"Noor." Her voice had gone very quiet. "These are ours."

"Yes."

"When did—" She looked up. The lantern light caught the ink crescent on her hand and the wind caught her hair again and she pushed it back without looking at it, all her attention fixed on Noor's face as though the page might be a forgery but the face could not be. "When did you copy this?"

"The last night. In the workshop. After you went to make the ink."

"You said you were finishing the marsh district."

"I was."

"And then you—" Freya looked down at the page again, and Noor saw her finger find the blank column, the empty space where the promised date should be, and rest there. Understanding moved across her face slowly, the way weather moves across the mere in the early morning, from the far shore inward. "You left it blank."

"I left it blank."

"Every other name in that book has a promise in this column," Freya said. "Every single one. That's what the ledger *was*. That's what the republic swore and then unsweared. A promise with a date attached, and then no date kept. And you put us in their book, in their format, in your hand, and you left the promise column empty."

"Not empty," Noor said. "Unwritten."

Freya was silent. The river worked at the hull. One lantern on the quay went out—a load delivered, a family aboard—and Noor counted it automatically, the habit of arithmetic refusing to die even now.

"You should have told me," Freya said at last. But there was no accusation in it. Noor heard instead the shape of something else, something she was still building the words around.

"I almost didn't write it at all." Noor turned to face her fully, back to the water now, back to the burning town and its beautiful cruel light. "I sat there for—I don't know how long. Because I thought, who am I to enter names? I'm the courier. I'm the one who carried the order that said you were all expendable. I certified a destruction, or nearly did; I forged my way out of certifying it, but the hands that held that pen were the same hands either way. What right does that hand have to add anyone to the book of the protected?" Noor looked down at those hands, pale on the dark rail. "And then I thought about what the ledger actually was. Not the republic's promise. That was always the lie—that it belonged to them, that the promises were theirs to make and unmake. Aldous told me, that night he rallied, remember? He said the book was only ever as strong as the people willing to carry its pages. The republic just wrote in it. We're the ones who made it true."

"So you made us true," Freya said.

"I made a record. That we existed. That on the night I finally told you everything—all of it, the rescinded orders, the lie I'd carried, the worst of me—that you were still there at dawn. That seemed like a thing worth writing down. In the proper format. Because if it's worth doing, it's worth doing in the registry's hand." Noor tried to smile and found it came out crooked. "Your father's rules. He'd be horrified at the content and proud of the penmanship."

Freya did not laugh. She looked at the page for a long time, and the wind dropped for a moment, and the fires across the water made her face warm-colored and still, and Noor saw that her eyes were bright in the way that people's eyes get when they are holding something very carefully and do not trust their hands yet.

"Do you know what my father would actually say?" she said finally. "He'd say the entry is invalid. Date of promised passage cannot be left blank; the column exists for a reason; an incomplete record is worse than no record, because it claims authority it hasn't earned. He drilled that into me before I could write a straight line. *A ledger that half-promises is a cruelty, Freya. It lets a person hope on insufficient evidence.*"

"He's not wrong."

"He's usually not wrong." Freya unfolded the page fully now, smoothed it against her knee, and Noor realized she was seeing it properly for the first time—not skimming, but reading, the way she read everything, completely, the way she had read Noor across a binding table before either of them had spoken a word aloud. "But my father also taught me that a keeper's job doesn't end at entry. Entry is just the beginning of the record. Anyone can enter a name." Her finger moved to the blank column again, hovering above it. "Only the person making the promise can fill the date."

The night bird called again, three notes. Downriver, the last lantern on the quay began to move toward the barge, and Noor heard the punt poles of the returning boat knock softly against the pilings—two loads left, then one—but none of that reached Freya, whose whole attention had narrowed to the page and the space in it the way her attention narrowed over a forgery, everything else in the world going soft and distant while the essential thing grew sharp enough to cut.

"I don't have a pen," she said.

Noor had one. Of course Noor had one; there was a stump of pencil in the inner pocket, and a nub of black wax crayon taken from the workshop in the last hour before the soldiers came, and a steel-nibbed pen wrapped in cloth against rust, because a courier learns to measure possessions by what can be abandoned in a ditch and decides, somewhere on a flooded road, that certain things will never be in that category. Noor took out the pen and unwrapped it and did not offer it.

"If you write it," Noor said, and found that speaking required more air than the night contained, "it has to be a real promise. With a real date behind it. Not the republic's kind. Not a column filled in because the form requires it. My father—" Noor stopped. Started again. "The people who raised me taught me to carry things. Dispatches, seals, lies. You carry a thing by forgetting you're a person; you're only your cargo. I did that for years and it worked, right up until it didn't, right up until a woman caught me looking too long at an open book and left bread outside my door without a word. Everything I know about being a person instead of a parcel, I learned from you. So if you write a date in that column, I need it to be the kind of promise that survives the thing that killed all the others. Not a government. Not an army. Just two people, deciding, on a barge, in the smoke of everything we couldn't save. Can that kind hold?"

Freya took the pen.

"You're asking the wrong person," she said. "I forge documents for a living. I know exactly how little paper weighs." She uncapped the ink bottle Noor hadn't noticed her bringing up from the cabin—the last of the wine-and-soot mixture, half gone, the smell of it sharp and sweet and ruined—and set the page flat on the oilcloth on her knee. "But I also know this: every name in that ledger was worthless the moment the republic treated it as paper. And every one of them became priceless the day we carried it to a door and pressed it into a hand. Paper is worth exactly what we do with our bodies to keep it. That's the only exchange rate there's ever been." She bent over the page. "So I'm not filling in a column. I'm telling you what I intend to spend."

The nib scratched. In the registry's hand, in the ledger's grammar, in letters small and exact and unmistakably hers, Freya Galloway Venn wrote the date. Not yesterday's date. Not the date of the confession, though that one stayed where Noor had entered it, marking when the record began. She wrote tomorrow's date—the day the barge would make the far bank, the day the copies would start crossing into other hands, the first morning of whatever came after Haldenmere.

She handed the pen back and blew on the ink, and neither of them spoke until it dried.

"There," she said quietly. "Complete record. Valid entry. Two names, promised passage, dated." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and her voice was steady. "Witnessed by the river, the fire, and one insomniac bargeman who is absolutely pretending not to watch us from the tiller."

Noor laughed—the sound cracked out of them, unexpected, enormous, the first laugh since the burning started—and pulled Freya in, and she came, folding the finished page with one hand and putting it inside Noor's coat, over the heart, patting it flat the way she patted every completed bundle: *done, true, carried.*

They stood at the rail together as the returning boat knocked against the hull and the last lanterns came aboard, and the town burned beautifully behind them, and ahead the river ran dark and patient toward the border and the morning.

It was Freya, later, wrapped in the one blanket between them, drowsing against Noor's shoulder, who said it—half asleep, in the tone of a ledger-keeper closing a book:

"We should check the marsh stacks one more time before dawn. And Noor—" a pause, her breathing evening out, "—the ferryman says there's a family waiting at the Millbrook landing who never got their page. He wants to know if we'll carry it ourselves, come morning. Door to door. One more delivery."

Noor looked down at the sleeping weight against their shoulder, at the ink-stained hand curled closed over the rail, at the page riding warm over their heartbeat, one promise among three hundred and eleven, dated and witnessed and true.

"Yes," Noor said to the dark, to the river, to the woman already dreaming. "Tell him yes."
