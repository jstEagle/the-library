## 22. Carried

The town across the border was called Wrenholt, and it smelled of tallow and river clay and, in the autumn, of apples rotting sweetly in the orchards no one had the hands to strip. Freya had learned its smells the way she had once learned Haldenmere's—by walking it at dawn, before anyone could ask her business, mapping escape routes out of habit long after there was nothing left to escape.

Seven years, and still she woke some mornings with her ears straining for boots on stairs.

The house she kept now stood at the end of a lane that dead-ended at the water, which she had chosen deliberately. One road in. One road out. A person who had watched a workshop burn learned to think in terms of doors. The house was small, stone at the base and timber above, with a lean-to shed that had once stored nets and now stored something else entirely.

She went to the shed first, as she did every morning, before the bread, before the kettle, before the window that looked out on the river where the light came up like slow brass.

Inside, along three shelves her own hands had planed and pegged, the archive waited in the dark.

It was not grand. Anyone expecting the great registry of Haldenmere—the thousand-page testament of Aldous Venn, the ledger that had made a river town famous across two kingdoms—would have been disappointed. These were copies of copies. Pages copied by candlelight from pages copied by candlelight, ink made sometimes from wine and soot when the real ink ran out, paper that had traveled in flour sacks and false book bindings and, in one memorable case, sewn into the lining of a ferryman's coat while he wore it across three checkpoints, sweating.

Forty-one families. That was the number Freya could account for, the number she had verified over seven years of letters and travelers' rumors and one extraordinary afternoon when a whole contingent of them had shown up in Wrenholt's square because someone's cousin had married someone's daughter and word traveled. Forty-one families whose names had gone into the fire's path and come out the other side, pressed into palms, whispered at doorways, carried by hand.

Some pages were originals—the ones delivered door to door on the night Haldenmere burned, returned to her afterward by families who understood what they were giving up. The Marta family's page, water-stained where a child had wept on it, or perhaps the river had gotten it; Freya had never asked. The Ostrander page, written in Aldous's careful clerk's hand before his fingers had failed him. The page for the Kessler twins, who were now, according to last spring's letter, apprenticed to a chandler in Marrowgate and argued constantly about wick thickness.

And beneath the shelf, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, the strangest document of all: a single sheet in Noor's handwriting, entered in the republic's own format, columns ruled straight and true, bearing two names and a date. She did not unwrap it often. It was enough to know it was there, the way it was enough to know the sun would come up behind the orchards.

She checked the shed's lock anyway—habit, habit, always habit—and went inside to put the kettle on.

* * *

The house filled slowly, the way it did every day, the way rivers fill from a hundred nameless streams.

It had started as necessity. In the first winter, a woman named Petra Ostrander had arrived with nothing but her mother's delivered page and a bruise on her cheekbone from the road, and Freya had fed her because that was what you did, because Freya of the second floor had spent a war leaving bread outside storeroom doors without a word, and some habits ran deeper than borders. Then Petra's sister came. Then a boy of sixteen who had crossed the mountains carrying nothing but a copy of his family's names folded against his heart, and who cried for two days and then turned out to be excellent with horses. Then more.

Wrenholt called it a boarding house. Officially, on the tax rolls the new kingdom required—which were, Freya noted with dry amusement, ledgers again, always ledgers, the world apparently incapable of counting human beings any other way—it was listed as *Galloway's House, lodgings, six beds*. The name had been Noor's idea, chosen in a market line when a clerk demanded one, plucked from the air: Galloway, for a grandmother Freya had never met, a woman who had died decades before the war and therefore could not be endangered by association.

Unofficially, the house was an archive, a waystation, and occasionally a forgery shop, though Freya's forging days had narrowed to papers of the most defensible kind—discharge records, travel passes, the occasional death certificate for someone who very much needed to be legally dead. She had learned the lesson of Tomas years ago and never forgotten it: everyone was trading something, survival was arithmetic, and the only question worth asking was whether your sums came out on the side of mercy.

Six people lived there full-time now. Petra, who kept the accounts and had opinions about everything. Young Col, the horse boy, grown into a young man of twenty-three who still slept with his boots by the door. The Widow Sarelle, who cooked and sang hymns slightly off-key and asked no questions, which in Freya's experience made her the most valuable person in the building. And old Danya, past seventy, half-blind, who had been a bookbinder in Vessany City before the republic fell and now rebacked volumes with trembling, perfect precision, training Freya's hands in arts her father would have recognized.

Aldous Venn would have recognized it too. That thought came to her every morning lately, more sharply as the anniversary approached. Her father had survived the fire and the cellar and the garrison, had crossed the border with them on the barge, wrapped in blankets among the pages he had spent his life ruling lines beneath—and then had lasted only four years in exile, his lungs full of smoke that had never quite cleared, dying in this same house in a bed positioned so he could see the river.

His last coherent words had been about margins. "Leave generous margins," he'd said, gripping her wrist with the ghost of his old clerk's strength. "Names need room to breathe."

So Freya left generous margins. On every copied page, every restored record, every forged pass. Room to breathe. Room, if the world ever turned kind enough, for additions.

* * *

She was kneading bread when the dog announced the road.

They had acquired the dog—Bram, an enormous shaggy thing of indeterminate breeding—precisely because of his announcing. He didn't bite, couldn't have caught a determined chicken, and feared umbrellas, but he had a voice like a landslip and deployed it for every arrival, friend or otherwise. Freya wiped her hands and listened the way she always listened: to the rhythm of the footsteps coming down the lane.

One set. Unhurried. Familiar weight, familiar gait, a slight drag on the left foot that had never fully healed from the quay, from the night Ilsen's soldiers—

No. Not that memory today. She let it pass through her and dissolve, the way she had taught herself to let the worst ones pass, and went out onto the step.

Noor came up the lane through the apple-light, leading the mule, which was laden with exactly nothing heavier than itself required. That was the joke between them, seven years old now: *carrying nothing heavy.* Noor had come back from supply runs to Haldenmere territory, to Marrowgate, even once all the way to the coast, with news and bread and salt and thread, and never—not once, by deliberate vow—with cargo that could be measured in danger. The courier's life was over. The courier's habits, they had both agreed, could stay.

"You're late," Freya called.

"The ferryman wanted to argue about politics." Noor reached the gate and leaned on it, and the sight of them—wind-burned, road-dusted, gray threading the dark hair at the temples in a way Freya found privately, shamelessly beautiful—loosened something in her chest that seven years had never quite managed to unclench permanently. "I let him win. It was cheaper than the argument."

"Was it?"

"No." Noor came through the gate, and Bram performed his ceremonial greeting, entire hindquarters involved, and Noor endured it with the patience of a person who had once walked into an occupied town with sealed dispatches and a face like still water, because Bram did not care about still water. "He was wrong about everything and I agreed with all of it. I've become a diplomat."

"You've become tired."

"I've become *home*," Noor said, correcting her gently, setting down the satchel on the step. "Tired is just what home looks like from the road."

Freya looked into the satchel out of seven years of reflex—bread, wrapped in cloth, still warm; salt in a twist of paper; a bundle of letters; a small clay pot of ink, good ink, black as a confession—and then made herself stop looking. Inventory was for other lives.

"News?" she asked.

Noor's face did something complicated. "Sit down news or stand-up news?"

"Stand up first."

"Marrowgate has a printing press now. An actual press. A man named Corvin is running pamphlets off it—history, testimony, whatever people bring him. He's calling it the Record Project." Noor paused. "He asked about us. About whether the Haldenmere pages survived, whether copies exist, whether anyone would be willing—" Another pause, the kind Noor used when choosing between truths. Noor had always paused there. Even now, even here, especially here, because the withholding habit had died hard and the choosing-to-speak habit had been built on its grave, deliberately, stone by stone, starting the night of the confession at the binding table. "Whether anyone would be willing to contribute to a printed edition. Names. Accounts. So it isn't forty-one families' private memory anymore. So it's history."

Freya stood very still in the doorway, flour on her hands, the smell of bread behind her and the river running silver at the end of the lane.

"Sit-down news," she said finally, and her voice was not entirely steady. "That's sit-down news."

"It gets better. Or worse. Depending." Noor sat on the step, heavily, the way the road permitted, and Freya sat beside her, their shoulders together, the old geometry of it. "There's a girl in Marrowgate. Nineteen, maybe twenty. Name of Ilse Venn-Kessler."

Freya stopped breathing.

"Her grandmother was one of the Kesslers," Noor said. "One of the twins' younger sisters. The family kept their delivered page—framed it, actually, which I find both excessive and correct—and the girl grew up with it on the wall. She's training as a typesetter. She wrote to Corvin asking if anyone knew who had carried the names out of Haldenmere. She said—" Noor's voice did the thing it did rarely, roughening at the edges. "She said her family tells the story that a courier and a ledger-keeper's daughter delivered their names by hand on the night the town burned, and that they have celebrated that date every year since, and that she would like to shake the hands that did it, if the hands still exist."

The yard was quiet. Bram had flopped down in the dust. Somewhere upstream a boat horn sounded, low and mournful, and the light moved on the water the way it always moved, indifferent and gorgeous, and Freya sat with her flour-white hands in her lap and let the enormity of it arrive.

A child. A child born after everything, who knew the story, who had it framed on her wall, who wanted to set it in movable type so it could never burn.

"She thinks we're a story," Freya said at last.

"We are a story," Noor said. "That's rather the point of the press."

"I'm covered in flour."

"So am I covered in road. We'll make a striking pair."

Freya laughed—the laugh that surprised her, that still did, that came out wet around the edges—and pressed her shoulder harder against Noor's, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was its own oldest language between them, older than the quarrels and the confessions, going back to bread left silently outside a storeroom door.

* * *

That evening, after supper—Sarelle's stew, Col's report on a lame mare, Petra's opinions rendered at length on the Marrowgate press ("a press prints what pays, Freya, mark me")—the house emptied toward sleep, and Freya took the lamp down the garden path to the shed.

Noor followed without being asked. This too was ritual.

She unlocked the door and lifted the oilcloth bundle from beneath the shelf and unwrapped it on the worktable, and the lamplight fell across the single page: two names in Noor's steady hand, ruled columns, generous margins, dated the night of the confession. *Entered in the registry of the Republic of Vessany,* read the header her father had designed, *as persons under protection and promise.*

The republic was dust. Its seals were curiosities in museums. Its promises had been lies the moment the second seal broke open on Noor's table in a burning town.

But the page remained.

"Do you ever regret it?" Freya asked, the way she asked maybe once a year, needing the answer renewed the way a wall needs repointing. "Signing the certification wrong. Burning the orders. All of it. You were dutiful, once. You could have been somebody's model courier."

Noor considered this with the seriousness Noor brought to everything, turning it over like a coin tested by teeth.

"I regret the parts where I lied to you," Noor said. "Day by day, week by week. I can still feel each of those days separately, like stones in a boot. But the rest—" Noor touched the edge of the page, not the names, never the names; that superstition had held for seven years. "The rest was the first time I understood what duty was actually for. Everything before was just obedience wearing duty's coat."

"And now?"

"Now I carry bread." Noor smiled, tired and lit from inside. "News. Salt. Nothing heavy."

Outside, the river went on talking to itself in the dark, the same water that had borne the barge, the pages, the three of them, away from the fire. Freya wrapped the page back in its oilcloth with hands her father would have praised—steady, precise, leaving room at the edges—and blew out the lamp, and took Noor's hand instead of the lamp's handle, and they walked back up the garden path together under a sky salted with stars, carrying nothing heavy, carrying everything that mattered.

In the morning, she decided, she would write to Marrowgate.
