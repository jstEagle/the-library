## 1. The Weight of Lead

My father taught me to weigh everything before I trusted it. A sheet of paper, he said, has a weight you can feel in your hand and a weight you cannot, and the second one is always heavier. He meant the words. He meant what a page could do once it left the shop—how it could travel through pockets and prayer books and the linings of coats, how it could whisper in a market square or shout from a pulpit, how it could get a man hanged or make him a hero, depending on who read it first and which way the wind was blowing that week.

I was nine years old when he put my fingers on a piece of type for the first time. *Feel that,* he said. *That's lead. That's the heaviest thing in this shop, and it's also the lightest, because a single letter weighs nothing at all until you set it next to another one.* I didn't understand him then. I understand him now. Everything I know about survival, I learned from metal and paper.

The shop sits on Tanner's Lane, wedged between Marta's dye house and a cooper's yard that smells of oak shavings and sour wine. The sign above the door reads FAIRWEATHER & SON, which is a lie twice over: there is no son, and there hasn't been fair weather in this city since the harvest failed two autumns ago. My father keeps the sign because replacing it would cost money, and because a lie that everyone can see through is safer than a truth no one wants printed.

His name is Aldous Fairweather, and his eyes are going. Not all at once—the way a candle goes, guttering longer each night before it finally drowns in its own wax. He holds the galleys closer to his face every month. He mistakes an *n* for an *h* and doesn't notice until I catch it at the stone. Last Tuesday he set an entire column of the customs ledger upside down and laughed about it, that terrible bright laugh he uses when something frightens him, and then he sat very still with his hands flat on the composing frame like a man steadying himself on the deck of a ship.

*You'll take the deliveries now,* he told me that night. Not a question. He had already divided the type cases between us in his mind, already decided which burdens were mine. That is how love works in our family. It is never spoken. It is assigned.

So now I am a compositor by day and a courier by necessity, and I have learned to measure my life the way my father measures type: in what can be carried, and what must be left behind. A woman who owns nothing heavy owns nothing that can be taken. I have three dresses, one good coat, a knife with a broken tip, and a memory I keep oiled like a lock. When the soldiers came last spring to inventory the shop—I'll come to that; everything in this city comes back to inventories—they found nothing worth confiscating and no one worth arresting, because we had made ourselves into exactly that: nothing worth the trouble.

It is a kind of art, being invisible. I have been practicing it since I was small enough to hide under the press.

* * *

The pamphlets were ready by candlelight on Thursday morning, four hundred of them, folded twice and smelling of fresh ink and the tallow candles we burn because wax costs too much. My father had set them over three nights, working by touch more than sight, his fingers reading the type like a blind man reads a face. The text was short. Short things travel farthest. It named the grain merchants who had bought up the winter stores and were holding the city's bread hostage while the price climbed past what a laborer earns in a day. It named the magistrate who signed their licenses. It did not name us. It never names us. We are the voice, not the mouth.

*Four hundred copies,* my father said, running his thumb along the stack without counting. His eyes had that wet shine they get when he's been squinting too long. *The bridge checkpoint first. Then the Custom House if the bridge is quiet. Take the river route back.*

*I know the route.*

*Take the river route anyway.* He pressed the bundle into my arms, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with plain twine. Under the pamphlets, sewn flat against the cloth, lay six coins—not for spending but for bribing, should bribery become necessary. *And Rafael.* He caught my wrist. His grip surprised me; he is not a strong man anymore. *If they stop you, you are delivering invoices to Vasseur's warehouse. You are bored. You are cold. You want your supper. Boredom is the best armor a girl can wear.*

*I'm twenty-two, Papa. I stopped being a girl some years ago.*

*You're my daughter carrying seditious print across a military checkpoint. You're whatever age keeps you alive.* He let go of my wrist and turned back to the press, and that was the end of the conversation, because in our shop every conversation ends with someone turning back to work. Work is how we say the things we can't say. The press says *come home safe* better than he ever could.

I wrapped the bundle inside a delivery of blank invoice paper—real invoices, real orders, Vasseur's actual warehouse stamp, because Tomas forged them so well that even Vasseur's own clerks couldn't tell—and I dressed the part. Gray wool dress, mended at the hem. Plain shawl. No jewelry, no ribbon, nothing that catches a soldier's eye or a thief's. My mother's ring stays in a crack under the floorboards with the emergency money, and I have not worn it in three years. A married woman gets asked where her husband is. An unmarried woman gets asked other questions. A gray woman gets asked nothing at all.

Tomas was already up, feeding the stove. He's sixteen, all elbows and ink stains, apprenticed to us since he was eleven when his mother died of the fever. He pretends he knows nothing about the pamphlets, which is his way of knowing everything. This morning he looked at the bundle under my arm and looked away too quickly, the way people look away from things that might hang them.

*Bridge again?* he said.

*Bridge again.*

*They doubled the guard on Tuesday. New orders from the citadel.* He said it to the stove, not to me. *Hessler's men.*

Hessler. Even the name has a sound like a boot heel. Colonel Hessler runs the security apparatus in this quarter of the city, and he has been tightening the checkpoints all autumn, hungry for conspirators the way a starving man is hungry for bread. Whether he actually believes there are conspirators, or whether he needs them for his career, no one knows. In a city like ours, the distinction stops mattering. A man looking for traitors will find them, because finding them is the only thing keeping him fed.

*Then I'll be extra bored,* I said, and Tomas almost smiled, and I went out into the dark.

* * *

The city at five in the morning is a different animal than the city at noon. At noon it snarls and bargains and shoves. At five it lies still and breathes steam—breath and chimney smoke and river fog all rising together into a sky the color of dishwater. I moved through it the way I've learned to move: not hurrying, not dawdling, a woman with somewhere dull to be. Hurrying marks you. So does hesitation. The trick is to be so unremarkable that the eye slides off you like rain off oilcloth.

Tanner's Lane down to the tannery sluice, along the drainage channel where the ice was forming in gray feathers at the edges, past the shuttered fish market where the gulls were already quarreling over nothing. The river showed itself between the warehouses, wide and brown and slow, carrying its cargo of broken ice toward the sea. Somewhere upstream, beyond the bridges and the checkpoints and the whole apparatus of the state, the world was supposedly still sane. I have never seen it. I only deliver paper to the people who claim they're trying to build it.

The bridge checkpoint came into view the way it always does: two lanterns, a barrier of crossed timbers, a brazier throwing orange light on the frost, and four men in the citadel's blue-gray coats stamping their feet against the cold. I counted them out of habit. Four guards, one sergeant, one wagon being searched plank by plank—a grain cart, ironically enough, the driver standing apart with his hat in his hands wearing the expression of a man rehearsing his innocence.

New orders, Tomas had said. I felt them before I saw them. There was a fifth man at the table now, a clerk with a ledger, checking papers against a list. They were writing down names. Names written down become lists, and lists become arrests, and arrests become the notices my father prints for the government—the ones we set in the smallest type we own, as if small letters could shrink what they say.

I joined the line of foot traffic with my bundle under my arm and my face arranged into mild, patient boredom. Ahead of me a fishwife argued with her daughter. Behind me a man coughed the wet cough that half the city carries since the frost came. The line shuffled forward. The sergeant waved through a nun. He waved through two laborers after a glance at their hands—he wanted calluses, proof of honest work. He waved through an old priest whose papers the clerk examined for a long moment before nodding.

Then it was my turn, and the fear arrived, right on schedule, the way it always does: not as panic but as precision. My hearing sharpened. I could hear the brazier popping, the river grinding its ice, the scratch of the clerk's pen. Fear, in my experience, is not the enemy. Panic is the enemy. Fear just tells you to pay attention.

*Papers,* the sergeant said. He was maybe forty, red-faced, with the permanent squint of a man who spends his life outdoors guarding things he doesn't care about.

I handed him Vasseur's invoices. *Deliveries for the warehouse office. Invoices and order forms.* I let my breath show in the cold, let my shoulders slump the way a tired workingwoman's shoulders slump. *He'll complain they're late again. He complains when the sun rises late.*

The sergeant glanced at the papers without really reading them. The clerk took them, though. The clerk was the new element, the variable, and I watched him the way you watch a dog you don't know: politely, and with your weight on your back foot. He compared the invoices against his list. My name wasn't on any list—I don't exist on paper, that's the whole architecture of my life—but Vasseur's stamp was genuine, the orders matched the season, and boredom, my father's armor, sat on my face like a well-fitted mask.

*What's in the parcel?* the clerk said.

*Invoice duplicates. Waste paper, mostly.* I shrugged. *Vasseur makes us carry the copies back rather than trust the post. He trusts no one. Especially not the post.*

Something flickered at the corner of the sergeant's mouth. Everyone in this city distrusts the postal service; it is the one conviction that unites all classes and factions. The clerk hesitated a moment longer—long enough for me to count the coins sewn into my wrap and calculate, in the cold arithmetic part of my brain that operates independently of my hammering heart, whether six coins would buy two men's blindness or only one man's silence and the other's ambition.

Then the sergeant said, *Move along, mistress, you're holding up the line,* and the clerk stamped the invoice copy, and I walked across the bridge with four hundred treasons under my arm and my pulse ticking in my ears like a slow clock.

Halfway across, the wind came off the river and nearly took my shawl, and below me the ice turned and ground against the piers, and I allowed myself—one breath, no more—to feel what I felt. Not triumph. Triumph is for fools; triumphant people get noticed. What I felt was the particular exhaustion of competence, the weight of a thing done well that must be done again tomorrow and the day after, forever, until either the regime falls or I do. The pamphlets would be distributed by noon. By evening some merchant's clerk would find one tucked in his bread, and read it, and pass it on, and somewhere in the citadel a man like Hessler would add another mark to another list, hunting a printer he could not find in a city full of printers.

My father thinks the words will save us. He sets them in lead because lead endures, because a sentence properly composed outlives the man who set it. Maybe he's right. But I am the one who carries them, and I know what they weigh. Every crossing, they weigh more.

* * *

I made the drop at the chandler's shop on the far bank—old Brecht, who hides pamphlets inside hollowed candle molds and sells them, literally, by the pound—and I took the river route home as instructed, along the frozen towpath where the fog was lifting off the water in long white banners. The city was waking as I came back through it. Smoke from ten thousand chimneys. The bell of the cathedral tolling six. Women queuing already at the bakeries, though everyone knew there'd be no flour until the barges came through, and everyone knew why the barges weren't coming, and no one dared say it above a whisper except on paper.

At the corner of Tanner's Lane I stopped, because habit is a discipline and discipline is survival, and I checked the street both ways the way my father taught me before I could read. Empty. Frost on the cobbles. Marta's dye house silent, its vats cold, though a thread of smoke from her chimney said she was up. The cooper's yard quiet. And there, ahead, our shop with its lying sign and its one lit window, FAIRWEATHER & SON glowing faintly gold against the gray morning—

—and something else.

A shape at the doorstep. Small. Wrapped in blanket. I thought at first it was refuse, a bundle of rags someone had dumped against the door the way people dump everything on printers, believing us to be a public service. Then it moved.

I stood very still on the frosted cobbles with the empty oilcloth under my arm and the river fog closing behind me, and I watched the bundle move again, and heard—for the first time, faintly, through the wool and the cold and the early hour—a thin, furious, unmistakable cry.

A child. Someone had left a child at our door, in the last dark hour before dawn, in the coldest week of the year.

And pinned to the blanket, catching the light of our window, a sealed letter.
