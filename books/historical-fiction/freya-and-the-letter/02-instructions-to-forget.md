## 2. Instructions to Forget

I did not touch the letter first, though my hands wanted it. That is a thing you learn in my trade: want is the enemy of every careful act. My father taught me to set type with my breath held and my fingers reading the letters like a blind man reads a face, and he taught me that the moment you *want*—to hurry, to finish, to be done—you smudge, you misalign, you ruin. So I stood on our own doorstep with a crying child at my feet and evidence pinned to her blanket, and I made myself do nothing at all for the length of ten heartbeats.

The fog was coming off the river in slow grey ropes. Our window still burned behind me; my father kept a light in the shop all night now, though he would not say why, and I had stopped asking. Down the lane, Marta's dye-house was dark. The whole street was holding its breath the way cities do in that hour—not asleep exactly, but listening.

The bundle cried again, thinner this time, as if the child were learning that crying cost warmth she could not afford. I crouched. Wool blanket, good quality, army-grey, the kind of cloth that does not come from any market in our quarter. Under it, a face so small it seemed more idea than person—red with cold, eyes shut hard, a scrap of fury no bigger than a loaf of bread. Her fists were up by her ears in the ancient posture of someone bracing against the world, and something in my chest moved in a way I did not permit things to move.

*Do not*, I told myself. *You know what happens to people who pick things up.*

I picked her up anyway. There was no decision in it. My hands decided, the way they decide how to catch a falling forme of type, before my mind had finished objecting. She weighed almost nothing and she gripped my finger with a strength that seemed frankly unreasonable for someone three weeks old at most, and she stopped crying the instant she had hold of me, which felt like a bargain being struck without my consent.

Then I saw the coins.

They had been tucked into the fold of the blanket—a small stack, wrapped in paper. Not coppers. Silver, and at least one coin that caught the lamplight with the soft yellow answer of gold. Enough to buy silence from anyone who found them. Enough to make the finding look like payment rather than abandonment, if anyone ever asked.

And beneath the coins, sealed with plain wax, pressed flat by whoever had carried it: the letter. I recognized the hand even unopened—the direction line written in a quick, educated slant, the wax applied by someone who had done it many times and never thought about it. It was addressed to no one. Just folded paper, waiting to be a problem.

I looked up and down the lane. Fog. Frost. A cart wheel turning somewhere streets away. No hooded woman, no footsteps, no shadow at the corner of the dyer's wall where a shadow might stand.

But someone had stood there. Recently. The frost on our step was broken in two places—heel marks, small ones, a woman's shoes—and beside the step, half-scuffed already, the print of toes pointed toward the river. Whoever she was, she had stood here long enough to arrange a blanket, tuck in coins, pin a letter. Long enough to watch the door and choose her hour. And then she had gone toward the water, which in this city at this hour means only one of several roads, and none of them are roads you return by.

I stood in the doorway with the child against my chest and understood, with the particular clarity that comes at five in the morning in winter, that my life had just divided into two parts, and that the seam between them was this: a heel print in frost, pointing away.

* * *

My father was awake, of course. He was always awake lately; his eyes had begun failing him the way old rivers fail, not all at once but by channels silting up, and he compensated by living in the hours when no one expected anything of him. He was at the workbench when I came in, feeling the face of a galley of type with his fingertips because his eyes would no longer swear to it, and he turned at the sound of the door and then went very still.

"Rafael," he said. "What have you got there."

Not *what is it*. He knew what it was. Any man who has spent forty years around presses knows what a baby sounds like in a room built of paper and ink.

"It was on the step," I said. "There's a letter."

"I see the letter." His voice had gone into the register he used with customs men—flat, courteous, giving nothing. "Put it down on the stone by the stove. Don't warm it. Don't open it."

"I wasn't going to open it."

"You were thinking about it."

I put the child down in the clothes basket we kept for rags, lined with a clean apron, and I laid the letter on the cold hearthstone exactly as instructed, and I noticed that my hands were not entirely steady, and I hated them for it. My father came and looked at the child for a long time. His ruined eyes did their best. Then he looked at the coins, and then at the seal, and I watched him arrive at the same arithmetic I had arrived at on the step, only faster, because he had been doing this arithmetic longer.

"This isn't a foundling," he said quietly. "Foundlings come with a name stitched in the hem and a saint's medal and an address of sorts. This is a delivery. Someone chose us."

"Who chooses a print shop?"

"Someone who knows what a print shop can do with a piece of paper." He sat down slowly on his stool, and for a moment he looked every year of his age and several he had been saving. "Or someone who knows what a print shop can be hanged for."

The stove ticked. The child in the basket had gone silent—not sleeping, I realized when I looked; watching, with those unfocused newborn eyes, in the way of someone who has no idea yet what she has been delivered into. There was ink under my fingernails. There was probably ink on the apron lining her. Born wrapped in army wool, lying in printer's rags: she was already better dressed for our life than I was.

"We wake Marta," I said. "Marta takes her to the Sisters of—"

"And the letter?"

"The letter burns."

"The letter burns," my father agreed, "and two days later Hessler's men kick in our door asking why a woman was seen leaving a child here, or asking nothing at all because they already have their answer written. Or—" he touched the wax seal once, lightly, the way you'd test an iron, "—we know what's in it, and knowing is worth something, or it's worth nothing, but either way it's only worth it while it exists."

"That's not an argument for keeping it. That's an argument for drowning."

"It's an argument for thinking before the city wakes up." He rubbed his eyes with a knuckle. "Go and see to her. Feed her something. God knows what—there's bread, there's milk gone sour—"

"She's a week old, maybe."

"Then God help us," my father said, "because I don't know what a week-old child eats, and neither, I suspect, do you."

I was still working out that he had just said *us*—that somewhere between the doorstep and the stove he had stopped talking about the child as cargo—when the knocking started.

Three knocks. Pause. Three knocks. Not the fist of a neighbor. The measured, official rhythm of men who knock because the law says they must knock before entering, and whose patience for the law ends at the third repetition.

* * *

Later I would reconstruct those ninety seconds as the finest piece of composition I ever set, and I would mean it. But in the moment there was no craft. There was only the animal arithmetic of a household with one door, one cellar hatch, and about a minute.

"Through the yard," my father said, already moving, already lifting the basket, and I took it from him because I was faster and younger and because some stubborn part of me had already claimed the child the way you claim a form of type—mine to lock up, mine to answer for. "Cellar. Under the drying racks. Rafael—"

"I know."

"Don't be brave. Be quiet. Bravery is loud."

The knocking became pounding. I went through the kitchen, through the yard where we hung proofs to dry in fair weather and where frost hung now on the lines like white laundry, and down the cellar steps into the smell of paper and damp and solvent. Above me the shop door opened and voices began—my father's, unhurried, courteous; another voice I didn't know, with the clipped vowels of the customs service; and a third voice, lower, that gave instructions in the tone of a man reading a list he had read many times.

I put the basket under the drying racks, back among the stacks of dampened proof sheets, and pulled a canvas sheet over it, and then—because the canvas was thin and newborn cries are engineered by God to penetrate walls—I did the only clever thing I did in those ninety seconds: I dragged the tub of wash solution for cleaning rollers across in front of the rack, and wedged myself into the gap behind it with my shoulder against the canvas, so that my body, such as it was, became the last layer between the child and the world.

She was awake. I could feel her looking at me, or whatever looking amounts to at that age. I put one finger back into her fist.

"You are going to be quiet," I whispered, "because you are a very intelligent child who understands contracts, and ours is this: quiet now, and I will figure out the rest later. Quiet *now*."

Boots on the floor above. The specific creak of our floorboards betraying the exact geography of the shop to anyone who cared to learn it, which these men did. Drawers opening. The rustle of paper being flipped—not reading, sorting; they sorted the way magpies sort, by shine. And then a voice, close, right over my head apparently, saying:

"Papers of registration for the press."

"Current," my father said. "Third drawer, the iron cabinet, under the copy of the trade gazette."

Paper. A pause long enough to be a message in itself.

"You take government contract work?"

"When the government pays."

A short laugh with no friendliness in it. "Everyone prints for everyone in this city, eh? Half the pamphlets on the bridges come out of shops like this one. You'd know something about that."

"I'd know nothing about that," my father said, "but I'm happy to show you the ledgers, if your warrant extends to ledgers."

Another pause. I pressed my palm flat against the basket rim through the canvas, feeling the absurd small warmth of her, and I did the breathing my father had taught me for carrying loads past checkpoints—in through the nose, four counts; out through the mouth, six; face empty; heart rate lied to. Above me, boots crossed to the window, crossed back. A drawer rolled shut on wooden runners.

"Warrant covers the premises," the list-reading voice said at last, bored. "Ledgers included. Move."

So I lay in the dark under my own shop and listened to my father's ledgers being thumbed, page by patient page, by men who could read our whole year in columns of figures—every job, every customer, every suspicious gap—and I fed my finger to a child who gripped it like a rope over deep water, and I thought, with a lucidity that felt like a crack running through glass: *this is what she will do to me. She will make me lie badly. She will make me slow. Everything I am—useful, invisible, quick—ends today, and I am lying in a cellar holding the end of it in a rag basket.*

Because invisibility, for people like us, is not a mood. It is a practice, daily as prayer: deliver and don't linger, see and don't be seen to see, know the names of no one who matters and let no one matter enough to give yours. Twelve years I had practiced it, since the year the old regime tightened and my mother's family discovered that a printing surname is a target with legs. Twelve years of being furniture. Furniture does not hide infants under canvas. Furniture does not whisper promises in cellars.

She sneezed. A tiny, furious, cat-sized sneeze.

I clamped my hand gently over the blanket and held my breath, and above us a boot stopped mid-stride.

"What was that?"

"Cat," said my father, without a heartbeat's hesitation. "We keep a cat. Rats after the size."

Silence. The length of it told me a man upstairs was deciding something, and I understood in my ribs that whatever he decided, my father's voice had bought it or failed to, and there was nothing left in me to add. Then:

"Your papers are in order. The ledgers are dull. Congratulations." Boots toward the door. At the threshold, the list-reader paused. "Cold winter for strays. Keep your doors latched, compositor."

The door closed. The bar dropped. Footsteps faded down the lane, unhurried, in no doubt whatsoever of their welcome.

I stayed under the racks until my father's cane tapped twice on the floor above—the all-clear, older than I was—and then I came up into the grey light of morning with the basket and the child and the letter still sitting on the cold hearthstone where I'd left it, untouched, patient as a landmine.

My father was standing at the window with the curtain drawn back two fingers' width, watching the lane. He didn't turn around.

"They'll come again," he said. "That one wasn't looking for anything. That one was measuring the room." He let the curtain fall. "Which means whatever we decide about this letter, we decide it today, Rafael. Before noon. Because I watched you come up those stairs just now, and you weren't carrying a basket."

"No?"

"You were carrying a *child*. There's a difference, and it's written all over you, and it is precisely the difference that gets families hanged." He lowered himself onto his stool and looked at me with those failing eyes that had never once failed to see me. "So. Before you tell me what you've already decided—we should know what we're protecting. Open it."

I broke the seal.

* * *

The paper was good stock—rag paper, the expensive kind, chosen to survive folding and weather. Inside, a single dense page, and I read it twice because the first time my eyes refused the meaning and slid off it like rain off oilcloth.

It was a list. Names, offices, meeting places, dates, sums of money—set out not like a confession but like an invoice, tidy and itemized, in the same educated slant I'd seen on the wrapper. Nine names. I knew four of them. One of them was the man who had warned my father, three years ago, that our press was on a registry. Another owned the warehouse that stored half our paper. Two were magistrates' clerks. Men who protected us, in the ordinary transactional way of this city—or men who would soon be corpses, and whose protection would curdle, overnight, into association.

And at the bottom, unsigned, a single line in a different hand, smaller, hurried, as if written at a doorway with the writer already leaving:

*If you are reading this, you were chosen because you owe no one and no one owes you. Burn it and forget the face at your door, and live.*

I looked at the basket. She had finally gone to sleep, one fist escaped over the blanket's edge, ink-smudged from my finger.

Live, the letter said. As if living were simply a thing still available, like credit, like daylight—as if it hadn't just spent the night in our cellar hiding from a customs man.

My father reached out and took the page from me, and read it with his fingertips and his failing eyes both, and when he finished he sat for a long time without moving, and the stove ticked, and outside, faintly, the city began to wake.

"Rafael," he said at last, in a voice I had heard him use only once before, the year my mother died, "do you know any of these names?"

"Four of them," I said. "Two of them have drunk wine at this table."

He nodded slowly, folding the letter along its original creases, making it small, making it a thing that could be hidden anywhere—including, his expression said, in the one place no search would ever reach: inside us.

"Then sit down," he said, "and tell me everything you remember about the woman who left it."
