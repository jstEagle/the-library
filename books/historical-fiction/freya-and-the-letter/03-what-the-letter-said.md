## 3. What the Letter Said

Rafael waited until the shutters were barred and the stove had swallowed its last hiss of light before she took the letter from the hollow beneath the composing stone. She had carried it against her body all day—first sewn into her bodice during morning deliveries, then pressed flat between her ribs while she set type for a consignment of hymnals, and now it lay warm in her palm like something living. Paper should not feel alive. She knew paper the way a shepherd knows sheep, and this sheet was wrong: heavier stock than any merchant used in the quarter, watermarked with nothing, folded twice with the crisp deliberation of hands that had done terrible things carefully.

Her father sat at the workbench where he always sat, his spectacles pushed up into his thinning hair, his clouded eyes aimed at a point slightly left of wherever he meant to look. The cataracts had taken the fine print from him a year ago; they had not yet taken the shapes of things, or faces, or firelight. He could still see Rafael when she stood close enough, though she suspected what he saw was more memory than present—a girl assembled from older girls, smoothed of whatever the last two years had written on her.

"The woman's coin," he said. "You didn't spend it."

"No."

"Then you mean for us to read it."

Rafael turned the letter over. The seal was plain black wax, unmarked—not because the sender lacked arms, but because arms could be traced. She thought of the hooded woman at the door, the whisper like wind through a keyhole: *forget my face*. She had tried all day. The face kept returning anyway, the way a tune returns, half-remembered and therefore worse.

"There's an infant asleep in my room," Rafael said quietly. "Someone left a letter that smells of gun oil and a child who smells of milk. I'd like to know which of those we're keeping."

Her father said nothing for a long moment. In the stove's ember-light his hands looked carved from old wood, knuckles swollen, one finger crooked from a press accident years before Rafael was born. He had set type for forty years. He knew, better than any man in the city, that words were never only words—that every letter locked into its chase became a small permanent thing, and some permanences could hang you.

"Read it," he said at last. "I'll hold it after, in my head, and then we'll decide if it stays anywhere else."

Rafael broke the seal.

The handwriting was slanted right, hurried but controlled, the hand of someone taught to write by a tutor rather than a priest. There were dates. There were names.

The first name stopped her breath.

*Colonel Aurel Vance.* Who dined, twice monthly, at the table of the Commandant. Who had once, when soldiers were turning over shops on Bridge Street looking for seditious presses, walked past Fairweather & Son without so much as pausing at the door—and who had returned the following week, alone, out of uniform, to buy a book of pastoral poetry he plainly never read, paying with a coin too large and leaving without change.

The second name: *Dr. Emrys Kettleby*, who delivered half the neighborhood's babies and signed none of their birth notices, because birth notices went to the registry and the registry went to the ministry.

The third: *Aldous Renner, dyer's factor* — Marta's late husband's old trade connection — *and the widow Sorel, lodging-house, Quay Lane.*

And then, in the same slanting hand, a line underlined twice:

*The press on Tanner's Row is protected. It moves what we cannot. Its blindness is our fortune. So long as it remains blind.*

Rafael read the line aloud. Her voice sounded strange to her, flattened, as if the words had been printed and she were merely the press reproducing them.

Her father's hands went still on the bench.

"So," he said. "We are worth protecting."

"Papa—"

"Blindness." He pronounced it slowly, tasting the shape of the insult, or perhaps the shape of the truth underneath it. "Is that what they call it? A man loses his eyes and gains a use."

"You didn't know." She said it firmly, because she needed it to be true, because the alternative was a weight neither of them could carry up the stairs tonight. "How could you know?"

He laughed then, a short dry sound with no floor under it. "Child. When a colonel pays full price for poetry he doesn't read, a printer learns not to ask why the pamphlets he prints on commission arrive unsigned, wrapped in brown paper, collected after dark. A printer learns not to ask who pays the fines that keep getting reduced. You learn not to ask the way you learn to set an em-dash. It becomes part of the trade." He reached across the bench, and his fingers found her wrist with uncanny accuracy. "I asked nothing for eleven years. That was my part in it. Whatever this letter costs, understand that I have been paying toward it a long time."

Rafael wanted to argue. Instead she looked down at the page, at the remaining names, and felt the dread settle into her the way cold settles into a house—first the corners, then the marrow of the walls.

There were fourteen names in all. Fourteen men and women who met in cellars and lodgings and, she now understood, behind the unexamined counter of her father's shop. Fourteen people whose necks depended on this single sheet of paper staying shut. And one of them—she read the entry three times to be sure—was listed as *taken, talking as of Tuesday*. Which meant somewhere in the city, right now, a woman was being persuaded to give up names. Perhaps the hooded woman. Perhaps the mother whose milk still stained the blanket upstairs.

"They'll come looking for this," Rafael said. "If she talked—"

"If she talked, they'll come looking for everything." Her father held out his hand. "Give it to me."

She gave it to him. She watched him hold the unread page close to his ruined eyes, close enough that it must have been only a white blur with shadows on it, and she understood that he wanted to touch it without reading it again—to know it existed without carrying its contents twice.

"The stove," he said. "It would take four seconds. We have burned braver things than this for less reason."

"Yes."

"And the child sleeps upstairs, and the men named here have kept soldiers off our step for a decade, and Hessler's agents search every warehouse on the quay except print shops, because even tyrants need their proclamations set neatly." He lowered the letter. "Burning it protects us and abandons everyone whose name we'd carry out of this room inside our skulls. Keeping it—" He stopped.

"Keeping it makes us the letter," Rafael finished. "Not people. Evidence walking around in shoes."

Her father smiled, and it was the saddest thing she had seen all winter, and there had been much competition. "You've been reading the proofs of your own life, Raffie. Go on up. Sleep—if it will come. We'll decide in daylight. Everything looks less like a gallows at dawn."

But when she reached the door of the workshop she looked back, and saw him sitting alone in the ember-dark with the letter laid flat on his knees and both hands resting on it gently, the way a man rests his hands on the lid of something buried.

* * *

Elias Fairweather did not sleep.

When Rafael's steps faded on the stair, he took off his spectacles and polished them, though polishing no longer helped. Then he did what he had done every night since his eyes began to fail: he read the room from memory. The press at his back—he knew its weight, twelve hundredweight of iron his grandfather had shipped upriver. The type cabinets, forty drawers, each with its order he could recite drunk or dreaming. The composing stone worn into a shallow valley by three generations of thumbs. He had spent his life making things permanent, and permanence had made him careless. He saw that now, clearly, the way the dying are said to see whole landscapes at once.

Because there was a name on that letter he had known longer than the rest. Longer than the colonel, longer than the doctor.

*Aldous Renner.*

Aldous, who had come to the shop nine years ago with ink-stained cuffs and a request to print five hundred broadsheets of a memorial poem—for a man, Aldous said, hanged on false evidence at the Custom House. Elias had printed them. He had asked nothing, per the new rule of his life. The broadsheets appeared on walls across the quarter within a week, and within another week a boy had been arrested for pasting one, and whipped through the streets, and Aldous had vanished upriver and Elias had told himself, in the voice printers keep for such tellings, that the poem itself was innocent, that poems could not be prosecuted, that the boy's whipping was misfortune rather than arithmetic.

It was arithmetic. Elias had known it was arithmetic. Five hundred broadsheets, one boy's back, one man fled—the sum of a decision he had made at this very bench with these very hands, and then filed away under *commissioned work* and never reopened.

That was the guilt he kept buried, and it was not the only spade of earth in the yard. There was the year he printed customs schedules that he suspected—only suspected—were falsified to ruin a rival. There was the notice he refused to run for the widow Sorel when her husband died, because refusing cost less than running. Small cowardices. Ordinary ones. Every man in the quarter owned a drawerful.

But the letter upstairs-in-his-head, the letter he had read once and could not unread, had reorganized all of it. That was what knowledge did. It was not a lamp; it was a solvent. One name in a certain light, and eleven years of prudent silence dissolved into complicity, itemized.

He thought about his daughter and was ashamed.

He thought about the infant and was more ashamed, because his first thought, when Rafael had carried the bundle down at dawn—had it really been only yesterday?—had been *not here, not in my house, we are too visible already*. And his second thought, watching Rafael's face, had been the recognition that his daughter had already decided, and that her deciding was the finest and most terrifying thing he had ever witnessed, because it was precisely the thing he had trained himself out of doing for eleven years. Deciding. Choosing a side in the open, where consequences could find it.

The regime did not fear printers, he understood now. It feared exactly this: someone holding a piece of paper and choosing.

He rose, joints cracking, and climbed the stairs on the memory of each step. At Rafael's door he paused. Inside, faintly, he heard her humming—the low absent tune she'd hummed since she was four, bent over the case, sorting letters. She was awake, of course. She was sorting something else entirely.

He pushed the door open a hand's width. By the light of the banked candle he saw them: Rafael in the chair by the cradle they had improvised from a shipping crate, and in the crate the child, a fist curled against her cheek, and Rafael's eyes meeting his over the top of the blanket—unblinking, resolved, already guarding.

"We keep it," Elias said. Not a question. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "The letter. We keep it hidden, and we keep it ready, and God forgive us for the arithmetic of that."

Rafael nodded once. Neither triumph nor relief crossed her face, only the grim settling of a load accepted. She was, he thought, her grandfather all over again—the one who'd shipped the press upriver through a blockade because the wrong side needed printing done.

"Papa," she whispered, "one of the names. It says *taken, talking*."

"I heard it." He closed the door softly behind him and stood a moment in the dark hall, listening to the house breathe: the tick of cooling beams, the wind testing the shutters, the small wet sounds of a sleeping child who did not yet know she was the most dangerous object in the city.

Fourteen names, and one of them already loosening under interrogation somewhere across the rooftops. It would take days—perhaps only hours—before the talkers' talk reached the ears that mattered. Before an agent with a polite voice came asking after a woman with a child, after a blind printer, after a sheet of paper that must, at any cost, be found.

Elias went down to the workshop one last time. He did not light a candle. He unlocked the type cabinet marked *Ornaments — Foreign* and lifted out the tray of decorative borders nobody had ordered in six years, and beneath it, in the space he had prepared, he laid the letter flat and covered it with lead. Type-metal, a solid layer of it, twelve pounds of dead grey metal pressing the paper into darkness. Lead over words. It was, he supposed, how he had always solved problems.

He stood with his hand on the closed drawer and admitted the truth he had been circling since dusk: that they had not chosen to keep the letter out of courage. They had kept it because it was the only coin in the city that might, someday, buy back a life—and because the moment they burned it, they would become merely guilty instead of necessary, and necessary men survived winter, and guilty men did not.

Dread of knowledge, he thought. His old master, long before the cataracts, had told him: *the first thing a compositor learns is that once a word is set, it cannot be unset, only distributed back into the case.* Distributed, not destroyed. Waiting.

Somewhere across the city a coach rolled over cobbles, and Elias Fairweather, listening, could not tell whether it passed the corner or stopped before his door.

Upstairs, Wren slept on, and the century had eleven weeks left to live.
