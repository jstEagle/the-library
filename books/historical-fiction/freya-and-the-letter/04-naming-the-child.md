## 4. Naming the Child

*Tomas*

The child had been in the shop for three days before Tomas was allowed to say so out loud.

He had known from the first morning, of course. He knew everything that happened in Fairweather's print shop the way he knew the location of every loose plank in the floor—by listening, by watching, by keeping his head down and his eyes open. He'd heard the mewling through the wall of the type room at dawn, a thin cat-cry that resolved, when he pressed his ear to the plaster, into unmistakable infant fury. And he had watched Rafael Fairweather come up the cellar stairs with her sleeves damp and a look on her face that he had never seen there before: not fear exactly, but something adjacent to it. Something startled awake.

She had said nothing. He had said nothing. That was their arrangement, unspoken since his apprenticeship began—the whole architecture of the shop depended on things neither of them said.

But on the third day she came into the composing room while he was setting a column of almanac verses, and she was carrying the bundle against her chest like a sack of grain she was fond of, and she said, "I need you to fetch milk."

Tomas set down his composing stick very carefully, as if it might go off.

"Milk," he repeated.

"From Marta next door. The dyer. Tell her—" Rafael paused. He could see her constructing and discarding lies the way he constructed and discarded turns of phrase, hunting for one that would hold weight. "Tell her we have a lodger who can't take cow's milk. Goat, if she has it."

"A lodger," Tomas said.

"Yes."

"Who is nursing."

"Yes."

"In the cellar of a print shop."

Rafael looked down at the bundle. The edge of a blanket had slipped and Tomas saw the face beneath—a furious red scrap of a face, all brow and indignation, with a wisp of black hair standing straight up like a quill set wrong in its holder. It was, he thought absurdly, the ugliest thing he had ever seen, and something in his chest turned over anyway.

"Tomas," Rafael said quietly.

He had worked for her father four years. He had carried pamphlets sewn into coat linings and stood guard at the shop door during presses that were never logged. He had never once asked what all this was *for*, because asking questions was how apprentices disappeared, and Tomas had every intention of becoming a master printer someday, which required being alive. But here was the thing about Rafael Fairweather: she did not ask permission either, and if he refused to fetch milk, she would simply go herself, with the child wrapped under her cloak past two checkpoints in December, because that was who she was. She would do the reckless thing competently and alone, and competence alone did not keep people breathing.

"Goat's milk," he said, taking up his coat. "And I'll tell Marta the truth, or near enough. She's not a fool, and lies curdle faster than milk."

* * *

*Rafael*

Her father named her Wren.

Rafael had been calling her nothing—*the baby, the girl, the little one*—as if refusing to name her might make her easier to give away. Every night she laid out reasons for giving her away like type laid out for a page: the parish orphanage across the river; the convent at Aldgate that took foundlings without questions; leaving her, even, on some respectable doorstep in a wealthier quarter where a child was a blessing instead of evidence. And every morning she woke to the small hot weight against her ribs where the child had slept, and the reasons lay scattered like sorts spilled from a case, impossible to gather back into sense.

The letter stayed hidden. She had sewn it into the ticking of her own mattress after the second night, and now she slept above it the way one sleeps above a grave, or a seed.

On the fifth evening her father came down from the living quarters, feeling his way along the wall with one hand—he no longer trusted the stairs in low light—and lowered himself into the chair by the stove. Rafael was feeding the baby mashed pear from the tip of a spoon, an operation resembling a siege more than a meal.

"You haven't named her," her father said. It wasn't a question. His eyes, gone milky at their edges, still found her face with unnerving accuracy.

"I haven't decided to keep her."

"Hm." He held out his arms. This was new too; three days ago he had not known the child existed, and now he reached for her with the blind confidence of a man who had once held two daughters before the fever took them, thirty years gone. Rafael surrendered the spoon. The baby regarded her grandfather with deep suspicion, then grabbed his thumb and stuffed it toward her mouth, and the old man laughed—a rusty sound, like a press turning over after long disuse.

"Wrens," he said, "sing all winter. Did you know that? When every other bird has gone quiet, the wren keeps singing. Small bird. Loud voice. People used to hunt them on St. Stephen's Day for the impertinence of it." He let the child gnaw his knuckle, his clouded eyes fixed somewhere around her middle distance. "The world will tell this one to be quiet her whole life. I think she should start out with a name that tells her not to listen."

Rafael looked at them both—the old man and the furious scrap—and felt the last of her arguments collapse like wet paper.

"Wren," she said, testing it. The baby kicked, striking her grandfather's knee, and Rafael chose to take it as ratification.

"She'll be the death of us," her father said cheerfully. "Go tell Tomas he's an uncle."

* * *

*Tomas*

Uncle. As if that settled anything. As if *uncle* were a word that came with instructions.

Tomas spent the following week in a state of low continuous dread that he disguised as industry. He worked longer hours than ever—set the almanac, cleaned the rollers, re-inked the frisket, took over the deliveries Rafael could no longer make with an infant in the house—and all the while some cold arithmetic ran beneath his thoughts like groundwater. *A child in a house of seditious paper. A child who cries. A household already on lists.* He knew the shop was on lists. He had seen the way Hessler's men walked past it, slow, looking up at the windows, the way a man looks at a purse he intends to cut later.

The trouble—the unbearable trouble—was that the arithmetic kept failing. He would be in the type room at midnight, working by candle because the daylight hours belonged to orders, and Rafael would come down with the baby against her shoulder, pacing the narrow aisle between cases because the child would only sleep in motion, and she would talk to him. Low, half-whispered, so as not to wake the thing she was rocking. About nothing. About the price of tallow, about a misprint in the psalter they'd run that morning, about whether Marta's blue dye would take on wool in cold weather. Ordinary talk. But her voice dropped into that register she used only for the child—softened at the edges, worn smooth like a stone in a current—and Tomas found himself staying later than the work required, and lying awake after, doing sums that refused to come out to danger.

That was her recklessness, he decided. Not the milk, not the smuggling plans he knew she was turning over behind her eyes. It was this: she made the dangerous thing feel like home, and homes were the hardest things in the world to abandon.

"You're glaring at the em quads," Rafael observed one such midnight. "What did they ever do to you?"

"Nothing. They're the only honest ones in this building." He kept sorting. "Have you thought past Tuesday?"

"Tuesday."

"The day the inspector comes back. Quarterly visit. He counts our presses and our beds and asks whether anyone else lives here, and your father answers him, and now the answer has a voice that doesn't answer back but *screams*, Rafael, at two in the morning, at a volume I can hear through two walls."

"We've managed."

"*Managed* is not a plan. Managed is what people say right before the soldiers come." He heard his own voice climbing and forced it down. "I'm not saying give her up. I'm—" Was he not? He examined himself and found, with genuine alarm, that he was not. "I'm saying we need a story that holds. If Hessler's man finds an unregistered child here, he doesn't ask questions, he writes reports, and reports travel to people whose job is hanging. So. Whose child is she?"

Rafael stopped pacing. In the candlelight the baby had finally gone slack against her shoulder, one fist curled under her chin, and Tomas hated how his own voice gentled looking at her.

"Your sister's," Rafael said slowly. He watched the lie assemble itself behind her eyes, piece by piece, each element fitted and locked like a line of type. "Our sister Miriam, dead this summer—she died in the summer, Papa can grieve properly, he's done it before. Child born in October, too late for anyone to count months. You came to fetch milk for a sick sister's orphan. Marta will confirm the deliveries. Neighbors remember milk." She looked up. "Can you forge a burial record?"

The question landed in the silence like a coin dropped in a church.

"That's a hanging offense," Tomas said. "Forgery of parish documents."

"So is everything else in this building. I'm asking if you *can*."

He should say no. He had a whole future planned out, ink-stained and lawful-ish, master of his own modest shop someday, alive. He looked at the sleeping child, at Rafael's face above her—terrified and resolute and utterly certain—and heard himself say, "I need a sample of the vicar's hand at St. Anselm's. And better vellum than we keep for receipts."

Rafael exhaled, and something in her shoulders let go, and she smiled at him—quick, crooked, real—in a way she had not smiled in the year he'd known her, and Tomas understood with perfect clarity that he had just signed something. Not on paper. Somewhere less recoverable.

"Uncle Tomas," she said.

"Don't." He turned back to his case. "Don't make it a joke. Jokes get sloppy."

"It isn't a joke." She shifted the baby higher. At the door she paused. "Thank you, Tomas."

He set type for an hour after they went up. The words wouldn't line. He gave it up, banked the candle, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the shop breathe—the tick of the iron frame cooling, the wind at the shutters, upstairs the faint rhythm of a child's breath through the floorboards, woven in among the sounds of the family he had, apparently, joined.

*Four years* he had kept himself a stranger here. Four days, and he was an uncle.

* * *

*Rafael*

Marta came with the goat.

Rafael had expected deliveries, discretion, perhaps a raised eyebrow. Instead the old dyer arrived at the cellar door on the eighth day with a rope halter, a brown goat with a disagreeable expression, and a basket of boiled rags suitable for clouts, and installed the entire arrangement in the cellar as though she had been consulted from the beginning.

"A goat in a print shop," Rafael began.

"Goats don't read." Marta tied the halter to the coal bin and looked around the cellar with a professional eye—damp, draft, the seam in the wall where the letter presumably slept overhead. "You'll want straw down, and the brazier moved away from the timbers, and you'll want to stop looking at me like that, girl. I've been leaving milk at your door since the second night. You think a woman my age carries milk uphill in December for her health?"

Rafael said nothing. There was nothing to say that Marta had not already deduced, and saying it aloud would only make it true in ways that could be testified to.

"Three houses on this street know," Marta continued, unpacking the basket with terrible calm. "The Feenys know. Old Sutter knows, though God knows what he thinks, he thinks the Virgin left it personally. Nobody knows everything. That's how it stays." She straightened and met Rafael's eyes, and hers were sharp as needles in all that wrinkled softness. "Your mother delivered my twins, may she rest. Your father printed my husband's funeral cards for nothing. You don't carry a secret alone in this neighborhood unless you want to. So." She brushed off her hands. "When does the widow's story begin? Because if men with red armbands ask me about milk, I'll need to know what I'm confirming."

For a moment Rafael could not speak. She had spent five days building a fortress of secrecy around this cellar—alone, load-bearing, exhausting—and this small fierce woman had walked in and put her shoulder to the wall as if it were nothing, as if it were Sunday.

"October," Rafael managed. "Born in October. My sister's child."

"Dead in the summer, the sister." Marta nodded. "Grief makes girls careless. Everyone will believe it because everyone wants to believe something sad and simple." She patted Rafael's cheek with a hand that smelled of indigo and lye. "Sing to her, that one. Wrens sing all winter. My mother told me that."

*Everyone knows that,* Rafael almost said, and then understood that everyone knew it because her father had been telling this street that same thing for forty years, in season and out, and the street remembered him even as he forgot himself.

That night she wrote the first entry in a small account book she kept for the shop's private ledgers—prices, debts, the ordinary commerce of survival—and beneath the column of figures, in a fair hand that would pass for bookkeeping to any inspector, she recorded:

*Milk, 2d. Straw, 1d. Wren: singing.*

* * *

*Tomas*

The forgery took him nine days, and he hated every hour of it, and he had never in his life been prouder of work.

He practiced the vicar's round, unconfident script on waste sheets until his wrist ached, aging the vellum with weak tea and smoke, cracking the surface along fold lines with a warm iron. A burial record for Miriam Fairweather, dead of the summer fever, survived by an infant daughter—one line among hundreds, deliberately boring, buried in a page he copied from the genuine register while waiting for the sexton at St. Anselm's to finish his dinner. His hands had shaken the entire time. No one had noticed. He had noticed, and filed the shaking away as a thing to be ashamed of later.

He presented it to Rafael on a Thursday evening, laid flat on the stone bench between the goat and the press bed like an offering at a strange altar.

She read it twice. Her finger traced the line of the false entry, and Tomas watched her face and waited for judgment—for *this is good enough* or *this will hang us*—and prepared himself equally for both.

"Where did you learn this?" she asked finally.

"Nowhere. Everywhere. I've been handling other people's hands for four years—compositors learn every hand there is, it's half the trade. Setting someone's words in their own letterforms." He shrugged, embarrassed. "It's only typesetting with a pen."

"It's only typesetting with a pen," Rafael repeated softly, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't parse, and folded the record into oilcloth, and hid it somewhere in the cellar that he made himself not watch.

They stood together a moment in the lamplight. Above them the shop creaked; below the floor the child slept, and the goat chewed thoughtfully on a rag that had once been part of a royalist broadsheet, which Tomas chose to find satisfying.

"There'll be a third thing," Rafael said. "Eventually. The record gets us past an inspection. It doesn't move the letter, and the letter has to move—I won't have that kind of paper sleeping over my father's head one day longer than it must." She looked at him sideways. "There are checkpoints between here and where it needs to go. Three of them. I'm telling you now so you can argue with me in advance and get it over with."

Tomas looked at her—at this reckless, competent, impossible woman holding a forged death certificate and planning the winter's campaign as calmly as a Christmas order—and felt the two halves of himself, the afraid one and the devoted one, arrive at least at an armed truce.

"I don't argue in advance," he said. "I argue throughout. It's a longer process."

"I know," Rafael said. "Why do you think I keep you?"

From the loft above came a sudden thin cry—not distress, merely announcement, a small loud voice declaring itself to the dark—and they both looked up instinctively, like sailors hearing land.

Then Rafael's face changed, and Tomas saw her remember, and the word came down the stairs ahead of her feet: *"Hessler's man does the quarterly count on Tuesday."*

The goat, chewing, regarded them both with ancient indifference.
