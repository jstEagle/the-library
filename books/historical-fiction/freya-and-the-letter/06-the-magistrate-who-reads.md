## 6. The Magistrate Who Reads

The magistrate came on Thursdays. That much Rafael had learned by November—Corvane arrived like weather from the north, in a gray coat and a carriage too plain for his office, and he bought books the way other men bought sin: quietly, in quantity, and with an appetite he did not discuss.

She heard the bell over the door and knew the sound of it before she looked up. There was a particular hesitance to his entrance, a pause on the threshold as though he asked permission of the room itself.

"Signor Fairweather," Corvane said, and then, finding only Rafael behind the counter, inclined his head. "Signorina. Your father is well?"

"He is composing," Rafael said. It was not entirely a lie. Her father sat upstairs with a page he could no longer properly see, setting type by memory and touch, calling down for her to check his spacing. "He sends his regards."

"And you are minding the shop alone again." Corvane ran a gloved finger along the shelf of new bindings without pulling any forward. "That seems a great deal of responsibility for one young woman."

"We manage."

"We always do," said the magistrate, "until we don't."

She kept her hands where he could see them, flat on the counter, the way one does with skittish animals and officers of the law. Wren was asleep in the back room, in the drawer of a cabinet lined with clean rags, twenty feet and one thin wall from a magistrate of the Crown. Rafael had considered, briefly and absurdly, that if it came to it she would lie again—she had grown fluent at lying—and that this fluency frightened her more than any search.

"I've come for the Aurelius," Corvane said. "You wrote that it had arrived."

"It arrived Monday." She moved along the counter to the parcel shelf, unwrapping the oilcloth with deliberate slowness. Let him watch her hands. Steady hands argued better than steady words. "The binding is new—the original was ruined before it ever crossed the mountains. The text itself survived."

"Texts usually do," Corvane murmured. "It's the men who get ruined carrying them."

Rafael looked up despite herself. He was watching her with pale, unhurried eyes, and there was nothing in his face she could read as either threat or kindness, which unsettled her more than open menace would have. Menace she knew how to answer.

"You read philosophy, signore?" she asked, and heard the trap she was building even as the words left her—a magistrate who bought Aurelius might be a man worth knowing, or a man testing whether she noticed what he read.

"Aurelius? No." He took the book, weighed it in his palm like something edible. "I buy him. I intend to read him someday, when my conscience permits me leisure. Mostly I buy books because it gives me a reason to walk through this quarter without escort. Do you know what happens to a magistrate who walks his own streets without escort, signorina?"

"People look at you strangely."

"People look *through* you," Corvane said. "As though the uniform were a pane of glass. I find I dislike being invisible. Perhaps that is why I come here. You have never once looked through me."

"That," Rafael said evenly, "sounds less like praise than accusation."

"It is neither. It is observation. My trade is observations." He counted out coins, laid them in a neat stack, and did not push them across. "May I ask you something impertinent?"

*No*, she thought. *Leave. Take your book and your coins and your glass eyes and go.*

"You may ask," she said.

"You are distracted. You have been, these three weeks. You miscounted change twice last Thursday—you gave me six sous too many, which I returned, and you blushed as though I'd caught you at something far worse than arithmetic. Your father has stopped coming down. There is a cradle smell in your back room—I have a nose trained on worse things than milk, but a nose nonetheless." He paused. "I am not asking what has changed. I am asking whether you are safe."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere above, her father's chair creaked; below, the river muttered under the ice taking hold of it. Rafael became aware that she was holding her breath and made herself release it slowly, the way she released pamphlets into coat linings: smoothly, so nothing showed.

"Signore Corvane," she said, "with respect—that is a question a friend asks. And I do not know what you are."

"No," he agreed, after a moment. "Neither do I, entirely. That is rather the trouble."

He took up his book and his coins, bowed—not deeply, not shallowly, a bow calibrated like everything else about him—and paused at the door.

"The city is changing," he said. "There are men arriving whose names I don't recognize, asking questions in quarters they have no business walking. If such men come here—and shops like this one attract them the way lamps attract moths—remember that questions are not orders. You may decline to answer a question. Most people forget that."

The bell rang. The gray coat dissolved into the fog of the street, and Rafael stood a long while with her hands flat on the counter, feeling the way one feels when the ground shifts by half an inch: everything still standing, nothing quite trustworthy.

*Questions are not orders.* She turned the sentence over all afternoon like a coin of doubtful metal.

* * *

Corvane had lied to her, and he noted the fact the way he noted all facts: without heat, in the ledger he kept behind his eyes.

The lie was small and structural. He had told the girl there were men whose names he didn't recognize. In truth he knew one name perfectly well—Hessler, seconded from the Interior Bureau, installed three weeks ago in offices overlooking the Custom House with a warrant broad enough to drive a cannon through. Corvane knew Hessler's name and Hessler's reputation, and knew further that Hessler's arrival in a river city in winter meant only one thing: someone above had decided the district required cleaning, and magistrates were expected to hand them the mop.

He also knew—he had known within days, because it was his trade—that a woman had been taken at the eastern checkpoint with seditious correspondence on her person, and that she had been pregnant when taken, and that she was no longer pregnant, and that the child was unaccounted for. The report had crossed his desk annotated in Hessler's hand: *locate infant; locate letter.*

And now there was milk-smell in a printer's back room, and a father gone upstairs, and a girl who gave away six sous because her mind was elsewhere, and Corvane sitting in his carriage in the fog outside the shop, doing arithmetic of his own.

He had come today partly for the Aurelius. That much was honest. But he had lingered longer than a purchase required, and probed harder than courtesy required, and he understood exactly why he had done both, and the understanding did not flatter him.

Because he liked them. That was the beginning and end of it. He liked the old compositor's dry courtesies and the daughter's watchful competence; he liked a shop where ideas were stacked in spines like cordwood; he liked being looked at instead of through. And liking things, in his profession, in this year, was a liability that could be exploited against him by anyone—including, eventually, himself.

His driver coughed politely. Corvane gazed at the shop's dark windows a moment longer. *You may decline to answer a question*, he had told her, and it had sounded like protection when he said it, and like cowardice now, in the fog, because he knew how quickly questions became orders in cities like this one, and how quickly declining became evidence.

There was a time—he dated it precisely, seven years ago, the night of the bread riots, when he had released forty starving women with a fine of one sou each and a stern lecture he hadn't believed—there had been a time when he believed the law could be bent quietly, case by case, a half-degree at a time, until the sum of half-degrees amounted to mercy. Seven years of half-degrees. And the regime had grown not softer but sharper around his compromises, and each year the bending required more force, and he had begun to suspect that the arc did not bend toward mercy at all but simply wore thin, like a coin handled too long, its face rubbed smooth, its metal spent.

*Locate infant; locate letter.*

A child. He had prosecuted smugglers, forgers, two murderers, one poet. Never yet a child. He found his thumb pressed hard against the spine of the Aurelius and made himself stop.

"Home," he said to the driver, and then, as the carriage lurched: "No. By way of the Custom House. Slowly."

If Hessler's men were already sniffing at printers' row, Corvane wanted to see the shape of the net before anyone told him to pull its ropes. A magistrate who saw things early had options. A magistrate who saw things late had only orders.

He watched the streets slide past—fishwives banking their coals, a patrol checking papers at the fountain, boys chalking something on the Custom House wall that a soldier was scrubbing off with a wet rag, badly, leaving the ghost of letters legible beneath the smear. *The century is dying*, Corvane thought, *and everyone feels it in their teeth, the way animals feel weather.*

At the Custom House he told the driver to wait, and walked the quay, hands clasped behind him, an old man inspecting commerce. Crates stood in ranks under tarpaulins. Clerks stamped papers. Two men in the plain coats of the Interior Bureau stood at the head of the ramp with a list between them, and every third crate was opened, and every opened crate was searched not for contraband goods but for paper—Corvane watched a customs man run his fingers along the seams of a shipment of candle molds, feeling for folded sheets, and understood that whatever was moving through this city, Hessler believed it was still moving.

*Still moving.* Which meant whoever carried it had not yet been caught. Which meant, if his arithmetic was right—a printer, a letter, a missing child—that the carrier was a nineteen-year-old girl with flour on her apron who miscounted change when she was frightened.

Corvane got back into the carriage and gave the order for home, and sat with his doubts the whole way like fellow passengers, none of whom would meet his eye.

* * *

Thursday next, the fog came in off the river so thick the streetlamps burned as smudges, and Corvane arrived at the shop on foot, without carriage, which Rafael noticed at once and understood as either carelessness or message.

He bought nothing today. He stood at the counter turning his hat in his hands—a magistrate, turning his hat like a schoolboy—and said, "Your father's health. How is it truly?"

"Worse," Rafael said, because it was true, and because lying to this man had begun to feel like a debt accruing interest.

"And the business?"

"Surviving."

"Hm." He set the hat down. Through the wall, faintly, Wren stirred—a small sound, a sigh, the creak of the cabinet drawer. Rafael's heart went loud in her ears, but Corvane's eyes did not even flick toward the sound, and she realized he had heard it, catalogued it, and chosen silence, and that this choice was the entire conversation.

"Signorina," he said quietly, "I am going to tell you something I should not, and you are going to pretend I never said it, and we will both continue to be people who buy and sell books. There is a man named Hessler. He has a list. His method is to arrest widely and sort later, and his superiors reward volume, not accuracy. When he comes—and I believe he will come to this street before the month ends—it would be well if your household appeared utterly dull. Duller than dull. No unusual visitors. No unusual cargo. Nothing a bored clerk could underline."

Rafael held his gaze. Under the counter, her thumbnail pressed a groove into the wood. "Why," she said, "are you telling me this?"

"Because you looked at me once like a person instead of a uniform, and I am weak, and vain, and tired." He smiled, and it was the first true smile she had seen on him, and it aged him ten years. "Also because the law is an instrument, signorina, and instruments can be held by different hands. Remember which hand holds yours this winter. That is all the advice I can afford."

He put on his hat. At the door he paused, hand on the bell-frame, and added—so softly she nearly lost it in the fog-muffled street:

"If ever you needed to place something somewhere no one would look—there are worse places than a magistrate's library. Books travel where paper cannot. Consider it."

The bell rang. Gone.

Rafael stood motionless in the empty shop, the letter's weight suddenly present against her ribs where it lived sewn into her everyday shawl, and felt the thing Corvane had called dangerous hope take root in her chest like a seed she had no business planting.

Behind her, on the stairs, floorboards groaned. Tomas came down two at a time with his cap in his hands and his face white as fresh paper.

"Rafa," he whispered. "There's a man across the street. Gray coat, plain boots. He's been there since noon, sketching the fronts of the buildings. He drew ours twice."

Rafael looked out through the frost-ferned window. The fog had thinned just enough. Across the street, a figure leaned against the dyer's wall with a portfolio on his knee, unhurried, patient, drawing.

Not a surveyor's man. Not an architect.

Hessler's list had begun.
