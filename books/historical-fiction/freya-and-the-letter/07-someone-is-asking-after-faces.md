## 7. Someone Is Asking After Faces

The knock came at half past three on a gray Thursday, the kind of hour when the city pretended to work and everyone with sense pretended to sleep. Rafael was at the stone sink in the back room, washing Wren's linen in water so cold her knuckles had gone the color of river ice, when she heard the knocker fall—three measured raps, deliberate, the sound of a man who expected doors to open for him.

Her hands went still in the basin.

They had a system for knocks. Two quick strikes meant a customer; one long drag meant Tomas forgetting his key again; four meant trouble and everyone out the cellar before the second breath. Three measured raps belonged to no system at all, which meant whoever stood on the step had never needed to learn their language.

"Papa," she said quietly. Her father looked up from his composing stick, lead type glinting between his fingers like dark seeds. His eyes had worsened through November; he could no longer read a galley proof without holding it to the window, and now he squinted toward the front of the shop as though the sound itself were printed too small to make out.

"Customers don't knock like that," he said.

"No," Rafael agreed. "They don't."

She dried her hands on her apron, and in the four steps it took her to cross the workroom she performed the small arithmetic of survival she had practiced since girlhood. Wren was asleep in the laundry basket behind the curtain, wrapped in a blanket, invisible to anyone who did not already know. The letter—God, the letter—was sewn into the spine of a ruined copy of *Pilgrim's Progress* on the shelf of damaged stock, where it had lived since the failed attempt at the bridge. Nothing seditious lay loose on the premises except the pamphlet forms locked in the press cabinet, which every print shop in the city contained and no inspector had ever bothered to break. Her face arranged itself into the mild, faintly bored expression of a shopwoman interrupted at her labor.

She opened the door.

The man on the step was not large, but he occupied space the way frost occupies a window—quietly, completely. He wore a good coat cut for travel, dusted with road dirt, and gloves of soft kid that he flexed as he looked past her into the shop with the unhurried gaze of a man taking inventory. His face was long and pleasant and utterly unremarkable, the kind of face you would forget while looking at it, and his eyes were pale as weak tea and just as transparent, showing you nothing beneath.

"Fairweather's Printing," he said, reading the faded sign above the door as if confirming something he already knew. "I'm looking for the proprietor."

"My father is the proprietor. I manage the counter. How can I help you, sir?"

"Hessler." He produced a leather wallet, flipped it open just long enough for an official seal to register—a stamp, a ribbon, the color of authority—and closed it again. "Bureau of Public Order. You're the daughter, then."

"I'm a daughter. There are three of us." A lie delivered smoothly, though it cost her something; the Bureau would know the family registers. She softened it into a smile. "The others married out. You'll want my father for accounts, but he's resting his eyes—he doesn't see well anymore. If it's about an order, I can fetch the ledger."

Hessler stepped inside without being asked. That was the trick of him, she understood at once: he didn't demand entry, he simply behaved as though walls were theoretical. He moved down the workroom aisle trailing one gloved finger along the cases of type, reading the labels—Baskerville, Caslon, the battered fount of Dutch pica her grandfather had bought used—and Rafael followed two paces behind with her heart beating somewhere up around her ears and her face saying, patiently, *another customer, another day*.

"Fine establishment," Hessler said. "Older than the war. Older than the last two wars, I'd guess."

"Paper and ink outlast governments," Rafael said, and instantly wished the word *governments* back, but Hessler only smiled at the shelves.

"That they do. Which is why we keep such close account of them." He turned. Up close his pleasantness resolved into something more precise: a man who listened the way other men spoke. "I won't take much of your time, Mistress Fairweather. I'm making inquiries in the district. A woman, young, dark-haired, was seen in this street in September carrying an infant. We have reason to think she may have passed through here."

"An infant." Rafael let the word sit, then shook her head with the small regret of someone who wished she could help. "We see a thousand faces a week at that counter, sir. Authors, clerks, chandlers' wives wanting billheads. I couldn't swear to one woman from September. And no children that I recall—ink and babies don't mix. My father would have said so himself, loudly."

"And yet." Hessler walked to the window, unhurried, and looked out at the street as though expecting it to confess something. "The woman has given testimony. Under difficult circumstances." He said it lightly, the way one mentions weather. "Her memory is imperfect, but improving. She recalls a door. A green door, paint peeling at the corner. A light burning late inside. She remembers leaving something behind her when she went away from that door."

The cold from the washbasin had never left Rafael's hands; now it climbed her arms and settled in her chest. The green door. Their door. She had painted over the peeling corner herself in October—had she done it before or after? After. She remembered the smell of the paint, remembered thinking it made the shop look respectable for the winter markets. A repair. An innocent, fatal repair, noted by a woman who had since been taken apart by men like this one until even a doorway became evidence.

"We had our door painted in October," she heard herself say, in a tone of mild cooperation, offering the fact the way a neighbor offers sugar. "It was peeling terribly. If that helps your inquiry—there's the painter's mark still at the bottom corner, Voss and Son, Copper Lane. They'll remember the job better than I will."

Hessler turned from the window and regarded her for a long moment. The silence had weight, like deep water. Somewhere behind the curtain, in the basket, Wren stirred—and Rafael's whole body clenched around the sound the way a fist clenches around a blade—and then, mercifully, the child sighed and settled, one small dream shifting, nothing more.

Hessler's eyes flicked toward the curtain. Held there. Came back.

"You have a baby," he said.

Rafael laughed. It came out of her before fear could stop it—a short, genuine-sounding bark of amusement—and later, in the dark, she would marvel at that laugh, wonder whose throat it had come from, because it certainly hadn't come from hers. "Sir, that curtain hides our mangle and a month of washing. If there's a baby behind there, the mangle's been busy." She lifted the edge of the curtain an inch, displaying a glimpse of gray linen and clothespins, and let it fall. "My sister leaves her boy with us sometimes when she's at market. Toddlers are chaos. You'd know if one lived here—the whole street would know."

"Mm." Hessler drew off one glove, finger by finger, considering her. "You're very composed, Mistress Fairweather."

"I run a counter in a district where soldiers search flour sacks with bayonets. Composed is the trade we're all in."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite approval, but the acknowledgment of a well-made argument. He replaced the glove. "I'll leave my card. If the woman remembers anything further—if a face comes back to her—I'll call again. These inquiries tend to improve with repetition." He set the card on the counter, squared its edges precisely with the counter's edge, and put on his hat. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and said, without turning, in the tone of a man remarking on the price of coal:

"They hanged a woman in the capital last month for harboring. Just a woman, just a child that wasn't hers. The papers called it mercy that they didn't hang the child too." He tipped his hat a fraction. "Good day to you, Mistress Fairweather. Mind the ice on your step. It's the season for falling."

The door closed behind him. Through the fogged glass Rafael watched him pause on the pavement, look up at the sign, look down the street both ways with that inventory-taking patience, and walk off eastward toward the Custom House quarter without hurry, a man with many doors left to knock on.

Then, and only then, did her knees inform her that they had opinions about the last ten minutes. She gripped the counter. The card sat there on the wood, heavy as a type slug: **J. HESSLER, BUREAU OF PUBLIC ORDER**, and beneath, in small copperplate, *Inquiries conducted courteously.* Courtesy. The word was worse than a threat. Threats ended; courtesy renewed itself.

She pushed through the curtain. Wren lay blinking up at her from the blanket, wide dark eyes, fists curled by her ears, entirely unaware that her existence had just been weighed in a government balance and found, for the moment, unrecorded. Rafael gathered her up and held her against the cold window glass where the light was, and the baby grabbed a fistful of her collar and pulled it to her mouth and chewed it, and Rafael pressed her lips to the small hot head and breathed there, once, twice, until her heartbeat came down out of her ears.

"He knows," her father said softly. He had not left his frame; he stood with one hand flat on the cabinet top, steadying himself against the dimness. "Not everything. But he knows the shape of it. That man wasn't fishing, Rafa. He was measuring the door."

"Then we make the door smaller," Rafael said. Her voice came out level, and hearing it level frightened her more than shaking would have. "He has a description of a woman and a suspicion of a green door. By tomorrow morning neither will serve him. Papa, get your coat. We're going to Marta's—her yard wall backs onto ours and her dyevats burn anything you ask them to. The blanket, the shawl, the basket—everything she touched. Everything the child ever touched goes in the fire tonight."

"And the letter?"

Rafael looked at the shelf of damaged stock, at the gray spine of *Pilgrim's Progress* with its patient, terrible secret. "The letter moves tomorrow. Downriver. Before he calls again."

"It nearly killed you at the bridge once already."

"The bridge was the wrong door." She shifted Wren to her shoulder. "There are three checkpoints between here and the border of this district, and we've only ever tried one of them. A man who measures doors expects people to use doors." Something was hardening in her as she spoke, some molten fear cooling into shape, and she recognized it the way one recognizes weather changing—not as courage exactly, but as the end of pretending there was a safe way through. "We'll find what isn't a door."

* * *

Tomas heard none of the conversation. That was the design of the building: the apprentice slept in the loft above the press, and the loft heard the floorboards and nothing else. He came down at dusk to bank the stove and found the shop transformed into a funeral—blankets, the shawl, a little knitted cap with ear-flaps, all going into a sack in Rafael's arms, and Rafael herself standing at the case with a face like a locked door.

"What's happened?"

"A man from the Bureau came." She said it plainly, which was how he knew it was bad. Rafael saved plainness for catastrophe; in small troubles she was theatrical enough. "Asking after a woman with a child. Asking about our door."

Tomas felt the blood leave his face in the physical manner of it, a draining, a lightness in the fingertips. He was seventeen and had been with the Fairweathers three years, and in three years the shop's great virtue had been its unimportance—too small to notice, too poor to bother. "What did you tell him?"

"That we've never seen a woman with a child in our lives." Rafael slung the sack over her shoulder. "And he believed me. Or he wants me to think he did, which amounts to the same thing tonight and a different thing soon. Watch the counter till I'm back. Don't open for anyone. Anyone, Tomas. Even me, unless I whistle the third tune."

She was gone before he could answer, out into the blue winter dusk, and Tomas stood in the emptying shop with the stove poker in his hand feeling suddenly like the child in the household. He banked the coals. He trimmed the lamp. He tried to compose himself the way he composed type, letter by letter, upside down and backwards, everything in its proper compartment—and found that the compartments wouldn't hold.

Because he had seen her lie.

He had watched it from the stair, actually—he'd come halfway down when the knocking started, and frozen there like a fool, and so he had witnessed the whole performance from above: Rafael opening the door, Rafael smiling, Rafael laughing—that laugh, God, it had gone through him like a splinter because he knew her real laugh, had heard it perhaps six times in three years, and the thing she gave Hessler wore its skin. He had watched her lift the curtain and show the man the mangle with the baby twelve inches away, breathing, and her voice never moving a hair's width off true. And when it was over and the door shut, she had stood for exactly three seconds with her hand pressed flat against the counter, white-knuckled, eyes closed—three seconds, no more—and then turned and begun giving orders as if terror were a delivery she'd signed for and put on the shelf.

That was the thing Tomas understood now, standing alone in the lamplight among the cases of lead. He had always thought of Rafael as careful, and she was—but what he had watched today wasn't care. Care was calculation, and calculation flinches. What Rafael had was something colder and more total: she had decided, sometime in the months since the basket arrived on the doorstep, that certain things were simply going to be true regardless of consequences, and having decided, she no longer spent anything on doubt. She would lie to the Bureau. She would stand in front of it, laughing. If it came to it, he understood with a clarity that frightened him, she would put her body between that child and whatever came, and she would do it without drama, the way she set type, as work that needed doing.

And the thought that arrived next was the one he'd be ashamed of later, though not very: *she'll hang, and she'll hang well, and none of us will be able to stop her.*

Unless someone made sure hanging never came to the question.

Tomas set down the poker. In the loft under his mattress he kept a pencil stub and half a quire of cheap paper, and on that paper, in a hand he had disguised by slanting it wrong, he had copied out certain names and dates some weeks ago—for safety, he'd told himself, insurance against fire and theft, though he'd known even then that insurance was a story he told children. He thought about that paper now. He thought about the underground network of printers that old Kessel at the wine-wharf had spoken of, one winter night, when the drink made him trust the room: *a chain of shops, lad, each knowing only the next link, passing word downriver faster than any courier of the Crown.* He thought about Rafael at the bridge checkpoint, going gray, turning back, and how the letter might sit on that shelf forever because she was too wise to risk it and too stubborn to destroy it.

One link, he thought. One link could carry it where she couldn't.

He climbed to the loft, took out the paper, and read it by the light of a shuttered candle: eleven names, a date, a place. Then he folded it small, smaller than a coin, and sat a long time listening to the wind worry the sign outside, the green door with its fresh paint, until he had finished becoming the kind of person who could do what he was about to do.

Below him, the shop bell chimed—Rafael back from Marta's, whistling the third tune, low and steady. Tomas slid the paper into his boot and went down to open for her, wearing his ordinary face, which was, he reflected as he lifted the latch, a skill he had learned from watching hers.

Outside, far off toward the Custom House, a lantern was moving along the quay where lanterns had no business being, slow and methodical, pausing at each warehouse door. Counting doors. Measuring them.

Tomorrow the shipment of type molds was due to go downriver, and the river, come morning, would have new eyes on it.
