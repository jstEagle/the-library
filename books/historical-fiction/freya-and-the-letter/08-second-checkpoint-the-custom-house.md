## 8. Second Checkpoint: The Custom House

The molds arrived on a Tuesday, in the gray hour when the river fog had not yet decided whether to be weather or wall.

Rafael heard the cart before she saw it—the particular complaint of an axle that had carried too much lead in its life and resented every mile. She put down her composing stick, wiped her inking fingers on her apron out of habit rather than necessity, and went to the door.

Two men from Duval's foundry stood in the street, breath steaming, a crate between them the size of a coffin lid set on end. New type molds, ordered by her father in the autumn when money had still seemed like something that might come back. Rafael had forgotten about them. Now she looked at the crate—sturdy, innocuous, addressed in a clerk's careful hand to *Fairweather & Son, Printers*—and felt her heart begin to move faster without her permission.

"Sign here," said the older man, thrusting a board and chalk at her.

She signed. She watched them heave the crate down onto the shop floor, where it sat among the standing cases like a stranger at a funeral, and she thanked them, and she closed the door behind them, and then she stood with her hand flat against the wood for a long moment, listening.

Her father was asleep upstairs. His eyes had been getting worse all winter; the surgeon had used a word Rafael refused to repeat even inside her own head. Tomas was at the back of the shop, distributing type, his movements quick and nervous as ever, glancing at her every few seconds because he had learned that when Rafael went quiet it meant something was about to happen.

"Tomas," she said. "How soon could we have a shipment ready to go downriver?"

He came around the press, wiping his hands. "Downriver? To whom?"

"To the Vasseur brothers. They've wanted that consignment of ornaments and spare matrices since October. We promised it before the freeze."

"That's three days' packing at least—"

"We have until tomorrow afternoon."

He stared at her. Then his gaze went to the crate, and she watched him understand—not everything, not yet, but enough. "Rafael. What are you thinking?"

"The bridge is closed to anyone carrying papers now. Hessler's men turn over every satchel, every book, every seam of cloth." She kept her voice low and level, the voice she used for instructions. "But the custom house downriver isn't the same animal. Freight goes through with a manifest and a nod, so long as the papers match the goods. Crates of metal aren't interesting. Crates of metal are heavy and dull and everyone wants them gone."

"And what would be inside this shipment of metal," Tomas said slowly, "besides metal?"

She looked at him.

"No," he said. "No, Rafael. Not after the bridge. You nearly—" He stopped himself, but the words were already in the room: *You nearly lost everything because Wren cried.*

"Wren stays here with my father," she said. "That's settled. And the letter doesn't go sewn into anything or folded into anything or hidden anywhere clever. Clever hiding is what gets people searched twice. It goes into the open. Into the middle of a hundred pounds of type molds, wrapped in oiled paper like a spare part, packed where any man who reaches into the crate touches cold iron before he touches anything else." She tapped the crate lid. "They'll strip this at the custom house, Tomas. I'm counting on it. Let them take out every piece and hold it to the light. It will all be exactly what the manifest says it is."

"Except one thing."

"Except one thing that looks like its neighbors."

Tomas ran both hands through his hair, which did nothing to improve it. He was nineteen and had hands that were made for fine work—he was the best closer of lockup lines she had ever seen, better than her father in his prime—and he wrung them now like a washerwoman.

"The Vasseurs," he said. "Do they know?"

"They'll receive what the manifest says they'll receive. Whether there's one extra piece inside is no fault of theirs. If it's found, it's found in a crate shipped to two respectable founders who never asked for it. Deniable. That's the whole architecture of it—everyone along the line can say they didn't know, because almost no one does."

"Almost no one," Tomas repeated. "Meaning you. Meaning me."

"You don't have to carry it. I can pack it myself."

"I didn't say that." He said it too fast, and she saw him hear himself say it too fast, and something crossed his face that she would remember later, though she didn't name it then: the look of a boy discovering that his fear has limits after all, and that those limits sit further out than he'd believed. "I'll do the shipping papers," he muttered. "If we're doing this, the papers will be perfect. Yours would pass. Mine won't be questioned at all."

There was vanity in it, and fear dressed as pride, and underneath both something steadier. Rafael put her hand briefly on his shoulder—a thing she did perhaps twice a year—and felt him go very still under it.

"Perfect papers," she said. "Then let's build a lie worth typesetting."

* * *

Tomas

The forgery took him six hours, and it was, he thought somewhere in the fourth hour, the finest work he had ever done, which was a terrible thing to think.

He had learned shipping documents the way he'd learned everything—by taking apart the genuine article to see where the strength lived. A real waybill from the Duval foundry had four hands on it: the foundry clerk, the carter, the customs broker's stamp, and the receiving house counter-sign. Each hand aged differently. The clerk wrote fast and mean, his letters leaning like men walking into wind. The carter signed with an X-adjacent scrawl and always smudged the date because he signed while the ink was fresh on someone else's page. The broker's stamp was applied crooked nine times in ten because the man was lazy and stamped before squaring the sheet.

Tomas sat at the low bench by the stove with three genuine waybills spread before him and a fourth sheet of the correct rag stock—aged properly in the smoke of the lamp, because new paper smelled like new paper to a man whose trade was paper—and he became, for six hours, four different men.

It was not the drawing of letters that frightened him. Letters were safe. Letters were his country. What frightened him was the arithmetic underneath: that a document was only ever a frozen moment of other people's trust, and he was manufacturing trust out of lampblack and linseed, and if he was wrong, Rafael would hang for his handwriting.

He wrote the clerk's hand first, mean and windblown: *One crate, cast-iron type molds, assorted, forty-one pieces, gross weight 214 lb.* Then the carter's scrawl and the deliberate smudge. The broker's stamp he carved himself, in red composition, from a wax impression taken off a genuine sheet Marta had procured for him from a man who owed her favors—crooked, he reminded himself, stamp it crooked, laziness is the signature of authenticity.

By the time the stove had burned down to coals he held the finished sheet at arm's length and could not find the seams. Every hand was true. Even the little coffee ring near the bottom edge was right—a real waybill lived on real tables.

"There," he whispered, and then, because the room was empty and the confession cost nothing: "God forgive me, there."

He told himself he did it for Wren. That was the story he had built in the nights since the bridge—since the night he'd stood in the doorway of the shop with his heart hammering while Rafael came back through the fog with the screaming bundle pressed to her chest and snow in her hair, and something in his chest had permanently rearranged itself around the sight. He did it for Wren, who had a cry like a small rusty hinge and a grip that could hold a finger with the seriousness of a drowning man, and who would grow up in a city where a letter could hang half a neighborhood unless somebody moved it somewhere it couldn't.

But lying awake afterward, staring at the rafters, Tomas admitted the truer thing, the thing he'd admit to no one: he had copied the letter weeks ago. He had sent word down the underground to a printer in Cormansey. He was already in the river up to his chin, and this shipment, these perfect papers, were just one more stroke toward the far bank. You could not wade back to being the person who only set type and swept the shop. That person had been washed away somewhere between the first milk he'd stolen for the baby and the first names he'd memorized from a document that could kill them all.

He fell asleep at last with his hands burning, and dreamed of letters marching off their composing sticks in ranks, armed, going somewhere without him.

* * *

Rafael

They packed the crate by lamplight with the shutters closed, working like priests preparing an altar.

Rafael had taken the letter out of its hiding place behind the loose brick only once since the bridge—once, at midnight, to rewrap it—and her hands remembered the shape of it now: stiff, sealed, heavier than its ounce of paper. She had stopped reading it months ago. Reading it changed nothing except how well she slept.

Now she laid it flat on the bench and studied it as an object rather than a weapon. Then she cut a rectangle of thin sheet lead from the scrap bin, folded it into a sleeve, and encased the letter completely—so that even a man who knew what paper felt like through canvas would find only metal. Around it she built a fiction: oiled paper, twine, a stenciled number matching the manifest line, and above and below it, packed tight as teeth, the honest cast-iron molds themselves, forty pounds of them, cold and blameless.

"Forty-one pieces on the manifest," Tomas said, watching her. "You're putting in forty-two."

"Am I?" She tied the last knot. "Then whoever counts them had better count carelessly."

At dawn the carter came, a stooped man named Pell who asked no questions because questions slowed the horse. They loaded the crate, Tomas handed up the papers with a casualness so studied that Rafael had to step on his foot, and the cart rolled away through streets still blue with morning, down toward the water and the custom house at Quai des Salines.

Rafael did not follow the cart. Following freight was what a guilty person did. Instead she opened the shop, set type for a run of wedding invitations—a rich man's daughter, cream stock, a dove motif—and tried to lose herself in the small honest tyranny of justified lines.

She lasted until noon. Then she put her coat on, told her father she was buying candles, and walked down to the river with her heart beating in her ears like a second pulse.

* * *

The custom house at Quai des Salines was a long brick shed with its doors open to the cold, and freight went in one side and came out the other, and between the two sides stood a table, and behind the table stood the searchers.

Rafael could not go inside—that was for drivers and brokers—but the yard beyond the doors was public cobbles, and carts waited there in a tangle of horses and breath, and she found a place against a wall where a brazier's smoke half veiled her, and she watched the shed mouth like a woman watching a surgeon's door.

She saw Pell's cart go in. She saw the crate lifted down by two searchers who handled it with the resentment heavy things deserve. And then she saw the tall searcher—the one with the ledger and the bored mouth—tap the crate lid twice and say something that made the other one laugh, and her stomach dropped through the cobbles, because that laugh had a shape she recognized. It was the shape of *let's make work for ourselves.*

They broke the lid.

From twenty yards she could see almost nothing—shoulders, arms, the flash of iron pieces coming out and going onto the table in rows. But she could see the tall searcher's hands, and she watched those hands the way sailors watch weather. Piece after piece, held to the light, weighed, returned. The manifest consulted. The count begun.

Forty. Forty-one.

His hands stopped moving.

Even at that distance, even across cold air and coal smoke, Rafael saw the pause land on him like a dropped stitch—the small straightening of the shoulders, the tilt of the head, the way a man leans toward a thing that isn't on his list. His fingers came up holding something smaller than the molds. Turning it over. Finding it heavier than paper should—

And then a voice at the table said, "Papers," and Tomas stepped out of the crowd of carters into the lamplight with the waybill in his hand, and Rafael forgot how to breathe.

She had not known he would come. She had not allowed him to come. He was supposed to be at the shop, sanding her alibi smooth with his presence, and instead he was here, walking toward the table with his cap in his fist like a country boy delivering eggs, holding sheets of paper that were four lies deep.

The tall searcher held out his palm for the documents without looking up from the thing in his other hand.

Tomos gave him the waybill. Rafael saw the searcher read it upside down first, the way professionals do, hunting for wrongness in the shapes of words before their meanings. Then right way up. Then the broker's stamp. Then he looked at Tomas—looked at him properly, the full head-to-boots inventory that could strip a man to his probabilities—and said something.

Tomas shrugged. Smiled. Said something back, and gestured at the crate with the easy disgust of a workman whose freight is being delayed, and the gesture was so perfectly ordinary, so boringly human, that Rafael felt tears start behind her eyes and hated herself for them.

The searcher with the ledger ambled over. The two of them conferred over the papers. One of them—she couldn't tell which—tapped the coffee ring on the corner of the sheet, and Tomas leaned in and laughed, and whatever he said made the ledger man laugh too, because everyone knew the broker, everyone had stories about the broker, and a shared contempt is the fastest bond two strangers can forge.

The tall searcher was still holding the small dense thing.

Put it down, Rafael begged him silently, pressing her skull back against the bricks. Put it down. It's a mold, it's a miscount, it's nothing, it's the most boring object in this entire building—

A whistle went up the quay—a harbor signal, a ship coming in on the tide—and the whole yard shifted, carters shouting, gulls rising. In the confusion the ledger man slapped the waybill against the crate lid for the counter-sign, crooked, the way the real broker always stamped, and shouted the magic words: "Papers square! Count's off by one, foundry's problem, log it and move it!"

Move it.

The tall searcher stood one more second with the lead-sleeved letter in his hand, and Rafael understood with terrible clarity that the entire rest of her life was balanced on the point of that man's boredom. Then the whistle blew again, longer, and he sighed from his boots upward, tossed the piece back into the straw with the others, hammered the lid down under Pell's indignant supervision, and waved the cart through to the far doors, where the river light waited.

Rafael stayed against the wall until Pell's cart was a speck on the downriver road. She stayed until her legs agreed to hold her. When she finally pushed off the bricks, Tomas was standing beside her—she hadn't seen him cross the yard—with snow in his hair and a grin breaking across his face like ice cracking off a hull.

"Papers square," he said. "Count's off by one."

She hit his shoulder, hard, once. Then she gripped his sleeve, hard, once, which from her was the same as weeping, and they both knew it, and neither said so.

"It went through," he said. "Rafael. It went *through.*"

"It went through," she agreed.

And then, because she was who she was, she made him walk her through it, every detail, twice—the pause, the papers, the laugh, the stamp—and only when his account matched her watching did she let the relief in, let it fill her like warmth into frozen hands.

Which was, of course, the mistake. Relief was the moment the careless died in this city.

They were halfway home, turned up the Rue Basse with the light failing, when she saw the figure waiting outside the print shop, leaning against the doorpost with the patience of furniture, and her blood went from warm to stone in a single beat.

Not a soldier. Worse: a clerk-shaped man with a leather folio, and a face she knew from the checkpoint on the bridge.

Hessler's man. And behind the shop's lit window, her father was standing, and in his arms, wrapped in blanket, Wren was crying.
