## 9. Winter Takes the Shop

The cold came for the shop the way debt comes for a debtor—slowly at first, in polite increments, and then all at once, with its hand around your throat.

I noticed it first in the ink. Father kept the good iron-gall mix in the cellar where the temperature held steady, but even there it had begun to thicken, crawling sluggish across the composing stone like something trying to remember how to move. Then the damp crept up through the floorboards and the paper began to buckle in its reams. Then Wren woke at three in the morning with a cry that had a new note in it, thin as wire, and I put my bare hand against the brick of the chimney and felt nothing but stone.

The coal was gone. That was the plain arithmetic of it. We had burned the last of it in the second week of December, and since then we'd been feeding the stove with broken furniture—first the chair from the office, then the packing crates, then the length of handsome mahogany that had been our display shelf back when we'd had books fine enough to deserve displaying. Tomas pried up a section of the back stairs and I let him, though every board that came up sounded to me like a rib cracking.

"We'll manage," my father said, which was what he always said, and which this winter had become less a reassurance than a symptom.

He sat by the stove wrapped in his own blanket plus the one that had been my mother's, working by lamplight at the proofing table because he could no longer afford candles enough for two rooms. His cough had arrived in November as an occasional interruption and had settled in now like a tenant paying rent in phlegm. When he leaned over a galley of type his breath whistled faintly, a sound I could hear across the room if everything else went quiet, and everything else went quiet often, because the city outside had gone quiet too. Snow muffled the streets. The patrols moved slower. Even the checkpoints seemed bored of us.

Wren, oblivious, had learned to grab. She seized whatever came within reach of her fist—my sleeve, Tomas's collar, a stray piece of type she would certainly have tried to eat—and held on with the astonishing, unreasonable strength of infants, as if the whole world were a rope she refused to let go of. She was five months old. She had no idea what winter meant. She thought the world was made entirely of faces, and that all of them belonged to her.

I envied her that, the way I envied everyone anything, quietly and without letting it slow me down.

* * *

On the morning my father could not get out of bed, the arithmetic stopped being metaphorical.

Tomas found him first. I came downstairs to the smell of the fire Tomas had built from the last of the stair-boards and heard him calling my name in a voice pitched wrong—not alarmed, exactly; Tomas did not permit himself alarm—but urgent in the flat way he reserved for genuine emergencies.

"He won't wake properly." Tomas stood at the foot of the bed with Wren balanced awkwardly on his hip, an arrangement that had become common without either of them discussing it. "He opened his eyes when I shook him and asked me if I'd set the forme for the *Gazette*, and then he closed them again, and Rafael, the *Gazette* hasn't been printed since October."

My father's face was gray in the gray morning light. The skin over his cheekbones had gone translucent, papery, so that I could see the architecture beneath—the skull asserting itself through the man. His breathing was quick and shallow and wet. When I got my arm behind his shoulders and lifted him toward the water cup, heat poured off him like a shutter opened onto a bake-oven. He weighed almost nothing. When had he come to weigh almost nothing?

"Father. Papa. Open your eyes."

"Mm." His eyes opened, unfocused, finding me somewhere near me but not at me. "Rafael. Don't take the—don't sell the long primes."

It took me a moment to understand, and when I understood I felt the floor tilt under my understanding. He had been dreaming of this. Of course he had. He dreamed in type the way other men dream in faces.

"I won't," I said.

"You'll want to. The long primes fetch well. There's never enough of them." His eyes drifted shut again. "Don't sell the long primes, Raffie. A press without its range is a—" The cough took him, a long tearing sequence that bent him double and left a rust-colored spot on the cloth when he turned his head away. Not blood. Not quite. Rust-colored, though, which is a color that lives next door to blood and borrows its things.

Tomas looked at me over Wren's head. "The physician on Chandler Street charges a crown for a visit," he said. "And more for the medicine after."

"And the apothecary?"

"Less. But he sells less medicine, so you buy twice."

I looked around the shop. I had been looking around it all winter, doing inventory of what remained, measuring survival the way I always measured it: in what could be carried, or in this case, in what could be sold. The press itself was unthinkable. The stock of paper was already mortgaged against the pamphlet work. The furniture, mostly burned. What remained—what had always been the real wealth of the place, the wealth that mattered more than chairs or shelves or even the press, though Father would have struck me for saying so—was the type.

Four cases of it, maybe five hundredweight of lead alloy, sorted into compartments as fine and various as a city seen from above. Every letter the language required, from the great fat display romans down to the long primes, those narrow elegant figures Father loved because they let a line hold more meaning in less width, which he called economy and I called showing off.

You cannot print without type. You can print with *less* type, but only just, and only if you are willing to reset every page by hand between impressions, which turns an afternoon's work into three days' and makes every job a small act of penance. Selling type was selling the shop's blood. Everyone in the trade knew it. It was the thing you did last, the thing you did instead of dying.

"Don't sell the long primes," my father said again, softly, to the ceiling.

* * *

The type founder on Foundry Lane bought by weight and paid by the pound, which is the coldest possible way to think about letters.

His name was Herr Brandt and he was a broad, burn-scarred man with forearms like hams and a face that gave away nothing, which I respected. I brought the type to him in a flour sack, having chosen it the night before while the others slept—or pretended to sleep, in Tomas's case, because I felt his eyes on me from his cot in the corner as I sat at the stone with a candle stub, going compartment by compartment through the case like a woman deciding which of her children to send away.

I did not take the long primes. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here. Whatever else I did that winter, I kept my father's long primes.

What I took instead was the case of old blackletter—a face half out of fashion, used mostly for proclamations and funerary cards, worth a fraction per pound of what the modern faces fetched, which meant I had to carry more of it, which meant the sack dragged at my shoulder like a corpse through the snow-slicked lanes, which seemed fitting.

Brandt weighed it. The scale's needle swung and settled and he named a figure so low that I laughed before I could stop myself, a short ugly sound.

"It's worn," he said. "See the shoulders, there, and there. Rounded off. This face has been worked hard."

"It's been *used*. By a master. There's a difference."

"There is, and it's worth a quarter-crown more than I offered, and not a pfennig beyond that." He looked at me over the scale. He knew who I was; everyone in the printing trades knew everyone, and everyone knew Fairweather's shop had been running thin since the crackdowns started strangling the legitimate work and the illegitimate work got dangerous enough that only fools and idealists took it. "You can walk it to Voss across the river, if you don't mind the checkpoint and the extra hour and Voss cheating you differently. Or you can take my money and buy your father's medicine, because I can see from your face that it's medicine."

The snow was coming through the door when I opened it. My shoulder ached where the sack had cut it. Somewhere across town my father was burning at a temperature no fire could account for, and Wren would need feeding within the hour, and the stove had perhaps two boards left in it.

"The blackletter's worth more as blackletter than as lead," I said, one last time, hearing the foolishness of it even as I said it.

"Everything is worth more as what it is," Brandt said. "That's the whole trouble with selling things."

I took his money.

* * *

The apothecary on Chandler Street—less expensive, twice purchased—sold me a brown bottle of something bitter and a twist of paper containing willow bark and another containing something he described as *warming*, and warned me with the gravity of a priest about keeping the fever down, keeping liquids in him, keeping the room warm. Keeping the room warm. I thanked him for his precision and walked home past the bridge checkpoint where a guard I knew by sight—he had a lazy eye and a habit of tapping his baton against his boot—was stamping papers for a cartload of frozen fish, and I felt the letter's shape against my body where it wasn't, because I had hidden it months ago under the floor of the press pit, but I still felt it, the way an amputee feels a hand.

That evening I brewed the medicine in the dented pot we used for glue-size, having washed it, and fed it to my father a spoonful at a time, and he surfaced from his fever enough to know me, and the knowing me was worse than the not, because his eyes filled.

"Raffie. What did you—"

"Old stock. Blackletter. Half obsolete anyway."

"Half obsolete," he repeated, and closed his eyes, and a tear ran sideways into the pillow, and I understood that he understood exactly what I had done, down to the pound and the pfennig, and that there was nothing he could say about it that would not make it heavier, so he said nothing, and neither did I, and the silence between us was the truest conversation we had had in a year.

* * *

Winter did not relent because we had suffered adequately. Winter does not read the room.

Through the week that followed I sold three more trips to Brandt: the display roman, half the italic case, and—the blow that hurt worst, worse than any of it, because it was not strategy but simple hunger—the brass ornaments, the fleurons and rules and the lovely engraved headpiece of a laurel wreath that had framed every title page of consequence the shop ever produced. Brandt paid better for brass. Brandt was cheerful about brass. I hated him a little more each visit and he never noticed or never minded.

Each trip home the sack was lighter and the shop was poorer and I told myself the story that keeps such transactions survivable: *this is temporary. Spring comes. Work returns. We rebuild.* It was a good story. I told it well. But every night I lay beside Wren's basket and listened to my father breathe—better now, the medicine working, the fever breaking in sweats that soaked the sheets and were worth soaking—and I did the honest accounting underneath the story, which went like this:

A press with half its type cannot bid for the almanac work, or the municipal printing, or the merchant manifests, the steady bread-and-butter jobs that keep a shop alive between risks. A press with half its type can print pamphlets, and pamphlets are what get shops raided. I was not weakening the business to save us from winter. I was weakening the business in a way that made the *dangerous* work the only work left, which meant that the medicine I bought for my father was purchased, in part, with the coin of future raids. The present was eating the future alive, and I was holding its jaw open.

And underneath even that accounting, in the cellar-dark of my own thoughts, sat the other ledger, the one I tried not to open: the letter. The names. The leverage sewn into its folds. There were men in this city who would pay very well to have it destroyed, and men who would pay better to have it delivered, and either payment would have bought a winter's coal and a physician and a new case of type and left change besides. I had carried it past nothing yet. I had let it lie in the dark under the press like a debt accruing interest, and every day the interest compounded in coal and bread and now in lead, in the hollowing-out of everything my father had spent forty years casting letter by letter.

The letter was worth more than the shop. That was the truth I kept arriving at and kept refusing. And I did not know anymore whether I was protecting my family from it or hoarding it against my family's need, and the not-knowing sat in my chest some nights like a second set of lungs.

* * *

On the ninth evening, my father came downstairs.

He descended slowly, one step and a pause, gripping the rail where the missing boards showed their pale saw-teeth, and Tomas hovered behind him with his arms half-raised, ready to catch a falling patriarch. Wren sat on a blanket on the warm-stone hearth practicing the art of falling over sideways, which she had nearly perfected. When my father reached the bottom he stood a moment getting his bearings, and I watched him look at the shop.

At what was left of the shop.

He did not say anything. He crossed the room—heavier on one foot than the other now, as if the illness had given him a limp to remember it by—and stood at the empty composing stone and laid his palm flat upon it, the way you'd touch the flank of a horse you feared you'd have to sell. The case where the blackletter had lived stood open and vacant, its compartments like empty beds in an orphanage.

"The ornaments too," he said. Not a question.

"The ornaments too."

He nodded slowly. Then he said, in a voice still scraped raw, "Set me a stick of the long primes."

"Papa, you should be—"

"*Set me a stick,* Rafael."

So I set him a stick of the long primes, and I put it in his hands, and he held it up to the lamplight and read it upside down as compositors do, and what it said was: *THE WORD OUTLASTS THE HAND THAT SET IT.* He read it twice. His eyes were wet again but his mouth had found its old stubborn line.

"Yes," he said. "Well. That's true, at least, whatever else is."

Then he looked up at me, and the fever was out of his eyes but something else had moved into the vacancy, something clear and cold and deliberate, and he said: "You've been carrying it too long, girl. The letter. I've known since the night it came—you read it by candlelight and then lied to my face about burning it, and you're a bad liar and always have been." He set the stick of type down on the stone with a click like a cocked trigger. "Whatever you mean to do with it, do it soon. Winter's eating us faster than your sales can feed us, and Hessler's men were at the Custom House on Tuesday asking after shipments out of this street. Asking after *paper* shipments, Rafael."

Behind me, Wren toppled gently sideways on her blanket and laughed at the floor.

I looked at my father, who had nearly died and come back with nothing but clarity; at Tomas, who had gone white as unprinted paper at the mention of Hessler; at the empty cases and the boarded windows and the small laughing child at the center of it all, holding nothing, needing everything.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow I would sew the letter into a returned library book and start walking toward the bridge.

But tomorrow, as it turned out, had plans of its own.
