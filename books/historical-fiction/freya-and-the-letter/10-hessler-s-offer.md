## 10. Hessler's Offer

He came on a Tuesday, when the snow had turned to that grey porridge that is neither falling nor melting but simply hanging in the air like a decision no one will make. I was alone in the shop. My father was upstairs sleeping off the fever, Tomas had been sent to the paper merchant in the next quarter, and Wren—Wren was asleep in the drawer of the old proofing table, which had become her cradle by degrees, first a place to set her down for a moment, then an hour, then simply hers.

The bell over the door gave its small broken sound—it had never rung properly since the winter before last, when my father said he would fix it and instead fixed three other things—and Agent Hessler stepped through it stamping slush from his boots with the neatness of a man who resents mess but expects to make it.

"Signorina Fairweather." He removed his hat. Underneath, his hair was combed with mathematical precision, not one strand out of place, as though disorder itself were something he had personally arrested. "You are alone?"

"My father rests. He is unwell."

"So I have heard. Illness walks this district like a creditor." He glanced around the shop with the unhurried appetite of a man pricing furniture at an estate sale. His eyes went over the cases of type, the press with its iron arm raised like a gallows beam, the samples of recent work pinned to a corkboard—a wedding announcement, a bill of lading, a funeral notice with a heavy black border. They did not go, so far as I could see, to the proofing table. But I had learned already that Hessler's eyes were a distraction from Hessler's attention, and that the two were rarely pointed at the same thing.

"How may I help you, sir? We are printing nothing seditious today. Only invitations."

"Invitations." He smiled. He had good teeth; it seemed unfair. "To what?"

"A name day. The Vosshall girl."

"Ah. Then I congratulate the Vosshall girl." He walked to the press and laid two fingers on the platen as if taking its pulse. "May I sit? I find I think better sitting, and I have come to think."

I pulled out the stool we kept for customers who waited on proofs. He sat, arranged his coat, crossed one ankle over the other knee, and studied the sole of his boot with mild disgust.

"You will want to sweep," he said.

"I will want you to state your business, sir, so that I may sweep sooner."

That earned me a real look—not the smile, not the performance, but a quick flat assessing glance, the kind a moneylender gives a ledger. I had seen it once before, the day after the Custom House, when he had stood across the market square watching our crate being reloaded and had looked at me exactly that way, the way a man looks at a lock he has decided is worth picking.

"Your business," he said, "is ink and metal and paper. That is what I am told. A respectable trade, a struggling trade, a trade kept barely breathing by a father going blind and a daughter doing the work of three men. Am I close?"

"You are describing my life, sir. You have not yet stated yours."

"Mine," he said, "is questions." And then, without any change of tone whatsoever: "Where is the child?"

I had prepared for this. I want that understood—not by you, whoever you are, but by myself, now, writing it down: I had prepared. Since the day Hessler first appeared at the bridge checkpoint with his list of faces, I had rehearsed this moment the way Tomas rehearses a forgery, over and over, adjusting the weight of each word until no stroke showed. The child sleeps upstairs. The child is my cousin's, left in our care while she seeks work in the capital. The child—

"The child?" I said, and heard my own voice do something terrible. It hesitated. Not long. A quarter of a second, perhaps less—the space between two heartbeats—but Hessler's whole face changed the way a still pond changes when a fish turns under the ice. Nothing moves and everything is different.

"Mm," he said softly. "So there is a child."

"There is a drawer," I said, "full of type." I did not look at the proofing table. I would like to report that I did not look, but the truth is my eyes flicked there—one small traitorous movement—and Hessler's followed mine, and rested, and stayed.

* * *

Let me tell you what happened in the next four seconds, because four seconds is a long time when everything you love is asleep in a wooden drawer four paces from a government agent.

Hessler stood. I stood. He took a step toward the table and I moved—not between him and it, quite; that would have been a confession in itself—but sideways, toward the shelf where we keep the customer accounts, and I lifted the ledger down as though to consult it, and said, loudly enough to reach the stairs, "Papa! Come down and mind the shop!"

It was a gamble. My father could no more come down those stairs quickly than he could fly. But the voice of an old man carries, and above us I heard the groan of the bed, the shuffle of slippers, the theatrical cough he had perfected in forty years of dealing with creditors—and Hessler stopped. He stood with one hand resting on the proofing table's edge, fingers inches from the drawer where Wren lay breathing her small warm breaths, and he listened to my father descend the stairs at the speed of pouring honey, and he smiled again, and withdrew his hand.

"Another time," he said, and put on his hat.

My father came through the doorway buttoning his coat over his nightshirt, white-faced, playing his part without knowing there was one. "Sir. Welcome. Forgive the disorder—"

"There is no disorder," Hessler said courteously, "that I can see." And at *see*, very lightly, he laid a stress, the way a compositor might italicize a word, and looked at me while he said it. "Good day to you both."

The bell made its broken sound behind him. Through the window I watched him cross the street, pause to buy roasted chestnuts from the brazier on the corner—paying, shaking his head at the offered bag, eating one as he walked—and turn toward the Custom House as though he had all the time in the world, which, I understood suddenly and with a coldness in my stomach, he did.

My father looked at me. "Rafaela. What was that?"

"Nothing, Papa." The lie came out worn smooth, like a coin passed too many times. "An inspector. Rates and licenses."

He was losing his sight, not his mind. He held my gaze for a moment with those clouded eyes that still saw more than they let on, then nodded slowly and turned back toward the stairs. Halfway up he stopped.

"The drawer," he said quietly, "should be moved to Marta's. Days like this, it should live at Marta's."

* * *

He was right, of course, and that evening Wren slept in the dyer's back room among the hanging bolts of cloth, wrapped in wool that smelled of madder and indigo, and I sat up late in the shop alone with a candle and my fear, doing sums.

I have always been good at sums. It is the compositor's curse: you learn to count everything. Em-quads and en-quads, lines of measure, sheets per hour, sous per week. You learn that every page has a cost and every cost must be met. So I sat and I counted what I had and what I owed, and here is the honest ledger of that night:

I had a father going blind, coughing blood-flecked spots into his handkerchief when he thought no one heard. I had an apprentice of seventeen whose quick hands had forged shipping papers once already for my sake and who would forge them again, and whose neck was therefore already in the rope from the collarbone up. I had a neighbor breaking the law daily out of simple kindness, leaving milk at doors. I had a letter sewn into the lining of my father's winter coat—yes, we had moved it again; the coat hung in the closet like a fat silent accomplice—with nine names written in a dying woman's hand, nine men, any one of whom, arrested and questioned, would give up the print shop inside an hour because everyone gives up everything inside an hour; I have seen it; that is what questionings are *for*. And I had a child named Wren who would never know, if things went wrong, whether she had ever existed at all.

And against all that, Hessler had set a single number. He set it the next morning, because of course he came back. Men like Hessler always come back; the second visit is the real one; the first is only reconnaissance.

* * *

He arrived at ten, when the light through the shop windows is best for reading proofs, and he did not bring soldiers. That was the first thing I noticed, and I understood it was deliberate: he wanted no witnesses except the ones who already knew. He brought instead a small paper-wrapped parcel of candied ginger, which he set on the counter like a bribe from a parish priest, and he said, "For your father. I hear ginger settles the chest."

"Your informants are well informed."

"It is why I employ them." He declined the stool this time; he preferred to stand near the door, I noticed, in the position of a man keeping his exit. Or perhaps—I revised this within the same breath—in the position of a man who wishes to be seen through the window by anyone passing. A courtesy, or a threat. With Hessler the two shared a border.

"Signorina Fairweather," he said, "I am going to speak plainly, and I ask you to believe that plain speaking is a luxury I permit myself perhaps twice a year."

"Then I am honored to be the second."

"You are the first. The second has not yet been chosen." He folded his hands. "Before the New Year, this district will be swept. You have seen the new orders at the bridges; you will see more. When the sweep comes, houses are searched, papers are read, presses are—examined. A shop such as this one, with its history—" he touched, lightly, the frame of the corkboard where our lawful work hung—"would fare poorly. Your father in particular. Old men do not do well in the questions I told you about. There is a cell under the Custom House that is cold even in summer, and it is not summer."

"And if I weep, sir, will you stop?"

"If you weep, I will wait." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "But there is another road, and it is short, and I am the only map. Give me the letter. You know the one I mean; do not insult us both. A woman wrote it before she died talking. Names, dates, meeting places. It exists. It passed through this quarter in December like a coin through a beggar's fingers, and every hand it touched is now a hand I must account for. Give it to me, and give me freely—freely is important—the testimony of what you know, and I will tell you what happens: nothing. No search. No sweep of this address. The shop prints its invitations and its bills of lading through spring. Your father grows old upstairs. The girl—" and here his voice did not change at all, and that was the masterpiece of it—"grows up behind a press, learning letters, safe as any citizen's child. That is the offer. It does not expire today, but it expires."

I made myself breathe. I made myself look bored, which is the hardest expression to fake when your blood is ringing in your ears like a struck bell.

"You are offering to sell me my own family," I said, "with goods you admit you cannot prove I have."

"I am offering to *not* prove it," Hessler said. "There is a difference, and the difference is worth more than you think. Proof is expensive. I would rather have the letter than the proof. Consider which you would rather lose."

He tipped his hat then—he had not even taken it off—and left the ginger on the counter, and the bell made its broken sound, and I stood alone in the best light of the morning hating him with a completeness that frightened me, because underneath the hate, in a cellar of myself I do not like to enter, something else was counting.

Here is what the something-else counted: that the letter named men who had protected us, and giving it up would hang them. That refusing might hang my father. That Hessler had said *the girl* and meant it—he knew she existed, he had known since Tuesday, and he had done nothing, which meant either that he was waiting or that Wren was already part of the price he intended to collect. That he had eaten a chestnut on the corner like any man. That evil, I was learning, does not arrive shouting; it arrives early, well dressed, carrying ginger for your sick father, and it waits politely while you do the arithmetic yourself, because arithmetic done by the victim is so much more thorough than anything interrogators can manage.

I took the ginger to the back room and stood holding it over the stove for a long moment. Then I did not burn it. Waste is a sin in a house like ours; my father taught me that; and besides, some cold practical animal in me noted that ginger settles the chest, and my father's chest needed settling, and the ginger cost me nothing now that my soul was already on the counter.

* * *

Tomas found me there an hour later, still standing at the cold stove. He read my face the way he reads proofs—at speed, catching errors.

"He made an offer."

"How do you know?"

"Because you're standing exactly the way Papa stands after the rent collector leaves." He hung his coat on its peg. "What did he want?"

"The letter. And my testimony. In exchange, the shop survives the sweep." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin as tin. "Do you understand what he has done, Tomas? He has made betrayal sound like housekeeping. Sweep the district, spare the shop. Tidy. Efficient. As if the only question were where to store my conscience."

Tomas was quiet. He picked up the composing stick from the case—his habit when thinking, weighing the empty brass in his palm—and I watched the thought move across his face and I knew, with the awful clarity of the sleepless, that whatever he was about to say would change the shape of the winter.

"Rafael," he said slowly. "If he wants the letter that badly—if he'll trade a whole sweep for it—then it isn't evidence anymore. It's currency. Which means someone else might pay for it too."

I stared at him. Outside, the grey snow finally committed itself and began to fall, thickly, erasing the street, the checkpoint smoke, the tracks of a man who had walked here twice in three days and would walk here again.

"Someone else," I repeated. And then, from the doorway behind us, in a voice like wet wool, came old Marta, breathless, snow in her hair, still wearing her apron:

"Girl. You'd best come. There's a woman asking after faces at my door—and she isn't Hessler's."
