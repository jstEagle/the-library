## 11. The Neighbor Who Knew

Marta Voss had been dyeing cloth for fifty-one years, and in that time her hands had taken on colors that would not wash out: the blue-black of woad under her nails, a rust-brown stain across both palms from madder root, and along the creases of her knuckles a violet shadow that no amount of lye could lift. When she was young she had been ashamed of those hands. Now they were old and the shame had worn away like dye in sun, and what remained was this: she knew color. She knew it the way priests know scripture, the way butchers know bone. She could tell a lie dyed over truth at forty paces.

Which is why, when the woman came down Tanner's Row in the thin gray light of an October morning with a bundle under her cloak and terror coming off her like heat off a kiln, Marta looked up from her vats and *knew* her.

Not by name. Names were dangerous currency and Marta had stopped trading in them years ago. But by color — oh, yes. The woman had come to her stall twice the previous spring, once to buy a length of undyed linen and once, curiously, to buy a swatch of cloth dyed the particular deep green that the wife of Captain Aldous Renner wore to market. Marta remembered thinking then: *a servant buying her mistress's color.* And now here was the same servant, or the same kind of woman anyway — same narrow shoulders, same way of walking close to walls — and where her belly had swelled in spring there was now only the bundle, and the bundle was making small sounds.

Marta did not move. She stood among her vats with her dripping paddle in her hand and watched the woman stop at the door of Fairweather's print shop, stand there a long moment as if the door might open on its own, and then bend down and set the bundle against the threshold. She tucked something white beneath the child's blanket. Then she straightened, pulled her hood lower, and walked away without once looking back, which was itself a kind of confession, because innocent people always look back.

The whole thing took less time than it takes to dye a handkerchief. The street stayed empty. The city, at that hour, belonged to cats and bakers and people who wished not to be seen, and Marta Voss had long ago earned her place among them.

She thought: *someone will find that child.*

She thought: *it will be me, or it will be worse than me.*

She dipped her paddle back into the vat and stirred, and the wool beneath it turned slowly from pale gray to the red of arterial blood, and above the row the sky went on being the color of nothing at all.

* * *

The milk began three days later.

It was not sentiment, Marta told herself. Sentiment was for women whose hands were clean. It was arithmetic. A child fed was a child quiet, and a child quiet was a shop unsearched, and a shop unsearched was a street unmolested, and Tanner's Row had survived thirty years of regimes by being too dull to bother with. If the Fairweather household had lost its mind enough to keep an abandoned infant — and Marta, who heard everything through the shared wall of her drying loft, knew within a week that they had; she had heard the crying, heard the girl's low singing at two in the morning, heard old Fairweather's cracked voice attempting a lullaby that sounded like a hinge complaining — then the least the street could do was keep the child's lungs from summoning the watch.

So she left milk. A jug at first, set by their door before dawn, wrapped in sacking against the cold. Then bread when she had bread to spare. Once, a twist of dried chamomile with no note, because notes were how people got hanged. She never knocked. She never let herself be seen leaving it. When Rafael Fairweather stood in the doorway one morning holding the jug and looking up and down the row with that sharp, guarded face of hers — a face like a blade someone had tried very hard to make plain — Marta was already back inside her shop, stirring blue into wool, listening to the girl set the jug down carefully, the way one sets down something that might be evidence.

They think I'm a fool, Marta thought, with something that was almost amusement. They think nobody sees anything on this street.

Nobody saw anything on this street because everybody on this street had agreed, wordlessly, over decades, to see nothing together. That was the arrangement. That was the whole fragile treaty of the neighborhood: the dyer does not ask about the pamphlets that go out of the print shop sewn into returned library books; the printer does not ask why the dyer buys lampblack in quantities no cloth-worker needs; the fishmonger does not ask where the second bed in Marta's loft is slept in during bad months by people who arrive after dark and leave before dawn. Everyone owed everyone. No one signed anything. It was the only constitution Marta had ever believed in, and she had outlived four governments by keeping faith with it.

But a child changed the arithmetic. A child was not a pamphlet, not a fugitive who had chosen his risks, not a debt between neighbors. A child was a fire in a house of paper, and everyone on Tanner's Row was now sitting around it pretending to warm their hands at something else.

* * *

In December the cold came down like a verdict, and old Fairweather took to his bed, and Marta heard through the wall the particular rhythm of illness: the coughing that starts performative and becomes real, the silences that grow longer, the daughter's footsteps wearing a groove between the press-room and the stairs. She also heard something else, later that month — heard it because she had gone up to her loft at night to check the drying racks and had stood frozen with a candle guttering in her hand.

Voices below the window. Two men. One of them had the flat, unhurried delivery of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice to be obeyed.

"—asking after a woman with an infant, arriving in autumn. The mother is in custody. She has given a district." A pause. "This district."

And the other voice — Fairweather's girl, low and even and so fluently calm that Marta felt the hair rise on her arms — saying something about tenants, about turnover, about how half the quarter moved through rooms like these, sir, you'd have to be more specific. Lying. Lying beautifully. Marta had lied to officials for thirty years and she knew mastery when she heard it through a wall.

The men moved on. Marta stood in her cold loft a long while afterward, the candle trembling, and understood that the treaty of Tanner's Row was about to be tested in a way it had never been tested. Hessler's men did not pass through districts the way weather passes through. They settled. They asked. They came back.

That night she sat up with her accounts ledger open in front of her — the false one, the one for the officials — and she thought about the woman in October, the green-dyed swatch bought last spring, the captain's wife, the child crying through the wall. She thought: *I am seventy-three years old. I have buried a husband and two sons, one to fever and one to a press gang, and the government of this city owes me nothing and I owe it less.*

She thought: *if they take that child off this street, they will have learned that we can be made to give things up. And then everything ends — not loudly, not all at once, but the way a dye bath dies, gradually and then completely.*

She blew out the candle.

* * *

She chose the morning after the first hard frost, because frost meant the street would be empty, and she chose the moment, because moments mattered. Rafael Fairweather came out early to fetch water, wrapped in her father's too-big coat, her face raw with weeks of short sleep, and found the door wouldn't open more than a hand's width — blocked, as it happened, by a jug of milk, a wrapped loaf still warm from the ovens Marta bribed, and a knitted cap the size of a fist, dyed the soft yellow of weld.

Rafael stared at it. Then she stared up the row, and there, inevitably, was Marta Voss in her doorway, arms crossed against the cold, watching her with eyes the color of old brass.

"You," Rafael said.

"Me," Marta agreed. "Bring that in here before it freezes. Both of you."

"I don't—"

"The child," Marta said, "has been drinking my milk since October. Do you imagine I put a jug at your door every third morning because my household runs a surplus?" She turned and went inside, trusting — this was the gamble, the whole gamble laid down in a single gesture of a turning back — that the girl would follow.

She followed. Marta heard her scoop up the jug and the loaf, heard her hesitate over the cap, heard the door close behind her with the careful click of someone closing a door quietly for years and unable to stop.

* * *

Marta's shop smelled of everything at once: wet wool, madder, oak galls, tallow, the mineral tang of alum, and underneath it all the deep animal smell of decades of work. Rafael stood just inside it with Wren on her hip — she had brought the child in from the cold rather than leave her alone, and Marta noted the instinct approvingly — and looked around the room the way a soldier looks around a room he may have to defend.

"Sit," Marta said. "There's tea. There's no sugar. There hasn't been honest sugar since the Committee fixed prices, which is a story in itself if you ever want to lose faith in revolutions before they happen."

"We haven't been introduced," Rafael said. She had not sat.

"Marta Voss. Dyer. Fifty-one years at these vats, thirty-one years next door to your father, and I attended your mother's funeral, though you were too small to remember me and I wore my good shawl, which you would remember if you could, because it was hideous." She poured tea into two clay cups, pushed one across the scarred table, and nodded at Wren. "And this is the little one I've been feeding since she was a few days old. Which makes us acquainted, whether you like it or not."

Rafael lowered herself onto the stool slowly, as if the floor might be watching them. "You knew. From the beginning."

"I saw her leave the child. That morning in October. I was up at dawn with a batch of madder — it wants an early start — and I watched a frightened woman lay her baby at your father's door and walk away without looking back." Marta wrapped her stained hands around her cup. "People look back, girl. When they're innocent. When they're only leaving a parcel. She didn't look back, because looking back would have been the death of her, and she knew it, and knowing it told me everything."

"Did anyone else see?"

"No one. The street knows how to be empty when it needs to be." Marta drank. The tea was bitter and perfect. "But I'll tell you what else I know, since we're being honest, which I suspect neither of us enjoys. I know the mother. Not her name — names are for magistrates and graves. Her color. She came to my stall last spring and bought a swatch of the green that Captain Renner's wife wears to Sunday market. A servant buying her mistress's shade. I thought nothing of it then. I've thought a great deal of it since."

Rafael had gone very still. Wren, in her arms, reached up and seized a fistful of her mother's — her keeper's — collar with the absolute confidence of a child who has never once doubted she would be caught when she fell.

"The letter," Rafael said carefully. "You know about the letter."

"I know a woman left paper under a blanket along with coins, and I know that paper in this city is either worthless or worth dying for, and I know which one makes a mother abandon her child at a stranger's door in October." Marta set down her cup. "I don't want to read it. Understand that. I have survived by not reading things. But I want you to understand what you're standing in, Rafael Fairweather, because I don't think you fully do."

"Tell me, then."

"This street." Marta gestured toward the wall, beyond which Tanner's Row ran its short crooked length from the river road to the tannery gates. "Old Kessler at the fishmonger's, who has 'forgotten' to mention your deliveries for fifteen years. The Widow Anselm, who leaves firewood scraps by your back gate and tells her lodger they fell off a cart. Ferenc the farrier, who shoes every horse Hessler's men ride and hears every word said in the saddle, and passes what matters to no one — except, lately, to me, because I'm old and harmless and I bake better bread than God." She leaned forward. "There is a web, girl. There has always been a web. We built it out of silence, thread by thread, and none of us ever spoke of it aloud, and it has kept every one of us alive through four governments. You've been living inside it your whole life and calling yourself invisible."

"And now?"

"And now there's a child at the center of it, and a letter, and a man named Hessler asking questions in the night, and the web is straining." Marta held her gaze. "I'm not telling you to give the child up. I watched you carry her past my window for three months; I know what she is to you; I'm not a fool and neither are you. I'm telling you that you are not alone in this anymore, whatever you decide, whoever you run to, however it ends. When the knock comes — and it will come — you will not be the only one on this street deciding what to say to it."

Rafael looked down at Wren, who had fallen asleep against her shoulder with the totality that only infants and soldiers manage, and when she spoke her voice had a crack in it that all her fluent lying could not plaster over.

"Why now? Why tell me now?"

"Because your father is sick, and your apprentice looks like a boy carrying a secret too heavy for his back, and yesterday a man stopped me in the market to ask whether I'd noticed anything unusual at the print shop, and I said, 'What print shop?' and he wrote something down anyway." Marta smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "And because I recognized the mother, girl, and I've been carrying that recognition alone since October, and a web where one thread knows something the others don't isn't a web. It's a noose waiting for a neck."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The vats breathed their slow chemical breaths. Somewhere outside, a cart passed, iron-shod wheels on frozen cobbles, and both women tracked it by ear until it faded.

"She was the Renner household's maid," Rafael said at last, quietly. "The letter names the captain. Among others."

"Renner." Marta absorbed it the way cloth absorbs dye — completely, irreversibly. The man who commanded the river garrison. The man whose signature appeared on the checkpoint orders that tightened every few weeks like a knot. "Then that letter isn't leverage, girl. It's a lit match. And you're carrying it through a powder store."

"I know what I'm carrying."

"Do you?" Marta reached across the table — an old woman's liberty, taken without apology — and touched the sleeping child's yellow cap with one blue-stained finger. "I knew your grandmother. Did your father ever tell you that? A sharper woman than he'll ever be, and kinder than I managed. She used to say there are two kinds of secrets: the kind you keep to protect yourself, and the kind you keep because someone has to hold them until the world is safe enough to tell." She drew her hand back. "You've been holding this one alone because you thought that was the price of survival. But survival isn't solitude, Rafael Fairweather. Solitude is just the slower way to die."

Rafael's eyes were bright and hard and wet all at once. She did not cry. Marta suspected she had unlearned it.

"The milk," Rafael said finally. "Every third morning. Since October. And I never—" She stopped. Started again. "Thank you seems too small."

"It is too small," Marta agreed briskly, rising to cover the moment before it could become a scene, because scenes were dangerous and she was seventy-three and had a reputation for flint to maintain. "So pay me in honesty instead. From now on, when something moves against this shop — a question in the market, a lantern under your window, a face that doesn't belong on this row — it comes to me within a day. Not to spare me. To arm me. Do you understand? A web only holds if every thread pulls."

Rafael stood, shifting Wren gently to her other shoulder, and for a moment she looked younger than her years and older than her century, and Marta thought: *there she is. The one the letter landed on. God help us all.*

"I understand," Rafael said.

"Good." Marta opened the door, and the cold poured in like water. Down the row, Tanner's Row lay white and silent under the frost, chimneys smoking, shutters mostly closed, and every closed shutter — Marta saw it now with fresh eyes, and hoped the girl saw it too — every single one of them was a pair of eyes, pretending. "Take the bread. Take tomorrow's jug without wondering where it came from. And when this is over — whatever 'over' means for people like us — you'll sit at my table again and I'll tell you about your grandmother and the magistrate's carriage and the winter of the bread riots, and you'll wish I hadn't."

Something that was nearly a laugh escaped the girl — brief, rusty, startled out of her like a bird from cover. Then she stepped into the cold and turned back, one hand on the doorframe.

"Marta. The man who stopped you in the market. What did he look like?"

"Pale coat. Soft boots. The smile of a man who counts other people's teeth." Marta pulled her shawl tighter. "He gave his name as Hessler."

Rafael went still. And Marta watched the color drain from her face — watched, with a dyer's precision, the exact shade of fear as it took her — and heard her say, very quietly:

"He came to the shop yesterday. He bought a book."

He bought a book, and paid in new-minted coin, and stood in the shop for eleven minutes, and asked what other work the house printed besides Bibles and almanacs — and Marta, hearing it, felt the whole silent web of Tanner's Row tremble on its threads like a curtain in a draft, and reached out and gripped the girl's wrist with her old strong stained hand.

"Then it's begun," she said. "Go home, child. And send Tomas to me — yes, I know about Tomas — there's a delivery of wool coming through the alley tonight that needs two backs to carry, and I'd rather it wasn't seen."
