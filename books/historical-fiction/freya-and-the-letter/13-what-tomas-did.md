## 13. What Tomas Did

Tomas had been carrying the secret for six weeks, and it had grown heavier every day, the way a stone grows heavier the longer you hold it in one hand. He had rehearsed this confession on the walk from the type room to the kitchen above the shop perhaps forty times. In every rehearsal, Rafael listened. In none of them did she look at him the way she was looking at him now.

"You did *what*?"

Her voice was low, which was worse than shouting. Shouting could be muffled by walls and distance; this quiet was a blade laid flat against the table between them. The kitchen was dim—candle stubs were rationed now like everything else—and the snow coming down past the window made a sound like sand poured on paper. Wren slept in the basket by the hearth, her small fists curled against her ears as though even in sleep she knew what was coming.

"I copied it," Tomas said again. His hands would not stay still, so he pressed them flat against his knees. "The letter. That first week, before you tried the bridge. I waited until your father went to bed and I set the names by hand—only the names, and the dates, and the places where they meet. Two pages. Then I broke it down and distributed the type back into the cases so no one could ever prove it had been composed."

"You distributed—" Rafael stood up. The chair scraped. Wren stirred, and both of them froze, listening, until the baby sighed and settled. When Rafael spoke again her voice was lower still. "You reset forbidden intelligence in my father's press and then put it back into the *cases*, Tomas. Those are our letters. Every conspirator's name is now sitting in our type cases wearing an innocent face. If Hessler's men ever go through that cabinet with a compositor's eye—"

"They won't."

"You don't know that."

"I know it because I'm the one who sorts those cases," he said, and hated how much it sounded like pride. "I scattered them through fourteen different jobs since. Wedding announcements. A funeral bill for old Pesek. Half those letters have been inked and printed a dozen times over. There's nothing to find. That's the whole point of what I did—there's nothing to find, and yet it exists."

"Where?" she demanded. "Where does it exist?"

He hesitated, and he saw her read the hesitation instantly, the way she read everything, and he understood that the next thirty seconds would decide what she believed about him for the rest of their lives.

"The network," he said. "The printer's underground. Brother Anselm's people—the ones who move pamphlets downriver through the monasteries. I wrote to them through the flour merchant, like we do with the seditious orders nobody signs. I sent the names in cipher. Three copies went out, three different routes, weeks ago. By now there are more copies in this country than either of us can count."

The silence after that was the longest of his life.

Rafael did not shout. He had half expected her to—half hoped, because shouting burns off fast, like spirits flaming on a spoon, and what's left behind is only warmth. Instead she sat down slowly, as if her legs had been taken from her, and put both hands over her face, and breathed.

"Do you understand," she said through her fingers, "what you've done? Do you understand that I have spent two months keeping that letter alive like a coal carried in cupped hands? Not using it. Not showing it to anyone. Because a letter with those names is worth something only while it is *singular*. The moment it becomes common knowledge it stops being leverage and starts being evidence—evidence against everyone named in it, yes, but also against whoever spread it. Against us." She dropped her hands. Her eyes were dry and terrible. "Hessler has been circling this shop since autumn. If he learns the contents reached the underground through *this building*, we are not suspected printers anymore. We are the conspiracy."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"That's why I'm telling you now," Tomas said, and here at last his voice cracked, and he let it, because hiding the crack had become impossible. "Because I couldn't carry it anymore. Because every morning when the soldiers pass on their rounds I count them and think: today they come for us, today, today. Six weeks of that. And Rafael—I didn't do it to be clever. I did it because I watched you sew that letter into a book and walk toward the bridge knowing they'd search you, and I thought, if she's caught, everything dies with her. The names die with her. Wren grows up never knowing why her mother burned. So I made sure it couldn't die. I made copies so that no one person—not you, not me, not the magistrate, not Hessler himself—could end it alone."

Wren made a small sound in her basket, a dream-noise, a chick's chirp. The fire shifted. Outside, the snow kept up its patient work of burying the city.

Rafael looked at him for a long time. And Tomas, who had loved her since before he could name it—who had followed her from the checkpoint districts to the print shop like a dog follows a whistle—braced himself to be sent away.

"You're right," she said.

He blinked. "What?"

"You're right, and I hate it, and I need you to understand how much I hate it." She laughed then, one short, exhausted breath that wasn't laughter at all. "I have been guarding a single copy of that letter like it was the last soul in Christendom. One copy. One bridge, one custom house, one gate left between it and safety, and every checkpoint we've lost this winter has taught me the same lesson: things entrusted to one place die with that place. Your house raided—that was the lesson, Tomas. I walked to the safe contact's door and found it kicked open and I stood in the snow thinking, it's over, it all ends here. But if you're telling the truth—"

"I am telling the truth."

"—then it doesn't end there. It ends nowhere. It can't be stopped by burning one letter or hanging one courier." She pushed a hand through her hair, and for the first time all evening she looked less like someone bracing for a blow than like someone setting down a weight she had forgotten she was carrying. "God help me. You may have saved us."

"Or damned us."

"Yes," she said simply. "Or damned us. That's always the price of the same coin."

* * *

They talked until the candle drowned in its own wax.

It went the way Tomas had learned all important work went in that shop: slowly at first, argumentatively, each of them testing the other's reasoning like a compositor testing a font for bad sorts. Rafael wanted to know exactly which routes his three copies had taken. He told her—the flour merchant's double-bottomed barge to the monastery at Sankt Lorenz; the peddler woman who wintered with the dyers' guild in the south quarter; and the third, the riskiest, folded inside a psalter sold to a chaplain known to skim his purchases. She made him repeat the ciphers twice and then write them out and burn the draft in the stove. She asked whether Anselm's people knew the source of the information. He said no; the letter had arrived anonymous, unsigned, its origin disguised as a typesetter's error discovered in another shop entirely.

"You lied well," she said, and it sounded almost like praise.

"I learned from you."

"Don't learn too well. It's a short trade." She was quiet a moment, turning something over. Then: "Does my father know?"

"No. Only you."

She nodded slowly. "Keep it that way. He'd carry it like I do—one copy, one heart, one failure away from the end." She looked toward the basket where Wren slept, and her whole face changed, softened and sharpened at once, the way it always did. "That's what having something to lose does to a person. It makes them conservative. It makes them guard the coal instead of lighting fires."

"And you?" he asked carefully. "Are you conservative?"

She smiled without humor. "I'm a mother now, apparently. It happened without my consent." Then the smile faded into calculation, and Tomas recognized the look—he had seen it the night she decided to hide the letter instead of burn it, the night she decided to deal with Corvane rather than run. "Here's what changes. Before tonight, my plan rested on delivering the original into the right hands and praying the right hands held. Now the original matters less than the *fact* of the copies. Which means I don't have to be a courier anymore. I can be something better."

"Which is?"

"A witness," Rafael said. "A witness who cannot be silenced, because silencing her proves nothing and stops nothing. Do you see it, Tomas? Hessler's whole game is singular things. Singular letter, singular family, singular shop—he takes them one at a time because we are alone and he is not. But a truth living in forty monasteries and peddler's packs across three provinces—" she shook her head slowly, wonder and fear fighting in her expression, "—that isn't a secret anymore. That's a reckoning waiting for its day."

"It's also a noose waiting for its neck," Tomas said. "If any one copy surfaces badly. If it falls into Hessler's hands before it falls into the right ones. He could use it to hang every man named in it and call it loyalty to the crown."

"I know."

"And once the names are loose, the men in that letter will tear this city apart trying to find who loosed them. They'll start with the shops. With the presses. With—" he gestured around the little kitchen, the sleeping child, the whole fragile architecture of their winter, "—with us."

"I know that too." Rafael leaned forward into the failing light. "But listen to me, because this is the part I've understood since the bridge, and tonight you've finally made it true: we were never going to survive by being careful. Careful was the plan for a year without Hessler in it. We survive now by being *known*. By being too many, too spread, too witnessed. The neighborhood already shelters us—Marta and her milk, the flour merchant and his blind manifests, half the south quarter pretending not to see what comes and goes from this door. That's not weakness, Tomas. That's armor made of people, and it doesn't break the way paper breaks."

He wanted to believe her. He found, sitting there in the cold kitchen with the ashes settling in the stove, that he did—not easily, not completely, but the way you believe in spring at the bottom of January: on faith, and on the evidence of thaw in other years.

"There's one more thing," he said.

Her eyes narrowed. "There had better not be."

"I kept something back. Not from dishonesty—from shame." His mouth was dry. "The copy I sent to Sankt Lorenz. I didn't send it anonymously. Anselm knows it came from this shop. He asked me directly, weeks ago, whether Fairweather's was behind it—said the hand that set those names had a distinctive kerning habit, a certain slant on the long-s. He's a printer, Rafael; he reads composition the way you read faces. I confirmed it. He knows."

For a moment she said nothing. Then, incredibly, she began to laugh—a real laugh this time, low and helpless, muffled against her hand so as not to wake the baby.

"What?" Tomas demanded. "What's funny?"

"You," she said. "Us. This whole cursed winter." She wiped her eyes. "Do you realize what you've just told me? Anselm knows. Which means the most connected man in the underground network owes his intelligence to this shop and has kept it quiet through a season of raids and inspections—which means he has already chosen us over the regime, whether he ever says so aloud or not. You didn't just copy the letter, Tomas. You bound a prince of the church's printers to our fate with your slanted long-s." She shook her head. "I ought to dismiss you for reckless insubordination."

"But you won't."

"No," she said softly. "I won't. Because you did the bravest and stupidest thing anyone has done in this family since my father printed the pamphlet that got the last shop burned, and you did it for the same reason he did—for the idea that words outlive the men who try to own them." She reached across the table and, for the first time since the night Wren arrived, took his hand. Her fingers were cold and strong. "Thank you. For carrying it. For being afraid and doing it anyway. That's the whole of courage, I've decided—everything else is just noise people make afterward."

Tomas looked down at their hands on the scarred table, at the candle guttering, at the snow beyond the window falling on a city full of soldiers, secrets, and sleeping people who did not yet know what was loose among them.

"There's still the problem of Hessler," he said.

"There was always going to be a problem of Hessler," Rafael answered. "And now, thanks to you, there's finally an answer to him. He thinks he's hunting one letter. Let him hunt it. Let him find it, burn it, hold it up in court as his great prize—" her grip tightened on Tomas's hand, and her eyes had the light he had learned to trust and fear in equal measure, "—because the day he does, he'll discover he's holding a copy. And every honest soul in that courtroom will wonder how many others exist, and who else is telling the truth, and whose word a magistrate ought to take."

"Corvane," Tomas said quietly.

"Corvane," she agreed. "He buys his books from us. He reads them, too. And tomorrow morning, Tomas Kellner, you and I are going to compose him a letter he cannot refuse—one that tells him exactly what his witness can prove, and exactly how unkillable that proof has become."

Through the wall came the sound of the elder Fairweather coughing in his sleep, thin and persistent as a rat behind plaster. Neither of them moved. Below the floor, beneath the boards and the broken-flagged cellar, the press sat in the dark with all its silent letters waiting—every name, every accusation, every dangerous word sorted neatly into its case, patient as seeds under snow, asking only to be set.
