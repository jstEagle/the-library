## 14. The Raid

Later, Rafael would remember it in pieces, the way a smashed pane of glass remembers the sun—each fragment catching its own small fire, none of them fitting back together into the whole.

She remembered the sound first. Not boots—that came after—but the bell. The shop's proofing bell, the little brass thing her father rang to call apprentices from dinner, swinging on its hook in the dark though no hand had touched it. She would learn afterward that the front door had been thrown open hard enough to shake the whole frame, and the bell had rung from the violence of it, and her sleeping mind had translated that clang into something almost gentle. Almost like her father calling her down to set type by lamplight, the way he had when she was small and could not sleep.

Then the boots. Many boots. On the stairs inside the house, which meant they were already past Marta's door, already past the dyer's yard, already through the alley where Tomas kept his bicycle leaning against the wall. There had been no warning. No knock. No polite voice through the wood announcing itself in the name of whatever authority had been invented this month.

She was out of bed with Wren before her mind caught up to her body, one arm around the child's warm weight, her other hand already reaching for the satchel she kept packed beneath the floorboard—the one she had packed in October and repacked every week since, as if preparedness were a kind of prayer. Blanket. Bread wrapped in cloth. The small vial of laudanum Marta had pressed on her with a look that asked no questions. Coins sewn into the hem. Her fingers found the board's edge and lifted, and her hands were steady, and she observed this the way one observes weather happening to someone else: *my hands are steady; how interesting; how strange.*

Wren did not cry. That was the first miracle, though Rafael would not recognize it as such until much later. The child woke, felt the urgency in the arms holding her, and went utterly still, her dark eyes wide and reflective as a bird's, watching her mother's face as though reading instructions there.

*Good girl. Good girl. Whatever you see tonight, however loud it gets, you hold still like that and I will carry you through the whole rest of your life.*

Downstairs, the world ended politely, in order.

First her father's voice—not shouting, never shouting, his voice pitched in the reasonable tone he used with customers who wanted to haggle over the price of a broadside. "Gentlemen. Gentlemen, this is a place of business. If you have a warrant—" And then the sound of a body meeting furniture, the wet percussion of it, and Rafael's heart did not clench or seize but simply stopped, suspended, like a compositor's hand frozen above the case when he realizes he has set the entire line backward.

Then the press.

She heard it die. That is not a metaphor she would have chosen; it is simply what happened. There was a groan of iron being levered, a crack like green wood, and then the voice of the press—a voice she had known all her life, the deep rhythmic breath of it, in and out, platen and bed, the heartbeat under the floorboards of her childhood—went silent in the middle of its cycle. Not wound down. Cut off. She had never once in twenty-two years heard that sound interrupted mid-pull, and something in her chest tore along a seam she had not known was there, quietly, without blood.

*They are killing it,* she thought, from the top of the stairs with her daughter against her sternum and the satchel strap biting her shoulder. *They are killing it and it cannot even cry out.*

The cellar hatch was behind the paper store, beneath the racks of dried quires. Her father had shown her the way of it when she was nine: lift here, brace there, mind the hinge that wants to shriek. Down the worn steps into cold earth and coal dust, along the passage that ran, according to her father's rambling explanations, somewhere beneath both gardens and came up—where? She had never asked. She had been nine and the dark had been an adventure. Now the dark was the only country left to her and she crossed its border carrying everything she owned.

Behind and above her, the soldiers' voices rose and braided together, words losing their edges until only their tones remained: triumph, boredom, the particular flat cruelty of men doing sanctioned work. A name called out—her father's name—and her father's voice answered it once more, fainter now, and she heard in it something that undid her more than any blow: apology. He was apologizing. To them, or to her, or to the dead press, she would never know.

She put her hand flat against the earthen wall and breathed, and Wren breathed against her neck, two small warm engines in the freezing dark, and Rafael Fairweather—who could lie to magistrates, who could walk forbidden paper past armed men, who had spent a winter becoming fluent in the language of survival—found that she could not think at all.

Her mind simply declined. It presented her, instead, with images: the type cases with their hundred small drawers, each letter in its compartment, the whole alphabet sleeping in order; the smell of ink and oil; her father's spectacles folded on the ledger, because he took them off to sleep and would not have had time tonight to fold them; Tomas's coat on its peg. Ordinary things, catalogued with insane precision, while the actual emergency proceeded around her like a play performed for another audience.

*Mother,* said a voice in her head that might have been her own and might have been her long-dead mother's, *you are standing in a cellar holding a baby and nothing else, and everything else is gone.*

Not quite nothing else. The letter. God help her—the letter was sewn into the lining of the satchel where it had lived for three weeks since the failure at the Gate of Ashes, since the contact's house had stood gutted and doorless in the snow, since Tomas had confessed his copy and changed the arithmetic of her courage. The letter was with her. They had come for the shop and her father and perhaps for her, and the thing that could hang half the neighborhood rode against her hip in a blanket-lined bag next to bread and a child's spare stocking, and if they searched her at the mouth of this tunnel—

But they did not know about the tunnel. Or if they knew, they believed it bricked up; her father had let them believe it years ago, showing inspectors a wall that opened onto a coal chute. Bless him. Bless his careful lies, his patient decades of small deceits, all of it spent tonight in a single transaction.

She walked. She counted her steps because counting was a task and a task kept the horror at bay the way a dam holds a river—not beautifully, not forever, but enough. Forty-one steps to the bend. Sixty-three to the old root door. The frost came through the ground above her and the cold found the gap at her collar and lay against her skin like a blade laid flat, not cutting, only reminding her it could.

At the root door she stopped and listened.

Snow-silence. Then, muffled and distant, from the direction of the street: the crash of the type cases being emptied into the yard. She knew that sound too intimately to mistake it. Hundreds of hours of her father's labor, sorts scattered into slush, letters that had formed proclamations and psalms and price lists and love notes all mixed indiscriminately in the dirty snow, pi and pica and long-s together, unmade, unreadable, the whole vocabulary of their lives poured out like grain before pigs.

Rafael did not weep. She noted, distantly, that she probably ought to, that weeping was the correct response to what was happening, and that its absence frightened her more than the sounds upstairs had. She felt the way the press must have felt: functional, then interrupted, then nothing.

*Later,* she told herself, in the firm tone she used with apprentices. *You may break later. Now you climb.*

* * *

Marta's kitchen smelled of oak ash and dyer's alum and forty years of living, and it received Rafael the way the shore receives a drowning woman—with no ceremony at all, just solid ground appearing where there had been none.

The old dyer was already awake, already dressed, already had the kettle on. Rafael understood, in her fractured way, that Marta had been awake all night, that the old woman had perhaps heard the boots cross her own yard and made her choice about which door to leave unlatched. There was milk warming in a pan. There was a chair drawn out from the table at an angle that said *sit here, this one, the one away from the window.*

"Marta," Rafael began, and found she had nothing to follow the name with. Her mouth continued to stand open a moment, offering words that were not forthcoming. She closed it. She sat.

"Give her here." Marta held out her arms for Wren, and Rafael—for one irrational half-second—clenched. Every instinct honed over a winter of hiding screamed against surrendering the child into any arms but her own. Then she looked at Marta's face, seamed and steady and lit gold by the single lamp, and her arms unlocked as though someone had turned a key she hadn't known was in her.

The moment Wren left them, Rafael's hands became strangers. They sat in her lap, palms up, stained black to the second knuckle with ink she had no memory of touching—had she grabbed the type case on her way through? Had she, in some spasm of salvage, thrust her hands into the drawers? She stared at them. Black hands. A compositor's hands. The only part of the trade they had not been able to smash, because it was not made of iron; it was made of her.

"You're in shock," Marta said, conversationally, turning the child against her shoulder. "Drink this." Tea appeared. Rafael drank it because refusing required sentences and she had none. It scalded her tongue and she registered the pain with gratitude, as proof of embodiment.

"They took Father."

"I know." Marta's voice did not soften with pity, which Rafael was abruptly, enormously thankful for. Pity would have drowned her. Marta spoke as one workman to another. "I saw them march him out the front. He walked straight. He looked at the window of my parlor as he passed." A pause. "He didn't look afraid, girl."

"He should be afraid." The words came out of Rafael level and precise and very far away. "They'll question him. Hessler questions people, and then Corvane—" She stopped. Corvane. The magistrate who bought books, who read poetry in the front room with his gloves off, who had asked her twice, gently, whether all was well at the shop. The magistrate whose conscience was a coin still spinning in the air, and she had no idea on which face it would land, and her father's life was the wager. "There will be a trial. They'll want a trial. Theater needs an audience."

"Then you'll need to be invisible until it happens."

"Invisible." Rafael laughed—one note, wrong in pitch, startling Wren, who had begun to drowse against Marta's shoulder. The laugh died as fast as it was born. "Marta, they emptied the type into the street. They know the shop. Which means they know the family. My face has been on their lists since autumn, I've only ever been worth ignoring, and tonight Hessler spent the ignoring." She looked down at her inked palms again. "I carried everything out of that house that can be carried. The letter. The child. The coins. And there is nowhere left to carry it to. Do you understand? I am full. I am a vessel with no room left in it, and everything I love is either in this room or in a cell."

Marta set Wren gently in the crib-basket that had appeared—when had it appeared? days ago? weeks?—beside the stove, tucked the blanket to the child's chin with the competence of a woman who had raised children and buried some. Then she crossed the kitchen and took Rafael's ink-stained hands in her dye-stained ones, blue-black meeting black, and held them hard.

"Your father set my wedding announcement," Marta said. "Fifty-one years ago. First thing he ever printed that mattered to anybody. He charged me nothing and spelled my husband's name wrong, and I loved him for both." Her grip tightened. "He is not dead. The press is not dead—it's iron, it can be recast. You are not dead. So we do not act like the dead tonight. We act like the living, and the living make plans."

"The living make plans," Rafael repeated. The words went into her like the tea's heat, external, borrowed, slowly becoming hers.

"Tomas comes at dawn," Marta said. "I sent the boy from the tannery the moment I heard the boots—he knows the rooftop way. Tomas will take you out with the milk cart, Thursday, when the roads are busiest. There's a loft over the cooper's yard by the river gate—"

And so it went, plan following plan, Marta laying them out like cards, and Rafael sat among them nodding at intervals, participating in her own rescue the way a mannequin participates in being dressed. Some portion of her mind remained in the cellar, counting steps. Another portion stood on the stairs listening to the press die. Only a thin bright thread of her actually sat in the kitchen, and it was enough—barely—to keep breathing, to keep her eyes on the basket by the stove where Wren slept with one fist curled against her cheek, utterly unaware, trusting completely in a world that had just demonstrated, loudly and with iron levers, exactly what it was.

Near dawn, the thread thinned further, and Rafael slid sideways into a doze that was not rest—images churning behind her closed eyes: her father's spectacles folded on the ledger; the alphabet in the snow; a hooded woman whispering *forget my face* into the dark of a doorway a lifetime ago, a whole year ago, an hour ago. She woke with a start each time, hand flying to the satchel, finding it there, finding the child there, finding the kitchen real, and each waking cost her something she could not name and could not spare.

When gray light finally stood in the window, there came a soft triple knock at Marta's door—Tomas's knock, the one they'd agreed on, the pattern of his old apprentice bell.

Marta looked at Rafael across the kitchen. Rafael looked at the basket, at the sleeping child who had arrived on their doorstep as a stranger and was leaving it as a fugitive with a new name waiting somewhere in her future. Everything carried. Nothing left to carry it in. Except, she supposed, except—each other.

She rose. Her legs held. Her hands were still black with ink, and she decided she would not wash them off.

"It's Thursday," she said, and her voice came out low and even and entirely her own, the first fully hers since the bell had rung in the dark. "Then we go."

She opened the door—and stopped, because Tomas's face was white as unwashed paper, and the first word out of his mouth was not *hello*, or *are you safe*, or any greeting at all.

It was: "Rafael—they're saying it in the streets. Your father isn't going to a cell." He swallowed. "Hessler's asked for Corvane himself to preside. They're building a gallows case."
