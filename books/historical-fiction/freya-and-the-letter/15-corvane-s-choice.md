## 15. Corvane's Choice

The writ arrived at the ninth hour, carried by a clerk who would not meet his eyes.

Magistrate Elias Corvane read it twice at his desk in the Hall of Sessions, the winter light falling thin and gray through windows that had not been washed since the summer of riots. The document was brief. It always was, when the important words had been left out on purpose. *In the matter of Fairweather, Aldous — printer — charged with sedition, dissemination of seditious material, and conspiracy against the peace of the realm: Magistrate E. Corvane is appointed to prosecute.*

Prosecute.

He set the writ down and looked at it for a long moment, the way a man looks at a card he has been dealt and cannot return to the deck.

In thirty years of service he had prosecuted smugglers and coin-clippers, arsonists and one memorable woman who had poisoned three husbands with stewed plums and wept beautifully through every deposition. He had done his duty as the law required, because the law was the only wall between a city like this and the darkness it kept threatening to fall into. He believed in the law the way other men believed in God — imperfectly, sometimes angrily, but believing still. It could be wrong. It could be cruel. But it was *theirs*, all of them together, and the alternative to it was whatever waited outside its walls.

He knew what this case was before he read a single page of evidence. He knew it the way one knows weather by the ache in an old wound.

*Fairweather.* He said the name aloud to the empty chamber, testing it. The print shop on Chandler's Row. He had bought books there for eleven years — a folio of Plutarch with foxed pages he'd haggled over, a slim volume of Lucretius that the old man himself had wrapped in brown paper against the damp, pressing it into Corvane's hands with the shy pride of a craftsman whose work outlives him. Aldous Fairweather's eyes had been failing even then. He'd held each coin up to the window light to count it, apologizing for the slowness, and Corvane had pretended not to notice because pretending not to notice was a kindness, and kindness between strangers was the only kind that cost nothing.

That man was in the cells beneath the Hall now. That man he was appointed to hang.

Corvane opened the case file. It was thick — too thick, assembled with a thoroughness that told him everything about who had assembled it. Depositions from witnesses whose names were written in a hand he recognized. Seized pamphlets, catalogued and numbered like specimens in a killing jar. An affidavit from Agent Hessler himself, stamped with the seal of the Commission for Public Order, swearing that the print shop had been the hub of a network stretching across three districts, that forbidden texts had moved through its doors by the hundredweight, that the printer had conspired with persons unknown to circulate calls for insurrection.

Persons unknown. There it was. The whole architecture of the case balanced on that phrase, and Corvane felt it sway under his hands the moment he touched it.

He read until noon. Then he sent his clerk for bread and cheese and ate at his desk, and read until the light went yellow and then went out entirely, and lit his lamp, and read some more. And by the time the watchman called the hour past dark, he had found what he had known he would find, and finding it gave him no satisfaction at all.

Every witness statement described the same shop. Not the same crime — the same *shop*. A place where paper moved and ink dried and men came and went, which described every print shop in the city and half the bookshops besides. Two depositions contradicted each other about whether Fairweather had been seen speaking with a man in a gray coat; both agreed the coat had been gray, neither could produce the man. The seized pamphlets were genuine enough — he had seen their like circulating for months, ugly things, reckless things — but nothing tied a single one to Fairweather's press except the testimony of a typesetter named Groll, whose last two employers had both closed within a week of hiring him, and whose new lodgings, Corvane happened to know, were paid for out of the Commission's own accounts. He had signed those disbursements himself, months ago, without reading them closely. Without wanting to read them closely.

He sat back in his chair. The lamp hissed.

*You are being asked to do theater,* he thought. *And you have known since the writ came, and you let yourself pretend otherwise for six hours because the pretense was warm.*

Because here was the shape of it, plain as a knife on a table: Hessler needed a prosecution, not a conviction — or rather, needed a conviction so public and so theatrical that the district would learn from it. The river quarter had grown restless. Leaflets appeared on doorsteps faster than they could be confiscated. Someone had burned the tollhouse gate in November. The regime's answer to restlessness had never once, in all Corvane's years, been patience. Its answer was an example. And examples were best made of men who were beloved, visible, and weak. A blind old printer whose shop every child in the neighborhood had sheltered inside during rainstorms. A man whose guilt mattered less than his name.

And a magistrate willing to carry the case would be rewarded. Corvane was not naive about where he stood. He had spent thirty years climbing a staircase made of exactly these kinds of favors, each step small enough to pretend it wasn't a step at all. A judgeship on the upper bench had been murmured about for two years — murmured by people whose murmurs became appointments. This case, handled properly, would put him there before spring.

Handled improperly, it would put him in a cell beside the printer.

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and listened to the building settle around him, beams contracting in the cold like an old ship, and he measured his soul the way he had learned to measure everything: carefully, honestly, and with a growing dread of the total.

* * *

They let him see the prisoner on the third day, in the room beneath the stairs that the guards used for cards when the magistrate wasn't looking.

Aldous Fairweather sat with his back very straight on the plank bench, hands folded on his knee like a man waiting for a service to begin. His eyes — milky, drifting, aimed somewhere just left of Corvane's shoulder — were worse than eleven years ago. Nearly gone. But when the door opened, the old man turned his head toward the sound of boots on stone with an accuracy that unsettled, and he inclined it slightly.

"Magistrate," he said. His voice was dry but steady. "I wondered if they'd send you."

"You knew it would be me?" Corvane remained standing. He did not want to sit. Sitting made conversations intimate, and intimacy here was a thing he could not afford and did not deserve.

"I know the sound of your boots." A faint smile. "You've walked my shop floor a hundred times. You drag the left heel when you're thinking. You dragged it coming down those stairs."

Corvane looked at his own boots. Absurdly, he wanted to apologize for them.

"Master Fairweather. I am appointed to—" He stopped. The sentence would not finish itself. He tried again. "I am appointed to prosecute. I want you to understand that I will do my duty as the law requires. I will not pretend otherwise to you. But I will also not lie in the conduct of it. I will examine the evidence as it stands, and I will say in open court what the evidence says, and only that."

For a long moment the old man's clouded eyes regarded him, or seemed to — it was impossible to tell where they rested, and Corvane realized with a small cold shock that this was precisely how Fairweather must experience every conversation now: unable to see faces, reading everything else. Posture. Voice. The drag of a heel.

"And what does the evidence say, Magistrate?" Fairweather asked quietly. "When you look at it plainly? Not as a prosecutor. As the man who haggled me down four sous on Plutarch because, you said, the foxing offended you — though I think you paid full price anyway, and left the difference in the till when you thought I wasn't watching."

Corvane felt his face heat. Eleven years, and the old man had seen. Had *known*, all along, without seeing.

"The evidence," Corvane said slowly, "is theater. The witnesses are purchased. The affidavit is a story wearing a uniform." He heard himself saying it and could not stop; thirty years of careful silence pouring through a crack he hadn't meant to make. "I do not believe you are guilty of what they charge. I believe you are guilty of owning a press, which in this season is treated as the same crime. And I believe that whoever arranged this does not care whether I believe any of it, because my belief was never requested. Only my voice."

"Then refuse," said Fairweather simply.

"I cannot refuse. Refusal is ruin — mine, and it changes nothing; they would appoint another within the day, someone hungrier, and your position would be no better and my family would—" He stopped again. Closed his eyes briefly. "There is a daughter," he said. "Rafael. She runs the deliveries. She has run them past my own checkpoint clerks a dozen times, and I have watched her do it, and I have never once stopped her, and I no longer know what that makes me."

It was the truest thing he had said in years, possibly ever, and he said it to a blind man in a cellar because a blind man could not report it, and because the shame of carrying it alone had begun to outweigh the safety of silence.

Fairweather's folded hands tightened, then relaxed. "So you have been keeping her safe," he said. "All this while. In your small way."

"In my coward's way. I told myself it was mercy. Perhaps it was only the cheapest form of it — the kind that costs nothing until suddenly it costs everything." Corvane crossed to the barred window, where a blade of gray light fell through. "Your daughter has been seen, Master Fairweather. Hessler's people are asking after a young woman connected to your shop. If she comes near this trial—"

"She'll come near it," the old man said, with terrible mildness. "She's my daughter. She'll come near it the way iron comes to a lodestone. You cannot legislate against love, Magistrate, though I believe your Commission is drafting the statute now."

Despite everything — despite the cell and the stakes and the noose being fitted in his imagination — Corvane almost laughed. Almost. What escaped instead was something smaller and more dangerous: a decision, taken in that moment below the stairs, that he did not fully admit to until he was climbing back up into the gray daylight.

*If the evidence is theater,* he thought, *then the court is a stage. And a stage can be turned. Every stage can be turned, if the player knows where the machinery is.*

Hessler wanted a prosecution that performed obedience. Very well. Corvane could perform obedience. He could stand in the hall and speak in the flat cadence of duty and lay out the Commission's case piece by piece, exactly as instructed —

— and lay it out *plainly*. Fully. Before witnesses. Before the gallery, before the record, before whatever eyes the regime had not yet bought. Let the purchased witnesses be heard in the open. Let Groll's contradictions sit in the transcript where anyone with a candle could read them. Let the affidavit be entered into evidence and therefore into history. Theater requires darkness to work; it dies under honest light, and a courtroom, for all its corruptions, was the one room in this city where a lantern still had to be lit.

It was not defiance. Defiance would be noble, and noble would fail, and fail loudly. This was something quieter and stranger: a prosecution conducted so scrupulously, so *exactly* according to law, that the law itself would become the weapon. He would give Hessler his trial. He would simply give the truth a seat at it as well.

Or he would ruin himself trying.

* * *

That evening Hessler came to him.

Corvane had expected the visit the way one expects frost — inevitably, and dreading the damage it would find. The agent arrived at Corvane's chambers unannounced at dusk, shaking river fog from his coat, declining wine, seating himself across the desk with the easy posture of a man who considered all rooms his.

"Magistrate Corvane." Hessler smiled. He had a clerk's face and a fencer's patience, and the combination had unsettled better men than Corvane. "Three days you've had the file. Tell me — how does our printer look?"

"He looks," Corvane said, "like an old man with failing eyes who wrapped my books in brown paper against the damp."

"What a memory you keep." The smile didn't move. "Sentiment is a fine quality in a neighbor, Magistrate. In a prosecutor it is a liability. The Commission expects conviction. The district expects a lesson. Neither expectation is negotiable."

"The Commission will receive a trial," Corvane said evenly. "Conducted according to law. Every witness examined. Every document entered. Every question asked that the law permits me to ask."

Something shifted behind Hessler's eyes — the flicker of a man recalculating, weighing what was said against what was meant. "Of course," he said softly. "Scrupulous. I'd expect nothing less from you. Scrupulousness is your reputation, isn't it? Thirty years of it." He rose, buttoned his coat, paused at the door. "Do be careful with scruples, Magistrate. In my experience they're like loose type — harmless in the tray. Dangerous in the wrong hands. And everyone's hands are the wrong hands, sooner or later."

The door closed. Corvane sat motionless for a long time, listening to the fog thicken against the window, understanding perfectly what had just been said to him and in precisely how many words: *I see the machinery turning. I have not yet decided whether to break it — or break you.*

And beneath the fear, unexpected and unwelcome, something else stirred. Something that felt, alarmingly, like resolve — and like relief. The choice he had been measuring for days was not a choice anymore. It had chosen itself somewhere between the cellar and the closing door, and all that remained was to see whether a good man with a lantern could burn down a theater from inside it.

He took out fresh paper. He began, in his precise prosecutor's hand, to draft his opening statement — and to list, beneath it, the questions he intended to ask in open court.

Then his housekeeper knocked. A boy was downstairs, she said. Wouldn't give his name. Wouldn't leave.

Corvane descended. In the doorway stood an apprentice — broad-shouldered, ink-stained, jaw set with the particular courage of someone terrified and committed to it anyway. Tomas, he thought. The boy from the shop. He carried a wrapped parcel the size of a prayer book and held it out like an accusation.

"From Rafael," the boy said. "She said — she said you'd understand. She said to tell you: *she doesn't come to beg. She comes to trade.*"

Outside, the bells of the river quarter began to ring the hour, and Corvane took the parcel, and felt through the paper the unmistakable weight of names.
