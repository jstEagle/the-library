## 18. Hessler's Reckoning

The summons came at midmorning, folded into the hands of a boy too young to understand what he carried: *All persons of the Vintners' Row district to appear in the Fountain Square by noon, there to witness the administration of justice.* Justice, administered like a dose of physic, in public, with witnesses. Rafael read it twice and felt the words settle in her chest like cold coins.

Her father sat by the dead hearth with a blanket over his shoulders, three days free of the interrogation rooms and still wearing their grayness like a second skin. They had returned him with his hands trembling and his mind intact, which was more than some had gotten, and Rafael had spent those three days learning to sleep in snatches again, one ear open for boots on the stair.

"You don't have to go," he said now, watching her dress with the care she reserved for battlefields. "Summons or no. People are hiding in their houses all over the Row."

"Those people have nothing he wants." She pinned her collar. In the glass, her face looked the way it had always looked: unremarkable, forgettable, a face built for passing checkpoints. She wondered if it still worked, that face. She suspected it did not. Something had broken open in her since the trial—since Corvane had stood in that courtroom and read the letter's contents aloud in his dry, precise voice, name after name after name, until the conspirators sat exposed on the benches like fish on a slapper's table. Since Hessler had watched his careful edifice come down around his ears with an expression that never changed, not once, only his knuckles whitening against the rail.

"Rafael." Her father's voice pulled her back. "He lost everything yesterday. Men who lose everything stop being dangerous in one direction and become dangerous in all of them."

"I know."

"He'll want a hostage. Something small enough to carry."

She met his eyes in the glass. Behind him, in the basket by the shuttered press, Wren slept with one fist curled against her cheek, breathing the small damp breaths that Rafael had learned to count the way other people counted money.

"Then I'll be there to carry her back," Rafael said.

* * *

The Fountain Square had been the market square before the troubles, and it remembered its old shape even now: the fountain dry and furred with frost, the stalls gone, the cobbles scarred where tent-poles and gallows-footings had been sunk and pulled up and sunk again. By noon the crowd had gathered thick as winter starlings, drawn by the summons and by something hungrier underneath—the animal curiosity of people who wanted to see how the story ended.

They stood on the steps of the old corn exchange because there was nowhere else to stand, a wooden platform thrown up overnight with two chairs and a table and, behind them, the green banner of the Provisional Authority hanging from a pole like something flayed.

Hessler was already there.

Rafael saw him from thirty rows back, over the caps and shoulders of her neighbors, and the sight of him moved through her like river water through a lock—cold, controlled, finding its level. He wore his gray uniform as immaculately as ever. He stood at parade rest beside the table where the magistrate should have been, and the absence of Corvane told her more than any speech could: this proceeding had been arranged without him. This was what a man did when the law abandoned him and he refused to abandon himself.

Beside the platform, flanked by two soldiers, stood a clerk from the custom house and a woman Rafael half-recognized from the grain market—witnesses, or props for witnesses, the theater already dressed.

Tomas pressed close at her shoulder. Marta was somewhere behind with the dyer's women, and Rafael had made them swear to stay back, to keep Wren's blanket-wrapped weight out of the front ranks. But the crowd had its own opinions about ranks, and it compressed and shifted like ice on a warming day, and Rafael found herself carried toward the platform whether she willed it or no, until she stood perhaps ten paces off, close enough to see the powder-blue shadow under Hessler's left eye where sleep did not visit.

He saw her. Of course he saw her. And she watched the recognition move across his face—not surprise, not triumph, but the flat settling of a man who has finally been dealt the card he knew was in the deck.

The reading began. A herald with a voice like gravel pronounced the findings of the tribunal: the conspiracy named and dismantled, the sentences—strippings of rank, forfeitures of property, two names that drew gasps when they came to transportation. The crowd absorbed each pronouncement with the soundless attention of people memorizing ammunition. Rafael understood, standing among them, that every name spoken aloud was a debt entered into the neighborhood's ledger, and that ledgers of this kind were kept for years, sometimes generations.

Then the herald stopped. He looked at his paper. He looked at Hessler.

Hessler took the paper from him.

"The tribunal further notes," he said, and his voice was quieter than the herald's, which somehow made it carry farther, "one matter unresolved. During the course of these investigations, agents of this office received testimony regarding an infant abandoned at premises on Vintners' Row—premises subsequently established as a center of seditious printing. The child's origins remain undocumented. The circumstances of her abandonment suggest a mother engaged in conspiracy, seeking to dispose of evidence." He paused, letting the word settle over the square like ash. "*Evidence.*"

Rafael's blood went thin and ringing.

"It is therefore the determination of this office," Hessler continued, "that the child known as *Wren* be taken into protective custody of the Authority, there to be examined as material proof of—"

He got no further, because Rafael Fairweather stepped out of the crowd.

She did not decide to do it. Or rather, she decided it long ago—in a doorway before dawn with a letter in her hand, in a cellar passage with soldiers overhead, in a courtroom gallery watching a good man gamble his soul—and today her body simply caught up with all those earlier decisions. She walked the ten paces to the foot of the platform with Wren's basket on her arm, and the crowd parted before her the way water parts before a keel, and the silence that followed her was so complete she could hear the fountain's frozen pipes ticking in the cold.

"That child," she said, and her voice surprised her—it did not shake; it rang, a compositor's voice, set firm in its frame—"is my daughter."

Every head in the square turned. Hessler's mouth curved by a fraction.

"You are unmarried, Mistress Fairweather."

"Yes."

"You keep a print shop under investigation for sedition."

"My father's shop. Forfeited, I'm told, this morning."

"And yet you claim a child of unknown parentage, delivered to your door in secrecy, whose very existence your family concealed for a year." He descended one step, then another, until he stood above her at the platform's edge, close enough that she could smell the bay rum on his well-kept hands. "You mistake the nature of this proceeding, mistress. It is not a debate. The child comes with me. Whether you accompany her is a matter we can discuss later—at length."

He signaled. The two soldiers started down.

And Rafael, feeling the moment balance on its point like a coin on its rim, did the thing she had been doing all her life, the thing the whole neighborhood had paid her to do—she moved paper. Words. Truth, in the end, which was the most contraband cargo of all.

"Examine me, then," she said, loud enough to reach the last rows. "Go on. Examine the child, examine me, examine every record your clerks can pull. You will find no mother's name because her mother died of fever in the charity ward last spring—you may look her up, a laundress, married, three older children, husband lost at the checkpoint on Bridge Street; ask the ward sisters, they'll tell you themselves. You will find no conspiracy in her cradle because she was born in a hospital bed, not a plotting room. What you will find, Magistrate Hessler—" she let the title land on him like a slap, since he held no such office anymore— "is a year of your own written orders. Orders detaining a laundress. Orders questioning her neighbors. Orders that drove a sick woman to give her baby away in the dark because staying would have killed them both." She lifted the basket slightly, an offering or an accusation, she could not have said which. "That is your proof of treason, sir. Not hers. Yours. Written in your hand, dated, filed, and read into the record yesterday by Magistrate Corvane."

It was not entirely true. The laundress was invention stitched to probability, the kind of forgery Tomas might have cut in lead. But it was built on true timbers—the detained, the questioned, the driven-underground, all of them real, all of them Hessler's work—and truth gave the lie its bones. She saw it strike home. She saw the front row of the crowd lean in, and the second row, and she heard the low murmur begin, the sound a crowd makes when private knowledge suddenly finds itself shared.

"She lies gracefully," Hessler said to the square, but softly, and softness was the wrong register. "Graceful liars are the most dangerous citizens of all."

"Then arrest me," Rafael said. "Here. Now. Before all these witnesses. Tell them what the charge is—a woman feeding a fatherless baby. Let them watch the escort take us." She turned, slowly, deliberately, and let her eyes travel the crowd, and the crowd let her, hundreds of faces she had passed a thousand times invisibly—the fishwife, the cooper, the dyer's women, old men who bought pamphlets they claimed to burn. "Go on, sir. The square is full. No one will miss it."

For a moment—one heartbeat, two—she thought he would do it. His hand actually moved toward the soldiers, and hope and terror fused in her chest into a single white note.

But a crowd is a thing that reads, too, and it had read this page before. Somewhere in the mass a shoulder set. Then another. The fishwife planted her baskets down on the cobbles like fence posts. The cooper folded his arms. Marta appeared at Rafael's side as though conjured, dye-stained apron, chin up, and behind her the dyer's women, and behind them more, a slow closing like a fist forming around a small bird.

"They're blocking a lawful escort," Hessler said, but he said it to no one, because authority requires someone obliged to hear it, and no one was listening anymore.

"No," said a voice from the back, an old man's voice, cracked and carrying. "We're standing in a square. It's a free city. Or it was. Or it means to be again."

Someone laughed. Then more. Not a jeer—an ugly laugh, aimed, and Rafael heard in it the two transported names, the smashed presses, the neighbor taken in the night, a whole winter of grievance finding its voice at once. Hessler stood on his platform and the crowd stood in its square, and between them passed the understanding that the arithmetic had changed. He could order the soldiers forward. He could not order the square forward, and the square outnumbered the soldiers forty to one and knew it, and knew he knew it.

The soldiers looked at Hessian—looked at Hessler—for orders. He gave none. His jaw worked once, twice, like a press biting down on nothing.

"This isn't finished," he said to Rafael, quietly.

"No," she agreed. "It's just begun, and not the way you planned it. Go home, sir. If you still have one. I hear forfeiture travels in circles."

The laugh that followed him off the platform followed him down Vintners' Row and around the corner, and when it faded, what remained in the square was not laughter at all but something quieter and stranger: hundreds of people looking at each other, many of them for the first time, discovering that they had been a neighborhood all along and had only now been introduced.

Marta's rough hand closed on Rafael's arm. "You mad girl," she breathed, half prayer, half curse. "You magnificent mad girl."

Wren, awake now in her basket, reached up one small hand and seized Rafael's finger with the absolute confidence of the deeply held.

"All right," Rafael said, mostly to herself, mostly to the child. But then Tomas was pushing through toward her, white-faced, and the words tumbling out before he'd finished the pushing: the shop—soldiers at the shop—Hessler's men, moving fast, moving *now*, and Rafael felt the morning tilt beneath her feet like river ice beginning to groan.

He had lost the square, so he was taking the only thing left with her name on it.

"Come on," she said, and turned toward Vintners' Row at a run, the crowd breaking apart behind her into running figures, spreading the news ahead of her street by street like typeset pages through the city—*they've gone to the shop, they've gone to the shop*—and somewhere beyond the rooftops, faint on the wind, the bells of the old cathedral began to ring out of hour, or out of season, or perhaps out of century.
