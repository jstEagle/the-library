## 20. The Century Turns

The bells began at dusk, and they did not stop.

Rafael stood in the doorway of Marta's dyehouse with Wren on her hip and listened to the sound roll up from the river district—first the cathedral's great bourdon, then the Franciscan chapel answering, then some smaller, meaner bell in the tannery quarter that she had never once heard ring, cracked and joyous and half a beat behind all the others. The city had not rung its bells like this since the old king's coronation. It was not ringing them now for a king.

"They've gone mad," Marta said beside her, arms folded against the cold. "Or free."

"Can those be the same thing?"

"In my experience, girl, they arrive together more often than anyone admits."

Down the lane, doors opened. Rafael watched lamplight spill onto frozen mud, watch faces emerge one by one into the blue evening—the cooper's widow, the two brothers who carted night soil, the boy from the bakery who had once refused to sell her bread because her father's name was on a list. That boy was running now, bareheaded in the snow, shouting something she could not yet make out. His voice came up the street the way rumors came: broken into pieces by distance, reassembled by hope.

"—Paris—" she caught. "—the palace—candles burning in the Tuileries—" and then, closer, clearer, delivered at full sprint: "*The century is dead! They say the century is dead!*"

Wren kicked her legs and crowed at the noise, eleven months old and certain that every gathering crowd existed for her benefit. Rafael pressed her lips to the child's cap—the wool Marta had dyed madder-red so that no one could ever mistake the girl for anything but beloved—and felt the year settle onto her shoulders like a coat made entirely of lead type.

One year measured in what they carried. She had learned to weigh everything that way now. A handpress: four hundred pounds, immovable, smashed to kindling in an hour. A case of ten-point Caslon: carryable by two men, sold piecemeal for medicine, mourned like a limb. A letter naming names: lighter than a wren's egg, heavier than everything else in the world put together. And the wren herself, who had grown from an armful to a burden who insisted on walking everywhere and falling down every six steps, and whom Rafael would have carried across fire rather than ice if it came to that.

It had come to ice instead.

* * *

Inside the dyehouse, Tomas had laid out their whole lives on Marta's workbench, and the whole of their lives fit between two vats of indigo with room to spare.

"That's it?" he said, not mocking—checking. He had developed the habit over the winter of checking things aloud, as though saying them made them truer. "That's everything."

Rafael set Wren down on a stool and went to stand over the inventory. Her father's Bible. Two shirts each, one worn, one saved. The medicine bottle, three-quarters empty now that his fever had broken and he was sleeping in the back room, thin but breathing, alive because she had traded a font of Baskerville for the physician's silence. Marta's dye-stained account book, useless anywhere else, kept anyway because Marta said a woman ought to have proof of what she'd built. A bundle of letters from the underground printers in the river towns—Tomas's doing, Tomas's warren of correspondents—and one clean forged shipping manifest that would never again fool anyone but might, Tomas argued, amuse them on the road.

And at the bottom of the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into a false spine that had crossed three checkpoints without ever being asked to show itself: the letter.

She touched the satchel and did not open it. She knew what it said better than she knew her own prayers now. She had read it so many times that the names had stopped being names and become faces—the customs officer who looked away from her bundles, the alderman who had warned her father twice before the third warning never came, the captain whose men had searched her crate and found nothing because he had been paid to find nothing. Half of them were dead already, she'd heard. The revolution Hessler's masters feared had begun eating its own informants first.

"Say it again," Marta demanded from the doorway, shaking snow from her shawl. "The plan. All of it. I'm old, I want to hear it fresh."

"You're not old, you're dramatic," Tomas said, but he obeyed her, ticking points off on his ink-stained fingers. "We leave before first light, while the checkpoint garrison is drunk on New Year's wine and the sentries change at dawn. We go north along the towpath to the reed beds, where no one watches the bank because the bank belongs to no one. We cross at the long shallows below the millrace, where the ice is thickest—Marta's cousin swears he drove a loaded cart across it last week."

"Marta's cousin also swears he wrestled a bear," Marta said.

"He swore the bear started it."

Rafael almost smiled. Almost. On the far side: forty miles of country road to the river town where the underground press operated behind a paper-seller's shop; Tomas's correspondent had written twice promising work, promising typesetting for a man named Freya nothing about—promising anonymity. After that, whatever the new century decided to do with people like them.

"And Papa?" she said, and the question took all the air out of the plan, the way it always did.

Tomas's hands stilled. "He says he stays until the trial records are sealed. He says a Fairweather signs his own testimony." He hesitated. "He says you're not to argue with him, because he's your father and he has spent fifty-one years winning arguments."

"I've spent twenty-two losing them on purpose," Rafael said quietly. "He can have this one."

* * *

She left the packing to Tomas and carried Wren upstairs to the loft where her father slept. He was awake—she could tell by the quality of the silence, the listening silence of a man whose eyes had failed him and who therefore saw with his ears now. His face was turned toward the window, toward the sound of the bells, which had changed character as darkness deepened: less proclamation now, more plain celebration, wild and unregulated, the whole city ringing itself hoarse.

"You should be downstairs," he said. "Packing."

"Tomas is packing. He alphabetizes under stress. It's very soothing."

Her father laughed, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough passed, and he lay breathing while Rafael sat on the edge of the cot and put her hand over his hand, both of them stained faintly with ink that would never fully wash out—his from fifty years at the case, hers from twenty-two years of being born into it.

"Do you remember," he said, "when you were small, and you asked me why we didn't print ordinary things? Almanacs. Receipts. Weddings."

"You told me a printer who only prints safe things is just a stationer with expensive tools."

"I told you that to make you proud. I should have told you the truth." His grip tightened on her fingers, surprisingly strong. "The truth is I printed dangerous things because I couldn't help it. Because the words sat there in their cases begging to be set, and I lacked the imagination to refuse them. You, Raffie—you have the imagination. You've hidden what needed hiding, moved what needed moving, burned what needed burning. You've done it for a year with a baby on your hip. Whatever courage I had, I only ever rented it. Yours is owned outright."

"Papa—"

"Hush. An old man gets to talk on the last night of the old world." He turned his ruined eyes toward her, and she saw that he was trying to look at her, really look, and that it hurt him that he couldn't. "Describe yourself to me. Humor a blind man on New Year's Eve."

So Rafael, kneeling by the cot in the smell of camphor and cold plaster, described herself as though composing a page: tall, or tall enough; her mother's dark hair gone lank with a winter of poor food; a burn scar on the left forearm from the press, shaped like a comma, which she had always thought fitting—a mark that meant *not finished*. And Wren asleep downstairs with red-dyed wool on her head and her father's stubborn chin.

"The chin is mine?" her father asked.

"She got it from you the way she got her lungs from nobody at all. She simply arrived with them."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "When you cross tomorrow—if the ice holds, when the ice holds—don't look back at the bank. Not once. Promise me. Looking back is how Lot's wife became a pillar, and pillars cannot carry children."

"I promise."

"Liar," he said fondly. "Good. Liars keep promises better than honest folk—they've had practice understanding what's at stake."

* * *

Near midnight she went out alone into the lane, wrapped in Marta's spare cloak, leaving Wren asleep against Marta's broad side. She wanted, she realized, to see the old city one more time without pretending she wasn't saying goodbye.

The snow had stopped. The sky over the rooftops was the color of iron cooling, and against it the steeples stood like compositors' rules dividing lines of text. Fires burned in the streets—not riot fires, or not yet, but celebratory ones, barrels set ablaze and people gathered around them, passing bottles, singing songs whose words had changed since summer. She heard the old royal anthem sung once, ironically, and then drowned under something faster, something with the Marseilles in it, or what passed for it this far east, and she understood that the rumor from Paris had reached even the tannery quarter and that everyone, everyone, had decided tonight to believe it.

At the corner by the cooperage, someone had pasted a broadsheet to the shutters, still legible by firelight. Rafael read it and her chest tightened with recognition—not of the content, though the content was treasonous enough—but of the *typeface*. Fourteen point, loose leading, the distinctive nicked *e* she had sorted a thousand times.

Ours. Or ours once. Printed on a press like ours, by hands like Tomas's, distributed through channels her father had helped dig twenty years before she was born. The underground was older than she was. It would outlive the shop, outlive Hessler, outlive, perhaps, the regime itself. Regimes fell; the trade endured. Somewhere across the frozen river, a compositor was setting tomorrow's treason right now, breathing lead dust, keeping the faith.

A woman near the fire noticed her looking and raised her bottle. "Happy end of the world, sister!"

"Happy beginning," Rafael answered, and was startled to find she meant it.

She walked down to the river. The towpath was empty; sensible people were indoors or at fires. The river itself lay below the bank like a road to another country, which was precisely what it was—white, vast, moonlit, scarred here and there with the dark seams of pressure ridges. Forty feet of water, sleeping. In spring it would wake and move and grind whatever stood upon it, but tomorrow at dawn it would hold. It would hold for her, for Wren, for Tomas and Marta and two satchels and a bundle of shirts and one letter. She had measured the year in what they carried, and tomorrow everything they had left would cross on a floor of frozen water, and that seemed exactly, perfectly right. Survival had always been a matter of trusting thin things. Paper. Ice. Promises.

Out on the white expanse, a lantern bobbed—a fisherman, or a smuggler, or a soldier counting the hours until desertion. It paused, considered the far shore, and continued.

* * *

Back at the dyehouse, the bells had stopped at last, replaced by a softer sound: church clocks striking midnight all over the city, slightly out of accord, each one insisting on its own version of the same moment. In the workshop, Marta had opened the good bottle she'd been hiding since her husband died, and Tomas sat cross-legged on the floor letting Wren attack a wooden composing stick, showing her solemnly how to hold it, grip and knuckles, as though she might set her first line by spring.

"She's left-handed," Tomas reported. "I'm teaching her left-handed. It'll confuse the authorities."

"Everything about her confuses the authorities. That's her inheritance."

Marta poured three cups of wine and then, after a glance at the sleeping child, tipped a spoonful of honey into a fourth cup of warmed milk. "To the old century," she said. "May it rot."

"To the new one," Tomas said. "May it be better than we deserve."

They looked at Rafael. She thought of a hundred toasts she might give—to her father upstairs, to the smashed press, to the alderman who had warned them, to the magistrate Corvane sitting somewhere tonight in a warm house with a sealed report on his desk that would either save or damn them all depending on what he wrote in it tomorrow. She thought of the mother in the hooded cloak, wherever her body lay, who had carried Wren through these same streets one December night and chosen the print shop door out of every door in the city. She lifted her cup.

"To what we carried," she said. "All of it. Even the heavy things. Especially the heavy things."

They drank. Wren woke briefly, drank her honeyed milk with tremendous dignity, and went back down. The fire settled. Marta began humming an old dye-song, tuneless and steady, the kind of song that exists to keep hands moving and fear quiet, and Tomas sharpened the little knife he'd use to cut them from the ice if anyone went through, and Rafael sat apart with the satchel on her knees and did not sleep.

She counted the hours the way her father had taught her to count em quads—by feel, in the dark, knowing each one by its weight. Three hours until the garrison's wine did its work. Two until the coldest hour, when the ice would be hardest. One until she woke Wren and dressed her in every stitch of wool she owned.

At the bottom of the satchel, the letter waited, patient as lead. Tomorrow she would cross with it. Tomorrow night, or the night after, when the far shore was solid underfoot, she would finally do the thing she had refused to do for a year—she would feed it to a fire and watch the names go up in smoke, and she was not sure whether that would feel like freedom or like burying her father twice.

But that was tomorrow's arithmetic. Tonight there was only this: the dying century ticking itself out overhead, the living child breathing against Marta's side, the ice lying hard and bright and faithful beneath the moon, holding.

Then boots in the lane. Fast ones, several pairs, coming up from the river district—not the stagger of drunken soldiers but the disciplined rhythm she knew in her bones, the rhythm of the raid. Tomas was on his feet before she spoke, knife open, and Marta had snuffed the candle with one wet thumb, and in the sudden dark Rafael pulled Wren against her chest and listened to the knock land on Marta's door—hard, official, unmistakable—and a voice call through the wood:

"Open in the name of the magistrate. We're looking for the printer's daughter."
