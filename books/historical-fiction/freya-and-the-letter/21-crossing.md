## 21. Crossing

The ice spoke to us before we ever stepped onto it.

We heard it from the bank—a long, low groan that traveled under the frozen river like a word passed down a line of sleeping soldiers, each of them murmuring it onward without waking. Marta crossed herself. Tomas shifted the bundle on his back and said nothing, which from Tomas was a kind of agreement. The sound was not cracking, I told myself. It was settling. A river learning to hold its breath.

I had Wren against my chest inside Father's old coat, wrapped in two shawls and the wool blanket Marta had dyed madder-red the summer before everything went wrong—the only bright thing any of us owned now, and I had chosen it deliberately. If the river took us, let it take something red. Let them find color on that gray shore. I had spent one entire year teaching myself to be invisible, to move through checkpoints like paper moves through a press, unremarked and unremembered, and I was done with it. Not today. Today I would cross this river as myself.

"Say it again," Marta whispered. She liked the plan repeated aloud, as if repetition could stitch it more securely to the world.

"The far bank is Brandish territory past the willow line," I said. "The checkpoint there is understaffed in winter—they pull men for the city garrison when the frost comes. Corvane confirmed it twice. We go up the east road half a mile before anyone sees us, and by then we're laborers with a sick grandmother going to the shrine at Aldenmoor." I touched Marta's shawl, the gray one she'd worn to her husband's funeral twenty years ago and had kept folded in cedar ever since. "You make a very convincing dying woman."

"I've been practicing my cough." She demonstrated: dry, rattling, pitiful. It would have convinced me.

"And the lanterns?" Tomas asked. That was the question none of us had asked aloud since last night, and he asked it now because dawn was close and there was no more time to be delicate.

"On the far bank. Soldiers keep lanterns burning all night at the crossing point—it's regulation—but their light only reaches thirty feet onto the ice. The rest is ours." I looked at the river, a vast white plain stitched with moon-shadows, wide enough to swallow a cathedral. Three hundred yards of nothing between the life we were leaving and the life we hadn't met yet. "Their own rules will hide us. Hessler never once imagined his regulations could work against him. He thought they were walls."

"They are walls," Tomas said quietly.

"Then we'll walk through one."

* * *

We left Marta's cellar at the hour when even the cats have given up on the night—four bells, or near enough, the sky still black but black the way coals are black, holding fire somewhere deep. Father had wanted to come to the bank and watch us go. I had refused him, gently, three times, and on the fourth refusal he had taken my face in his hands—his hands, which had set ten million pieces of type and could feel a letter out of alignment blindfolded—and held them there a long moment as though checking me for crookedness.

"You were always my straightest line, Rafael," he'd said. "Go be true."

Now I stood on the south bank with my daughter's heartbeat tapping against my ribs and my breath freezing in the wool over my mouth, and I made myself look back once. The city rose behind us in tiers of dark roofline and chimneysmoke, and above it, on the hill, the print shop stood with its windows broken since the raid, pale rectangles like eyes gone sightless. My father's whole life pressed into silence behind those windows. Every book he'd ever made, every pamphlet, every wedding notice and grain price and funeral announcement—all of it forfeit, all of it smashed or confiscated, and him released two days ago into Marta's care with a cough that sounded too much like the beginning of the end and hands that shook now even when they weren't lifting anything.

He had told me to go where he couldn't follow, and I understood finally that it wasn't geography he meant. It was grief. He could not cross this river carrying what he carried, and so he had given the carrying to me.

Wren stirred against my chest. Fifteen months old now, walking on unstable legs across solid floors, saying *da* and *no* and—her newest word, learned from watching Tomas hunched over his forgery papers—*wrote*. She had no idea what a river was. She had never been outside after dark. And I was about to carry her onto three hundred yards of frozen water in the coldest week of the century's final winter, because staying had become the more dangerous thing, because Hessler's people were already asking at the gates about a woman and a red-haired child, because the century itself was ending and everyone with sense could hear the old order groaning exactly the way the ice was groaning, and I intended to be standing on new ground when it broke.

"Check your knots," I said, and we checked each other's knots, Tomas pulling the rope that linked the three of us into a loose triangle—he'd read somewhere that drowning men on the frozen lakes tied themselves together, and though none of us liked thinking about why, we did it. Then Marta spat over her shoulder, an old boatman's charm, and we stepped down onto the ice.

* * *

It held.

That first step is with me now the way certain moments stay—the moment the seal broke on the letter, the moment Corvane's pen scratched *released* beside Father's name. My boot came down on white river and the white river bore me, and beneath the sole of my boot I felt not solidity but a vast, indifferent patience, the way one feels standing on a sleeping animal's flank. I took the second step anyway. Then the third. Then I stopped counting steps and started counting the lanterns on the far bank: four, hung on poles at the official crossing, their flames small and steady as punctuation marks at the end of a sentence I refused to finish.

We kept north, away from the light, angling toward the willow line where Corvane swore the bank went unwatched for a hundred yards. The moon was down; the sky had begun its slow bruise toward morning, violet-gray at the edges. Our shadows were faint things. For once I was glad of my own shadow. For once I didn't need to erase it.

The ice changed character as we went. Near the banks it had been snow-dusted, dull; here in the middle it was bare and glassy, wind-scoured, and beneath our boots we could see *into* it—dark currents locked mid-motion, bubbles strung like beads, a leaf suspended a foot down, perfectly preserved, some autumn thing caught in winter's amber. Wren stared down at it, fascinated, her mittened hand patting the surface.

"No," I breathed, mostly to myself, and she patted it again, delighted by the smooth cold hardness of the world.

Marta fell into her funeral cough behind me—not performance now; the cold was finding her old lungs—and Tomas slowed the pace to match her without being asked. He had changed since October. Or perhaps I had simply finally seen him. The boy who'd hidden in the type room during inspections had become a young man who forged shipping papers with a steady hand and copied forbidden letters into a network of printers stretching three cities wide, who had done the bravest and most foolish thing anyone had ever done for me and then stood in front of me shaking while I raged, waiting to learn whether love survived anger. It had. It does. I reached back without looking and found his hand, and we walked a dozen steps that way before the rope between us made it awkward, and neither of us mentioned it, and both of us remembered it.

Halfway across, the river spoke again.

It began as a sound I felt before I heard it—a tremor through my boot soles, faint as a word spoken in another room. Then the ice *groaned*, long and enormous, a sound with no top and no bottom, the sound of something immeasurably heavy turning over in its sleep, and it rolled beneath us and away across the whole white width of the river, and every hair on my body stood up, and Marta gasped, and Tomas's grip crushed my fingers—

And Wren laughed.

She threw back her head inside the great collar of the coat and laughed, high and clear and entirely without fear, the way children laugh at thunder, at dogs, at faces—at everything vast and strange that adults have taught themselves to dread. Her laugh rang out over the frozen river like a bell over open water, and the sound of it did something to the terror in my chest that I cannot properly explain except to say this: it did not erase the terror. It sat down beside it. They occupied me together, laughter and dread, and I understood in that ringing moment that they were the same sensation wearing different clothes—that what I felt, crossing that ice, suspended between a burned life and an unbuilt one, was freedom indistinguishable from fear, and that I would spend the rest of my days unable to say which one had carried me.

"Hush, little wren," Marta whispered, but she was smiling, and her smile cracked the frost on her lips, and I saw that the laugh had done for her what it did for me.

"She's right, though," Tomas said. His voice was unsteady. "It's just the ice settling. Expansion. It's the sound of it getting *stronger*."

"It's the sound of it getting stronger," I agreed, having no idea if this was true, and we walked on.

* * *

Two hundred yards from the far bank, the lanterns moved.

I saw it first—one flame detaching from the cluster, swinging in a slow arc, the way a lantern swings when it is carried by someone walking. Then a second. Voices carried flat and distorted across the ice, words I couldn't parse, and my heart went from steady to hammering in the space of two breaths.

"Don't run," I said. Running was death on ice; running was attention. "Walk. Shrine pilgrims walk."

"We're not near the shrine yet," Marta said, with terrible reasonableness. "Pilgrims don't start walking until they reach land."

"Then we're lost travelers. Walk like you're lost and cold and want nothing but a fire."

We walked. The lanterns came on—two of them now, moving along the far bank parallel to our course, and I made myself count our strides instead of watching them, made myself breathe in the rhythm Father had taught me for steadying my hand at the composing stick: in for the letters, out for the spaces. In for the letters. Out for the spaces. Wren had gone quiet against my chest, sensing the change in my body the way animals sense weather, and I murmured to her, nonsense, a lullaby fragment, anything, my lips barely moving.

A voice came across the ice. "*Ho! Who walks?*"

"Travelers!" I called back, pitching my voice into the flat weary register I had used at three checkpoints with a letter sewn into my coat. "Bound for Aldenmoor! We're nearly dead with cold!"

A pause. The lanterns stopped swinging. Then, distant, bored, the voice of a man whose war had not started yet and who resented the hour: *"Crossing's closed till full light! You'll wait where you are or come to the post!"*

And here was the arithmetic of the next ten seconds, performed at the speed of falling: if we went to the post, we would stand in lantern light, and lantern light showed faces, and my face was known now—known to Hessler's people, described in whatever circular had gone round after Wren's mother talked. If we waited on the ice, we waited in the open, visible, until full light brought the shift change and possibly worse questions. But the voice had said *you'll wait where you are*, and a man gives orders to be obeyed, and a man whose orders are obeyed goes back to his fire, and the willow line was eighty yards north of where we stood, and the sky in the east had gone the color of a healing wound.

"We'll wait here, thank you!" I called. "God keep you warm, sirs!"

"*God keeps nobody warm on this river,*" the voice muttered, almost lost in the wind, and the lanterns turned back toward the post.

We did not run. I want that written somewhere: we did not run, though every nerve sang the way type sings in a frame before the screw is tightened. We walked, north and slightly east, unhurried, three travelers and a child, and when the willow line rose out of the gloom ahead of us—a ragged fringe of bare gold whips against the graying sky—I put my arm through Marta's and gave her my shoulder to cough against, and we climbed the low bank among the willows, and my boots left the ice, and the ground received me with the shocking tenderness of earth.

Behind us, on the white plain of the river, dawn arrived.

It came all at once, the way winter dawns do—light pouring flat and pink-gold across the ice from horizon to horizon, turning the frozen river into a road of hammered copper, and on that road our shadows stretched back long and dark toward the city, toward the shop, toward the whole drowned year. From the checkpoint, tiny with distance, a soldier emerged to begin the morning watch, saw the tracks stitching the ice, and stood a long moment with his lantern guttering in the growing day. He looked at our tracks. He looked at the empty brilliance of the river. And then he turned back to his stove, because it was cold, because the century was ending, because some mornings a man decides not to see what he has seen.

Tomas sank down against a willow trunk and laughed until he cried. Marta patted his knee and coughed her real cough and said we'd best get off the road before full light, and I stood a moment longer, facing back across the river with Wren awake and solemn against my chest, both of us watching the sun come up over the city where I had learned to carry things.

"One day," I told her, "I'll tell you about the river."

She put her mittened hand flat against my cheek, as if taking my face in her small hands to check me for crookedness, the way her grandfather had done.

Down the east road, half a mile on, a wagon stood waiting where Corvane had promised it would—the first kindness of the country we hadn't entered yet, driven by a man who owed the magistrate a favor and asked no questions. But as we came up the rise toward it, I saw that the driver was not alone. There was a second figure on the wagon bench, hooded, thin, sitting very still.

And even at that distance, even before the light fully came, I knew the set of those shoulders. I had seen them once before, before dawn, at a print shop door, fifteen months and one lifetime ago.
