## 22. Freya and the Letter

The far bank was a smudge of gray willows and pale morning light when Rafael Fairweather's boots finally met solid ground. Her legs shook—not from the crossing, or not only from the crossing, but from the strange vertigo of standing on earth that did not move beneath her, after an hour of ice that had groaned and sighed and told her, in its slow tectonic language, exactly how expendable she was.

Behind them, the river stretched white and vast, back to the city she had been born in, back to the black shell of the print shop with its windows blown out and its press lying in pieces like a slaughtered animal. Soldiers' lanterns still bobbed along the near shore, small and futile as fireflies. No one had followed. No one would. The ice was already softening at the edges where the current ran quick beneath, and by afternoon the river would be a gray scar of broken plates, impassable until deep winter came again. By then, Rafael Fairweather intended to be a rumor in a town with no name she knew.

Tomas stumbled up the bank behind her, carrying Marta on his back—the old dyer had refused twice to be left behind, and had refused a third time to let anyone else carry her bundle of indigo-stained linens, her whole portable life. He set her down gently in the snow beneath the willows, and Marta looked back across the river at the smoke rising from the city's chimneys, and said nothing at all. There are goodbyes too large for the mouth. They go out through the eyes instead.

Wren woke in the sling against Rafael's chest as the adrenaline drained out of her, and began to fuss—that thin, demanding mewl that meant milk, that meant morning, that meant the world was continuing whether or not anyone had given it permission.

"Not yet, little one," Rafael murmured. "Let me have this first."

She set the child carefully in Marta's arms, unwound her own scarf, and knelt in the snow.

From inside her coat, from the inner pocket she had sewn herself three winters ago for papers worth more than her life, she drew out the letter.

It had traveled far since a hooded woman had pressed it into the world along with a squalling infant and a handful of coins, before dawn, on a night that felt like it belonged to another century—which, Rafael supposed now, it technically did. It had been read by candlelight by a blind man running his fingers over the seal like a fortune-teller. It had been folded into the spine of a returned book and turned away from a bridge. It had ridden downriver inside a crate of type molds, wearing another document's face. It had passed through the Gate of Ashes hidden in a funeral wreath, close enough to lilies that for years afterward Rafael would never smell them without her heart stuttering. It had been copied by an apprentice's stubborn hand and scattered into an underground network of printers who had, in the end, done what printers do: made the truth too numerous to strangle. And it had been read aloud, at last, in a courtroom, by a magistrate with a careful voice and a conscience he had measured and found heavier than his career—read aloud while Hessler's face went the color of old tallow, while the conspirators named in its neat columns turned on one another like rats in a flooding cellar, while the law bent, briefly and beautifully, toward right.

The letter had hung half the neighborhood's masters and saved one compositor from the gallows. It had cost a print shop, a way of life, a century. Its work was done.

Rafael struck the flint from Tomas's pocket. The paper caught slowly—it was good rag stock, heavy, made to last—and she held it in both gloved hands and watched the flame walk across the names.

She had expected to feel triumph. She had expected grief, maybe, or the hollow lightness of a debt discharged. What she felt instead was something closer to gratitude—an odd, quiet thankfulness toward the thing itself, toward the woman who had written it and the woman who had delivered it and the child who had arrived attached to it like a second, secret message. *Everything you need will come through your door,* her father used to say about the printing trade, meaning commissions and news-sheets and the occasional dead rat the cats dragged in. He had been more right than he knew.

The flame reached her fingers and she dropped the last corner into the snow, where it guttered, flared blue, and went out. A circle of black ash on white, already being covered by new fall.

"There," she said softly. "That's finished."

Marta rocked the baby and hummed something tuneless and old. Tomas stood with his hands jammed in his armpits, watching the horizon the way he always did—checking, checking—and Rafael loved him for it with a fierceness that surprised her every time.

"Freya," she said.

Tomas glanced over. "What?"

"Her name." Rafael took her daughter back from Marta and held her up, so the child could see the sky, the willows, the ash, the wide unopened country lying north and west like a book no one had read yet. "I've been thinking on it all the way across. Wren was a name for hiding—a small brown bird you'd overlook. And she was right for hiding then." The baby grabbed at Rafael's scarf with the imperious grip of someone who has survived a frozen river at eleven months old and intends to survive considerably more. "But we're done hiding. So—Freya. After the goddess who survives every ending. Even this one. Especially this one."

"Freya," Marta repeated, testing it, and nodded once, the way she nodded at a dye bath that had come out true. "It suits. She'll grow into it."

"She'll grow *past* it," Rafael said. "That's rather the point."

Tomas smiled—his rare, crooked, whole-face smile—and said, "Freya Fairweather."

And the wind came down off the northern hills and carried the name out over the snow, and the old century lay dying on the wrong side of the river, and nobody sang, because they were too tired and too alive, which is sometimes the same thing.

**Twenty-three years later**

The girl at the composing stick had ink on her nose again. It was, Rafael had long ago concluded, a permanent condition, like freckles or opinions. Freya sat cross-legged on the workbench of their small shop in the free town of Aldenmere—*Fairweather & Daughter, Printers, Bookbinders, and Dealers in Honest Paper*, though the sign also said, in smaller letters underneath, *and Son-in-Law*, which Tomas pretended not to be pleased about—setting type by lamplight with the fierce concentration of someone doing surgery on sentences.

"Read it back," Rafael said, from the press.

"'The rights of women are not granted by governments,'" Freya recited, sliding each line of type with her thumb, checking it against the manuscript propped on the stone. "'They are remembered by them.'" She frowned. "Grandmother Marta says this one will get us run out of three towns."

"Grandmother Marta," Rafael said, "has been run out of four towns and considers it a form of travel." Which was true; Marta was seventy-nine now and dyed cloth in colors that scandalized the respectable, and had pronounced opinions on everything, and had outlived every official who had ever tried to silence her, which Rafael considered the most elegant revenge available to a private citizen.

Rafael's father had not lived to see Aldenmere. He had lasted five years past the crossing—five years of sitting in the sun outside whatever shop they'd built, listening to the presses run, his ruined eyes aimed at the sound the way other men aimed them at a view, saying *that's a clean forme, that one, I can hear it*—and he had died in his bed with his hand in Rafael's and the whole town's typesetters walking behind his coffin. She still missed him the way you miss a wall you leaned on for twenty years: not constantly, but completely, whenever you reached for it.

Corvane had lived long enough to receive, every year on the winter solstice, a single sheet printed by Fairweather & Daughter containing nothing but the words *A good man measures his soul* and the year. He had never once acknowledged them. Last solstice the letter had come back unopened, marked *deceased*. Rafael had printed next year's anyway and burned it, because some debts should be paid even when the creditor is gone.

"Here." Freya pulled the finished galley and held it out. "Proof it."

Rafael read her daughter's setting. It was flawless. Of course it was flawless. The girl had learned her letters before she could reliably reach the washbasin, had learned them from a mother who taught reading the way other people taught religion—as salvation, as inheritance, as the difference between being a subject and being a citizen. Freya could set type faster than Tomas, could argue typography with traveling booksellers, could smell cheap ink at forty paces. She had her mother's dark stubbornness and Tomas's habit of checking the window twice, and when she laughed, which was often, Rafael heard the ice under her feet again, that astonishing laugh on the frozen river, a baby laughing at the thing everyone else feared.

"You know," Rafael said, "when you were small—you wouldn't remember this—I carried you across a frozen river."

Freya rolled her eyes, but gently, the way you roll your eyes at scripture. "I know. The checkpoints. The letter in the wreath. The magistrate. The tyrant Hessler, whose name must be spoken with dramatic pauses." She counted them off on ink-stained fingers. "You tell it every year on New Year's Eve."

"I tell it every year because it's true every year. That's how truth works. It doesn't expire like a government's promises." Rafael set the proof down. "But there's a part I've never told you. Not the whole of it."

Freya paused, mid-motion, a line of type suspended between stick and stone. Twenty-three years old and she still knew when her mother's voice changed register. "Which part?"

"The letter itself." Rafael reached into the drawer under the counter—the deep drawer, the locked one, the one Freya had been forbidden since childhood and had, Rafael happened to know, picked the lock of at age nine, because she was her mother's daughter—but tonight the lock was open. Tonight Rafael lifted out a flat tin box, and inside the box lay a scrap of scorched rag paper, one corner, no bigger than a hand, its edges burned to lace.

"I kept this," Rafael said. "Off the end, where the names stopped. Where the signature was. I told myself it was evidence. Then I told myself it was a relic. The truth is I kept it because I couldn't bear for there to be *nothing*—no page, no proof that a frightened woman wrote down what she saw at the cost of her life, and that it mattered." She laid the fragment on the counter between them. "Her name is half-burned. See? *—ora*. Everyone else called her a traitor or a martyr depending on their politics. Nobody ever knew her name. But you should know it, because you set type, and somewhere in the years ahead some other frightened person is going to write down what they saw, and it's going to come through your door, and you'll have to decide what to do with it. When that day comes, I want you to remember that a piece of paper with a half a name on it crossed a river, ended a conspiracy, freed a prisoner, and raised you."

Freya looked at the fragment a long time. Then she did something Rafael hadn't anticipated, though perhaps she should have: she fetched a sheet of good rag stock and her finest pen, and copied the surviving letters onto it, and beneath them, in her own precise compositor's hand, wrote out the full name as the family had privately reconstructed it—*Eleanora*—and beneath that: *who wrote the truth and sent it into the world with her daughter.*

"There," Freya said. "Now she's typeset. Now she can't be burned." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and fierce and twenty-three years old in a country that still, imperfectly, stubbornly, existed. "And someday I'll tell my children the story, and keep the proof sheet in the drawer, and forbid them to touch it."

"It's tradition," Rafael said gravely.

Outside, the bells of Aldenmere began to ring—not alarm bells, celebration bells, the solstice ringing, the whole town turning out with lanterns and bad wine and worse singing. Through the window Rafael could see Tomas lighting the lamp over the door, and Marta coming up the street in a shawl the color of a riot, and the snow starting again, soft and unhurried, covering the roads north and south alike.

New century's eve, some said, though the counting was confused and the calendars disagreed and Rafael had stopped caring which year it officially was. Time, she had learned, was not measured in centuries. It was measured in what you carried across, and what you set down on the far side, and who picked it up after you.

"Come on," Freya said, pulling on her coat, holding the door open so the cold and the bells poured in together. "The others are waiting. And you promised Wren—"

"*Freya*," Rafael corrected automatically, and her daughter grinned, because the joke was twenty-two years old and still running, a small brown bird and a goddess sharing one body and one name and one incorrigible laugh.

"—you promised *Freya* you'd tell the river story again," Freya said, "from the beginning."

Rafael banked the lamp, locked nothing—for the drawer stood open now, its secret typeset and published, its work done—and stepped out with her daughter into the falling snow, and began, as she had begun every year, and would begin every year after, as long as there were ears to hear and honest paper to hold it:

"Before dawn, on the coldest night of the old year, a woman came to our door carrying two things the world wanted very badly to destroy..."

And the presses waited, patient as rivers, for morning.
