## 1. The Question Too Many

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded into Beatrix Whitlock's letter slot like a knife slipped between floorboards. *The Observatory Master Clock has failed. Present yourself with tools at your earliest convenience. — Office of the Town Registrar.* The registrar's seal was stamped crooked, as if the hand that pressed it had already grown bored of formality. Beatrix read it twice by lamplight, then set it beneath the brass weight of their smallest clock, where it could be flattened into obedience like everything else in the shop.

They did not want to go. That was the first truth of the day, and Beatrix held it the way they held all inconvenient truths: quietly, in a pocket, where it could not be seen to exist.

The second truth was that they had no choice. The observatory clock governed every lesser clock in Harrowgate — the bank's, the schoolmaster's, the ones in the stationmaster's office that decided when trains departed and when lives were permitted to begin moving. A town that could not agree on the hour was a town that began asking questions, and questions were the one currency Beatrix had spent thirty years refusing to carry.

So they packed the toolbox. Mainspring hooks, pliers of three sizes, the loupe that hung at their breastbone like a third eye, a tin of clock oil that smelled of whale fat and patience. They buttoned their coat to the throat, pulled the brim of their hat low, and became what the town expected: Whitlock the apprentice clockmaker, narrow-shouldered and unremarkable, a person shaped like a question mark filed down into a full stop.

*Be invisible,* their mother had told them, the last thing she ever said that mattered. *Not hidden — invisible. Hidden things get searched for.*

The hill up to the observatory was steep enough to make the lungs argue. October had come down hard on Harrowgate overnight; the gorse along the path wore frost like lace mourning veils, and the sky above the tower was the pale, rinsed grey of unwashed linen. Beatrix climbed with the toolbox swinging at their knee and counted the bells of St. Aldric's tolling seven — forty seconds slow, they noted automatically, against whatever the observatory was no longer telling anyone.

At the tower door stood a man from the Registrar's office, holding a clipboard as though it might bolt. He looked Beatrix over with the particular blankness officials reserve for tradespeople, checked a name off his list, and waved them through without a word. Good. Invisible still.

The stair wound upward inside the tower's stone gizzard — two hundred and six steps; Beatrix counted, because counting was prayer and prayer was calm — past shelves of disused instruments, past a window slit through which the whole of Harrowgate lay revealed in miniature: chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold, the market square's awnings faded to the pink of old skin. Their town. Their hiding place. From up here it looked almost small enough to forgive.

Then the stair ended, and the clock room opened around them like the inside of some enormous devoted heart.

The master clock dominated the far wall — a case of black walnut two stories high if one counted its housing, its face pale as a moon behind glass, its hands stopped at 4:17 of some forgotten morning weeks ago. Beside it the great pendulum hung dead still, and the silence of the thing was worse than any noise; a stopped clock of this magnitude filled a room with a silence like held breath. Gears showed through the case's open panel, brass teeth the size of dinner plates, furred with dust where oil had failed them.

And beneath the clock face, at a folding table that had no business existing in a house of astronomy, sat a woman with census forms.

She was writing when Beatrix stepped through the doorway, and for a moment they simply watched her — watched the economy of her hand, the way she squared each completed sheet against the table's edge as if paper, too, deserved precision. She wore the slate-grey coat of the Census Bureau, plain and correct, but she wore it the way an actor wears costume: convincingly, and not quite naturally. Her hair was the colour of dark honey caught in lamplight, pinned back with what appeared to be, improbably, the spring clip of a pocket watch.

She looked up. Her eyes were grey-green, clear as bottle glass, and they went through Beatrix the way light goes through a lens — gathered, bent, focused somewhere Beatrix preferred not to be.

"Whitlock?" she said. "The clockmaker."

"The apprentice," Beatrix said. Their voice came out lower than intended, worn smooth from disuse. "Master Corwin's apprentice. Sent in his place."

"Sent in his place." She repeated it without inflection, writing it down. *Occupant of record declined to appear; agent dispatched.* Something about watching her record them made Beatrix's skin prickle — the strange intimacy of being turned into a line in another person's ledger. "I'm Freya Ashworth. Census taker, District Nine." She gestured at the stool across from her with the end of her pen. "Sit, if you like. I'm told the repair will take hours. I'm required to observe."

"Required to observe what?"

"The work. Any civic instrument under assessment must have a Bureau witness present." A flicker crossed her face — something dry and quick, gone before it settled. "I suspect the requirement exists mostly so that someone from the Bureau is seen to be doing something."

It was so nearly a joke that Beatrix blinked. They sat, set the toolbox between their feet like a shield, and opened it, taking comfort in the familiar ratchet-clicks of preparation. "Then observe."

"I will." She drew a fresh form from her folder and smoothed it flat. "While you work, I'll finish your household's enumeration. Your file is thin. Thinner than the regulations allow."

The word *household* went through Beatrix like a pick through a lock.

They turned to the clock instead. The access panel came away under their hands with a groan of complaint, and the smell rose up out of the works — old oil gone to varnish, brass oxide, the sweet dust of decades. Up close the failure was legible everywhere: the great escape wheel seized with corrosion, the maintaining-power spring snapped clean through, a hairline crack running through the fourth wheel's arbor like a scar on a wrist. Months of neglect. Perhaps years.

"You've let it die," Beatrix said, half to themselves.

"The town lets many things die," Freya Ashworth said. "It prefers things it doesn't have to look at."

Beatrix glanced at her sharply. She was looking at the clock, not at them, but there was something in the set of her mouth that suggested she was speaking of other things entirely, and knew it.

They worked. It was easier to work, always, easier than talking; the hands understood language better than the tongue did. They cleaned the pivot holes with pegwood and solvent, dressed the corroded teeth of the escape wheel with a file fine as an eyelash, and slowly, gear by gear, the machine began to give up its secrets to them the way machines always did — willingly, gratefully, without asking who they were first. This was the mercy of clockwork. A gear did not care what a person was. A mainspring asked nothing about household composition.

"Name," Freya said presently, pen poised.

"You have my name."

"For the form. Full name, as registered."

"Beatrix Whitlock."

"Mm." Pen scratching. "Date of birth?"

"Nineteen-oh-eight. March." A lie of only a season. Small lies were load-bearing walls.

"Occupation?"

"Clockmaker's apprentice."

"And—" she turned the page, and Beatrix heard the page turn the way one hears a stair creak in an empty house— "household composition. Who resides at the workshop premises with you? Spouse, children, parents, siblings, lodgers, servants? The form requires all persons regularly resident, their relationship to the head of household, and their sex."

The toolbox went very still in Beatrix's hands.

Outside, wind found the tower and leaned against it, and somewhere below a shutter banged once, twice, and gave up. The dead clock ticked nothing. Beatrix knelt there with a pivot hole half-cleaned and their heart going like a broken escapement, *tick-tock-tick-tock-tick* without the tock ever landing, and thought: *this is the question. This is the one I have dodged for eleven years, in five towns, on nine forms, and here it is again, wearing a different grey coat.*

"I live alone," they said at last. It came out steady. They had practiced it until it sounded like weather.

"Mm," Freya Ashworth said again.

But she didn't write it. Not immediately. Beatrix risked a glance up, and found her watching them — not the clock now, them — with her pen resting against her lower lip and her eyes narrowed slightly, not in suspicion exactly. In *measurement*. The way Beatrix themselves measured a mechanism whose behaviour didn't match its blueprint: not yet condemning the machine, merely wondering whether the fault lay in the gears or in the drawing.

"That's the second time," she said, "that you've answered a simple question like a man defusing something."

"The third," Beatrix said, before they could stop it.

Something happened then to her mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat. She laid the pen down parallel to the form's edge, deliberately, and folded her hands.

"Mr. Whitlock. Or Miss. Or—" and here she tilted her head a degree, and Beatrix felt the tower tilt with it— "whichever the form has failed to anticipate."

The wind pressed at the windows. Two hundred and six steps below, the town went on believing its own time.

"I don't know," Beatrix said slowly, "what you're meant to write."

"No," Freya agreed softly. "But I know what I'm *required* to write. And I've begun to wonder lately whether those are the same thing." She picked the pen back up, and left the household line — Beatrix saw it clearly — blank. "Show me the escapement," she said. "I've been told it's the part that decides when everything else is allowed to move. I'd like to understand how a thing earns that authority."

Beatrix reached for the lantern, and their hands were not entirely steady, and she pretended — courteously, precisely, like a form left unfinished — not to notice.

* * *

By noon they had the escape wheel free and turning in Beatrix's lap like a great brass flower, and Freya Ashworth had abandoned her forms entirely, chin propped on her fist, asking questions no census had ever thought to ask. It was when she leaned close to watch the pallet stones engage — close enough that Beatrix could smell rain on her coat and graphite on her fingers — that she said, lightly, as though remarking on the weather:

"They'll send me back tomorrow, you know. To verify the enumeration. It's how the Bureau does things. Twice, always twice." A pause. "And it's when they'll ask me whether the workshop's occupant answered *all* the questions."

The escape wheel gleamed between them, patient, indifferent, keeping its own counsel about time.

Beatrix met her eyes, and understood suddenly that they had not climbed this tower alone at all.
