## 2. Gears and Ledgers

Beatrix Whitlock worked best at dawn, when the observatory was theirs alone and the only witnesses were instruments that could not gossip. They arrived before the lamplighter had finished his rounds, toolbox in hand, coat collar up, moving through the tower's spiral stair with the ease of someone who had long ago learned to make themselves slight — a trick of posture, of silence, of never quite meeting anyone's eyes.

The master clock filled the clock room like a patient in a sickbed. It stood twice Beatrix's height, its brass skeleton open to the air, and it had not struck true since spring. The town below told the time by it, or believed it did; the trains ran on its word, the market bells, the school hours. A machine that lied to eleven thousand people twice a day.

Beatrix set down the toolbox, unlatched it, and let their hands begin the conversation they were too careful to have with anyone else. Fingertips along the escapement. A loupe raised to the pallet stones. The great pendulum swaying its slow, wounded rhythm — three seconds late each day, drifting like a ship with no rudder.

They had been at the third wheel for an hour when the stair door opened below, and they knew before they turned who it would be by the particular quality of the footsteps: unhurried, deliberate, the tread of a woman who had been trained to announce herself.

"Good morning," Freya Ashworth said from the doorway. She held her leather folio against her chest like a shield, or perhaps like something one protects rather than something that protects. "I'm verifying the building census. There are discrepancies."

"There usually are," Beatrix said, and did not stop working.

She came closer. Beatrix heard her set the folio on the stone sill, heard paper unfold — though Beatrix would have wagered their whole workshop that she wasn't reading it. She was watching their hands. Most people watched the clock. She watched the hands.

"You hold the tweezers like a surgeon," she said.

"Like a clockmaker. Surgeons get to drop things."

The laugh surprised out of her was short and real, and Beatrix felt it land somewhere beneath the ribs, inconvenient as a dropped screw rolling under the case.

* * *

Freya had counted four thousand two hundred souls that autumn, and she knew what the forms did to them. The questions flattened people. Widow Whitlock of 14 Chandler's Lane became a tick in a column; the Kessler twins became a single entry with a doubled number beside it; the ferryman who slept summers on the water became a problem she'd solved by inventing an address. Every form was a small machine for making people smaller, and she operated it six days a week and told herself it was honest work because the alternative was to admit it was a kind of violence.

But the clock room was different. In the clock room there was Beatrix Whitlock, who did not flatten, who grew more intricate the longer one looked — and Freya found excuses to look. Verification, she wrote in her log. Records review. Follow-up required.

"Tell me what it's doing," she said on the fourth morning, settling onto the stool that had somehow migrated from the corner to a spot near the workbench. "The sickness. Of the clock."

Beatrix glanced up. In the cold light through the clock face their eyes were the grey-green of weathered bronze. "It's lonely," they said, and Freya thought it was a joke until they went on. "Its partner weight was removed during the war effort. Melted down for shell casings, they say. A clock built for two driving weights, running on one. It keeps time, but it keeps it grieving. Everything drifts."

"And you can fix grief with gears?"

"I can give it back what it lost." Beatrix lifted a new-machined wheel into the light, turning it slowly. "This is cast from the old alloy. I had to reverse-engineer the weight from the wear marks on the remaining one. Twenty years of friction taught me everything the ledgers forgot." A pause. "Machines remember honestly. People's records don't."

"Is that so." Freya opened the folio on her knees, more for something to do with her hands than anything else. "And which am I? Honest ledger, or dishonest one?"

Beatrix looked at her then — really looked, which they almost never permitted themselves — and Freya felt catalogued down to her serial number.

"Neither," Beatrix said. "You're the hand holding the pen. That's the part no form has a box for."

* * *

By the second week, Beatrix found they listened for her footsteps on the stairs the way sailors listen for gulls. It alarmed them. Their entire life was built on the principle that no one should come up the stairs at all.

They worked while she sat near, and between them grew a language of machines. She described the census: the carbon paper, the triplicate forms, the great sorting cabinets in the provincial office where every citizen lived as a card in a drawer. He showed her — hesitantly, then with growing hunger to be witnessed — the eight-day movement, the recoil escapement, the way a wheel's teeth must meet like hands clasping. Once she brought her own pocket watch, stopped these ten years, and laid it on the bench without a word, and Beatrix repaired it over three afternoons without remarking on the tremor in her hands when she took it back.

"It's strange," Freya said one grey afternoon, watching the pendulum's restored arc. "We spend our lives inside other people's systems. You keep the machine that tells everyone when to obey. I fill the forms that tell everyone who they're permitted to be. And neither of us fits in either."

"No," Beatrix said quietly. "We don't."

The word *we* hung in the clock room like dust in a sunbeam. Neither of them swept it away.

It was Freya who reached first — or perhaps it was Beatrix; later neither could say — but their hands met on the workbench over the scattered screws, and stayed. Her fingers were ink-stained. Theirs were brass-burned. The pendulum swung its great slow arc above them, keeping time for a town that didn't know their names, and Beatrix thought: *this is the most visible I have ever been, and I am not afraid.*

"Careful," Freya murmured, but she was smiling. "Someone might see."

"Let them," said Beatrix, who had spent twenty-seven years letting no one.

* * *

It was the inspector's clerk who came on Friday, a thin man with a clipboard and a smell of cheap tobacco, who asked the caretaker downstairs why the observatory received so many visits from the census office regarding the *irregular occupant* — Miss Whitlock, or Mister Whitlock, or whichever it was this quarter — whose household composition had been flagged twice and returned once.

Freya was on the stair when she heard it. She stood very still in the shadows of the turn, folio pressed to her chest, and listened to the clerk read aloud from his notes: *"Occupant declines categorization. Occupant's declaration of 'clockmaker' conflicts with registered trade of 'widow.' Recommend referral to district supervisor for—"* 

For noncompliance. For seizure. She had seen the referrals before; she had even, in her first credulous year, written two.

That night Freya sat at her kitchen table with her draft report on Beatrix Whitlock spread before her — the flags, the inconsistencies, the neat bureaucratic language that would put men with padlocks at the door of 14 Chandler's Lane by month's end — and her pen hovered, and hovered, and did not fall.

Above the town, in the dark tower, the master clock ticked on one weight, faithful and grieving, and Beatrix worked late by lamplight fitting the final wheels, unaware — humming, actually humming, a thing they had not done in years.

Freya read the report once more. Then she rose, opened the stove, and held the pages to the coals until the categories curled and blackened and let go of Beatrix Whitlock entirely.

What she would tell them — what she had already, in some chamber of herself, decided — would have to be said in the clock room, among the turning gears, where things could finally be made to run true.
