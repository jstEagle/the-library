## 3. What the Census Cannot Hold

The summons came on a Tuesday, in the form of a memorandum typed on the office's second-worst typewriter, the one with the sticking *R* that made every refusal look like a snarl.

*Mrs. Ashworth — District Fourteen requires immediate clarification regarding the Whitlock premises, Clock Lane. Subject has declined to complete Schedule B (Household Composition). Escalate per Regulation 11. Noncompliance to be documented and forwarded.*

Freya read it three times at her desk by the window, where the light fell in a way that let her hold the paper close to her chest without anyone behind her reading over her shoulder. Regulation 11. She knew Regulation 11 the way a clockmaker knows a broken tooth in an escapement wheel: the small mechanism by which everything else was brought to a stop. Documented noncompliance. Seizure of premises pending review. The subject remanded for *classification*.

Classification. The word had always sounded administrative to her ears, harmless as *filing*. It had taken Beatrix Whitlock's hands moving inside an open clock, sleeves rolled, ink on their knuckles, to teach her what the word actually meant: that somewhere in a ledger in the district office, there was a column with no row for you, and that the clerks, finding no row, would simply rule a line through your name.

She looked down at the draft report she had begun that morning — half a page, all of it true, none of it bearable. *Subject states the census categories do not describe them. Subject declined amendment. Subject's work on the observatory master clock is* —

Her pen had stopped there, four days ago, and the ink had dried into a small brown period like a full stop in the wrong place.

She took the draft with her when she left, folded into her coat against her ribs, where it felt less like paper than like a coal.

* * *

The district office kept its records in the basement, in long oak drawers that smelled of dust and sealing wax, and it was there, pulling the file on Clock Lane for the escalation, that Freya discovered the seizure had already been decided.

The folder was thin — the Whitlock file was nearly empty, which should have warned her; invisible lives make thin folders — but clipped inside was a pink slip, the color reserved for actions taken rather than proposed. *Premises, Clock Lane: flagged. Workshop and contents scheduled for assessment and seizure, pending completion of clock repairs. Note: observatory master clock contract to be reassigned upon clearance of occupant.*

Upon completion. Upon clearance. The machinery of the state, she thought, was exactly like the machinery of the clock: nothing moved until everything was ready, and then everything moved at once, and no single gear could be blamed for the striking of the hour. Someone had set this train of wheels in motion weeks ago — before her first visit, perhaps, before she had ever climbed the tower stairs and found Beatrix Whitlock standing in the doorway of the clock room with their toolbox and their careful, guarded eyes. She was not being asked to begin anything. She was being asked only to be the last small gear that let it strike.

She thought of Mr. Halloran at his desk upstairs, his fondness for saying that the census was not interested in persons, only in households. She thought of how he had smiled last month when the new forms arrived, with their additional columns, their finer teeth.

And she thought of Beatrix, three nights ago, standing among the turning gears with lamplight gilding the dust, telling her — haltingly, the way one confesses to a crime — about the year they had stopped answering questions. *You learn to be weather*, they had said. *Weather isn't counted. Weather is only reported.*

The pink slip trembled very slightly in her hand, and Freya Ashworth understood that she had already made her choice, days or weeks ago, in the clock room, in the tower, at the moment she had begun returning to verify records that needed no verification. The memorandum was only informing her of herself.

She put the file back in the drawer. She did not put the pink slip back in the file.

* * *

That evening she stopped at the chandler's on Marrow Street and bought, of all things, a taper, and felt absurd carrying it through the fog like a woman out of a century with better excuses for fire.

The workshop door stood open. That in itself was a message; Beatrix never left it open. Inside, the room was warm from the small stove, and Beatrix sat at the bench with the observatory's broken remontoire spread before them on chamois leather, each piece laid out in order like an anatomy lesson. They looked up when she entered, and their face did that thing it did — the quick closing, the shutter drawn halfway down — before it recognized her and opened again, halfway, which was as far as it ever went in daylight.

"You're late," Beatrix said. "For someone who isn't officially here at all."

"I need to tell you something." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "And I need you to let me finish it."

They set down their tweezers. That was how she knew they understood something was wrong; Beatrix Whitlock interrupted everyone, always, delighting in it, but now they only turned on the stool to face her, hands quiet in their lap, and waited.

Freya told it plainly, because she had rehearsed plainness on the walk over and plainness was all she could manage. The memorandum. Regulation 11. The pink slip, produced from her coat, unfolded, laid on the bench beside the dismantled clock so that its pinkness sat obscenely among the brass. *Scheduled for seizure. Upon completion of the repairs. They're using your own hands to build the date of your eviction.*

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls of the old workshop a pipe knocked twice, like a cautious visitor.

"They want me to write the report," Freya said. "Documenting your noncompliance. It's due Friday. And I started it, Beatrix, I did start it, because I am a coward in an incremental way, I do my cowardice in installments—" Her breath caught. "I wrote half a page of the truth, which is the most dangerous thing you can give them, half a page of the truth."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to burn it." She took out the folded draft. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; terror, she discovered, lived somewhere below the hands. "But burning it only buys time. They'll send someone else. There will be another form, another inspector, another column with no row for you. I can't fix this with an eraser, Beatrix. I've been trying all afternoon to find a way to fix it with an eraser, and there isn't one."

Beatrix rose from the stool. They crossed the small room slowly, the way they moved when they were frightened — carefully, as though the floor were a mechanism that might be triggered — and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Freya could see the oil under their fingernails and the exhaustion worn smooth into their face.

"Why," Beatrix said quietly. "Why burn your career for a person you've known three weeks? You don't owe me a rebellion. I never asked anyone to—"

"No," Freya said. "You didn't ask. That's rather the point."

The word was there, had been there for days, sitting in her chest like a weight waiting for its hour. She had supposed she would choose the moment, dress it properly, deliver it with some grace. Instead it simply struck, unbidden, the way a released clock strikes — all at once because everything before it had been preparation.

"Because I am in love with you," she said. "Not with the person the census can't find. With you, specifically, entirely, in whatever category or none, and if the state requires me to choose between my forms and your safety, then the forms can go to hell, and I will say so on the record if it comes to that. That's why."

The taper fell from her coat pocket onto the floorboards between them, absurd, forgotten. Neither picked it up.

Beatrix made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not anything else, and pressed the heel of one hand briefly against their eyes. When they lowered it, they were not hiding anymore. That was the thing Freya would remember afterward, in all the years after: not any declaration returned in kind, but the shutter going up instead of down, the whole face opening at once like a case opened to show the works.

"I've spent nine years being weather," Beatrix said. Their voice cracked on a gear-tooth of a laugh. "And you're telling me you'd stand in the middle of the square and be *seen*."

"With you. Yes."

"Then we can't just hide the report." They turned to the bench, to the pink slip, to the ordered anatomy of the great clock spread on its leather. Something had changed in their posture — the fear was still there, Freya could see it, but it had been harnessed to something, made to turn a wheel. "If they're going to seize the workshop *upon completion*, then completion becomes our lever. A finished instrument has a different legal character than a workshop — instruments of civic timekeeping are protected, there are statutes older than the census, there are precedents—"

"Beatrix." Freya heard her own voice go very soft. "What are you building?"

They looked at her, and for the first time since she had known them, Beatrix Whitlock smiled without checking the door first.

"A defense," they said. "Help me set the last wheels. We have until dawn."

Outside, the fog was thickening over Clock Lane, and somewhere above it the observatory dome turned blindly against the dark, keeping its own counsel, waiting for the hour it had not struck in months.
