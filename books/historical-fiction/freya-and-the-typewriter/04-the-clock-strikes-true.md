## 4. The Clock Strikes True

Dawn came up the tower stairs before the inspector did — a grey seeping of light through the eastern slit windows, catching the brass of the great escapement and turning it to fire. Beatrix had been awake all night. Their hands ached in the way that meant good work, the deep ache of having held something precious and not let it fall.

The final wheel sat on the cloth beside them, no larger than a saucer, its teeth cut over three sleepless days from a blank of hard bronze. It was the heart-wheel, the one that governed the striking train, and for eleven years it had carried a crack no one else had ever thought to look for. Eleven years the clock had faltered at the hour, hesitating as if gathering courage, then striking wrong — four when it should be five, seven when it should be eight — until the town stopped trusting noon itself.

Beatrix lifted the wheel with both hands, breath held, and set it onto its arbor.

It seated with a sound so small it might have been nothing at all: a whisper of metal finding the place it had always been meant to occupy. Then the click of the pawl, the tension of the spring, and the whole vast mechanism above them shivered once, like a sleeper drawing breath, and settled into motion.

*True*, Beatrix thought. *It runs true.*

They did not say it aloud. They had learned long ago never to speak a thing before it was finished. But their chest felt too large for their ribs, and their eyes stung, and they turned to find Freya watching them from the doorway with her coat already buttoned and her case in hand.

"You didn't sleep," Beatrix said.

"Neither did you." Freya crossed the clock room, her heels loud on stone worn smooth by a century of caretakers' feet. She looked tired and terrified and utterly resolved, and Beatrix loved her so fiercely in that moment that it frightened them more than anything waiting down the stairs. "He's coming at seven. Herr Kessler sent word last night. He wants to catch the workshop 'in a state of disorder.'"

"It is in a state of order," Beatrix said, looking around at the tools laid out in their precise rows, the parts cleaned and catalogued, the great clock ticking overhead like something alive. "It's the most orderly thing in this town."

"That's what I told him." Freya set her case down on the workbench and opened it. Inside lay a folder of heavy cream paper, sealed with wax, stamped with an emblem Beatrix knew: the Ministry of Records, the same ministry that had sent men to inventory their mother's shop after she died, that had filed their existence under *irregular* for eleven years. "Beatrix. Look at me."

They looked.

"Whatever happens in the next hour, I need you to let me do the talking. Can you do that?"

"What have you done?" Their voice came out flat. They had spent a lifetime reading people by the small betrayals of their faces, and Freya's face was betraying everything now — the fear pushed down beneath purpose, the terrible calm of someone who has already jumped and is simply waiting for the ground. "Freya. What have you done?"

She closed the case. "The best lie I've ever told."

* * *

Inspector Halloran arrived at seven o'clock precisely, which Beatrix supposed they should have expected from a man whose whole life was the enforcement of other people's precision. He climbed the tower stairs badly, breathing hard, his grey suit dusted with the observatory's eternal falling plaster, and behind him came Kessler, wringing his soft hands, and two men from the municipal office carrying a leather portfolio that could hold only one kind of document.

"The workshop of the late E. Whitlock," Halloran said, consulting a card though Beatrix was certain he knew it by heart. "Occupant: irregular. Status: unregistered since 1931. Repair contract: unauthorized." He looked up, and his eyes were the colour of ledger lines. "Miss Ashworth. You're here early."

"I'm here officially," Freya said.

She stepped forward into the narrow space between the inspector and the clock, and Beatrix watched her become the other Freya — the census taker, the woman of forms and seals, spine straight as a plumb line. Only their eyes met hers, and only for a heartbeat, and in that heartbeat passed everything neither of them could say in front of these men.

"By what authority?"

"By this one." She drew the cream folder from her case and opened it on the workbench, turning it so the seal faced him. "Civic Instrument Designation 44-C. Issued under the Preservation of Public Works Act, 1929, section twelve." Her voice did not shake. Beatrix marveled at that, would marvel at it for years afterward. "The master clock of this observatory has been designated an instrument of civic record. All timekeeping in this municipality — births, deaths, court sessions, rail schedules — is legally referenced to its strike. Its maintenance is therefore a protected function, and its custodian—" here her voice did pause, one full second, the length of a pendulum swing— "is a protected appointment."

Halloran read the page. He read it twice. Beatrix stood very still among their tools and counted his breaths, and hated how well they could read him: the suspicion, the arithmetic running behind his eyes, the slow discovery that every line of the document was authentic — because it was; Freya had used real forms, real seals, obtained through channels Beatrix did not dare imagine — while the substance of it was invention from top to bottom.

"There's no custodian appointed here," Halloran said slowly. "There's no name on this line."

"The name is pending certification," Freya said. "Which requires my supervisor's signature. Which he will decline to give, because I am resigning my post as of this morning, and he'll want nothing further to do with any of it." She laid a second paper beside the first: a letter of resignation, signed in her careful upright hand. "The designation stands or falls on its own merits, Inspector. The clock is civic property. The repair is complete. The custodian question will resolve itself in thirty days, or it won't, and either way it isn't yours."

The room went very quiet except for the clock. The clock went on ticking, steady as rainfall.

Kessler made a small strangled sound. One of the municipal men leaned toward Halloran and murmured something Beatrix couldn't hear. And Beatrix understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, what it had cost her: the career, the standing, the tidy life built inside the ministry's categories — she had traded all of it, forged herself into the paperwork the way Beatrix had forged bronze, to buy one person the right to keep existing outside of everyone's columns.

"You understand," Halloran said at last, and his voice had lost its edge, gone thin and uncertain, "that this ends your advancement. Permanently. The ministry doesn't forgive—"

"I know exactly what the ministry does," Freya said. "I've worked there six years."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then at Beatrix — a different look, slower, the look of a man seeing a person where he had only ever seen a discrepancy — and then at the clock, huge and golden and turning above them all, striking-train armed, escapement swinging, ready for the hour.

"Well," said Inspector Halloran, closing the portfolio. "Then there's nothing further this morning. The seizure order is void against a protected instrument. Obviously." He straightened his cuffs. "Good morning."

* * *

They walked down together past him, Beatrix with the toolbox in one hand and Freya's cold fingers laced tight in the other, and they were halfway down the tower stair when the hour arrived.

Eight o'clock. The strike train caught, the great hammers rose, and the bell above them spoke — deep and round and perfect, one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, *eight* — the sound pouring down the stairwell and out through the open door below, rolling across the waking town, into kitchens and offices and the railway station where a clerk would glance up and, for the first time in eleven years, trust what he heard.

Freya stopped on the stairs. In the wash of the sound she laughed, and then she wasn't laughing anymore, and she pressed her forehead to Beatrix's shoulder and stayed there while the eighth stroke faded into the ordinary morning.

"It's true," she said. "You made it true."

"We made it true." Beatrix heard their own voice break the rule of a lifetime — speaking a thing before it was finished, because this, at last, was finished. "Freya. Where do we go now?"

She lifted her head. Below them the tower door stood open on the square, on light, on a town that had never once made room for them and no longer mattered half so much as it had yesterday.

"Nowhere," she said, smiling, pulling them down into the morning. "Anywhere. We've got thirty days before anyone thinks to ask."
