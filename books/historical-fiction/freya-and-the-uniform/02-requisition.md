## 2. Requisition

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, when the light through the workshop's east windows was still thin and grey as fish scales, and Coral Dravenmoor had ink on three fingers and a steady hand poised over the last of Master Vey's coastlines.

She did not answer at first. In two years of finishing the atlas she had trained herself against interruption — against the fishmonger's boy who thought every door in Vellmark was an invitation to haggle, against the widow upstairs who believed a young woman living alone above a mapshop was a public concern rather than a private one. The knock came again, harder, and with it voices on the stair, and Coral understood that this was neither fishmonger nor widow.

She capped the ink. It was a small act, done without thinking, but she would remember it afterward: the little glass stopper pressed down into the well, sealing the black liquid that had been part of her life's blood for two years, as though the gesture could seal anything at all against what was coming up her stairs.

There were four of them when she opened the door. Two soldiers in bureau grey with carbines slung across their backs, looking embarrassed by their own presence in a room that smelled of linseed oil and paper; a clerk with a leather document case chained to his wrist; and behind them, slightly apart, wearing the same grey but wearing it badly, Lieutenant Casper Ilyen.

"Coral," he said.

Of all of them, only he took off his cap.

* * *

She had known Casper Ilyen since she was sixteen, since the year Master Vey had taken her on as an apprentice and the war bureau — still called the survey office then, in those innocent last seasons before the century's troubles began taking its measurements — had sent a junior cartographic officer to consult on coastal soundings. He had been younger than his commission, narrow-shouldered and earnest, with ink stains on his cuffs that matched her own and a habit of apologizing before he asked a question. They had spent one whole autumn arguing about the depiction of tidal flats, politely and then impolitely and then not at all, because they had discovered they agreed. He had eaten supper at this table a hundred times. He knew where Vey kept the good tea.

He stood now in the doorway of the workshop he had once helped her sweep, and he would not quite look at the plates laid out on the long table like specimens under glass.

"Lieutenant Ilyen," she said, because it seemed important, suddenly, that they both hear the title spoken aloud.

"Coral." His voice was low, pitched under the clerk's attention. "I'm sorry. There's an order."

"An order."

"For the atlas. All of it. The plates, the field notes, the working drawings, the proofs if any exist. Everything in Aldous Vey's hand or derived from it." He said it quickly, the way one pulls a splinter. "It's been designated material of strategic value. For the duration."

*For the duration.* The phrase sat in the room like weather. Everyone in Vellmark had learned what duration meant; it meant the war, which meant no one could say.

"May I see this order?" Coral said, and was proud that her voice did not shake, though her hands, hidden in the folds of her apron, had closed into fists around the fabric.

The clerk unchained his case with the ceremony of a man who enjoyed ceremony. The order was written on heavy paper with a foil stamp, signed by a Colonel Brase of the Bureau of Military Geography, and it named Aldous Vey — dead two years, drowned off the Skearing Light with his body never recovered — as custodian of materials requisitioned henceforth to the Crown. It named no apprentice. It did not need to. An apprentice, Coral understood reading it twice through, was furniture.

"This is Master Vey's life work," she said. "Twenty-two years of surveys. He died for it. Literally — the *Merrow* went down running the last soundings of the northern coast, and every plate in this room is built from those notebooks. You're taking his grave."

"Madam," said the clerk, not unkindly, in the tone of a man reciting, "the bureau is aware of the atlas's significance. That is precisely why it is being requisitioned. A work of this accuracy cannot be left in private hands while the nation is at war."

"In whose hands does it lie now?"

He blinked at her. The question had no place in his formulary. Behind him, one of the soldiers shifted his weight, and Casper looked at the floor.

"You'll be issued a receipt," the clerk said. "An inventory will be conducted. If you'll stand aside."

* * *

They inventoried for four hours, and Coral stayed because leaving would have been a kind of consent.

She watched strangers' hands on everything she loved. She watched them lift the copper plates by the edges, clumsily, fingers too near the engraved lines, and she corrected their grip twice before she made herself stop, because every correction was cooperation and she could not yet decide what cooperation meant here. She watched them bundle the finished drawings of the Kelvern Pass sheet into a canvas portfolio, the mountain passes she had inked stroke by stroke through the whole of last winter, Vey's pencil ghosts still visible beneath her hand where he'd planned the hachures. She watched them find the locked drawer where the proofs lived — she gave up the key when asked directly, because refusal was theft now, apparently, and theft from the Crown was a word with cells attached — and she watched the soldier count them and write *fourteen (14) proof impressions, various sheets* in the inventory book, and something in her chest went very quiet.

Fourteen. Yes. Fourteen proofs pulled before the plates were confiscated — pulled by her own hand, on her own press, in the weeks after Vey died, because some instinct older than reasoning had told her that a masterwork should exist in more than one place, that paper survives by scattering the way seed survives. Fourteen proofs, listed, receipted, official. And beneath the false bottom of the plan chest, unlisted because never counted, fourteen more.

She had not known why she'd pulled doubles. She told herself it was archival habit. She stood in her own workshop watching the state fold her life's work into canvas bags and understood, with a coldness that surprised her, that some part of her had seen this coming the way animals feel weather.

Casper saw her face, whatever it did in that moment. She saw him see it, and saw him decide not to have seen it, and that small mutual fiction — performed between them in less than a second, over the heads of soldiers — was the first thing they ever kept from each other, and it would not be the last.

* * *

By noon the workshop looked robbed even though nothing was missing that a thief would want. The long table lay bare. The plan chest drawers hung open like mouths. The wall where the Kelvern Pass proof had hung for a month — she had pinned it up to admire the hachuring, the finest work she'd ever done, Vey's design and her execution — showed a pale rectangle where the light hadn't reached the plaster.

The soldiers carried the loads out to a requisitioned dray. The clerk followed with his chained case. And Casper lingered at the bottom of the stairs with his cap in his hands, and Coral, unable to help herself, came down to him.

"You could have warned me," she said.

"I got the order last night at nine." His jaw worked. "I argued it. I'm not permitted to tell you I argued it, so please forget that I've told you."

"Forget what?"

Something almost like a smile crossed his face and died there. He was thinner than he'd been a year ago; the uniform had taken the softness off him, or the war had, and there were new lines at his eyes that belonged to a much older man. "Coral. This isn't targeted at Vey. It's everything. Every private survey office in Vellmark has had visits this week. Harlond & Prue. The old Fenwick charts. The hydrographic society woke up Tuesday to men in their cellar." He said it fast and flat, information offered in lieu of comfort, which was how Casper had always loved: by being useful. "It's the duration. When there's peace again—"

"When there's peace again, the plates will be worn to ghosts in military presses, printing maps with someone else's corrections on them." She heard her own voice rise and forced it down. The widow's window across the yard faced this stair; the whole city was learning to listen. "Casper. Who ordered the northern sheets pulled first? Because they took Kelvern Pass first, before the coastlines, before anything, and an inventory goes in the order things are listed, not the order things are wanted."

He looked at her for a moment too long. She knew that look. It was the look he wore when a sounding didn't match the chart — when the water was deeper than the paper said it should be.

"The priority schedule comes from the Colonel's office," he said. "Not mine."

"Then your Colonel wants the mountains before the sea."

"Everyone wants the mountains," Casper said. "There's a war in the mountains."

"Yes," said Coral. "Isn't it convenient, then, that we had the best maps of them."

The words were out before she could weigh them, and she watched them land on him like dropped tools. He glanced up the stairs, past her, at the emptied workshop, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped until she had to lean in to catch it, close enough to smell the wool of his uniform, the government soap, the ghost underneath both of the linseed oil they'd both been steeped in since girlhood and boyhood.

"Coral. Whatever you're thinking — don't think it where walls can hear. Sign your receipt. Keep your head. Wait out the duration." A pause, barely a breath. "And if anyone from the bureau comes asking what proofs survived, what copies exist, anything at all — send for me before you answer. Promise me that."

"Why?"

"Because—" He stopped. Rebuilt the sentence from safer parts. "Because I know the forms. Because I'd rather you had me beside you when you give answers than answering alone." Which was true and not all of the truth, and she could hear the seam where the truth had been trimmed.

She promised. She would have promised him almost anything on that stair, in the last hour of the world as she had known it, and hated herself a little for knowing it.

He put his cap back on and became a lieutenant again in the space of a motion, spine straightening, face closing. At the gate he paused, one hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"They're burning nothing yet," he said, to the street in general. "Whatever people say. Nothing's burned yet."

Then he was gone, and the dray with him, and Coral Dravenmoor climbed the stairs to her master's emptied workshop and stood in the pale rectangle on the wall where Kelvern Pass used to be.

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She lit the lamp she usually saved for fine engraving work and sat at the bare table with her copy of the receipt, reading it the way she read a suspect chart — line against line, looking for the discrepancy, the place where the recorded world and the observed world refused to agree.

*One (1) atlas, incomplete, 47 sheets of 52.* True; five sheets unfinished at Vey's death, finished by her since — but the finished versions existed only as drawings, and the drawings had been taken, and the inventory said forty-seven, and the inventory had been signed by a corporal who could not tell a hachure from a hairline.

*Proofs, fourteen (14).* Also true. Also false. Also the only lie she owned outright, folded away under the false bottom of a plan chest that smelled of her master's pipe.

She thought about the order of the loading. Mountains first. She thought about Casper's voice saying *everyone wants the mountains,* and the pause before it, the half-breath of a man standing at the edge of something he could not say. She thought about the war itself, which everyone in Vellmark was required to think of as having begun with the border incident at Kelvern Pass in the spring of '96 — the skirmish, the ultimatum, the marching — begun, everyone said, because the other side's maps were wrong, because they believed the pass ran where it did not run, because error, honest error, had marched two nations into each other's guns.

Everyone said the other side's maps were wrong.

Nobody, in two years, had asked whether our side's were right.

Coral sat very still in the lamplight, in the silence of a workshop stripped of its voice, and felt the thought complete itself slowly, the way a coastline completes itself under a pen — one small decision at a time, irreversible, changing everything downstream.

Fourteen proofs slept under the false bottom of the drawer. Somewhere across the city, in a bureau cellar, forty-seven sheets and twenty-two years of a dead man's devotion lay in canvas bags, already perhaps stamped with property marks, already perhaps feeding a printing press that added corrections of its own.

She snuffed the lamp and sat in the dark and listened to the tide come up the harbor, and it was three nights later — three nights of that thought turning over like a plate on a press, printing itself deeper each pass — that the knocking came again at her door, near midnight, and a stranger fell across the threshold holding papers written in a dead man's hand.
