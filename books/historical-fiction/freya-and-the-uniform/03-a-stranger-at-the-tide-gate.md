## 3. A Stranger at the Tide Gate

The tide gate bell rang at half past four, as it always did, though the sound had grown strange to Coral since the requisition. For two years she had measured her days by bells — the harbor bell, the workshop's own cracked clock, the cathedral's noon peal that meant Master Vey would want his tea — and all of them had run the same way before the war bureau came with its sealed orders and its polite, immovable men. Now every bell sounded like a summons.

She was working on proofs when the knocking began. Not the confident knock of a client, nor the double-rap of the bureau's couriers, who announced themselves like minor officials even at a tradesman's door. This was a fist, dragged down the wood twice, then silence, then a single feeble tap — the knock of someone who had spent their last strength getting to the door and had none left for what came after.

Coral set down her burnisher and listened. The wind off the strait had turned in the night, dragging sleet against the eastern windows in long sideways sheets, and the workshop seemed to lean into it, the old beams creaking like ship's rigging. She told herself it was a drunkard from the Anchor Step, blown off his route home. She told herself it was nothing.

The knock came again, fainter.

She crossed the workroom past the empty shelf where the atlas plates had lived — the shelf still bore the pale rectangles of unfaded wood where they had stood, ghosts of paper pressed into the grain — and lifted the bar.

He fell inward more than walked. He was a narrow man, thirty perhaps, dressed in a fisherman's oilskin gone stiff with salt and age, and he was wet in the way that meant not rain but immersion — the sea itself, or the slush of the frozen canal, soaked through to the skin. His lips were the color of slate. One hand was pressed to his chest, and only when he pitched forward and she caught his arm did she feel the stiffness of papers beneath the oilskin, bound flat against him like a second rib cage.

"Please," he said. His voice was ruined, scraped down to gravel. "Master Vey's shop. You're — the apprentice. The maps in the window." His eyes, fever-bright, went past her shoulder to the framed coastal survey Coral kept in the glass case for passersby. "I found it. I followed the coast line. I knew the hand."

"Who are you?" She held his weight without meaning to; he was lighter than he looked, the lightness of a body consuming itself. "How did you find this place?"

"Rell," he said. "My name is Rell. Please. Before they come. I was seen at the tide gate."

That word — *they* — moved her before her caution could. She got the door barred behind him and half-carried, half-walked him through the workroom to the old drying room at the back, where Vey had hung his washes in summer and where the low bench still stood beneath the vent. She stripped the oilskin from him — it came away crackling, frozen into a shell of itself — and wrapped him in the felt blankets from the map press, the ones they used to keep humidity off the vellum, and forced watered wine between his cracked lips until he stopped shivering in the long, ugly waves that meant the body was losing its argument with the cold.

Only when he lay breathing — shallowly, but breathing — did she turn to the papers.

* * *

She should have called for help. That was the thought that would return to her later, in darker hours: *I should have called the watch, or the surgeon, or anyone.* Instead she knelt by the bench and unwrapped the oilcloth packet he carried against his chest, and the first thing she saw was the handwriting, and after that she did not think clearly again for a long time.

It was Vey's hand. There was no mistaking it, no possibility of coincidence or forgery so crude as this. She had spent six years inside that hand. She had watched it move across field books and fair copies, had learned its tempers — the small, impatient crossbar he gave his *t*s when he was tired, the way his *8*s opened slightly at the top like doors left ajar, the peculiar flat-topped *V* of his initials that he joked he'd inherited from his father and would pass to no one because no one should write so stubbornly. A.V., flat-topped and unrepentant, at the foot of the dispatch.

And beneath the initials, a date.

Coral read it three times. Her eyes kept sliding off it the way eyes slide off an error in a chart, refusing to fix upon what cannot be there. The fourteenth of September, 1899.

Master Aldous Vey had died on the ninth of January, 1897.

Eight months. The hand was dated eight months after the grave in the Northchurch yard, eight months after Coral had stood in freezing rain beside Tam Vey and watched the coffin go down, eight months after she had sworn — though no one asked it of her — to finish the atlas he died halfway through. Someone had written orders in a dead man's hand and dated them in the living world, in autumn, in a season Coral had herself lived through, inking proofs by lamplight while the dead man's pen supposedly traveled.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them as if they belonged to someone else, a cartographer's hands, trained to be steady over a ruling pen, trembling now so hard the paper rattled.

The dispatch itself was brief — a courier's routing, waypoints along the coast, instructions written in the clipped shorthand of military post but bearing none of the bureau's stamps. It was the margins that stopped her heart. Around the formal text, in the same unmistakable hand, ran annotations in a script that was almost, but not quite, Vey's ordinary writing: compressed, angular, each letter reduced to its minimum strokes.

Coral knew that script. She had copied it out as a girl of seventeen, at this very bench, while Vey dictated: *"This is how you write when the writing must survive being read by the wrong eyes."* A cipher of his own devising — not a code of invented symbols that would announce itself as a cipher, but a compression of ordinary letters, legible only to someone who had been taught the reductions. He had taught her in a single afternoon, laughing, saying no one else alive needed to know it, and she had never once used it since. She had believed it a game. An old man's whimsy, a puzzle for his favorite apprentice.

The marginal script wound around the dispatch's edges like ivy around a fence post, and her eye, trained for six years, began unpicking it before she had decided to try.

*The Kelvern survey is false. They know I know. If I am taken, the proof is where we made the summer maps. Tell C. the key is split.*

*Tell C.*

She read her own initial in a dead man's hand, and the room tilted.

* * *

The patrol came at dusk.

She heard them before she saw them: boots on the frozen cobbles of Tanner's Wynd, the particular rhythm of men walking in step who have been told to walk in step. Coral had banked the fire down to embers and lit only the single lamp over her proof table, so that from the street the workshop showed one modest square of light, the light of a woman working late on honest commissions — harbor charts, insurance surveys, the dull paid work that had filled the shelf where the atlas used to live.

She had put Rell in the drying room with the vent shutter closed and a warning whispered at the door like a prayer: *not a sound, not a breath, not if the house falls down around you.* Then she had sat back down at her proofs and made herself ink a compass rose, because a woman interrupted at her work looks different from a woman waiting for something, and the rose needed doing anyway, and because her hands would shake less holding a pen than folded in her lap.

The knock, when it came, was the double-rap of the bureau.

She let them in. Two men and a corporal carrying a shuttered lantern, their uniforms beaded with sleet, stamping the cold from their boots on her floor without apology. The taller one she recognized distantly from the requisition day — a Sergeant Moul, she thought, or something like Moul — with the flat, patient face of a man who had searched many rooms and found the searching itself sufficient.

"Routine," he said, in the tone that meant nothing about it was routine. "Reports of a landing on the east shore last night. A man off one of the blockade runners, maybe. You'd have heard nothing?"

"I heard the tide gate bell," Coral said. "I always hear the tide gate bell. And the wind. The wind has been remarkable."

Moul looked at her a moment too long, the way a man reads a margin for annotations. Then his gaze went past her, moving over the workroom with professional slowness: the proof tables, the press, the glass case, the dark stair to the loft, the door of the drying room standing — she saw it with a lurch of pure animal fear — standing open the width of two fingers, exactly as she had pulled it shut in her hurry, exactly not shut.

"The wind," Moul agreed. "Remarkable." He drifted toward the shelves, trailing a gloved finger along the spines of Vey's old folios. "They say the master here was the finest hand in Vellmark. Shame, what became of the atlas. The colonel speaks well of your work, miss. Keeping it in the family, so to speak. Bureau's business now, but your name will be on the finished thing. Credit where due."

"The atlas was never mine," Coral said. She heard her own voice perform steadiness and marveled at it from somewhere outside herself. "Only the ink stains."

Moul smiled without warmth and drifted, and drifted, and stopped before the drying-room door. He stood there longer than a man stands before an uninteresting door. Through the wall, Coral could hear the stove ticking, her own pulse ticking louder, and beneath both — or did she imagine it? God, let her imagine it — a single suppressed cough, small as a mouse behind the wainscoting.

"That room?" Moul said.

"Drying room. Wash racks and felt. It smells of size and camphor; you're welcome to confirm it." She said it lightly, and stepped — the boldest thing she had done in her life, done before she knew she was doing it — between him and the door, lifting the lamp as if to lead him elsewhere. "The loft's colder but drier, if you're checking for a sleeper. Though whoever crossed the strait last night chose his weather better than to sleep in my loft. He'll be ten miles inland by now, if he knows the coast."

Something in the phrase *if he knows the coast* caught Moul's ear; she watched it catch, watched him weigh whether a mapmaker's apprentice ought to speak so knowledgeably about blockade runners and routes inland. But the lamp was up, her face was arranged, and the arithmetic of a routine search on a filthy night — two fingers' width of open door against a calm young woman quoting tides — came out, barely, in her favor.

"Lock this behind us," Moul said at last, turning back toward the street door. "Strange weather brings strange traffic. If anything knocks after dark, miss, you send for the posthouse and you don't open it yourself. Orders."

"I understand orders," Coral said, and hated how true it sounded.

She barred the door behind them and stood with her forehead against the wood until the boots faded into the wind. Then she crossed the workroom on legs that did not feel attached to her and opened the drying-room door, and Rell's eyes met hers from the dark under the felt blankets — feverish, watchful, and full of something that looked terribly like gratitude, which was worse than suspicion, because gratitude expected something in return.

"You didn't give me up," he rasped.

"No," Coral said. "I didn't."

Why hadn't she? The question sat in her chest like swallowed stone. Fear would have given him up — fear argued for it even now, coolly, reasonably: a stranger, a spy or smuggler or worse, hunted by men with sealed orders, hiding in her rooms with evidence of forgery in a dead man's hand. Self-preservation would have given him up. Love of Casper — and the thought of Casper arrived unbidden, Casper in his bureau gray, uncomfortable at the requisition, dutiful always — love of Casper should have given him up, and did not.

What had stayed her hand, she understood, kneeling there in the camphor-smelling dark, was the same thing that kept her now from summoning the surgeon, the watch, anyone: the date. The impossible date, and beneath it, in the dead man's compressed and secret hand, two words addressed to her across eight months of earth.

*Tell C.*

She was C. There was no other C. In all the wide and warring world, whatever game the bureau was playing with dead men's pens, the message ended at this bench, this lamplight, this apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a promise already two years old.

"Master Vey," she said slowly, watching Rell's face the way she watched a coastline for the moment the bearings lie. "You knew him."

Rell was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere beyond the walls the sleet had thickened to true snow, falling on Vellmark like ash, like paper, like pages torn from a book no one was permitted to read.

"I knew him," Rell said at last, and his voice had changed — steadier now, or pretending hard to be. "Miss Thornbury, I knew him better than the grave lets on. And before you decide what to do with me — before you send for anyone, or burn those papers, or do the sensible thing, which is any of it — you should ask yourself one question." He pulled himself upright against the bench, and his fever-bright eyes fixed on hers. "Ask yourself why a man nine months buried signed orders dated in September. Ask what kind of lie needs a dead man's hand to sign it — and which side of the war is telling it."

Coral looked down at the dispatch in her hands, at the flat-topped *A.V.* at its foot, at the ivy-script winding round the margins, already half-unpicked, already whispering.

She did not ask. Not yet. She banked the stove higher instead, and locked the street door, and sat down across from a stranger with a dead man's handwriting, and began, line by line, to copy out the cipher — because whatever the truth was, it was written in a language only two living people could read, and she intended, with an obsession that frightened her more than the patrol had, to read every word.
