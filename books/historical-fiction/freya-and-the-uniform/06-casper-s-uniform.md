## 6. Casper's Uniform

The requisition crates smelled of salt and old paper, and Casper Ilyen hated every one of them.

He had signed for them himself — that was the worst of it. His own signature, in his own careful officer's hand, on the manifest that declared Master Aldous Vey's life's work *property of the War Bureau, held for the duration*. Eleven crates of copper plates, four of proofs, two of field notebooks, and one long crate of instruments that Coral Dravenmoor had polished every evening of the two years he'd known her. He had signed, and a corporal had stenciled numbers on the lids, and now the whole of it sat in the archive cellar of the bureau's Vellmark annex like a body awaiting an inquest.

Colonel Brase had ordered the audit for good reason. Requisitioned cartographic material was to be catalogued within fourteen days, assessed for military value, duplicates culled, sensitive items segregated. It was the kind of work Casper was actually good at — patient, exacting, immune to boredom. He had expected three quiet days among plates and proofs.

He had not expected to spend them listening for what was missing.

* * *

The cellar lamp threw his shadow long across the sorting table. He worked methodically, as the regulations demanded: each plate examined under glass, each proof checked against its plate, each item logged with its condition and apparent completeness. The atlas itself — the grand survey of the northern coasts and passes that Vey had spent nineteen years assembling — was a marvel even in this raw state. Casper had known it would be. He had heard Vey lecture once, years ago, at the mercantile hall, a small stooped man with ink-stained cuffs who spoke about coastlines the way other men spoke about scripture: *a map is a promise, gentlemen, and a false map is a promise made to be broken.* The room had laughed politely. Vey had not.

Casper laid out the Kelvern Pass section first, because Brase had marked it with a red ribbon in his instructions — *priority assessment, border definition, report directly.* Three plates: the pass itself, the approaches from the south, the watershed detail. He set them side by side and bent close.

They were beautiful. That was the trouble with Vey's work; you could not look at it without believing it. The hachures shading the slopes fell in perfect logic, the streams branched like veins, the boundary line ran along the crest of the divide with the confidence of something drawn by God and merely copied by men. No wonder the general staff had wanted it. A soldier reading this plate would trust every line of it with his life.

Casper checked each plate against its proofs, because that was procedure, and because procedure was the only thing holding his hands steady this morning.

Two proofs were absent. Not damaged, not misfiled — absent. The register pasted inside the crate lid listed proof impressions pulled for every plate; the numbers were in Vey's hand, tidy as arithmetic. Plate forty-one, the watershed detail: six proofs pulled, dated March 1896. In the crate: four. Plate thirty-eight, the southern approaches: five proofs pulled. In the crate: three.

Five missing proofs, all told, scattered across the border sections and nowhere else. The coastal plates were complete to the last impression. Only the border work had gaps, neat and deliberate, like teeth drawn from a jaw.

Apprentices misplace things. That was the charitable explanation, and Casper reached for it the way a drowning man reaches for driftwood. Proof sheets got used as scrap, as wrapping, as kindling. Workshops burned paper freely; it was cheaper than storing it. There was no reason to read anything into it.

Except that these were not scrap-grade proofs. These were border sections, the very sections the war bureau had seized the workshop to obtain, and any apprentice with two years' sense — and Coral Dravenmoor had ten times that — would have known exactly which papers mattered.

He thought about her face when they came. He had been unable to stop thinking about it since.

* * *

The day of the requisition, he had stood in the workshop doorway with the sealed orders in his glove and watched her the way you watch weather turn.

He had known the room before he knew her — or rather, he had known her through the room, because in those first months after his transfer to Vellmark, garrison-bored and friendless, he had taken to walking past the tide-gate lane just to see the lamplight in the workshop window and the silhouette bent over the drafting table. A girl making maps. It had seemed impossibly romantic to his twenty-three-year-old self, and then he had contrived to meet her — some pretext about a chart for his commanding officer — and discovered that the reality was better than the romance. She argued. She laughed at him without cruelty. She could name every headland between Vellmark and the Skerries and drew them, when she talked, in little absent gestures on tabletops, on frost, once on the back of his hand with a fingertip, a coastline he had not washed for the rest of the day.

Her master had tolerated him the way an old cat tolerates weather. *Your bureau draws maps too,* Vey had said to him once, over tea he hadn't drunk. *Do you ever wonder whose promises yours are keeping?*

Casper had wondered, in the way young men wonder at things that do not yet have teeth. Now the teeth were in him and closing.

Because he had seen her face on requisition day. He had seen the moment her eyes found the missing space where the atlas had lived for two years — she had looked at the empty table first, not the officers, as if the absence itself were a wound — and then her gaze had come up and passed over him, Lieutenant Ilyen, her old friend, standing in uniform among men carrying her master's work out the door in crates. Her expression had done something very small and very final. Not surprise. Not pleading. Recognition, almost — as if she had known all along that the state would come one day, and had only been waiting to learn which of her friends would be wearing its colors.

She had said, "You'll want a receipt," and her voice had been level, and she had written out an inventory of the seized items herself, unprompted, in her beautiful drafting hand, and handed it to the sergeant rather than to him.

And Casper had taken it, later, and compared it against the crate registers, and that was how he knew.

Her inventory matched hers. The bureau's registers matched Vey's. And the arithmetic between them — five proofs, all border sections — matched nothing at all.

* * *

"You've been down here two days." Colonel Brase filled the cellar doorway the way weather fills a horizon. He was not a tall man but he had the gift of seeming to occupy more space than matter allowed, and his voice never needed raising; it simply assumed it would be attended to. "Report."

Casper straightened, closed the ledger, kept his thumb marking the page. "Cataloguing is complete, sir. Military value is high throughout. The coastal series alone is worth the requisition — soundings, tidal streams, anchorage details the Hydrographic Office doesn't hold. The general staff will want the whole atlas reproduced."

"Border sections."

"Also high value, sir." He opened the ledger and delivered the sentence he had spent half the night building. "Plate forty-one, Kelvern watershed — finest terrain representation I've seen outside the Survey's own archives. The staff will want it enlarged for operations."

Brase crossed the cellar and looked at the plates laid out, hands clasped behind him. He did not touch anything. He never touched anything if someone else could do the touching. "Proof counts reconcile?"

Here it was, then. The hinge. Casper felt the whole morning balance on it — his oath, his career, her face on requisition day, Vey's voice saying *whose promises yours are keeping.*

"Mostly, sir. Standard workshop attrition. Some proofs were pulled for reference and consumed — that's normal practice in a working studio. The apprentice's own inventory notes a handful of spoiled impressions discarded over the years. Nothing significant."

It was, he realized with a sensation like stepping off a ledge in the dark, the first lie he had ever told a superior officer. He waited for it to taste of something — ash, iron, triumph. It tasted of nothing at all, and that frightened him more than any flavor would have.

Brase's eyes came off the plates and settled on him. They were pale, unhurried eyes, and they did the Colonel's listening for him. "Nothing significant," Brase repeated.

"The missing impressions are proofs of plates we hold originals of, sir. Whatever the studio lost, the information didn't leave the city with it." Technically true. Delivered flat, with the small shrug of a technical man. He had not known he could do that either.

"Mm." Brase turned back to the plates. For a long moment the only sound was the harbor clock above them, muffled through stone. Then: "The apprentice. Dravenmoor. She gave up the material without protest?"

"She assisted the inventory personally, sir. Wrote it out herself."

"And her manner?"

Casper kept his face at parade rest. "Grieving, sir. It was her master's life work. But cooperative throughout."

"Grief makes people stupid or clever." Brase picked up his cap from the table edge, having decided, apparently, that the inspection was over. "Which sort is she?"

*Say ordinary,* Casper thought. *Say dull. Say whatever keeps the watch off her door.* "Thorough, sir. Thorough people are usually clever, but her thoroughness works for us. She wants the atlas finished and respected. We're the only ones who can guarantee that now."

Something shifted at the corner of Brase's mouth — not quite a smile, more the acknowledgment of a well-made argument, the way a quartermaster acknowledges a correctly tallyed crate. "See the duplicates are culled by Friday. And Ilyen —" he paused at the stair, and the pale eyes came back once more — "if anything else turns up missing from this collection, however insignificant, I want to hear it from you before I hear it from anyone else. That is not a request about paper. It is a request about you."

"Understood, sir."

His boots receded up the stairs. Casper stood in the lamplight with the ledger against his chest and discovered that his hands were shaking, very slightly, and that he was not sure whether it was fear or relief or something with neither name.

* * *

That evening he walked home the long way, along the harbor wall, because the long way passed the tide-gate lane, and because he had no honest reason to go there and wanted one badly enough to invent a dishonest one. He would ask after her welfare, he told himself. A courtesy call. Officers did that — maintained relations with the civilian trades the bureau depended on. It was practically regulation.

The workshop windows were lit. Through the frost-furred glass he could see her at the long table, proofs spread around her, her head bowed over them, one hand moving in that absent drawing gesture he knew so well — tracing a line only she could see. Tracing, he understood with a cold settling in his stomach, a border. Checking something. Comparing something. Keeping, in defiance of everything his signature had carried away that day, some part of what was hers.

He could have knocked. He could have gone in and said: Coral, whatever you're doing, stop. Give them back. Burn them. This is Brase, and Brase is not a man who loses interest, and I cannot protect you from what I am beginning to think you are already doing. He could have said: I lied for you today, and I don't know yet whether that makes me your friend or your accomplice, and I'm not certain there's a difference anymore.

He stood in the cold until his feet went numb, and he did not knock, because he understood — dimly, unwillingly — that the moment he spoke to her again as a friend, everything he was would have to choose a side, and he was not ready. His whole life he had been the dutiful one: second in his class, first in conduct, praised by instructors for reliability the way one praises a well-made lock. The uniform had always fit him the way good coats fit — snugly, warmly, without question.

Now, standing in the frost with lamplight on his face, he felt a seam give somewhere across the shoulders.

She looked up, suddenly, from the proofs — the way people look up when watched — and her eyes went to the window, and Casper stepped back into the shadow of the gatepost a heartbeat too late or perhaps exactly late enough, because she looked at the darkness where he stood for a long moment, and then, deliberately, rose and trimmed the lamp low, and returned to her table where the light would not reach her work from the street.

Not hiding, exactly. But not wanting to be read, either. And she had known — she had known instantly — that there was someone at the window worth trimming a lamp for.

Casper walked home with his collar up, rehearsing lies he might have to tell tomorrow and despising how fluent he had become, and told himself that covering for a grieving apprentice over a few spoiled proofs was loyalty, was friendship, was nothing.

But he counted the proofs again in his mind, all the way up the hill. Five. All border sections. All Kelvern.

And Kelvern Pass, he knew — every officer in Vellmark knew — was the border the war had been built on.

Above the harbor, the January stars were hard and clear, the kind of sky a surveyor loves and a soldier distrusts. Somewhere beyond it, across the frozen strait, the enemy's guns were quiet for the season. Casper let himself into his lodgings, hung the uniform on its hook with the care of a man handling evidence, and lay awake a long time, listening to the wind come off the ice, wondering which of the two — the man in the bed or the coat on the hook — was going to survive the winter.
