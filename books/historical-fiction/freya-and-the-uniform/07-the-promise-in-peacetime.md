## 7. The Promise in Peacetime

The lamp had burned low before Coral noticed the hour. She sat at the workbench with Rell's dispatch spread flat beneath a sheet of glass — the way Master Vey had taught her to hold a document under examination, so that no finger, no breath, no careless weight could add its own testimony to the ink. The handwriting looked up at her from the page like a drowned man seen through clear water: unmistakable, unreachable, impossible.

*Eight months after he died,* she thought, for the hundredth time that day. *The hand of a dead man, walking.*

She pressed her palms against her eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark, and the sparks became something else entirely — became lamplight on a summer window three years gone, and Tam Vey's voice saying her name.

* * *

In June of 1895 the strait had been only water, and the border at Kelvern Pass had been only a line drawn by men who trusted their own instruments, and Coral Dravenmoor had been nineteen and certain of nearly everything.

She remembered the season by its smells: pine resin softening in the heat above the workshop's south window, the tallow-and-linseed scent of the sizing pot, the sharp vinegar bite of iron-gall ink fresh from the batch she had mixed too strong and Master Vey had refused to let her throw away. *"Waste nothing, Coral. Ink remembers being oak galls. Paper remembers being rags worn by someone's grandmother. Everything you put your hand to has already lived a whole life before you get to it."*

That summer the atlas was eleven years old and still unfinished, an enormous patient animal that filled the flat cabinets along the north wall — forty-two plates projected, thirty-one engraved, nine proofed, none published. Master Vey had been working on it since before Coral was born. He spoke of it the way other men spoke of their children, which Coral had always found faintly absurd, because he had an actual child, and that child was currently sitting backwards on a surveyor's stool in the corner of the workshop, eating plums over an open folio and driving her quietly insane.

"You're going to drip on the base map," she said, not looking up from the coast plate she was burnishing.

"I'm going to drip on *your* coast plate," Tam said, "which is worse, I know." He was twenty-three that summer, newly commissioned as a junior surveyor with the Northern Trigonometrical Service, and he wore the fact the way other young men wore a first beard — constantly touching it to confirm it was still there. His field uniform hung on the coat peg by the door, grey-green wool that would have cost his father a month of engraving fees if Master Vey had ever been asked to pay for anything he could instead argue about. "It's a good coastline. You've got the Skerries almost right."

"*Almost* right?"

"Half a cable east. I sounded them myself in April."

"Your soundings were taken from a deck in a swell. Mine are reduced from three independent fixes." She turned the plate toward him, and the lamplight slid across the engraved water so that the skerries seemed to rise wet out of it, which they were supposed to do, because she had spent four days of her life learning exactly how much pressure a burin needed to make rock look like it was thinking about the tide. "*Look* at them."

He looked. He was good at looking; it was the one thing they had always shared without effort, the habit of attending to the world as though it might be examined later. Then he said, in the mild tone he used when he was about to be stubborn, "They've moved since April."

"Coastlines don't move."

"Skerries do. Frost-heave, storm-scour. My father will tell you the same."

"Your father," Coral said, "will tell me whatever keeps me at this bench until midnight."

As if summoned, Master Aldous Vey came up the stairs from the cellar printing room carrying two cups of the barley coffee he drank instead of tea and claimed tasted like honesty. He was fifty-eight that summer and had begun to go thin at the shoulders in a way that Coral had noticed and decided not to think about — the way she had also decided not to think about the cough, or the way he now read proofs with the paper held at arm's length and then, lately, at half an arm's length and then not far enough away at all. He set one cup by her elbow without asking and one by Tam and stood looking down at the coast plate for a long moment, coffee steaming in his hands.

"The Skerries," he said at last, "have moved half a cable since my son was in short coats. Which is why, Tam, you do not correct a master plate from memory, and why, Coral, you do not fall in love with your own lines. The map is not the country. The country does not know we exist. Hold both of those truths at once and you may live long enough to make an honest map."

"That's very grim for a Tuesday," Tam said.

"That's very Tuesday for a grim," his father replied, and the two of them smiled at each other across the bench in the closed, abbreviated way of people who had been having the same argument for twenty years and had long ago stopped wanting either side to win.

Coral watched them and felt the particular ache she felt whenever all three of them were in the workshop together — the sense of standing inside a thing so complete it could only be temporary. She was nobody's daughter. Her mother had died when she was nine and her father had followed the herring fleets south and then simply followed them, in the way of men who write letters that arrive further apart each year until they stop arriving at all. Master Vey had taken her as an apprentice at sixteen because she could draw and because he said she had "a suspicious respect for evidence," which she understood, eventually, was the highest compliment in his vocabulary. In four years the workshop had become the only room in the world shaped exactly like herself. Tam's presence in it, two weeks home between survey seasons, made the shape feel briefly like a family.

Which was, she would think for the rest of her life, precisely when the world liked to take its measurements.

* * *

It happened on his last night before deployment. The Service was sending him north — past Kelvern, past the last Vellmark benchmark, into the disputed high country where for a decade now the two nations' surveyors had worked within sight of each other's theodolites and pretended, diplomatically, not to. Officially it was a boundary demarcation exercise. Unofficially, everyone in the workshop knew what a survey party meant when it carried flag-staves as well as ranging poles.

Master Vey had gone to bed early, coughing, refusing to call it coughing. Coral stayed to close the workshop properly, which took an hour when done to the master's standard — blades wiped and wrapped, inks stoppered and shelved by pigment family, plates interleaved and cabinet-locked against damp. Tam stayed to hinder her, sitting on the engraving bench swinging his heels like the boy he had been when she first arrived, and she let him stay because tomorrow he would be gone until the snows and possibly past them.

"You're frightened," she said. It was not a question and not quite a kindness. She was frightened too, and had discovered that naming it out loud was the only way to keep it from settling in the chest.

"I'm not frightened of the mountains." He turned a plum stone over in his fingers, set it down, picked it up again. "I'm frightened of what the mountains are for now. When Father surveyed Kelvern — the first time, before I was born — it was science. Sounding the world. Now I'll stand on a ridge with a heliograph and everything I measure goes into somebody's war book." He looked around the workshop the way a man looks at a harbour. "This is the only honest room in the country, Coral. Do you know that? Every line in these cabinets is true because he decided it would be. Not because anyone checked. Because he decided, every morning, for thirty years."

"He'd say the checking is the whole point."

"He'd say that. And then he'd decide it anyway." Tam stood, and crossed to the great flat cabinet where the master plates slept in their interleavings, and laid his palm flat on the closed lid — a gesture Coral had seen his father make a hundred times without ever noticing, and seeing it on the son's hand did something strange to her throat. "If anything happens to him—"

"Nothing is going to happen to him."

"Something is going to happen to everyone," Tam said, with a gentleness that was worse than harshness would have been. "I'm a surveyor. I deal in probabilities. He's fifty-eight, he works fourteen-hour days at a stoop, he coughs in winter and lies about it, and the man drinks barley coffee like it's a moral position. Something will happen. And when it does—" he turned to face her fully, and the lamplight caught him the way it caught the engraved skerries, making him seem briefly more vivid than the room deserved, "—the atlas can't die with him. And neither can the truth in it. Promise me."

"Promise you what?" Coral asked, though she already knew; she had known since he touched the cabinet lid, and some part of her was already grieving the ease of the evening they had just lost.

"Carry the maps to me. If anything happens to Father — if he can't finish it, or protect it, or if anyone ever tries to take the truth out of his own work — you finish it, and you bring the true maps to me. Wherever the lines fall." He said the last words lightly, almost jokingly, because in 1895 the lines fell where they fell the way rivers ran downhill, fixed features of a permanent world, and neither of them imagined a future in which Tam Vey would be reachable only across one.

"It's a strange promise," Coral said. "I'm his apprentice. Of course I'd finish the atlas. Of course I'd keep it true. That's not a promise to you, that's just — the work."

"I know. That's why it costs you nothing to swear it." He smiled, but his eyes didn't join in. "Swear it anyway. People keep promises better than principles. A principle sits in the head and argues. A promise sits in the chest and won't shut up."

She laughed despite herself. Outside, the tide was turning in the harbour below the workshop windows, the mooring lines creaking through their small daily arguments, and the whole city of Vellmark lay peaceful under a sky the colour of unprinted paper.

"All right," she said. "Wherever the lines fall."

"To me. Personally. Not to the bureau, not to the Service, not to any office with a stamp. To me."

"To you, personally," she agreed. "Tam, honestly, nothing is going to—"

He hugged her then, quickly, hard, plum-smelling and wool-smelling and warm, and she heard him say into her hair, very quietly, "Thank you," in the voice of a man accepting a delivery he did not expect to survive the postage, and then he stepped back, picked up his field cap, and was cheerful again by the time he reached the stairs, because that was a skill the men of that family had and Coral had never learned.

She stood alone in the workshop among the sleeping plates, in the only honest room in the country, and told herself the promise weighed nothing at all.

* * *

Now the lamp guttered, and Coral came back to the present the way one surfaces from deep water — too fast, ears ringing, the cold rushing in.

Rell slept in the loft above the press room, breathing shallowly, fevered, alive because she had chosen to make him so. Below her hands, under glass, the dead man's handwriting held its impossible dates. Across the strait, in an army whose uniform she had been taught to think of as a colour rather than a person, a junior officer turned senior one was reading captured dispatches by some other lamp, unaware that somewhere behind enemy lines of a different kind, a woman was remembering a plum stone and a closed cabinet and the words *wherever the lines fall.*

She had sworn it as a formality. She had sworn it the way one swears to see a friend safely aboard a ship that will return in spring.

And now the lines had fallen between them, and fallen again, and the atlas she had finished at the cost of two years of her life sat locked in a bureau vault being corrected into a lie, and the promise that had weighed nothing in 1895 sat on her chest tonight like a stone, whispering, exactly as Tam had predicted, and refusing to shut up.

Coral lifted the glass away from the dispatch, folded the dead man's handwriting small, and hid it in the false spine of a coastal gazetteer — the third hiding place in two days, which meant it was time to admit she was hiding something worth finding.

Tomorrow, Casper would come. He came every week now, cataloguing the seized proofs with his careful officer's handwriting, asking questions that were almost idle. And for the first time since she was nine years old, Coral Dravenmoor rehearsed a lie before she had even been asked the question.
