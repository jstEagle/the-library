## 9. Colonel Brase's Dinner

The summons arrived on cream-laid paper with a wax seal the color of dried blood, and it did not ask; it invited. *Colonel M. Brase requests the pleasure of Miss Coral Dravenmoor's company at dinner, Thursday next, seven o'clock, Bureau House. Matters pertaining to the Vey atlas will be discussed.*

*Matters pertaining.* As though the atlas were a disputed inheritance, a matter of probate rather than of war. Coral read the card four times by the workshop's grey window light, and each time her eye snagged on the same phrase the way a thumbnail catches on a rough edge of vellum.

Rell was in the loft above the drying racks, where she had hidden him for eleven days now, sleeping by day among the rolls of obsolete charts and coming down after dark to eat bread and onions and argue with her in whispers about cipher keys. When she carried the card up to him, he read it and went still in a way that frightened her more than any exclamation would have.

"Brase doesn't dine with apprentices," he said. "Brase dines with people he intends to use."

"Then I'll be used carefully."

"You don't understand what you're walking into." He set the card down on a crate of Blaeu reproductions as if it might stain them. "He is not a stupid man, Miss Dravenmoor. Stupid men don't run requisitions this quiet. He'll have counted every sheet that left this workshop. He knows there should be proofs. What he wants from tonight is to watch your face while he asks about them."

"Then my face will be very boring," said Coral, and was surprised — genuinely surprised — at how steady the words came out, when beneath her sternum her heart was performing its small frantic drumbeat, the one she had carried since the night Rell collapsed against her door with a dead man's handwriting in his fist.

She spent Wednesday preparing the way she would prepare a plate for engraving: scraping away everything superfluous until only the essential lines remained. She laid out her proofs openly on the workbench — the honest ones, the ones she was meant to have — and moved the two sheets of the Kelvern Pass survey into the false bottom of the plan-press, under a decade of discarded trial impressions. She rehearsed answers aloud while grinding ink, hearing how each one sat in the air. She chose her dress: the dark grey wool with the high collar, plain enough to say *I am only a craftsman*, fine enough to say *I am not ashamed*.

It did not occur to her until she was pinning her hair before the cracked mirror that she was, for the first time in her life, dressing for a performance. The realization settled over her like the cold weight of a wet coat.

* * *

Bureau House stood on the Parade Square, a building that had been a merchant's palace in the days when Vellmark grew rich on herring instead of fear. Its façade was all pilasters and heraldic cartouches — Coral knew, professionally, that the stone globes flanking the door bore the old trade routes worn nearly smooth — and its windows glowed amber against the January dusk like a row of held breaths.

A corporal took her coat. A footman took her name. She was walked down a corridor lined with framed maps — she could not help reading them as she passed, could not help noticing that the corridor map of the northern frontier was the falsified one, Kelvern Pass drawn a full three miles into enemy territory — and delivered into a dining room where Colonel Brase stood alone at the hearth, warming his hands like a farmer.

"Miss Dravenmoor." He was shorter than his reputation, broad through the shoulders, with the ruddy weathered face of a man who had once surveyed things in the field himself, years ago, before desks thickened him. His smile was warm in exactly the way an oil lamp is warm. "You honor us. I know these are hard months for the bureau's guests of state, and I wanted you to see that we are not barbarians."

"Colonel." She let him take her hand. His grip was dry and precise.

"Vey's workshop," he said, gesturing her toward the sherry on the sideboard. "I confess I've never actually been inside it. They describe it to me — ink everywhere, paper stacked to the ceiling, that smell of gall and lampblack. Is that accurate?"

"It smells like work, Colonel. Most people find it offensive."

He laughed, delighted, and Coral felt the first small foreign thrill of it: *he believed me*. It was such a little thing, a joke accepted at face value, and yet something in her chest lifted and turned over like a key in a lock she hadn't known was there.

They dined in a room hung with a chandelier that had been electric in better times and now burned candles again, which struck her as a private national allegory. There were six other guests — a port authority man, two officers whose names slid off her, a thin civil woman from the Press Censorship Office who watched Coral eat — but Brase arranged the seating so that she was beside him, and by the fish course he had begun, gently, expertly, to fish.

"I read your master's correspondence," he said. "All of it, of course, since the requisition. Remarkable man. Remarkable discipline. Forty years on the coast survey and never once a complaint filed, never a deadline missed." He swirled his wine. "You must have absorbed a great deal of that. Two years finishing the atlas alone — that's discipline beyond most soldiers I've commanded."

"He was worth the discipline."

"And loyal." Brase set the glass down. Not abruptly; nothing he did was abrupt. But the word landed on the tablecloth between them like a dropped coin. "That's what interests the bureau, you know. We seized his atlas because we had to — wartime, regulations, all that tedium — but between you and me, some of us regarded it as an opportunity. Vey's methods were better than ours. His triangulation of the Kelvern approaches alone is twenty years ahead of our own survey office." A pause, calibrated. "I wonder — did he leave working materials? Drafts? Earlier states of the plates? It would be a service to the crown, Miss Dravenmoor, to see those preserved properly. Catalogued. Understood."

Here it was. The whole evening had been built toward this sentence the way a bridge is built toward its center span, and now Coral felt the load come onto her, and she was astonished to find that she did not buckle.

"Master Vey burned his drafts," she said, and took a sip of wine to give the lie time to breathe. "Every year, at midsummer. It was almost religious with him. He used to say a map should have one author and no ancestors."

It was a beautiful lie. It even contained truth — Vey *had* burned drafts, ceremonially, muttering about humility — but it had never once covered the proofs, which were not drafts but finished corrections, and which sat at this moment under ten years of trial impressions in a plan-press on Marrow Lane. Coral heard herself deliver the sentence with a small rueful smile, heard Brase laugh softly and shake his head — *"one author and no ancestors, God, I'd have liked the man"* — and felt the second thrill arrive, darker than the first.

*I can do this,* she thought. *I can sit in candlelight with a man who has stolen a dead man's life's work and make him laugh.*

The thought frightened her more thoroughly than any patrol ever had.

* * *

Dessert was a pear poached in something amber, and with it came the evening's true examination, dressed as reminiscence.

"Lieutenant Ilyen speaks highly of you," Brase said, and Coral kept her eyes exactly where they had been, on the pear, on her spoon. Casper's name, in this house, was a hand placed flat on a table. "He audited the requisition personally, you know. Very thorough young officer. He mentioned that the workshop inventory showed certain plates listed in Vey's own catalogue that were not found among the seized materials."

The catalogue. Of course the catalogue. She had known the moment the bureau sealed her doors that the inventory would betray her eventually — twelve proof sets recorded, nine surrendered — and she had chosen, in the small hours of the past week, to meet that arithmetic head-on rather than hide from it.

"Three sets destroyed," she said. "By me."

Now Brase did go still. The whole table seemed to lean an inch toward her.

"Damaged in the flooding of '96," Coral continued, calmly, laying the lines of her fabrication as smoothly as she would lay a coastline. "Water got into the flat files. Three sets were ruined — stained, buckled, the ink offset. Master Vey's standard was absolute; he wouldn't allow imperfect impressions out of the workshop, even for reference. So I burned them, per his practice, and corrected the catalogue. I can show you the entries in my own hand, Colonel, dated the following week. I assumed the bureau would want accurate records."

For a moment — one long, ringing moment — she thought he did not believe her. His eyes were pale grey, the eyes of a man who reads terrain for a living, and they moved across her face as though across a chart, looking for the sandbar under the fairway.

Then he smiled, and raised his glass, and the tension broke like ice at thaw.

"Burned per his practice," he echoed. "Well. That's a pity for the archive, but I admire the principle. Accuracy in records." He drank. "You'd make a decent intelligence officer, Miss Dravenmoor. Half our work is knowing what to destroy and having the stomach to do it."

*More than half,* Coral thought, and smiled back.

Later — after the women from Censorship had departed, after the port man had grown tedious — Brase walked her himself to the corridor, past the framed frontier maps, and paused at the falsified one as though it were a portrait of a relative.

"Do you know," he said, conversationally, "that in another life I was a surveyor? Field work on the eastern ranges, before your time. I drew borders for a living. Do you know what I learned?" He tapped the frame lightly with one knuckle. "A border is the most honest thing on a map and the least honest thing in the world. Everyone believes it because it's a line. Lines look like facts. But every line I ever drew was a decision first and a fact afterward." He turned to her, and his warmth was back, seamless, terrible. "Your master understood that too, I think. Better than anyone."

"Yes," said Coral. "Better than anyone."

In the carriage she had not asked for — a bureau carriage, sent to fetch her and return her, its driver silent in his greatcoat — she pressed her gloved hands together in her lap to keep them from shaking, and discovered that the shaking was not entirely fear.

The whole ride home through the gaslit streets she turned the evening over like a plate held to the light, examining her own impression of it. She had lied. Not once but a dozen times — small lies, structural lies, lies with dates and catalogue numbers and dead men's practices built into them — and every one had held weight. Brase had dined with her for three hours and come away believing she had nothing but burnt paper and loyalty. She had looked into the pale eyes of the man who owned her master's atlas and given him, gift-wrapped, the exact story he wished to find.

And it had felt — she made herself admit it, alone in the dark of the carriage, watching the lamplight slide across the glass — it had felt *good*. Not the way vice feels good. The way competence feels good. The way the first clean pull of a burin through copper feels good, the line coming up true out of the metal. All her life she had been honest the way apprentice masons are plumb: by training, by rule, without ever testing the wall. Tonight she had tested it, and the wall stood, and some secret architect in her chest was quietly, appalledly proud.

*God forgive me,* she thought, and did not mean the lying. She meant the pride.

* * *

The workshop was dark except for the shielded lamp she'd left burning in the loft. She climbed to Rell half-frozen, her ears still ringing with the clink of colonels' glasses, and found him awake, sitting up in the nest of old charts with her father's old knife open on his knee — he slept badly, always between him and the door.

"Well?" he said.

"He believes me." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger, and told it all plainly: the corridor of false maps, the fishing, the catalogue trap she had walked into knowingly and walked out of with a story about floodwater and fire. Rell listened without moving, and when she described Brase's speech about borders — *a decision first and a fact afterward* — he closed the knife very slowly.

"That's the truest thing he said all night," Rell murmured. "That's the creed they govern by. Decision first, fact after." He looked up at her, and in the shielded lamplight his hollowed face looked briefly younger, and afraid. "Do you understand what you did tonight? You lied to the directorate of military cartography and he toasted you for it. There are men who've been broken on less than that dinner table. You went in an apprentice and came out a courier."

"I'm not a courier."

"No." He almost smiled. "Couriers carry things. You're becoming something else. Something he didn't plan for." He nodded at the plan-press below them, where the true Kelvern Pass slept under its shroud of trial impressions. "There's a problem, though. You bought us weeks, maybe. But Brase asked about proofs at dinner, which means the arithmetic is already bothering him — twelve sets, nine accounted for, three burned. Men like him don't stop being bothered. They send clerks."

"Clerks check catalogues. My entries are dated and consistent—"

"Clerks also talk to other clerks. Clerks check the fire-wood accounts of Marrow Lane in December of '96." He said it gently, the way one delivers a diagnosis. "You gave him a story with a date in it, Miss Dravenmoor. Stories with dates invite verification. We have to move soon — combine the key, finish the reconstruction, get it out of this city before someone decides to count ashes."

Coral sat down on the crate beside him, and the exhaustion of the evening — the held voice, the held face, three hours of being a smooth surface over deep water — came down on her all at once. But underneath the exhaustion, steady as a benchmark cut in stone, was the new thing. The thing she had discovered at Brase's table.

"Then we work tomorrow," she said. "Both halves of the key. Yours and mine. Tomorrow night we rebuild Kelvern Pass."

Rell studied her face for a long moment, the way Brase had studied it over dessert — hunting the sandbar under the fairway — and whatever he found there made him nod slowly, and reach over, and turn up the lamp.

But sleep, when it finally took her near dawn, brought her not the colonel's smile nor the frozen pass but Casper Ilyen's face, unaccountably, set in that particular expression she had seen on him only twice — the day of the requisition, and once years ago at the tide gate — the expression of a man watching a thing he loves walk toward a cliff edge in good order, saluting as it goes. And in the dream he was saying her name, and in the dream she was lying to him too, smoothly, beautifully, and waking in the grey light with the taste of it still in her mouth, Coral understood that the hardest performance was yet to come: not Brase's dinner table, but the next time Casper stood in her doorway and asked her, kindly, in his careful officer's voice, whether there was anything she needed to tell him.
