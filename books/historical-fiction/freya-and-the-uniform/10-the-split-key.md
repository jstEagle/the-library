## 10. The Split Key

Coral had always thought of lamplight as the color of work. In the workshop it had pooled gold over Vey's drafting table for twenty years, over her own for two, and she had never once considered that the same warm circle could feel like a conspirator's lantern. Tonight it did. She had drawn the shutters closed before full dark, wedged a towel beneath the door where light might leak into the alley, and told herself it was only prudence. It felt like guilt.

Rell sat across the table from her, his hands — long, scarred, careful hands, a courier's hands — flat on the wood as though he feared to touch anything until invited. Between them lay the two halves of the dead man's mind: the dispatch with its marginal cipher, and Coral's retained proofs, pulled from the false bottom of the plan chest where she had hidden them the day the bureau came.

"You taught yourself his shorthand," Rell said. It wasn't quite a question.

"He taught me. I only thought I'd learned it all." Coral smoothed the proof sheet flat. Kelvern Pass stared up at her in clean engraved lines: contour upon contour, the river Kell braiding through its valley, the border itself drawn as a confident double stroke of ink. Two years she had printed this plate in proofs and checked it against nothing, because there had been nothing to check it against. That innocence was gone now; she could see the wrongness the way one sees a face after it has been described as familiar and is suddenly known. "The cipher uses his survey marks as numbers. The little triangles — station points. He numbered them in sequence along each traverse."

"And the numbers do what?"

"Give distances. Bearings." She traced a triangle near the pass's western lip. "He built the corrections into the map itself, so that anyone who knew the key could rebuild what the bureau erased. The dispatch holds half the key. The atlas plates hold the other half. Neither means anything alone." Her voice caught on the elegance of it, on how like him it was — Aldous Vey had trusted paper more than people, and had made the paper keep his secret even from fire.

"Then let's mean something," Rell said quietly.

She dipped her pen.

The work consumed four hours and gave back a country. Triangle by triangle, bearing by bearing, the true Kelvern Pass assembled itself on a fresh sheet of her own paper — not redrawn, but *recovered*, each line fought for out of cipher and memory and the faint pencil ghost of Vey's original field observations that survived in the margins of the proofs. Where the bureau's border ran high along the northern ridge, claiming the whole watershed for Vellmark's side, Vey's corrected line dropped south and east, honest to the survey stones he himself had set in the summer of 1891, when both nations' commissioners had stood on the same snowfield and shaken hands over the same line.

"That's the falsification," Rell said, when the last contour closed. His voice had gone very soft. "Sixty square miles. Three villages. The mill at Sorrel Ford. All of it moved onto the Vellmark side of the line by a draftsman's pen, and then the war built on top of it like a house on a lie's foundation."

"I know what it is." Coral set down the pen. Her hand was steady; she made a small study of keeping it so. "I've known since I compared the sheets. What I don't know — what you keep not answering — is why it should go to *them*. To Tam's command. Across the strait."

"Because they're the ones fighting on the forged map."

"And we're the ones who'll be fighting on it too, if they take it and use it." She heard her own voice sharpen and could not stop it. "You want me to hand the true positions of the pass to an enemy army, Rell. Not to a court. Not to a newspaper. To artillery officers. Do you understand what that is? That's not truth anymore. That's ammunition wearing truth's coat."

* * *

Rell watched her across the lamp and understood, with the particular ache of the long-trained courier, that this was the moment every delivery finally came to: not the ice, not the patrol, but the moment the carrier looked at the package and asked what she was carrying. He had asked it himself, once, in a boathouse outside Tarn with Vey's last dispatch in his boot, and he had answered it the way Vey had taught him to answer everything — by trusting the man who wrote the words.

"Coral," he said. "Do you know why your master didn't publish this himself?"

She looked up. In the lamplight her face was all angles and exhaustion, and under the exhaustion something fiercer that he suspected she hadn't yet named.

"He was going to," she said. "He told the bureau he would. That's what got him—"

"That's part of it. But there's a second reason, and he made me memorize it because he didn't trust it to paper." Rell leaned forward. "A truth published by one side is a weapon. A truth published by *both* sides is a fact. If Vey had put that map out in Vellmark alone, Vellmark's government calls it treason, burns it, hangs whoever printed it, and the war goes on with the lie intact — stronger, even, because now it has martyrs. But if the corrected border appears on *both* sides of the strait at once — if Tam's own command sees it, if their commissioners confirm their own 1891 stones against it — then no government on either shore can call it enemy invention. It stops being propaganda and starts being geography."

"And until it can be published on both shores at once?"

"Then it has to be carried carefully. That's what couriers are for." He spread his hands. "I'm not asking you to arm them. I'm asking you to finish what your master started. He chose Tam's route himself. He said—" and here Rell hesitated, because the words were heavy, and he had carried them eight hundred miles already and they had not gotten lighter, "—he said the son would know what the father died for, even if the son hated the father. He said maps outlive loyalties. He said it the week before they killed him."

Coral had gone very still. "They killed him," she repeated. "You said before that he survived the reported death. You haven't said—"

"No," Rell said. "I haven't."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Outside, far off, a bell buoy clanged against the winter tide, patient as a metronome, and Rell thought: tomorrow, or the next night, or whenever her grief finished breaking and reformed into something with edges, he would tell her the rest. Not tonight. Tonight the map lay between them like a sleeping animal, and either of them might still bolt.

But Coral surprised him, as couriers are sometimes surprised by the people they carry things for. She pulled the corrected sheet toward her, blew gently across the wet ink, and said, "Then we do it properly. If this goes anywhere, it goes as Vey would have sent it — with the survey record attached. Station coordinates. Instrument corrections. Dates. Enough that Tam's engineers can verify it against their own archive without trusting a single word we say." Her mouth curved, and it was not entirely a kind expression. "If I am going to be a traitor to my city, Rell, I intend to be a rigorous one."

"It isn't treason," he began. "It's—"

"Don't." She held up the pen like a small black blade. "Don't tell me what it is. I've spent two years learning exactly what things are by drawing them, and I know better than most that a line looks different depending on which side of it you stand. Let me name it myself, when I'm ready."

* * *

Coral worked until the third hour past midnight, and somewhere in the second hour she stopped arguing with herself and simply worked, and it was the working that frightened her most.

Because it was easy. That was the horror she turned over while her pen moved through contour lines: how *easy* it was. Every decision had a clean professional logic to it. Attach the survey record — good cartographic practice. Use cipher for the annotations — security, obviously; any bureau clerk intercepting the packet would see only weather notes. Copy the master onto thin paper that could be folded to the size of a prayer book — standard field procedure. Each step was the step a competent mapmaker would take, and so she took them, and only afterward did she notice what she had done: she had built a complete, portable, deniable instrument of war out of an act of truth, and she had enjoyed the building. Her hands had been sure. Her mind had been clear and quick and almost happy.

*I can deceive,* she thought, remembering Colonel Brase's dining room, the candlelight, the smooth lies that had poured out of her like water finding a channel. *And now I can scheme. Which comes next?*

She cut the cipher key in half, as Vey had taught her the principle demanded: the dispatch's half copied onto one slip, the atlas's half onto another, so that no single capture could give an interceptor the whole. Then she sat looking at the two slips of paper, each barely larger than a stamp, and understood that she was holding the entire war in fragments smaller than either army's pride.

Across the room, Rell slept badly in the chair by the cold stove, twitching toward sounds only he could hear. He had crossed the strait on ice that sang, he'd said. He had buried two other couriers between Tarn and Vellmark. And he believed — with a faith she envied and distrusted — that paper honestly made could survive anything, that the map would outlive them all the way Vey said it would.

Coral looked down at the corrected Kelvern Pass and saw, superimposed on the ink, all the things a map does not draw. The three villages. The mill at Sorrel Ford. The boys from the Vellmark docks who had marched north two winters ago whistling, and the ones who had come back without their whistling, or without themselves. Somewhere beyond her shutters, on the other side of the frozen strait, Tam Vey was wearing an enemy uniform and hating his father, and did not know that his father had spent his last months building him an inheritance out of triangles and bearings.

*Truth has a price,* her master used to say at the drafting table, tapping the paper. *Everything honest costs. That's how you know it isn't a forgery — forgeries are free.*

She folded the slips together and sewed them into the lining of her work coat with thread and three stitches she would remember forever, and then she blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and admitted, at last, in the privacy of her own skull, what she had been refusing to say aloud even to herself:

It had to go to Tam. Not because Rell argued well. Not because the logic was clean. But because she had sworn it in 1895 in a sunlit workshop, when borders seemed permanent and promises were cheap — *wherever the lines fall*, she had said, and the lines had fallen, and the debt had come due with interest she could never have imagined at twenty-two years old.

The price, then. She had found it. It had a shape now: her city, her craft's reputation, Casper's face when he understood, and possibly — she let herself think it once, plainly, so it would stop circling — her life.

She was still sitting in the dark, pricing herself, when knuckles sounded softly on the shutter — three taps, a pause, one more. A signal. Rell came awake instantly and totally, hand inside his coat, and in the blackness Coral heard him breathe one word that undid the whole quiet night:

"That's a network knock. But my network's dead."
