## 11. What Casper Saw

The audit was scheduled for the ninth of December, but Casper Ilyen arrived on the eighth, alone, in the grey hour before the city had properly woken. He told himself it was efficiency — that an officer who inspected a requisitioned site ahead of his own inspection would find nothing disturbed, and could therefore conduct the formal visit as a formality. He told himself many things on the walk from the bureau barracks, his breath streaming behind him like a pennant, and none of them survived the moment he stood again at the door of the Vey workshop.

It had been four months since the confiscation. Four months since he had stood in that same room with sealed orders in his gloved hands and watched Coral Dravenmoor's face perform a composure she did not feel. He remembered the exact sound her fingers had made against her skirt — not a fist, not a tremor, just a deliberate flattening of the hand, the way a mapmaker smooths a curling corner of paper. As if she could press the whole situation flat and read what lay beneath it.

The bureau's seal cracked under his thumb. The lock on the workshop door had been changed after the requisition; the key was his now, along with everything else that had belonged to Aldous Vey's life's work. That was the arithmetic of war: a dead man's atlas became the state's atlas, and a living woman's two years became a line in a ledger marked *duration*.

He let himself in.

The cold inside was different from the cold outside — older, stiller, the cold of a room that had been left to hold its breath. The lamps were dark. The great drafting table stood bare where the plates had been, its surface scarred with decades of pinholes, burnished along its edges by the passage of forearms and elbows, so that it gleamed faintly even without light, like something that remembered being useful. The presses were shrouded in the canvas the quartermaster's men had thrown over them, careless and gray as snowdrifts. Shelves of rolled proofs lined one wall — the ones Coral had been permitted to keep, duplicates and near-duplicates the assessors had judged valueless.

Casper walked the room slowly, because that was what the job required, and because walking slowly was the only way he knew to delay the thing he was actually doing, which was remembering.

He had been twenty-two when he first came here, a junior cadet seconded to the coastal survey with no aptitude anyone could find for anything except the patient copying of coastlines. Aldous Vey had looked at Casper's handiwork for a long minute and said, "You draw water like a man who fears drowning," which was either an insult or the most accurate description anyone had ever given him, and then taught him how to stipple a shoreline so that the sea seemed to breathe. Coral had been there through all of it — the master's apprentice, ink to her knuckles, quick and unimpressed and luminous in the lamplight, correcting his gradients without asking permission. He had loved her by winter. He had never found the courage to say it in any language except the language of maps: a survey mark here, a rendered headland there, a small bay named in one of his practice charts with a name that was nearly hers if you knew how to read it.

She had never said she couldn't read it. She had also never said she could. For six years that ambiguity had been enough to live on, the way a candle is enough to live on if you never look directly at how little light it makes.

Now the state had taken the atlas, the war had taken the strait, and the only language they shared anymore was silence — and Casper had come here, ahead of his own inspection, to break a piece of it.

Because he had seen something three days ago in the requisition ledger, and he had not reported it, and every day since, the unreported thing had grown heavier, the way ice grows on a rigging line — invisibly, and then all at once past bearing.

The coal store behind the workshop. It appeared on the property inventory as a disused outbuilding, structure sound, contents nil. But when Casper had walked the perimeter on a routine check, he had noticed that the frost on the store's north wall was melted in a patch the size of a man's shoulder. Melted from *within*. And the snow before its door — this was the detail he could not stop seeing, the detail that sat behind his eyes when he tried to pray, when he tried to eat, when he stood at parade review watching Colonel Brase's mouth move — the snow before its door bore the ghost of a track that someone had brushed away, badly, with a bundle of straw. Brushed snow does not disappear. It only changes its texture. Any surveyor knows this. Any cartographer teaches it.

Someone was living in the coal store of the Vey workshop.

Casper stood in the middle of the drafting room and let the knowledge finish settling, the way sediment settles in a jar of river water, until he could see through it clearly to the bottom. A stranger had come off the frozen strait or out of some bolt-hole in the dock district and into the one building in all of Vellmark where Coral Dravenmoor had reason to linger. The patrols had orders to watch the workshop — Brase's standing instruction, *the apprentice is sentimental about the material; sentimentality bears observation* — and yet no report had crossed Casper's desk about activity at the coal store, which meant whoever was there moved at night, carefully, or meant no harm, or both.

Or Coral did not know. That possibility cut him worse than the others, because if she didn't know, then the watcher in the walls of her master's workshop was a threat to her, and Casper would have to act as an officer acts. But if she *did* know —

He found out within the hour, and finding out undid him entirely, because she did not behave like a woman harboring a secret. She behaved like Coral. She came in through the side entrance just past nine with a wrapped loaf under her arm and a sack of charcoal dragging her shoulder down, and she stopped dead when she saw him, and her face did exactly what it had done in April: smoothed itself flat, pressable, unreadable.

"Lieutenant." The word was perfectly correct. Her eyes were not. Her eyes went past him to the drafting table, to the shrouded presses, to the shelf of proofs, counting, always counting, and he understood suddenly and completely that she was checking whether he had touched them.

"Routine audit," he said. "Tomorrow will be the official one. Today I'm just—" He gestured vaguely at the room, and heard how thin the vagueness sounded. "Checking the seals."

"The seals are on the door," she said. "You used a key."

"Yes."

She set down the charcoal sack with a grunt of effort that was probably real and possibly theater, and began unpacking her things onto the corner of the table where she apparently still worked — they had permitted her that, proofs only, private work, Brase's indulgence dressed up as generosity — and for a while neither of them spoke. The silence between them had a grain to it now, like wood that had weathered. He hated it. He would have given his commission to sand it back to the old silence, the comfortable one, the one filled with the scratch of pens and her muttered corrections of his coastlines.

"I need to inspect the outbuildings," he said.

Her hands stopped moving. It was very slight — the pause of a needle lifted mid-stitch — but he saw it, because he had spent six years learning to see what her hands said when her mouth stayed correct.

"The coal store?" she asked. Too quickly. Then, recovering, with a small dry smile: "There's no coal in it. Your own inventory says so."

"Inventories say many things." He kept his voice level, administrative, and marveled from a distance at how easily the lie came out of him, how the uniform fit his mouth even as it stopped fitting anywhere else. "I have to look. If I don't, the man who comes after me will."

That last part was true, and saying it cost him something, and he watched her understand it. He watched her weigh him. Six years of reading each other across a drafting table, and it came down to this: whether she believed that when he opened that door, he would see what he saw and do his duty, or whether he would see what he saw and become something else.

"Casper," she said quietly, and it was the first time in months she had used his name instead of his rank, and it went through him like a stipple needle through paper. "The coal store hasn't been used in years. There's damp in the walls. Nothing lives there but rats."

"Then it will take me two minutes," he said, "and we can both go back to pretending this is an audit."

* * *

The coal store smelled of old char and newer breath. That was the thing that decided it, though he pretended to himself for another few seconds that the decision hadn't already been made. Breath has a smell when it has been breathing in a closed space — warm, human, faintly sour — and the air inside the store was hours old and alive. Against the far wall, where the melted frost had showed from outside, a pallet lay folded with military precision, a soldier's folding, a courier's folding. Beside it: a tin cup, a stub of pencil, and a scrap of paper covered in route calculations in a cramped hand — distances, tide tables for the strait, wind bearings. Casper did not touch any of it. He stood in the doorway of the coal store with his hand resting on his belt, in the posture of a man conducting an inspection, and performed in his mind the entire sequence of his duty: note the occupation, secure the site, report the discovery, place the workshop under immediate guard, detain the apprentice for questioning regarding possible contact with enemy agents.

He performed it completely. He watched it play out to its end — Coral in an interrogation room, Coral's name entered in Brase's ledgers, Coral's two years of devotion confiscated a second time, this time as evidence against her.

Then he closed the door, and swept the snow-tracks before it clean with the side of his boot, more thoroughly than the stranger ever had, and walked back into the workshop, and lied.

"Empty," he said. "Rats, as you said. I'll note it as structurally unsound and recommend demolition in the spring." He met her eyes and held them, and made himself add, in a voice pitched low enough that only she could hear it, "Demolition. In the spring. Whatever anyone keeps in a building scheduled for demolition, Coral, they should think hard about moving it somewhere safer. Buildings fall down. Walls get ears. Brase signed an order last week putting a watch on this street — a clerk named Pethis, evening rotation. He reports Thursdays."

Her face did not change. That was the terrifying thing, and the marvelous thing: she received all of it and gave back nothing, smooth as vellum. Only her hands betrayed her, closing around the wrapped loaf she had brought, gripping it hard enough that he could see the dough deform.

"A watch," she repeated. "On my street."

"On your workshop. There's a difference, and you're clever enough to know what it means." He straightened, resumed his officer's posture, raised his voice to its ordinary register as if resuming a conversation about seals and inventories. "I'll be done here within the hour. The official audit tomorrow will confirm my preliminary findings: materials secured, premises orderly, no irregularities."

"No irregularities," she echoed softly.

He gathered his ledger and his seal-case and went to the door, and there he stopped, because there was one more thing, and he had carried it the whole way across the city in the pocket of his coat like a stone, and if he did not say it now he never would.

"Coral." He did not turn around fully. He looked at the doorframe, at the old burn mark where Vey had set a hot pressing iron down carelessly sometime in the nineties, a small black crescent moon on the painted wood. "When we were surveying the Kelvern coast, in ninety-six, you told me something. You said a map isn't the territory, it's an argument about the territory. You said whoever draws the line decides where men die. I thought it was shop talk. I was too young to hear it properly." His throat was dry. "I hear it now."

Behind him, the room was silent. He did not let himself turn to see what her face was doing. Some things a man cannot see and remain capable of walking out a door.

"For what it's worth," he said, "whatever the argument turns out to be — I find I am less certain than I was about whose lines are drawn straight. Good morning, Miss Dravenmoor. Expect me at ten tomorrow with the assessment team. Pethis comes at dusk."

He let himself out. The cold took his breath the way it always did, and he stood on the step for a moment in the falling light, aware of the weight in his chest that was not the ledger, aware that he had just, in the space of forty minutes, falsified a government inspection, concealed the presence of an unauthorized person on requisitioned property, and warned a suspected sympathizer that she was under surveillance. Treason, he thought, was supposed to arrive like a storm — dramatic, declared, flags flying. Instead it had arrived the way frost arrived: silently, in the night, on the inside of the glass, so gradually that a man only noticed it when he breathed against the pane and saw his own breath fail to fog it.

He had chosen. Trembling, half-blind, without ever speaking the word aloud, he had chosen her over the uniform — and the strangest part, the part he would lie awake with that night staring at the barrack ceiling, was that it had not felt like falling. It had felt like finally standing on ground that was actually there.

* * *

Thursday dusk came gray and early, and Casper kept his appointment with it. He had arranged to be on the street himself — legitimate enough, a supervising officer observing his watcher — and he watched Pethis, the junior clerk, take up his post in the doorway of the chandler's opposite, folding his arms, exhaling steam, a boy of maybe nineteen doing his duty badly and boredly and honestly.

And across from the boy, in the blue hour, the workshop's side door opened, and Coral stepped out with a market basket on her arm, and paused on the step to shake the frost from her shawl — pausing, Casper noted, precisely long enough to be seen, precisely visible, a woman running an errand. She turned toward the chandler's shop, nodded pleasantly to Pethis as though noticing him for the first time, asked him the time, remarked on the cold, and walked on toward the fish stalls. All of it natural. All of it flawless. And Casper, watching from the shadow of the corner, understood two things at once, and both of them frightened him.

The first was that she had made Pethis, catalogued him, and was already managing him — that the woman he loved was better at this game than the bureau's watchers, and that her flawless performance meant there was something that needed covering, something moving at night through the coal store's cold dark while the city slept.

The second was that whatever she was covering, whoever was folded onto that pallet with tide tables and route calculations written in a courier's cramped hand — she was going to carry forward, alone, without him, into danger he could not follow and could not officially see, because seeing it would destroy them both.

Pethis noted the time in his little book: subject exited premises 16:40, direction north, carrying market basket, nothing unusual to report.

Nothing unusual to report, Casper thought, and turned up his collar, and walked the other way through the gathering snow, carrying the most unusual report in Vellmark folded small and silent behind his ribs — knowing that by Thursday next, one way or another, it would be too heavy to hold.
