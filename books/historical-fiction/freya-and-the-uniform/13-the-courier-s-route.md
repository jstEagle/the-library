## 13. The Courier's Route

Rell had learned long ago that fear lived in the hands. The mind could lie to itself for hours—could dress a mission in ceremony, could recite routes like prayers—but the hands always told the truth. They trembled when they knew. So he kept them busy.

He spread the old sea chart across the workbench, weighting its curled corners with the ink pots and the brass rule and the cold stub of a candle, and began to draw the strait.

The workshop was quiet in the way that only hunted places are quiet. Coral sat at her master's desk behind him, ostensibly mending a pen, though he had heard the nib scrape against nothing for the better part of an hour. She was watching him. He could feel it the way one feels weather coming through a cracked windowpane—not seeing it, but knowing.

He dipped the pencil and drew the coastline of Vellmark first: the harbor, the breakwater, the ridge of the tide gate where their trouble had begun. Then, northward, the frozen strait itself—a gray-white band two miles wide on paper, which in life was eleven miles of ice and darkness and patrol lamps, separating everything Rell had left from everything he still had to do.

"The ice bridge forms here," he said, tapping the narrows off Gull Point. "Every winter it's crossed. Smugglers, deserters, lovers, fools. The bureau knows it. They post pickets at the Vellmark shore because that's the only place you can *start*."

"And on the far side?"

"Kelvern garrison holds the coast. Which sounds like the worst possible destination until you understand that the garrison is not the border. The border is a line drawn by the same men who drew your atlas." He allowed himself a thin smile. "The enemy has no idea what's coming to them. In the most literal sense."

Coral set down the ruined pen. "Say it plainly, Rell. You've been circling it all morning."

He had been. Because saying it plainly meant admitting it to himself, and there were still a few hours of cowardice left in the day if she'd let him keep them.

But her eyes were on him now, gray and steady over the lamplight, and he remembered Master Vey saying once, in that dry voice he used for things that mattered: *A courier who cannot tell his principal the truth is worse than none at all.* Rell had failed that test already, once, with men who died for it.

"My network is gone," he said plainly.

The words came out smaller than he'd intended. He pressed on before she could soften them with pity.

"Vey built it over six years. A fisherman who ran messages in his herring barrels. Two clerks in the bureau archive—one of them is the reason I know the raid schedules. A lighthouse keeper at Skarrow Point who flashed signals disguised as weather warnings. Eleven people, Coral. I can name every one of them, and I can tell you what happened to every one of them." His finger moved along the coast, touching names that were no longer names but sentences. "Herring barrels burned in a customs sweep in October. The clerks were transferred—I don't know where; men who get transferred like that don't come back. The keeper at Skarrow shot himself rather than be questioned." He stopped his finger there. It was shaking again. He flattened it against the chart. "That leaves me. One courier, half-frozen twice over, with a face the bureau has seen on three separate warrants, carrying the single most dangerous piece of paper produced in this war."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the wind worried at the shutter.

"I made it this far," he went on, "because I had places to sleep and people to lie for me. Now I have a cellar and you, and you are watched—the workshop is watched, we both know it, there was a man on the corner yesterday pretending to read the same broadside for forty minutes. If I leave alone, I am a lone man crossing eleven miles of ice with a border patrol on one end and Colonel Brase's whole apparatus behind me. I will be taken. And when I'm taken, the maps go with me, and Vey dies a second time, permanently, and everything—" his hand swept across the chart, across the corrected Kelvern Pass, sewn into proofs and hidden in the false bottom of the map drawer, "—everything was for nothing."

He hadn't meant to say that last part loudly. It came out loudly anyway, and then the room was very quiet, and he busied himself straightening the candle stub as though the fate of nations hung on its alignment.

"You should have told me sooner," Coral said.

"There wasn't time. There's never been time since the day I collapsed at your door."

"There's time now." She rose and came to stand across the workbench from him, and he saw that her hands were not trembling at all. That frightened him more than anything else in this conversation. "Show me the route."

"Coral—"

"*Show me.*"

So he showed her. It was easier than fighting, and God forgive him, some tired traitorous part of him wanted her to see it—to see the length of it, the cold of it, the arithmetic of survival—and to understand why he would not let her come.

"Out through the salt gate after the changing of the watch, third watch, moonless night. There's a drainage culvert under the seawall that the harbor children use; it floods at high tide, so the timing must be exact—we go within the hour before low water, when it runs shallowest. Then north along the shingle under Gull Point, four miles in the dark, off every path. Then the ice." He tapped the white band. "Eleven miles, but you never walk eleven straight. The ice has leads—cracks where the water shows black as ink. Some freeze thin and will take a man's weight; some won't. There's a channel near the middle called the Widow's Seam where the current runs fast underneath and the ice above it is rotten all winter. You cross it here—" a mark, "—where the fishermen throw planks in hard frost. If there are no planks, you go around, and around adds five miles and an extra dawn, and a dawn on the ice is death, because you're visible from either shore for two miles in any direction."

"And the patrols?"

"Pickets on the Vellmark side, here and here—a hut at Gull Point and a sled team that runs the shore line at irregular hours. On the Kelvern side, a wire and lamp line, but the enemy posts are lazy; they watch for soldiers, not couriers. The middle of the strait belongs to no one, which means the middle is where the ice decides whether you live." He looked up at her. "It's a courier's route. Every step of it is knowledge—where to put your feet, when to move, how to listen to the ice groan and know whether it's settling or breaking. I've done crossings like it four times. I know the sound the ice makes before it fails. That knowledge cannot be handed over in an evening. It lives in the body."

"Then teach it."

"It took me years—"

"How long did it take you to learn the sound?" she demanded. "The settling from the breaking. How long?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. "...I grew up on the strait. My father fished it."

"Then you cannot give me that in an evening, and you cannot give yourself another pair of legs, and we are both sitting here pretending there is a version of this where you simply walk away and survive." Her voice did not rise; that was the terrible thing about her, Rell thought—it never rose, it only got closer, like a hand reaching into his chest. "You said it yourself. If they take you, they take the maps. But if they take you, they don't take me. Nobody in Vellmark knows my face. Nobody has ever needed to. I have spent my whole life being unremarkable in this city, Rell—small, quiet, the apprentice, the girl who inks other people's lines. The bureau seized my atlas and I smiled at them. Brase sat in my parlor and lied to my face about my master's notebooks and I poured his tea. Do you understand? I have been rehearsing for this my entire life without knowing it."

"That's not rehearsal. That's just obedience. It's different."

"Is it?" For the first time something cracked in her voice, hairline-thin. "Casper used to say the same thing to me. That I was good at following because I'd never been asked to lead. Well." She pressed both palms flat on the chart, over the white band of the strait, as if warming them. "I'm asking."

"No."

"Why—"

"Because you're the atlas!" It burst out of him before he could shape it into argument. He stood too, and the stool scraped back, and suddenly they were both on their feet with the whole small war spread between them on the bench. "Don't you see it? The maps are in your head as much as in the lining of that coat. The cipher, the proofs, the corrections—if I die on the ice, the truth dies with a courier. If *you* die, it dies with its author. Vey didn't build this network to carry his own work out—he built it to carry *you* out, someday, if it ever came to that. He told me so. 'If anything happens, the girl finishes it, the girl delivers it.' Those were nearly his last instructions to me. I will not spend his last instruction buying us both a grave."

"That's a lie," Coral said quietly.

"What?"

"He said that to comfort you. Or you've dressed it up in memory until it became that. Aldous Vey believed in the work, not the vessel—he told me the same thing my first year, when I begged him to let me redo a plate I'd spoiled. *The map is not the mapmaker, Coral. The map is truer than any of us. Serve it, not me.*" Her eyes were bright and wet and furious. "You want to know why I should go and you should stay? Here it is, plainly, since we're being plain: you are known. Three warrants, you said. Your face is worth money in this city, and this city is hungry. Mine is worth nothing. You know the ice, yes—but you also carry grief in you like ballast, and grief makes men fast and careless. I have been careful my whole life. Careful is what I *am*."

They stood there, breathing, the lamp flame bending between them.

And Rell felt it then, the thing he supposed parents and commanders must feel—the awful vertigo of realizing that the person in front of you has done the same arithmetic you have, and arrived at the same sum, and refuses to show their working. She was afraid. He could see it now that he looked properly: the pulse at her throat, the whitening knuckles on the chart. She was terrified down to the marrow.

She was volunteering anyway.

"That's what brave is, isn't it," he said, and his voice came out hoarse. "I used to think it was the opposite of frightened."

"It isn't."

"No. It's frightened with somewhere to be." He laughed once, without humor, and dragged a hand down his face. When he spoke again it was to the chart, because he could not look at her. "We're arguing about who gets to be brave because neither of us wants the other to die. That's the whole argument. Strip the tactics off it, strip the warrants and the ice-sense and the atlas, and it's just two cowards trying to be noble at each other."

"Yes," Coral said. "Probably."

"So we split the difference." He pulled the pencil from her hand before she could react, and bent over the chart, and began to draw again—fast, decisive strokes, the kind he hadn't trusted himself to make all morning. "Two go or none go. That's the courier's rule; a network broken doesn't get to be proud. We cross together. You carry half the maps, I carry half—if one of us falls on the ice, the other still delivers, and no patrol searching one body finds everything. You'll learn the ice-sound tonight, on the shingle, listening to the floes grind. I'll teach you what can be taught. The rest—" he glanced up at her, and found her watching him with an expression he suspected he'd remember on his deathbed, whichever deathbed it turned out to be, "—the rest we do frightened. Together. Frightened with somewhere to be."

For a long moment she didn't speak. Then she nodded, once, the way her master must have nodded over a finished plate—measured, final.

"Together," she said.

Rell exhaled, and it seemed to him the room warmed by a degree. He bent back over the chart to mark the tides, muttering the almanac hours aloud, low-water windows, moonless nights—*the twenty-second, maybe the twenty-fourth*—and he was halfway through calculating the culvert's clearance when he heard it.

Boots. Not on the stair—the stair creaked and they both knew its song by heart. Boots in the street outside, many of them, unhurried, in the deliberate rhythm of men who were not passing by but taking position.

Coral's head came up. Through the gap in the shutter, in the blue light of the falling afternoon, they both saw them: soldiers at the corner where the broadside-reader had stood yesterday, and more drifting toward the far end of the lane, quiet as silt settling, closing like a hand around a held match.

"They're not waiting for the twenty-second," Rell breathed.

* * *

Below the workshop, in the dark of the cellar where the corrected proofs slept in their false drawer, someone knocked—softly, twice, in a rhythm Rell did not know.

Coral did. He saw it hit her like cold water.

"That's Casper's knock," she whispered. "It's from when we were children. He wouldn't use it unless—" She swallowed. "Unless he was already lost."

The knocking came again. Twice, softly. Outside, the boots kept closing, patient as winter.

Rell blew out the lamp.
