## 14. Betrayal in the Archive

The archive of the Ninth Bureau of Cartographic Warfare occupied the basement of the customs house, and Casper Ilyen had long ago decided that whoever designed it had understood something essential about the empire: that its true treasures were kept below the waterline, in the dark, where no one would think to look for them. He descended the iron stairs that morning with his collar up against the draft and the taste of last night's bad coffee still coating his tongue, and he was halfway to his desk before he noticed the clerk.

Pell Marsh was twenty years old at most, a narrow-shouldered boy with ink-stained cuffs and the eager, watery eyes of a man who had never once been trusted with anything important. This morning he stood beside Casper's desk as though planted there, holding a folder against his chest like a breastplate, waiting.

"Lieutenant." His voice cracked on the rank. "Lieutenant, may I — there's something I'm required to report. Through channels."

"Then report it through channels," Casper said, hanging his coat. "Not through my morning."

"Yes, sir. No, sir. It's only — Colonel Brase instructed that anything concerning the Vey workshop come to you first, sir, being as you're the audit officer, but this—" The boy swallowed. "This concerns the audit itself. Sir, I think you should sit down."

Casper did not sit down. He stood very still by his desk and felt the cold of the stairwell follow him into his bones, because Pell Marsh's hands were shaking, and clerks whose hands shook were clerks who had found something, and everything in Casper's life for three weeks had been arranged around the hope that nothing would be found.

"Speak," he said.

* * *

It had been, Pell explained, an accident of arithmetic.

The requisition ledger for the Thornbury & Vey workshop — Coral's workshop now, though the sign still bore both names — recorded every item seized in the winter action: plates, proofs, notebooks, instruments, the atlas itself under its numbered seal. Pell had been cross-referencing the inventory against the paper stock manifests, a tedious duty Brase had assigned him as punishment for some earlier sloppiness, and the numbers had refused to balance. The bureau held eleven reams of Vey's proofing paper by manifest. It held ten by count.

One ream of cartographer's paper, unaccounted for. Perhaps fifty sheets.

"It could be miscounted," Pell said quickly, as though hoping to be told so. "Paper walks, sir. Everyone knows paper walks in a warehouse. But then I pulled the delivery chits, and there's a chit from December — signed by the apprentice, C. Thornbury — for replacement stock. Which means she knew there was a shortfall. Which means she knew what was missing before we did, sir, and she ordered more to cover it."

"To cover it," Casper repeated.

"For printing, sir." Pell's voice dropped to a whisper, as if the shelves themselves might inform on them. "She's been printing. There's a boy at the tide-gate chandlery who sells lamp oil to the workshop, and he told my cousin he'd seen the shutters lit past midnight, twice a week since the new year, and heard the press. And sir — my cousin's cousin is in the watch, and he was on patrol along the harbor road two nights ago when he stopped a man half-frozen near the gate. Foreign-looking. Didn't have papers on him worth the name. Before the sergeant could run him in, a woman came out of the mapshop and claimed him — said he was her journeyman, come down from the northern counties, and vouched for him, and the watch let it go because the watch lets everything go if a woman cries at them. But the man limped, sir. Badly. Like a soldier. And he went inside the workshop and didn't come out."

The basement was very quiet. Water dripped somewhere in the dark between the shelving, patient as a clock.

"You believe," Casper said carefully, "that Miss Thornbury is harboring an unregistered foreigner."

"I believe," Pell said, miserable and triumphant at once, "that she is hiding him, feeding him, and printing with bureau-seized materials, and I am required to report all of it, sir, and I've written it up here, and I don't want to. I like her, sir. She sold me a map of the fishing grounds once when I couldn't afford it and marked it down herself and pretended it had always been that price." He held out the folder with both hands, an offering and an accusation. "But the colonel said anything touching that workshop goes straight up, and I'm not going to hang for her."

*No*, Casper thought, taking the folder. *You're going to hang her with it and call it duty.* But he looked at the boy's face — the genuine anguish in it, the terrible relief of having handed the weight to someone else — and could not even hate him. Twenty years old, ink-stained, terrified. They built the empire out of such boys, one small honest report at a time.

"You did right, Clerk," he heard himself say. "Leave it with me. I'll take it to the colonel personally."

Pell saluted, hesitated, and said, "Sir — whatever happens. I'm sorry." Then he fled up the stairs, and Casper was alone with the folder and the drip of water and the sound of his own heart arranging itself into a new and unfamiliar rhythm.

* * *

Colonel Brase received the report in his office above, where the windows overlooked the harbor and a fire burned as though fuel cost nothing, which for men like Brase it did not. He read it twice. He wore spectacles now for reading, which softened his face into something almost gentle, and Casper had learned never to trust the softening.

"One ream," Brase said. "Fifty sheets. You audited her yourself, Lieutenant. Twice. How does a ream of paper walk out from under your nose?"

"The inventory was taken before I was assigned, sir. If there was a shortfall at seizure—"

"There wasn't. I checked the original manifest myself an hour ago." Brase set the report down and squared its corners with one finger, a habit he had, making the world neat before he acted upon it. "Which means the paper was there when we sealed the plates, and gone afterward. Which means someone in this building signed it back out, or someone in that workshop took it after our seal was on the door." He removed the spectacles and looked at Casper with eyes like cleared weather. "You've been soft on her."

"She's the finest cartographer in Vellmark, Colonel, and Vey's heir. Humiliating her without cause costs us the atlas expertise we confiscated the thing to acquire."

"That is a reason," Brase agreed pleasantly, "not an answer." He rose and went to the window, hands clasped behind him, watching the harbor ice. Below, gulls quarreled over something dead on the quay. "I met Aldous Vey once. Years ago. He came to the bureau to protest the Kelvern survey — stood in that room where you're standing and told me his maps were being altered and that history would remember who allowed it. I told him history is written by the bureau that funds it." A thin smile. "He didn't take it well. Stubborn man. Brilliant, and stubborn, and wrong about how the world works. Or right about it, I suppose, which amounts to the same problem."

Casper said nothing. In the fire, a knot of resin popped and flared.

"He's dead, and his maps won the war's justification anyway, and none of that matters now. What matters is this." Brase turned from the window. "If the Thornbury woman is printing from our plates, she isn't printing fishing charts. Paper doesn't lie, Lieutenant, but people do, and she has had three weeks and a midnight visitor to decide which she is. We raid the workshop at dawn tomorrow. Full action. I'll want your survey of the premises — you know the layout, you conducted the audit, you'll lead the entry team."

"Sir." The word came out level. Casper marveled at it from somewhere outside himself, the way a man might marvel at his own hand continuing to write while his thoughts ran for the door. "If she's innocent—"

"Then we apologize, compensate her for the door, and gain a loyal asset frightened into usefulness. If she's guilty, we've caught a traitor and a spy in one net. Dawn serves either way." Brase picked the report back up, folded it, and slid it into his desk with the finality of a coin into a lockbox. "There is one more matter. If she is guilty, Lieutenant, she did not become so alone. Someone audited her twice and found nothing. Someone walked those premises and saw no press, no stranger, no midnight lamplight. I find myself wondering whether that someone was deceived or chose not to see."

The fire ticked. The gulls cried. Somewhere below their feet, the archive held its breath in the dark.

"I saw what was there to see, sir," Casper said.

"See that you keep seeing it." Brase sat, and became suddenly, horribly kind. "You're a good officer, Ilyen. Better than this city deserves. Go home. Sleep. Lead your team at first light."

* * *

He did not go home.

He walked. That was the truth of what remained to him — that he walked, out of the customs house, along the harbor road, past the fish market where women scaled silver mountains with quick red hands, past the chandlery where a boy who sold lamp oil would not meet his eye, past the tide gate where the watch had turned back a limping stranger because a woman had wept at them. He walked with Brase's folded report sitting in his memory like a stone in a coat pocket, and the afternoon went gray, and the lamps were lit one by one along the quay by a man on a ladder who worked with the bored efficiency of someone who had done nothing else for forty years and would do it forty more, and Casper understood that he was approaching something slowly, the way one approaches a frozen pond in darkness — testing each step, hearing it hold, daring the next.

At the corner of Lantern Row he stopped beneath the sign of Thornbury & Vey, Cartographers, and looked up at the workshop windows.

They were dark.

Of course they were dark. Rell would not risk the lamp again; the chandlery boy had already talked. Dark windows, a locked door, a sealed bureau stamp over the lock that he himself had pressed and signed and countersigned three weeks ago, in another life, in a uniform that had still fit. Behind that glass, if Pell Marsh was right, a wounded courier slept and a woman he had loved since before he had words for it sat bending over stolen paper by the light of a shuttered candle, committing treason one impression at a time.

He tried to construct the alternative. It would not construct. He had audited her honestly — God help him, he had *looked* — and he had found nothing because she had hidden it well, and the fact that she had hidden it well was itself the confession. Fifty sheets of proofing paper. Midnight presses. A limping man who entered the workshop and did not come out. Every fact fit together the way good cartography fits together: seamlessly, inevitably, and damning of any hand that had falsified it.

And dawn was coming. Dawn always came; it was the one border no one could smuggle across.

He thought of Brase's kindness and understood it precisely. The colonel had given him the entry team not as an honor but as a test and a trap: if Casper led the raid cleanly, he was clean. If he faltered, he was implicated. If he warned her — the thought arrived and stood in the street with him, patient as the lamplighter — if he warned her, then he was not merely soft, he was a traitor to the Ninth Bureau in wartime, and the tribunal for that offense sat behind a door in the customs house he passed every morning, and the sentences it wrote were read aloud on the quay.

His career. His name. The commission his father had mortgaged the house to buy him. The whole architecture of the man he had been permitted to become — and on the other side of the scale, one ream of paper, one woman, one truth.

*It shouldn't even be difficult,* he thought, and was ashamed, and was freed by the shame, because shame meant he already knew which way the scale tipped and had known it for some time, perhaps since the day he had sealed her door and watched her face over the wax and said *for the duration* as though duration were a mercy instead of a sentence.

He knocked. Three soft raps, the old signal, the one they'd used as children at each other's shutters across the alley — two for come out, three for *are you awake, are you there, is anyone home.*

Silence. Then, faint, the scrape of a chair. Footsteps, stopping short of the door. Her voice, low and guarded, through the wood:

"Who is it?"

"Coral." He closed his eyes and leaned his forehead against the cold panel, against the place where her hand might be on the other side. "It's Casper. Open the door. I haven't much time, and God forgive me — you have less."

The bolt slid back.

Inside, the candle was shielded behind a tin plate, and by its light he could see the proofs spread across the worktable, the press with its roller still faintly inked, and Rell rising from the corner shadows with a knife already open in his hand — and Casper stepped through the doorway of his old life and pulled the door shut behind him, and heard, very clearly, in the space of that single closing click, the sound of dawn arriving early.

"They come at first light," he said. "You have until then. And Coral — you cannot be here when they come."
