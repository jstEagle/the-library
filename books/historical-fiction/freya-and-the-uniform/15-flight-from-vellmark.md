## 15. Flight from Vellmark

The note came folded inside a delivery of goose feathers, which was absurd enough that Coral almost laughed before she understood.

She had ordered no feathers. The workshop had not used goose quills in two years — she cut her pens from reed, as Master Vey had taught her, because reed took ink honestly and goose did not. The parcel was left on the step by a boy who would not meet her eyes, and when she slit the twine the note fell out into the lamplight, small and square and written in the careful upright hand she knew better than her own name.

*They come at dawn. The Salt Row gate is watched after six. Go now. Burn nothing that matters; carry everything that does. — C.*

Beneath it, drawn small as a grain of rice, the old surveyor's mark he had used on their student exercises: a triangle over water, the sign for safe passage.

Coral stood very still in the middle of the workshop, listening to the building settle around her, to Rell's uneven breathing from the loft where he dozed propped against her old chart rolls, to the wind coming off the strait with ice in its teeth. Dawn was four hours away. The words *they come at dawn* seemed to hang in the air like smoke, refusing to disperse.

She thought, with a strange detached clarity, of how many times in her life she had risen before dawn to work. How many first lights she had spent hunched over a plate, chasing a coastline toward truth. She had never once spent one running from the city that raised her.

Then she climbed the ladder and put her hand over Rell's mouth before he could wake enough to cry out.

"Get up," she whispered. "We have until dawn."

* * *

They worked in silence, and the silence had a texture to it, dense and woolen, like felt pressed over the ears. Rell dressed clumsily, his wound still pulling at him, and Coral moved through the workshop touching things as she went — not lingering, not weeping, only touching, the way a sailor touches the rail of a ship being scuttled. The burnisher she had held since she was fourteen. Vey's chair, its cane seat worn to the shape of him. The great table scarred with twenty years of compass points, each one a small wound in the wood where a truth had been anchored long enough to be traced.

She could take none of it. That was the arithmetic Casper's note had forced upon her: everything that mattered could be weighed in ounces or not carried at all.

The maps were already sewn. She had done it three nights ago, alone, without admitting even to herself that she was doing it, sitting by the stove with a sailmaker's needle and waxed thread while Rell slept. Into the lining of her heavy coat, between wool and oilcloth, she had laid the corrected sheets of Kelvern Pass — six leaves of Vey's true border, folded to the size of a prayer book, flat against her ribs. When she buttoned the coat now, she felt them there like a second heartbeat, like something swallowed that refused to go down.

Rell paused at the door with the dispatch papers wrapped in oilskin against his chest, and looked back at the workshop the way men look back at shoreline.

"Two years I imagined this room," he said quietly. "Vey talked about it. The light off the harbor at noon, he said. He said you kept his ink pots in order better than he ever had."

"He lied," Coral said. "I re-ordered them constantly behind his back."

Rell smiled — the first true smile she had seen from him since the tide gate — and then the smile went out like a pinched wick, because somewhere across the rooftops a whistle blew. One short note. Not near. Not yet. But moving.

"The Salt Row gate is watched after six," Coral said. "It isn't six yet."

"Then let's spend the hour well."

* * *

Vellmark at four in the morning was a city holding its breath under snow. They went down through the tanner's yard behind the workshop, where the smell of lye covered their passing from any dog, and out along the drainage channel to the lower lanes, moving in the blue-black hour when the lamps had guttered and the lamplighters had not yet begun their rounds. Coral had walked these streets since childhood, but she had never walked them like this — reading them, the way she read a proof sheet, hunting for errors that would cost everything. Here a corner too open. There a window where a light had no business burning. The city she loved had become a document full of mistakes, and she was the flaw in it, fleeing through its margins.

Twice they heard patrols. Once, near the cooperage, boots came down a lane they needed to cross, and Rell pulled her into the black mouth of an archway so close she could feel the heat of fever coming off him, his heartbeat rapid and irregular against her shoulder blade. The boots passed. A lantern swung its slow arc across the cobbles, touched the edge of the archway, slid away.

"They've increased the watch," Rell breathed when it was gone. "That's the third pair since the channel."

"Brase doesn't double the watch for a stolen atlas."

"No," Rell said. "He doubles it when he knows the courier is still in the city." His voice had gone flat and cold, the voice of a man doing sums he hated. "Coral. If your lieutenant's note reached you, it means he chose. Men like him don't get to choose twice."

She did not answer. There was nothing to say that wasn't a prayer, and she had stopped praying somewhere over Kelvern Pass, bent over proofs, measuring the width of a lie.

They came down onto Salt Row as the sky began to turn — not light yet, but the stars thinning overhead like a fabric wearing through, the eastern dark going grey-green, then the color of old pewter. The fishing fleet sat low in the harbor water, riding lamps swaying, and the smell of tar and salt and herring blood rose up to meet them, the smell of every working morning of her life. At the foot of the row, past the net-sheds, the ferry stairs descended into black water where a boat waited: a lean, ugly craft Rell had bought three days ago from a Smuggler's Guild man with a face like a closed fist, paid for with the last of the gold sewn into his boot heel.

Twenty steps from the stairs, the whistle blew again — and this time it was near, and this time it was answered.

Coral spun. At the head of Salt Row, lanterns. Four of them, five, swinging fast, and beneath them the grey shapes of men in bureau grey spreading out across the top of the street in a line, unhurried, the line of men who know the net is already closed. And stepping ahead of them, taller than the rest, his greatcoat collar up against the wind —

Casper.

Her heart did something complicated and painful, folding in on itself like a map creased wrong. He was walking at the center of the raiding party. Of course he was. He would not have been allowed to stay behind, not on a night like this. She saw him lift his lantern, sweep its beam deliberately wide, left, along the empty face of the net-sheds — while his body, quite precisely, blocked the sightline of the sergeant beside him for exactly the seconds they needed.

"Down," Rell hissed, and pulled her behind a stack of crab pots reeking of brine and death. Through the slats she watched the lanterns. Watched Casper stop at the top of the ferry stairs, hold his light high, call out something to the sergeant — she could not hear the words, only the shape of them, official, unhurried. Ordering a methodical search. Room by room. Shed by shed.

Buying minutes. Spending them like coins from a purse that would not refill.

"Go," Rell whispered. "Now, while they think the stairs are clear."

They ran bent-double along the waterline, boots silent in wet shingle, and came up the ferry stairs on the blind side, and the little boat was there, and the Smuggler's Guild man was not — gone, run off at the whistles, leaving them the painter tied and the tide right and nothing else. Rell got over the side badly, his wound tearing, and Coral heard him bite down on the sound. She cast off the painter and stepped in after him and the boat swung out on the ebb, and the harbor opened before them, black and enormous, and Vellmark fell away behind them stone by stone.

From the top of Salt Row, faint across the water, she heard shouting. Then a single command, sharp and furious. Then — quieter, carried on the offshore wind the way sound carries over cold water — a voice she knew, level and formal, answering questions.

She looked back. Lanterns clustered at the ferry stairs. One of them stood apart from the others, arms bound — she could not see that far, could not truly see anything but a shape among shapes — but she knew, the way she knew a forged hand from a true one, that the shape was Casper's, and that he was standing straight, and that he had known, when he wrote *go now*, exactly what the ink would cost him.

"Coral." Rell's hand was on her wrist. "Coral, look at me. You can't go back. Whatever you're doing in your head right now — you can't."

"I'm not going back." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"What it costs." She turned her face toward the strait, toward the north and the dark water and the ice beyond it, and found that her eyes were dry, and that the dryness itself felt like a kind of vow. "He gave us an hour. I intend to make the hour worth more than they can take from him."

Rell said nothing to that. He took the oars, shipping them carefully, and began to pull them out past the moored fleet, past the harbor bell, out toward the open grey water where the current took hold. Behind them the city woke in patches — a lit window here, smoke from a bakery chimney there — utterly ordinary, utterly unaware that it had just finished becoming a place she could no longer live in.

* * *

By full light they were a mile off shore, and Vellmark had resolved itself into the skyline she had drawn a hundred times: the customs tower, the spire of Saint Ferren, the long grey spine of the war bureau with its new flagpole standing bare against the pale sky. Coral sat in the stern with the tiller line in her hand, watching it shrink, and made herself memorize it — not as it appeared in her plates, idealized and fair, but as it was: beautiful and mistaken. A city that had redrawn its own conscience and called the result accurate.

Rell shipped the oars to rest, breathing hard, his face grey. "There'll be a price on us by noon. Brase doesn't lose a prisoner and a courier in one night and file no report."

"I know."

"And your lieutenant—" Rell hesitated, choosing words the way one chooses footing on rotten ice. "If they offer him a way out, it will be to give you up. Name you. Denounce what he did tonight."

"He won't."

"You're certain?"

Casper's handwriting rose in her memory, upright and careful, the surveyor's triangle beneath it. *Carry everything that matters.* No man writes those words casually. But she heard her own voice answer anyway, quiet over the water:

"He measured the maps, Rell. For ten years he measured them beside me. Somewhere in all that measuring he learned the difference between a border and a lie. He'll hold to it. It's the only thing tonight I'm certain of."

Rell studied her for a long moment, then nodded slowly and took up the oars again. The boat climbed the long grey swell, and the strait opened ahead of them, thirty miles of winter water, and beyond it — further than the water, further than the lines on any map — the frozen crossing, and Tam, and the promise.

Coral put her hand flat against her coat, against the paper lying over her heart, six folded leaves warm from her own body. An hour purchased. A debt incurred.

She fixed her eyes on the north and steered into the cold.

Behind her, growing smaller on the pewter horizon, the war bureau's flagpole stood empty against the sky — and above the strait, far off where sea and weather blurred together, she could just make out the white line of the ice, waiting.
