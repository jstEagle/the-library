## 17. Enemy Lines

The road down from the hills was a scar of mud and thawing snow, and Coral walked it as though she had always meant to walk it — as though she were nothing more than what her papers claimed: a refugee mapmaker out of Vellmark, fleeing the requisitions and the bread queues like ten thousand others that winter. Her coat hung heavy on her shoulders with more than cold. The corrected maps lay against her ribs where Rell had sewn them into the lining, flat and patient as sleeping birds, and every step of the eleven miles from the strait's far shore pressed them a little closer to her heart.

She had buried Rell on the ice at dawn, or as near to burying as frozen ground and frozen grief would allow. She did not let herself think of it now. Thinking of it opened something in her chest that could not be closed again while there were still miles to walk and soldiers ahead, so she folded him away the way she had learned to fold everything — Master Vey's hands on a brass rule, the atlas plates going into bureau crates, Casper's face the last time she had seen it across a lamplit table. Folded small. Carried forward.

Kelvern showed itself first as smoke, then as roofs, then as a garrison wall rising gray above the town like the spine of some enormous animal. The enemy's flag hung motionless over it in the windless afternoon, and Coral studied the flag rather than the wall, because studying things was what she did, because if she looked at the flag long enough she would not have to look at the fact that this was the flag her master's maps had been used to fight, and that she had walked eleven days to stand beneath it voluntarily.

*The lines are drawn by men who cannot hold a pen steady,* Vey used to say, bending over her shoulder in the workshop while she inked coastlines. *You can see the trembling in every border ever put to paper, girl. Find the trembling and you find the lie.*

She found the trembling now in her own hands, and hid them in her pockets.

* * *

The gate to Kelvern was not a gate so much as a checkpoint — sandbags, a stovepipe smoking from a guard hut, two sentries whose greatcoats had been cut for men broader than they were. A queue of refugees waited before it, and Coral joined the queue's tail with the practiced slouch of exhaustion, which required no practice at all. Ahead of her a woman carried a birdcage with no bird in it. Behind her, a man recited names under his breath like a rosary. Nobody spoke to anybody. Cold made each of them a country of one.

"Name," said the sentry when her turn came. He had a clerk's voice, incongruous in a soldier's throat, and a ledger open on a plank across two barrels.

"Coral Vey."

She had decided on the name three rivers back. It was half hers already; she had earned it in a workshop for two years of ink-stained mornings. But saying it aloud here, under this flag, felt like laying her master's ghost on the barrels between herself and the rifle.

The sentry wrote it without looking up. "Occupation?"

"Mapmaker." She let the word sit a moment. "Journeyman cartographer. Trained under Aldous Vey of Vellmark, if any of that means anything here."

Her heart was behaving disgracefully. She kept her eyes level and her voice bored, the way one spoke about weather and ration cards, and watched the sentry's pen slow, stop, lift.

"Aldous Vey." He said the name the way one tests a coin with one's teeth. "The surveyor."

"There was a surveyor by that name," Coral said carefully. "He died two years ago. I finished his work. I'd like that work to be of use to someone now, since it was of no use to me."

It was close enough to true that it came out smooth, and lying had become easier since Brase's dinner, though she still hated the taste. The sentry studied her — cracked boots, ink-ruined fingers, the particular squint of someone who had spent years leaning over fine lines by lamplight. Whatever he saw satisfied him or failed to interest him.

"Wait there."

He disappeared into the hut. The queue shuffled behind her. The birdcage woman coughed. Somewhere beyond the wall, bugles sounded the change of watch, and Coral thought absurdly of Casper, who could never remember which call was which and had once confessed it to her in the dark of the workshop doorway, laughing at himself, his breath fogging the glass.

The sentry returned with an officer: a captain, young through the cheeks and old through the eyes, frost on his collar where his breath had frozen.

"You knew Aldous Vey."

"I apprenticed under him. Two years after his death I finished the atlas he died making. Then the war bureau took it." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than the walk had. "I'm told you people are at war with the people who took it. That seemed worth walking to."

The captain's face did not change. "And you came to Kelvern — a fortified garrison city, on the direct order of its commandant closed to unregistered civilians — to offer your services."

"I came to deliver something," Coral said. "To Major Tam Vey, if he's stationed here. His father was my master."

The silence that followed had a texture to it, like the hush before ice cracks. The captain looked at her for a long moment, and then past her, at the queue, at the road behind, at the empty white hills she had crossed with a dead man's maps sewn against her body — and Coral understood, with the slow cold certainty of water finding its level, exactly what she must look like to him.

A refugee. A mapmaker. A woman asking for an enemy-of-nothing major by name, claiming kinship of craft with a dead surveyor whose work had started the very war this garrison existed to fight. Eleven days ago the story had been a coat she wore. Now she saw it from the outside, and it fit her like a spy's confession.

"Major Vey is with the army," the captain said. "Not here."

"Then I'll wait for him here."

"Will you." Not a question. He turned to the sentry. "Take her to the provost. Register her as a detainee pending verification. Separate her effects."

*Separate her effects.* Four words, spoken like folding a letter shut, and Coral's whole body went rigid around the lining of her coat.

"They're just maps," she said, and heard how badly the lie rang. "Drafts. Studies. My master's teaching exercises—"

"Then they'll be returned," the captain said, "when we've confirmed that. Walk ahead of the sergeant, please, madam. Hands visible."

* * *

They did not take the coat.

That was the only mercy in the hour that followed, and afterward Coral understood it had not been mercy but oversight — the search room was understaffed and overflowing, a warehouse annex smelling of wet wool and lamp oil, and the corporal who processed her was more interested in the queue outside than in a thin woman's threadbare outerwear. They took her satchel: the proofs she carried openly, harmless teaching sheets she had prepared for exactly this eventuality, blank paper, her pens, her tin of ink that the corporal held up to the light with genuine distaste before setting it aside. They took her papers, examined the refugee pass Rell had forged with a forger's love, found it good. They patted down her sleeves and waist. They did not think to run their hands along the inside seam of a coat's lining, because coats were for wearing, not for carrying wars.

Coral stood very still and thought about breathing.

The proofs in the satchel were decoys — Vey's early exercises, coastlines and river systems, nothing of borders at all. She had chosen them on the ice, methodically, kneeling over Rell's body while the sky went gray, because even then some cold cartographer in her head had been drawing contingency lines. If they take the satchel, they take nothing. If they take you, they cannot take what is sewn into wool. She had not expected to be grateful to her own foresight. Gratitude felt obscene in a room where somewhere upstairs a man named Rell was being written into a ledger as *unknown male, deceased, strait crossing* — if anyone had written him at all.

They put her in a room with a table, a chair, and a barred window looking out on a courtyard where soldiers were stacking firewood with the weary rhythm of men who had done nothing else all winter. No one came for two hours. She counted the firewood logs to keep from counting anything else — four hundred and six — and then, because she was who she was and would be who she was until they shot her or didn't, she began mentally surveying the courtyard. Sight lines. Drainage. The slope of the ground toward the river quarter, which meant, which meant, her mind supplied unasked, that the old town stood below the waterline of the mill pond, which meant flood defenses, which meant—

*Stop.* She pressed her palms against her eyes. *You are a prisoner, Coral. Stop mapping your prison.*

But the habit of twenty years did not stop. It was the only thing the war had left intact.

* * *

The interrogator came at dusk. He was not what she expected — not brute, not wolfish, but a gray, tidy man with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, carrying a folder under his arm like a schoolmaster late for lessons. He set it on the table, sat, aligned it with the table's edge, and only then looked at her.

"Coral Vey. Journeyman cartographer, Vellmark." He read it as though checking sums. "Apprenticed to Aldous Vey. You'll forgive me, madam, but your timing interests me. In eighteen months of war, no one has come across the strait to offer us that name. Now, in the same week our army reaches the approaches to Kelvern Pass, a young woman arrives requesting Major Vey by name and claiming to carry his legacy." He tapped the folder once. "You understand my difficulty."

"The pass," Coral said. Her mouth was dry. "What about the pass?"

His eyes came up over the folder, mild and terrible. "I believe I asked the questions here."

*The same week.* The words rang in her like a struck bell. The enemy army was moving toward Kelvern — toward the pass her master had measured honestly and the bureau had redrawn falsely, the pass whose falsified border was the wound the whole war bled from. And she had arrived, at this exact confluence of events, carrying the truth of it in her coat. If she had been designing evidence against herself, she could not have improved upon her own life.

"I'm not a spy," she said. "I know everyone says that. I know it means nothing coming from a woman sitting where I'm sitting. So let me say instead what I am." She leaned forward, and her hands, which had trembled at the gate, were suddenly steady, because this — this was the one thing left that she was sure of. "I am the person who finished Aldous Vey's atlas. I spent two years inking plates by lamplight while the city that killed him pretended to honor him. And then the war bureau seized the atlas, burned his notebooks in the Fishmarket square, and used his life's work to justify a border they invented. I have proof of that. I walked eleven days in the dead of winter to bring that proof to his son, because I promised him I would, when borders were still things we believed in. Do with me what you like. But Tam Vey should hear his father's name from me before anyone hears it from you."

The interrogator regarded her for a long moment. Steam rose faintly off his tea, untouched.

"That," he said finally, "is either the truth, or the best-prepared cover story I have heard in this war. And I will tell you the trouble with the best-prepared cover stories, madam." He removed his glasses, polished them on a handkerchief, put them back. "They are always prepared by professionals. And professionals never make the mistake you've just made."

"Which mistake?"

"They assume the person they're asking for will want to see them." He closed the folder. "Major Vey has been informed of your claim. He is with the army group, as Captain Ferren told you, and the army group is engaged. Whether he comes — whether he *can* be spared to come and look at the face of a woman who says she carries his father's legacy across a frozen sea — is not my decision, and may not be his." He rose, gathered the folder, paused at the door with the tired courtesy of a man who had done this too many times to enjoy it. "You'll be kept comfortable, madam. Consider, meanwhile, what a court-martial makes of coincidences. And consider, too—" he glanced back, almost gently, "—that if even half of what you said about falsified borders is true, you are holding the most dangerous documents on this continent. There are people on *both* sides of the strait who would prefer such documents never reached daylight. Some of them wear our uniform."

The door shut. The bolt slid home with a sound like a ruler laid flat.

* * *

Night came down over Kelvern and filled the courtyard window with a darkness pricked by garrison lamps. Coral sat on the cot in her confiscated coat, arms wrapped around the secret sewn against her ribs, and listened to the sounds of an enemy fortress settling into sleep — boots, a laugh, the creak of the firewood stack settling, a sentry's slow rounds.

*Keeping a promise to the wrong side of a line.*

Rell had warned her, in his stubborn courier's way, on one of the last nights before the strait: *The maps don't care who holds them, Coral. People do. And people on the other end of a promise don't check which flag you carried it under.* She had believed she understood him. She had not understood that understanding would feel like this — like being inked onto the wrong plate of her own atlas, a feature placed precisely, deliberately, in enemy territory.

Somewhere out there, beyond the dark hills, an army was moving toward Kelvern Pass, and her master's son was with it — the boy who had made her swear an oath in a sunlit workshop in 1895, when the border on the wall was still a lie waiting to be told. She had crossed a frozen sea to reach him. The sea had taken Rell. The war had taken the route, the reason, nearly the truth itself. And now the last mile — the only mile that mattered — was guarded by men who thought she was a spy, dependent on the chance that a major she hadn't seen in five years would be pulled from a battlefield to vouch for a woman the evidence condemned.

She drew the coat tighter and felt the papers shift against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Toward midnight, boots stopped outside her door. Keys turned. The gray interrogator stood in the frame, lamplight behind him, and though his face gave away nothing, something in the set of his shoulders had changed — the change of a man carrying news he had not expected to carry.

"Madam," he said. "It appears Major Vey has requested, in writing, through channels I will not describe, to meet the woman claiming to be his father's apprentice." A pause. "He is sending a escort. It arrives tomorrow."

He hesitated, and then, quietly, almost against his own profession's judgment:

"He asked one question before agreeing. He asked what you were carrying." The interrogator's eyes rested on her coat, then rose to her face. "I told him I hadn't searched thoroughly enough to say. See to it, madam, that whatever happens tomorrow — I am not made a liar."

The door closed again, and Coral sat alone in the dark with her heartbeat and her maps, understanding that the promise she had carried across a frozen strait was now, at last, one night away from the man it had been made for — and that everything she had survived to deliver it might still burn, if Tam Vey looked at her tomorrow and saw not his father's apprentice, but the enemy the uniform said she was.
