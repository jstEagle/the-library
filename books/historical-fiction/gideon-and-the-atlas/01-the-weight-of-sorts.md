## 1. The Weight of Sorts

The world, Cyrus Eastwick had learned before they learned to read it, could be measured in ems and picas.

An em was the width of the letter M in any given size of type — a small square of space, patient as a held breath. A pica was twelve ems. Twelve picas made an inch, and an inch was only the world pretending to be something larger than type. Cyrus's father had taught them this at six years old, holding a brass rule to the morning light slanting through the shop's eastern window, saying: *Everything that happens, happens somewhere on a page. And every page has margins you can hold between your fingers.* It was, Aldous Eastwick believed, the closest a person could come to touching the bones of fate — to know precisely how much room a grief required, or a birth notice, or a war.

Cyrus rose before dawn because the shop demanded it, and because dawn in Marrow's Bend belonged to them alone: the gray hour when the river fog pressed its flat palm against the panes and the town still smelled of last night's banked fires. They dressed in the dark by touch — the wool waistcoat first, then the coat with the ink-stained cuff they had never let Wren scrub, then their boots, laced tight over feet that had stopped growing sideways years ago from standing at the stone. In the looking glass by the door, a shape assembled itself that was neither the daughter Aldous's mother had expected nor the son the town had once assumed. Cyrus looked at the shape a moment, adjusted the collar, and did not look again. The name had been their own idea, cut and set like a piece of type: *Cyrus*, chosen at fourteen from a book of Persian kings, meaning *the sun*. Their father had cast the sorts himself — a private alphabet, one word long — and printed the name on a slip of paper Cyrus still kept folded inside their watch case, soft as cloth now from handling.

Down the stairs, where the air turned thick and familiar as a hand laid over the mouth: lamp oil, tallow, the vinegar bite of the damping water, and under all of it the deep iron smell of molten lead — the smell, Cyrus had always thought, of things being decided.

* * *

Eastwick & Sons said the sign above the door, painted by a man long dead who had not thought to ask why the family had only daughters. Aldous had declined to correct him. "Sons," he told Cyrus once, tapping the faded letters, "is a category of usefulness, not of sex." The sign had been relettered twice since, and twice the paint had peeled back toward its original lie, as though the wood itself remembered what the town expected of a household.

The shop occupied the ground floor of the house on Chandler's Lane, three rooms deep: the composing room where the cases stood in their sloped ranks like a congregation of small drawers; the press room, housing the great Stanhope that Aldous had bought secondhand from a bankrupt newspaperman in the capital, its iron arm polished by thirty years of pull-bar work to the color of dark honey; and the foundry at the back, half workshop, half kitchen-annex, where the pot of type-metal kept its low blue murmur through the working day. Above it all, up stairs that complained, slept the Eastwicks: Aldous and Cyrus in the front rooms facing the lane, Wren above them, and Tam at the very top beneath the eaves, which everyone agreed was fitting, since Tam had never once put down roots lower than a rooftop.

Cyrus lit the lamps, opened the shutters, and began the day as every day began: with the sorting of yesterday's distribution.

Type came apart after printing the way crowds came apart after a hanging — everyone returned to their place or did not, and it was the compositor's task to know which. Cyrus worked the case with both hands moving independently, eyes on the galley, fingers reading the letters by their notched shanks, dropping each sort into its compartment with a click that sounded, in the empty shop, like slow rain on a tin roof. *A* to the left upper. *E* beside it. The long *s* and the short, the italic swash caps in the row Aldous called Foreign Royalty because no ordinary sentence ever needed them. Cyrus knew the case the way other people knew their own hands — better, some mornings, when the hands were cold.

By lamplight the metal letters gleamed like a field of black ice, each one bearing its letter backward, so that the whole case was a country written in mirror-script, legible only from the far side of the world. Cyrus had been seven when they first understood that everything printed was written in reverse before it became itself — that truth, to be readable, had first to be reversed and pressed and endured. They had never entirely stopped thinking about it.

"You're talking to the sorts again."

Aldous stood in the doorway to his sleeping room, coat already on, white hair flattened on one side where the pillow had argued with it. He was sixty-one and carried himself like a man perpetually listening for a bell. His hands, enormous for the rest of him, hung at his sides with the particular looseness of tools set down carefully.

"I'm counting," Cyrus said. "There's a difference."

"Your mother counted out loud."

"My mother," Cyrus said, "counted money. There's a difference."

Aldous smiled the way he did when Cyrus quoted the dead woman back at him — a wincing pleasure, a coin with two faces. He crossed to the foundry and unlidded the metal pot, and the blue flame leapt up between them, and for a while neither spoke, because the first hour of the day in a print shop is a liturgy and does not require speech. Aldous took the mould from its hook, checked the jet against the light, and began casting: lever down, matrix home, thumb on the plunger, the *spat* of liquid lead finding the cavity, the flick of the wrist that sent the new-born sort clattering into the cooling tray. Cyrus knew the rhythm well enough to breathe with it. Every letter in every book ever printed had been born in exactly this unlovely spitting manner, they thought — history arriving one hot drop at a time.

"Today we finish the order for Halloway's granary notices," Aldous said, without looking up. "Then the gazette forms. Then—" he paused, and the pause had a hair of weight on it, "—I want to recut the twelves."

"The twelves? The body twelves are sound."

"They're worn. Look at your *e*. The counter's filling in." He held one up to the lamp, squinting. "We'll cut a fresh punch. You'll do the lowercase."

It was an honor disguised as a chore, and both of them knew it. Cutting a punch — the steel master-letter from which matrices and thence all type descended — was the crown of the trade, the thing Aldous had let Cyrus touch only in the last three years, and slowly, like a man teaching someone to handle a weapon he hoped would never be fired. Cyrus felt the familiar tightening under the breastbone, part pride, part dread, and said only, "The *e* first?"

"The *e* first. The eye of the alphabet." Aldous set the worn sort down and finally looked at his child across the blue murmur of the fire. Something moved behind his face — the shadow Cyrus had been watching gather there for weeks now, like weather coming upriver. "And Cyrus. When the paper comes, bring it to me before you read it yourself."

That was wrong. That was eleven years of wrongness, compressed into one sentence. The morning paper was Cyrus's by right of ritual: they fetched it from the door, read it standing, and read the shipping news aloud to Aldous while he ate. Cyrus searched their father's face for the joke and found none.

"Anything I should be braced for?" they asked lightly.

"No," Aldous said, which was how he said *yes*.

* * *

The morning paper arrived with the fog, more or less — delivered by Josiah Pell's boy, who left it folded on the step with the reverence the townsfolk had lately taken to showing anything printed in the capital. Cyrus brought it in, set it by Aldous's plate untouched as instructed, and went out to sweep the walk, which gave them time to be dishonest about their obedience. Through the window they watched their father open the sheet, unfold it to page two, and go very still in the particular way of a man reading bad news he had predicted.

Cyrus swept the same four yards of flagstone for a quarter hour.

When they finally came in, Aldous had folded the paper into quarters, a geometry of containment, and pushed it across the counter. On the front page, beneath the masthead Cyrus knew almost intimately — having once set a pirated copy of it as a joke, and been confined to the house for a week — ran the item:

*Reliable reports from Fort Mercer indicate the movement of two regiments of foot and a train of artillery toward the river settlements. The Ministry assures the public these are routine dispositions. Persons spreading contrary rumors will be answerable to the authorities.*

"Routine dispositions," Cyrus read aloud, flatly.

"That's what it says."

"Father. Two regiments is not a disposition, it's an occupation with the paperwork pending."

Aldous took off his spectacles and cleaned them on his sleeve, which bought him ten seconds, which he used. Below the fold — Cyrus saw it now, the reason for the stillness — a smaller item, unremarkable to any eye but one trained in this shop: a government notice, *requisitioning* printing services along the valley "for the public convenience," with compensation to be assessed by military commissioners. Set in eight-point Caslon. The very face Aldous sold to the capital papers by the hundredweight. Someone downriver had ordered type from them — or requisitioned a printer's stock entire — and the army was coming to collect what the valley produced.

"They'll want us," Cyrus said. Not a question. Marrow's Bend was the last press between the fort and the falls. Everything flowed past their door: grain, gossip, gunpowder, news.

"They'll want the press," Aldous said. "Whether they want *us* depends on what they find when they arrive." He refolded the paper along its original creases with the exactness of a man packing a wound. "Which is why we spend today making certain there's nothing to find. Nothing out of order. Nothing—" his eyes went, for less than a heartbeat, to the floorboards at the back of the foundry, and came away, "—irregular."

Cyrus caught the glance. Filed it. Their father had taught them to catch such things the way he'd taught them to kern: by example, endlessly, until the eye flinched where the error lived.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the river, and the sound of Marrow's Bend beginning its day drifted up Chandler's Lane: the ferryman's chain, the granary bell, someone's dog conducting its ancient argument with the tide. An ordinary morning. Cyrus had lived twenty-six years of them in this town, invisible in plain sight — the Eastwick middle child, the one who ran ledgers and cut type and answered to nothing so fixed as *miss* or *master*, useful enough to be tolerated, strange enough to be forgotten. They had built the invisibility themselves, plank by plank, and called it freedom.

But the paper sat on the counter with its creases sharp as a fold in sheet lead, and the words *two regiments* sat in Cyrus's stomach like a swallowed sort, and the river below the town — the great gray atlas of their whole world — lay under its fog with its ice going rotten a month early, groaning at night in its sleep.

* * *

They worked. That was the answer to dread, always, and both of them knew it: the granary notices first, set solid in long primer, then the gazette forms locked and planed. At midmorning Aldous heated the blank steel and handed Cyrus the graver, and they began the *e* — the eye of the alphabet, the counter to be opened with a single confident stroke, no going back once steel gave up its first shaving.

Cyrus worked an hour before speaking.

"The floorboards in the foundry," they said, eyes on the punch, voice level as a rule-edge. "Third from the wall. You've been walking around them like a man avoiding church."

The graver whispered through steel. Behind them, the pot murmured. For a long moment Cyrus thought their father would deny it, would laugh, would say *mind your counter, the shoulder's too thin* — all the comfortable deflections they had traded for twenty years.

Instead Aldous Eastwick said quietly, "Finish the *e*. Then we'll talk."

And downriver, faint under the ordinary noises of the morning, Cyrus heard — or imagined hearing — the sound of many boots keeping step on frozen ground, coming north with the thaw.
