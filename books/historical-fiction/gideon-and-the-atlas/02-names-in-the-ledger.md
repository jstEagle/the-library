## 2. Names in the Ledger

The ledger weighed nine pounds, four ounces. Cyrus knew this because they had weighed it once, on a Tuesday in November when the shop was empty and the question had come to them unbidden — *how much does a town weigh?* — and the only honest answer had been sitting open on the counter, its calf-skin cover gone the color of strong tea, its spine stitched twice where the pages had grown too many for the first sewing to hold.

Nine pounds, four ounces. That was Marrow's Bend. That was every soul who owed a debt or claimed a credit, every birth and burial and bargain struck at the counter below the type room window, all of it pressed into paper that smelled faintly of tallow and river damp.

Cyrus opened it now with both hands, the way one opens something sacred, and dipped the pen.

Morning came late to the shop in winter. The light had to cross the whole breadth of the river first, picking up mist on the way, so that it arrived already softened, gray as wash-water, pooling on the composing stone without conviction. Cyrus had learned to work in this half-light the way other people learned to work in silence. The nib found its groove. The ink, mixed the night before — lampblack, linseed, a whisper of turpentine that made the whole back room smell of headaches and industry — sat in its tin like a small dark pond waiting to be disturbed.

*Harrow & Sons, flour, 12s 6d, carried forward.* The Harrows bought their flour from the mill upcounty but their sacks, their labels, their broadside advertisements from the Eastwicks, and they paid the way most families in Marrow's Bend paid: slowly, partially, in goods and promises. Old Harrow had settled his last account with a barrel of salt pork and an apology. His son would settle his with something else; Cyrus could feel it in the way the man's name sat on the page now, heavier than ink had any right to be.

They wrote the town's names in a hand their father had taught them — Aldous holding Cyrus's wrist in his callused fingers when they were seven, saying *no, smaller, smaller, the letters must leave room for the life around them* — and by now the hand was so thoroughly theirs that looking at the ledger was like looking in a mirror that showed only what they had made of themselves.

Downstairs, or rather across the shop, for the ledger lived on the counter where business happened, Aldous was cutting. Cyrus could hear him before seeing him: the thin bright complaint of the graver against steel, three strokes, a pause, two more. He worked the way rivers worked, apparently idle, actually relentless. This morning he was cutting a new pica capital R, having declared over breakfast that the foundry's stock had gone soft, *all legs and no spine*, and that if the newspapers of this country were to say anything worth hearing, the letters would have to be cut by someone who meant them.

Cyrus did not yet know, would not know for another week, how much meaning those words would come to carry. This morning the R was only an R, and the morning was only a morning, and the rumor that had arrived folded inside yesterday's paper — soldiers massing downriver, requisition carts, a colonel's name nobody recognized — was still small enough to fold back up and put away.

*Born to Merrit and Annalee Cole, a daughter, the 9th, named Susannah.* Cyrus recorded the birth in the ledger's second section, beneath the debts, because their father had insisted on the order years ago: *what you owe first, what you make second, and never confuse the two*. The Coles had traded a side of venison for the announcement card, fifty printed cards on cream stock with a dove-cut border Aldous kept for exactly such occasions. Somewhere in a house on Tanner's Lane, Susannah Cole existed now not only in flesh but in print, her name multiplied fifty times over, sent out to aunts and cousins and God knew who else. That was the power of the shop, quiet and total: nothing in Marrow's Bend became official until it passed across this counter and under this press. Weddings, deaths, debts, deeds. The town told itself stories about itself, and Cyrus wrote them down, and the writing made them true.

Their own name appeared in the ledger too, though never quite the way others' names did. There was no column for what Cyrus was. Their father had solved the problem eleven years ago with a single line in his own blunt hand: *Eastwick child, apprentice, wages waived, keepership of accounts granted.* Not son. Not daughter. A category of one, invented by a typeface cutter who understood better than most that when the existing sorts will not compose your sentence, you cut new ones.

Cyrus ran a thumb down the margin beside that line, the way they did some mornings, a habit they would have denied under oath.

Outside, the river moved under its skin of ice, patient and secretive. A crow dropped onto the sill of the type room window, regarded Cyrus with one flat eye, and left, unimpressed.

* * *

Tam arrived the way weather arrives off the water — announced by nothing, felt everywhere at once.

The shop door banged against its stop and let in a gust that scattered the paper slips Cyrus had sorted by price. Tam came through it sideways, coat unbuttoned despite the cold, cheeks burned red from the walk up from the ferry landing, and he was talking before the door had finished shutting.

"—and Halfpenny says the ferry'll run twice weekly once the thaw comes, twice, do you understand, which means cargo rates double and anyone holding warehouse space on the north bank—" He stopped. He looked at the ledger, open on the counter, then at Cyrus behind it, and something in his face performed a small rearrangement, the look of a man remembering he has entered a church. "Still keeping the kingdom's books, I see."

"Keeping the town's books," Cyrus said. "The kingdom hasn't asked."

"The kingdom will." Tam peeled off his gloves, finger by finger, taking his time about it. He was twenty-three, four years Cyrus's junior and determined, always, to seem older. He had their mother's coloring and their father's hands, though he used them for nothing finer than shaking and pointing. "That's rather the point of everything, isn't it? Books. Lists. Names." He smiled, and it was a good smile; it had opened doors for him all his life. It did not open this one. "Where's Father?"

"Cutting."

"Of course he is." Tam drifted along the counter, reading upside-down entries with the fluency of a boy raised in this shop, because he had been, all of them had been; there had been no other school in Marrow's Bend worth the name. His eyes snagged, as Cyrus knew they would, on the Harrow line. On the Cole birth. On the tidy columns of obligation that held the town together the way caulk held a boat. "You know what I did this morning, Cy? I stood on the landing and counted berths."

"I know. Wren told me. She counts them too."

"Wren counts everything. Counting isn't doing." Tam leaned both fists on the counter, on the ledger, and Cyrus watched the shadow of his fingers fall across forty years of neighbors' names. "Here's what doing looks like. I've spoken with the factor from Corvane & Whitlock. They're expanding north. They need men who can talk to buyers and read a contract, and they pay in coin, real coin, not salt pork and apologies—"

"They want you to go north."

"They want me to *stay*, is what they want, if certain arrangements could be made regarding—" and here it came, the thing he had walked up the frozen hill to say, arriving at last in all its shabby finery, "—regarding my stake. My share. One quarter of this establishment, Cy, that's the customary division among heirs, you can ask any lawyer in the county, and since Father won't hear it and you won't carry it to him, I'm asking you directly, face to face, like a man."

Cyrus set down the pen. They did it carefully, laying it in the groove of the counter where pens were laid, because the alternative was throwing it, and the counter deserved better, and so perhaps did Tam, though he was making it difficult.

"There are no heirs," Cyrus said. "There's no inheritance. There's a shop, and it belongs to the man who cuts the steel, and while he lives and after he dies it will belong to whoever keeps it. You don't want a share of a shop, Tam. You want a share of a story, and you've never liked this story. You left it at fourteen and you've been circling it ever since like a gull after a fishing boat."

"A gull gets fed."

"A gull gets scraps."

For a moment the shop was very quiet. Even Aldous's graver had stopped — not obviously, not rudely, but the rhythm had gone out of the sound, the way a heartbeat changes when it overhears its own name. Tam's jaw worked. Under the anger Cyrus could see the other thing, the thing they had seen in him for years now and pitied more than hated: the fear of a man who suspects that everything he has was given to him by people he has treated badly, and who cannot bear to owe.

"You think because you sweep the floor and write in Papa's big book you're indispensable," Tam said quietly. "You think ink makes you family. Ink makes you a clerk, Cy. Family is blood, and blood has shares."

"Ink is older than blood," said Aldous's voice, from the back of the shop, mild as milk. He did not appear in the doorway. He did not need to; his voice filled rooms the way his type filled formes, edge to edge. "Blood forgets. Ask any gravestone."

Tam flinched as though struck. Then he laughed — a wrong laugh, pitched for company neither of them could see, the officers and factors and ferry-landing men whose admiration he had begun to farm like a crop — and buttoned his coat, and went to the door.

"I'll have my share," he said from the threshold. "One way or another. Everyone settles their accounts eventually. Isn't that what the book says?"

He let the door bang shut behind him, and the paper slips scattered again, and this time Cyrus did not sort them. They stood at the counter with their hand resting flat on the ledger, feeling the nine pounds, four ounces of it, the weight of every name in Marrow's Bend — Harrow and Cole and Halfpenny and all the rest, and Eastwick, four times over, in four different hands, written at four different ages of the same stubborn family — and understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, that Tam meant it. That somewhere in that smiling, circling head, a calculation had already been made, and the currency of the coming days would be names, and their brother had just discovered that the town's entire supply of names slept in a book under his sister-brother's hand.

Aldous resumed cutting. Three strokes, a pause, two more. The R was nearly done.

Cyrus picked up the pen, dipped it, and wrote the next entry in a hand that did not shake, because the hand had been trained not to, and because someone had to keep the record true for as long as records still mattered:

*Halfpenny, ferry lease, renewed through Michaelmas.*

And beneath the door's fading draft, from downriver where the morning paper had come from, faint as a rumor but gathering, came the sound of wheels on the frozen road — many wheels, heavy ones, and a horse's breath steaming in the cold, and a voice calling orders in a tone Cyrus had not heard in ten years and would have known anywhere, asleep, drowned, dead.

The knocker fell against the shop door. Once. Twice.

Cyrus rose to answer it.
