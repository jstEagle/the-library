## 3. What the Captain Carried

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for ink orders and the cleaning of the rollers — a day of small, honest labors — and it seemed to Cyrus that the army might at least have had the courtesy to arrive on a day already ruined.

They came down Ferry Street in a column of grey, their boots striking the cobbles in a rhythm that the town had never learned, a rhythm with no give in it, no swing. Marrow's Bend walked to the river's time: a lurching, patient meter, the beat of water worrying stone. This was something else. This was a metronome. Cyrus stood in the doorway of Eastwick & Sons — *and Sons*, though there was only ever Tam, and Tam was not yet out of bed, and Cyrus had long ago stopped correcting anyone about what they were or weren't a son of — and watched forty men fill the street like water filling a lock.

At their head rode a captain on a bay horse too fine for him, or perhaps he was too fine for the horse; it was hard to say from this distance. He wore his greatcoat open despite the cold, as though winter were a rumor he had declined to believe, and his hat sat low over eyes that swept the storefronts the way an assessor's eye sweeps a field — measuring, valuing, claiming.

Cyrus knew the set of those shoulders before they knew the face.

It arrived in the body first, the way recognition always did with them — a tightening under the ribs, a heat climbing the throat — before the mind consented to name it. Ten years. Ten years since that back had last passed through the shop door, narrower then, still carrying the stoop of a boy who had spent three years bent over a composing stone. The shoulders had broadened. The stoop was gone, drilled out of him by whatever academy or army had taken him south. But Cyrus would have known that spine anywhere. They had set type beside it for a thousand mornings, close enough to feel the warmth of another body in the cold room, close enough to hear him breathe when the press fell silent between pulls.

Elias Vane. Who had left without a goodbye.

* * *

Aldous had said nothing, in the days after. That was the cruelty of it — not anger, not explanation, just nothing, the way Aldous met most things that could not be set in metal and locked into a chase. Elias Vane had simply stopped coming. His apron hung on its peg for a month, gathering the fine grey dust that lived in print shops the way damp lived in cellars, until one morning it was gone and neither parent would say where. Wren, who was six and missed nothing, asked if the tall boy had died, and their mother — she was alive then, their mother — had laughed in a way that made Cyrus's stomach turn over, because it was the laugh you used when the truth was worse than death and not fit for children.

Cyrus had been fourteen. They had taught him everything they knew. How to read a galley upside down and backward, because a compositor's eye must learn to live mirrored. How to hold a quoin key so the lock came even. Which letters lied — the lowercase *l* against the numeral *1*, the *rn* masquerading as *m* — and how to catch them before they shamed you in print. He had been better at the cutting than Cyrus was, in those years; his hands were steadier, his patience less a performance. Cyrus had hated him a little for that, and loved him entirely for everything else, though at fourteen they had possessed no word for the second thing and so had called it nothing at all, had folded it away unlabelled like a sort dropped into the wrong case, to be found years later by someone else's fingers.

And then gone. No note among the galleys. No word carried north or downriver. Just absence, wearing his apron's shape.

Now the absence had come back wearing an officer's coat, and it was reining up before the shop, and Cyrus discovered that ten years had done nothing whatsoever to prepare them.

* * *

"Eastwick." The captain dismounted without waiting for the boy who scrambled to take his reins. His voice had changed — deepened, sanded smooth of the river-town vowels, given the clipped finish of the capital — but underneath the polish Cyrus heard the old timbre, the voice that had once read proof aloud in the evenings while the two of them cleaned the type with a brush and lye, reading anything, almanacs, sermons, smuggled pamphlets, his finger moving down the column while Cyrus listened and pretended not to be listening. "Aldous Eastwick. Proprietor."

"Aldous Eastwick," their father agreed, from the doorway, wiping his hands on his apron with the unhurried dignity of a man who had decided in advance exactly how much fear he intended to display, and had settled on none. "You're standing in front of my shop, Captain."

"I'm standing in front of the town's press." Vane produced a paper, unfolded it with a snap. "By order of the Provisional Command, all printing apparatus in occupied districts is subject to requisition for the production of official gazettes and proclamations. Your press will continue to operate. Under military supervision. You will be compensated at a rate determined by the quartermaster."

"Compensated," Aldous repeated, tasting the word.

"At a rate determined," Vane said, "by the quartermaster."

Behind their father, Cyrus watched the captain's face. It was a good face for the work — composed, faintly bored, the face of a man reciting terms he had recited forty times downriver. But Cyrus had spent three years learning to read that face in profile across a composing stone, and faces, like type, showed their wear in the details. The jaw was set too carefully. The eyes, when they moved off the paper, went first — before the shop, before the soldiers, before Aldous — to the figure half-hidden in the doorway. To Cyrus. And then away again, quick as a hand snatched back from a hot platen, and Cyrus understood two things in the same white instant: that he knew them, knew them absolutely, and that he had decided, in the space of that single glance, to pretend otherwise.

The wound opened along its old seam. Not wider. Exactly as wide. That was the terrible thing about some wounds — they did not deepen with the years, they only waited, patient as dried ink, for the solvent that would make them run again.

* * *

The requisition took the day. Soldiers carried the smaller cases out into the square — Cyrus watched a private stagger under the job case holding the twelve-point Caslon, their grandfather's cut, the one with the slightly crooked *k* that every compositor in the family could find blindfolded — and stacked them under canvas awnings like loaves at a market. Two officers with clipboards inventoried the stock: paper by the ream, ink by the keg, leads and reglets, the brass gauge Cyrus had used since they were tall enough to see over the stone. A lieutenant wrote *1 press, hand, double-framed, serviceable* in a ledger of his own, and Cyrus thought: so the army keeps ledgers too. So the whole world is kept in books, and the question is only whose hand holds the pen.

Vane directed it all from the middle of the floor, and he did it well, Cyrus had to grant him that. He knew presses. When the sergeant proposed dismantling the frame to move it, Vane overruled him with a flat shake of the head — "It stays assembled; it prints truer assembled; I want gazettes, not kindling" — and when a soldier reached for the imposing stone with his bayonet scabbard swinging, Vane caught the man's shoulder and turned him aside with two words. He moved through the shop like someone revisiting a house from a former life, touching nothing longer than necessary, and Cyrus trailed the requisition with the ledger under their arm, recording what was taken because recording was what they did, because the ledger was the shop's memory and someone had to keep it even now, especially now.

They did not speak. Four hours in the same room, and not one word passed between them that the army could have heard.

But twice — twice — the pretense slipped its stitch. Once, when Cyrus reached past him for the ink knife hanging on its nail, and Vane went very still, the stillness of a man refusing to flinch, and Cyrus saw his gaze drop to their hands, to the ink stains worked so deep into the knuckle-creases they looked like age lines, and something crossed his face that no clipboard recorded. And once, near dusk, when a corporal knocked the case of eight-point Baskerville from the bench and the sorts scattered across the floorboards in a bright metallic rain, and both of them — both, simultaneously, before either could stop themselves — dropped to their knees to gather the letters, sorting as they gathered, *a b c d* into the cupped palm, the old reflex, the shared grammar, and for a moment they knelt a foot apart in the spilled alphabet with their hands full of the same language, and Vane looked up, and Cyrus looked down, and neither of them said a single word, and it was the loudest silence Cyrus had ever stood inside.

Then a sergeant barked an order, and the moment was swept up with the rest of the type.

* * *

That night the shop felt wrong in its own skin. The press stood where it had always stood but the cases around it were thinned, the walls showing pale rectangles where the job cases had hung, ghosts of furniture. The smell was the same — oil and lye and the sweetish bite of ink — but underneath it now was the army's smell, boot leather and woodsmoke and horses, an occupation written into the air itself.

Aldous sat by the stove turning his spectacles over and over in his hands, not putting them on. Tam had come home at dusk full of the spectacle — *did you see the horses, Father, did you see the captain's horse* — and had been sent to bed with a look that would have felled a weaker boy. Wren was upstairs, pretending to sleep, listening the way she always listened, which was to say completely.

"They'll want more than the press," Aldous said at last, to the stove. "Presses are the excuse. They'll want the ledger next. Every debt, every birth, every name in this town, written in our hand. An army loves a list, Cy. It's the closest thing to scripture a soldier trusts."

"The ledger's ours," Cyrus said. "It's got nothing in it but what people owe us."

"That's precisely what an army wants. Everything people owe." Their father put the spectacles on at last, and behind the glass his eyes were tired and very clear. "Get some sleep."

Cyrus did not get any sleep. They climbed instead to the loft above the shop, where the window faced the square, and watched the sentry walk his post beneath the canvas awnings that covered the town's stolen voice. Frost was forming on the glass. Downriver, the ice on the Marrow still held, grey-white and groaning sometimes in the dark, though the thaw was weeks out yet — weeks in which anything could happen, in which everything would.

And it was there, in the small hours, that Cyrus admitted what the day had actually been about. Not the press. Not the requisition. The press was iron and would survive; iron always survived, it was people who broke. The day had been about a man on a bay horse who had looked at them once, quickly, and then spent four hours not looking, and the not-looking had been louder than any greeting could have been. Because you did not refuse to look at a stranger. You refused to look at a fire you were trying not to feed.

He had known them. In the first instant, before the mask came down — he had known them, and chosen the mask, and the choosing told Cyrus everything the mask was built to hide.

Why? The question turned over and over like a forme under the platen. Shame? Duty? Or something colder — that he had come for the shop specifically, had asked for this district, this route, this errand, and the uniform was the only way left to walk through that door?

Below, in the square, the sentry paused at the corner nearest the shop. Stood a moment too long. Tilted his head up toward the loft window, where the frost had been breathed away from the inside in one small patch, a flaw in the glass, a sign of life.

Even at forty yards, even in the dark, Cyrus knew the shape of him.

He held there perhaps five seconds. Then he resumed his post, boots striking the cobbles in that rhythm with no give in it, and Cyrus pressed their forehead against the cold glass and understood that the war had not come to Marrow's Bend with the soldiers at all.

It had come ten years ago, without a goodbye. And tomorrow it would knock on the shop door again, wearing his face, and ask for something no requisition order had words for.
