## 4. Requisition

The knock came at seven, and it was not a knock so much as a verdict — the flat of a musket stock against the door's upper panel, twice, with the patience of men who had all morning and the whole country to take.

Cyrus was already awake. They had been awake since the fourth bell, lying in the narrow bed above the shop, listening to the river mutter under its skin of ice, rehearsing the day the way one rehearses a confession. Now they dressed in the dark — waistcoat, the collarless shirt their mother had sewn before Wren was born, sleeves rolled to the elbow because type grime did not forgive good cloth — and descended the stairs two at a time, arriving at the door just as Aldous arrived from the back room, still buttoning his coat, his beard pressed flat on one side from sleep.

"Through the shop," Aldous said quietly, which was not an answer to anything but was understood by everyone. Wren appeared on the landing above with Tam behind her, and Cyrus saw that Tam had dressed carefully, hair combed, as though for church or for an audition.

Cyrus opened the door.

Four soldiers stood in the grey light, and behind them the square was already wrong — transformed overnight into something borrowed from a woodcut of another town. Wagons drawn up in a line along the fountain. A cookfire smoking in front of the meetinghouse steps. Canvas tents wedged between the elms like mushrooms after foul weather. And everywhere the sound: boots, harness, the clatter of iron on stone, the low continuous murmur of three hundred men conducting the business of occupation before most of Marrow's Bend had put kettle to fire.

"Lieutenant Hargreaves," said the foremost soldier, a young man with a face like a closed ledger. He did not offer his hand. "By order of General Ashcombe, commanding the Northern Department, this establishment is requisitioned for the use of the army. The press, the stock, the premises." His eyes moved past Cyrus, cataloguing: the cases of type along the north wall, the press itself squatting in the center of the room like a black ox, the reams of paper stacked to shoulder height. "You will continue such printing as we direct. You will print nothing else. Is there another entrance?"

"The alley door," Aldous said. "Locked."

"Unlock it. Leave it unlocked."

Hargreaves produced a paper, unfolded it with ceremony, and held it out. Cyrus took it because their father did not move to. The seal was intact, the ink crisp, the language exactly the language of chapter headings in gazettes — *whereas, therefore, henceforth* — and beneath it, itemized like a debt, the words Cyrus had known were coming since the rumor first came downriver:

*Item: all books, registers, ledgers, and records of account maintained upon the premises, to be secured as evidence.*

"Evidence," Cyrus said. Their voice came out steadier than they expected. "Evidence of what?"

"Of what's written in them." Hargreaves almost smiled; the expression died of loneliness on his face. "Where is the ledger?"

Aldous crossed to the counter without hurry. He moved through his own shop the way he moved through everything — with the unhurried economy of a man who had spent forty years making heavy things obey him. From beneath the counter he drew the great ledger, bound in calfskin gone soft and dark as old bread at the corners, and set it down on the oak with a sound like a door closing.

"Eleven years of this town's accounts," he said. "Who owes whom, who was born, who married, who died, what every family in Marrow's Bend bought on credit when the winter took their harvest. If your general wants evidence, he'll find no better inventory of the place than that."

Hargreaves looked at the ledger the way a customs man looks at a crate — weighing, estimating, indifferent to contents. Two soldiers came forward and wrapped it in oilcloth with a thoroughness that struck Cyrus as obscene: eleven years of births and debts, bundled like contraband salt fish.

"You'll have a receipt," Hargreaves said, and actually wrote one, there on the counter, using the shop's spare pen and a corner of the shop's own paper, and Cyrus thought: *he doesn't even know he's doing it.* The army would requisition the press and leave its mark in the shop's ink without ever noticing the irony. That, perhaps, was the whole of empire in miniature.

The receipt named the ledger, described it, dated the seizure. Aldous signed for it with the same hand he used for invoices. And then the soldiers were filing out through the shop toward the alley door, boots loud on boards that had only ever known the careful tread of craftsmen, and the door stood open behind them, letting in the smoke-smell and the noise, and the family stood in the middle of the shrunken room and looked at one another.

Tam spoke first. "They took the ledger." As if announcing it might un-announce it.

"They took a copy of the ledger," Aldous said.

Wren went very still on the stairs. Cyrus kept their eyes on their father.

"There's only ever been one book," Cyrus said carefully.

Aldous began rolling down his sleeves, which he had rolled up for nothing. "There's always more than one of everything worth having," he said. "That's why we cut type. Go light the stove, Cyrus. It's cold enough in here to crack a forme, and I imagine the army will want its first notice printed by noon."

* * *

They printed the army's notice by half past ten. Two hundred copies of a broadsheet declaring martial law, fixing prices, forbidding assembly after dusk, and offering — clause nine, in type so small Cyrus had chosen it deliberately from the long primer case, a pettiness of craft that felt like the only rebellion available — a reward for information regarding *any person or persons engaged in the unauthorized production of printed matter.*

Vane's name was not on it. The order bore Hargreaves's signature and the general's seal, but Cyrus knew whose hand had drafted the clauses, whose mind loved the architecture of a document like this — the way each provision locked against the next, no gaps, no mercy in the joinery. Ten years ago that mind had been bent over a composing stone three feet from Cyrus's own, learning to justify a column so the spacing sang. Some apprentices learned the trade. Elias Vane had learned the trade the way water learns a channel.

Cyrus pulled the last copy, hung it to dry, and watched soldiers paste the first sheets to the meetinghouse wall while the townspeople gathered at the square's edges and read in silence. Old Mistress Corven spat on the cobbles and was moved along. The Ferris boys laughed at something and stopped laughing when a sergeant turned. Marrow's Bend, which had never in Cyrus's life assembled anywhere but the meetinghouse and the tavern and the ice in February, discovered overnight the peculiar intimacy of being watched.

Through it all, Aldous worked beside Cyrus at the stone, setting the army's words in the army's service, and hummed.

It was barely a tune — a thread of sound under the breath, some hymn fragment from before Cyrus's memory began — but Cyrus had heard their father hum in exactly this register exactly twice before: the night the shop flooded, standing in six inches of river water rescuing type cases by lantern light; and the week after their mother died, when he had hummed through four days of funeral arrangements and one night of sitting alone in the kitchen until dawn. It was the sound of a man distributing an unbearable weight across a smaller, steadier surface. It frightened Cyrus more than the soldiers had.

"Father," Cyrus said, when the soldiers at the door had drifted out to watch the pasting. "The ledger."

"What of it."

"Hargreaves has our accounts. Every family. Every debt. They'll use it — they'll call it sedition, they'll call owing money to us complicity—"

"They'll use what they find," Aldous said. He inked the brayer with the smooth two-stroke motion he could perform asleep, had perhaps once performed asleep. "Which depends on what there is to find."

"You said there's a copy."

"I said there's always more than one of everything." He laid the first stroke of ink across the forme, and the smell of the ink rose up familiar as bread, and for a moment the shop was only ever a shop. Then he set the brayer down and looked at Cyrus, and his face in the grey window-light was suddenly very legible, every line cut deep. "Cyrus. Listen to me, because I'll say this once and then we won't speak of it again until it's time. Tonight, after the soldiers change watch — they change at the second bell, I counted their rounds this afternoon — go to the corner of the cellar where the paper safe stands. There are two boards that don't sit true. What's under them is yours to know now, and yours to decide about, and I want you to understand that whatever you decide, I'll have decided the same thing, because I've been deciding it for eight months and I'm tired of carrying it alone."

Cyrus's mouth had gone dry. "Under the floorboards."

"A printer who keeps only one copy of anything," Aldous said, taking up the brayer again, "deserves whatever happens to him."

* * *

The day crawled. The army made itself at home in the ways armies do — commandeering the tavern's cellar for stores, posting sentries at both bridges, stopping Wren in the street to demand what she carried in her basket and finding only bread and turnips and being disappointed in a way that boded ill for later. Tam vanished shortly after noon and reappeared at supper wearing the particular shine of a boy who had spent the afternoon being useful to officers, carrying messages, learning names, positioning himself in doorways where important men must pass through him to get anywhere.

"They're not monsters, you know," Tam said, mouth full of potatoes. "Half of them are farm boys same as us. Lieutenant Hargreaves asked after Father's health. Politely."

"Politely," Wren repeated, in the tone she used for measuring flour — exact, unsentimental, final.

"He's from Ashcombe's staff. He knows people. If the family stays right with the army—" Tam caught himself, but too late; the shape of the sentence was already in the room.

"Stays right," Cyrus said.

"With them. While they're here. It's sense, is all I'm saying. Sense isn't treason."

Aldous ate steadily throughout, and said nothing, and hummed under his breath, and Cyrus watched Tam watch their father hum and saw something flicker across the boy's face that might have been fear and might have been opportunity, and could not tell which frightened them more.

* * *

At the second bell the watch changed, and Cyrus lit no lamp, and went down the cellar stairs by feel and the thin wash of light from the alley grating.

The cellar smelled of size and damp stone and the river, which announced itself even here, under everything, a low wet breathing through the foundations. The paper safe stood where it had stood since before Cyrus could reach its latch. Cyrus knelt beside it, found the near board, the far one — both loose, both lifting at a touch, and beneath them a shallow cavity lined with waxed cloth, and in the cavity—

Type.

Fresh type, bright as new coin, rows of sorts packed in tissue in shallow trays, and even in the dark Cyrus's fingers knew them, read them like a page: these were not the shop's worn faces, not the long primer or the pica Cyrus cut for ordinary work. This was a new alphabet, a clean bold roman, larger than any body type in the shop's inventory — display size, poster size, the kind of letter that could be read across a square.

And the letters had been recut. Not cast from matrices — *cut*, hand-cut in steel, every sort, which was work of months, hundreds of hours at the graver, the kind of labor a man undertook only when he intended his letters to say something that must survive.

Cyrus lifted a tray into the grating's light. Set the sorts in order on the cloth with the unconscious fluency of a lifetime.

*The many are obliged.*

Three words. Poster size. Cut by hand, letter by letter, over months, in secret, by a bearded craftsman who hummed hymn fragments while he inked the army's proclamations.

Cyrus sat back on their heels in the dark cellar with the words in their hands and understood several things at once, the way one understands a finished forme all at once when the paper pulls. That their father had been at war longer than the army had been in the square. That the fresh passes, the hidden work, whatever else lay beneath the other floorboards of this house, were part of a design Cyrus had never been shown — until tonight, until now, until their father had said *what's under them is yours to know now.*

Yours to decide about.

Above, through the floor joists, came the sound of the shop door opening — late rounds, a lantern's swing of light across the grating, the measured boots Cyrus would know at forty yards or four hundred. Vane, walking his post past the shop where he'd learned his trade, past the cellar where Cyrus knelt holding treason in letterpress.

The boots passed. The light passed. The dark returned, complete.

Cyrus replaced the trays, seated the boards, climbed the stairs with legs that felt borrowed — and found Aldous waiting at the top, coat already off, sleeves already rolled, a single candle burning on the composing stone and the stone cleared, wiped ready, waiting.

He didn't ask what Cyrus had found. He looked at his child's face instead, reading it the way he read proof — hunting flaws, finding none — and something in his shoulders let go, a settling, like a man setting down a load he'd carried up a long hill.

"Eight months I've cut that alphabet," he said quietly. "Every letter but five. I saved those for you."

Cyrus looked at the cleared stone, at the candle, at the father who had measured their whole life in ems and picas and had apparently also, all along, measured them — their hands, their patience, their nerve — and found the measurement true.

"Which five?" Cyrus heard themselves ask.

Aldous smiled, and it was the flood-night smile, the funeral-week smile, terrible and calm. "Sit down," he said, "and bring the light."

From the yard beyond the alley door, faint under the river's mutter, came the sound of the ice shifting in the dark — a long groan, like something vast turning over in its sleep — and neither of them looked up.
