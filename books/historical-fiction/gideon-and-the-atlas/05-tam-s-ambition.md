## 5. Tam's Ambition

The soldiers paid in scrip, and Tam Eastwick learned on the first morning that scrip could be made to sing if you held it right.

He had discovered this by accident, or rather by appetite, which for Tam had always served the function other men assigned to luck. The requisition had swallowed the town square whole — gun carriages where the fish carts stood, sentries at the wellhead, a quartermaster's tent pitched over the spot where old Mother Ives had sold apples for forty years — and the town had folded inward like a hand closing around a coin. But the soldiers themselves were only boys, most of them, far from their own tables, hungry for something warmer than army bread and something newer than army jokes. Tam had jokes. Tam had always had jokes, the way his father had chisels and Cyrus had ink-stained fingers: not learned but grown, part of the body's original architecture.

So when Corporal Hesse — a broad, pink-faced boy from somewhere north of the marshes, with a laugh like a bucket dropped down a dry well — complained within Tam's hearing that the commissary's coffee tasted of boiled rope, Tam turned on the tavern step and said, "That's because they're brewing it in the same kettles they used to tar the fences. My father did that once. Whole fence came out blacker than sin and twice as bitter."

Hesse stared. Then the laugh came up out of him like something surfacing from deep water, and he clapped Tam on the shoulder hard enough to rock him, and said, "You. You're the printer's boy."

"I'm the printer's *son*," Tam said, and let the correction sit there in the cold air, round and useful, before he added, "Tam Eastwick. If you want coffee that won't strip paint off a musket, my sister makes it with chicory and river sugar. Better than anything your quarter master's ever smelled."

It was not entirely true. Wren's coffee was ordinary coffee. But the lie was generous in shape, and generosity, Tam had found, opened doors the way keys did, only cheaper.

By noon he had drunk three cups of his sister's ordinary coffee with four soldiers, told the story of the tarred fence twice, and been introduced, by degrees of increasing formality, to Lieutenant Ashe, who kept a thin notebook and a thinner smile, and who looked at Tam the way Tam's father looked at a flawed piece of type — measuring what could be salvaged from the casting.

"You're the family that runs the press," Ashe said.

"My father runs the press," Tam said. "I run the charm."

Ashe's smile did not change, but something behind it moved, like a fish under ice, and Tam understood — with the sudden cold clarity that sometimes visited him after the wit had already spent itself — that he had just been weighed.

* * *

He walked home along the river road as the light went long and coppery, and he counted his gains the way Wren counted coins: the corporal's shoulder-clap, the lieutenant's attention, an invitation of sorts — *come by the mess tomorrow, printer's son, we'll see what use you are* — and the general fact of himself, established now, planted like a flag in occupied ground. His breath smoked. The river lay under its first honest ice, white-grey and groaning occasionally, like an old man turning in bed, and Tam thought: *this is how it's done.* Not with rifles, not with his father's stubborn silence, not with Cyrus's ledgers and lowered eyes. With presence. With usefulness worn lightly, like good cloth. The army was going to be in Marrow's Bend for the winter regardless; the war was coming up the valley regardless; and a man — a *son*, an heir, a somebody — could either be run over or be standing at the gate when the riders came through, saying, *Yes, gentlemen, this way, I know the town.*

His father would call it whoring after occupiers. His father called most things whoring. Aldous Eastwick had a genius for making virtue look like a punishment he alone was willing to serve.

And Cyrus — Tam's steps slowed on the frozen ruts — Cyrus would say nothing at all. That was the trouble with Cyrus. You could live twenty-odd years beside a person and know them no better than you knew the inside of the press, all machinery and oil and closed casements. Cyrus would stand in the doorway of an evening with the ledger under one arm, watching Tam talk to the officers as though watching weather happen to someone else, and there would be nothing in that quiet face Tam could read, nothing he could use, nothing he could even argue with, and somehow that nothing was worse than any accusation. It made Tam talk more. It made him funnier, louder, hungrier. He hated that about himself, and he blamed it, with real precision, on Cyrus.

The shop's windows glowed amber when he came up the lane. Inside, the smell hit him as it always did — ink and oil and the tallow-sweetness of hot metal, the smell of his entire childhood, so familiar it had become invisible to him, the way a name spoken every day stops meaning anything. His father bent over the cutting bench, shoulders rounded like the hills upriver, and the sound of steel on steel came in small patient ticks. Cyrus sat at the ledger table, and the lamplight pooled on the pages, and Cyrus did not look up, and Tam felt the old irritation rise in him like water into a crack, ready to freeze there.

"Officers' mess wants bread," Tam announced to the room in general. "Four loaves, fresh, delivered to the square by eight. They'll pay in army flour, double weight." He hung his coat. "I volunteered us."

His father's chisel stopped. One tick, then silence.

"You volunteered us," Aldous said, without lifting his head.

"The flour alone is worth it. And it costs us nothing but Wren's morning—"

"It costs," Aldous said, and now he did raise his head, and his eyes were the flat grey of the ice outside, "the appearance of friendship. Which in wartime is a currency, and you've just spent some of ours without asking."

"They're going to take what they take regardless," Tam said. He heard his own voice go bright and reasonable, that salesman's pitch he couldn't switch off. "Wouldn't you rather they took it smiling? Wouldn't you rather they thought of this house as *friendly*?"

His father looked at him a long moment. Then, quietly, in the tone he saved for the worst things, worse than anger: "Your grandfather thought the last tax man was his friend. There's a story there if you ever want it."

The chisel resumed. Tick. Tick. Tick. Small patient steel against smaller patient steel.

Cyrus still hadn't looked up. But Tam saw — because he was looking, because he always ended up looking, hating himself for needing it — Cyrus's pen pause above the ledger line, hover there, and then continue in the same unbroken hand, as if Tam had said nothing worth recording. As if Tam himself were not worth recording.

*Four loaves,* Tam thought, snatching his coat back off the hook. *I'll get the bread myself. I'll carry it through the snow on my own back, and I'll smile while I do it, and one day — one day soon — this family will understand who kept them fed.*

The door slammed behind him harder than he'd intended. The cold took his face like a slap. Down the lane, the river groaned again beneath its ice, long and low, and Tam walked toward the town lights without listening to it.

* * *

The mess tent was warmer than any room Tam had entered since autumn, and that warmth was the first thing they gave you, before the bad coffee, before the thin wine, before the officer's table where Lieutenant Ashe presided with two captains passing through on the supply road. Warmth, and then laughter at whatever joke you brought, and then questions. Always the questions, arranged casually among the courses like stones set in a shallow stream — *your father's business, does it do much government work, has he held contracts before, what does he print besides the paper, and who buys it, and where do the buyers come from, and have there been strangers in the town lately, strangers with papers, strangers asking after routes.*

Tam answered everything, and answered well, he believed. He gave them the history of the Eastwick press with color and detail, made his father sound half legendary — *cut his first alphabet at fourteen, cut the matrices for half the newspapers in the valley* — and made himself sound indispensable, the public face of the firm, the one who dealt with customers and creditors both. He laughed at Captain Rourke's story about the mule and the pontoon bridge, even though everyone at the table had clearly heard it twice. He drank moderately, having learned early that a man who drinks less than his company remembers everything and owes nothing.

It was working. He could feel it working, the slow warm accumulation of regard, the way Ashe's notebook stayed closed tonight, which meant Tam had passed beyond the stage of being *written about* and arrived at the stage of being *counted on*. By the second bottle Captain Rourke was calling him "our Marrow's Bend man," and the title went into Tam like wine goes into an empty stomach — instant heat, immediate dizziness.

Then Captain Rourke, wiping his mouth, said pleasantly, "Shame about the sedition, though."

The word landed on the table between the plates and the candle flames, and nobody moved for a moment except the flames.

"Sedition," Tam repeated. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of that later.

"There's rebel money moving through this valley," Rourke said, as if discussing drainage. "Rebel papers too — we've confiscated two presses upriver in the past month, both of them printing passes and proclamations no legitimate shop would touch. Somebody hereabouts is feeding it. Command thinks a printer." He smiled, easy as Sunday. "But you'd know if it were your own father, wouldn't you, printer's son? Blood tells."

And here was the moment — Tam saw it open before him like a hole in the ice, black water underneath — here was the moment where a man chose what kind of creature he was going to be. He heard himself laugh, the bucket-down-the-dry-well laugh, borrowed from Hesse, and heard himself say:

"If my father were printing seditious passes, Captain, he'd have printed me a dozen by now. I can't even get him to print wedding invitations for friends at cost. The man's a saint about legitimacy and a miser about everything else." A ripple of laughter, Rourke's included, and Tam pressed on, riding the laugh, heart hammering somewhere up near his teeth. "No, whoever your printer is, he's not in my family's shop. We're too busy trying to get paid for four loaves of bread."

More laughter. Rourke raised his cup to him. Ashe's thin smile stayed exactly where it had been all evening, and Ashe's eyes — grey, colder than his father's — stayed on Tam a heartbeat longer than laughter warranted.

Tam raised his cup in return and drank, and the wine tasted suddenly of iron.

Because — and the thought arrived whole, unbidden, unwelcome, in the middle of his own triumph — because he had lied. Smoothly, fluently, without a flicker, and he did not actually *know* it was a lie. He did not know what his father cut at that bench after midnight, what those crates came in wrapped in sacking, why the forge was stoked on nights no legitimate order required metal. He had assumed it was ordinary work. He had assumed — he realized now he had spent months carefully, deliberately *not assuming anything*, the way you walk wide around a weak place in a floor — because if he let himself wonder, he would have to know, and knowing would put him at this same table with the same question and nowhere left to laugh.

He had just vouched for a darkness he hadn't examined. He had spent his family's blood on a joke, and the joke had landed.

*They'll never hang Father,* he told himself on the walk home, the stars hard and close overhead, his breath tearing white. *Nobody hangs printers. It's paper, it's only paper, and command needs the press working more than it needs a corpse. Besides, I'm inside now. I'm their Marrow's Bend man. Whatever happens, I can steer it. That's the whole point of what I'm doing — I'm the rudder.*

The metaphor comforted him for nearly the length of the lane. Then he remembered that rudders are attached to ships, and ships go where the current sends them, and he stood a moment in the dark outside his own door, letting the cold into his lungs, before he could make himself go in.

* * *

The shop was dark except for the banked glow of the forge, and Tam would have gone straight up to bed if the forge had not been glowing at all. It was past two. No order needed fire at this hour.

He stood in the doorway of the workroom, and the light touched his boots, and from the shadows by the composing stone came his father's voice, low and even, addressed to Cyrus, who must have been sitting where Tam couldn't see:

"Not these. These are done — sanded, no marks. The next case. And mind you don't set them in gallery order; mix them, always mix them, so the forme reads as nothing."

A pause. Then Cyrus's voice, so quiet Tam had to strain: "And the ones from Tuesday? Are they already across?"

"Already moving," his father said. "Which is why we don't speak of Tuesdays."

Tam did not understand the words, not wholly, not yet. But he understood the *shape* of them — the furtive grammar, the mixed sorts, the *already moving* — and understanding arrived in him not as shock but as a terrible settling, like a debt long dreaded finally coming due. He backed away from the doorway one silent step at a time, heart filling his throat, and climbed the stairs to his cold room, and lay down in his clothes, and stared at the ceiling.

*I could fix this,* he thought. *I'm inside. I know what command suspects, and now I know what Father does, and I am the only soul in this house who stands in both rooms. That makes me the valuable one. That makes me the one who saves everybody.* The thought expanded warmly in his chest, crowding out the fear, reshaping itself as he held it — *savior, indispensable, the son who kept them all alive* — until it bore no resemblance to the fear underneath.

Downstairs, faintly, steel ticked against steel. Patient. Unhurried. As if there were worlds of time.

Outside, the wind shifted south, and the river answered with a sound Tam had never heard it make before — a long groaning sigh, like something vast beginning to wake — and he pulled the blanket over his ears and did not listen, the way he had not listened to so many things, and slept at last, and dreamed of applause.
