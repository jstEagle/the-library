## 6. The Forged Pages

The discovery began, as most catastrophes do in a print shop, with a miscount.

Cyrus had learned arithmetic twice over — once from the ledger's columns of debts and births, and once from the type room, where a case of sorts was its own mathematics. A font was a country with a population: so many e's, so many t's, so many small and silent citizens of lead and antimony. You came to know the demography of a drawer the way a shepherd knows his flock. When one went missing, you felt it in the hand before the eye confirmed it.

It was the lowercase *r* that betrayed them. Tuesday's job — the gazette, four pages, set and locked by lamplight — had eaten more *r*'s than usual, and when Cyrus went to redistribute from the reserve case on the high shelf above Aldous's cutting bench, they found the case lighter than it should have been. Not empty. Lightened. Someone had been skimming, a dozen sorts here, two dozen there, in the furtive way a cook trims candle ends. And someone had done it with clean hands. No dust lay in the grooves of the compartments where dust always gathered like gray snow. The dust had been disturbed recently, and recently wiped.

Cyrus stood very still beneath the shelf and let the shop speak to them. The press with its great iron wing folded like a sleeping bird. The stone trough where the leather balls soaked in their bath of glue and water. The bank of windows, diamond-paned, letting down the thin gray light of a morning that smelled of river mud and thaw. All of it familiar as their own pulse. And all of it, this morning, arranged around an absence.

They took down the reserve case and carried it to the composing stone, and there they did what their father had taught them to do with any problem: break it into sorts. Count. Sort. Order. They tipped the compartments out onto the stone in little metallic avalanches and counted each letter against the tally sheet Cyrus kept in their own fair hand, because Aldous trusted no arithmetic but theirs.

The count came back wrong in a pattern, and patterns were the signature of intention. It wasn't random loss. The missing letters were not scattered across the alphabet like plague deaths. Whole ranks had thinned in clusters — *a*, *b*, *e*, *i*, *l*, *n*, *o*, *p*, *r*, *s*, *t*, *v* — and Cyrus's fingers stopped moving on the cold lead and lay flat on the stone as though pinned there.

Those were not the letters of chance depletion. Those were the letters of names. Of places. Of dates.

Those were the letters of documents.

* * *

There was a lockbox in the type room that Cyrus had never been permitted to open. It sat at the back of the lower cabinet, beneath the drawer where the brass rules lived, and it was old iron, older than the press, older than Cyrus, with a keyhole shaped like a teardrop. As children, Wren had once picked at it with a hairpin until Aldous caught her and did not shout — Aldous never shouted — but only took the hairpin gently from her fingers and said, *some doors are load-bearing, little bird; you don't knock on them*, which meant nothing then and everything now.

Cyrus knew where the key was. Everyone in a family knows where every key is, even the keys that are never spoken of. This one hung inside the collar of Aldous's coat on the tenth hook, strung on a bootlace, and it hung there every night except — Cyrus realized with a slow, cold settling, like silt in standing water — except on nights when the lamp in the type room burned past two o'clock and Aldous believed the whole house asleep.

The coat was on the hook now. Aldous was at the market, bargaining for potash and lampblack, and would be gone an hour, and Cyrus stood in the empty shop holding the reserve case's wrongness in their hands like a bird with a broken wing.

They had never stolen anything in their life. They had never lied to their father, not truly, not beyond the small evasions all children practice and pay for. Their whole existence had been built on a different architecture: on being the one who kept the accounts straight, who reconciled the town's debts column by column, who made the numbers agree because somewhere in Marrow's Bend there had to be one place where things added up. If Cyrus was anything — if the name they had carved for themselves out of the world's indifference meant anything — it meant that the ledger balanced.

They took the key off the bootlace with hands that did not shake, and were surprised to find that they did not shake, and were surprised at their own surprise, because some part of them had known since the miscounted *r*'s that this hour was coming, had perhaps been waiting for it the way the river waits under its ice for the warm wind.

The lockbox opened on paper.

Not money. Not deeds. Paper — and type, a shallow tray of loose sorts laid out in the peculiar order of a document rather than the order of a font: the letters grouped as they would be plucked for setting, pre-sorted by a compositor mid-job. Cyrus lifted the tray aside. Beneath it, folded in bundles of twenty, tied with waxed thread, lay travel passes.

They were beautiful. That was the first thing. That was the betrayal underneath the betrayal. Each pass bore the county seal — a stag courant under a chevron — rendered in a fine woodcut Aldous must have cut himself, because no seal-cutter in the province would sell blocks to a country printer, which meant Aldous had sawn and graved that stag by lamplight with his own aging hands, and the work was exquisite, better than the original, the stag's legs gathered mid-leap with a tension in the haunches that seemed to promise the animal might bolt off the page. The paper was right. The watermark, held to the window light, showed the crowned fleur that belonged to the provincial mill — Aldous had even sourced the correct stock, or aged the wrong stock to match, and Cyrus could not tell which, and could not decide which possibility frightened them more.

The names were set in the shop's own type. Cyrus recognized the *e* with its slightly nicked serif, the flaw their father called *the Eastwick tooth* and refused to grind away, saying a face without a flaw was a face without a history. Ten passes. Twenty. Forty. Names Cyrus knew from the ledger — Harrow the cooper, the Vosse girls from up the valley road, a man named Quill whose birth Cyrus had recorded eleven years ago in the book that sat three feet from this box, in this room, in this shop, in the same building as the crime.

Each pass granted leave to travel north. Each bore a date within the next fortnight. Each bore a signature — *By order of the Commissioner of Ways* — that Cyrus compared, hands trembling now at last, against the genuine commission notice the army had posted in the square last week, of which a proof copy still lay in the scrap drawer.

The forgery was perfect. The signature was Aldous Eastwick's best work, a signature copied so faithfully that Cyrus could see where he had practiced — dozens of attempts on scrap, each one closer, the hand learning the other man's hand the way Aldous had learned to cut steel, patiently, obsessively, with the terrible devotion of a craftsman.

Cyrus put the passes down on the composing stone and stood in the middle of the shop while the light moved a finger's width across the floor, and understood, in the manner of understanding that arrives all at once like ice giving way, exactly what they were holding.

These were gallows papers. The army was hunting forged passes — Captain Vane had said so in the square, in that voice Cyrus had known once as a boy's voice and now heard as an officer's, saying the words *sedition* and *aiding unlawful passage* while the whole town stood in the slush and listened. Anyone caught carrying one would hang. Any printer found producing them would hang faster, and everyone connected to the printer would hang in whatever order the army pleased.

And forty people from Marrow's Bend — people whose names Cyrus entered in the ledger in their own careful hand, whose debts and christenings and marriages Cyrus had recorded through years of believing that ink was a kind of keeping, a way of saying *you existed, you mattered, you are written* — forty of those people were going to walk north out of slavery-to-the-war on papers made in this room.

Cyrus counted the bundles again. Sixty-eight passes. Enough for sixty-eight souls, if families shared. The town had perhaps six hundred. The arithmetic sat in Cyrus's chest like swallowed stone.

* * *

Aldous came home an hour late, smelling of potash and river wind, and knew before he reached the type room. Cyrus saw the knowledge arrive in him at the door — saw his eyes go first not to his child's face but to the cabinet, to the box sitting open on the stone with its contents laid out in evidence, arranged the way Cyrus arranged everything: sorted, ordered, legible.

He did not reach for the doorframe or blanch or drop his market basket. He set the basket down on the bench beside the ink fountain, took off his coat, hung it on the tenth hook, and said, "You counted the reserve case."

"You've been skimming the *r*'s since winter," Cyrus said. Their voice came out level, which astonished them. "I thought it was shrinkage. Bad casting. I re-tallied the font twice and blamed myself."

"I know." Aldous came forward and stood on the opposite side of the composing stone, and between them lay the open box and the beautiful, deadly stag. "I left the dust undisturbed as long as I could. But a good counter can't be fooled forever, and I didn't want to fool you anyway. I only wanted—" He stopped. His hands, those hands that could raise a letterface out of blank steel with a graver finer than an eyelash, opened and closed once at his sides. "I wanted more time. To finish."

"Finish." Cyrus heard their own voice climb and hated it. "Finish what? Finish hanging us? Father, the army has posted notices. They're searching printers' shops from here to the ford. Vane himself read the proclamation —"

"I heard him read it." Aldous's voice stayed low, the voice he used at the cutting bench, the voice that had guided Cyrus's hands through their first alphabet when Cyrus was seven and the world had already decided what a child with no settled name would amount to. "I stood in the square and listened to that boy read the death warrant for my work, and I walked home, and that evening I cut twelve more *a*'s. What else would you have me do?"

"What I would have you do is stop!"

The word cracked off the rafters. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked — Wren, listening, or Tam, listening, and Cyrus could not tell which was worse — but Cyrus had gone past caring about the walls having ears. "Do you understand what you've built? Do you understand what's in this box? Every one of these is a rope with our name punched into the seal. If a single carrier is taken — one courier searched at a checkpoint, one pass found in a coat lining — they trace the blockwork, they trace the paper, they come here, and they will not need a trial, Father, they'll need a beam and a cart, and they'll take Wren and Tam and me for company whether we knew or not—"

"You knew." Aldous said it quietly, and it stopped Cyrus's breath. "Not the particulars. But you knew something. Don't tell me you didn't feel it, these months — the extra potash I ordered, the lampblack, the odd hours, the paper stock that doesn't match any job in your ledger. You're the finest counter I ever met, Cyrus. You knew the shop had a secret the way you know a case is short an *r*. You just hadn't let yourself count it yet." He looked down at the passes, and something moved across his face that was not pride exactly and not shame but the strange alloy of the two that Cyrus would spend years learning to name. "So count it now. Tell me what you see."

Cyrus looked. Against every screaming instinct, they looked properly, with a craftsman's eye, because their father had trained that eye and it answered him even now, even in mutiny.

"I see the seal is yours," Cyrus said hoarsely. "The stag. You cut it. The haunches are too alive for anyone else's knife. I see the watermark's right, which means either you bought sealed stock through someone at the mill, which is another felony, or you sized and pressed it yourself, which took months. I see the signatures are near-flawless and that you practiced, because the third bundle's hands are surer than the first. I see sixty-eight passes and I know there are six hundred of us, so I also see that you know you cannot save this town, and you're doing it anyway, a bundle at a time, like a man bailing a lake with a thimble." Their throat closed. "I see that it's beautiful work. That's the worst part. I look at it and I want to burn it and I want to frame it, both at once, and I don't know which impulse is mine and which one you put in me."

Aldous made a sound that might have been a laugh if grief hadn't gotten to it first. He reached across the stone, slowly, giving Cyrus time to pull back, and touched the nearest bundle with one fingertip, the way he touched finished type.

"Harrow the cooper came to me in December," he said. "Stood where you're standing. He'd buried two sons to the press gangs already and they wanted his third, and there was a widow on the valley road with four girls and no pass in the world, and he put his hat in his hands like a beggar and asked me — me, a printer, a maker of receipts and almanacs and the gazette nobody reads past the second column — asked me what my letters were *for*. And I have been asking myself that question for thirty years, Cyrus, thirty years behind this stone, and I discovered I did not have an answer that would keep. A type cutter makes letters. Letters make words. Words make —" he gestured at the box, the gazette frame, the whole shop, the whole trembling apparatus of the world — "everything. Authority prints its words and calls it law. So I printed mine, and I will call it law too, and let God sort the dockets."

"The law will sort *us*," Cyrus whispered. "Father. Papa." The second word escaped before they could stop it, the childish one, the one they hadn't used in a decade, and Aldous flinched as if struck, and Cyrus pressed on because there was no pulling it back now. "Vane knows the shop. He was apprenticed here — he knows your hand, he knows your flaws, he knows the Eastwick tooth in every *e* you ever cast. If he finds one pass, one, he will not need an investigator. He will stand in this room and he will *read* it, the way I read it this morning, and he will know it was you by supper. Is that what you want? Him? Of all the officers in all the companies, the universe sends us the boy who left without goodbye, carrying the warrant?"

Something crossed Aldous's face at the name Vane — a flicker, an old knowledge — and Cyrus seized on it. "You knew he'd come back. You knew when they massed downriver. You've known since before the requisition, haven't you? That's why the box moved to the lower cabinet. That's why the lamp burns past two. You've been preparing for a search, not avoiding one."

Their father was quiet a long moment. Outside, the river made a sound it had begun making lately at odd hours — a deep, structural groan, the sound of a thing vast and patient changing its mind — and both of them turned their heads toward the window at the same instant, the same inherited reflex, and looked at the ice shining dull gray between the banks.

"A search I can beat," Aldous said finally. "I've beaten searches before, in the old country, in the troubles before you were born. There are ways. There are formes within formes, Cyrus; there are rooms in a forme the examiner's eye slides over, because the eye sees what it expects. But a search is not my worry." He raised his eyes to Cyrus's face, and for the first time that morning his steadiness failed, and Cyrus saw underneath it the thing he had been carrying alone through the whole gray winter: not fear of the army. Fear of this conversation. "My worry is that the requisition puts soldiers in this shop day and night. My worry is that my work has painted a mark on this family, and I am too old to run, and your sister counts coins at midnight, and your brother dines with captains. My worry —" his voice dropped to almost nothing — "is that the army doesn't need to find my passes. They only need to find someone willing to trade. And every soul in this town knows the ledger in this shop holds all our true names."

The cold that went through Cyrus then had nothing to do with the weather. They thought of Tam at the officers' table, loud and shining, buying rounds with money he didn't have. They thought of the ledger lying open on the front desk downstairs, unguarded, ordinary, full of every name in Marrow's Bend written in Cyrus's own obedient hand.

"Papa," they said, "what have you made us into?"

Aldous came around the stone. He took Cyrus's hands — the hands he had trained, the hands with the tiny silver scars of the graver, his own gift returned to him — and held them the way he had held them over the first alphabet, firm and warm and absolutely certain.

"A printer's family," he said. "Nothing more. Nothing less. We make the words, Cyrus. We have always made the words. The only sin I've committed is finally choosing which ones." He released their hands and nodded at the open box, at the stags frozen mid-leap, sixty-eight small animals waiting to run north. "Now. The army comes within days, and the box cannot stay in the cabinet, and it cannot stay anywhere obvious, and I am one pair of hands and going blind by lamplight. So I am asking you — not telling, asking, the way I'd ask a fellow craftsman — to help me hide the work where no examiner will think to look." A pause. The river groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere far downstream something cracked like a cannon. "And if your answer is no, close the box, and I will cut no further passes, and we will live small and safe and ashamed, and Harrow's boy goes to the press gang, and the widow's girls go wherever fatherless girls go in a war. Your choice. You're the one in this house who keeps the true accounts. Tell me how they balance."

Cyrus stood over the box for a long time. The light had gone from gray to white to the pale gold of noon, and the dust motes turned above the composing stone like the smallest sorts in creation, and downstairs the ledger lay open with all its faithful, damning names.

Betrayal and pride had braided themselves together somewhere in Cyrus's chest into a single cord, impossible to unpick, and they found they could not tell anymore where the one strand ended and the other began. Their father was a felon. Their father was the finest craftsman in the valley and the bravest man Cyrus had ever stood in a room with, and both of those facts were the same fact, and the army was coming, and the river was waking under the ice, and somewhere in the house Tam was laughing at an officer's joke.

"Under the gazette," Cyrus heard themselves say. Their voice sounded strangely calm, strangely their own, the voice they used at the stone. "We lock the passes inside tomorrow's official forme. The one printed by requisition order, under the captain's own authority. No searcher in the province will break a forme bearing his seal to look for sedition." They looked up at their father. "Get the waste sheets. We have maybe three hours before anyone misses us."

Aldous smiled — a terrible, proud, breaking smile — and reached for the box.

And from the foot of the stairs, in a voice bright with wine and borrowed importance, Tam called down: "Shop! Company at the door — it's the army. They want the ledgers today, and Father — they're asking about paper stock."
