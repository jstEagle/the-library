## 7. Ice Sounds at Night

The river spoke first, before the wind, before the dogs of Marrow's Bend began their midnight arguments. It was a sound Wren Eastwick had known all her life and had never once gotten used to: a deep, grinding groan that rose from under the ice as though the water beneath were turning over in its sleep, restless, dreaming of the sea. The house shivered with it. The window glass hummed in its leaded frame. In the room she shared with her sister-brother — for Cyrus was neither and both and would not be pinned to any word that did not fit — the washbasin water trembled in rings.

Wren lay awake and listened, and what she heard was arithmetic.

She had inherited their mother's gift for numbers, or perhaps their mother had simply died early enough that the gift had nowhere else to go. Aldous counted in ems and picas; Cyrus counted in names and debts owed to the ledger; Tam counted in everything he did not yet have. But Wren counted in coin, and coin was honest in a way people were not. Coin did not flatter. Coin did not promise. A penny was a penny even when it was all you had, and tonight, wrapped in a stocking beneath her mattress, hidden inside a hollowed-out quire of spoiled foolscap, buried in the flour crock and sewn into the hem of her winter coat, the Eastwick fortune came to thirty-one pounds, fourteen shillings, and sixpence.

She knew it to the sixpence because she had counted it four times since supper, moving from cache to cache by candle stub while the house slept, gathering the whole sum onto the kitchen table where it made a modest hill of silver and copper and three gold half-guineas that had been their mother's and were worth more than money.

Thirty-one pounds, fourteen, six.

And against it, the price of the thing.

* * *

She had gone down to the wharf that afternoon in the gray hour between the market's closing and the lamplighter's round, when the fog came up off the water thick enough to drink. The *Northern Lark* sat low at her moorings, black-hulled, her paint gone the color of old tar, and on her deck Master Hobbe Cray stood with his arms folded, watching the ice sheet downstream the way a farmer watches a sky gone green with hail.

Wren had known Cray since she was small enough to steal apples from his hold. He was a smuggler in the way that everyone on the river was a smuggler when the customs men looked elsewhere, which lately was never. He was also, in this season of soldiers and requisitions, the only captain still willing to run north.

"Three berths," Wren had said, keeping her voice flat, her hands in her pockets where they could not betray her. "My father, my sister-brother, my brother Tam."

Cray had not laughed, which she respected. Some men would have.

"Ice breaks within the fortnight," he said. "Maybe less. Wind's come around warm twice this week; you felt it." She had. All afternoon the air off the river had carried the smell of mud and rot and running water, the smell of the year turning over, and every soul in Marrow's Bend had walked a little faster without knowing why. "After the break, nothing moves on this water but army barges. I take who I take before the ice goes, and after that I don't exist."

"The price."

He told her. Then he told her again, slower, in case she had misheard, and then he took off his cap and looked at the planks of the deck as if ashamed to be the man saying it out loud.

"War prices," he said. "Every boat north is booked double. Every skipper knows what his cargo's worth now, and what I'm carrying isn't cargo, it's lives, and lives are dear." He put the cap back on. "One berth, girl. Maybe. If the weather holds and nobody official asks me questions I can't answer. One. And I'm sorry for it, because your family kept my accounts straight for twenty years and I'd sooner cut off my hand than—"

"One berth," Wren repeated, because if she said anything else her voice would crack, and Cray was kind, and kindness was unbearable just now. "When?"

"Dawn after next. Sooner if the ice goes early. I'll fire three shots off the point when I'm ready to load. You hear three shots, you come."

She walked home through fog so thick the town dissolved around her, and she did her arithmetic the whole way, and the numbers never once came out kind.

* * *

Now, past midnight, the sums circled her like the ice circling the pilings.

Thirty-one pounds, fourteen, six. Berth price: forty guineas. Forty-two, if Cray's conscience needed feeding, and consciences always did. Even taking the gold — even melting down the half-guineas their mother had worn at her wedding and pretending they were only metal, which felt like a second, smaller death — she was short. Short by eight pounds or more. Short by a season of mending other people's seams, of selling her hair, of every honest desperate thing a woman could do in a town filling up with soldiers who paid for what they took in scrip worth less than the paper.

Short by one human being.

That was the arithmetic that would not resolve, no matter how she turned it. Four Eastwicks. One berth. And subtraction, in her family, meant leaving someone behind on a bank about to be overrun by an army that already believed there was a traitor's press in Marrow's Bend, because there was — because Father had made certain of it, printing God knew what in the small hours with the shutters drawn, and Cyrus knew, Wren was sure Cyrus knew, though neither of them had said it aloud, because saying it aloud would make it a fact and facts could be seized as evidence.

She got up. The floor was cold enough to bite through her stockings. Cyrus slept in the narrow bed by the wall, turned toward the door as always, one hand open on the blanket, the way they had slept since childhood — ready, Wren had always thought, to be called upon, to rise and serve whoever needed them, as if some part of Cyrus had decided long ago that being useful was the only safe way to be seen. Their breathing was slow and even. Their face in the moonlight was young and old at once.

*I cannot choose,* Wren thought, standing barefoot in the dark. *I am not built to choose.*

But the river was going to choose for all of them, and soon, and it would not ask permission.

* * *

Another groan from the water, longer this time, rising through a register — a moan, almost musical, the sound of a frozen mile of river flexing its whole spine. Then a crack like a musket shot, sharp and close, somewhere near the point, and Wren's heart answered it beat for beat. The old men of the town said you could read the ice like type if you'd lived beside it long enough: the flat dull booming of January ice, thick and trustworthy; the brittle ticking of February; and this — this wet, articulate groaning, this conversation the river was having with itself — meant the thaw had already begun underneath, in the dark, where no one could see it working.

The last ferry north. Dawn after next. Three shots off the point.

She dressed properly, quietly, and went downstairs with the candle unlit, because she knew every stair and board in this house the way Cyrus knew the type cases, and light was a witness she didn't want.

The kitchen was cold. Her breath hung in front of her. She set the money out on the table again, all of it, stacking the coins in towers by denomination, and worked the problem one more time with her lips moving, the way their mother used to when she totted up the shop's accounts on a slate.

Forty-two guineas. Thirty-one pounds, fourteen, six. Even at the old rate, before war inflated every price on the river, she was short. Unless —

Unless she sold the coat with the coins sewn in its hem, which was itself worth two pounds. Unless she asked Mrs. Quill at the chandlery to buy back the mourning brooch, which Mrs. Quill had wanted for three years and would pay shamefully little for precisely because she knew Wren was desperate. Unless she went to the officers' mess and let Lieutenant What's-his-name buy her a sherry and mistake her for the kind of sister Tam pretended to be, and charmed requisition scrip out of him at rates no honest exchanger would give—

Her hands stopped over the coins.

No. Not that. She would sell her mother's gold, her own hair, the roof over her head, before she sat down at a table with those men and smiled. Tam could do it. Tam did it nightly, laughing, drinking their family deeper into danger with every toast — *the Eastwicks are loyal tradespeople, Captain, ask anyone* — while the same captains held papers proving otherwise. Tam thought charm was a currency. Wren knew better. Charm was credit, and credit was debt wearing a friendly face, and someday soon the army would call in everything her brother had borrowed.

Which left, in the end, the only arithmetic that mattered, the one she had been avoiding since the wharf:

Four names. One berth.

Father, who would refuse to go anyway — she knew it the way she knew the sixpence count. Aldous Eastwick would stand in his shop among his cases and formes and ink stones and say something quiet and terrible like *a printer does not abandon a live press*, and he would mean it, and arguing with Aldous was like arguing with a foundation stone. He had already chosen his place. Perhaps he had chosen it years ago, when he first set forbidden type in the dark.

Tam, who assumed the berth was his — she had heard him say it at supper, casually, monstrously casually, *when I'm settled up north I'll send for the rest of you*, as if the family were cargo to be shipped in lots, as if he were the heir to something besides his own appetite. Tam would fight for it, too. Tam would make it ugly.

And Cyrus.

Wren looked up at the dark ceiling and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Cyrus, who had spent a whole life being the invisible one, the useful one, the one whose name wasn't on anything because Cyrus had carved a new name for themself and the world had declined to learn it. Who kept everyone's accounts, including, Wren suspected, the dangerous ones. Who would give up the berth without being asked, would insist on it, would dress the sacrifice up in practical language — *someone has to mind Father, someone has to keep the shop, you go, I'm needed here* — and would stand on the wharf and wave and mean every word and be lying with every word, the way unselfish people lie, generously, completely.

If Wren let the arithmetic decide, the arithmetic would kill Cyrus. Quietly. Efficiently. The invisible one stays behind; the ledger balances; the story ends without them in it.

No.

She opened her hands. The coins caught a little moonlight off the window, dull silver, honest copper. Love measured in coin — that was what she had, that was what she was good for, the plain daughter with the plain gift for numbers. Very well. Let the love be plain. Let it be fierce and specific and unsentimental as a scale.

She would find the missing pounds. She would sell the brooch, the coat, the last of her dignity if it came to that, and she would put every soul she had onto that boat if she had to row them out to it herself through the broken ice. And if Cray truly took only one —

Then it would be Cyrus. Cyrus on the boat, north, alive, carrying the steel punches and the knowledge in their hands, the alphabet no army could confiscate. Cyrus would rage. Cyrus would refuse. Cyrus would have to be lifted bodily over the gunwale, and Wren would do it, and Cyrus would never forgive her, and Wren could live with that. Being forgiven was cheap. Keeping her sibling breathing was the expensive thing, and she intended to pay.

She was still standing there, hands flat on the table, when the third sound came.

Not the groan. Not the crack. Something worse: a long, wet, tearing sigh from downstream, near the point, like a great seam splitting — and then silence, an enormous attentive silence, the whole river holding its breath at once.

Wren went to the window. The fog had thinned. Moonlight lay along the ice in a long white road, and far out, near the channel, she saw it: a seam of open water, black as ink, no wider than a ribbon, widening as she watched. The river had found its first wound. Under the moon the water moved in it, quick and living, and flakes of rotten ice drifted down on the current and did not return.

The thaw was not coming within the fortnight.

The thaw had begun tonight.

Behind her, upstairs, a floorboard creaked — her father's step, or Cyrus's, someone else awake and listening to the same water. Down at the wharf, faint across the distance, she heard Hobbe Cray's voice raised, and the creak of halyards, and she understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the season that the schedule was dead, that *dawn after next* had become *dawn*, that the three shots might sound while the household still slept.

She swept the coins back into the stocking with shaking hands and stood in the freezing kitchen doing the final sum, faster now, the numbers blurring:

One night. Four Eastwicks. One berth.

And somewhere below the window, the river kept opening, patient as arithmetic, ready to take whatever the family failed to carry away.
