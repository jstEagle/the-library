## 8. An Old Apprenticeship

The knock came at the hour when Marrow's Bend had no right to visitors — past ten, when even the garrison's lanterns in the square had burned down to embers and the river was a black seam stitching the town to its own reflection. Cyrus was alone in the shop, trimming quills by tallow light, and knew before they crossed the boards who stood outside. There is a way certain people knock: twice, a pause, then once more, as if the last knock were an afterthought or an apology. Ten years had not worn that rhythm out of Elias Vane's hands.

Cypress opened the door to cold and the smell of wet wool and army leather.

"Captain." The word sat strangely on Cyrus's tongue, like type set in the wrong face — correct in shape, wrong in spirit.

"Lieutenant Eastwick," Vane said, and then, catching himself, "I beg your pardon. You were never a lieutenant. I've forgotten my ranks and kept everything else."

He held a folded paper. Snow had gotten into the brim of his hat; it melted there in small beads, catching the lamplight, so that he seemed crowned with something cheaply brilliant. Behind him the street lay empty, the drifts unmarked except by his own boots, which meant he had come the long way around, past the tannery, avoiding the sentries' route. A man arriving secretly to a requisitioned shop he already commanded. Cyrus stepped aside and let the contradiction in.

* * *

Memory has a composing stone of its own. Cyrus had learned long ago that recollection, like type, could be redistributed — that the same letters rearranged told different truths. So it was with the summer of the apprenticeship, ten years gone, which Cyrus had re-set a hundred times in the dark behind the eyes.

In one setting, it went like this: two young people at the casting bench, August heat pressing the shop's smell into their skin — molten type-metal, oak gall, the vinegar bite of fresh ink. Elias with his sleeves rolled, forearms dusted silver with antimony, laughing because Aldous had caught him kerning an *A* too tight against a *W*, and Aldous had said, *Space is not emptiness, boy, space is what makes the word legible,* and Elias had whispered sideways to Cyrus, *Your father talks about empty space like other men talk about God,* and Cyrus had laughed until the laugh became something else, something warm and low in the chest that had no name then and did not need one.

In another setting, the same summer ran differently: Elias bent over the stone, dark hair falling across his brow, and Cyrus watching him rather than the forme, learning nothing about spacing and everything about wanting, though neither of them called it that. Their hands had touched once over a chase — reaching for the same quoins key — and neither had pulled away quickly enough for innocence, nor slowly enough for confession. The metal between them had been cold. Their fingers had not been.

And in the final setting, the one Cyrus hated but returned to most often: October, frost on the windows like ferns pressed in glass, and the bench empty. No note. No farewell cut in type, which would have been the obvious cruelty for a printer's apprentice to commit — a single line of twelve-point set and proofed and left where it could not be missed. Nothing. Just the absence, distributed through the shop like a wrong font mixed into a page, every line slightly off, no single letter to blame.

Aldous had said only, *Some sorts get pied. You sweep them up and go on.* But Cyrus had swept for years and never found all of him.

* * *

Now Vane stood inside the door, dripping, holding his paper as though it might go off.

"You've come at a strange hour for official business."

"It isn't official." He removed his hat, hesitated, and put it back on again — a man who had forgotten the rituals of rooms without rank in them. "Colonel Ashbury wants a notice posted by morning. Condemnation of the ferry-house attack. It's to run under the town's name."

"The town doesn't have a name anymore. You requisitioned it along with everything else."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had been amputated. "The notice needs setting tonight. Composing and proofing before the gazette locks tomorrow. I'm told the only compositor left in Marrow's Bend is—" He stopped, corrected course. "Aldous is ill?"

"My father coughs like a bellows these days. It keeps the fire going, at least."

"And you stayed."

"I stayed."

They regarded each other across ten feet of floor and ten years of silence, and Cyrus thought: this is how it will be done, then. Not with questions about where he went or why he left, but with work. Printers forgive printers the way sailors forgive the sea — by returning to it.

"Show me," Cyrus said, and held out a hand for the paper.

* * *

The notice was four hundred words of army prose — *whereas* and *hereby* stacked like sandbags, condemning men Cyrus had drunk cider with, naming no names but promising names soon. Cyrus read it twice under the lamp while Vane hovered at the edge of the light like a moth that had learned caution.

"You disagree with it," Vane said. Not a question.

"I disagree with its justification lines. Whoever drafted it double-spaced his reasoning and triple-spaced his threats." Cyrus laid the sheet down flat. "It'll compose cleaner than it deserves. Bad argument sets up fast; the letters know they're temporary."

Vane made a sound that in another man might have been a laugh strangled by rank. "You haven't changed."

"You have. You used to talk."

"I used to be allowed to."

Cyrus began pulling sorts.

The work took over the way it always did — hands moving on their own ancient errands, the case speaking to the fingers before the mind caught up. Lowercase first, then the small stock of italic reserved for emphasis, the brass rules, the leads. The shop filled with its old liturgy: the click of metal into metal, the shush of the stick filling, the soft iron music of a page assembling itself out of chaos. Cyrus set the colonel's condemnations line by line and felt, with each justification, the peculiar doubled consciousness of the compositor's treason of craft — making the enemy's argument beautiful because beauty was the trade, and the trade did not ask whose.

Vane watched from the shadowed side of the stone. Cyrus could feel his attention the way one feels weather through a wall.

"You hold the stick differently," he said at last. "Lower. Closer to the body."

"Aldous corrected me after you left. Said I'd learned bad habits from an apprentice who set with his elbows out like a startled goose."

"Did he say that?"

"He said *startled heron.* I improved upon it."

This time Vane did laugh — briefly, guiltily, like a man spending money he owed elsewhere — and the sound unlocked something in the room. The tallow flame leaned. Outside, far off, the river gave one long groan, ice flexing in its sleep, and both of them turned their heads toward it and then away from it at the same speed, the same angle, and Cyrus understood with a lurch that some synchronies do not lapse; they only wait.

* * *

Memory again, unbidden, mid-sort:

The winter of their third year, the presses down with a broken spring, and the two of them assigned to redistribute a font of battered Brevier — sorting thousands of loose letters back into the case, an evening's tedium made bearable by talk that had stopped being about type hours before. Elias telling about his father, the wool factor, dead of a lung fever; about the north country he meant to see; about the word *soldier* spoken carelessly, the way boys speak of distant weather. And Cyrus, sorting *a* into *a* and *b* into *b*, saying the truest thing they knew how to say, which was: *You could stay. There's enough work. Father would take you on permanent.*

And Elias looking at the letter in his hand as if it had spoken instead — a lowercase *e*, worn thin at the crossbar — and saying, *There's a whole atlas of places I haven't been, Cy. A man can't be a comma in someone else's line forever.*

*Cy.* He had called them that. No one else ever had. The name had fit like a quoin — small, hard, holding everything else tight — and when he left without goodbye he had taken the name with him, and Cyrus had gone unnamed in that particular way for a decade, answering to Eastwick, to *the compositor*, to nothing.

Until the day they carved their own name out of the silence he'd left. That was a story for fewer tellings. Some type you keep locked in the chase.

* * *

The forme was half full when Vane came around the stone and stood too near, close enough that Cyrus could smell gun oil under the ink, and snowmelt, and beneath both, faint as a watermark, the shop-soap they had both used on their hands at nineteen — the army hadn't managed to wash that out of him after all.

"You're angry," he said quietly.

"I'm composing. Anger ruins justification."

"Cy."

The name landed like a piece of type dropped from a height. Cyrus's hand stilled over the case, and for one dangerous moment the whole shop tilted — ten years collapsing into the space between *whereas* and *hereby* — and then discipline, that older loyalty, closed its fist and the hand went on picking letters.

"Don't," Cyrus said. "Don't spend that name now, at midnight, over a sedition notice. It's been in the case too long. It may have shifted."

"I wrote you. Twice, from the depot at Harlow. Care of the shop."

"You didn't."

"They must have gone astray."

"Or they were intercepted by a coward's address." Cyrus heard their own voice sharpen into something they did not recognize — the edge of the wound finally finding its whetstone. "You walked out of here with everything you owned and none of your reasons. Do you know what my father said? *Some sorts get pied.* He let you become spilled type, Elias. Scattered. Unrecoverable. And I swept this floor for six years looking for—"

The sentence broke against what it was carrying. Cyrus bent lower over the stick and set three lines in furious, perfect alignment.

Vane did not apologize. Cyrus felt him decide not to — felt the apology rise in him and be pressed, like a proof pulled too early, and set aside. Instead he said, "Harlow was a depot for deserters being marched back in irons. I stood at the gate and watched them pass and I wrote you that night and I tore it up, because what could I say that wouldn't sound like either a recruitment poster or an epitaph? Then I tore up the second one for the same reason, eight months later, and by the third year tearing it up was simply what I did instead of writing. Habit is the cheapest ink, Cy. It writes anything."

The river groaned again, closer now, a sound like a giant turning over under blankets. Neither of them mentioned the thaw, or the ferry, or the berths. There would be time — there would have to be time — for arithmetic.

"You watched them in irons," Cyrus said. "And you stayed in."

"I stayed in." His voice had gone flat as a lead slug. "Because the alternative was a rope, and because staying let me do small crooked kindnesses from inside, and because—" he stopped, started again, "—because I told myself the uniform was just a very large chase, locking me into a forme I didn't compose, and that someday there'd be a press run worth having been locked in for."

"And is this it? This notice?" Cyrus gestured at the half-built forme, the colonel's *whereases* standing in their ranks like soldiers of the alphabet. "Is this the run?"

"No," Vane said. Too quickly. Too softly.

Then he reached past Cyrus — close enough that his sleeve brushed theirs, close enough that the memory of a quoins key rose up between their hands like a ghost with excellent timing — and lifted the galley proof of the finished notice. He read his superior's arguments in the lamplight, all four hundred words of them, and Cyrus watched his face while he read, and saw there the expression of a man proofing his own gallows bill.

"There are irregularities in this text," Vane said at last, and set the proof down.

"It composed exactly as supplied."

"Mm." He tapped one line. "But look here. *Whereas the persons responsible shall be apprehended and punished to the fullest extent* — and then the next clause contradicts it, promises pardon for informants. Sloppy drafting. Almost as though whoever drafted it wanted room in the margins."

"Room in the margins," Cyrus repeated, not understanding, understanding completely, refusing to understand.

Vane looked at them then, fully, for the first time since the door — the lamplight in his eyes, the snow gone entirely from his hat brim, the ten years standing openly between them at last, counted and confessed — and he said, in the voice of the boy at the redistribution bench and not the captain at the door:

"I need to know what Aldous has been printing at night. Not for Ashbury. For the edition we're going to run after this one, when this notice goes up on the post tomorrow morning and comes down by noon, torn off by the same hands that put it there. There are typesetters in this town still loyal to something better, Cy, and I intend to find out if one of them lives above this shop."

Somewhere below the floorboards, in the dark beneath the stone, fresh type lay hidden in a shallow drawer, and Cyrus thought of it lying there like a heart under ribs.

"You'd be asking me to hang beside him," Cyrus said.

"I'd be asking you to set the stick," Vane said. "One line at a time. Like always."

Outside, the ice spoke again — longer this time, a cracking note running the length of the river like the first letter of a word too large yet to read — and Cyrus, hand already moving toward the case, could not have said whether they were reaching for the type or for him.
