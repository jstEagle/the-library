## 9. The List

The summons came the way winter comes to Marrow's Bend — not announced but simply arrived, seeping under doors, frosting the windows of ordinary hours.

A boy in army blue knocked at the shop at half past seven, breath steaming, and handed Cyrus a card of heavy cream stock with a printed line across its face: *Captain E. Vane requests the presence of the Eastwick ledger-keeper at his quarters, eight o'clock.* The type was clean, well-cast, kerned with a hand Cyrus almost admired before they remembered whose hand it was.

They read the card four times on the doorstep while the boy waited, stamping his boots against the cold, and each reading rearranged something in their chest. *Requests.* Not orders. Vane had chosen that word deliberately — Cyrus could see him choosing it, standing over his case the way he used to, tilting his head at a line until it agreed to be beautiful. Ten years dead silence between them and still he knew how to speak in the grammar of print: a request instead of a requisition was a lowercase letter where a capital would do, a whisper set where shouting belonged.

"I'm to wait for an answer," the boy said.

"Tell him the ledger-keeper will come."

Cyrus put on their good coat — the one without ink at every cuff — and did not look back at the shop, because looking back at the shop meant looking at Aldous, who sat at the cutting bench pretending to examine a burin, and Aldous's pretense was never good enough to survive being witnessed.

* * *

Vane's quarters were the old customs house, its brass fittings long since stripped for the war, its tall windows fogged with river damp. A sentry let Cyrus through without taking their name, which was itself a kind of message: the captain had already given it.

He stood at the far side of the room when Cyrus entered, dressed down to shirtsleeves despite the chill, and he had done something Cyrus had never seen him do in the old days — he had cleared the composing stone that served as his table and left it bare. Nothing between them but wood grain and lamplight. He knew what a stone meant to someone raised on one. He was saying: *no work here today. Only words.*

"Cyrus." He said it carefully, the way you set a fragile sort, testing the fit before locking it in.

"Captain." They kept their voice level as a galley floor.

"You cut your hair differently."

"You still stand like a man afraid to touch anything he might be ordered to burn."

Something crossed his face — the ghost of the boy who'd once laughed so hard over a misprint of the word *providence* that he'd had to sit down among the galleys. It went as quickly as it came. He gestured to a chair. Cyrus stayed standing. Some part of refusing the chair was refusing everything else that would follow it.

Vane did not press. He lifted a leather folio from the shelf behind him and laid it open on the bare stone, turning it so Cyrus could read.

It was not an army document. It was better made than that — a private compilation, pages of fine rag paper stitched by hand, and every page covered in names. Names in three columns. Names annotated with occupations, with streets, with small careful marks beside certain entries. Cyrus's eye, trained since childhood to read a page whole before reading it in parts, took it in all at once, and their stomach went to ice water.

Beside the entry for *Harrow & Sons, cartage* there was a mark. Beside *Merrick, midwife*. Beside a dozen others. And near the bottom of the second column, written in a different hand — cramped, official, the hand of clerks everywhere — a single line:

*Forged passes recovered at Ferry Point. Superior execution. Professional. Believed produced within town. Printer unknown.*

"The army does not suspect a printer," Vane said quietly. "The army knows one. What it doesn't know is a name." He closed the folio, but too slowly; he wanted Cyrus to see the last page, and Cyrus saw it. A blank column waiting at the bottom. Room for one more annotation. One more mark.

"And this?" Cyrus asked. Their voice held. Later they would marvel at how it held.

"This is what I was sent here to finish." He came around the stone, close enough now that Cyrus could smell the lamp oil on him and, beneath it, faint as a watermark, the memory of lead and solvent that clings forever to anyone who has worked a press. "Requisition of the press was the pretext. Everyone knows armies don't need printing offices; they have plenty. I was sent for the list. To complete it." He paused. "I've been slow. I've been as slow as slowness can be made. But there are men upriver who count my days like a creditor."

"Then be slow no longer," Cyrus said. "Take your list to them."

"Cyrus—"

"Take it, and leave this town, and may your boat find the same thin ice you found when you left here ten years ago."

The words came out with a violence that surprised them both. Vane flinched — actually flinched, a small motion at the shoulder like a man hearing a forme crash — and Cyrus understood, in the space of a heartbeat, that he had expected the anger, had perhaps rehearsed for it, and that it hurt him anyway. Good, some iron part of them thought. Let it hurt him accurately.

"That's fair," he said. "That's fair, and I'll carry it. But hear me first, because I only get to say this once and then I'm either your enemy or something worse." He braced both hands on the empty stone, and for a moment he was seventeen again, leaning over a locked chase, telling Cyrus about the sea towns he'd print his way to someday. "If I give them nothing, they send another officer. That man will not have set type beside you. He will not know what a quoin smells like or why a compositor's hands are sacred. He'll have the passes examined by men who know forgery, and they'll trace the paper stock, and the paper stock comes from Marrow's Bend, and after that it is arithmetic. House by house. Name by name." He touched the folio. "This list is not my invention, Cyrus. It already exists in the colonel's head. All I hold is the pen. And a pen can be slowed."

"Slowed how?"

"By a name that satisfies." He said it fast, the way you tear bandaging. "Give me a printer. Give me any name that plausibly answers to those passes, and I can write *resolved* beside the inquiry, and the inquiry dies in a drawer in the capital, and this list dies with it, and nobody hangs. Nobody." His eyes were very steady and very tired. "You know this trade. You know how a lie must be plausible, not true. Give me a plausible lie."

* * *

The room went very quiet. Outside, somewhere along the quay, soldiers were drilling, and the sound of their boots reached the customs house as a distant pulse, like the river knocking at its own banks.

Cyrus thought of the floorboards. Of the fresh type hidden beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, cast in the last month in secret — Aldous working nights, the old man's shoulders bent over the mould like a priest over a reliquary. They thought of the passes themselves, discovered folded inside a hymnal two weeks ago: *Permit the bearer to travel north,* signed with a commandant's flourish Aldous had copied so perfectly it made Cyrus weep with something that had no clean name. Passes that had already carried the Merrick boys out of a conscription camp. Passes that would carry more.

They thought of Wren counting coins by candlelight, though they didn't yet know she was doing it; they only felt her doing it, the way you feel weather coming through a scar.

And beneath all of it, rising like groundwater, they thought of the blank column at the bottom of Vane's list, and understood with terrible clarity what was actually being asked. Not a betrayal. Not exactly. Something more delicate and more damnable: an invitation to choose, openly, on the record, whose side their loyalty fell on. The army did not want a printer's name. The army wanted proof that the Eastwick ledger-keeper could be bought, and if it could, then every quiet family in every quiet river town could be bought, and the rebellion's great network of ordinary hands would rot from its ends inward.

To refuse was to mark themselves. To accept was to mark everyone else.

And Vane — God help them — Vane was offering the only version of this trap that came with a ladder built in. He was risking his own neck to build it. Cyrus could see the risk on him the way you see underpainting through thin glaze: the falsifiable report he'd have to file, the colonel who would eventually ask why the inquiry into a professional forger had been closed by a single uncorroborated name. If this ever unraveled, it unraveled through Elias Vane first.

"Why?" Cyrus asked. The word cracked down the middle. "Ten years, Elias. Ten years of nothing. Not a letter, not a postmark. You left me to learn from Father alone, and you know what that means — you know what his teaching costs. And now you stand in a stolen customs house offering me treason wrapped in mercy, and you want me to trust the motive?"

For a long moment he looked at them, and whatever he had prepared — Cyrus could see he'd prepared something, some composed and typeset explanation ten years in the making — he abandoned it. He shook his head slowly.

"I don't want you to trust the motive," he said. "Motive is the first thing a good forger fakes. I want you to watch what I do. Judges of character are fooled by reasons. Compositors aren't — they judge by the impression left in the paper." He straightened. "Here is the impression I intend to leave: no name from me reaches that colonel except one you give me freely. If you give none, I will stall, and stall, and take what falls on me when the stalling ends. That is the whole offer. There's no clause hidden in the leading. I learned typography from the same hands you did, Cyrus. We don't cheat the measure."

Silence. The drill boots pulsed. Somewhere below, the river shifted a plate of ice and let it groan.

And then Cyrus Eastwick lied for the first time in their life, and found — this was the terrifying part, the part that would keep them awake for years — that they were fluent.

"It was a traveling man," they said. Smoothly. Without a flicker. "A journeyman compositor who worked the shop three seasons ago. German-trained; Father hired him off the Philadelphia road for the gazette run. He lodged with us through the summer, then moved north. He had samples of every commandant's signature — collected them, the way some men collect stamps. He used our stock without Father's knowledge; Father discharged him for it, that's the truth of the discharge, though we never reported the theft because there was nothing to report it *to*." They heard their own voice lay the lie into the room with perfect spacing, word after word locked tight in its line, justified flush to both margins of belief. "His name was—" and here Cyrus performed the masterpiece, the hesitation, the small frown of retrieval, the scrupulous refusal to invent beyond memory — "Kessler. Anton Kessler. He'd be forty now, heavy in the shoulders, a scar through his left eyebrow from a slipped burin. He spoke of going on to Albany. He'll be long gone, Captain, and good riddance."

Every detail was false and every detail was load-bearing. The scar was invented; the eyebrow gave a searching officer something specific to search for. The age and build gave him a body that could not be found matching any description of Aldous Eastwick. The direction of flight pulled the inquiry north, away from the river, away from the passes' real routes. And the theft-and-discharge story explained the paper stock, explained the expertise, exonerated the shop while explaining why the shop had stayed silent — silence that would otherwise have been the first noose.

Vane stood very still through all of it, watching them the way he used to watch a pull come off the press — that absolute attention, that withholding of judgment until the sheet was dry. And Cyrus watched him watch them, terror and exhilaration running through their blood in a single braided current, because this was the moment of truth inside the lie: if Vane believed it, the lie was good enough for the army. If Vane did not believe it, then he knew Cyrus was protecting the forger, and he would have to decide, right now, whether to hang them both.

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Straightened.

"Anton Kessler," Vane repeated. He took up a pencil — a pencil, not a pen; even now, even here, leaving himself room to erase — and wrote the name at the bottom of the blank column. Then, beside it, the annotation Cyrus had dreaded and now watched become salvation: *Resolved. Subject departed jurisdiction. No further inquiry warranted.*

He closed the folio. His hand was not quite steady. So he had known. Of course he had known — he'd set type beside these hands; he'd seen Cyrus catch a misregistered color at fifteen; he knew precisely how fluent a liar this shop could raise, and he had asked anyway, and accepted the answer anyway, because the answer was the thing they had both agreed upon without agreeing, the way two compositors fall into rhythm at the same stone.

"He sounds like a remarkable man," Vane said quietly. "This Kessler."

"He was taught by the best," Cyrus said. "Then he left without saying goodbye."

The blow landed. Vane received it standing. "Some goodbyes," he said, "take ten years to set properly. The type has to cool between casts."

Cyrus turned for the door, because staying one minute longer would break the whole lock, and because their knees had begun to understand what their voice had concealed. At the threshold Vane spoke again, low, not asking to be answered.

"There's a warm wind coming up the valley, Cyrus. The ferry masters feel it in their bones. Whatever your family means to do on that river — do it soon."

* * *

Cyrus walked home along the quay with the drill boots fading behind them and their own heart hammering out a new rhythm, strange and swift and not entirely unpleasant: the pulse of a person who has just discovered a capacity in themselves the way you discover a hidden tray in an old case — *so this is what the type was for.*

They had lied. Fluently, artfully, with craft — with *their father's craft*, the whole discipline of measure and impression turned for one morning into a weapon, and the town's hundreds slept safer for it, and the price would come later, prices always came later, but oh, the exhilaration was a clean fire all its own.

They were halfway home before they noticed the light burning in their own kitchen window past midnight hours, and stopped in the lane.

Wren was awake. Wren was awake and the candle was moving — he could see the shadows shifting behind the curtain, circling the table, gathering and recounting. Counting something. Coins?

Through the frost-furred glass, Cyrus watched their sister's silhouette bend low over the table, sweep the little bright heap together, and begin again.
