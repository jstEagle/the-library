## 10. Wren's Bargain

The smuggler's name was Hollis Fen, and he smelled of tar and river rot and, faintly, of oranges — a smell so foreign to Marrow's Bend that Wren knew at once he had been farther north than any of them, past the checkpoints, past the burned bridges, into the country the newspapers still called by its old name.

She found him the way one finds anything forbidden in a small town: by listening. He kept a room above the cooperage on Slate Lane, and he drank at the Anchor & Chain only on nights when the moon was too thin for patrols, and he had a way of sitting with his back to the wall that told her everything she needed before he said a word. Wren Eastwick had spent nineteen years learning to read people the way her father read proofs — quickly, silently, catching the errors before they set.

So on a Tuesday raw with sleet, she pulled her hood down and went to the Anchor & Chain alone.

The taproom was nearly empty. A fire guttered in the hearth, and two soldiers from the occupation garrison played dice in the corner with the boneless boredom of men who had not yet been ordered to do anything terrible. She did not look at them. She looked at Hollis Fen, who sat where she knew he would sit, nursing a cup of cider gone flat, and she crossed the room and took the chair across from him without asking.

Fen raised his eyebrows. He was perhaps fifty, weathered to the color of old rope, with a beard cropped close enough to show the white scar that ran from his ear to his collarbone. His eyes were pale as river ice, and they moved over her face the way a man checks a knot.

"You're not the landlord's girl," he said. "Landlord's girl is prettier."

"I'm Wren Eastwick."

That landed. She watched it land — the small stillness in him, the flicker of recalculation. Everyone in Marrow's Bend knew the Eastwicks. The printer's family. The family whose ledger held every debt and birth and burial in town, the ledger the army had carried off wrapped in oilcloth like a relic, or a weapon. The family whose name was now spoken in half-voices, in doorways, with glances toward the square.

"Eastwick," he repeated. He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. "You've got nerve, coming here."

"I've got money," she said. "I'm told you have berths."

He studied her for a long moment. Outside, the wind came off the river and rattled the shutters, carrying with it the sound of the ice shifting — that deep, groaning complaint the thawing river made at night, like a giant turning over in a bed too small for him. Wren had heard it every night this week. It got under her skin worse than the cold did.

"Who told you that?" Fen asked quietly.

"No one. I can count. There are boats going north when the ice clears, and there are men who run them who aren't the ferry company, because the ferry company keeps manifests, and manifests get read by soldiers." She leaned forward. "I want two places. North, past the narrows. I'll pay in coin."

"And your father knows you're buying passage behind his back?"

"My father doesn't know I'm here, and if you tell him, neither of us gets paid."

Something moved at the corner of Fen's mouth. Not quite a smile; more the shape a smile leaves behind after it has died. "Two places," he said. "When the river opens. That could be a week. Could be three days. Could be tomorrow, if this wind holds warm."

"Tomorrow suits me fine."

"It won't suit you fine. Nobody's ever ready when the river goes." He rubbed the scar at his throat, an unconscious gesture, like worrying a rosary. "It's thirty crowns a head, and I'll be honest with you, girl, because you came here straight and didn't waste my time: honest prices are what I deal in. Thirty each, paid in advance, no refunds if you lose your nerve. And I take payment knowing what I know."

"Which is what?"

"Which is that Eastwicks are marked goods right now." He said it without cruelty, the way a man reports the weather. "There's an army captain asking questions about printers. There's a brother of yours drinking with officers at the Anchor most nights — don't look surprised, everyone in this town can see him doing it. And there's a warrant being drafted, or the shape of one, for sedition. If I carry an Eastwick and we're stopped, I don't lose cargo. I lose my boat and possibly my neck. That's built into the price."

Wren felt her jaw tighten. She made herself unclench it. Tam. Drinking with officers. She had suspected; hearing it said aloud by a stranger, flatly, as common knowledge — that was different. That was a stone dropped into water she couldn't see the bottom of.

"Twenty-five each," she said. "Fifty for the pair. Paid in advance, tonight if you like."

"Fifty-five."

"Forty-eight."

He laughed then, a short bark that startled the dice players into a momentary silence. "You haggle like a fishwife and count like a banker. Where'd a printer's daughter learn that?"

"Counting is the family trade. Printing is just counting with letters." She held his pale eyes. "Forty-eight. Because you'll want the goodwill of the family whose shop prints whatever government ends up standing when this is over, and goodwill is worth eight crowns even in a war."

The smile-remnant returned to his mouth, and this time it almost reached his eyes. "Done. Fifty less four. When the ice breaks, you come to the willow stake below the mill, an hour before dawn, and you bring nothing you can't run with and nobody you haven't told me about." He paused. "Nobody you *haven't* told me about," he repeated, meaningfully. "I've had passengers bring surprises before. Surprises get people shot."

"Two," Wren said. "Me and my sister. That's all."

"Sister," Fen repeated, and something in his tone made her realize she had said it easily, naturally, the way she always had, and that in a town where Cyrus Eastwick answered to neither *son* nor *daughter*, the word might mean more than she intended. She lifted her chin.

"My sibling," she corrected, and hated the correction even as she made it, because Cyrus had never needed correcting. "Cyrus. You know the name."

"Everyone knows the name now." Fen drained his flat cider and stood, dropping coins beside his cup. "Willow stake. Hour before dawn. Whenever that is." He pulled his coat collar up. "And girl — don't tell your father either. Fathers make plans, and plans are heavier than luggage."

* * *

The walk home took her along the river road, and she walked it slowly, because the river at night deserved slow walking. The ice had gone gray and rotten at the edges, dark water weeping up through cracks, and beneath the whole frozen breadth of it the current muttered and knocked like a prisoner demanding release. In the lamplight spilling from the tannery windows she could see the surface heaving in long, slow swells, as though something enormous were breathing under the glass.

Two berths. She had two berths. Her heart was still beating too fast from the bargaining, from the reckless joy of having *done* something, of having converted fear into arithmetic and arithmetic into escape. Fifty minus four crowns, sewn into the lining of her coat, which meant —

Which meant the tin box under the floorboard in her room, which meant counting, tonight, before Aldous woke, before anyone stirred, because hope without a sum attached to it was just another word for dreaming, and Wren Eastwick did not dream. She planned.

Her room was at the top of the stairs in the east wing of the shop-house, under the slope of the roof, cold enough in winter that frost bloomed on the inside of the panes in fern patterns. She lit no lamp. She knelt, worked loose the floorboard with her fingernails — she had done it a hundred times since she was twelve, and the nails of her right hand were permanently bent from it — and lifted out the tin that had once held tea and now held everything the family had not spent.

She poured the coins out onto the blanket in the dark, sorting by feel: the heavy crowns, the lighter half-crowns, the shillings that rang thinnest, the odd foreign pieces Aldous had taken over the years from travelers paying for broadsides and pamphlets. She stacked them. Counted them again. Forty-one crowns' worth, all told, with the shillings making up the difference in ragged fractions.

Not enough. Not yet enough. Seven crowns short, plus the four she'd haggled away, eleven in all, and the household purse in the kitchen crock held barely three, and taking from it would raise questions she could not answer.

Eleven crowns. She sat back on her heels in the freezing room and did the arithmetic of desperation. Her good shawl could go to Mrs. Prewitt, who had wanted it since autumn — three crowns, maybe four if she threw in the mittens. The silver sugar tongs, Grandmother Eastwick's, that Tam had already tried to sell twice — two. The rest, the rest she would find somehow, she would—

The floorboard at the doorway creaked.

Wren's whole body went rigid, coins fanned across her knees, moonlight through the frosted glass laying pale stripes over the scattered silver. In the doorway, filling it, stood a slight figure wrapped in a blanket, hair loose, face invisible against the darkness of the hall behind.

"Cyrus," she breathed. "You scared ten years off me."

"What are you doing?" Cyrus's voice was low, careful, stripped of everything. They stepped into the room, and the moonlight caught their face — those watchful eyes, their father's eyes but sharper, eyes that had learned young to see everything because being seen had never once been safe.

"Nothing. Go back to bed."

"You're counting money on your knees at midnight with the window frost for light." Cyrus crouched, unhurried, and picked up one of the crown pieces, turning it. "This is the escape fund. Mother's fund. We swore we'd never touch it except—"

"Except when touching it was the only thing left," Wren finished. "It's the only thing left."

Silence. The river spoke through the walls, that long groaning note, nearer now, or perhaps only louder because the night was stiller. Cyrus set the coin down among the others with great gentleness, the way they handled type — everything handled gently, everything placed exactly.

"Tell me," Cyrus said.

And because it was Cyrus, because there had never been a lock between them that mattered, Wren told. The Anchor & Chain. Hollis Fen. The willow stake, the hour before dawn, the price. Fifty less four, and seven crowns short of true, and the plan — she heard how it sounded as she said it aloud, how small and desperate and enormous — that she and Cyrus would slip away together when the river broke, before the warrant came, before Aldous's forging caught up with all of them, before Tam sold them piece by piece at some officer's table.

She had not planned to say Tam's name. It came out anyway.

"He wouldn't," Cyrus said, but their voice wavered on it, and they both heard the wavering, and neither pretended otherwise.

"He might not mean to. He might just drink too much and talk too much and let them draw it out of him like drawing a splinter." Wren gathered the coins back into the tin, her fingers stiff with cold. "That's why it has to be soon, Cy. Before he does it without meaning to. Before the captain decides. You and me, north, past the narrows, and we send for Father when—"

"No."

The word was quiet and absolute. Wren looked up. Cyrus sat back on their heels, arms wrapped around their knees, and in the striped moonlight their face was very still, the stillness Wren had learned to dread because it was the stillness of someone who had already decided something and was only waiting for the world to catch up.

"Cyrus—"

"You're going," Cyrus said. "One berth, Wren. Take mine."

"There *are* two berths. I bought two."

"You bought two because you'd have bought six if you could, and given them all away but one." Cyrus reached out and took her cold hands, and their palms were warm, printer's warm, ink-and-steel warm. "Listen to me. Father won't leave the press. You know he won't — he sleeps beside it like it's a sick animal. Tam won't leave unless it's first class and someone else pays. So the berths belong to whoever will actually use them. You, and—" They stopped. Started again. "You. Just you. I'm staying."

"The army is drafting a warrant. Fen said so. For *sedition*. Against our family."

"I know what it's for. I know what's under the floorboards downstairs, too — don't look surprised, you're not the only one who listens in this house." Cyrus's thumb moved over her knuckles, back and forth, the old soothing rhythm from childhood fevers. "Someone has to stay with Father. Someone has to keep the passes moving north. Every forged page he printed is somebody's life, Wren, and half those people don't even know their own names are on rebel paper. If we both run, who locks the forme? Who answers the captain's questions with the right lies?" A breath, almost a laugh. "I'm very good at invisible. I've practiced my whole life. Invisible people survive occupations."

"That's a lie and you know it. You're not invisible. You've never been invisible — not to me, not to this town anymore, not to *him*—" She bit off the captain's name, but it hung in the room anyway, *Vane*, unspoken, enormous, ten years old and brand new at once. Cyrus's hands tightened almost imperceptibly around hers.

"Seven crowns," Cyrus said softly, changing the subject the way they changed the subject in the type room when Father's coughing started. "Take the sugar tongs. And I have eleven shillings hidden in the forme cabinet, third drawer, under the bogus — don't ask why, I have my reasons."

"I'm not leaving without you."

"Yes, you are." Cyrus said it without heat, the way one says *the sun will rise*, and it frightened Wren more than shouting would have. "Because if you stay out of stubbornness, then we've wasted fifty crowns and Fen's discretion, and the ice will break one morning whether we're ready or not, and none of us will be on any boat, and the warrant will come, and that's the end of the whole story. Whereas if you go—" Their voice caught, just slightly, the first crack in the calm. "If you go, then somewhere north of the narrows, the Eastwicks continue. Somebody carries the name somewhere it can't be seized."

Wren bowed her head over their joined hands. The river groaned again, longer this time, and beneath the groan she thought she heard — she couldn't be sure, the wind was wrong, the window sealed — a softer sound, a rushing, like water finding a new channel, like the winter loosening its grip weeks too soon.

"Promise me one thing," she whispered. "When it happens — when the ice goes — you'll stand at the willow stake. Even if you don't board. Promise you'll come to the water."

Cyrus smiled then, the rare full smile, and for a moment they were children again on this same floor, dividing coins into towers, inventing countries in the atlas.

"I promise," they said.

But later, after Cyrus had gone padding back down the hall, after Wren had reburied the tin and lain staring at the frost-ferns on the glass, she heard the river again through her dreams — and this time there was no mistaking it. The cracking came sharp and fast, gunshot after gunshot down the dark length of the valley, and dogs began to bark street by street through the sleeping town, and somewhere below the mill a man named Hollis Fen was already walking his shoreline, counting the hours until dawn.
