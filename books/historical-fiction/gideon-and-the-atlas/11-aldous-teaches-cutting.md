## 11. Aldous Teaches Cutting

My father woke me before the lamps of the town had gone out, in that blue hour when the river fog comes up the bank like a slow animal looking for a place to sleep. He stood at the foot of my cot with a candle in one hand and the steel case in the other — not the type case, but the long wooden box lined with felt where he kept his gravers, his punches, the little hammers and files he had carried across an ocean when he was younger than I was now.

"Get your coat," he said. "We're going to work."

"It's not morning."

"It's the only morning we're sure of."

I did not ask what he meant. In Marrow's Bend we had learned to read each other's silences the way I read proof sheets — by what was missing from them. Downstairs, Wren was already awake; I heard her moving in the kitchen, banking the stove coals, setting water to heat without being asked. She looked at Aldous, then at me, and something passed over her face like weather. She said only, "There's bread on the board," and turned back to the fire, because she understood that some lessons are private even when everyone in the house can hear them.

The shop smelled of ink and cold iron and the tallow smoke of the candle my father carried ahead of us through the dark. He lit no lamp. He set the candle on the composing stone, cupped his hand around the flame as if it were a small bird he meant to keep warm, and waited until my eyes adjusted.

"Sit," he said.

I sat on the low stool at the cutting bench, the one I had sat on since I was six years old, when my feet didn't reach the floor and I watched his hands move like two animals that loved each other. The bench was scarred all over — fifty years of slips and catches, every mistake recorded in wood the way mistakes were recorded nowhere else. My father ran his palm across it once, slowly, the way another man might touch the face of a sleeping child.

"I taught you letters," he said. "Now I teach you letters' mothers."

"The punches," I said.

"The punches." He opened the steel case. Inside, nested in their felt beds, lay the tools of the oldest craft in the shop: the gravers with their handles worn to the color of honey, the files fine as insect legs, the little square rods of soft steel that would become, under his hands, the masters from which every matrix was struck. And beside them, wrapped in oilcloth, the finished punches themselves — a full alphabet, capital and lower, cut over thirty years, each letter smaller than a grain of barley and harder than sin.

"You know how they're made," he said. It was not quite a question.

"I've watched you strike matrices since I could stand."

"Watching is not knowing." He unwrapped the oilcloth and laid the punches out on the bench in order, A through Z, and for a moment neither of us spoke. The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Somewhere outside, far downriver, the ice made a sound like a door opening in an empty house.

"Your grandfather cut these tools' grandfathers in a shop in Ghent," my father said. "His father before him. There is a chain, Cyrus, and you are standing in it whether you choose to or not. Tonight I show you the links."

* * *

He began with the O, because he always began with the O.

"The round is the hardest and the truest," he said, selecting a blank rod of steel and fitting it into the vise. "Every curve you will ever cut is a piece of this circle. Learn the O and the rest of the alphabet is just the O learning manners."

He took up the finest graver — the one he called the needle, its point finer than anything else in the world I knew — and showed me how to seat it against the steel, how to find the angle where metal stops resisting and starts obeying. His hands were steady. They were seventy-year-old hands, or near enough, knotted at the knuckles, spotted like old paper, and they were steadier than mine had ever been at any hour of any day.

"Push, don't carve," he murmured. "You're not digging a hole. You're persuading the steel to be empty where the letter isn't."

I watched the first curl of metal rise off the rod, thin as onion skin, catching the candlelight. It fell onto the bench and lay there shining, and I thought: that shaving is older than the country we live in, that shaving has been waiting inside this steel since the steel was ore in the ground, and my father is the first man ever to let it out.

"Now you."

He put the graver in my hand. It fit differently than it fit him — my grip was longer-fingered, less certain — and the steel rod sat in the vise like a small stubborn animal that had not yet agreed to anything. I set the point. I pushed. The graver skated sideways and left a bright scratch across the face of the metal, a wound with no meaning.

"Again."

"It slipped."

"It always slips. Again."

I cut again. And again. The candle burned down a thumb's width while I learned how much pressure lived in a fingertip, how the sound of a true cut was different from the sound of a false one — a whisper versus a scrape — and how my breath wanted to hold itself and must not. My father did not correct me with words after the first few tries. He corrected me by reaching over, laying two fingers on the back of my hand, and adjusting the angle by a degree I couldn't see. Each time he touched me I felt the lesson pass through his skin into mine, some current older than either of us.

"You're thinking about the soldiers," he said, after an hour or more.

"I'm thinking about the steel."

"You're thinking about the soldiers. Your shoulder is up around your ears." He pressed it down with one flat hand. "A man who cuts type with fear in his shoulders cuts crooked letters, and crooked letters hang men. So. Put the fear somewhere else. Put it in your knees if you have to. But the hands belong to the work."

* * *

Near dawn he brought out the counterpunches — the little steel shapes that bite the counters, the enclosed spaces, the bowl of the b, the eye of the e — and showed me how a letter is mostly nothing, how the white inside the black is what the reader actually reads.

"Ink is cheap," he said. "Space is the treasure. Anyone can make a mark. The whole art is deciding where the mark ends." He held a finished punch up to the candle, the letter d no bigger than a lentil, and turned it so the light caught the polished face. "Thirty years ago I cut this. My eyes were better. My hands were worse. Every punch in that box is a compromise between what I saw and what I could do, and that's what makes them honest. Perfection is for God and liars."

"Why tonight?" I asked. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Why now?"

He set the punch down very carefully, aligning it with its brothers in the felt bed, and for a long moment he looked at the row of letters instead of at me.

"Because tomorrow they may come for the press," he said. "Because the day after they may come for me. Because there is a version of events, Cyrus, in which everything in this shop is confiscated or burned, and the only thing they cannot take is what lives in here—" he tapped my forehead, then my chest, then took both my hands and pressed them together, "—and in these. An army can seize a press in an afternoon. It cannot seize a hand that knows how to make one."

"They'd need a foundry. Matrices. Steel."

"They'd need you." He said it plainly, without drama, the way he announced corrections at the stone. "That is why we begin with the O."

* * *

We worked until the windows went gray. He taught me the straight stems first, then the serifs, which he cut with a graver he called the widow-maker because one slip and the letter was dead. He taught me how to harden the finished punch in the little forge behind the shop, heating it to a color he named by taste rather than sight — cherry, orange, the pale yellow he called *truth* — and quenching it in oil with a hiss like a curse. He taught me tempering, the second fire, gentler, that takes the brittleness out of the hardness. "Hard alone shatters," he said. "Tempered, it lasts. Remember that about people too."

By full light I had cut — badly, crookedly, with three false starts ground away — a single letter. Not an O. He had moved me to the i, the simplest of the lowercase, a stem and a dot, and still it had taken me four hours and cost me a blister and one small crisis of despair when the stem came out leaning like a drunk outside a tavern.

He examined it under the loupe for a long time. I braced myself for the verdict.

"It leans," he said.

"I know."

"It leans like it's walking toward something." He handed it back. "Keep it. Cut the rest of the alphabet and keep them all, and someday you'll set a line of type with your own hand-cut sorts, and every letter will lean a little, and anyone who knows anything will look at it and know exactly whose shop it came from. That's not a flaw. That's a signature."

I closed my fist around the little steel i. It was warm from the forge and from my father's fingers, and it weighed almost nothing, and it weighed everything.

* * *

Wren brought tea up sometime past sunrise and found us surrounded by steel dust and curled shavings, the candle drowned in its own wax, the punches laid out in their ranks like a tiny army at rest. She set the cups down without comment, but she lingered, looking at the alphabet.

"Papa," she said. "If they take the shop—"

"They'll take the shop," Aldous said. He drank his tea standing, looking out the window at the river, where the fog was burning off and the ice lay white and rotten-looking from bank to bank. "Shops are taken. Presses are taken. Ledgers—" he glanced at me, quickly, and away again, and I understood that he knew I knew about the passes beneath the floorboards, and that he had known for some time, and that this too was part of the inheritance, passed in silence, "—ledgers burn. What remains is what we put into people. That has always been the whole trade, Wren. We never sold paper. We sold memory, fixed."

"And if they take you?" she asked. She asked it the way she counted coin — flatly, precisely, refusing to flinch from the sum.

My father smiled. It was the smile he wore when a difficult forme locked up true on the first try.

"Then you'll print the memorial notice yourselves," he said, "and it will be beautifully set, because your sibling knows how to cut an i."

It should have been a joke. Wren laughed, a short wet sound, and turned away to hide her face, and my father reached out and touched the back of her head, briefly, the way he touched the tympan — checking that it was seated, that it would hold.

* * *

He worked beside me the rest of the day, and the next, and the next, though the requisition notices went up in the square and the boots of sentries could be heard passing the shop at all hours. He taught me the s, which he called the serpent, and the g with its double story, and the k whose arms must balance like a dancer. He taught me to strike matrices, packing the softened copper around the hardened punch with blows timed like a heartbeat. He taught me dressing the mould, casting the first sorts, trimming them with a knife until they stood square as soldiers — and I thought, filing the rough edge off a fresh-cast n, that I would never again hear that word without tasting irony.

On the third night he grew tired early. He sat down on the cutting stool — my stool — and watched me work at the m, and I felt his eyes on my hands the way I had felt sunlight through a window all my life: constant, warming, easy to forget until it moved.

"Cyrus," he said.

"Yes."

"When I'm gone, don't cut my alphabet again. Don't try to make my letters. Cut yours." He nodded at the little row of practice punches I'd begun keeping in a pillbox, each one leaning its own direction, each one unmistakably mine. "The chain doesn't want copies. It wants links."

Outside, the wind shifted. It came up from the south, warm for the first time in five months, carrying the smell of mud and open water, and we both stopped and listened as the river answered — a long groan, then a crack, sharp as a graver slipping, running the length of the valley.

My father looked at the window. The look on his face was not fear. It was arithmetic.

"That's early," he said quietly. "That's weeks early." He rose, joints cracking louder than the ice, and began putting away the tools with hands that suddenly hurried. "Wake your sister. Wake Tam. The thaw is coming faster than anyone planned — and I need to know, before the water runs, exactly who in this town means to be on it."
