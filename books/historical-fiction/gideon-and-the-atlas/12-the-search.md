## 12. The Search

They came at the hour when the light is thinnest — that gray seam between night and morning when even the river seems to hold its breath. Cyrus heard them before they knocked: boots on frost-stiffened cobblestones, many pairs, moving without any attempt at quiet, because men who intend to take a thing rarely trouble themselves with courtesy about how they arrive at its door.

Cyrus had not slept. No one in the Eastwick house had slept, not since Aldous's arithmetic at the window, not since the word *early* had passed between them like a coin too hot to hold. Wren had sat up counting coins by candlelight until the candle drowned in its own wax. Tam had gone out and come back smelling of officers' brandy and someone else's tobacco, saying nothing, which from Tam was the loudest warning of all. And Cyrus had spent the hours doing what Cyrus always did with fear: turning it into work.

The forged passes lay where they had always lain, beneath the third floorboard in the type room, wrapped in oilcloth — two dozen of them, maybe more, each one a small masterpiece of Aldous's hand. False names on true paper. Watermarks copied so faithfully that Cyrus, holding one to the lamp, had felt something close to awe and something close to horror in the same heartbeat. Each pass bore a name. That was the thing Cyrus could not stop thinking about as the boots came closer. Every document the army wanted was, in the end, only a list of names — and Cyrus had spent a lifetime learning that names were the heaviest things a press could carry.

*Three dozen families,* Cyrus thought. *Perhaps more.* The passes would tell an officer everything: who meant to run, who had paid, who had trusted a printer with their lives written in ink that could hang him.

The knock came anyway, though the door was not locked. It never was, during occupation. A knock was simply the sound power made before it entered.

"Open in the name of the Provisional Authority."

Cyrus opened it. There was nothing else to do, and there was — this was the thought that arrived, absurdly, at the threshold — there was a correct way to open a door, just as there was a correct way to set a line of type, and if a thing must be endured it might as well be endured with craft.

* * *

There were eight of them. Cyrus counted the way their father had taught them to count — quickly, completely, without appearing to look. Six soldiers in the blue-gray of the Provisional regiments, rifles slung but hands loose near the locks. A sergeant with a face like a closed ledger. And behind him, unhappily, the civilian — Magistrate Corve's man, Pell, who had sold flour to half the town and informed on the other half, and who now held a paper he kept unfolding and refolding as though the creases might change what it said.

"Warrant of search," Pell announced, though no one had asked. "Sedition. Printed matter. You'll stand aside."

From the back room came Aldous's voice, level as a perfectly justified line. "You'll wipe your boots. Ink dust on the floors takes a day to scrub, and I don't scrub for free."

It was such an ordinary sentence, such a father's sentence, that one of the young soldiers actually hesitated on the sill, looking down at his boots as if the requisition order might have covered mud as well. The sergeant shoved past him.

What followed took two hours, and Cyrus stood through all of it with their hands folded and their heart beating somewhere high in the throat, watching men who knew nothing about anything take apart the only world Cyrus had ever understood.

They were not careful. That was the first wound. They pulled books from the shelves by their spines and let them fall open, faces down, spines cracking. They overturned the ink cabinet and let a jar of the good lampblack — imported, hoarded through two blockades — shatter on the floorboards, and then walked through it, so that for years afterward, Cyrus supposed, there would be bootprints in the grain of the wood, ghost-prints, the army's signature pressed into the Eastwick shop like a bad stamp on soft paper. They ran their bayonets along seams in the plaster. They shook out every apron, every rag, every drawer of leads and rules and quoins, and the small tools sang and scattered and were kicked underfoot, and each little clatter went through Cyrus like a needle finding the quick.

The type cases they did not understand. This Cyrus noted with the part of the mind that never stops working, even in terror — the clerk's mind, the compositor's mind, cold and clear at the center of the fire. A soldier lifted the lid of the upper case and frowned at the hundred small compartments, the hundreds of small leaden letters lying in their bays like seeds, like teeth, like an alphabet asleep.

"What's this, then?"

"Type," Cyrus said. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Letters. You put them together and they make words. Words make — well. Notices. Gazettes." A pause, measured exactly. "The ones you requisitioned the press to print."

The sergeant looked at the case, and at Cyrus, and Cyrus watched the incomprehension settle over him like snow — the beautiful, saving ignorance of a man who had never considered where printed things came from, who believed that broadsides and gazettes and wanted-posters simply *existed*, the way weather existed. He dropped the lid.

"Nothing there," he said. "Just letters."

"Just letters," Cyrus agreed, and inside their chest something turned over — rage and contempt and a wild, giddy relief braided together so tightly they were indistinguishable.

*Just letters.* If the man only knew. Letters were the whole war. Letters had started it, letter by letter, pamphlet by pamphlet, grievance by grievance, each one set by hands like these hands, in shops like this shop, in towns like this town that no atlas bothered to mark. The army had come with rifles to hunt pieces of metal smaller than a thumbnail.

* * *

They found the floorboard on the second sweep.

Cyrus saw it happen the way one sees a forme slip in the press — that horrible slow moment when the whole heavy mass begins to go and the body knows it before the mind will admit it. Pell had brought the paper to the sergeant again, reading aloud now, his reedy voice gaining confidence: "...intelligence received that seditious matter is produced within the premises... travel documentation, falsely made..." And the sergeant, who had been growing bored, straightened, and said, "Flooring. Pull the flooring."

Six men with bayonets can do a great deal to a floor in ten minutes.

Cyrus stood very still. Stillness was the only disguise left. Behind them, at the edge of the ruined room, their father sat on the stairs with his hands flat on his knees, and his face was the face from the window at dawn — not fear, arithmetic. Cyrus caught his eye for half a second and read the sum in it: *the passes are under the third board from the wall, beside the hearth; the boards are pulled; the oilcloth is gray; there are perhaps ninety seconds between discovery and the gallows for everyone whose name is on that paper.*

Ninety seconds. Cyrus had set a full column of newsprint in less.

The soldiers worked outward from the middle of the room, prying boards with the careless violence of men who have been told a thing is hidden and therefore believe it is everywhere. One board came up. Another. Dust rose in the lamplight like snow falling upward. And Cyrus's body was standing still in the middle of it while Cyrus's mind did the only thing it knew how to do with catastrophe:

It measured.

The type room. The press room. The stone. The forme — and there it was, the thought arriving not as hope but as simple fact, the way solutions arrive to people who have spent their whole lives solving problems of arrangement. Tomorrow's gazette stood locked on the imposing stone by the far wall. The official edition. The army's own paper, ordered, approved, stamped with the Provisional seal, waiting for the morning: proclamations, grain tariffs, the colonel's tedious tribute to discipline. A forme of standing type, locked tight in its chase, iron-framed, weighing — Cyrus calculated without meaning to — forty pounds if it weighed an ounce. Quoined fast. Locked so thoroughly that to break it open a man would need a key, or a hammer, or a reason to suspect that inside the frame of the army's own loyal newspaper, something else entirely might be sleeping.

*They will never look there,* thought Cyrus, and the thought was immediately followed by its twin, colder: *because no one has ever looked there. Because it has never been done. Because it is mad.*

The third board from the hearth groaned under the pry-bar.

* * *

Afterward Cyrus could never reconstruct those ninety seconds in order. Memory gave them back later as a single bright instant stretched thin, the way a line of type stretches when you justify it — spaces widened, words pushed apart, everything forced to fit the measure.

The soldier nearest the hearth set his bar under the board's edge. Two more drifted toward the stone, drawn perhaps by boredom, kicking at a fallen composing stick.

"Aldous Eastwick." The sergeant had turned to the stairs, warrant in hand. "These documents. Falsified passes. You'll tell me where."

"I'll tell you nothing with a rifle in the room," said Aldous, "and nothing true anywhere," and Cyrus heard — they both heard — the old man's voice shake slightly on the second half, not with fear but with the effort of the joke, the last joke, the joke he might hang on.

And Cyrus moved.

Not toward the floorboards — that would have been suicide twice over, theirs and the town's. Cyrus moved the way water moves, the way a compositor moves through a familiar shop, unremarkably, usefully, with the particular aimlessness of a person performing a chore. Cyrus crossed to the imposing stone, muttering — audibly, deliberately — "*He won't find them, they're not here, Father keeps the good stock at the house*" — a lie pitched perfectly at the soldiers' backs, half-heard, dismissed — and lifted the tympan of the gazette forme as though checking the packing, as though this were the morning routine of every occupied print shop in the province.

The oilcloth bundle had been beneath the third board since autumn. But Cyrus had moved it last night — this was the part that still made their hands tremble weeks later, remembering — had woken at some black hour knowing, simply *knowing*, the way one knows a page is short-fouled, that the thaw would bring the search, and had carried the passes out wrapped in the rag used for wiping the rollers, and had lain awake with them under the blanket like a stolen child, waiting for the moment that was now.

The bundle slid from beneath the rag into the hollow of the forme, into the white space between the column of tariff notices and the colonel's tribute to discipline — the generous blank margin every compositor leaves, the silence around the words that makes the words legible. Cyrus laid the tympan back over it. Pressed it flat. Slid the frisket across, and let their palm rest one moment on the iron of the chase — warm, somehow, always warm, the press holding heat the way a stove does, the way a body does — and then stepped away, wiping their hands on the apron, a journeyman finishing a task.

"Third board!" someone shouted. "Sergeant! Third one!"

The board came up. Underneath: darkness, dust, and nothing. The oilcloth was gone. A rat-gnawed rag remained, ink-black, smelling of turpentine, and the soldier held it up dripping like a dead thing while Pell leaned in and pronounced, with enormous disappointment, "Rags. Cleaning rags. He burns them, likely. Burns them or — "

"He buries them," said another soldier, with the sudden certainty of stupidity, and pointed his bayonet at the yard beyond the broken window. "Out there. Digging."

And so they poured out into the frozen yard, six men with bayonets, to excavate a printer's garden down to the frost line, while the sergeant remained, and Aldous remained on the stairs wearing an expression of profound and dangerous innocence, and Cyrus remained by the imposing stone where, under the army's own seal, inside the army's own tomorrow, ninety families' worth of freedom lay locked in iron, quoined tight, waiting for the morning press.

* * *

The sergeant stayed eleven minutes. Cyrus counted.

He walked the ruined shop with his hands behind his back, a man reluctant to report failure, pausing at the shattered ink jar, at the gutted shelves, at last at the imposing stone. He looked down at the locked forme the way a man looks at a strongbox he has neither the key nor the authority to open. His eyes moved across the visible type — CYRUS could feel him reading without comprehension, seeing only the tops of the letters, the uniform silver forest of an even surface — and Cyrus stood beside him with their pulse tapping in their ears like a compositor's stick against a case, and offered, in the flattest apprentice's voice they owned:

"Gazette sets at first light, sir. Colonel wants it distributed by nine. Shall I — that is, should the edition be delayed, sir, on account of the search?"

Something flickered in the sergeant's face. Duty, annoyance, the ancient friction between a man's orders and a man's schedule. To delay the gazette was to explain the delay; to explain the delay was to explain the search; to explain the search was to write a report; and somewhere in the upper offices of the Provisional Authority there waited a colonel who punished reports of failure the way winter punishes late crops.

"Print your gazette," the sergeant said finally. "It's the only honest thing left in this building." And he left, boots printing mud and ink across the floor, and the door slammed, and the frost-stiffened quiet of Marrow's Bend closed back over the street like water over a thrown stone.

For a long moment nobody moved. In the yard, faintly, came the sounds of soldiers digging where nothing was buried.

Then Aldous began to laugh.

It was a terrible sound, a rusty hinge of a laugh, wrung out of him in shakes, and he pressed both fists against his mouth to stop it and could not, and Cyrus realized with a kind of vertigo that they had never heard their father laugh — not once in the whole carved memory of their life — and that the first time would be *this*, in a wrecked shop at dawn, over a rag and a lie and a forme of borrowed treason.

"You moved it," he said, when he could speak. "Last night. Before they came. You *knew*."

"The way you know a page is short," Cyrus said. Their legs chose that moment to inform them they had been standing on courage alone, and they sat down hard on the imposing stone, directly atop the hidden passes, as though a chair had been placed there for them. "I didn't know where to put them. Then I looked at the gazette and thought — nobody searches their own Bible, Father."

"No," Aldous said, wiping his eyes with an ink-black thumb, leaving a streak across his cheekbone like a scar. "No, they don't." He looked around at the destruction — the cracked spines, the bootprints in lampblack, the floor opened like a dissected chest — and his laughter died into something quieter, older. "They'll be back. Searches are like weather; one proves the climate. And when they come back, they'll bring someone who knows what a chase is. Someone who served a trade before he served a flag."

"Then we print it tonight," Cyrus said. "Before anyone who understands comes. The forme is locked. The press is — " they glanced at the great machine, untouched, sacredly ignored amid the wreckage, too heavy and too dull for bayonets — "the press is ready. First light, the army gets its gazette. And every pass goes north inside it, stamped under their own seal, riding their own roads, in their own mailbags, to the very ferries that are leaving early."

Aldous looked at his child for a long moment — at the ink on their hands, at the steadiness they were borrowing and returning, at whatever it was he saw there that made his arithmetic finally finish its sum.

"You set that lie beautifully," he said softly. "'Shall the edition be delayed, sir.' Gave him a problem he couldn't afford to own. That's not nerve, Cyrus. That's craft." He rose from the stairs, joints cracking, and crossed to the press, and laid his hand on the frame as if greeting an old horse. "We print at first light. Which means we compose now, and we compose *fast* — because the passes need a carrier page, something new, something the army doesn't know to look for, and there's only one pair of hands in this town I trust to set it blind."

Outside, the digging stopped. Boots gathered, muttered, withdrew down the street toward the square. And from the direction of the harbor, faint under the gray light, came a sound that had no place in any plan: the river, groaning — long and low and splitting — as the thaw reached Marrow's Bend a full week before the ice was ready to forgive it.

And with it, from the lane, whistling, unmistakable, cheerful — the whistle of Tam Eastwick, coming home.
