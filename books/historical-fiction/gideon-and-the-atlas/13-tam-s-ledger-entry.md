## 13. Tam's Ledger Entry

The officers drank in the back room of the Gilded Pike, and Tam Eastwick learned that a man could be made of other men's laughter.

He had not meant to stay past the first hour. He had come, as he came every evening now, to sit at the edge of the lamplight where the smoke thinned and the talk turned from cards to war, and to be — for once in his life — the interesting one. Lieutenant Hask wore his coat unbuttoned against the stove's heat, and Sergeant Pell had a laugh like a cart going over cobbles, and when Tam told the story of the press's devil — the old superstition that a misprinted word summoned a small red imp who lived in the ink — Pell laughed so hard he knocked his glass into his own lap and did not even curse. He just pointed at Tam and said, *You again. You'll be the death of me,* as if they were old friends. As if Tam were someone worth dying of.

Ten years of being the second child in a house of shadows, and all it had taken was a uniformed audience.

*You again.* Tam lifted his own glass — the third, or the fourth; the numbers had gone soft at their edges — and let the wine sit on his tongue before swallowing. It was army wine, thin and sour, requisitioned from some cellar downriver, but drunk in company it tasted like victory. Everything tasted like victory lately. Even the cold.

Outside, Marrow's Bend lay under its fifth week of occupation. The square was striped with sentries. The church bell rang only by permit. And here in the Pike's back room, with its low beams and its fire and its smell of tallow and spilled beer, the war was a warm animal you could scratch behind the ears.

"So your father," Hask said, leaning forward, elbows on the table, "cuts letters. Actual letters. Out of steel."

"Type metal, mostly. Lead with antimony in it — gives it spine." Tam heard himself say it with pride and wondered, distantly, whose pride it was. His father's? The shop's? He had spent twenty years refusing to inherit either, and here he was reciting alloy ratios like scripture. "Every letter in every notice posted in this town since before the war was born in our shop. Every debt recorded, every birth, every death. My family has written this town down."

"And now we've requisitioned the pen." Pell grinned. "That's poetry, that is. Somebody write that down."

They laughed. Tam laughed with them, and the laugh went down easier than the wine.

It was Hask who turned the conversation, later, when the room had emptied to the faithful few and the candle had burned down to a thumb's width of tallow. Hask was the dangerous one — Tam had known it from the first night, had known it the way you know weather coming, by the pressure behind the ears. Hask asked his questions sideways, smiling, and only afterward did you feel the hook.

"Your father kept accounts," Hask said. "For the whole town, you said. Debts, births, deaths."

"The ledger, yes. Twenty years of it. There isn't a family in the Bend that isn't in those pages somewhere."

"Mm." Hask turned his glass slowly on the scarred wood, watching the ring of moisture it left. "And a man who keeps accounts like that — a careful man, a precise man — he'd notice things. Irregularities. Pages that don't add up."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a chair scraped, and a woman's voice said something too low to catch, and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary inn went on around them while Tam sat very still inside his own skin.

"Irregularities," he repeated.

"Paperwork is how sedition breathes, Eastwick. Passes. Permits. Bills of lading. A rebellion is mostly ink." Hask looked up then, and his eyes were friendly and bottomless. "And your family, conveniently, is ink."

Tam should have said: *My father is a printer, not a traitor.* Should have said it loudly, indignantly, the way a loyal son would. The words were right there. They had been rehearsed for weeks, ever since the soldiers marched in and Tam had begun his slow, deliberate campaign of charm — because someone in this family had to be safe, and Cyrus would never bend, and Wren counted coins in the dark like a miser counting her way out of a burning house, and Father had gone strange and calm in a way that frightened Tam more than any threat.

But what came out instead, in the warm haze of the fourth glass, was:

"There are pages my father doesn't let anyone see."

The silence after it lasted three heartbeats. Tam heard each one.

Hask smiled. Not broadly — a small smile, the kind a man wears when a trap he set months ago finally closes. "Doesn't he."

"He's particular about his work. All printers are. You'd know — you commandeer a press, you must have learned something about—"

"Pages," Hask said gently, cutting through him like a blade through wet paper. "He doesn't let anyone see."

And Tam, who had spent his whole life wanting to matter, felt with terrible clarity that he had just mattered — that he had just become, in one sentence spoken over sour wine, the most important Eastwick in Marrow's Bend — and that the price of mattering was a thing he could never take back.

* * *

He walked home through the occupied streets with the cold sobering him in layers, like a man being peeled.

The town at night under occupation had a sound he had come to know: boots on frost, the creak of the guard post's sign, and underneath it all the river, working at the ice. The thaw was early this year. Everyone said so. The old women crossed themselves at the sound the river made after midnight — a groan, a long complaint, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Spring was coming up the valley whether the army liked it or not, and with spring the ferry, and with the ferry the last chance to go north before whatever was gathering downriver arrived here with bayonets.

Tam stood a while on the bridge. Below him the ice gleamed dully under a half moon, seamed and gray, still holding, still lying about its strength. He spat over the rail and watched the spittle vanish into the dark between the planks.

*There are pages my father doesn't let anyone see.*

Why had he said it?

Because it was true — that was the ugly answer, the one he kept turning over like a coin with two identical faces. It was true, and he had known it was true for longer than he admitted. He had seen the fresh type under the floorboards the same night Cyrus had, weeks ago, when the soldiers first came and Father grew calm. He had said nothing then either. He had told himself he was protecting the family by knowing and staying silent, but silence was just betrayal with better manners, and now he had graduated from silence to speech, and speech, once given to a man like Hask, could not be unsaid.

He thought of his father's hands. That was the worst part — that his mind went there, to Aldous's hands, cracked and ink-stained, guiding Tam's small fingers over a graver when Tam was six, before Tam decided he hated everything the shop stood for. *Feel the steel want the line,* his father had said. *Don't force it. The letter is already in the metal. Your whole job is to stop getting in its way.*

Tam had gotten in the way of everything his whole life. That was, if he was honest — and the cold and the wine between them forced a kind of honesty — the entire architecture of his grievance. Cyrus belonged to the shop the way a keel belongs to a boat. Wren belonged to the family the way a hearth belongs to a house. And Tam belonged nowhere except in his own appetite, which grew the more he fed it, and tonight he had fed it the lives of everyone who had ever shared his name.

No. No, that wasn't — he hadn't sold anyone. He'd mentioned pages. Pages weren't people. Hask had fished and Tam had let a minnow go off the hook, that was all, and if Hask chose to make something of it—

But even drunk, even peeling himself layer by layer in the freezing dark, Tam knew how men like Hask worked. They didn't need confessions. They needed threads, and they pulled them one at a time, patiently, and each thread seemed harmless until you saw it was attached to your own throat. Tomorrow Hask would mention the pages again, casually, over cards. Next day he'd ask about the floorboards — no, he wouldn't ask, he'd say, *Funny thing about old shops, the floors,* and watch Tam's face do whatever Tam's face did. And Tam's face, he knew with despair, had never once in thirty years done what Tam wanted it to do.

* * *

The shop was dark when he let himself in, but not silent. It was never silent anymore. The press breathed in the corner like a sleeping animal, and the type cabinets ticked faintly as the temperature changed, thousands of little lead bodies settling against each other, and somewhere overhead a beam groaned with the river's restlessness transmitted through the ground.

A candle burned low in the back room. Tam moved toward it without deciding to, drawn the way he had been drawn as a boy.

Cyrus was at the composing stone.

Of course. Cyrus was always at the stone, or the case, or the ledgers — Cyrus lived inside the shop's rhythms the way other people lived inside their skins. They sat with their sleeves rolled, setting type by touch in the near-dark, and the sight of it produced in Tam his nightly cocktail of contempt and envy, shaken not stirred, poured over ice.

"You're late," Cyrus said, without looking up. "Or early. Whichever way you're drunk."

"I'm not—" Tam started, and stopped, because denying it would only confirm it. He hung his coat on the peg. The shop smelled of oil and lampblack and the ghost of his childhood, and he hated how much the smell undid him.

"Wren's asleep. Father's asleep. Which means," Cyrus said, still setting type, the little steel sounds precise as a clock, "you can stand there swaying in the doorway all night if you like, or you can tell me what happened at the Pike."

"Nothing happened at the Pike."

"You came home whistling the first week. Then you came home quiet. Now you come home *careful*, and careful is worse than quiet." Cyrus looked up then, and the candlelight caught their face — that face the town had argued over for thirty years, neither one thing nor another until Cyrus had carved their own name out of the argument — and their eyes held Tam the way a vise holds work. "What did you tell them, Tam?"

"Nothing. I told them nothing. We talked about the press, about the imp in the ink, about—"

"Cyrus." Their father's voice, from the stairs.

Aldous descended slowly. He had been aging in fast-forward since the requisition — Tam could see it even in candlelight, the flesh falling away from the jaw, the hands grown translucent at the backs. But his eyes were clear, and calm, and that calm had terrified Tam for weeks, and it terrified him now, standing caught between his father and his sibling with Hask's small smile still printed on the inside of his eyelids.

"Father," Tam said. "I was just—"

"Come here."

Tam came. It was absurd, at thirty, to cross a room because his father said *come here*, and he did it anyway, and hated himself for doing it, and hated his father for having the authority, and hated most of all that some starved corner of him was glad.

Aldous took Tam's chin in one ink-stained hand and turned his face to the light, examining him the way he examined a freshly cast sort — for flaws, for bubbles in the metal, for the places where it would break under pressure.

"You've been feeding them," Aldous said quietly.

"I haven't—"

"Don't." Not cruel. Worse than cruel: tired. "I was young in a garrison town once too, before you were born. I know what flattery costs and who sets the price. Did you tell them about the ledger?"

The question dropped through Tam like a stone through ice water. "The ledger?"

"The ledger," Aldous repeated. "Twenty years of this town's names. Births, debts, deaths, and three pages at the back that don't belong to any of it." He released Tam's chin. "Did you tell them the ledger holds irregularities?"

And there it was — his own sentence, spoken over sour wine, returned to him in his father's voice, and it sounded nothing like the innocent observation he had believed himself to be making. It sounded like exactly what it was.

"I said—" Tam's mouth was dry. "I said there were pages you didn't let anyone see. That's all. It was nothing. It was talk. They can't do anything with talk."

"No," Aldous agreed. "Talk alone, no. But talk tells them where to dig, and digging finds the floorboards, and the floorboards find the passes, and the passes—" He stopped. For the first time since the occupation began, the calm flickered, and Tam saw beneath it something vast and carefully banked, like a fire raked over ashes. "The passes find gallows, Tam. Not mine. Ours. All four of ours, and half the families in the ledger besides, because a pass signed in my hand is a pass they gave, and the army does not trouble itself much over who forged and who carried."

The candle guttered. Nobody moved to trim it.

"They'd hang *Wren*," Aldous said, softer. "For paper she never touched."

Tam looked at his sister-brother across the composing stone. Cyrus hadn't spoken, hadn't moved, but their face had gone white and still, and their eyes on Tam held no fury at all — and that was the unbearable thing. He had braced for fury. Fury he could fight. What he saw instead was something older and sadder, the look of someone watching a wall crack and measuring, already, whether anything could be salvaged.

"I didn't sell you," Tam said, and heard how it sounded, and pressed on anyway because the alternative was drowning. "I didn't. I would never. You're my family. Whatever I've said about the shop, about the business, about my share — that's money, that's *fairness*, that's not — I would never give them *you*."

"Tonight you gave them a map," Cyrus said quietly. "Whether you meant to or not. Maps don't care about intentions."

The words went in and stayed. Tam opened his mouth to answer and found nothing there — no cleverness, no grievance, none of the well-worn arguments about inheritance and invisibility and the years he'd spent as the unchosen child. Just the image: Hask tomorrow, smiling, saying *funny thing about old shops, the floors*, and Tam's face doing what it always did, and boots on frost, and the floorboards coming up.

"I'll fix it," he said. "I'll go back tomorrow. I'll tell them I was drunk, that I invented it, that printers' families exaggerate—"

"You'll go back," Aldous said, "and you'll drink less, and you'll listen more, and you will tell me — every night, word for word — what they say. If my son has opened a door, then my son will stand in it and tell us what comes through." He turned toward the stairs, then paused, his back to them both. "That is not forgiveness. It is employment. Take it."

His footsteps climbed and faded. The shop settled around them, ticking and breathing.

Tam stood in the dark with his shame, waiting for Cyrus to say something cutting, something he could resent and carry out into the night like a lantern of grievance. It was how they survived each other, he and Cyrus — trading wounds, keeping score.

Instead Cyrus began, silently, to distribute type back into the case, putting the letters away one by one, and said only: "The river breaks soon. When it does, everything gets decided at once. Pray you learn to listen faster than you learned to talk."

Tam went up to bed. He did not sleep. He lay listening to the river groan beneath the floor, and to the wind worrying the sign outside, and somewhere past the deepest hour he heard Cyrus rise and move quietly below, and the soft industrial whisper of the press beginning to turn — printing God knew what, for God knew whom — and Tam stared at the ceiling and understood, with the clarity that arrives only after ruin, that he loved them. All of them. That he had always loved them, clumsily, hungrily, in the only language he knew, which was taking.

And that love might not be enough to save any of them from what he had already said.

Downriver, three days' march away, an army was waiting for a name. And in the back room of the Gilded Pike, a lieutenant was writing his reports by candlelight, and Tam knew — the way you know weather, the way you know ice — that somewhere in those pages, in a neat officer's hand, his own name was already drying.
