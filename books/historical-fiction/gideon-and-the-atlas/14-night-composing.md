## 14. Night Composing

The shop at midnight was a different country.

Cyrus had always known this — had known it since childhood, when they would steal down the stairs barefoot to stand among the sleeping cases, the air cool and metallic, the great press crouched in the dark like a patient animal. In daylight the shop belonged to Aldous, to customers, to the town's small commerce and its smaller grievances. At night it belonged to Cyrus alone. Or it had, once. Tonight it belonged to two of them, and to the thing they were about to do.

They had shuttered the windows with the old wool blankets, hung so long in the storeroom that they came down smelling of camphor and mouse, and now the single lamp threw its light in a low gold circle that touched the composing stone and no farther. Beyond that circle the shop went on in shadow: the tiers of type rising like a dark city, the paper stacked in reams under the counter, the press waiting. The stove still held heat from the day's last firing — Aldous had banked it heavily before going up, saying nothing, asking nothing, though Cyrus had seen him pause on the stair, seen his eyes go to the forme locked on the stone, and understood that their father knew exactly what kind of night this was going to be.

Vane stood at the far side of the stone in his officer's coat, and he had taken the coat off. It hung over the back of Aldous's chair like a shed skin, and beneath it he was only a man in a shirtsleeves gone soft at the elbows, sleeves rolled now to the forearm, hands hovering over the galley as if he were afraid to touch what was already set.

"You've been at this since I knocked," he said.

"Since before you knocked." Cyrus slid the last line into the stick and locked it with a quoin key, the small iron bite of it familiar as their own pulse. "I started when you were still crossing the square trying to look like a man out for an evening stroll."

"I was out for an evening stroll."

"In full uniform."

"It's cold," Vane said. "The coat is warm."

Cyrus looked at him over the stone. The lamp caught the planes of his face — older than ten years should have made him, weathered at the corners of the eyes, a scar through one brow that had not been there when he was seventeen and learning to justify his lines. He had been beautiful then in the careless way of boys who did not know it. He was something else now. Something with weight to it, like lead, like a sort held up to the light.

"The copy," Cyrus said. "Read it back to me."

Vane picked up the smuggled sheet — three pages of rebel news, written in a clerk's cramped hand, carried into the shop folded inside a hymnal by a woman selling eggs — and read it aloud in a low voice, the words of men in the capital declaring what could no longer be unsaid: the requisitions, the hangings downriver, the names of the dead printed plain so that no one could pretend later they had not known. His voice did not shake. But his thumb pressed hard against the paper's edge, whitening it, and Cyrus watched that thumb and thought: *he is more afraid than I am, and he will never say so.*

"Set it," Cyrus said, when he had finished. "Twelve point body. We'll run four hundred before dawn."

"Four hundred copies of treason."

"Four hundred copies of the truth," Cyrus said, and reached for the case.

* * *

Vane's hands remembered.

That was the first astonishment of the night, arriving quietly, the way water arrives under ice — not all at once but everywhere at once. He reached into the case without being told which one, found the twelve-point Bodoni where it had always lived, third tier from the top, and drew letters with a fluency that made something twist behind Cyrus's ribs. His fingers moved in the old rhythm: *pick, place, pick, place*, the brass of the type catching lamplight in small flashes, and ten years fell off him between one letter and the next until he was Eli again, the printer's boy from downriver, quick and laughing, ink to the knuckle by noon every day.

"You kept the cases in order," he said. Not looking up.

"My father is a tyrant about order."

"He always was." A pause. The click of type against type. "He used to rap my knuckles for a wrong sort. Said a man who couldn't feel the difference between an *n* and a *u* upside down had no business near a press."

"He'd have rapped mine harder. He says I set too fast and read too little."

"He's wrong there." Vane set the line, justified it, slid it into the galley beside the others. "You read everything. You read me, that winter. Better than I wanted to be read."

Cyrus kept their eyes on the case. The lamplight lay warm across the compartments, each letter in its little cell, thousands of them, an alphabet in captivity — and thought of how strange it was that a person could be trained so young, so deep, that the hands would carry the knowledge away whole even when the heart left, that Eli's fingers had spent a decade holding a musket and a sword and a pen full of orders, and still knew where the *e*s lived.

"You left," Cyrus said. Quietly. To the case, not to him. "You left without —"

"I know what I did." His hands stilled over the galley. In the lamplight the set lines stood in their tray like a field of small standing stones, sentences waiting to be inked, waiting to be multiplied, and Vane looked down at them as if they might accuse him. "I wrote you a letter. Did your father never give it to you?"

The shop was very still. Under the floor, somewhere deep, the river shifted in its bed and groaned — a long, grinding note that traveled up through the joists and the soles of their feet, the sound of a season deciding.

"No," Cyrus said. "He didn't."

"I asked him to burn it, if he thought —" Vane exhaled. "God. I was nineteen. They were offering a commission and a way off the river and I took both, and then I sat in the barracks at Fort Anne writing you six pages of cowardice dressed up as duty, and then I decided you were better off with the cowardice burned, and then I decided *I* was better off, and that's the whole of it. That's the man you're setting treason with tonight."

Cyrus lifted a sort from the case — an asterisk, for no reason except that their hand needed to do something — and turned it in the light. The little star of metal. The mark printers used to say: *here, look here, there is something beneath this worth finding.*

"He burned it," Cyrus said. "Or he hid it. He won't say which. But he never once spoke your name after you went, and I learned not to either, and we got very good at it, the two of us, building a silence ten years wide." They set the asterisk back in its cell. "And now you stand at my father's stone in your shirtsleeves setting seditious type, and I find I don't know whether to hate you or —"

"Don't finish that sentence," Vane said. "Not yet. Not while there's a forme unlocked between us and four hundred reasons for us both to hang."

But his voice was not steady when he said it, and Cyrus saw that his hands were not steady either, and across the width of the composing stone, in the small gold country of the lamp, they looked at each other the way people look across a river in thaw: knowing the crossing is possible, knowing it may kill them, wanting it anyway.

* * *

The press took both of them to run.

That was the mercy of it, Cyrus thought, somewhere in the second hundred. That the work demanded four hands, that there was no polite distance to keep. Vane laid the dampened sheets on the tympan while Cyrus inked the platen, the brayer singing softly back and forth across the type, and then Cyrus folded the frisket down and Vane pulled the bar and the press closed on the sheet with a sound like a held breath released, and then they changed places, over and over, trading sides of the machine like partners in a figure, and there was no way to do it without touching.

His hand brushed theirs at the tympan, adjusting a corner of paper. Their shoulders met at the pull of the bar, his arm along theirs, bearing down together, and every contact was nothing — a sleeve, a knuckle, the accidental geography of shared labor — and every contact was everything, and neither of them mentioned it, and both of them felt it, and the pile of printed sheets grew on the counter, four hundred small declarations, wet-inked and smelling of linseed and lampblack, each one a hanging offense.

"She'll be hanged for this," Vane said at some point, meaning the egg-woman, or the clerk whose handwriting it was, or the whole chain of hands between the capital and this press. His voice had gone rough. "If any sheet surfaces. You understand that. It won't stop at the printer."

"Then we make sure none surface wrong." Cyrus pulled the bar. The press sighed shut and open. "Four hundred copies, and every one delivered before light, and no proof ever found. That's the craft, Captain. My father taught me that much. A good printer leaves no evidence, only editions."

"A good soldier leaves no witnesses," Vane answered, and then heard himself, and flinched as though the words had come back to him with edges.

Cyrus set the next sheet. "Which are you tonight?"

He was quiet through one full impression, the wheel turning, the screw descending, the sheet taking its letters. Then: "Neither. Both. A man who has spent ten years being handed orders and telling himself that obedience was a kind of honor, and discovering tonight, at a stolen press, with ink on his cuffs, that it was only obedience." He looked at the growing stack. "These pages say things I have known since winter and put in no report. Do you understand? Everything true in this room, I already believed. I just needed someone to make me carry it."

"And the someone had to be me."

"The someone had to be you," he said, and there was nothing of the captain in his voice at all.

Cyrus reached for the frisket and their hands met at the wire — properly met this time, not brushing, his fingers closing over theirs on the cold metal, and neither of them moved, and the press stood open between them with its half-laid sheet like a page paused mid-sentence. The lamp fluttered in a draft from under the door. Downstairs in the foundations the ice muttered and boomed, a sound like distant artillery, like the world rearranging itself, and Cyrus stood with their hand inside a soldier's hand and thought, with terrible clarity: *this is the moment. Not the sheets, not the names, not the ledger. This. Whatever I am afterward began here.*

"Eli," they said.

"Don't," he whispered. "Say anything else and I'll stay past dawn, and staying past dawn kills the egg-woman and the clerk and probably your father."

"Then don't stay." Cyrus turned their hand under his, so that palm lay against palm, so that the touch became deliberate, chosen, signed like a document. "Go. Come back. Keep coming back. Print with me until this war is over and one of us is allowed to say the rest of it out loud."

His grip tightened once — a whole letterpress of things said in a single squeeze — and then he let go, and stepped back, and they finished the edition in silence that was not empty at all but full, packed solid, like a well-justified line.

The last sheet came off the press gray-pink in the window light. Neither of them had noticed dawn assembling outside the blankets. Vane put his coat back on and became the captain again by degrees, collar, buttons, face — but at the door, with the bundle of four hundred truths under his arm and the street still safe and empty beyond the threshold, he stopped.

"The ledger," he said, not turning around. "Tomorrow I'm going to ask you for names, Cyrus. It's been ordered. And you're going to lie to me, and I'm going to write down whatever lie you tell, because I can't refuse the order and I won't act on it. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That we're both forgers now."

"That we're both forgers now," he agreed. And he was gone into the blue morning, footsteps fading on the frozen mud of the lane, leaving Cyrus alone in the ink-and-lampblack dark with the press cooling, the formes unlocked, the type ready to be distributed back into its cases as though the night had never happened — and under the floorboards, where the fresh hides of type lay hidden, and upstairs where their father slept, and down at the wharf where Wren counted coins by feel in the dark, the whole delicate machine of their survival ticking onward toward whatever the thaw would bring.

Cyrus banked the stove. Washed the stone. Put the asterisk back in its cell one last time and held it a moment longer than necessary.

Then, from the lane outside, faint but unmistakable in the still morning air: voices. Soldiers' voices. And running feet — Tam's running feet — coming up the hill toward the shop faster than Cyrus had ever heard their brother move.
