## 15. The Ice Breaks Early

Wren Eastwick had learned to read the river before she could read her letters.

Her father used to hold her at the window in the blue hour before dawn, her small mittened hand pressed to the cold glass, and teach her its language. *That groan means the ice is settling,* he would say. *That hiss means it's thinning from below. And when you hear it crack like a rifle shot — like God snapping His fingers over Marrow's Bend — that means winter is losing its grip.* She had been five years old then, and the cracks had been a lullaby, a promise of spring, of mud puddles deep enough to drown a boot in, of the first ferry boats nosing up through the broken floes with their cargo of sugar and cloth and news from the world beyond the bend.

She was twenty-two now, and the sound that came across the water in the small hours of the morning was not a lullaby. It was a countdown.

It began as warmth.

She woke past midnight to air that felt wrong against her face — soft, damp, breathing through the gap under the door where a month of bitter cold had sealed everything shut like wax. The south wind had arrived in the night, heavy and saltless and smelling of thawed earth from valleys far downriver, carrying with it a rumor of rain. She lay in her narrow bed above the print shop and listened to the eaves drip. Drip. Not the slow, patient drip of a sun-warmed icicle, but a quick, greedy dripping, as though the whole roof had decided at once to give up what it had hoarded all winter.

*Too fast*, she thought, sitting up. *This is too fast.*

She dressed in the dark without lighting the lamp, out of habit — lamps in windows had become a thing to avoid since the requisition, since soldiers drank coffee in the tavern across the square and any light was an invitation for a knock. Her fingers found her boots, her shawl, the little drawstring purse she wore beneath her bodice day and night now, against her heart, like something born rather than carried. Inside it: the coins. Three months of skimming and saving, of selling eggs from the neighbor's hens on commission, of mending officer's uniforms by lamplight until her eyes burned, of trading her mother's silver thimble — forgive me, Mama — to a quartermaster who didn't know what it was worth. One hundred and forty-two crowns. Enough, the smuggler Corven had said last week, tapping his pipe against the pilings, for two berths on the northbound when the ice went out. Two. He'd named the price and she'd paid a deposit and he'd spat into the black water and said, *Middle of next month. When the thaw comes proper. I'll send word.*

The thaw had come proper tonight, three weeks early, and no word had been sent.

* * *

She went down the back stairs on feet she made silent by will alone, though the house was hers to walk loudly in if she pleased. The shop below lay in its familiar dark — the great press a sleeping animal in the corner, ink-smelling, patient; the cases of type ranked along the wall like books in a library only her father and Cyrus could fully read. The composing stone glimmered faintly gray under the window. Everything was where it always was. Everything was about to be nowhere.

Cyrus was awake. Of course Cyrus was awake. They sat at the ledger desk with a single candle stub burning low, and they were not writing in the ledger — that book had become too dangerous to touch, its pages full of names the army wanted — but sharpening a graver against a whetstone with slow, even strokes, the way their father did when he couldn't sleep. The sound was a whisper, a heartbeat, a metronome. In the candlelight their face was half shadow, half gold, and utterly calm, which was the most alarming thing about them. Cyrus was always calm. It had taken Wren twenty years to understand that the calm was not absence of fear but a decision made every hour, a coat worn buttoned to the throat.

"You heard it," she said.

"I heard it." Cyrus did not look up from the graver. "The wind changed around midnight. Warm off the southern hills. Father says a thaw wind like this can take a foot of ice off the bend in two days."

"Two days." Wren laughed, and hated the sound of her own laugh, high and cracked as the river itself. "Cyrus. I went down to the pilings an hour ago. Before I woke you. The ice is already talking — groaning like something dying. There's black water showing past the mill race. Black water, in March, a week after we had snow." She gripped the back of a chair. "And there's more. Corven's boy came through the alley behind the cooperage. Left this."

She set a scrap of paper on the desk. On it, in pencil, in a hand that could not be traced to anyone: *Ice goes early. Boat leaves dawn tomorrow — day after this one. Berths held till first light, not past.*

Cyrus set down the graver. For a long moment they looked at the paper the way one looks at a diagnosis, or a sentence handed down. Then: "Tomorrow dawn. Not next month. Tomorrow."

"Yes."

"That's—" Cyrus stopped. Wren watched them do the arithmetic she had already done, the terrible arithmetic that was becoming the family trade. "One boat. How many berths?"

"One hundred and forty-two crowns' worth," Wren said quietly. "Two."

The candle guttered. Somewhere outside, the river let out a long, grinding moan that seemed to come up through the floorboards, through their boots, through the marrow of them, as if the whole town were standing on the back of some enormous waking creature.

"Cyrus," Wren said. "We have hours. Not days. Hours. And four of us, and room for two."

* * *

They woke their father gently, which was how everything concerning Aldous Eastwick had to be done now. He had grown thinner since the requisition — the army took his press by day and his peace by night, and lately he coughed in the mornings, a dry rattle he waved away like smoke. When Cyrus shook his shoulder he surfaced from sleep quickly, the way soldiers do, and sat up in the cold room with his gray hair loose on his shoulders and listened without interruption while Wren laid it all out: the note, the dawn departure, the money, the two berths.

He heard it all. Then he said, "Get Tam."

"He'll be at the tavern."

"Then go fetch him out of it," Aldous said, and there was iron under the tiredness, the old voice from the type room, the voice that had taught three children that a letter cut true would hold its shape against anything. "If this family makes a decision, it makes it whole. Wake him. Bring him home sober enough to stand. We talk at this table or not at all."

The tavern was still lit at three in the morning. Of course it was; officers kept late hours when the news from the capital was bad, and bad news came daily now. Wren stood in the doorway with the warm wind pulling at her shawl and saw her brother at the corner table in a pool of lamplight, flushed with drink and flattery, holding forth to two lieutenants and a sergeant who were laughing the careful laughter men laugh at a man whose usefulness might outlast their patience. Tam had a glass in his hand and somebody else's pipe in his teeth and the easy, shining confidence of a man who believes he has found the current that carries him upward and cannot imagine it turning.

He saw her and his face did the thing it always did now — a flicker of guilt smoothed instantly into charm. "Wren! Come in, come in, gentlemen, this is my sister, the finest needlewoman in three counties—"

"A word, Tam. At home. Father's asking."

The word *father* worked on Tam like a hand on a winch. He excused himself grandly, promised the lieutenants the moon and several of its better stars, and followed her out into the street, where the darkness swallowed his performance and left only a man walking unsteadily beside his sister, complaining about the cold that wasn't cold anymore.

"Do you smell that?" he said, as they crossed the square. "Thaw. Good riddance to winter. Maybe things loosen up all round, eh? Maybe with spring the army relaxes, maybe—"

"Tam. Listen."

They stopped. Across the dark water the river spoke — a sharp, splintering report, like a pistol fired inside a coffin, followed by a long rumble running away north. Tam went quiet. Even drunk, he was a river-town boy; even corrupted, some part of him still knew the water's grammar.

"That's early," he said.

"That's early," Wren agreed. "And we're leaving on it. Dawn tomorrow."

* * *

At the kitchen table, by one shielded lamp, the family of Eastwick assembled for the second time in a year around a decision none of them could unmake. Aldous sat at the head with his hands flat on the wood, as if steadying it. Cyrus stood by the window, watching the black shape of the river, saying nothing yet — and Wren understood that Cyrus was doing what Cyrus always did, holding their own claim to the smallest possible size, waiting to be counted last. It made her furious and it broke her heart and she loved them for it, all at once, in the way family loves: without permission and without remedy.

"Dawn tomorrow," Aldous said. "Two berths. Four Eastwicks." He said it flatly, the numbers laid out like sorts on a stone, and let the silence do the rest.

"I'll go," Tam said, immediately, and had the grace at least to flush. "I should go. I'm known here now — to the officers, I mean, I have connections, I can make arrangements from the north, find work, send for the rest of—"

"Send for us." Wren's voice came out sharper than she intended. "With what, Tam? Your charm?"

"Wren." Their father's voice was quiet but it landed like a flat of type falling. He turned to her. "You've been planning this. Don't think I haven't seen the coin-counting, girl. You've had your sums done for weeks. So tell me the sum now. All of it."

So Wren told it. The purse at her breast, opened and poured onto the table so the lamplight could count what her heart had counted nightly: one hundred and forty-two crowns. Corven's price, per berth. Two berths bought, none remaining, no negotiation, no refunds, no second boat. She laid out the facts the way Cyrus laid out a page — everything in its place, nothing hidden — and when she finished, the coins lay gleaming in the lamp's small circle like a handful of frozen tears, and nobody touched them.

"The passes are forged," Cyrus said at last, from the window. Everyone turned. "Corven's travel papers. If the army boards that boat, anyone aboard without genuine documents hangs, and the ones with forged papers hang faster, because forged papers prove there's a forger. Which proves there's a printer. Which points here." They paused. "Whoever goes is taking a piece of Father's work with them, whether they know it or not."

"Then Father goes," Tam said. "He made the passes, he takes the risk, he goes north where the rebellion actually—"

"No," said Aldous.

The word was final as a locked forme. He pushed himself up from the table, walked to the window beside Cyrus, and looked out at the river he had lived beside for fifty years. When he spoke again his voice was lower, rougher.

"I'm not leaving my shop to be a barracks and my life's work to be melted for cannon fittings. And I'm not dragging this old cough onto an ice run to freeze in some northern garret while strangers burn everything I cut." He turned, and in the lamplight Wren saw how old he had become in one winter, and how completely unafraid, and that combination frightened her more than the soldiers did. "Two berths. My children will argue it among themselves. That's an order."

"Father—" Cyrus started.

"That's an order," Aldous said again, softer. "For once in my life I'm giving one. Take it."

The river cracked again, closer now, and under their feet, faint but unmistakable, the floor of the shop gave a single shudder — the ice flexing, testing, remembering that it was, in truth, only water.

* * *

Afterward, the arguing happened in fragments, in whispers, in corners, the way real decisions in that house always had. Tam cornered Wren by the cold hearth and hissed that he was the eldest son, that it was his by right, that he had cultivated the officers precisely so the family would have a future, and hadn't that future arrived, wasn't this it? Wren looked at her brother — at the drink-sweet mouth, the fine borrowed waistcoat, the eyes that could not hold hers longer than a heartbeat — and felt something in her chest turn over from anger into grief, the way ice turns into water: no change in substance, only in form.

"It's not yours by right," she said. "Nothing in this family is by right. It's by work. Ask Cyrus how much of the ledger is your work. Ask them."

But Tam wouldn't ask Cyrus anything anymore. And Cyrus, for their part, retreated to the type room and shut the door, and Wren stood in the middle of the kitchen with her purse of coins and understood, with the terrible clarity the hour before dawn brings, that whatever happened at the pilings in a few short hours, someone in this family was going to be left standing on the bank — and that she had already, silently, weeks ago, in the dark of her own heart, decided who it would not be her.

She found Cyrus in the type room. They were sitting on the floor by the loose floorboard, the hidden fresh type spread before them in its cloth, and they looked up at her with those steady eyes that had never once asked the world for room and had carved their own name anyway.

"Don't," Cyrus said, before she could speak.

"Don't what?"

"Don't give me the berth. I can see it in your face, Wren. You've been wearing it for days." They smiled, small and crooked and heartbreaking. "You're going to try to put me on that boat."

"And you're going to refuse," Wren said, "because you're you."

Outside, the sky had begun to pale at its eastern edge, a bruised gray-gold, and the river rose in voice beneath them — cracking, booming, breaking into speech — and down the lane, faint but coming nearer, came the creak of oarlocks and the low call of a smuggler who would wait for first light and not one minute past it.

Wren held out her hand to her sibling. "Then we decide now," she said. "All of us. Before the water decides for us."
