## 16. One Berth

The wind came off the river smelling of rot and running water, the smell of a winter dying badly. Aldous Eastwick stood in the doorway of the print shop with his coat unbuttoned and his hands hanging at his sides — the hands that had cut forty years of letters, that had pressed false names into true paper, that had taught a child to measure the world in ems — and he watched the smuggler's lantern bob down at the wharf like a low star refusing to rise.

One berth. The word had come up the lane an hour ago, breathless, from the cooper's boy: *the ice is going early, the boat leaves at dawn, and there is passage for one.*

One.

Aldous turned back into the shop where his family had gathered as if summoned by the same bell that tolled for requisitions. Tam sat on the composing stone with his boots swinging like a boy's, though he was twenty-two and had never once set type without complaining. Wren stood at the window with her arms crossed over her chest, counting something Aldous could not see — coins, perhaps, or heartbeats, or the hours between now and dawn. And Cyrus — Cyrus was nowhere and everywhere, the way they always were, folded into the shadow behind the press, present the way ink was present in paper: invisible until you looked at what it made.

"Sit down," Aldous said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. The cough was living in his chest again, coiled like a spring, and he did not want it speaking for him tonight. "All of you. Sit."

Tam slid off the stone. Wren did not move from the window. Cyrus stepped forward into the lamplight, and Aldous looked at them — at this child he had raised as neither son nor daughter, who had carved their own name out of the family's silence the way one carves a letter from steel, patiently, against the grain — and felt the whole weight of the night settle onto his shoulders like a forme locked too tight.

"One berth," he said. "So we decide. Not the smuggler. Not the army. Us."

"There's nothing to decide," Tam said quickly, too quickly. "It should be me."

* * *

**Tam**

Of course it should be him. Tam felt the rightness of it in his bones the way other men felt weather. He was the eldest son — well, the eldest *son*, which in Marrow's Bend counted for more than eldest anything else — and the business would be his someday, if there was ever a business again, and a man who inherited a thing ought to be the one to carry it through the fire.

Besides, he had earned it. Hadn't he? He'd sat with Captain Harrow's officers through six nights of bad wine and worse jokes, laughing at things that curdled in him even as he laughed, trading favors for information, keeping the family's name out of as many mouths as he could manage. He had learned which sergeant took bribes and which took offense, and he had used both knowledges. When the army came for the baker's son, it had been Tam's whispered warning — passed through a lieutenant who owed him a card debt — that got the boy to the marshes ahead of the soldiers. Nobody knew that but Tam. Nobody would ever know it, probably, and that was the shape of sacrifice too: the kind nobody saw, the kind that looked exactly like collaboration from the outside.

He looked around the room, waiting for someone to say the obvious.

Wren said it first, and not kindly. "You've been drinking with them for a month, Tam. Why don't you ask your friends for a second berth?"

"They're not my friends." The words came out harder than he meant, and louder. "And there's one berth because there's one berth, and I'm telling you it should be mine."

"Because you're the son," Wren said. Flat. Not a question.

"Because I'm the one with the connections. Because Father—" He stopped. Looked at Aldous, whose face had gone gray in the lamplight. "Father needs someone north who can speak for the family. Who can move among those people. You can't send someone who—"

He heard himself stop again. Heard the sentence he refused to finish, and hated it, and hated more that everyone in the room had finished it for him.

Cyrus hadn't moved. Hadn't flinched. That was somehow worse. Tam watched their face — that calm, unreadable face he'd envied and resented all his life, a face that never gave anything away because it had spent thirty years learning that being seen was dangerous — and he felt something crack along a seam in himself, thin as river ice.

"I didn't mean," he started.

"Yes, you did," Wren said.

The stove ticked. Down at the wharf, faint on the wind, came the sound of the smuggler's crew working the deck, readying for dawn.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren counted the coins again in her mind, though she'd counted them eleven times since the cooper's boy came up the lane. Thirty-one crowns and a half-crown bitten with someone's teeth marks, wrapped in oilcloth under her bed. Enough, she had thought, for two berths at the smuggler's old price. Now there was one berth and no price at all — the man was honoring some arrangement made in the dark weeks before, some promise Aldous had bought with forged paper and Wren did not want to know the details of — and money suddenly meant nothing, which frightened her more than scarcity ever had.

She had been doing arithmetic her whole life. Arithmetic was how love survived in a house like theirs: Father needed leather for the press rollers, so Wren went without new boots; Tam needed pocket money to court the officers, so Wren stretched the stew another week. Every coin was a small translation of care. She had never minded. It made her feel useful, and usefulness was its own kind of belonging.

But this sum had no solution. One berth, four Eastwicks. Even arithmetic failed when the divisor was cruelty.

She looked at Cyrus and the answer assembled itself in her chest with the terrible simplicity of all true sums: Cyrus goes. Of course Cyrus goes. Cyrus, who had kept every ledger line straight while the world tilted; Cyrus, whom the town had looked through for three decades and never once *at*; Cyrus, who deserved — who had earned with thirty years of patient invisible labor — one place on one boat to somewhere their name, their real name, the one they had cut for themselves, might be spoken aloud without correction.

"You go," Wren said, turning from the window. "That's my word. Cyrus goes."

"Cyrus," Tam scoffed.

"Cyrus," Wren repeated, and crossed the room, and took their hands — those clever scarred hands, cold as type metal — and held them where the lamplight could reach. "Listen to me. Whatever they decide in there—" she nodded toward the knot of them, the family, the argument already re-forming, "—I want you to hear it from me first, plainly, without any bargaining attached. You are the one who goes. I would rather stand on this bank and watch that boat carry you north than sleep another night knowing you were still in a town where the army has a list."

"The army has a list with all our names on it," Cyrus said quietly. "The ledger."

"Then the list is wrong." Wren heard her own voice shake and steadied it. "Let it be wrong. Let them chase names we never wrote."

She felt Cyrus's fingers tighten around hers, and for a moment — just a moment — she saw something move behind their eyes, deep, like current under ice, and she thought: *they're going to refuse. They're going to refuse me.* And she gripped harder, selfishly, a sister's grip, and hated herself for it, and did not let go.

* * *

**Aldous**

Aldous let them argue. There was a craft to letting an argument run, the same as there was a craft to letting ink dry — interfere too soon and you spoiled everything. So he sat by the stove with his hands flat on his knees and listened to Tam talk about inheritance and connections, listened to Wren spend herself like coin on her sibling's behalf, and understood, with the clarity that comes only to men who have begun measuring what remains of their time, that he had built this exact moment. Not deliberately. But inevitably. A man prints false passes for rebels, a man teaches all his children the same alphabet, and then he acts surprised when each of them sets the type of their own conviction.

The cough shifted in his chest. He pressed it down.

When the room had worn itself hoarse, he stood. His knees cracked like the river ice outside, and everyone quieted, because they knew the sound of their father about to pronounce.

"Tam goes," he said.

The word landed and lay still in the room like a dropped quoin.

"Father—" Wren began.

"Hear me." He raised one hand, the cutter's hand, the knuckles gone broad and wrong with forty years of steel and graver. "I have thought about nothing else since the cooper's boy ran up the lane, and I will tell you my reasoning once, and it is not sentimental. Tam, sit down and do not thank me yet."

Tam sat. His face was doing several things at once; Aldous chose not to read them.

"First: whoever goes north must be able to pass among strangers, to talk, to charm, to lie glibly when lying is required. God forgive me, but our Tam has spent a month training for exactly that. Second: whoever stays here will likely hang beside me when they finally prove the forgery, and I will not carry the end of the Eastwick name to the gallows. Third—" and here his voice caught on the spring in his chest, and he coughed once, hard, into his fist, and saw the bright thread of blood before he closed his hand, "—third. The third reason is mine, and I'll keep it."

He wiped his fist against his coat, casually, and met Cyrus's eyes across the room. Cyrus had seen. Cyrus always saw. That was the third reason, and it was not for saying: that of all his children, the one who most deserved rescue was the one most capable of surviving without it. That was not comfort. That was arithmetic of a crueler kind.

"It's decided," Aldous said. "Dawn. One of us puts Tam on that boat if we have to tie him to the mast."

Tam opened his mouth — to protest, absurdly, or to accept, Aldous couldn't tell — and Aldous watched his eldest son wrestle with the fact that he had gotten precisely what he had demanded, and found it tasted different in the mouth than it had in the wanting.

* * *

**Cyrus**

They talked a long while after Aldous retired to the back room, ostensibly to rest, actually — Cyrus suspected — to cough where no one could count the blood. Tam paced. Wren began, without discussion, to pack: bread into cloth, the oilcloth bundle of coins, a spare shirt of Tam's that smelled of officer's tobacco. The house performed its preparations around Cyrus like water around a stone.

Cyrus stood at the case, their case, the upper case where the capitals lived in their wooden compartments, and let their fingers walk the boxes without taking anything. A, B, C. The alphabet their father had put into their hands at seven years old, saying *learn this and no one can ever take your words from you.* The alphabet they had used, twenty years later, to cut their own name into steel — *Cyrus* — because the world had offered them no name that fit and they had decided, quietly, at the composing stone, to make one. No army could seize it. No ledger recorded it. It existed in steel and in the mouths of three people in this room and nowhere else on earth.

One berth.

They understood now what their father's silence meant, what Wren's grip had meant, what Tam's pacing meant. Everyone in this house was trying to give away the same thing, and the giving had become its own argument, and beneath all of it Cyrus could feel the truth assembling itself, letter by letter, the way a compositor feels the sentence complete before the forme is locked:

*The berth was never going to be mine. I was always going to give it. The only question anyone has been arguing tonight is who I give it to.*

Tam, who had almost sold them all and might yet. Tam, who was their brother anyway, blood and annoyance and childhood fevers shared in one narrow bed. Their father wanted Tam saved. Perhaps because Tam needed saving. Cyrus did not need saving; Cyrus needed — and this was the thought that arrived with the weight of a fully locked forme, immovable, true — Cyrus needed to *stay*. Needed to stay the way a letter needs its place in the line. Everything that mattered was here: the press, the hidden type, the forged pages sewn inside tomorrow's gazette, the town full of names the army wanted and could not have. And Vane. Somewhere out in the dark, Vane, carrying whatever torn thing he carried, choosing nightly, now, the opposite of his orders.

Invisibility had been an illusion. They had known it for weeks. But visibility — being *seen* — required standing somewhere, in the light, where the seeing happened. Marrow's Bend. Here. Exactly here.

"Cyrus." Wren's voice, close. "You've been standing at the case for an hour. Say something."

Cyrus lifted their fingers from the compartment marked **C** and turned around. The lamp guttered. Out on the river, the ice groaned — a long, deep, animal sound, the sound of a season surrendering — and beneath it, fainter, came the creak of the smuggler's hull working at her moorings, impatient for dawn.

"There's nothing to say," Cyrus said. "We put Tam on the boat."

Tam stopped pacing. For one naked moment his face showed gratitude, actual gratitude, unguarded — the face of the boy who had once shared their fever bed — and then he covered it, as he covered everything, with a shrug. "Well. Someone in the family ought to survive to be insufferable about it."

Wren searched Cyrus's face the way she searched a ledger, line by line, looking for the error. Cyrus gave her nothing. There would be time at the wharf — hours, a whole night — for the truth to surface, for the fight Cyrus knew was coming, for Wren to understand that the berth had changed owners somewhere in the last hour without anyone announcing it.

Above the stove, the kettle began to sing. Outside, the ice spoke again, longer this time, a crack running somewhere out in the dark toward the middle of the river, and Cyrus thought: *by morning, none of this may hold. By morning, everything moves.*

They reached for the kettle to pour, and froze — because through the shutter slats, down the lane, a lantern was coming up from the wharf. Two figures. One wore the coat of an army captain.

Vane was coming, hours early, and he was not alone.
