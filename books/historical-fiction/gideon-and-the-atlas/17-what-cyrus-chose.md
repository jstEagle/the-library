## 17. What Cyrus Chose

The wind came off the river smelling of rot and open water, and Cyrus stood at the shop window watching the last of the winter go to pieces below.

It had happened in an hour. All night the ice had groaned like a beast turning in its sleep, and then somewhere past three there had been a crack so loud that Wren cried out from her room, and by first light the river was a black, churning artery choked with plates of grinding floes. The smuggler's boy had pounded on the door before dawn — mud to his knees, eyes wild — saying the *Kestrel* would take on passengers at the willow landing at sunup, and sunup only, because after today the channel might close as easily as it had opened.

One berth. That was all the coin would buy, and now there wasn't even time to argue about it properly. There was only time enough to choose.

Cyrus dressed in the dark, fingers finding each button by memory, and listened to the house breathe. Aldous coughing in his room — that wet, torn sound that had started sometime during the occupation and grown roots. Wren moving downstairs already, quick and quiet, her arithmetic done days ago. Tam's bed empty. Tam had not come home until well past midnight, smelling of the officers' brandy and someone else's tobacco, and Cyrus had heard him stumble up the stairs and had lain awake hating him and loving him in the same long breath.

That was the thing no ledger could hold: two entries against the same name, both true, neither canceling the other out.

Cyrus went down to the type room, where the press stood under its dust sheet like a shrouded animal, and laid a hand on the cold iron of the platen. The forged passes still sat locked inside yesterday's forme, hidden beneath the official gazette's respectable columns where no soldier had thought to look — where they would sit until the paper printed tomorrow and the names scattered into a hundred hands across the county like seed. Cyrus did not let themselves think about what happened after. One catastrophe at a time. The river was handling this morning's.

"Cyrus." Wren stood in the doorway with the lamp, and behind her Aldous, wrapped in his coat though it was not yet fully light, his face gray but steady. He carried the ledger under his arm.

Not the copy. The original. The great book with its ten years of births and debts and deaths, every family in Marrow's Bend bound page by page to the Eastwick name — the book the army wanted, the book that could hang half the town if a clever officer sat down with it long enough. Cyrus had watched their father carry it through the requisition like a man carrying a sleeping child past wolves, and had understood only later what the calm had meant: Aldous had always known he would have to decide about this book someday, and had been waiting, all these months, to see who else in the family was strong enough to decide it for him.

"We should go," Wren said. "The landing—"

"I know where the landing is."

"And you've chosen."

It was not a question. Wren had counted the coins herself; she knew the sum bought one passage, and she had spent three nights telling Cyrus in a dozen small ways that it would not be her on that boat, and Cyrus had spent three nights refusing to hear it. Now there was no more time for refusal. The river had settled the calendar.

"It isn't me," Cyrus said. "And it isn't you."

Wren's face went very still. "Cyrus—"

"Tam."

The name came out of Cyrus sounding strange, like type set in a hand they didn't recognize. But once it was spoken, Cyrus found it held its impression cleanly. It was the right word, cut deep enough to print.

"He'll never forgive us," Wren said.

"He doesn't have to forgive us. He just has to live."

Aldous said nothing. He set the ledger down on the counter with a soft thump, and looked at Cyrus for a long moment — and whatever passed between them passed without language, in the way of things between people who had shared a composing stone for twenty years. Then he nodded once, and turned toward the stairs to wake his eldest son.

* * *

Tam fought them, which surprised Cyrus, and wept, which did not.

They got him down to the willow landing with the sky going from ash to dirty gold above the water, the four of them walking fast through streets that stank of thaw and woodsmoke, soldiers already stirring at the guard posts, nobody stopping them because nobody expected the printer's whole family to be abroad at this hour. Tam had raged and then bargained and then gone sullenly quiet, and it was the quiet that frightened Cyrus most — Tam silent was Tam calculating, and even now, even here, Cyrus could see the arithmetic running behind their brother's eyes: one berth, four Eastwicks, why him, why always the arrangement that cost him.

At the landing the *Kestrel* lay against the bank with her gunwales scraped raw from the ice, and the smuggler — a narrow man named Pell with frostbite whitening two fingers — stood with his hand out and his eyes on the treeline.

"Coin first," he said. "And one body. I'm not waiting on deliberations."

"You're not getting deliberations," Cyrus said.

Tam stood at the edge of the boards with the river roaring past behind him, brown water thick with grinding ice, and his face was a mess — fury and fear and the brandy still working in him, and underneath it all something Cyrus had never seen there before and never wanted to see again: terror without any floor beneath it. Their brother, who had swaggered through the occupation buying favors with jokes, who had believed so completely that charm was a currency the world was obliged to honor, stood at the edge of exile with nothing in his hands.

"I won't go," Tam said. "You can't make me. I'll tell Vane. I'll tell him everything — the ledger, the passes, I'll—"

"You'll get on the boat," Cyrus said quietly.

"Or what?"

And there it was. Cyrus had rehearsed a hundred arguments on the walk down — that Tam was the son, that Tam was the name, that someone had to carry the family north, that Aldous wanted it. All of it prepared, none of it usable, because Tam was past argument, past reason, standing on the edge of the known world with his heels dug in. There was nothing left but the thing Cyrus had sworn never to do: to become the force in the story instead of the invisible hand that set it.

So Cyrus stepped forward, took their brother by the collar of his coat with both hands — the grip their father used on a misaligned sort, firm and impersonal — and put their face close to his, close enough to smell the brandy and the sleepless night on him.

"Listen to me," Cyrus said. "There is no version of this morning where you stay. If you step off those boards I will drag you onto that boat myself, and if my hands aren't enough I will use every favor our name ever bought in this town, and if that fails I will stand here and tell you, in front of Pell and God and that soldier at the guard post, exactly what happens to men who stay in Marrow's Bend when Captain Vane finally gets the order he's been waiting for. You think the army wants the printer? They want witnesses, Tam. They want sons who sat drinking with officers and heard things. You've heard things. Everything you traded your jokes for is evidence now, and evidence doesn't get passage north — it gets a cell, or worse, it gets *used*. Go. Be angry at me for the rest of your life. Be angry on dry ground."

Tam stared at them. His mouth worked. And Cyrus saw the moment the fear finally found the shape of the truth — saw it land in their brother's eyes like a stone dropped down a well, taking a long time to hit bottom.

"You'd really put me on it," Tam whispered. "By force."

"Yes," Cyrus said. It came out steady. Inside, something enormous and quiet broke, the way the ice had broken, all at once and irreversibly. "I would. It's the first hard thing I've ever done, and I'm doing it because I love you, and you can hate me for the combination as long as you live."

For a moment Tam swayed, and Cyrus thought he would swing at them, and braced for it — almost welcomed it, some bitter part of them, wanting the last touch between brothers to be honest.

But Tam didn't swing. He began instead to cry — ugly, open-mouthed, the crying of a boy much younger than his years — and he let Wren push him toward the gangplank, and let Pell grab his sleeve, and he turned at the top of the boards with tears cutting tracks through the grime on his face and said, unrepentant to the last syllable:

"This isn't gratitude. You understand that? I'll never say it was."

"No," Cyrus agreed. Their voice cracked only slightly, and they were proud of the *only*. "I don't expect it was ever going to be."

Then Tam was aboard, and the lines were cast off, and the *Kestrel* leaned out into the current with her sail filling raggedly, and Cyrus stood on the landing and watched the boat grow smaller against the black water and the white grinding ice, watching until the figure at the stern — whether waving or cursing, impossible to say at that distance — became indistinguishable from the morning mist coming off the river.

Gone. Their brother, alive, gone north with the family's whole savings sewn into his coat and none of the family's goodness in his heart, and Cyrus loved him anyway, loved him precisely the way you love a letter you know is full of errors and send anyway because it must reach someone. That was the whole of it. That was everything.

Aldous put a hand on Cyrus's shoulder. It weighed more than it used to.

"Well cut," he said softly. Which, from a type cutter, was the highest sentence in the language.

* * *

They walked home slowly, the three of them, through streets waking into the ordinary business of an occupied town — a woman beating a rug over her sill, a sentry yawning at the corner, bread smoke from the bakery — and the ordinariness of it made Cyrus want to laugh, or scream, or both, because the largest act of their life had concluded before breakfast and the world hadn't paused to notice. Somewhere north of here, on a river running free at last, their brother was being carried out of the story. And here the baker was baking. Here the rug was being beaten.

Wren walked close, her arm through Cyrus's, saying nothing, needing to say nothing. She had argued for herself at midnight and lost by dawn, and she did not sulk and did not thank; she simply stayed pressed to Cyrus's side like heat, and Cyrus understood that this, too, was a kind of ledger entry — a debt recorded between sisters-brothers that would be repaid in years, in staying, in the slow coin of shared work.

Aldous stopped at the shop door, key in hand, and did not turn it. He looked at the sign above the lintel — EASTWICK & SON, the paint faded, the ampersand their mother's design, the word SON carved by Aldous's own chisel the year Cyrus was twelve and had stood in the type room and said, in front of a customer, that they were neither the one thing nor the other, and had waited to be struck — and Aldous had said nothing that day either, only climbed the ladder that evening and added the ampersand, so that the sign read EASTWICK &, which everyone read as EASTWICK AND SON and which Cyrus alone had always known read EASTWICK AND, full stop. A name left open. An invitation to finish it.

Their father was looking at that sign now with a strange expression, and Cyrus realized he was memorizing it.

"Papa," Cyrus said carefully. "The ledger."

Aldous looked down at the great book still under his arm, and his calm — that terrible requisition-day calm — settled back over his face like a sheet drawn over furniture.

"The army comes for it within the week," he said. "Vane has his order; he told me as much when he pretended to be searching my office. They mean to try half the town on the strength of my accounts." He turned the key. The door swung open on the smell of ink and oil and cold iron, the smell of Cyrus's entire life. "A book is ink, Cyrus. Ink is the least permanent thing in the world. We prove that every day at the case."

He crossed to the press and knelt and opened the little iron door of the firebox, where the heat for drying the ink still lived in its bed of coals, banked low overnight, breathing orange in the dimness.

Wren understood first. Cyrus watched understanding move across her face like weather.

"Papa—" she started.

"A ledger holds names," Aldous said, still in that mild craftsman's voice, as if discussing kerning. "This town cannot afford to have its names held. Not by them." He fed a sliver of pine kindling into the coals and blew, gently, twice, the way he had blown on molten lead a thousand evenings while Cyrus watched from the stool by the type cabinet, learning before language that fire was the beginning of every word.

Cyrus stood in the doorway with the river's roar faint in their ears and their brother somewhere north on the water, and felt the strange peace settle over them at last — not peace like stillness, but peace like a forme locked tight, every piece of lead and wood pressed into its place under the quoins, holding together under pressure, ready to take the impression of anything.

Tam was saved. Wren was beside them. Their father was kneeling at the fire with ten years of the town's history under his arm, and Cyrus knew — with the certainty of a hand finding its sort in the dark — what was asked of them next, and knew they would not flinch from it.

"Bring it here, then," Cyrus said, and shut the shop door behind them, and threw the bolt.
