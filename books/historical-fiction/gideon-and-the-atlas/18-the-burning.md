## 18. The Burning

The order came at dusk, nailed to the pump post in the square where the town had once posted weddings and lost dogs: *By command of Colonel Ashgrave, all records, ledgers, registers, and papers of account within the borough of Marrow's Bend are to be surrendered to the provost by nine o'clock tomorrow morning. Concealment of documents will be construed as sedition.*

Cyrus read it twice, because the first time their eyes refused the shape of the words. The paper was good stock — army stock, rag-laid, watermarked — and the type was clean, professionally set. Someone in the provost's office knew their trade. Someone had set that notice with hands that understood spacing, and Cyrus hated them a little for it, the way one hates a stranger who has touched something intimate.

The wind came up off the river carrying the smell of rotting ice and woodsmoke from the occupied quarter of town. Behind Cyrus, the lamplighter was making his rounds early, hurrying, as though light itself might be requisitioned by morning.

They walked home through streets gone strange with soldiers — sentries at the corners, boots on cobblestones in a rhythm the town had never owned. A sergeant checked their face against some memorized list and found nothing there worth remembering. Cyrus let him find nothing. Invisibility had been their craft long before it became their necessity, and they wore it now like a well-fitted coat.

In the print shop, Aldous sat at the workbench with a whetstone and his finest graver, honing an edge he did not need. He looked up when the door shut.

"They've called for the ledger," Cyrus said.

Their father nodded slowly, as if this were merely the next line in a proof he had been correcting all his life. "I heard the crier from here." He set down the graver. "And Wren?"

"Gone to the Harrow girl's to sit with the baby. I told her not to come back before midnight."

"And Tam?"

"Tam is drinking Madeira with Captain Bellamy and telling him what a loyal town we are."

Aldous almost smiled. Almost. It was a terrible thing, how far their father had traveled past fear into something flat and clear as plate glass. Since the search — since Cyrus had watched a lieutenant's gloved fingers walk the shelf of galleys inches from the forged passes locked inside tomorrow's gazette forme — Aldous had been calm the way rivers are calm over deep water.

"Then it's yours to do," Aldous said. "I cut the names. You kept them. It seems right that you should be the one."

* * *

The ledger lived in its iron-bound cabinet beneath the stairs, and Cyrus knelt before it the way other people knelt before altars, though they had never thought of it as worship until this moment. Ten years of their own hand was in that book. They had entered it at twelve years old, copying their father's cramped figures into fair script; they had entered births and debts and deaths, the price of paper by the ream, the cost of Wren's fever medicine against Tam's school fees, the widow Cetch's standing credit of four shillings that Aldous had quietly forgiven every Christmas and quietly restored every January so she would never have to know she was charity.

Every family in Marrow's Bend was bound to these pages. That had always seemed to Cyrus a form of belonging — the town held together in ink, each name given its measure of weight like a piece of type in a stick. Now the army wanted that same book for the opposite reason. Every debt recorded was a loyalty charted. Every irregular entry — and there were irregular entries, payments marked *received* that no honest merchant could account for, the quiet bookkeeping of a rebellion funded through a printer's till — every one of those was evidence. Not just against the Eastwicks. Against the Harrows, the Cetches, the ferryman Dawes, the mill families upriver whose contributions Aldous had laundered through columns of fictitious paper purchases. Two hundred names, give or take, and the army needed only to read them to have Marrow's Bend by the throat.

Cyrus lifted the ledger out. It was heavier than a book had any right to be — thirty pounds of rag paper sewn onto cords, the boards scuffed at the corners where it had been dragged across counters for twenty years. Their father's hand for the first third. Their own for the rest, growing steadier year by year, growing into a hand that was neither Aldous's nor anyone else's, a hand they had invented for themselves the way they had invented their name.

They sat with it on their knees for a long time while the shop ticked and settled around them.

There was a copy, of course. There had to be — Cyrus had made one, in their smallest script, on the thinnest paper, folded into a packet the size of a playing card and hidden where no searcher would think to look, inside the hollow heel of the old proofing press that hadn't turned a page since Cyrus was nine. But the copy held only the rebel accounts, the true entries, the things worth preserving. It did not hold the widow Cetch's forgiven four shillings. It did not hold the record of Tam's birth weight, entered in Aldous's hand between a job lot of pamphlets and an invoice for lampblack. It did not hold the page where Cyrus themselves, at thirteen, had first written *Cyrus Eastwick* instead of leaving the name column blank, and their father had seen it and said nothing, and the ink had simply stayed.

To burn the ledger was to burn all of that. To burn it was also to make liars of two hundred people — no, to make them innocent. Without the book, the army had rumor, suspicion, informers. Tam's drunken hints. But rumor could not be cross-referenced. Suspicion could not add a column.

And without the book, Vane had nothing to trade for. Cyrus pushed that thought away the way one pushes a hand from a wound, and it came back immediately, and they pushed again, and it stayed away only a little longer each time.

Outside, a patrol went by singing something coarse about hanging printers. Inside, Cyrus stood, and crossed to the hearth beside the press where the fire burned low and steady — the fire that heated the platen in winter, that dried the dampened sheets, that had warmed this room through every year of their life — and they began, methodically, the way they did everything, to prepare.

* * *

They did not burn it whole. A book that size would smother a fire; a book that size would fight, the way dense paper fights, curling and blackening in layers while its heart stays cold. Cyrus knew paper the way a priest knows scripture, and they would not do this thing badly.

So they broke the binding first.

The knife went through the sewing cords with a sound like a small gasp. The boards came away, and then the spine, and then the sections fell open into loose signatures, quires of pages that suddenly weighed nothing, that fluttered when Cyrus breathed. Twenty years of a town, undone in ten minutes by one blade. It occurred to Cyrus that this was the reverse of everything they had ever learned. All their life had been an education in making things hold — locking a forme so the type would not shift under pressure, tying a sheet so it would feed true, binding accounts so the sums agreed. And here at the end of it was the other craft, the shadow craft: unmaking, cleanly, completely, so that nothing could ever be read backward into being.

They fed the fire section by section.

Aldous sat nearby and did not help, which Cyrus understood as a kind of mercy. This was not to be shared, or witnessed even by the man whose hand had made half of it — except that Aldous did witness it, sitting very still with his ruined knuckles folded, watching his son and daughter burn the family into safety, and perhaps that contradiction was simply what love looked like in the Eastwick house: present, silent, refusing to look away.

The first sections caught reluctantly. Then the fire learned what was being asked of it and rose up orange and hungry, and the pages went fast after that, the way late pages of anything go fast.

Cyrus read as they fed. They could not help it; the eye was trained, the eye seized on familiar script the way a drowning man seizes driftwood. *Widow Cetch, cr. 4s.* Gone. *Dawes, ferry tolls, Michaelmas.* Gone. *Tam — born underweight, God keep him.* Gone, into a brightness that left ash fine as ground pigment sifting down through the grate. Their own careful columns dissolved one after another, the arithmetic of a whole community rising off the pages as heat, and Cyrus felt something tear loose in their chest that was grief and relief braided so tightly they could not have told the strands apart with a graver's precision.

Because here was the truth of it, the thing they had known since the night they found the forged passes under the floorboards and had not let themselves say aloud: erasure was not only destruction. Erasure was salvation. The army had come to Marrow's Bend hunting a list, and lists were the whole machinery of terror — names gathered, names sorted, names summoned. Let them hunt a book that no longer existed. Let them stand in this shop tomorrow morning with their warrant and their sack and find an iron-bound cabinet full of ash and a family full of ignorance, and let the whole rotten apparatus of names grind against nothing.

The fire roared. The room filled with the smell of scorched ink, acrid and strangely sweet, lampblack and linseed going up together. Sparks climbed the flue. Cyrus's eyes streamed, and they did not wipe them, because if they took a hand from the feeding to wipe their eyes they might stop feeding, and they could not stop; the book had to be entire in the flames before dawn or not at all. Half-burned evidence was worse than none. A fragmentary ledger still proved. Only total absence could protect.

So they fed it all. The debts and the forgivenesses. The births and the burials. The price of every ream. The page where a child had named themselves in another person's book and been allowed.

Near midnight, Wren slipped in through the back, smelling of cold river air and someone else's kitchen. She stood in the doorway with her shawl clutched at her throat, taking in the blaze, the scattered boards, the iron cabinet gaping empty, and Cyrus watched her understand it — watched her face move from alarm to calculation to a fierce, wet-eyed approval that she expressed, characteristically, by saying nothing at all. She came and knelt beside them and began feeding sections herself, matching their rhythm exactly, sister and sibling tending the same fire, and the three of them worked in silence until the last signature curled and collapsed and there was nothing left of twenty years but a grate full of pale flakes and a heat that pushed them all back a step.

"It's done," Cyrus said. Their voice came out scraped raw, smoke-damaged, unrecognizable. Good. Let the voice be new too.

Aldous rose, joints cracking, and laid one hand briefly on Cyrus's shoulder — the heaviest touch he had given them in years, the weight of it deliberate as type metal. "Now wash your face and burn your cuffs," he said. "Ash tells tales. And Cyrus—" He paused at the stair, silhouetted against the dark. "Whatever they ask you in the morning, you don't know what was in that book. You never knew. Practice it tonight."

"I've been practicing my whole life," Cyrus said, and their father nodded once, and went up.

* * *

They cleaned until nearly three. Wren scrubbed the grate and raked the ash into the ash-pail and carried it out to the privy pit herself, refusing help, because ash could be sifted and she wanted it beyond sifting. Cyrus took the scorched iron bands and the charred spine-board out to the riverbank and threw them as far as the arm allowed, into the black water below the broken ice, where they sank without ceremony. Coming back up the path, breathless, they saw the sky beginning to bruise gray in the east and realized with a lurch that morning was not a distant thing anymore. Morning was hours away. Morning was coming up the river like a gunboat.

Back in the shop, the gazette forme still sat locked on the stone, the forged passes sleeping inside it beneath the official news, waiting for the press, waiting for daylight, waiting for whatever the day intended to do with them. Cyrus stood over it a long moment, hands hanging empty at their sides, feeling the strangeness of having no book to keep. For ten years their hands had always been full — pen, burnisher, quire, ledger. Now they hung at their sides like tools belonging to someone else.

That was when the knock came.

Not the heavy military triple-knock of the patrols. A soft double rap, apologetic, in a rhythm Cyrus's body recognized before their mind did — the knock Elias Vane had used at sixteen, coming back late from the tavern, letting himself hope someone would leave the bar unlocked for him.

Cyrus's heart went once, hard, against their ribs. Through the window the street lay empty and blue-gray, and Vane stood at the door alone, out of uniform, wearing only a civilian coat and a face that had clearly not slept, and behind him somewhere downriver the ice boomed like ordnance, and behind him somewhere up the street dawn was assembling its witnesses, and Cyrus put their hand on the bar of the door knowing — knowing with the certainty of a forme locked tight — that whatever Vane had come to say, it would decide whether the fire had bought anything at all.

They opened it.
