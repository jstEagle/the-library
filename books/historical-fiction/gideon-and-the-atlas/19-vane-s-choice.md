## 19. Vane's Choice

The order came at dawn, folded twice and sealed with the army's wax, and Captain Elias Vane read it three times before the words would settle into anything like meaning.

*By direction of the Provisional Authority: apprehend Aldous Eastwick, printer, on charges of forgery and sedition. Secure all documents, plates, and papers within the premises of the Eastwick press. The ledger known to contain the names and obligations of the inhabitants of Marrow's Bend is to be delivered intact to the office of the Provost.*

*Intact.* The word sat on the page like a stone. He had written requisition orders himself — dozens of them, neat little instruments of taking — and he knew how the ink could be made to mean whatever the reader needed it to mean. *Intact* meant whole. It meant every name, every debt, every birth recorded in Aldous Eastwick's careful hand, delivered into the hands of men who would read those names aloud in a square that still smelled of smoke, and decide from them who was guilty of what.

It meant Cyrus.

Vane set the order on his desk and pressed both palms flat against the wood, as if he might hold the desk still while everything else moved. Outside his borrowed quarters — a merchant's house stripped of its family two weeks ago — the town was waking under occupation. A sentry's boots on frozen gravel. Somewhere a rooster, absurdly defiant. The river running gray and heavy under its last skin of ice, groaning all night like an old man turning over in bed.

Ten years ago he had stood at a composing stone with ink to his elbows and a girl — no, not a girl, he had understood even then, though neither of them had words for it yet; a person named Cyrus, answering to nothing anyone else had chosen for them — had leaned close enough that he could smell the solvent on their hair, and shown him how a letter cut wrong by half a hair's width could ruin a whole page. *The world is measured in ems,* Cyrus had said, laughing. *Everything else is opinion.*

He had left without saying goodbye. He had told himself it was mercy — that a clean cut healed cleaner than a ragged one — and for ten years he had believed it, the way a man believes a lie he has paid for dearly enough to want his money's worth.

Now the army had ordered him to arrest the Eastwicks, and Elias Vane sat very still in the gray light and discovered what he actually was.

* * *

He went first to the shop, because a captain gathering evidence for an arrest should be seen to gather it, and because he could not stay away.

The door stood open. That was the first wrong thing. The Eastwick shop never stood open — Aldous kept it shut against damp the way other men kept their children shut against fever — and the smell that met Vane on the threshold was wrong too: char and wet ash instead of ink and oil and the particular cold-metal sweetness of a case of new sorts.

The press stood where it always stood, scarred and magnificent, but beside it the fire-pot had been fed past all reason, heaped with burned paper, and the floor around it was black with a slurry of ash and melted sizing, stamped through with boot prints — small ones. Narrow ones. Boots he would have known anywhere.

Cyrus had done this. Cyrus had stood at this fire and fed the ledger into it, name by name, debt by debt, every family in Marrow's Bend, until there was nothing left but this gray lake on the floorboards.

And beneath the ash — Vane crouched, touched it, felt the grit of it between his fingers like a penitent feeling for a relic — somewhere under that slurry were the traces of forged passes. Travel documents printed in Aldous Eastwick's flawless imitation of government hand. The crime the army had been hunting for a month. The crime that would hang a printer from his own beam.

Except the proof was on the hearth now. Except the proof was in Vane's lungs when he breathed.

Aldous sat in the back room, in the chair by the cold stove, and he did not rise when the captain entered. He looked, if anything, relieved — a man who had been waiting a long time for a knock and was glad the waiting was over. His hands lay open on his knees, stained to the second knuckle with years of ink that no water would ever lift, and he studied Vane with the mild, measuring attention he had once turned on apprentices.

"You've come about the ledger," Aldous said. Not a question.

"I've come about the ledger," Vane said.

"It's gone."

"So I see."

"My child burned it." Aldous said the word *child* deliberately, and then, watching Vane's face, let it go, the way a man sets down a weight to show you he could carry it but chooses not to. "On their own judgment. Without my instruction. Though I confess—" and here the old printer smiled, thin and tired and terribly proud— "I have never been better pleased by an act of disobedience in my life."

"The passes," Vane said. "The forged documents. Where are they?"

"Burned. Or locked in formes for tomorrow's gazette, or cut up into confetti and scattered on the river — how should I know? Search. That's your trade." He gestured vaguely at the shop with its pried floorboards and emptied drawers. "You'll find type. You'll find a great deal of type. Type is not a crime, Captain. Yet."

They looked at each other across the ruined room: the soldier and the forger, the law and the sedition, and between them, unspoken, the third thing — the person whose boots had made those prints in the ash.

"If you take me," Aldous said quietly, "they will want more than me. They will want witnesses. They will want the family questioned. They will want—" he paused, and chose the sentence with the care of a man choosing sorts, "—they will want my child, Captain, and you know it, and I am asking you not to pretend otherwise while standing in my shop."

"I have orders," Vane said.

"Yes," said Aldous. "Everyone has orders. The whole world runs on orders. It's why paper matters so much — someone must print the things, after all." He folded his hands. "Do what you came to do. But think first. You were apprenticed here once. Think about who taught you to lock a chase so it wouldn't spill when the press came down."

Vane thought about it. He thought about it for the rest of the day.

* * *

In the end he wrote the report himself, alone, by candlelight, and it took him four attempts.

The first attempt told the truth: *Ledger destroyed by fire prior to seizure. Evidence of forgery likewise consumed. Forger unknown; presumed fled north across the ice.* He looked at it and saw that the truth, honestly reported, would bring the provost's men to Marrow's Bend within a week, would turn every house inside out, would put Aldous Eastwick in irons and Cyrus Eastwick in front of a tribunal that did not trouble itself over fine distinctions between printers and their parents.

The second attempt lied clumsily: *Ledger secured but damaged in transport.* A lie like that invited inspection. Inspection would find ash. Ash would find questions. Questions would find Cyrus.

The third attempt lied well, and lying well frightened him more than any order ever had, because he discovered he was fluent at it — that the same steadiness of hand which had once set perfect lines of eight-point Caslon could compose a falsehood with no visible gaps, no loose sorts, nothing to betray the spacing of the truth underneath.

*Report of Capt. E. Vane, Requisition Command, Marrow's Bend:* he wrote, *Regarding the document known as the Eastwick ledger. Upon attending the premises this date, I found the press intact and the premises recently damaged by fire, origin unknown. The ledger and associated papers were consumed prior to my arrival. Local informants indicate the suspected forger fled north prior to the thaw and cannot be pursued before the river opens. No documentary evidence remains. I recommend the matter be closed and the occupation of the printing premises continued pending withdrawal.*

He read it over. It was good work. It would pass any inspection, because it contained exactly one unverifiable claim — *fled north* — and unverifiable claims were the currency of every army report ever filed. No evidence remained, which was true. The matter could not proceed, which was true. A forger had fled, which was — he set down the pen — a kindness dressed in the livery of a fact.

His first falsified report in eleven years of service. He signed it with a hand that did not shake, and hated how steady it was, and sealed the wax, and understood that he had crossed a line that could not be uncrossed, and found, to his lasting astonishment, that he felt lighter. Not innocent — he would never feel innocent again — but *lighter*, as if the report had been ballast thrown overboard, as if duty and honesty had finally come apart in his hands and he had chosen, freely, knowing the cost, which one to keep.

Duty he would carry still. He would carry it badly, crookedly, in service of the people his duty commanded him to break. But he would not carry this. Not this one order. Not the arrest of Aldous Eastwick, who taught boys to cut steel; not the questioning of Wren, who counted coins like prayers; not the destruction of Cyrus, who had once measured the whole world in ems and believed, foolishly, generously, that everything else was opinion.

There would be a price. Reports were read; patterns were noticed; a captain who lost evidence once might be careless, but a captain who lost it twice was compromised. Some morning, months or years from now, a clerk with a good memory would pull his file and find the seam in the fabric, and pull the thread. Let him. Vane blew out the candle and sat in the dark with the sealed report and felt the future settle over him like weather.

* * *

He found Cyrus at the riverbank, because he knew he would. There was one place in Marrow's Bend where a person went to be unseen by everyone except the one person looking, and it was the shingle below the old ferry landing, where the ice was rotting in long dark leads and the water ran fast and loud with the spring coming on.

Cyrus heard him coming — Cyrus always heard him coming, had heard him coming across ten silent years — and did not turn.

"You burned it," Vane said.

"You read the ashes." Now Cyrus turned. Their face was smudged, their sleeves scorched at the cuffs, and their eyes held the terrible calm of someone who has done an irreversible thing and is waiting to find out what it cost. "Yes. Every name. Every debt. Mrs. Halloway's coffin money. The Fenns' flour account. Tam's — " a breath, almost a laugh — "Tam's tab. All of it, into the fire. Father watched. He didn't stop me. I don't think he could have, and I don't think he wanted to."

"They'll suspect you."

"They already suspected us. Now they can prove nothing." Cyrus looked out at the water. "Was it your report? This morning's? The one that says a forger fled north?"

Vane said nothing, which was an answer.

"You falsified it." Cyrus's voice was low, wondering, almost angry in its wonder. "Eleven years, Elias. Eleven years of being the straightest blade they had. And you spent it on us. On a lie written in a fine round hand."

"On one lie," Vane said. "Written carefully. Locked tight in the chase. It will hold."

"Nothing holds forever. You taught me that — no, you didn't teach me that. Your father's press taught you, and mine taught me. Everything spills eventually. Everything comes loose under pressure." Cyrus turned fully to face him then, and the last light off the water caught them, and Vane thought, with a grief so clean it was nearly joy, *I have loved this person since I was sixteen years old and I have never once said so properly.*

"Then it will hold long enough," Vane said. "Long enough for the army to withdraw. Long enough for the revolution to reach the capital, which it will — the dispatches say it plainly, whatever the proclamations say. Long enough."

"Long enough for what?"

"For me to do this." And he said goodbye.

Not the way he had left ten years ago — no clean cut, no merciful erasure, no slipping away before dawn with his apprenticeship folded in a sack. He said it out loud, standing on the shingle with the rotten ice grinding behind them, and he said all of it: that he had gone because he was afraid of how much he wanted to stay; that he had told himself silence was kindness because kindness was cheaper than courage; that he had carried the print shop in his hands for a decade — the smell of the ink, the sound of the treadle, the particular way Cyrus bit their lower lip when they were cutting a serif — and had called the carrying loyalty, and had known it was cowardice, and had worn the uniform anyway because a man can hide from almost anything except himself for only so long.

"I'm sorry," he said. "For the leaving. I owed you a goodbye and I paid it in absence, with interest, for ten years. This is the payment. Late. In the wrong coin. But it's honest, and I have very little honest left, so I'm spending it on you."

Cyrus stood very still. The river spoke behind them, loud with thaw.

"You're going back to them," Cyrus said at last. "After the report."

"I'm going back. If I vanish the same week the evidence vanishes, they'll look here next. Someone must be the careless captain who loses things. It may as well be the one who chose to lose them." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Besides — there are other presses south of here. Other towns with printers marked the way yours was marked. Reports get misfiled. Prisoners get transferred and escape en route. A man in my position can be usefully incompetent for years."

"That's treason."

"That's arithmetic," Vane said. "I learned my figures at a composing stone."

For a moment neither moved. Then Cyrus closed the distance between them — one step, no more, but it crossed the ten years as thoroughly as it crossed the gravel — and took his hand, ink-stained and cold, and held it the way you hold a forme before locking it: firmly, precisely, aware of everything that could spill.

"Come back someday," Cyrus said. "When there's a free press here. When the town has a name again. Come back and I'll set your name in type myself — twenty-four point, and cut the letters fresh, because I'll want them right."

"I'll hold you to it."

"Do. I keep excellent accounts." A breath of a laugh, wet at the edges. "Or I did. I burned my accounts."

They stood a moment longer, hand in hand, while the ice groaned and shifted behind them and the fast water ran north toward whatever country was being born. Then Cyrus let go, deliberately, the way a compositor lifts the last sort from the stick — cleanly, without fumbling, so that nothing falls.

"Goodbye, Elias," Cyrus said. "Properly, this time."

"Goodbye, Cyrus," said Vane. And this time he said the name like it was the whole truth, because it was.

He walked back up the bank without looking around, because looking around was a luxury for men with futures they trusted, and he had a report to deliver, a war to survive, and a promise to keep. Behind him the river broke a sheet of ice with a sound like a great door opening, and he did not turn, and he did not need to. Some departures are not endings. Some are simply the space between two lines of type — invisible, essential, holding everything apart so that everything can hold together.

Three days later, the army withdrew from Marrow's Bend. And in the dust of the empty barracks, on the desk the departing commander forgot to clear, Wren Eastwick would find a single sheet of paper in a fine round hand — unsigned, undated, containing nothing but the words *The matter is closed* — and would carry it home to the shop, where her sibling was already cutting new type, and where the first free edition was already taking shape.
