## 20. After the Requisition

Wren Eastwick counted the soldiers out of Marrow's Bend the way she had once counted coins: one by one, with a grim attention to what would not come back.

They went at first light, in that gray hour when the river still wore its fog like a borrowed coat and the town lay half-convinced the whole winter had been a fever. Wren stood in the doorway of the print shop with her shawl pulled tight and her breath ghosting before her, and she watched the column form up in the square — the same square where they had posted the gazette, where they had nailed the notices with the wax seals, where Tam had laughed too loudly with officers whose names she had never bothered to learn.

The boots were quieter now. That was the thing that struck her first, before the banners or the wagons or the thin gray line of men too young to have grown beards in a single winter. When the army had come, the square had rung like a bell. Now it only murmured, muffled by thaw and mud and the particular shame of men leaving a place they had failed to hold.

The revolution had reached the capital three days ago. The news had arrived not by newspaper — there had been no newspaper, not for weeks, not since the requisition swallowed the press and the ledger and every name in it — but by rumor, traveling upriver on the ice the way spring traveled, unevenly and all at once. The ministry had fallen. The old government had scattered like type from a kicked case. And the orders that had held Captain Vane's soldiers in Marrow's Bend dissolved somewhere between the capital and the coast, so that the men woke one morning to discover they were garrisoning nothing, guarding nothing, holding a town that no longer answered to anyone.

Wren watched them go and found, to her surprise, that she could not hate them individually. She hated the column. She hated the requisition order with its ribbons and seals. But the boy-soldier who dropped his musket strap and shifted it to the other shoulder, who glanced back once at the bakery as though he remembered warmth there — him she only pitied, and pity was a colder thing than hatred, and heavier to carry.

She counted them out. Four hundred and eleven, near enough. She had counted the town in, all those months ago, when the ledger still lived in its locked drawer and every family in Marrow's Bend owed the shop something — sixpence here, a cord of wood there, a christening notice paid in eggs. She had been raised on arithmetic. It was the Eastwick inheritance along with the ink: measure the world, weigh it honestly, know exactly what you have before you decide what you can lose.

What they had, this morning: a shop with its floorboards pried up and left gaping like a mouth missing teeth. A press scarred with bayonet points and soldiers' carved initials. An atlas with pages gone soft from handling. A father who could not walk to the corner without stopping twice to breathe. A sister-brother who had burned a book of names and stayed behind when the boat came. And a brother on the water somewhere north, weeping or not weeping, writing letters or not writing them.

And no name at all. That was the strange gift of it. The army had taken everything written down — the ledger, the records, the parish rolls carted off as evidence of sedition — and then the army itself had dissolved, and every document of Marrow's Bend now existed nowhere except in the heads of the people who had lived it. The town was officially nothing. Unrecorded. Nameless in any register that mattered.

Wren had thought, in the dark weeks, that erasure would feel like dying. It felt instead like being handed a blank page and a very sharp instrument.

* * *

Aldous was at the workbench when she came back inside, which he should not have been.

He had arranged himself there sometime in the night, evidently, propped on his stool with his hands folded over his cane, facing the empty type cases the way a man faces a hearth in winter. The lamplight caught the planes of his face and made them deeper, older, a face cut in relief the way he cut his letters — high parts and deep shadows, everything the tool had taken leaving a shape behind.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Wren said.

"I'm supposed to be dead," Aldous said mildly. "I've disappointed everyone. I might as well disappoint you too."

She wanted to laugh. What came out instead was closer to a sob, and she turned away to hide it, busying her hands with the kettle, with the fire, with anything that let her face the stove while her face did what it needed to do.

The cough started while her back was turned. She knew it by its rhythm now, had learned its whole vocabulary through the wall of her bedroom: the dry rattle of the ordinary mornings, the wet tearing sound of the bad ones. This one was bad. She stood with her hand on the kettle until it passed, because if she turned around he would wave her off, and if he waved her off she would strike him, and she could not afford to strike him on a morning like this one.

When it was done she brought the cup of tea and set it beside his hand, and she looked down at the bench and saw the cloth lying there, and the small dark spots on the cloth, rust-colored, already drying at the edges.

Blood. He saw her see it. He covered it with one hand, unhurried, the way he'd smooth a sheet of paper over a forme, and he smiled at her.

"Don't," he said.

"Don't *what.*"

"Don't look at me like I'm a debt coming due." His voice was roughed down by the coughing, gravel over paper. "I've been dying since before the soldiers came, Wren. They only hurried me along. I intend to be slow about it anyway, out of spite."

"You're smiling."

"I am." He unfolded the cloth, folded it again with the stain inside, tucked it beneath his palm. "Do you know why?"

"Because Cyrus didn't leave."

"No," he said. "Well. Yes. But also—" and he gestured with his chin at the shop around them, the wrecked cases, the gouged press, the pale squares on the walls where notices had hung and been torn away, "—because they took everything we wrote down, and none of it mattered. Do you understand? Forty years I kept accounts. Forty years I believed the record *was* the town — that if the ink said it, it was true, and if the ink burned, it was gone. And they burned it, or my children did, and the town is still here. Standing in my doorway. Arguing with me about tea."

"The town is mostly rubble."

"The town is *people,* girl, and people remember. Ask any of them what they owed the shop last Michaelmas and they'll tell you to the farthing, and some of them will even be honest about it." He lifted the tea, breathed the steam, did not drink. "The record was never the truth. It was only a shadow the truth cast. We kept the lamp. That was always the trade."

Wren sat down across from him, on the crate that had held Dutch type before the soldiers scattered it, and let herself be tired in front of him, which was a thing she permitted herself perhaps four times a year.

"Cyrus is at the river," she said, after a while.

"I know."

"They were there all night."

"I know that too."

"Doing what? They won't say. Vane came to the bank yesterday and they spoke for an hour and neither of them will say a word about it, and now Cyrus is at the water at dawn like—" She stopped.

Like someone deciding something. She didn't say it. Aldous nodded anyway, slow, the nod of a man who has already worked the problem further than his daughter has.

"There's steel in the back room," he said, which made no sense at all, and he smiled again, and this time Wren thought the smile looked less like peace and more like a man listening to a clock he alone could hear, counting down toward something he was glad of.

* * *

By nine the town had begun to put itself back together, and Wren went out into it, because that was what she did now; she had discovered during the occupation that she had a talent for showing up where things were broken and refusing to leave.

Marrow's Bend in the thaw smelled of mud and wood smoke and the green rot of the ice going out — a smell she had loved as a child and now heard as well as smelled, because the river groaned day and night, great cracking sighs from the middle of the current, the floes grinding and shouldering each other downstream. Half the wharf was gone, taken by the ice or the soldiers. The granary roof had caved under snow in February. There were shuttered windows on every street, and doors with boot-marks, and the church had been a stable for six weeks and would smell of it until midsummer.

But there were people in the streets. That was the miracle she kept re-finding, hour by hour, like a coin turning up in a swept room. Mrs. Halloway had her ovens lit and was selling bread at pre-war prices and daring anyone to comment. The Fenn boys were on the granary roof by midmorning with salvaged planks and a ferocity of hammering that sounded less like carpentry than argument. Old Cutter Marsh, who had spent the whole occupation invisible behind his fence, was out splitting rails in his yard, swinging harder than a man his age should swing, working grief out through his shoulders.

They talked to each other in the street the way families talk after a funeral — too fast, too loud, overlapping, hungry for the sound of unguarded voices. And everywhere, in every conversation Wren passed through, the same refrain: *do you remember what it said. Do you remember what we owed. Do you remember the date.*

The town was rebuilding from memory because memory was all the army had left it. The births, the debts, the deeds, the deaths — gone into wagons, gone into fires, gone into the general dissolution of a government that no longer existed to file anything. So Mrs. Halloway was keeping her accounts in chalk on the oven wall. Cutter Marsh had begun a list of the town's men on the inside of his barn door, in charcoal, starting with the ones who hadn't come home. People stopped Wren three times before noon to ask whether the shop would print again, whether the press still worked, whether — and here they lowered their voices, glancing up the street as though the column might double back — whether the Eastwicks meant to start the paper again.

"The real one," said the ferryman's widow, gripping Wren's wrist at the corner, her eyes fierce and wet. "Not their gazette. Ours. News that isn't bought. You'll do it? Your father will? Because if nobody prints the truth now, while everyone still remembers what a lie tasted like—"

"We'll do it," Wren said, and heard herself promise the whole town at once, and was not sorry.

It was past noon when the shouting started from the square — not alarm, but the other thing, laughter and halloos — and she went to find the cause and discovered a knot of children around the pump, and at the center of them, sitting on the mounting block like a returned pilgrim, Tam.

No. Not Tam. She knew that before her heart finished its stupid lurch. It was Josiah Crane, who had fled north in the fall, back now with a week of beard and a knapsack and news from the capital that he delivered standing up, arms wide, to half the town within the hour: the ministry fallen, the prisons opened, the old warrants voided, amnesty proclaimed for printers — *printers,* he said it twice, looking toward the shop — and a new assembly drafting articles that would make sedition impossible to charge, because there would be nothing to seditiously oppose.

"It's over," he kept saying, and the word went through the crowd like whiskey, and Wren drank it and felt it burn and thought: *no. It is beginning.*

That evening she cooked for four and served three, and nobody remarked on the arithmetic, because in the Eastwick house arithmetic was language. Cyrus came in late, damp to the knees, smelling of river fog, with a wrapped bundle under their arm that they set on the shelf above their cot and did not explain. Wren asked once, got silence, and did not ask again. In this family silence had always been a kind of speech; you learned to read it like a page.

Aldous ate little, but he ate at the table, upright, and told one story from the old days — the year the flood took the lower warehouse, the year he cut commemorative type on credit and nearly lost the shop — and laughed at his own ending, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough he mastered, barely, knuckles white on the table edge, waving off both his children with the same flat hand.

Later, when the house had settled, Wren heard the two of them in the back room, Cyrus and Aldous, low voices over the sound of metal on metal — a fine, small, patient sound, like rain beginning. She stood outside the door a long moment with her ear almost to the wood.

Her father's voice: *"No. From the angle. Let the punch do the loving. You're only guiding it."*

And Cyrus's voice, answering something she couldn't catch, and then Aldous again, softer, almost tender, the voice he used maybe once a decade:

*"Good. That's good. Again."*

Metal on metal. Rain beginning. Wren pressed her forehead against the cool doorframe and understood, all at once and completely, what the bundle on the shelf was, and what her father was doing with the time he had left, and why he had smiled at blood on the cloth.

He was teaching Cyrus to cut an alphabet. Every letter. Steel, from raw stock, by hand — the inheritance no army could requisition, no fire could burn entire, because it would live in Cyrus's hands now, and hands could not be carted off in wagons.

She went to her room and lay awake under the groan of the breaking ice, and did her sums one more time, the way she did every night. One father, spending down. One sibling, learning. One brother, north, silent. One town, nameless, waiting for a paper.

The ledger was ash. But Wren Eastwick, who had been raised to believe the record was the world, lay in the dark and felt the world begin to write itself anew — and in the morning, when she rose before anyone else and found the back room lamplit and Cyrus bent over the bench with a graver in their fist, cutting, stroke by patient stroke, the first letter of a brand-new alphabet, she picked up the broom and started sweeping the floor clear around them, and said only:

"Tell me what you need. I'll get it."

Cyrus lifted their head. Their eyes were red-rimmed and steady, and they looked at her — really looked, the way the town had finally learned to look — and said:

"A case. And a name to cut next."
