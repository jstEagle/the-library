## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The mule knew the path better than Casper did, which was a mercy, because by the time the tree line broke and the observatory revealed itself against the gray shoulder of the mountain, her hands had gone numb inside her gloves and her mind had run through the journey's arithmetic a hundred times without once arriving at a comfortable sum.

Three days from Innsbrück. Two nights in coaching inns where she had slept sitting upright in a chair, booted feet crossed at the ankle, hat brim pulled low, answering to *Herr Whitlock* in a voice kept flat and unremarkable as a coin. One letter of summons folded against her breast, bearing not her name but that of her late master — **A. Brandt, Clockmaker, Prague** — written in his hand before he died, offering his services to the Kessler Observatory. The director had replied to a dead man, and a dead man's apprentice had answered.

The observatory sat atop the ridge like a great iron beetle, its dome dulled with rime, its clock tower rising from the east wing in a slender finger of grey stone. Even at this distance she could hear it — or imagined she could — the labored tick of a mechanism dying by inches. A master clock that kept time for an entire institution: the transit instruments, the observation logs, the very seconds by which men measured the heavens. When such a clock failed, everything downstream of it failed too, quietly, and no one thought to blame the clock.

She dismounted where the path widened into a yard of frozen mud and led the mule the last hundred yards, partly out of caution and partly because she wanted to arrive slowly. Arrival was the dangerous hour. After arrival came introductions, handshakes, the scrutiny of strangers, and every stranger was a door she had spent nine years learning to keep locked.

*You are Casper Whitlock,* she reminded herself, adjusting the scarf over the collar that hid the line of her throat. *Apprentice to Anton Brandt. You have done this a dozen times. You will do it once more, quickly, and be gone.*

The plan was simple, and simplicity was its whole virtue: assess the clock, order any parts that could be ordered, effect what repairs could be effected within a fortnight, and descend the mountain before the first hard snows closed the seasonal road — the narrow stone bridge across the ravine being, according to the letters, the only approach passable by cart, and even then only until mid-October. She would work with her head down. She would eat alone when eating alone was possible. She would speak only of gears.

A woman opened the observatory's side door before she had finished knocking — a young woman, perhaps her own age, with flour on one wrist and an expression of careful, exhausted competence, as though she had been performing the duties of three people for so long that she had forgotten which of them she originally was.

"You're the clockmaker." It was not quite a question.

"Casper Whitlock," Casper said, and bowed the way men bowed, shallow and brisk. "Sent by Herr Brandt of Prague."

"Marta Halloran." The woman's eyes moved over her — the coat, the boots, the travel-stained jaw she had shaded with grease and soot each morning of the journey — and paused, just barely, at her eyes. Then the moment passed. "My father wrote to your master in the spring. We'd given up on a reply."

"He was away from his shop much of the summer," Casper said, and felt the small familiar shame of lying about the dead. "I manage his repairs now."

"Come in, then, before the light goes. Tobias will see to your mule." Marta raised her voice toward the dim interior. "Tobias! A horse for the gentleman!"

What answered was not a voice but a whistle, low and wandering, drifting down the stairwell like something that had gotten lost between floors. A moment later a man appeared — broad-shouldered, grey-bearded, somewhere past fifty, with a coil of rope over one shoulder and the unhurried air of someone who had never in his life been surprised by anything that happened above the snowline. He looked at the mule, then at Casper, and his gaze rested on her for exactly one heartbeat longer than courtesy required.

"Fine animal," he said. "Wrong saddle for her, though. Pinches at the shoulder. She'll be sweeter if you shift it back a hand's width." And he took the lead rope and walked off whistling, leaving Casper standing in the doorway with her ears ringing.

*He knows,* said a cold little voice. But that was absurd. He knew nothing; he had looked at a saddle, that was all. Men noticed tack. She was tired, and tiredness made the walls seem thinner than they were.

* * *

They took her up to the clock tower that same afternoon, because Marta Halloran was not a woman who believed in letting a hired man rest, and Casper was grateful for it. Work was safer than conversation. The tower stairs spiraled upward through cold stone, and at the top, behind a door banded in iron, the master clock waited for her like a patient in a fever ward.

It was a magnificent thing and it was dying. She saw it at once — the way one sees a face across a room and knows, before any detail resolves, that something is wrong. A regulator of Vienna manufacture, weight-driven, with a meridian-slip escapement she had read about but never touched, its pendulum suspended down a shaft into the tower's heart. The case was walnut and brass, and the brass had gone green at every joint, and the tick —

The tick stopped her where she stood.

It was not the sound of a healthy movement. It was the sound of a heart with a murmur: strong, strong, hesitation, strong. She set her ear against the case like a physician, eyes closed, and let the rhythm tell her its troubles. Escapement wear, certainly. Something wrong deeper down — a bent pivot, perhaps, or the beginning of a crack in one of the wheels. And the oil. God, the oil. Someone had greased it with tallow within the last year, probably in desperation, probably in winter, and the tallow had congealed into a glue that was strangling the train one tooth at a time.

"Well?" Marta stood in the doorway with a lamp. "Father says it loses four minutes a day. Some days six."

"Some days it's losing more than it admits to," Casper said. She opened the case and looked at the hands — fine blued-steel hands, elegant as calligraphy — and then at the dial, and felt the first cold thread of unease go through her that had nothing to do with gears. "How long has it been like this?"

"Two years." Marta's voice flattened. "Since my mother died. My father says he meant to write sooner. He says many things."

Casper knelt and began laying out her tools on a cloth with the ritual precision of her trade — each caliper and driver in its place, each in order of use. It steadied her, this liturgy. Hands busy, mouth shut, eyes on the work. That was how she had survived nine years in workshops and back rooms across half of Europe: useful enough to keep, quiet enough to forget. She would strip this movement down to its bare wheels, clean away two years of tallow and dust and grief, cut what new parts she could, and be down the mountain before anyone thought to wonder about the clockmaker with the steady hands and the careful voice.

"I'll need the tower to myself," she said. "Days, at least. Possibly a fortnight."

"A fortnight." Something flickered across Marta's face — relief, Casper thought, though relief about what she couldn't say. "That can be arranged. Supper is at seven. Father takes his meals in the study, so it's mostly me and Tobias, and now you."

*Mostly me and Tobias.* As if the household were a fire burning down to its embers, and a stranger's presence were fuel.

* * *

She found the discrepancy at dusk, and it should not have mattered. It did not concern her. She had come to mend a clock, not to audit an observatory.

But the ledgers were kept in the transit room just below the clock chamber — a long narrow room with a slit window opening on the western sky — and the door stood open, and she was carrying her tools past it when she saw the volumes stacked on the table, spines cracked, pages furred with ink corrections. Old habit made her look. A clockmaker reads any instrument left in front of her the way a preacher reads scripture, and observation ledgers recorded times, and times came from her clock.

The entries were beautiful. That was the first thing she noticed — the handwriting of a man who had once taken pride in his work, each transit of each star logged to the tenth of a second. The second thing she noticed was that certain stars appeared on nights when the sky, by her own memory of the almanacs, could not possibly have held them. And the third thing — the thing that made her stop in the doorway with her tool roll dangling from her fist — was that the times themselves ran ahead of the master clock by amounts too regular to be honest. Four minutes here. Six there. Exactly the error the clock was known to have, corrected in the ledger as if the observations had been made by a perfect instrument, when everyone in the building knew the instrument was broken.

Someone was writing down stars that had never been seen, at hours that had never occurred, and signing the pages *E. Halloran*.

Footsteps behind her. Tobias, come up with lamp oil, whistling his lost little tune. He followed her gaze to the ledgers, and then to her face, and something passed over his features — not accusation, but recognition, gentle and terrible, the look of a man seeing a truth he intended to keep.

"Bridge closes early this year," he said mildly, setting down the oil. "Snows are coming a month soon, they say. Mr. Brandt will want to plan for a longer stay."

He went past her into the dark of the stairwell, whistling, and Casper stood alone among the honest instruments and dishonest pages, and understood that her fortnight had died in a single afternoon — that the mountain had already begun quietly closing its hand around her, and that whatever she had come here to repair, she was now trapped inside the repairing of it.
