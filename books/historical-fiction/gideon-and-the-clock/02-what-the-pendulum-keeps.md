## 2. What the Pendulum Keeps

The clock tower smelled of iron filings and old pine resin, and Casper loved it the way one loves a room where no one has ever lied to you. She had been at the observatory nine days now, long enough for the shock of the closed bridge to settle into something like acceptance, and she had divided those days into hours the way her master had taught her: one for grief, two for work, and all the rest for pretending the first two did not exist.

The master clock was a monument to somebody's ambition — a regulator of the old Viennese school, weight-driven, with a deadbeat escapement that had once kept time to a half-second a day. Now it lost four minutes every night, and Dr. Halloran's observations were timestamped against it, which meant every star chart in the building was quietly, mathematically wrong.

She worked in the cage of gears with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her waistcoat unbuttoned against the heat of friction, and she tried not to think about how much she enjoyed it. Enjoyment was dangerous. Enjoyment made you slow to leave.

"You hold your tools like my mother held needles," Marta said from the doorway, arms crossed, a flour handprint on her dark skirt. "Sideways and stubborn."

"Your mother was a seamstress?"

"A cook. Which is what I am now, though nobody asked me to be." Marta came in anyway, drawn the way people always were to an open clock — the same pull that made children press their noses to jeweler's windows. "Papa says the mechanism is beyond saving. He says the mountain air has corroded something fundamental."

"Your father is wrong. The mountain air is fine. Someone let water into the case — see the bloom of rust here, on the crutch? A roof leak, years ago, never properly mended." Casper lifted the pendulum bob aside with two fingers. "Everything fundamental is only tired."

"Tired can be fixed?"

"Tired can be cleaned."

Marta laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if laughter were a language she'd stopped practicing. She sat down on the upturned crate that served Casper as a stool, and stayed, and asked questions: why the escapement needed oil so thin it ran like tears, why the hands were driven backward by the strike train when it misbehaved, why the pendulum was compensated with jars of mercury. And Casper answered, and found herself talking more than she had talked in two years, because Marta listened the way apprentices didn't — not to learn the trade, but to learn *her*.

* * *

The ledgers lived on a shelf in the transit room, bound in green cloth, one volume per month, and Casper began reading them at night partly from professional necessity and partly from a hunger she didn't want to name. They were beautiful things, Halloran's ledgers. His hand was precise without being fussy, each transit recorded with its sidereal time to the tenth of a second, each reduction shown in full.

That was what troubled her first — not errors but their absence. Nine hundred observations across three seasons, and not one rejected. Every instrument has bad nights. Fog, tremor, a tired eye, a cold that makes the eyelid water. Even the great observatories threw out a third of their plates. But these pages accepted everything, welcomed everything, as if the sky above the mountain had never once disagreed with Dr. Ambrose Halloran.

Then there were the timestamps.

Casper set two ledgers side by side under the lamp and checked them against her own repair log, because a clockmaker keeps records too, and hers were honest even when nothing else about her was. On the fourth of November, Halloran had timed the transit of a certain faint star in Cassiopeia at 9 hours 14 minutes 22 seconds sidereal. But she had spent that entire afternoon disassembling the great wheel, and the clock had been *stopped* — hands still, weights down, pendulum hanging dead in its case between 3:40 and well past ten, while she ate supper with the household and Tobias walked her back up through the dark.

A man cannot observe a star's passage across a wire with no clock running. Unless he observed it another night and wrote it down on this one. Unless the ledger was not a record of the sky but a composition.

She closed the volumes and stacked them neatly, exactly as she'd found them, and stood a long time in the cold of the transit room, listening to the wind test the shutters. The truth sat in her chest like a swallowed gear — too large to pass, too sharp to ignore.

* * *

*From the kitchen journal of Marta Halloran, undated, pencil:*

Tobias brought down a hind quarter today and I salted it wrong and he pretended not to notice, which is his whole character in one gesture. Papa took his supper in the study again. Mr. Whitlock ate with me and told me about a cathedral clock in some German town where the figures come out and joust at noon, and for a minute I wanted to see it so badly my chest hurt. It is strange how you can live with people for months and be lonely, and then someone new arrives who holds a screwdriver sideways and suddenly the house feels like a house again.

I think Papa is frightened of him. Of Mr. Whitlock, I mean. He watched him across the table last night like a man watching weather come over a ridge.

* * *

Tobias found her at the windlass on Thursday, hauling the counterweights back up after cleaning, and took the handle from her without asking, the way he took most things — firewood, buckets, awkward silences.

"You'll ruin your back," he said. "Or your reputation."

The words landed strangely. Casper kept her face still and counted turns. One, two, three. The ratchet clicked. Above them the tower drummed with wind.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Mm." He hauled another turn. He was a broad, weathered man somewhere past fifty, with a beard going to rust and hands like old rope, and he looked at the wall rather than at her when he spoke, which she understood, gradually, was his form of courtesy. "Third night you were here. Supper. You reached for the bread and I saw your hands. Then I heard you hum while you worked — that tune from the mill towns, the one the women sing at the looms. My mother sang it."

Casper said nothing. Her heart went hard and quick, like a train escaping its escapement.

"I've buried two wives and a brother," Tobias went on, mildly, "and I've learned there are doors people keep shut for reasons that are none of your business unless they make them your business. You keep yours shut. Fine. This house has enough broken things without me adding to them." He set the brake and finally looked at her, one brief, level look, blue eyes gone pale as winter sky. "But you should know the bridge keeper's dog knows more secrets than this mountain, and none of them ever got out. That's a skill. Worth more than fixing clocks, some winters."

He picked up his coat and left her alone with the weights, humming, and Casper stood in the ticking dark of the tower and felt something loosen in her that she had braced tight for two years. He knew. He knew, and he had simply... carried it, the way he carried everything, without dropping it or showing anyone.

She finished the haul alone, and she was smiling, and she distrusted the smile thoroughly.

* * *

The warmth arrived in small installments after that. Marta began appearing in the tower with excuses — tea, biscuits, a torn glove needing mending that she did herself while sitting on the crate, narrating the household's whole history through the needle's small stabs. How her mother had died of the lung fever eight years ago; how her father had gone north for a season and come home a stranger with a chair in an academy that had stopped writing to him; how the funding letters came twice a year now, thinner each time, and how Papa read them aloud in a voice like a man reciting his own sentence.

"He needs this next grant," Marta said, snapping a thread. "Not for the money itself. For the *being right*. He was passed over for the directorship in Prague, you know. Before I was born, even, he was already being passed over. This survey — the proper motion of the Cassiopeia stars — it's his last argument that they were wrong about him."

"And if the survey fails?"

"Then we sell," Marta said simply. "The instruments, the dome glass, the brass. There's a dealer in Graz who'd strip this place like a carcass. Tobias would find work. I'd find work. Papa—" she paused, threading her needle, "—I don't think Papa survives failing twice. So don't fix the clock *too* well, Mr. Whitlock. As long as it runs badly, he can blame the instrument."

She meant it as a joke. Casper laughed, because it was expected, and felt the swallowed gear turn in her chest.

* * *

*From the kitchen journal of Marta Halloran:*

Mr. Whitlock mended the good scissors today without being asked and oiled every hinge in the kitchen door and then blushed when I thanked him, actually blushed, like thanks was a thing he'd never learned to hold. I said, "You're very kind for a clockmaker," and he said, "I'm not kind, I'm thorough," which I am deciding is either modesty or armor.

There is flour on the ledger shelf in the transit room that isn't mine. Small things moved. Papa has been up there at night with candles, and tonight I heard him arguing with someone and opened the door on an empty room. I am writing this down because writing things down is how you keep them from turning into shapes in the dark.

* * *

On the last evening of the month, Casper climbed to the tower with her lamp and her repair log and sat cross-legged before the open case, and let herself admit what she had known since the second ledger. The discrepancies weren't sloppiness. Sloppiness scatters; these were placed. Observations written on nights the sky could not have been seen, timestamps matched to a clock that hung dead in its case, dates chosen to fill gaps where clouds had ruled for a week. Someone had built a sky that behaved.

And the survey's conclusions — the proper motions Halloran reported to the academy, the numbers that would decide whether this mountain kept its instruments and its people — rested on those pages like a house on rotting joists.

If she wrote what she knew to the academy when the bridge opened, the funding might survive on other grounds, or might not; the scandal would not. Marta would lose the house that had just begun to feel like a house. Tobias would lose the ridge he scanned every dusk. And Halloran —

Halloran would lose the only argument he had left, and take a stranger down with him if he could, because he had already looked at her across the supper table with that measuring expression, already suspected, perhaps, that the young clockmaker was carrying secrets of his own that would burn just as readily.

Casper trimmed the lamp, took up her file, and went back to the escapement, because the escapement at least told the truth when you asked it correctly. But her hands were slower than usual, and the pendulum swung behind its glass like a slow patient heart, keeping the only kind of time that never lied — and she understood, with a cold settling certainty, that tomorrow she would have to speak to Dr. Halloran alone.

The storm that had been building all week over the western ridges chose that moment to arrive.
