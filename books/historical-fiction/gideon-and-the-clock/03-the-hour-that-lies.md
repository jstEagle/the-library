## 3. The Hour That Lies

The discrepancy had been sitting in the ledgers all along, patient as dust.

Casper found it on the ninth of November, in the small hours when the observatory slept and only she and the master clock kept vigil. She had come down from the tower with grease to her elbows, intending nothing more than a cup of Marta's terrible chicory coffee, and there on the reading table lay Dr. Halloran's observation journal — left open, left carelessly, as though he no longer cared who saw it. Or perhaps, some quieter voice suggested, as though part of him wanted it seen.

She should not have looked. She looked.

The entry was dated the third of October. *Transit of Regulus observed at 04:12; position consistent with ephemeris.* But Casper had been at the observatory since the twenty-eighth of September, and she remembered that night — remembered it because the fog had come down the mountain like a tide and swallowed everything above the tree line. No one had observed anything. She had stood in the dome doorway herself, watching Tobias shake his head slowly in the dark.

She turned back through the pages. There were others. Nights of claimed clarity during weeks she knew had been clouded. Measurements too perfect, agreeing with the predicted positions to fractions of a second — a precision no instrument on this mountain could deliver through the atmosphere they actually had. The great refractor was old. Its clock drive wandered. Halloran himself complained of it daily over dinner, complaining loudly enough that no one would ever suspect the numbers came from anywhere but the eyepiece.

Casper closed the journal with hands that did not feel like her own.

She knew what it meant before she let herself name it. The funding letters that arrived monthly by the last mule train — the ones Halloran read alone in his study, his jaw tight. The new assistant he kept promising he could hire if the Institute renewed its grant. The roof repairs forever deferred because the money went instead to instruments, to proof, to the fiction of a man still at the height of his powers. He was manufacturing discoveries the way she manufactured a mustache of soot and beeswax: carefully, nightly, out of terror of who you'd be without the lie.

The thought made her sick, because it made her kind.

* * *

She waited three days for courage, which was two days longer than she'd waited for anything in her life. On the third evening, Marta took her father's supper tray up to the study, and Casper followed the smell of lamp oil and old paper down the corridor before she could turn back.

Dr. Halloran sat behind a desk buried under star charts, his spectacles pushed up into thinning white hair. He looked smaller than he did at dinner. Older. When he saw her, something flickered across his face — not surprise. Recognition, perhaps, of what her presence in this room meant.

"Miss— " He stopped. Corrected himself. "Whitlock."

"I need to speak with you. Privately." Marta was still somewhere in the house; the word *privately* tasted like a betrayal already.

He set down his pen. "You've been in my journals."

It wasn't a question. Casper's throat went dry. "The October entries. The third, the eleventh, the nineteenth. I was here those nights, Doctor. It was fog. It was fog for eleven days straight."

For a long moment he said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, the master clock ticked its wounded, dragging tick — the reason she'd been sent here at all, the heartbeat of the building that couldn't keep honest time any more than its master could.

"You're an apprentice," he said finally. "A boy barely out of his teens, sent because my old friend Aldous couldn't come himself and didn't trust anyone else with my clock. What would you know of what it takes to keep an observatory alive?"

"I know what it takes to keep a lie alive," Casper said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I've lived inside one for six years."

His eyes narrowed. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," she said. "But you will, if I have to make you understand. The Institute sends an inspector in the spring — you said so yourself at supper, you said it laughing. If he checks your logs against the weather records from the valley station, against the almanacs, against anything at all—"

"He won't." Halloran rose, and for a moment he was tall again, the celebrated astronomer whose portrait had hung in lecture halls when Casper was a child memorizing constellations she wasn't allowed to study. "Because you are not going to tell him. You are not going to tell anyone."

"And why is that?"

He smiled, and it was the saddest thing she had ever seen on a living face. "Because, Master Whitlock, if you go to the Institute with stories about falsified observations, the first question they will ask is why an apprentice clockmaker was reading private journals. And then someone will want references. Papers. A history. And I wonder—" he stepped closer, gently, terribly, "—what such an inquiry might find about the late Mr. Aldous Whitlock's apprentice. About where he studied. About whether anyone in Zürich or London or wherever you claim to have trained remembers a young man with your face."

The room tilted. Casper gripped the edge of the desk.

"I would never," she whispered.

"No," he agreed softly. "People like us never would. We simply survive. That is the whole of our creed, isn't it? Survive, and tell ourselves the cost was necessary." He turned away, back to his charts, dismissing her. "Go and fix my clock, boy. It's the only honest work on this mountain."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She lay in the narrow cot beneath the tower stairs and listened to the wind rise off the glacier, and hated him, and understood him, and hated herself most of all for the understanding.

Near midnight the wind stopped being weather and became a creature. It came screaming down the col with a sound like tearing canvas, and then came the rain — horizontal, glacial, driven hard enough to sting through wool. Casper was out of bed and pulling on her boots before she'd fully decided to move.

The crash, when it came, shook the whole east wing.

She found them in the corridor — Marta in a nightgown with a shawl clutched at her throat, Tobias already hauling the storm lanterns, Dr. Halloran barefoot and pale. Water was sheeting down the stairwell from above. Through the dome access door, flung open to chaos, Casper could see it: a section of the old roof, rotted where the deferred repairs had never come, had peeled back like a lid. Rain poured directly onto the great refractor's housing.

"The tarpaulins," Tobias shouted over the wind. "In the cellar, under the east stair!"

What followed was the longest night of Casper's life, and she had once spent one hiding in a hay barn while men with lanterns searched for her. They formed a chain up the dome stairs — Tobias passing, Marta receiving, Casper climbing onto the scaffolding itself with nails held between her teeth, hammering canvas over the breach while the wind tried to peel her off the mountain. Once a gust tore a corner loose and the whole sheet billowed like a sail, and Halloran — sixty years old, barefoot, soaked through in seconds — threw his whole weight against it and held it until she got the nails home.

They worked until the sky went grey. They worked without speaking, because speaking was impossible and also because nothing needed saying. When it was done, when the instruments were dry and the water mopped and the four of them stood dripping in the kitchen while Marta put the kettle on with shaking hands, Casper caught Halloran looking at her across the steam.

He had seen her up there. Seen her climb, and hold, and refuse to let go.

*Later*, his eyes said. *We will speak again.*

And Casper, wringing rainwater from her hair, thought: *Yes. Because now I know something else about you, Doctor. Now I know what you'll do to protect this place.*

The storm had torn open more than the roof. Whatever happened next — confession or ruin, truth or the long continuation of the lie — would be decided between the two of them, in the space the night had opened. And outside, the bridge was still months from reopening, and the clock in the tower still struck every hour a little wrong, and Marta was pressing a cup of chicory into her hands as though Casper belonged here, as though she had always belonged here.

Halloran cleared his throat. "Master Whitlock," he said quietly. "A word. In my study. Whenever you're ready."

The kettle screamed.
