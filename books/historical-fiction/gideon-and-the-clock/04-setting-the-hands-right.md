## 4. Setting the Hands Right

The study smelled of lamp oil, cold tea, and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled too often by too few hands. Halloran had lit every candle on the desk, as though he meant to conduct an interrogation rather than a conversation, and the light pooled over a spread of star charts that Casper did not need to examine to know what they were. She had seen them already — in the tower, in the ledger room, in the margins of her own sleep.

"Sit," he said. Then, catching himself: "Please."

She sat. The chair was leather gone soft with age, and it received her the way old furniture receives everyone, without judgment. Across the desk, Halloran looked smaller than he had in autumn. The storm had taken something from him along with the roof slates — or perhaps it had only finished taking what the winter had been loosening all along. His hands, she noticed, were not quite steady, and they were folded on the falsified charts like a man hiding a wound he no longer had the strength to conceal.

"You've known for some time," he said.

"Since December." Casper kept her voice level. "The declination figures in the October ledger. You'd corrected them twice, once in ink and once over the ink. A clockmaker learns to notice when someone has been adjusting a mechanism to say what they want it to say."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face and died there. "Yes. I suppose you would." He drew a breath that seemed to cost him. "Master Whitlock — or should I say—"

"Casper," she said quietly. "Casper Whitlock. My father's name. I only borrowed it."

The candles guttered in a draft from under the door, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Outside, the wind was working at the patched roof, but the patch held; Tobias's tarps and Marta's borrowed hands had done their work through the night, and the observatory stood. It would stand, Casper thought, if the people inside it chose to hold each other up. That was the whole of the mechanism, really. Everything else was escapement.

"I could unmake you with a letter," Halloran said. It was not a threat this time, though it had been one before. It sounded more like a man testing whether a bridge would bear his weight. "One letter to your guild, to the family who sent you. One sentence about who you are, and you would never hold a tool again."

"Yes," Casper said. "You could."

"And yet you sit there."

"And yet I sit here." She leaned forward, and laid her hand — bare now, she had stopped binding them weeks ago inside the tower where only Tobias watched and said nothing — flat upon the falsified charts. "Because I know what it costs to build a life out of a lie, Dr. Halloran. I know how much daily labor it takes. How brave it makes you feel and how exhausted, both at once. And I know this: my lie hurts no one. Yours is eating you alive, and it will eat your daughter next, because when the funding comes due and the truth comes out — as truth does, it is patient as a pendulum — they will not blame you alone. They will blame everyone who kept your house. Everyone who loved you."

Halloran closed his eyes.

"There is a version of this," Casper went on, softly, "where you write the corrections yourself. Where you publish them under your own name, before anyone forces them from you. Where you say: I erred, here is the true record, and here is what it means. It will cost you the funding. It may cost you the observatory. But it will not cost you Marta, and it will not cost you yourself. There is dignity in confession that ruin can never give you. Ruin takes everything and leaves you nothing to stand on. Confession takes almost everything and leaves you your name — your real one."

"You speak of names," Halloran said bitterly, "as though yours were intact."

"My name is intact," Casper said. "It has simply never been permitted to exist in daylight. That is a different wound, sir, and I have learned to live beside it. But you still have a choice I was never offered. Take it."

He was silent for so long that she counted the study clock — a small carriage watch ticking on the shelf behind him, faithful as anything small and overlooked always is. Sixty ticks. A hundred and twenty. When he finally opened his eyes, they were wet, and he did not wipe them, and somehow that undid her more than any argument could have.

"When I was young," he said, "I charted a variable star no one believed in. Forty nights of observation. They laughed me out of two journals. This place exists because I swore I would never be laughed at again." He touched the edge of the false chart, the way one touches a scar. "And so I built a fortress of being right, and forgot that being right was never the point. Seeing was the point. God help me, seeing was the entire point."

"Then see this clearly," Casper said. "And write it down."

* * *

He wrote through the night.

Casper left him at some small hour and found Tobias on the gallery landing, mending a shutter hinge by lantern light, though the shutter did not truly need mending and the hour did not truly call for work. He glanced up at her, and his eyes did their usual quiet arithmetic — coat, boots, hands, the truth of her — and dismissed it all with the same gentle indifference he had shown since November.

"He'll do it, then," Tobias said. Not a question. Tobias always knew everything first and told no one.

"If he holds to it."

"Men like him hold to things," Tobias said, turning the screw. "It's letting go they can't manage. Once he's written it down, it becomes another thing he holds." He tested the hinge, satisfied himself, and looked at her sidelong. "You'll be going when the bridge opens."

It was not a question either, which was fortunate, because Casper did not have an answer.

* * *

The last days of the repair passed in a strangeness of peace. Halloran emerged from his study pale and hollowed and sat at breakfast with his corrections manuscript stacked beside his plate like medicine he had resolved to swallow. Marta refilled his coffee without comment, but Casper saw her hand tremble slightly, and saw Halloran see it, and saw something pass between father and daughter that had not passed between them in years — an acknowledgment, unspoken, that the house was about to be set right even if it shook apart in the process.

Casper worked on the master clock.

The great mechanism lay open around her like a patient after surgery, every wheel cleaned, every pivot burnished, the escapement re-cut to a tolerance she had never attempted before and would not have attempted for any other clock. She had come to know this machine the way one comes to know a difficult person: its moods, its stubbornnesses, the one gear tooth worn just enough to steal a second every hour. She replaced it with a wheel cut new, from brass she had ordered in summer and carried over the bridge herself, not knowing then what she was carrying it toward.

On the third morning of May, with the snow gone from the lower slopes and meltwater loud in every gully, she set the hands to the true solar time she had calculated against Halloran's honest meridian observations — his real ones, the ones from before the rot, which he had dug out of his archives and given her without being asked.

She let the weights fall. The escapement took up its rhythm. Tick. Tick. Tick.

At noon, by the sun itself, the master clock struck twelve, and the twelve strokes fell clean and evenly spaced across the valley, and Marta, who had come up to the tower uninvited and unannounced, began quite without warning to laugh and cry at once.

"It hasn't done that," she said, "since Mother was alive."

* * *

The bridge reopened on a blue, rinsed morning, and the messenger came up the trail with the season's first mail: letters, journals, a catalogue of lenses Halloran paged through with careful, deliberate attention, ordering instruments his corrected charts might actually justify.

Casper stood at the south parapet with her bag packed, because a packed bag is a thing one owes oneself, whatever one intends. Below, the rope-and-plank span swayed gently over the gorge, ready to carry her back down into the world where Casper Whitlock did not exist — where there was only Master Whitlock, dead man's name, borrowed coat, a career made of other people's clocks, repaired and departed from, always departed from.

Tobias appeared beside her, saying nothing, which was his way of saying a great deal.

"Halloran's corrections go to the printer Thursday," he observed. "Marta's staying. Someone has to keep the kitchen from burning down." He looked out at the gorge. "Clock'll need winding. Every eight days, regular. Man's hands aren't what they were."

"No," Casper agreed. "They aren't."

They stood together in the wind. Far below, the river ran bright with snowmelt, going somewhere, indifferent, and Casper found she felt nothing like it at all. She felt rooted. She felt, absurdly, like a weight driven deep into good bedrock, holding something true.

She picked up her bag.

Then she set it down again, turned her back on the bridge, and started up the path toward the tower, where the clock would want winding in six days, and every eight days after that, regular, for as long as the stars kept their appointments and the mountain kept its people.

Behind her, without looking, she heard Tobias smile.
