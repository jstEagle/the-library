## 1. The Weight of Ordinary Paper

The capital was leaving itself behind in pieces.

Ivo Kavanagh watched it happen from the steps of the Ministry of Rivers and Boundaries, a building that had never once in his career required him to think about either. All morning the evacuation had unspooled across Vessel Square: clerks with armfuls of ledgers they were not permitted to burn, ministers' wives in fur coats over nightclothes, a colonel weeping quietly into his gloves as though grief were a duty he'd rehearsed. Motorcars idled at the curb, their tanks hoarded full while the queues outside the bakeries stretched around two corners. The government was fleeing upriver toward Gellbruck and beyond it the border, and it was doing so with the particular dignity of people who had decided, sometime around noon, that history was something happening to other people.

Ivo knew the feeling. He shared it. He had, in fact, perfected it.

He stood on the third step from the bottom—third step, always; it put his eyes level with most men's chins without making him look like he was waiting for anything—and let the crowd flow past him. This was the whole of his craft. A courier who ran was remembered. A courier who hid was hunted. But a courier who stood still on a cold afternoon in a grey coat with a parcel-carrier's satchel and an expression of mild professional patience became, within ninety seconds, part of the architecture. Men's eyes slid off him the way rain slid off slate. He had crossed six borders in eleven years wearing this exact expression, and the sum total of what any border guard could later recall about him was that he had been *some fellow*, tallish perhaps, or not, with a coat, or a bag of some kind. Once, in Ostrava, a customs officer had described him to another officer standing directly beside him, and gotten everything wrong but the boredom.

That was the trick, if you wanted to call it a trick: to be so ordinary that even your ordinariness went unnoticed.

"Ivo Kavanagh."

He did not turn immediately. That too was craft—you never gave them the quick turn, the guilty pivot. He finished watching a staff car grind its wheels on the ice-slicked cobbles, then looked around with the unhurried dullness of a man being summoned by someone whose name he would need a moment to place.

The caller was a woman he had seen twice before and had made a point of forgetting both times. She wore the uniform of the Second Chancery under a coat worth more than Ivo earned in a year, and she had the look of someone who had been awake for three days and intended to make that everyone else's problem. Her name, as far as Ivo allowed himself to know it, was Deputy Chancellor Vess. Which meant, presumably, that she was some cousin or collateral branch of the President-for-Life-Who-Was-No-Longer-President, because in Vessany the rot always started with the family names.

"You're late," she said.

"The square is crowded," Ivo said. It wasn't, anymore. Half the city had already gone. "And I don't believe we have an appointment."

"We do now." She turned and walked back toward the ministry doors without checking whether he followed, which was the mistake most people made with couriers: they assumed obedience came with the trade. Ivo counted to four, glanced once down the emptying avenue as though he might prefer to keep walking, and then followed her, because the alternative was to admit that curiosity had a hold of him, and he preferred never to admit anything in public.

Inside, the Ministry of Rivers and Boundaries was dying the way institutions die—not with fire but with absence. Desks stripped. Drawers hanging open like mouths. A portrait of the Chancellor already taken down and leaning against a wall, face to the plaster, as though the building itself had decided to be embarrassed. Somewhere above them a typewriter was going, one last clerk hammering out God knew what to God knew where, the sound carrying through the stairwell like a man digging.

Vess led him to a room on the second floor that still had furniture in it. A table. Two chairs. A single candle burning beside a lamp that no longer worked because the electricity had left before the government did. On the table lay a document case of oiled canvas, sealed with lead and ribbon, the kind Ivo had carried a hundred times, and beside it sat a man Ivo had seen on banknotes and newsreels and had never expected to see at a distance of four feet.

Minister Aldous Mirei.

He was smaller than his money. That was Ivo's first thought, unworthy and immediate. The Minister of Boundaries—the architect of the eastern settlements, the negotiator of treaties whose names schoolchildren recited—sat at a plain table in a rumpled coat, and his hands, resting flat on the wood on either side of the case, were trembling very slightly, the way an old man's hands tremble when the coffee has worn off. He was perhaps fifty-five. He looked older by the light of the candle, which threw his shadow huge against the wall behind him, so that the shabby little man and the towering silhouette seemed to be two different people occupying the same chair.

"Kavanagh," the Minister said. His voice was better than the rest of him—measured, warm at the edges, the voice of a man who had talked parliaments into things. "Thank you for coming."

"You didn't leave me much choice," Ivo said. "Deputy Chancellor Vess says I'm late."

"She's been saying that to everyone all week." A flicker of a smile, there and gone, like a match struck in wind. "Sit down, please."

Ivo did not sit. Sitting was for men who had been invited to stay.

The Minister studied him for a moment, and something in the study bothered Ivo—not appraisal, which he was used to, but recognition. As if Mirei had been told something about him beforehand, something more than a name and a route.

"You come highly recommended," the Minister said, confirming it. "By people who say you are invisible. Their word, not mine." He touched the document case once, lightly, the way a man touches a wound to check whether it still hurts. "Do you know what is happening outside?"

"The Republic is evacuating," Ivo said. "Or abandoning. The terms get interchangeable by Thursday."

Something moved behind the Minister's eyes—pain, or amusement, or both—and Vess shifted by the door like a woman restraining herself from editing the conversation.

"What is happening," Mirei said quietly, "is that my government has sold this country, piece by piece, for eleven years, and tonight the last of the receipts leaves the capital in the hands of whoever can be trusted to carry it." He pushed the case an inch toward Ivo. "The eastern corridors. The river tolls. The garrisons that were ordered not to fire. It is all written here, in cipher, with the signatures attached. When the loyalist armies take the capital next month, this is the only proof of how it happened—and who made it happen."

Ivo looked at the case. Oiled canvas. Lead seal. Perhaps three pounds in weight, including the strap. He had carried love letters that weighed more. He had carried munitions manifests that decided which villages burned.

"And when I deliver it?" he asked. "To whom?"

"Gellbruck first. There is a safehouse—Kessler's Chandlery, on the fish quay. From there, north. You'll receive routing at each stage." Vess spoke now, crisp, reciting. "You will be a grain factor for Havel & Sons, out of Drenthe. Papers are in the side pocket. Three checkpoints between here and the ferry crossing. Routine."

*Routine.* The word settled over Ivo like familiar clothing. Grain factor. Papers in the side pocket. He had been a grain factor for Havel & Sons twice before, out of two different cities, and neither time had anyone looked at him longer than a sentence. He felt the old, cold comfort of it settle into place—the comfort of being nobody, of carrying somebody else's catastrophe across a border and walking away from it clean. Eleven years, and he had never once been inside the story. He delivered the matches and declined to watch the fire.

"There is one more thing," the Minister said.

Ivo waited.

Mirei reached into his coat—Ivo noted, without deciding to, that the coat was fine wool, badly worn at the cuffs, a rich man's garment that had stopped being cared for—and laid a small object on the table beside the case. Not money. Not a weapon. A compass, brass-cased, the glass scratched nearly white with use.

"For my daughter," the Minister said.

The room got very slightly smaller.

"I don't carry children," Ivo said. "I don't carry personal items. I carry paper, and only what's on the manifest."

"She won't be with you in the capital. She may already be on her way north—there are arrangements. But if—" Mirei stopped. For the first time the voice failed him, the parliamentary warmth guttering like the candle. He began again, slower. "If she reaches you, if she is given to you, you will carry her. That is all. Carry her the way you carry that case: carefully, and without asking what she weighs."

"Minister," Vess said softly, a warning.

"I know." He waved her off. He looked at Ivo, and up close his eyes were bloodshot in a way that suggested he had not slept since some specific and datable hour. "I am asking you to do nothing unusual, Kavanagh. Only to remember that ordinary things—a ledger, a compass, a child—are heavier than they look. Will you take it?"

Ivo looked at the compass. Brass. Scratched glass. The kind sold at any market stall in the capital for the price of two loaves of bread, and here it sat on a minister's table as though it were a treaty instrument.

"I'll take the case," Ivo said. "The rest isn't mine."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Aldous Mirei nodded, slowly, as if Ivo had confirmed something about the world he had already suspected, and Ivo felt that nod land somewhere below his professional indifference, in a place he did not permit visitors. He ignored it. He picked up the case, felt its three pounds settle against his hip, and recited the route back to Vess—Gellbruck, Kessler's Chandlery, the fish quay—perfectly, flatly, the way a man proves he has already forgotten everything else.

"Good luck," the Minister said to his back.

"No such thing," Ivo said, and went out.

* * *

The streets north of the square were emptier than any street in the capital had a right to be at dusk. Snow had begun to fall, dry and small, ticking against the awnings. Ivo walked at the pace of a man going home to supper, because that pace was invisible, and he took inventory of himself the way he always did after a handoff: papers, side pocket; seal intact; cover story warm; hands steady. Everything accounted for. Everything ordinary.

Three pounds of paper. A route he could recite drunk. Checkpoints that were routine.

At the corner of the Fishmarket a boy was selling the last edition of the evening paper off a barrow, shouting the headline to an audience of almost no one: LOYALISTS FORTY MILES OUT. Ivo bought one without reading it—it gave his hands something ordinary to hold—and walked on, and behind him the boy's voice chased him down the emptying street, calling the fall of a republic at half a kreuzer a copy.

Forty miles. Three checkpoints. One river town called Gellbruck.

Ivo turned up his collar against the snow and thought, with the quiet arrogance that had kept him alive for eleven years, that none of it would touch him. He would pass the mill, cross the bridge, board the ferry, deliver the paper, and vanish. History could do whatever it liked with Vessany; he was only the man who carried its luggage.

He did not yet know that the luggage was already waiting for him—that in two days, in the dark, on the doorstep of Kessler's Chandlery, someone would knock.
