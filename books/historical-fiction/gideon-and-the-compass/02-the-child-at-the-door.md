## 2. The Child at the Door

Gellbruck in winter smelled of wet wool and coal smoke and the mineral breath of the frozen river. Ivo Kavanagh had been in the safehouse four days, which was three days longer than any courier should stay anywhere, and he had spent them doing what he did best: being no one.

The house was a narrow tall thing on Tanner's Lane, wedged between a ropewalk and a shuttered dyeworks, with a view of nothing but a drainage ditch that froze in gray teeth down its bank. It had been stocked years ago by someone with a fondness for tinned sardines and a distrust of curtains, so there were no curtains, and Ivo had nailed up a blanket over the street-facing window himself, leaving the river-facing window bare because no one looked at a river.

He had established his rhythms the way he always did. Bread from the bakery on the quay at seven, bought by a man with a limp he did not actually have — the limp was theater, a small falseness of gait that made the eye slide off him the way water slides off grease. Ink purchased he didn't need. A newspaper read slowly in public and never finished. He had learned long ago that people remember what you do to be interesting, and forget everything you do out of boredom. So he was boring. He was magnificently, professionally boring. He was a grain factor named Aldous Renn with bad lungs and no family, and by the fourth day even the woman at the bakery had stopped trying to guess where he was from.

The ledger sat where it had sat since the capital: inside the false bottom of his traveling case, beneath a layer of shirts that smelled of someone else's soap. He did not look at it. Looking at a thing too often was how you grew attached to it, and attachment was how couriers died. You carried, you delivered, you forgot. That was the whole liturgy. Eleven years he had kept it without deviation, through two wars and one revolution already half-finished, and he had come to think of his own unremarkability as a kind of craft, practiced and perfected, like a joiner's dovetails.

The government was dying behind him, forty miles downriver, and he felt about it the way he felt about weather in another province.

* * *

The knock came at eleven minutes past two in the morning.

Ivo knew the time because he was awake, which was itself routine; he slept in slices, never more than ninety minutes, another discipline. He lay in the cold bed above the kitchen and listened.

Not a fist. Not the flat official hammering of soldiers or the guilty urgency of the compromised. A small knock. Knuckles once, twice, then nothing, then again — the knock of someone who had been told to knock but not loudly, someone afraid of the sound of their own hand.

He took the knife from under the mattress and went down the stairs on the outsides of his feet, the boards that didn't speak.

The night through the peephole was the blue-black of deep river winter, snow coming down in that slow indifferent way it does when it intends to continue for days. On his doorstep stood a figure the size of a coat rack, wearing a coat clearly meant for someone much larger, sleeves rolled in thick cuffs, hood thrown back because whoever wore it wanted to be seen — wanted *him* to see.

A child. A girl, perhaps seven, maybe eight, small for it or large for it, he couldn't have said. Her face was the color of old candle wax except where the cold had slapped red into her cheeks, and her lips had gone the dusky blue of a bruise healing. She stood with her feet together, perfectly square to the door, like a soldier at inspection or a child who has been told precisely how to stand and is holding to the instruction because instruction is all she has left.

She was not shivering. That was the thing that undid something in him, later, when he let himself think of it — she was past shivering, and did not seem to know it was a thing to fear.

In her mittened hand she held out an envelope.

Ivo did what he had trained himself to do in eleven years of doors and thresholds: he assessed the street. Empty. Snow filling the wheel ruts of Tanner's Lane without prejudice. The ropewalk dark, the dyeworks darker. If this was a trap it was an elegant one, because no one sent a child as the hammer; they sent children as the bait, and the trap came after.

He opened the door on the chain.

"Are you the man?" the girl said. Her voice was hoarse, scraped thin, the voice of someone who had been crying earlier or breathing cold air for hours, or both.

"No," Ivo said.

She considered this with a gravity that did not belong to seven years old. Then she held the envelope higher, insistently, the way you'd hold something out to a horse.

"The note says you are."

* * *

He brought her in, of course. He would replay that decision for years — the moment the chain came off — but in the doorway, in the wind pushing snow past his ankles into the hall, there was no decision at all, only arithmetic done too fast to examine. A child on a doorstep in February was a corpse by morning, and whatever else Ivo Kavanagh was, he had never been a man who stepped over corpses. Not literally. That line he had kept.

He put her in the kitchen chair nearest the stove, fed the stove until the iron ticked and breathed, wrapped her in the good blanket from the bed and the mediocre blanket from his own shoulders. She watched everything he did with a stillness that unsettled him more than screaming would have. Children cried, flailed, asked questions. This one rationed herself like a garrison under siege.

He set milk warming in a pan. Only when steam began to lift off it did she move, her hands emerging from the blanket cocoon to take the cup with both mittens still on, and even then she waited, watching him, until he nodded, before she drank.

While she drank he opened the envelope.

Cheap paper, the kind sold in any stationer's in Gellbruck. One line, in ink gone slightly feathered where wet gloves had touched it:

*Forget the face. She was never here. Burn this.*

No signature. But the handwriting — Ivo held it to the lamplight and felt the first cold thread of recognition pull through him, though he refused it, refused it hard, the way you refuse a word that's on the tip of your tongue because you are not ready for what it will mean. He folded the note instead and moved to the stove, and only when his hand was already extending it toward the coals did he stop himself.

Burn first, read second. Old rule. He'd broken it in the time it took to recognize a slant of lettering.

He turned back to the girl.

"What's your name?"

She looked at him over the rim of the cup. Her eyes were brown, ordinary brown, and yet they held his with a steadiness that made him want to look away first, and he realized she was memorizing *him*, cataloguing him the way he catalogued checkpoint officers, and where in God's name had a seven-year-old learned to do that.

"Mirei," she said.

Just the name. No surname offered, and something in the way she withheld it — deliberate, guarded, a door of her own — told him she had been coached. Told him, further, that the coaching had worked.

"Who brought you here, Mirei?"

"A man." A pause while she decided how much truth the mission required. "I walked some by myself."

"In this? How far?"

She shrugged, a small movement inside the mountain of blanket. "From the mill road."

The mill road. Four miles, along the river, in the dark, in a storm, alone, at seven. He thought of the constable's station two streets over, the warm lamp behind its frosted window, Constable Brakel whom he'd passed twice on the quay, a broad tired man with a mustache full of pipe ash. There was a procedure for found children. It was a good procedure, an ordinary procedure, the whole weight of ordinary life arranged to catch exactly this: a lost girl goes to the constable, the constable makes inquiries, the inquiries produce an aunt, a governess, a frightened household in the capital, and Ivo Kavanagh returns to his sardines and his boredom and his nameless mission, unencumbered.

He was already composing the story he'd give Brakel — found her near the ropewalk, no idea whose she was, just did the decent thing — when Mirei set down her cup and said, "You can't."

It wasn't a plea. It was a statement of fact, delivered flatly, the way you'd tell a man the stove was hot.

"You can't tell anyone," she said. "The note says. Forget the face. I was never here." She recited it like scripture, and he understood suddenly that she had recited it many times, on the mill road perhaps, to herself, in the dark, to keep walking. "If you tell anyone, they'll find me. They found everyone else."

"Who found everyone else?"

She looked at the stove. She did not answer, and the silence had a shape to it, the shape of something she had seen and would carry forever, and Ivo felt the crack open in him before he could brace it — a thin fault line running down through all that practiced indifference, letting in the cold.

Because he knew, now. He had known since the doorway, if he was honest; the handwriting had only confirmed what the face had begun. The fine boots, he noticed, were fine indeed, city-made, English leather, scuffed now almost beyond saving. The coached stillness. The name that meant nothing and therefore meant everything. He had carried enough sealed papers out of that dying capital to know which ministers' daughters had nurses and governesses and shoes like that.

He should burn the note. He should walk her to Brakel's frosted window in the morning and hand her into the ordinary world where she belonged, and forget her face as instructed, because instructions were the whole of his religion and he had kept it immaculate for eleven years.

Instead he heard himself say, "Are your feet wet?"

She nodded.

He knelt — he hadn't knelt in front of anyone in years, it occurred to him distantly, kneeling was for men with gods or children, categories he'd assumed he was exempt from — and pulled off her mittens, then her boots, working stiff laces with his courier's fingers. Her socks were soaked through, her toes waxy-pale and painless-looking. Frostnip, not yet frostbite, if he was lucky. He rubbed them with the blanket, roughly, to bring the blood back, and she watched him do it without flinching, without anything, and somehow that was worse than tears.

"They're looking for a girl with fine boots," she said quietly, conversationally, as if reporting weather. "That's what Papa's man said. So I put mud on them but the snow washed it off."

Ivo's hands went still around her small frozen foot.

"Papa's man," he repeated. "Where is your papa, Mirei?"

"He stayed at the house." A beat. "With the noises outside it."

The fire ticked. Snow pressed against the dark river-side window. And Ivo Kavanagh, invisible man, bystander to history, professional nonentity, sat on a kitchen floor holding a child's foot and understood with perfect clarity that his old life had ended sometime between eleven minutes past two and now, and that the ledger in the false bottom of his case had just become the second most dangerous thing in the house.

Because the note's handwriting — the one he'd refused to read, refused to name — belonged to Lena.

And Lena had been dead for six years.
