## 3. What the Ledger Says

Ivo did not sleep. He told himself it was discipline, that a courier's first duty was to the package and only afterward to his own body, but the truth was simpler: there was a child asleep in the safehouse's back room, breathing in small, careful increments, and every one of those breaths was a question he could not answer.

So he worked.

The safehouse was a disused toll office on the Gellbruck waterfront, its windows painted over sometime in the last war, its single stove ticking like a patient clock. He had lit it low—smoke from a chimney in an evacuated town drew eyes—and by its grudging light he laid the ledger on the table and looked at it the way he had been trained to look at anything dangerous: without touching it first.

It was an ordinary object. That was the thing no one warned you about. Six years of carrying other people's secrets had taught Ivo that history never came wrapped in thunder; it came in plain calfskin, string-tied, water-stained at one corner, smelling faintly of a minister's cigar. It weighed less than the bread he'd bought in Marren. It could have been an account book for a wool merchant. In a sense, it was.

He untied the string.

The cipher was Vessany's ministry standard, the fourth revision—the one they'd rolled out with great ceremony two winters ago and which, rumor said, had been broken within the month by every chancellery from here to the coast. Ivo hadn't broken it himself. He'd memorized the key tables the way he memorized routes and faces and the license numbers of cars that followed him too long: as professional necessity, filed away in the locked room behind his eyes where he kept everything and felt nothing.

Or so he had believed until three days ago.

He worked slowly, copying figures onto scrap paper and burning each sheet in the stove before moving to the next. The ledger's entries were dated across fourteen months. Payments, at first innocuous—grain credits, railway leases, the usual arterial money of a dying state. Then, in the spring entries, the language shifted. *Border adjustment, western sector. Compensation schedule annexed. Ratification contingent upon non-interference corridor.*

Ivo read the phrase four times.

*Non-interference corridor.* He knew the western sector. He had run documents through it twice, past villages whose names the government had stopped printing on maps because printed names invited questions. The corridor wasn't a place. It was an absence—a stretch of border the army had been quietly ordered not to defend, so that someone else's soldiers could walk through it unopposed when the time came. Men in the capital had sold the country's front door, and they had written down the price, because men who sell countries always write down the price.

His hands were steady. He noticed that about himself, distantly, the way you notice weather. Steady hands, cold room, child sleeping one wall away. This was what he had told himself he was: a man who moved paper, untouched by what the paper moved.

He turned to the March pages, and the cipher hand changed.

Not the system—the same tables, the same methodical substitutions—but the execution. The minister wrote his numerals with a peculiar downward slash on sevens, cramped and impatient, as if the pen were being chased. Ivo recognized it immediately. He had carried enough of Minister Vael Mirenevic's correspondence to know his hand the way you know a voice through a wall. The man had written his treason the way he wrote everything: fast, certain, annoyed at the inconvenience of having to use letters at all.

But it was the margin that stopped Ivo's heart.

Beside the April entry—the largest payment, the formal confirmation of the corridor—there was a note in pencil, small, slanted leftward, in a hand that did not belong to any minister. Three words and a figure:

*Copy verified. —L.*

The stove ticked. The river muttered under its ice beyond the painted window. Ivo sat without moving for a long time, the way a man sits when the floor of a familiar room turns out to be thinner than he thought.

He knew that handwriting.

He would have known it upside down, underwater, burned half to ash. He had seen it on route cards slipped into his coat pocket at the Teren station coffee stand; on a note tucked into a book she'd returned to him with a page dog-eared and a penciled arrow at one line she'd thought was funny; on the envelope, final and unexplained, that had ended everything between them six years ago—a whole sentence long, *Forgive me, this is cleaner*, and then nothing, ever again, from Lena Aris.

She had vanished the way good couriers vanish: completely. No body, no arrest record, no farewell. For two years he had watched station platforms for her the way other men watch for ghosts, and then he had done what he prided himself on doing—he had stopped. Filed her away in the locked room. Felt nothing, professionally, permanently.

And now here she was, in pencil, in the margin of a betrayal, dated eleven months ago. *Copy verified.*

Which meant she had held this ledger. She had read these pages—or their original—and she had stood somewhere, alive, less than a year ago, with a pencil in her hand, doing exactly what Ivo was doing now: carrying the country's shame from one hiding place to the next.

And she had left a child on a doorstep in Gellbruck.

Ivo rose, crossed the cold hallway, and opened the back room door a hand's width. Moonlight came through a crack in the painted glass and lay across the cot where the girl slept. She had refused to give more than her name. Mirei, she'd said, when he asked on the first night. Nothing else. When he'd pressed—surname, parents, who sent her—she had turned her face to the wall with a finality that belonged to someone much older than seven.

Tonight, though, she had talked a little, by the stove, over bread. Small things. The kind of things children let slip when they are deciding whether to trust you. Her father worked with papers, she'd said. Important papers. He wrote very fast and was cross when the ink blotted. And their name—the family name, offered with the offhand carelessness of a child reciting something drilled into her—*Mirenevic*. We are Mirenevics. Papa says a name is a thing you must be able to say proudly even when it is dangerous.

Minister Vael Mirenevic. Author of the cipher hand. Architect of the corridor. Missing since the capital fell, presumed fled or dead, and his daughter seven hundred versts away in a river town, delivered to a stranger's doorstep with a note that said only *forget the face*.

Forget the face. Whose face? Hers? The person who left her? Ivo had assumed the instruction meant the girl—that whoever abandoned her wanted no trail. Now, holding the margin of the ledger up to the stove light, he understood it differently. The note hadn't been an order to him. It had been advice. Whoever wrote it knew precisely what happened to people who were seen near this book.

The pencil note was Lena's. The child was Lena's—the chin, the watchfulness, those fine city boots, that surname. Lena had not vanished six years ago. She had gone somewhere, become someone, married or not, borne a child, and then—when the minister she served began writing down the price of his country's door—she had come back to work. Come back to the only trade she knew. Carried the proof out of the capital. Verified the copy. And then, somewhere between there and Gellbruck, she had run out of road.

Ivo did not cry. He noted, clinically, that this surprised him. What he felt instead was older and colder than grief: it was obligation, arriving late. Six years late. She had called to him once before, in an envelope, and he had answered with silence because silence was safer and he was a man who specialized in safety. Now she had called again, in pencil, across eleven months and a country's collapse, and this time the message could not be filed away, because it was standing in the next room in a child's boots, breathing in small careful increments.

He went back to the table and worked until the sky behind the painted window turned the grey of dishwater, transcribing, burning, memorizing. Somewhere in the middle pages he found a second marginal mark—not words this time, just a small compass rose sketched in pencil, quick and sure, its needle pointing not north but east-northeast, toward some unnamed point along the river above Gellbruck. Beneath it, one word in that slanted hand:

*Mirei.*

Not the town. Not a route. The girl's own name, set beside a direction, as if Lena had drawn a map whose only landmark was her daughter.

Ivo sat back. His hands, so steady all night, were not entirely steady now.

Outside, somewhere down the frozen quay, a door closed—not slammed, closed—with the deliberate softness of someone who did not wish to be heard. Boots on frost, unhurried, passing. Ivo put out the stove's coals with the poker and stood in the dark listening, one hand already finding the ledger, the other reaching for the shutter gap.

Through the finger-width of cleared glass he saw, far down the embankment, a figure in a grey coat pause at the end of the quay. The figure looked up at the toll office—looked directly at it, for three full seconds, the way a man looks at a building he has already decided about—and then walked on into the white morning without hurry, as though the whole town belonged to him.

Behind Ivo, in the doorway of the back room, a small voice, awake:

"That's the third time he's walked past since yesterday."
