## 4. The Aide in Grey

The train into Gellbruck had no third class anymore, so Sorren Veyl rode first class in a coat that made him look like a man who could not quite afford it. That was the point of the coat. He had chosen it from a dead man's wardrobe in the capital — a wool overcoat, good quality, brushed thin at the cuffs by honest wear — and he had worn it every day since, because men trusted a coat like that. It said: I am careful with money. I am careful with everything.

He was careful with everything.

Through the window the river appeared between warehouses, grey and thickening at the edges, already wearing its winter skin. Gellbruck sat on it like a stone on a sleeping animal. Sorren counted chimneys out of habit. Smoke meant mills running; mills meant grain ledgers; grain ledgers meant he could find any man in this town within a day, because everyone was owed something by someone.

In his breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, he carried a photograph of a girl he had never met and a description of a courier he knew too well.

*Male, mid-thirties. Unremarkable. That is the whole of him — unremarkable.*

It was the truest thing in the file. Sorren had spent eleven days in the capital reading through the Ministry's evacuation wreckage, and the courier's file was the thinnest he had ever seen. No debts. No vices worth recording. No lovers listed, though there was a gap where a lover might have been — a page removed so cleanly the binding still showed its scar. Someone had loved this man enough to erase him, or he had loved himself enough to do it alone.

Sorren respected that. You could work with a man who erased himself. What you could not work with was a man who wanted to be remembered.

* * *

The checkpoint at the mill road was manned by two soldiers and a sergeant who had been a schoolteacher before the war and would be again after it, if there was an after. Sorren watched them for twenty minutes from the window of the customs house across the lane, drinking bad coffee, learning their rhythm. The tall one checked papers thoroughly and slowly. The short one checked nothing and smoked. The sergeant signed whatever they brought him and looked at the river.

When Sorren finally crossed the road, he did not go to the sergeant. He went to the tall soldier first, and he held out his papers upside down.

"Forgive me," he said, "long journey. My eyes are full of train smoke."

The soldier righted the documents, and in doing so leaned in, and in doing so read them twice as carefully as he otherwise would have — because a man who hands you his papers wrong is either a fool or testing you, and neither kind gets waved through without a second look. Sorren let him look. The papers were flawless. They always were. He had not forged them himself; he had paid a forger in the capital three times the going rate to forge them badly first, burn those, and then forge them well. Badly forged papers got you arrested. Well-forged papers got you remembered. Flawless ones got you forgotten, which was the only safety worth buying.

"Ministry of Internal Reconciliation," the soldier read aloud.

"A polite name," Sorren said, "for a department that mostly counts things."

"What things?"

"Losses." He smiled, tired and self-deprecating, the smile of a clerk who has seen too many columns of figures. "When this is all over, someone will have to write down what happened, and my department has been given the unpleasant honor. I'm chasing paperwork, Sergeant. Nothing more dramatic than that."

"I'm not the sergeant."

"No," Sorren agreed pleasantly, "but you will be, the way this army sheds officers."

The soldier almost laughed. Behind him, the smoking one glanced over, curious, and Sorren gave him a nod too — inclusive, unhurried, the nod of a man who sees everyone. It cost nothing. It never cost anything. Charm was the cheapest currency in a collapsing country, because everyone was starved of being treated as though they mattered.

By evening he had coffee with the sergeant — the former schoolteacher — and learned three things without asking a single question. First, that a stranger had come through Gellbruck some days ago and taken rooms above the chandler's shop, a quiet man nobody could describe. Second, that the constable had been called to a doorstep at night and had come away looking like a man who'd been handed something heavier than a bundle. Third, that the ferry at the ice was closed until the river froze solid, which meant anyone going north would have to wait, and waiting men left footprints.

"You're chasing a deserter?" the sergeant asked.

"A ledger," Sorren said, and told the truth, because the truth disarms. "Government accounts. If it falls into the wrong hands it will be used to hang men who don't deserve hanging, and if it falls into the right hands it will be used to excuse men who do. Either way, it should never have been written." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "I'd burn it myself if I could. But I'm a servant of procedure. Procedure says recover it."

"And the wrong hands? Who are they?"

"The loyalists," Sorren said. "The victors. Whoever writes the history next." He met the sergeant's eyes. "You were a teacher. You know how it goes. The book gets written by whoever's holding the pen, and the pen is always held by someone who wasn't there."

The sergeant looked at him for a long moment, and Sorren watched him decide — not to trust him, exactly, but to stop suspecting him. It was enough. In Sorren's experience, no one ever fully trusted him, and he had long ago stopped wanting it. Trust made people careless around you. Suspicion kept them precise, and precision could be steered.

* * *

That night, in his room above the chandler's shop, Sorren laid out what he knew on the bed like a surgeon laying out instruments.

The minister, Aldous Mirei, had built the ledger in his own cipher — a cipher Sorren had helped maintain, which was why he, of all the ministry's scattered staff, had been sent. The ledger proved that the government had sold the eastern border provinces to Ferrane in exchange for loans and quiet passage, and that the signatures ran up to the President's own office. It was true. That was the unbearable part. Sorren had verified it himself, line by line, in the smoking ruins of the records office, and it was all true, and that was precisely why it had to burn.

He thought about the war-weary country it would land in. A republic collapsing, loyalist armies forty miles east and closing, and somewhere in the churn, a document that would tell half the population that their government had sold their homes before the enemy even arrived. It would not topple the guilty. The guilty were already falling. It would land on the living — on the sergeant downstairs, on the schoolchildren he used to teach — and it would tell them their sacrifice had been auctioned. Some truths were like that. Not lamps but fires. You did not carry them into a crowded theater to see better; you carried them out before they spread.

Sorren believed this the way other men believed in God: completely, and with a private knowledge that the belief asked everything of him and would thank him for nothing.

There was also the girl.

He turned the photograph over. Seven years old, dark-haired, solemn. Daughter of Aldous Mirei. Last seen — the report was maddeningly vague — in the capital, in the final week. And now a child matching her description had appeared in Gellbruck, on a doorstep, in the night, delivered to a safehouse that should have been empty.

Someone had brought her here. Someone was keeping her. And wherever the child was, the ledger would be near, because the minister had not sent his daughter to strangers for her health. He had sent her as a seal on the package. No courier abandons the cargo and keeps the wrapping.

Unless, Sorren thought, turning the photograph in the lamplight, the wrapping was the cargo. Unless the child was the thing being protected, and the paper merely traveled in her shadow.

He considered that possibility honestly, the way he considered all possibilities, and found it changed nothing. If the courier valued the girl above the ledger, then the girl was leverage, and leverage was cleaner than violence. He preferred clean. He had done the other kind twice, early in his career, and had learned that the other kind followed a man to sleep and sat on his chest at night weighing nothing and everything.

A knock at the street door below. He went down, coat over his arm, unhurried.

The constable stood in the cold, cap in hand, stamping his feet. "You asked after newcomers, sir. Thought you'd want to know — the fellow above the chandler's, the quiet one. He bought a child's coat today. Wool-lined. Good boots too, from Kessler's, the expensive ones."

Sorren felt the night tilt slightly, the way ice tilts before it speaks.

"Did he now," he said warmly. "A father, then. Or an uncle."

"Or worse," the constable said, with the small cruelty of small towns. "Man like that, alone, no wife anyone's seen—"

"Thank you," Sorren said, pressing a coin into his hand and folding the man's fingers over it. "You've been very helpful. One more kindness — if anyone asks, we spoke about fishing."

"Fishing," the constable agreed, and stamped off into the dark, content.

Sorren stood a moment in the street, breath rising in the lamplight, and looked up at the shuttered windows of the safehouse. Somewhere behind those shutters: a man trained to be invisible, a child carrying her father's last secret, and a ledger that could set fire to everything the survivors were trying to build.

He could take it tonight. Kick the door, put a pistol in the courier's ribs, be on the morning barge north. It would be fast. It would probably even work.

But fast left bodies, and bodies left questions, and questions left trails, and a trail led back to the girl. No. There was a better trade available — there was nearly always a better trade — and trades required daylight, and patience, and a man's own face offered openly across a table.

Tomorrow, then. He would find the courier tomorrow, and offer him the only bargain worth having: the truth burns, and the child lives untouched.

Almost everyone would call that monstrous, Sorren thought, climbing the stairs to his room. Let them. History would call it mercy, if history ever heard of it — and it wouldn't, because that was rather the point.

Above the chandler's shop, in the dark, a floorboard settled under someone's weight. Sorren paused on the landing, listening, and smiled faintly at the sound.

The invisible man, keeping watch. Good. Sorren had never once lost to a man who was trying not to exist.
