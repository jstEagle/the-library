## 5. Three Checkpoints, One River

Ivo Kavanagh had spent nine years learning the geography of not being seen, and the first lesson of that geography was this: know every door before you need one.

So while Mirei slept in the loft of the safehouse — curled against a flour sack with her coat pulled over her like a second blanket — he sat at the table with a stub of pencil and the back of an old grain manifest, and he drew the river.

Gellbruck sat on the Vessan like a button on a coat, and everything that mattered ran along the water. The road upriver was no good; the loyalist vanguard had already cut it east of town, and anyone moving north by land now traveled in columns or in custody. That left the river itself, and the river had exactly three ways through before the border marshes took over and the checkpoints gave way to nothing but ice and reeds.

He drew the first as a small square with a millwheel beside it: *Harsch Mill*, eleven kilometers upstream, where the road pinched between the millpond and the bluff. The loyalists had hung a checkpoint off the old tollhouse there. Grain still moved through — the army ate even while it conquered — which meant carts moved through, which meant a man with a bill of lading and a bored expression could move through.

The second he drew as two strokes crossing: *the bridge at Gellbruck itself*. The stone bridge, three arches, with the customs house squatting at its northern foot like a toad. Harder. Vehicles were searched there now, properly, bayonets going into flour sacks and hay bales. But it had something the mill didn't — crowds. The bridge carried refugees out and soldiers in, and both streams blurred together at dusk.

And the third, furthest north, he drew as a jagged line: *the ferry at the ice*, where the river widened and slowed and froze hard enough every winter that the ferryman traded his boat for a sledge path staked with fir branches. There was no building there, barely a road — just the frozen crossing and, these days, a picket of soldiers collecting bribes from anyone desperate enough to walk out onto a river in February.

Three doors. He tapped the pencil against each one. Any one of them could close.

Behind him, a floorboard creaked.

"You're drawing," Mirei said.

"I'm working."

"It looks like a snake that swallowed three rocks."

He turned. She stood at the foot of the loft ladder, hair a dark ruin, watching him the way she watched everything — sideways, from behind some internal wall, cataloguing. Seven years old. She should have been afraid. She had been, the first night, shaking under her frozen silence. But the fear had burned down to something harder, and what was left was this: a child who read rooms the way other children read picture books.

"Come here," he said, and surprised himself by saying it.

She came. She looked at the map for a long moment without touching it, which he noted, and approved of, and distrusted.

"That's the river?"

"The river."

"And we go up it." Not a question. Of course it wasn't; she'd listened to every word he'd said to the chandler's boy about grain prices yesterday, and he knew it because she'd repeated them back, verbatim, that night, with a small frown, as if checking his work.

"Up it," he confirmed. "Through three places where men with guns decide whether you exist." He pointed. "Mill. Bridge. Ferry."

"Which one is worst?"

"The one where they look at your face too long." He said it lightly. He didn't feel light. "That's why we're going to practice."

* * *

They practiced on the chandler's shop.

It was a risk to let her out at all — the whole town felt thinner since the aide in grey had arrived, some animal part of Ivo insisting that Sorren was already circling — but a girl who'd been locked in a loft for four days would start doing something worse than walking out a door. Fear makes noise. So he wrapped her in a fishmonger's castoff shawl, pinned her fine boots under wool wrappings until they looked like every peasant boot in Vessany, and took her into the morning market as if taking his daughter on an errand.

His daughter. He tried the thought on like a coat and found it fit better than it should have.

"Tell me about the man selling rope," he murmured, steering past the chandlery stalls.

"Grey cap. Left hand missing two fingers. He keeps looking at the guard by the fountain, not at his customers. He's counting soldiers."

Ivo kept walking. His pulse did not change, because he had trained it not to, but somewhere beneath the training something cold slid into place. "What else?"

"There's another man by the fish barrels who isn't buying fish. He has good boots under a bad coat." She paused at a stall, picked up an orange with genuine childish interest, put it back. "Same boots as the man who came to our house in the capital. Before Mama—" She stopped. The sentence ended like a cut rope.

"Mirei."

"He had grey eyes," she said, to the orange. "This one does too. Or maybe all grown-ups' eyes are grey when they're hunting."

He bought her the orange. It cost more than his lunch, and he paid it without blinking, and while the vendor wrapped it he made himself look — once, briefly, the way an invisible man is allowed to look — at the man by the fish barrels.

Good boots. Bad coat. Watching the crowd the way Ivo himself watched crowds: not seeing faces, seeing patterns.

*I know your trade,* Ivo thought. *Because it's mine.*

He walked Mirei home by three unnecessary turns, and she matched every one without asking why, and when they reached the chandler's stair she said quietly, "You went left twice so the fish man couldn't keep a straight line to us. You count corners like Mama counted stairs."

That night he added a fourth item to his map, written small in the corner, underlined once:

*She sees.*

* * *

The next day he taught her the faces.

Not his trade, exactly — he'd never had a partner, never wanted one; a courier who travels with someone travels half as fast and dies twice as easily — but the mathematics had changed. A man alone is invisible. A man with a child is ordinary. And a man with a child who can memorize a checkpoint garrison in thirty seconds is something no one has ever planned for.

So in the loft, by candlelight, he made it a game, because she was seven, and because games were lies that children agreed to believe.

"When we pass the mill," he said, "you'll be sleepy. Children sleep in carts; nobody questions it. But you won't sleep. You'll look through your lashes — like this — and you'll tell me later: how many soldiers, how old is their officer, is there a wagon being searched, which way is the wind blowing the mill smoke."

"Toward the pond or away from it?"

"Exactly."

"Why does the smoke matter?"

"If it blows toward the pond, they're burning paper. If it blows away, they're cooking breakfast. Soldiers burning paper are nervous soldiers, and nervous soldiers search carts twice."

She absorbed that with a slow nod, filing it wherever she filed everything. Then, unexpectedly: "Mama played games like this. Counting things. She used to say, *count the hats, Mirei, count the hats on the tram.* I thought it was just a game." Her jaw set. "It wasn't."

"No," Ivo said quietly. "It wasn't."

Lena. Even now, five days into this, the name arrived in him like weather — pressure first, then rain. Lena, who had run this same ledger south through three cities and vanished somewhere between the second and the third. Lena, whose handwriting he would know in the dark, in ashes, in hell. Lena, who had taught her daughter to count hats because she knew, better than anyone alive, that survival was a discipline of small observations.

And Lena, who had carried this child through a falling country and left her on a stranger's doorstep with three words: *forget the face.*

He had hated her for it, for one black hour after deciphering the margin note. Hated her for making him the keeper of the thing she wouldn't carry herself. Then he had understood it, and understanding had been worse. Because Ivo Kavanagh, professional nonentity, was precisely the only kind of person who could keep a child alive across three checkpoints — and Lena had known that, had perhaps known him, better than he'd ever let himself believe.

"You knew her," Mirei said. It wasn't a question either. Nothing she said was ever a question.

"Everyone knew everyone, once," he said.

"You went quiet when I said her name. Like a room when someone leaves it."

He looked at her — really looked, for the first time letting himself see Lena in the set of that stubborn chin, the sideways attention, the terrible accuracy — and something in his chest rearranged itself, quietly and permanently, the way ice shifts before it breaks.

"Sleep," he said. "Tomorrow we go to the mill."

* * *

He finished the map by candle after she slept, adding the last details from memory: the sentry rotation intervals at the tollhouse, the culvert below the millrace, the stand of willows within sprint distance of the bridge's far arch. Then he sat back and did the thing he always did last, the thing that separated couriers from corpses.

He imagined failure.

The mill went wrong — how? A soldier notices the girl's hands. Peasant children don't have clean fingernails; hers were still city-soft. So: dirt under her nails tomorrow, ash rubbed into her palms. Fine.

The bridge went wrong — how? Sorren. The aide in grey whom the chandler's boy had described as *charming, tipped well, asked about river traffic.* If Sorren was ahead of them, the bridge was his ground, chosen, prepared. So: never cross the bridge on schedule. Cross early or late, or not at all.

The ferry went wrong — how? The ice. Everyone feared the soldiers, and Ivo feared the water under them. A frozen river in late February was a lie told by weather, and the river always got around to correcting itself.

Three doors, he thought, tapping the pencil against each one again. Any one could close.

But the girl had memorized the rope-seller's fingers and the fish-man's boots and the guard's post by the fountain, and had recited them back in order, and then asked which detail mattered most — *asked which detail mattered most* — and Ivo had realized, sitting there in the candle-smell with his map of doors and his stolen child, that for nine years he had survived by being unremarkable, and that for the first time since he'd taken his first commission he was not alone in the craft.

It was not a comfortable realization. It was better than comfortable.

He blew out the candle and lay down in his coat, and in the dark he heard her breathing slow and even above him, and he caught himself planning routes in pairs — *if we're split at the mill, regroup at the willows; if the bridge fails, the rail cutting west* — planning for two, the way you only plan for two when you have stopped being one thing and started being another.

At dawn, boots on the stair. Not the chandler's — wrong weight, wrong rhythm, deliberate.

Two knocks. A pause. A third knock.

The signal, Ivo knew with a cold drop of certainty, that neither he nor anyone in Gellbruck had arranged.

Below, Mirei was already awake. Already at the window. Already counting.

"Grey coat," she whispered. "Good boots. Coming up."
