## 6. The Mill

The mill at Vosk stood a half-day's walk upriver from Gellbruck, and Ivo spent every one of those hours rehearsing.

He had done this before—dozens of times, in a dozen winters. The trick was never the papers; papers could be bought, forged, stolen. The trick was the man carrying them. A soldier at a checkpoint did not read documents so much as he read faces, and Ivo had spent eleven years making his face into nothing at all: a grain merchant's face, tired and slightly stupid, worried about prices and weather and nothing else in the world. He had practiced it in shop windows until he could summon it like a coat.

"Your name is Aldous Vey," he told Mirei as they walked the frozen towpath, breath steaming. "You're my brother's girl. Your mother died of fever. You don't talk much because you're shy."

"I'm not shy," Mirei said.

"Then be quiet for another reason."

She considered this with the terrible seriousness of seven-year-olds. "I can be grieving," she offered. "Grieving people are allowed to be quiet."

Something in Ivo's chest turned over. "Yes," he said. "They are."

She walked beside him with her hands jammed in the pockets of the coat he'd bought her in Gellbruck—a plain grey thing two sizes too big—and said nothing more, which was its own kind of obedience. But her eyes never stopped working. He'd noticed it on the road: how she catalogued everything, the crows on the fence wire, the smoke from farmhouses, the shape of each passerby's boots. She counted soldiers before he did. Twice she had tugged his sleeve and stepped off the path a full ten seconds before the patrol came around the bend.

He should have found it reassuring. Instead it frightened him, because children who learned to count soldiers were children who had already learned what soldiers did.

The mill announced itself by smell first—wet stone and old grain and the sour breath of the wheel race—and then by sound, the groan of machinery that even war hadn't managed to stop, because people still needed flour whether the Republic lived or died. The checkpoint sat where the road crossed the millrace on a plank bridge: a hut of fresh-cut timber, sandbags, a barrier pole, four soldiers in loyalist grey with their collars up against the cold. Behind them, sacks of milled grain stood stacked like a wall, waiting for wagons that no longer came.

Ivo stopped twenty paces out and made himself breathe.

*You are Aldous Vey. You are nobody. Nobody is the safest thing to be.*

He hitched the sack of oats onto his shoulder—heavier than it needed to be, but a merchant without goods was a merchant without a story—and walked forward with the shuffling gait of a man whose feet hurt, which by then required no acting at all.

* * *

The soldier who met him was young, maybe nineteen, with chapped lips and the particular boredom of a boy posted to a backwater while his friends got shot at somewhere important. He looked at Ivo the way one looks at weather.

"Papers."

Ivo produced them—the grain license, the transit pass, the letter of consignment written in a clerk's careful hand. Forged in Gellbruck by a man who charged him three days' food for the work, and good work too. The soldier read them slowly, moving his lips.

"Vosk to Kellin Ferry?"

"And beyond, if the ferry's running."

"Ferry's not running." The soldier jerked his chin at the river. It lay under grey ice all the way to the far bank, thick near the edges, darker and less certain in the middle channel. "Ice is rotten past the shallows. Command says nobody crosses till it refreezes or till spring, whichever comes first."

"That's a long wait."

"That's an order." The soldier stamped his boots and handed the papers back, and for one shining moment Ivo let himself believe it was over—that they would walk through, sleep in the mill loft, move on at dawn.

Then the soldier's gaze dropped.

It dropped past Ivo entirely, down to the small figure standing at his knee, and fixed there. On her boots.

Mirei wore the boots she'd arrived in. There had been no time, no money, no way to know—they were fine leather, city-made, stitched with a delicacy no miller's niece had ever owned, and now they stood in the mud of a checkpoint road like a signature at the bottom of a confession.

"Nice boots," the soldier said.

"My brother's girl," Ivo said, and heard his own voice go flat and pleasant, the voice of a man with nothing to hide. "Her father had money once. Before the fever took him. She won't wear anything else. Children." He shrugged, a whole philosophy of life in the gesture.

The soldier didn't shrug back. He was looking at Mirei now, really looking, and Ivo watched the boredom drain out of his face and something sharper arrive in its place. Nineteen years old and not stupid after all. "Girl. What's your name?"

Mirei looked up at Ivo.

That was the mistake. A shy child would have hidden behind her guardian's leg. A grieving child would have stared at the mud. Mirei looked at Ivo for permission, and the look itself was a language, and languages get noticed.

"Mirei," Ivo said quickly. "She doesn't—"

"Mirei what?" The soldier's hand had come off his rifle sling and onto the rifle itself. One of the others by the sandbags straightened. "There was a notice come through yesterday. Minister's daughter, missing out of the capital. Seven years old. Dark hair." His eyes went to her hair, dark as river water under the hood. "Fine boots."

The world narrowed to the width of a rifle barrel.

Later—Ivo would think about this later, in the years when thinking about it was all he had—he understood that the moment broke open because of the oats. The sack on his shoulder, three days' food bought in Gellbruck, forty pounds of ordinary grain. He swung it off his shoulder and let it fall, and it burst on the frozen ground between them, a soft brown explosion, and every eye at the checkpoint followed the wheat as it scattered and rolled.

Then he had Mirei by the wrist and they were running.

* * *

Shouts behind them. A whistle, three notes, the signal for stop-or-be-shot. Ivo pulled Mirei left, away from the road, along the millrace where the wheel churned the water into a black foam that steamed in the cold. The race ran in a stone channel beside the mill building, fenced with rotting planks, and beyond it the wheel itself turned with a groan like something dying slowly and refusing to finish.

"The bridge!" Mirei gasped. "Back over the—"

"They'll hold the bridge." He could hear boots pounding the road behind them, more than two pairs now. "Through the mill."

"But—"

"*Through the mill.*"

He put his shoulder to the side door and it gave—not locked, because who locks a mill in wartime—and they were inside, into a cathedral of dust and noise. Grain sifted down through the dim air like snow falling upward in reverse. The great stones turned somewhere above them, shaking the floor, and the noise swallowed their footsteps and, he prayed, the shouts outside.

"There," he said. "The race gate."

At the downstream end of the building, where the wheel's channel exited toward the river, a wooden sluice gate stood half-raised, water roaring through the gap beneath it. Beyond the gate, the race channel ran thirty yards to the riverbank, walled in stone, deep enough to hide a running child, loud enough to swallow gunfire.

"It's water," Mirei said. "It's freezing water."

"Yes."

"You're going to make us go in the water."

"Yes."

She looked at him then with her mother's eyes—he knew that now, knew whose eyes they were—and whatever she saw in his face must have been answer enough, because she didn't argue. She pulled the compass from her pocket, pressed it to her chest through the coat, and nodded once.

The door behind them burst open.

Ivo didn't see the soldier clearly—just grey, and the flash of a raised rifle—and then the world went white and loud. He shoved Mirei toward the sluice and felt the impact take him high in the back, below the shoulder blade, a punch that spun him half around and put him on his knees among the spilled grain. Not a bullet. A rifle butt, swung hard. He would learn later how lucky that was.

"Stop—" someone shouted, absurdly, over the roar of the wheel, as if a word could do it.

Ivo got up. He did not remember deciding to get up; his body simply refused the ground. He caught Mirei's arm, hauled her to the sluice gate, and pushed her through the gap into the racing black water, and she screamed once—the only time he ever heard her scream—and then the channel took them both.

Cold like a fist closing. Stone scraping his hip, his elbow, his jaw. He wrapped himself around her and let the current do the work, kicking only to keep her face above the foam, and the walls of the race flashed past, and then the channel spat them out into the shallows of the river itself, among ice cakes grinding in the eddy, and he dragged her up the bank into the dead reeds and lay there, both of them heaving, while the shouts faded upstream behind them.

* * *

They lay in the reeds for a long time. Long enough for the sky to start going the color of ash. Mirei shook against his side, soaked to the skin, and he held her and listened to his own heart and waited for the pain in his back to become a word.

When it finally did, the word was *bad*. Not fatal—he'd carried men with worse—but bad. A deep bruise or a cracked rib, high on the right side, and every breath cost him something.

"We can't go back to the road," he said. His voice sounded like gravel in a tin cup. "They'll have riders out by dark. They'll have our description. They'll have *hers*."

Mirei lifted her head. Her lips were blue. Her eyes were furious.

"They said my name," she said. "They're looking for me. Not you. Me."

"They're looking for both of us now."

"No." She sat up, dripping, small and immovable in the reeds. "They were looking for me before. Papa told me. He said if anyone asked, I was to forget my name and forget his face." Her chin trembled and she mastered it, the way she mastered everything, with that awful adult discipline. "*Forget the face.* That's what the note said too. That's why they left me with you. Because you forget faces, isn't it? That's what you do."

Ivo said nothing. The river ground its ice together in the darkening channel, patient as a millstone.

"So we don't go to the ferry," Mirei said. "We go where the compass goes. Mama said it points to help. She said it twice." She pulled it out, wet and gleaming, and held it flat on her palm between them. The needle swung, steadied, and settled—not north, not toward the border, but east, into the hills above the river, toward a fold of country Ivo had never had reason to notice in eleven years of being nobody.

He looked at the needle. He looked at the child. His back burned with every heartbeat, and somewhere behind them, in the mill town, men were writing down descriptions: a tall courier, a girl of seven, dark hair, fine boots. Fugitives with a name. The safest thing he had ever been was gone, and it was not coming back.

"All right," Ivo said. "East."

Mirei closed her hand over the compass, and in the last light he saw her almost smile.
