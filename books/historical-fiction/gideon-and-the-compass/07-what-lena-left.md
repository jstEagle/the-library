## 7. What Lena Left

My mother's hands smelled of orange peel and lamp oil.

That is the first thing I remember, and I have decided it will be the last thing I ever forget. People say children don't remember, that seven is too young, that grief washes everything smooth like river stones. They are wrong. Grief does not smooth. Grief carves.

It was the third night before we left Vessany, though I did not know then that we were leaving, because my mother never told me things straight. She told them in pieces, wrapped up like parcels, and you had to carry each one a while before it opened.

She was kneeling at the wardrobe in her dressing room, and she had taken out the false back — the one the cabinetmaker swore no one would ever find, the one Father didn't even know about — and inside there was a hollow where winter gloves should have been. She put something in. She took something out. And while she did it she said a name, so quietly I thought she was praying.

*Ivo.*

I thought it was a saint's name. We had a picture book of saints, all of them with their eyes turned upward and their palms open, and I decided this Ivo was one too, the patron of whatever my mother was hiding. Patron of glove-shaped hollows. Patron of mothers who kneel in lamplight.

She saw me in the mirror. Most people would have startled. My mother never startled; she simply rearranged her face the way she rearranged a room when guests were coming, everything swept to its proper corner, and she smiled at my reflection instead of turning around.

"Mirei. Come here, little compass."

That was what she called me. Little compass. Because I always found north, she said, or because I always found trouble — she would never tell me which, and I think now it was both.

She held out her hand, and in it was a compass. A real one, brass, heavy for its size, the kind of thing that should live in a captain's pocket on some ship going somewhere important. The lid was dented on one side, and the glass over the needle had a small star-shaped crack, as if the compass itself had once been dropped from a great height and survived out of stubbornness.

"This is yours now," she said. "But listen carefully, because this part matters more than anything else I have ever asked you."

I listened. When your mother uses that voice, the one that makes ministers sit down and secretaries stop writing, you listen.

"A compass tells you which way is north," she said. "But the truth—" and here she tapped the brass lid with one fingernail, twice, the way she tapped the table when she wanted silence in the dining room— "the truth doesn't care about north. Sometimes you point it at a person instead."

I didn't understand. I want to be honest about that, because everyone in this story keeps pretending they understand things, and someone ought to say plainly: none of us understood. Not the tall courier with his careful face, not the man in grey with his reasonable voice, not me. We were all carrying things we couldn't read yet.

"Point it at a person," I repeated, because repeating bought time.

"And if anyone asks you where you got it," my mother said, "if anyone dangerous asks, if anyone with grey gloves asks — you tell them you found it. In a gutter. You picked it up because it was pretty. Do you understand? You *found* it. You have never seen me hold it. You have never heard that name I said tonight."

"What name?"

Her face did its rearranging again, but slower this time, and for half a breath I saw through it to something underneath that looked like fear wearing a smile's clothes.

"No name," she said. "Forget the face, Mirei."

*Forget the face.* That was her phrase, her private one, from a game we played. She would stand in front of me and let me study her — the mole under her left eye, the way one eyebrow sat higher than the other — and then she would leave the room and change something, remove an earring, pin her hair differently, and come back and ask: *what's different?* The game was called Forget the Face, and the rule was that a person who could forget their own face could keep other people's secrets safe. I was very good at the game. I am still very good at the game. It has cost me nearly everything, and I would play it again.

* * *

The man with the careful face — Ivo, though I did not know his name yet, would not know it until the night he stood in the doorway of the safehouse looking at me like I was a parcel addressed wrong — that man thinks he is the keeper of this mission.

He carries the ledger against his back, under the bandage, and he thinks the mission lives there, in paper and cipher. He talks to himself when he thinks I'm asleep, going over routes: mill, bridge, ferry, ice. He counts his steps. He sits facing doors. He is the most visible invisible man in Vessany, and he doesn't know it, and I have decided not to tell him yet.

Here is what he does not know, and what I only worked out three nights ago, sitting awake in the dark of a hayloft while he slept with his wounded back toward the wall:

The night at the wardrobe, after she gave me the compass, my mother took something else out of the hollow. A second parcel. Small, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed thread. She pressed it into my coat lining while I watched, and she said: "This goes to the place the needle loves best. Not to a person. To a *place*. If I cannot take it myself, you take it, and you do not open it, and you do not give it to soldiers or priests or policemen, no matter how kind they are. Kind ones are the most dangerous. They make you want to hand over everything."

I asked her what was in it.

"The truth," she said, "in case the other copy burns."

Then she sewed the pocket shut herself, right there, with small stitches, humming. She hummed the song about the ferryman's daughter who crosses the river alone. She kissed the top of my head and said my hair smelled like smoke, which was true, because the city had started burning that week and nobody had mentioned it at dinner.

So there were two copies. Ivo carries one. I carry the other, sewn into my coat, next to my heart, where it has ridden through three towns and two checkpoints and one terrible cold run through a millrace while men shouted behind us. It is heavier than paper should be. Truths usually are.

And the compass — I have tested it, secretly, holding it flat on my knees in the dark the way she taught me without teaching me. The needle does not settle north. It swings east, always east, and trembles at something past the horizon like a dog smelling home. There is a place the needle loves. There is a hole in the world somewhere east of us, dug by hands I will never see, waiting for my mother's parcel the way a letterbox waits.

Which means the tall man has been leading us the wrong direction on purpose, or the right direction on accident, or — most likely, knowing adults — some third thing entirely that he believes is sensible.

* * *

Tonight he caught me practicing.

He came around the corner of the barn where I was sitting on a feed sack, compass flat on my knee, watching the needle strain east, and I closed my hand over it fast, but not fast enough. He is quick for a man with a hurt back.

"That's not pointing north," he said.

"It's broken," I said, using my gutter voice, the one for grey gloves. "I found it."

He crouched down in front of me, slow, the way you approach a bird you don't want to frighten off, and he looked at me for a long moment. He has tired brown eyes and a face built to be forgotten, and standing that close I finally saw it — the thing my mother must have seen once, years ago, enough to whisper a name into a lamplit room. Something worth trusting. Something that had been hurt and had decided to become careful instead of cruel.

"Mirei," he said, and my name sounded strange in his mouth, like he'd been saying it to himself when he thought no one heard. "Where did you get that compass?"

"You know where," I said.

I don't know why I broke my own rule. Perhaps because rules are for keeping strangers out, and somewhere between the millrace and this feed sack, he stopped being a stranger. Or perhaps because I was tired of being the only one carrying everything.

His face did not rearrange the way Mother's did. It cracked, straight down the middle of it, quietly, the way ice cracks before it gives.

"Lena," he said. Just the name. Just once, to the empty air between us, like setting down something heavy.

"You knew her," I said. It wasn't a question. I am my mother's daughter; I do not ask questions I already hold the answers to.

"She taught me the ciphers," he said. His voice had gone rough. "Years ago. Before you were born. Then she was reassigned, and then—" He stopped. Then she vanished, his mouth finished, silently. "What else did she leave you?"

I almost told him. I got as far as putting my hand flat against my coat, over the sewn pocket, over the oilcloth parcel riding beside my heart. He saw the motion. He is trained to see motions; it is his whole profession. His eyes went to my hand and stayed there, and I watched him understand, watched him decide not to reach for it, and that decision told me more about him than any answer he could have given.

"East," I said instead, and opened my hand, and showed him the needle trembling toward the dark. "Whatever you're planning — bridge, ferry, all your clever routes — the compass says east first. There's a place it wants to go. She wanted it to go there."

He looked at the needle. He looked at me. Somewhere far off, the river was doing something under its ice, groaning like an old floor, like the whole world shifting its weight in its sleep.

"My mother said truths don't care about north," I told him.

He was quiet a long time. Then he said, "Tomorrow. First light. We go where it points." And he added, softer, almost to himself, "She's still steering, isn't she. Even now."

I didn't answer that either. Some things you let people believe gently.

But I will write down here, in the privacy of my own head, what I did not say: that the needle isn't pointing at a place at all. That night at the wardrobe, the last thing my mother did, before she sent me to bed, was hold the compass up between us and turn it — not east, not north — until the needle swung and settled, pointing directly at my chest.

"There," she said. "You see? Steady. Whatever happens, little compass — you're the true thing. Everything else just turns around you."

In the morning, men will be asking questions in the town about a girl in fine boots. In the morning, the tall man will shoulder his pack and wince at his wound and follow a child's toy across the frozen country. And somewhere ahead of us, east, where the needle strains — I do not know what waits. Only that my mother sewed the truth into my coat, pointed me at it like a weapon, and trusted a stranger's name to carry me there.

First light comes early in winter. I intend to be ready.
