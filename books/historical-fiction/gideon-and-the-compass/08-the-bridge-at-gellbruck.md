## 8. The Bridge at Gellbruck

The bridge at Gellbruck was older than the republic that had built it, and it showed. Its stone arches wore the lichen of three governments, and its guardhouse—once a tollbooth for a duke nobody remembered—now held two soldiers, a stove, and a sergeant who checked papers with the weary patience of a man who had already decided the war would outlast him.

Ivo watched it from the window of a chandler's shop across the quay, Mirei beside him on a barrel of tallow, both of them eating bread that had gone hard as scripture.

"You'll do the talking," he said. "If anyone asks."

"I don't talk," Mirei said. It wasn't shyness. She said it the way a surgeon says *I don't gamble*.

"Good girl." He tore his bread and chewed slowly. Below them, carts crossed the bridge in a thin, cold stream. Two soldiers waved most of them through without looking up. That was the trouble with checkpoints in a collapsing state: by the third week of an army's approach, the men on the bridges stopped believing anything they caught mattered anymore.

He should have felt confident. He had been unnoticed for eleven years. He had carried death warrants folded into hymnals and walked past the very ministers they were meant to kill, and no one had ever looked twice at his face because there was nothing in it worth the looking. That had always been his gift, his quiet pride: Ivo Kavanagh, a man history used as an envelope.

But envelopes, he was learning, could be opened. And the girl on the tallow barrel was fine paper with a seal on her, whatever coat they put her in. Her boots alone—the good leather ones she refused to abandon, her mother's boots—would hang them both at the mill checkpoint's memory. Word traveled upriver faster than warrants now. A grain merchant and a child in minister's leather, gone through the millrace, one soldier bleeding from a shovel he hadn't seen coming. Somewhere behind them, that story was growing teeth.

"New plan," Ivo said.

Mirei waited. She was good at waiting. Seven years old and better at it than any man he'd served under.

"We cross separately. You go with Mrs. Halloran." The chandler's wife, a broad, uncurious woman who owed Ivo's handler a favor older than Mirei. "She's taking flour up the towpath. You're her niece from the coast. Quiet as church, yes?"

"And you?"

"I cross the bridge like an honest man. Honest men are invisible. Nobody's ever been arrested for looking bored."

Mirei studied him with her mother's gray eyes, and for a moment he thought she would argue. She had her mother's stubbornness too; he'd recognized it the way you recognize handwriting. But she only nodded once and slid off the barrel, and something in Ivo's chest pulled tight—a sensation he refused, on principle, to name.

That was when he saw the man in grey.

He came along the quay without hurry, hat low, hands in his pockets, greeting the fish-sellers by name though he couldn't have been in Gellbruck more than a day. Sorren. Ivo knew him only from the mill—from the sound of his voice through a wall, level and reasonable even while his soldiers put a boot to a door. The aide. The man who wanted the ledger not read but *unmade*.

Ivo did not move. Moving was how watchers marked themselves. He stood at the window with his bread and let his face go empty, and Sorren walked past the shop, paused—only paused—and then climbed the steps to the bridge guardhouse as if he owned the mortgage on it.

"He's here," Mirei said.

"I know."

"He came for us."

"He came for the paper," Ivo said. "Remember that. Men like him don't want children. They want paper."

It was a lie, or near enough, and by the look on her face she knew it.

* * *

Sorren found him an hour later, which surprised Ivo not at all. What surprised him was where: the little tavern under the bridge abutment, where Ivo had gone to wait out the crossing hour with a glass of the local spirit, which tasted of kerosene and regret.

The aide sat down across from him uninvited, set his hat on the table, and ordered two more of the same. Up close he was younger than his voice suggested, with a clerk's soft hands and a dueling scar that had been stitched carelessly. He pushed one glass across the table.

"You're paying," Ivo said.

"Gladly." Sorren drank like a man who had earned it. "You put Havel's head through the milldoor frame. He'll live. He'll also tell the story wrong for the rest of his life, so perhaps you did him a favor."

Ivo said nothing. Invisibility was a discipline, and silence was its liturgy.

"I've been reading your file," Sorren went on, conversationally. "There isn't much of it. That's rather the point of you, isn't it? Eleven years, forty-some crossings, no incidents until this month. You were the best envelope the ministry ever licked shut." He smiled at his own phrase, genuinely pleased. "And then someone put a living thing inside the envelope, and now you can't help leaving fingerprints everywhere. A shovel. A stolen skiff. A witness in Gellbruck who remembers a girl with gray eyes buying apples and counting the change *twice*, like her life depended on it."

The tavern was nearly empty. The ferryman drank in the corner with his back to them, pretending deafness, which was the local religion.

"What do you want?" Ivo asked.

"The ledger. Only that." Sorren turned his glass slowly on the table, drawing wet rings. "And before you give me the speech—the righteous courier, the truth must out—let me tell you what's actually in it. Not the cipher nonsense about borders and concessions. The last pages. The lists."

Ivo kept his face still. He had deciphered some of those pages by candlelight while Mirei slept, and he knew exactly what Sorren meant: names. Names of border villages handed over in negotiation, with population figures attached, written in a minister's tidy hand as casually as a grocer counting stock.

"When the loyalists take the capital," Sorren said, "and they will, within the month—that ledger becomes evidence. Evidence in what? A tribunal? There will be no tribunal. There will be a purge dressed as one, and every name in that book becomes a sentence to be carried out by men who enjoy their work. The government that wrote it is dead. Burning it doesn't hide the crime. It just stops the crime from being *used*. Do you understand me? I'm not protecting the guilty. The guilty are already beyond everyone. I'm protecting the families of the dead from being made into instruments."

He spoke without heat. That was the terrible thing. Fanatics shout; pragmatists pour you a drink.

"Convenient," Ivo said, "that the families you're protecting all have the same surname as the men who signed."

"Yes," Sorren agreed pleasantly. "It often works out that way. Power protects power, even in defeat. Especially in defeat. I didn't design the world, courier. I'm just trying to leave it slightly less on fire than I found it."

He reached into his coat, and Ivo's hand moved before thought—but Sorren only produced a folded document and laid it on the table. Passage papers. Two sets. Genuine seals—Ivo had handled enough forgeries to know the real thing by its weight.

"One for the girl," Sorren said. "Coastal port, a ship, a name that has never appeared in any file. She lands somewhere with oranges and no memory. No one will ever look for her, because no one will know she exists. The other—" he tapped the second paper— "is for you. Same ship. You were never an envelope. You were never anything. You go back to being no one, which I understand is your heart's desire."

The stove ticked. The ferryman's glass clinked far away.

"And the ledger?"

"Burned. Tonight, before we cross. Together, if you like. I'll even let you hold the match—it seems to matter to your sort."

Ivo looked at the papers. He thought of the millrace and the wound in his side that ached when he breathed deep. He thought of three checkpoints and a broken river, of a seven-year-old memorizing faces because he had taught her that survival was arithmetic. He thought of the names in the last pages, whole villages reduced to figures in a margin, and of Lena's handwriting beside them—one small annotation in a hand he would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea:

*Ivo. If it must be carried, let him carry it.*

She had trusted him with it. And here sat a reasonable man offering to make that trust unnecessary—to make everything unnecessary, the crossing, the ice, the whole bloody river, in exchange for one book of dead men's arithmetic.

"That's the trade," Sorren said. "Paper for the child. Truth for a life. You've done harder math in worse light, I'd wager."

Ivo's hand was on the passage documents. He noticed it the way you notice you've already fallen—the ground arriving. His fingers had touched the edge of the nearest paper, and he could not remember deciding to reach.

One life against a book. One warm, specific, apple-counting child against an abstraction that would, at best, become a paragraph in someone else's history. Every honest part of him said *take it*. Every cowardly part said it louder.

"There's a condition," Ivo heard himself say, and hated how hoarse his voice came out.

"Name it."

"She never knows. Not the terms, not the trade. As far as her whole life goes, we simply left."

Sorren considered, then inclined his head, almost gently. "Agreed. She can believe you were brave instead."

And that—*she can believe you were brave instead*—was the hook that caught Ivo under the ribs and held him there, half-risen from his chair, fingers still resting on the paper, hating himself with a thoroughness that felt almost like relief.

Which was why he was looking at Sorren's face and not the doorway, and why neither of them heard the outer door open, or the small feet stop dead on the flagstones, or the breath catch—the sharp, audible breath of a child who had come looking for her niece-from-the-coast papers and had heard, instead, exactly what she cost.

The stool scraped. Ivo turned.

Mirei stood in the doorway with the compass clenched in her fist, her mother's boots planted wide, her gray eyes going from the passage papers to Ivo's hand still touching them—and Ivo watched the understanding arrive in her face, item by item, like a ledger being balanced.

Then she ran.

"MIREI—"

She was through the door and up the abutment steps before his chair hit the floor, small and fast and furious, a dark coat flashing between the fish stalls. Sorren rose smoothly, unhurried, putting on his hat.

"No," Ivo snarled, and shoved past him—

—but Sorren's hand closed on his arm with a clerk's deceptively strong grip, and the aide's voice stayed terribly kind.

"She'll come back to you," Sorren said. "Children always come back to the one adult they have left. Take the papers, courier. You've already decided. All that's left is deciding whether you get her back before I do."

Beyond the windows, on the white expanse of the river, the ice gave a long, cracking sigh—as if the water beneath had heard everything, and was already making room.
