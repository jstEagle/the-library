## 9. The Ice Breaks

The ferry checkpoint at Vassmuth was not a building so much as a wound in the riverbank—a wooden platform cantilevered over the ice, a stove-heated hut, and a chain strung between two poles with a hand-painted sign that read NOTHING CROSSES AFTER FIRST THAW. The thaw had come three days early. Everyone knew it. Nobody wanted to be the one to say it.

Ivo heard the river before he saw it.

It was a sound he had no name for, deep beneath the ice, like a cathedral groaning in its sleep—then a crack, sharp as rifle fire, running out across the white plain of the river in a blue-black seam longer than a train. Mirei's hand found his. She did not squeeze it. She simply held it, the way you hold a railing on a stair in the dark, and somehow that was worse than if she had been afraid.

"They'll close the crossing," Ivo said. "When it starts talking like that, they close it."

"Then we go now."

"We go smart," Ivo said, though his heart was already running ahead of him onto the ice. Seven years old, and she was the one teaching him courage. He hated what that said about him. He hated more that he was beginning not to care.

The ferryman's hut stank of coal smoke and wet wool. Two soldiers manned it—one old, one young enough that his greatcoat still held its factory creases—and behind them, warming his hands at the stove with the unhurried ease of a man who has never once needed to hurry, stood Sorren.

He was not holding a weapon. That was the first obscenity of it. His coat was grey wool, spotless despite three days of pursuit, and his gloves were folded over his forearm like a waiter's. He looked up when they came in, and his face did the terrible thing his face always did: it softened.

"Ivo Kavanagh," he said. "You look like a man who hasn't slept in a barn."

"A shed," Ivo said. "The barn had soldiers in it."

The young soldier laughed before he could stop himself, then went red. The old soldier did not laugh. The old soldier was watching the river through the frosted window, and his watching had the quality of prayer.

"You have it with you," Sorren said. Not a question.

"The mill, the bridge," Ivo said. "You're nothing if not thorough."

"I'm thorough because I'm right." Sorren stepped away from the stove, and the light moved across his face and made him look, for a moment, genuinely kind. "Sit down. Both of you. There's tea." He gestured, and the young soldier moved instinctively to block the door—not drawing his rifle, just becoming a wall. "Nobody is going to hurt the girl. I want you to hear me say that in front of witnesses. Nobody hurts the girl. She crosses. She goes wherever you were taking her. She grows up and forgets all this."

"And the ledger burns."

"And the ledger burns." Sorren spread his hands. "Think about what's actually on those pages, Kavanagh. Nine provinces sold. Forty thousand names of men who trusted their government. You put that in front of the world and what happens? The loyalists hang everyone whose name appears—collaborators first, then the families, then anyone who ever shook the wrong hand. It won't be justice. It will be arithmetic. A list of the dead, checked twice." He crouched slightly, bringing himself level with Ivo, voice dropping into something almost intimate. "Burn it, and the war ends next spring either way. Keep it, and it ends next spring too—but with ditches behind the barracks full of people who only ever wanted to be left alone."

Ivo had rehearsed arguments against this man for three days. Standing there, he found he had none left. Because Sorren was nearly right. That was the trap of him. He was a good man defending an unforgivable thing with unforgivable competence, and every word he said landed in Ivo's chest like a coin dropped down a well.

Mirei's hand was still in his. Small. Cold. Certain.

"That's her mother's handwriting," Ivo said quietly. "In the margins. Did you know that?"

Something flickered across Sorren's face—the first honest thing he'd shown. "I knew Lena," he said. "She was better than both of us."

"She was better than all of us," Ivo agreed. "And she died carrying this. So no. I don't think I'll burn it."

Sorren exhaled through his nose, slow, and stood, and became again the pragmatist, the grey man, the almost-right. "Then I'm sorry," he said, and he sounded it. "Vassmuth. Give it to me here, or my colleagues from the capital take it off you at the border, along with everything else, including your fingerprints and your teeth. I am offering you the gentle version. I don't always get to offer it."

From outside came a sound like the sky cracking open.

Not a crack this time—a *rend*, long and rising, running under the floorboards of the hut and out across the whole white world, and the old soldier said, very calmly, "Off the ice. Off the ice *now*," and then everything happened at once.

The young soldier turned toward the window. Sorren moved—fast, far faster than a man in a clean grey coat had any right to move—and his hand closed on the oilskin packet as Ivo pulled it from his coat. Ivo let him take it.

He watched the surprise bloom on Sorren's face, and he let himself smile, and it was not a kind smile, and he was glad of that.

"Take it," Ivo said. "Read it. Memorize it if you like. It doesn't matter anymore."

"What are you talking about?"

"I'm a courier," Ivo said. "Do you know what a courier actually is? Not the bag. Not the packet. The courier is the thing that can't be taken off you at a checkpoint." He tapped his own temple, once, hard, like knocking on a door. "Nine provinces. Forty thousand names. The dates, the sums, the signatures—including yours, Sorren, page thirty-one, you initialled the Gellbruck disbursement and I've seen your hand enough times this week to forge it drunk. It's all up here. It's been up here since the mill. The paper was just for the tribunals. For the historians who like their evidence cold."

The colour drained out of Sorren's face in the stove-light, and Ivo understood, with a lurch of pity he had not expected, that he had broken something in the man—not his plan, but his premise. Sorren had believed, all this time, that he was chasing paper. Paper could burn. Paper could be bargained for. Paper was a finite, manageable evil.

A man's memory was none of those things.

"You'll be killed for it," Sorren said. "Every tribunal, every purge—you understand that. You've made yourself a target for the rest of your life."

"Yes," Ivo said. "I finally matter. It's very inconvenient."

The river chose that moment to end the argument.

The ice field beyond the platform shifted—all of it, half a mile of frozen river moving as one animal—and the chain between the poles snapped taut and sang, and through the window Ivo saw the far channel open: black water, steaming faintly in the cold, widening even as he watched. Between here and there, the floes were already drifting apart, grinding, tilting, plates of white the size of chapel roofs riding a current that had waited all winter for this.

"The ferry's gone," the old soldier said. He pointed with his chin at the mooring, where the boat had been crushed against the pilings and was sinking with terrible dignity. "Nothing crosses after first thaw. That's the rule." He looked at Ivo, and in his eyes was neither mercy nor cruelty, only the exhausted arithmetic of a man who had enforced stupid rules for forty years. "Rules say stay on the bank."

"Rules also said nothing about a seven-year-old," Ivo said, and opened the door onto the wind.

The cold hit like a wall of glass. Out on the ice the wind ran free, straight down the channel from the mountains, and it took his breath and gave it back sharpened. Mirei stood beside him at the platform's edge, hair whipping, and looked at the drifting floes the way other children looked at a street to cross.

"It's like stepping stones," she said. "Only they move."

"Only they move," Ivo said. "So we wait for them to stop moving. And then we run."

Behind them, in the doorway of the hut, Sorren stood with the oilskin packet in his hands. He wasn't following. The young soldier was shouting something about orders; the old soldier was shouting back; and Sorren simply stood, grey coat snapping in the wind, looking at the packet as though it had betrayed him personally. Then he tore it open, held the pages to the wind, and began—to Ivo's astonishment—to feed them one by one into the storm. White leaves scattering over black water. Burning would have been quicker. This was slower, and deliberate, and it looked, from a distance, like grief.

The last page left his fingers and he raised his eyes and met Ivo's across fifty feet of dying winter.

For a moment, neither of them moved.

Then Sorren stepped off the platform onto the ice.

"Go!" Ivo shouted, meaning it in ways he couldn't untangle—"go back, don't follow, you'll die"—but the wind ate the words, and Sorren was coming anyway, fast and sure-footed across the near floes, and whether he meant to take the child, or take Ivo, or simply could not bear to let the river be the only witness, Ivo never learned. There was no time to learn anything.

"Mirei. Now."

They ran.

The first floe held. The second tilted as they landed, and Mirei went down on one knee and came up without crying, which frightened Ivo more than screaming would have. The third had a seam across it, breathing water. He threw her; she caught his hands; he hauled himself after. Behind them the ice spoke constantly now, a language of complaint and collapse, and ahead the channel widened and narrowed and widened, black water chewing at the edges of everything.

Halfway across, Ivo looked back.

Sorren was twenty yards behind, and losing. He had chosen heavier floes, safer ones, and safety was betraying him—the gap between his route and theirs opening foot by foot. He stopped, at the very centre of the river, on a raft of ice alone in all that rushing dark, and he looked at the water around him, and then up at Ivo, and his face was calm. That was the unbearable part. He was not afraid. He looked like a man doing sums, and arriving, at last, at a total he had always known was coming.

He called something. The wind stole most of it. What reached Ivo was a fragment—"the girl remembers"—and then the floe beneath Sorren rose, groaned, split down its spine, and folded him into the river without ceremony, the way winter folds up its own bedclothes. The grey coat bloomed once on the black water, spreading like ink, like a map of a country being erased. Then nothing. The current took even the paper it had carried.

Mirei was tugging his sleeve. "Ivo. The big one. Go."

He went. He did not look back again. Later he would try to feel something defined about Sorren's death—triumph, horror, absolution—and find instead only a strange, stubborn image: a man feeding pages to the wind, gentle as a farmer sowing. He had been almost right. Almost right was still drowning distance from the far shore.

The last floe ground against the bank, and Ivo lifted Mirei down onto solid earth, and then simply stood there, hands shaking, breath tearing, alive on the wrong side of a river he had spent nine days believing was the edge of his life.

Behind them, Vessany burned on, quietly, historically, without them.

Mirei pulled the compass from her coat and held it up. Its needle swung, settled, and pointed—not north, but onward, inland, toward the passes, toward whatever Lena had hidden at the end of it.

"He knew," she said softly. "About you remembering. He called it out. Why?"

Ivo watched the needle tremble, steady, point.

"Because it means burning the ledger bought him nothing," Ivo said. "And it means, little one, that somewhere ahead of us, your mother left something worth remembering for."

He shouldered the pack. The refugee road began at the tree line—a thin brown thread already dotted with carts and coats and people pretending, like him, to be nobody.

"Come on," he said. "We're expected."
