## 1. The Weight of Sealed Paper

There was an art to being forgettable, and Xanthe Thornbury had mastered it the way other people mastered pianos or languages — through years of daily, deliberate practice.

Forgettable began with the coat. Grey wool, once a soldier's, cut for a taller man so that it hung on Xanthe like a sack, swallowing the lines of their body. It had three patches and a mended seam at the shoulder, all of them visible, none of them interesting. The buttons didn't match. That mattered. A coat with mismatched buttons belonged to someone who couldn't afford to care, and people who couldn't afford to care were beneath notice.

Forgettable continued with the walk — not hurried, never hurried, because hurrying drew the eye — but purposeful, the walk of someone going where they were allowed to go. Shoulders slightly rounded. Chin down but not cowed. Eyes forward, catching nothing, reflecting everything.

And forgettable ended with the hands, which were always busy. Hands that were busy gave the eyes somewhere else to look. Today they were winding a scarf, badly, the wool twisting into loops that a person would have to be very bored indeed to watch for long. Under the scarf's shadow, pressed flat against Xanthe's ribs between lining and wool, lay eleven sheets of paper.

Eleven sheets. Eleven codes. Eleven threads of a republic unraveling, and every one of them sewn against Xanthe's heartbeat.

The morning was white and still, the kind of winter morning that made Vessany look already surrendered — snow lying unshoveled along the canal rails, the fountain in Corvel Square wearing a hood of ice like an old woman in mourning crepe. The occupation flags hung from the customs house, grey-orange-grey, too damp to snap in the windless cold. Soldiers stood at the corners in pairs, breath rising from them in slow columns. They had stopped checking papers weeks ago; now they simply watched, and their watching had a new quality to it. Not vigilance. Inventory. The way a man looks over a house he has already decided to buy.

Xanthe wound the scarf another useless turn and joined the flow of bodies crossing the square toward the market.

The refugees had been coming through Vessany since autumn, first in family clusters with too much luggage, then in single files with almost nothing, and now in a steady braided stream that no one remarked on anymore. The city had absorbed them the way sand absorbs water — unevenly, leaving dark stains. They slept in the tram depot and the undercroft of Saint Aurelia's and, since the shelling started upriver, in the corridors of the hospital itself. To move among them was to become one more shape in the crowd, which was precisely why Xanthe moved among them whenever a delivery required crossing open ground. A courier walking alone is a courier. A courier inside a river of the displaced is weather.

"Move along," said one of the soldiers, though no one had stopped.

Everyone moved along. It cost nothing to obey and might cost everything not to.

The market had retreated up the hill as the front came down the river, so that now it gathered in the churchyard of Saint Ferren, stalls wedged between leaning headstones, and sold a shrinking inventory at climbing prices: potatoes with frost-cracked skins, turnips, a bitter green called lamb's tongue that no one had eaten before the war and everyone ate now. There was no bread before ten and none after noon. There was no coal at all.

Xanthe did not go to the market. Not yet. First, the pharmacy on Tanner's Row, where a card of chalk marks on the doorframe told those who needed to know that the man inside could still be trusted, and that two of his suppliers had been arrested, and that he was frightened — each mark its own small sentence in a grammar only couriers read.

Inside smelled of alcohol and dried licorice root. Herr Vogel stood behind the counter with the particular stillness of a man who had spent four years deciding which of his customers were safe.

"The cough syrup," Xanthe said, low. "For my mother."

"There is no cough syrup."

"Herr Vogel—"

"There is no *cough syrup*." He said it louder, for the benefit of anyone listening, then reached beneath the counter and set a brown bottle on the wood without ceremony, covering it with a folded newspaper as though it were obscene. "There is honey. There is thyme. There is goose fat, if you can believe it, for chest plasters. And there is this, which is what your mother has been taking, diluted, because I will not sell her full doses when I cannot promise more. You understand? Diluted, so it lasts. Now you will take it whole, and she will have it undiluted, and we will both pretend the arithmetic works."

"What happens when it runs out?"

Vogel looked at Xanthe with the weary patience of the old for the young. "What happens to any of us when anything runs out. Next question."

Xanthe counted out coins. Vogel watched the money the way starving men watch food — not covetously, just counting, tallying the world's diminishing. Then, as Xanthe tucked the bottle into an inner pocket, the pharmacist's voice dropped further still.

"They took the Marlowe boy yesterday. From the queue. No reason given. His mother stood in the snow for two hours asking why, and then she stopped asking."

"I heard."

"You hear everything, I imagine." It wasn't quite an accusation. In occupied cities, knowing things without appearing to know them was a currency, and Vogel had guessed long ago that Xanthe was rich in it. "Be careful on the quay road. They've doubled the patrols since Sunday. Whatever you're carrying—" he raised a hand before Xanthe could deny anything, "—I don't want to know it. But carry it carefully."

"Always," Xanthe said, and meant it more than Vogel could know.

* * *

The codes had been sewn in by lamplight two nights earlier, in the back room on Chandler Street, while Tam slept sitting up in a chair because lying flat made him feel — he said — like cargo. Xanthe had worked with a curved needle and waxed thread, stitching each folded sheet flat against the lining panel over the ribs, left side, where the coat would bulge least against a body searched by hands expecting weapons or contraband food, not paper. Paper was supposed to travel in satchels. Paper was supposed to declare itself. That was the point of the new protocols: the occupiers had learned to respect documents, so documents had to stop looking like themselves.

Eleven sheets. Most were routine, if any of it could be called routine anymore — naval cipher rotations, a re-address list for ministries already scattered to three provincial towns, a personal letter from a deputy minister to his wife that Xanthe was paid nothing to carry but carried anyway because the man had wept openly while dictating it and Xanthe could not find it in themselves to refuse. But two sheets were different. Two sheets Xanthe had not been allowed to read, sealed in wax stamped with a signet Xanthe didn't recognize, which was itself unusual, because usually the handler let Xanthe see enough to lie convincingly about the contents.

"This one goes to the Admiralty liaison on the quay," the handler had said, tapping the larger seal. "This one to the signatory at the Hotel Meridian. Hand to hand. No drops, no intermediaries. If you are taken, you will say you found the coat. Do you understand? You found the coat hanging in a tram depot. You put it on because you were cold. You are a refugee from Dornholt who has never been inside a ministry in your life."

"I understand."

"Say it back."

"I found the coat."

The handler had studied Xanthe's face then with an expression that Xanthe had filed away and only lately come to understand: it was the look of a man memorizing something he expected to lose. Whatever was in those sealed sheets, it had frightened him. And Deputy Handler Corvin Emmerich was not a man who frightened easily; Xanthe had seen him take tea during an artillery barrage and comment unfavorably on the quality of the biscuits.

Now, walking the long way to the quay road with medicine cooling against Xanthe's breastbone opposite the warm weight of the state's last secrets, Xanthe turned the fear over like a coin, examining both faces of it. Fear of being caught — that was familiar, almost comfortable, a working tool honed by use. But underneath it, newer and less manageable, was the other fear: that it no longer mattered whether Xanthe was caught. That the eleven sheets were last rites. That the republic they served had died some weeks ago in a signing room far upriver, and everyone in Vessany — the soldiers inventorying corners, the pharmacist rationing hope, the mother diluting her own medicine — was simply waiting for the paperwork to catch up.

Snow began again, fine and dry, ticking against the coat shoulders of the crowd. Somewhere beyond the river, artillery spoke twice, a sound the city had learned to sort into near and far the way sailors sort clouds. Far, today. Everyone's shoulders loosened by a degree anyway.

* * *

Chandler Street again by early afternoon, taking the back stairs because the front stoop faced the square and the square had eyes. Their rooms were two above a shuttered chandler's shop — rope and tar smell baked permanently into the plaster, which Mother said reminded her of her father's boatyard and therefore of better decades, and so she refused to complain about it even when the damp got into her chest.

She was propped on pillows by the window, which was the only place she could breathe comfortably, doing what she always did: watching the street and cataloguing it. She had been a harbor pilot's daughter and then a pilot's wife until the occupiers interned the pilots, and she had the habit of people who had spent their lives reading water — she read streets the same way, noting currents, depths, hazards.

"You went the long way," she said.

"The short way was crowded."

"Mm." She took the bottle from Xanthe's hands, held it to the window light, read the label, and read — Xanthe was certain — the truth behind the label. "Diluted."

"He's stretching it. It's sensible."

"It's arithmetic," Mother said. "Sensible would be a doctor. sensible left in September with the ministry trains." She dosed herself with a precision that had once measured tides, then looked at her child for a long moment with those pale river-colored eyes. "You're carrying again."

"I'm resting."

"Don't insult us both. Your hands are wrong."

Xanthe looked down. The hands had gone still, finally, the scarf abandoned — and that was the tell, of course. Busy hands were camouflage; still hands were confession. Xanthe curled them around the empty bottle deliberately and resumed the appearance of idleness.

"There's talk at the market," Mother said, settling back. "That the armistice terms are drafted. Signed within the fortnight. That after signing, the borders close — properly close, not this dribble of checkpoints. Nobody out after that unless the occupiers say so." She paused. "Tam heard it too. He's been counting our tin like a miser ever since. Scrap metal prices are up because everyone's melting things, apparently. He's made enough for a month of potatoes."

"He shouldn't be trading alone."

"He shouldn't be living in a besieged city at fourteen, and yet." Mother's cough came then, deep and rattling, and she rode it out with her jaw set, refusing assistance, refusing even the pillow Xanthe moved toward her. When it passed she was smaller somehow, and grayer, and she smiled to cover it, which was the family habit — Xanthe hid behind forgetfulness, Tam behind fury, Mother behind cheer, and together they made a complete disguise. "I'll outlast the republic. Stubbornness is heritable."

From the doorway behind Xanthe, a voice said, "Outlast what?"

Tam came in sideways, snow in his hair, hauling a flour sack that clinked. Fourteen, all elbows and grievance, with a scab on his knuckle and the particular swagger of a boy who has recently discovered that the world will pay cash for nerve. He dumped the sack by the stove — copper fittings, brass valve wheels, a ship's lamp with the glass cracked — and began sorting it into grades with quick expert hands.

"Copper's up," he said. "Brass is holding. Zinc's dead. If the navy scuttles the fleet like everyone's saying, there'll be salvage for a year. I'm getting in before the occupiers' men do."

"If they scuttle the fleet," Xanthe said, "it means it's over."

"It means there's metal." Tam didn't look up from his sorting. That was new, too — the not-looking-up. He used to watch Xanthe constantly, hungry for attention; now he rationed it like Vogel rationed medicine. "It means there's work. Somebody's got to think past the end of things."

"Somebody does," Mother said mildly, and the look she gave Xanthe over Tam's bent head was brief and heavy and gone.

They ate barley and lamb's tongue soup, all three of them, and Tam talked about salvage routes and a dock clerk who owed him, and Mother nodded in the right places, and Xanthe sat inside the conversation the way they sat inside the coat — present, participating, sealed. Twice Xanthe caught themself listening past it all: to the distant guns keeping their far-off time, to the boots in the square below falling into the rhythm of men who expected to stay, to the small wet sound of Mother breathing, which had developed, over the winter, a faint catch at the top of each inhale that no syrup on Tanner's Row would fix.

Tomorrow there would be a delivery to the Admiralty liaison on the quay road, past the doubled patrols. Tomorrow, the sealed sheets would leave Xanthe's ribs and enter whatever machinery was winding down the republic. Tonight there was soup, and Tam's voice, and the catch in Mother's breath, and the lamplight making its small stubborn circle against a window full of gathering dark.

It was nearly nine when the knock came — soft, precise, three raps in the pattern of no one they knew.

Tam's head came up from his ledger of metals. Mother's eyes opened fully for the first time all evening. And Xanthe, moving quietly, felt the eleven sheets press against their heart like a held breath as they crossed the room and lifted the latch.

On the landing stood a narrow man in a civic coat, holding a leather folio and a clipboard, with a badge on a chain around his neck that caught the lamplight. Snow dusted his shoulders. His face was mild, his eyes were not.

"Good evening," he said pleasantly. "Census taker. Gideon Hale. May I ask you a few questions about the household?"
