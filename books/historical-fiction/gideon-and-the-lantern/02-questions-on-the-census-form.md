## 2. Questions on the Census Form

The morning Gideon Hale came to Salt Row, frost had painted the window glass of the census office in ferns, and the clerk on duty had burned his fingers trying to thaw the ink bottle over the stove. Gideon had simply taken the bottle from him, held it under his arm inside his coat for ten minutes while he reviewed his route, and returned it flowing. The clerk thanked him as if he'd performed a kindness rather than an arithmetic of patience. People thanked Gideon a great deal, mostly because he never gave them reason not to.

He collected his ledger — the heavy one, bound in government blue with the republic's seal stamped in silver that was flaking to gray — and checked his satchel: pens, blotting paper, a folding rule, the printed forms in their numbered sequence, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth where no clerk would ever think to look, a second notebook that belonged to nothing official at all. He buttoned his coat over it the way other men buttoned coats over their hearts.

Salt Row occupied the crook of the harbor like a beggar in a doorway: narrow houses leaning shoulder to shoulder, laundry frozen on lines, chimneys smoking thin because coal had become currency and currency had become rumor. It was a good district for counting. Bad districts were good districts, in Gideon's experience, because fear made people answer doors they might otherwise have kept shut, and desperation made them tell you things just to hear a voice respond.

The war had taught him this, though his war had been fought with pencils rather than rifles. Three years he had worked the eastern districts during the border relocations, tallying households that existed on Monday and not on Thursday, recording names that would later be read aloud in rooms he was never invited into. He told himself, in those first months, that he was only a recorder. That the ledger did nothing. That numbers were inert until someone else picked them up.

By the second year he had stopped telling himself anything. It was easier to count.

* * *

The first door of the day belonged to a widow named Ostrey who kept goats illegally in her yard and answered his knock with a poker in her hand. Gideon noted the poker, smiled the smile he kept for the elderly — unhurried, faintly apologetic, the smile of a man who wished he were anywhere else — and asked his questions.

"Persons resident as of the first of the month?"

"Just me."

"Any boarders, lodgers, or persons sleeping on the premises not registered to the household?"

"No."

Her eyes went, briefly, to the shed. Goats do not knock. He wrote *1 resident, sole occupancy* and did not look at the shed, and when she closed the door he heard the bolt go and then, faintly, the bleating of something she would deny to her grave. He left it out of the ledger. There were things worth more than accuracy, and anyway, the ministry had stopped paying for accuracy in anything but bodies.

That was the phrase the new forms used now, since the autumn directives: *bodies for provisioning purposes.* As though language could be laundered by printing it in a serif font. Gideon filled in the small boxes with a steady hand. Bodies, ages, origins, departures. Who is home. Who has fled. Who lies.

He was very good at the third category. It was, in fact, the only part of the job still worth doing well — but not for the ministry's reasons, and he kept that distinction locked in the oilcloth notebook, which recorded not what the forms asked but what the forms implied. Which households had emptied suddenly. Which had gained strangers. Which doors had opened too quickly or too slowly. Which names on the rolls matched faces that flinched.

He did not know yet what he was keeping it for. A record wants a purpose the way water wants a slope; it will find one eventually, or make one.

* * *

By midmorning the fog off the harbor had thickened to wool. Gideon worked down Copper Lane, then the tanners' court, then the long crooked run of Salt Row itself, where the houses were older and the doors thinner and every third window held a candle stub burning behind lace gone gray — old superstition, a light in the window to guide the dead home, revived lately by people who wanted guidance for the living and had nowhere official to ask.

At number eleven a man pretended not to be home, breathing audibly through a gap in the shutters. Gideon marked the house *no response, second visit required,* and moved on, and the breathing behind the shutters followed him half a lane in his imagination. At number fourteen, a family of six had become a family of two since the autumn roll; the eldest son was in the naval reserve, the mother said, and the daughters sent north to an aunt. Gideon wrote it all down in the correct boxes and then, in the hidden notebook, in his small compressed hand: *Daughters not seen at northern registry. Aunt's address matches a warehouse. Ask again differently.*

He knocked at number seventeen near noon, because the light was flattening and the fog was lifting into a fine cold rain, and because number seventeen was on his list twice.

Once as the Thornbury residence — three registered occupants, one flagged.

And once, in the oilcloth book, in an entry written by another hand entirely, pressed under his door in the night four days ago: *Thornbury. Ask about births outside the district.*

* * *

The woman who opened the door was perhaps nineteen, perhaps twenty-five — the war had flattened everyone's age into ranges — with dark hair pinned up carelessly and flour on one wrist, as though he had interrupted bread rather than the far less domestic business of surviving. She had the particular stillness he had learned to read in the eastern districts: not calm, but readiness. A person standing in a doorway the way a fencer stands on a piste.

"Miss Thornbury," he said. "Gideon Hale, Ministry of Records, district enumeration." He showed the badge. Everyone looked at badges the same way — a flick toward it, quick, assessing whether it was armor or weapon — and this one assessed faster than most. "I'm completing the winter roll for your household."

"We answered the autumn roll." Her voice was pleasant. Her voice was extremely pleasant. That alone was information; nobody's natural voice was that evenly pitched at their own front door.

"The winter roll supersedes it. May I come in? It's raining, and I'll only keep you a few minutes." He gestured at the sky with genuine mildness. The rain was real. Most of his sincerity was real; that was what made it useful.

She hesitated exactly as long as a careful person would, then stood aside. The room beyond was clean and poor and warm, a stove going despite the coal crisis, which meant either connections or hoarding, and he logged both possibilities without letting his eyes linger anywhere long enough to be caught lingering. A boy sat at the table doing something with wire and pliers — the younger brother, Tam, twelve or thirteen, registered, unflagged — and looked up with the flat hostile stare adolescents reserve for authority, then back down at his work. In the back room, through a half-open door, someone coughed. Deeply. Twice.

"Your mother?" Gideon asked.

"Resting." The word arrived a shade too fast.

He set his ledger on the table, uncapped his pen, and began, methodically, reading back the autumn entries. Names. Ages. Occupations. Xanthe Thornbury, courier, licensed, Ministry contract, stamp number recited without hesitation — recited, he noticed, not remembered; there is a difference in the mouth between a fact and a performance of a fact. He wrote *confirmed* beside each line. The boy's wire work clicked softly. The coughing had stopped, which meant she was listening, which meant she had been listening the whole time.

Then Gideon turned to a fresh page, and asked the question that was on no form in the republic.

"And household members born outside the district?"

The click of the wire work stopped.

It lasted less than a second. If he had been watching the boy he'd have missed it; he was watching the girl, so he caught both — the stillness spreading through the room like frost across glass, the fractional pause before she said, "Born outside? What does the birthplace matter for provisioning?"

"It doesn't," Gideon agreed pleasantly. "It matters for the residency supplement. Persons born within Greater Vessany proper qualify for the winter fuel allocation at the district rate. Born outside, the rate differs." He let the pen hover. "It's new. Since the directive in November. I apologize for the confusion — half my forms come back wrong and I have to walk the rounds twice."

The lie was smooth and nearly true; there *was* a November directive, and it *did* concern supplements, and none of that had anything to do with why he was asking. He watched her decide. He had learned, in the east, that you can see people choose between truths the way you can watch them choose between doors — the eyes go somewhere first, always, a quarter-second before the body follows.

"Our grandmother," she said. "Mother's mother. Born in Corvess, before the annexation. But she died years ago, so I don't suppose it counts."

"Deceased persons fall off the roll, yes." He wrote it down. Corvess pre-annexation was harmless, common, unfalsifiable, exactly the kind of answer a trained liar selects. Too good. He wrote, in the margin of his own mind: *supplied a decoy. means there's something to decoy.*

"And your parents? Both born in the district?"

"Our father was. Our mother—" the cough started again behind the door, and she raised her voice slightly to cover it, "—our mother was born here too, in Harrowgate, the harbor parish. Before it merged."

Harrowgate had merged into the district nine years ago; anyone born there was born in-district by every definition the ministry used. The answer was correct, complete, and delivered with the cadence of rehearsed testimony. Gideon smiled and blotted the page.

"Thank you. You've been very helpful, Miss Thornbury."

"Will there be a fuel supplement, then?" the boy asked, sudden, hungry, guileless where his sister was armored. "With the rates?"

"If you qualify," Gideon said gently, and lied to him about when the forms would be processed, because the boy's face when he asked was the face of every child in every district he had ever counted, and some habits of mercy are harder to break than others.

At the door, he paused, as if remembering something trivial. "One more thing. Your courier license — Ministry contracts require annual renewal at the central office, but the central office relocated last month. You may want to confirm your stamp is still valid. Things fall through cracks, in times like these." He tipped his hat. "Good day."

He felt her eyes between his shoulders all the way down the street.

* * *

He did not leave immediately. That was the discipline of it — never flee a scene you've disturbed; stand in it. He walked to the corner, consulted his ledger openly like any honest functionary finding his place, and used the reflection in a dark shopfront window to watch the Thornbury door.

It stayed shut a full minute. Then it opened a hand's width — not the girl, the boy — and the boy looked up the street, both directions, with a thoroughness no child uses for checking weather. Then it shut again.

Gideon turned the corner into Tanner's Walk and let out a breath he had been holding longer than he liked to admit.

There were two explanations for what he'd just seen, and he turned them over like coins, testing the weight.

Explanation one: the Thornburys were what they appeared — a courier, a scrap-collecting boy, a sick mother — and merely frightened, as everyone was, and the question about births had startled them the way any question startles people now. Frightened households behaved exactly this way. Nine households in ten would have shown him the same minute of silence behind a shut door.

Explanation two lived in smaller details. Flour on the wrist of a woman whose kitchen smelled of no baking. Coal burning freely in a house with no visible means for it. Ink — he had seen it as she took back her brother's registration card, a smudge along the side of her right hand, violet-black, the specific fugitive dye used in ministry dispatch seals, the ink that was supposed to be invisible by morning and often wasn't on cold hands. He had carried enough sealed dispatches himself, in another life, another uniform, to know that stain the way a priest knows incense.

A licensed courier with dispatch ink on her fingers, in a household that lied smoothly about questions no one should have needed to lie about, flagged in his own secret book by a hand he did not know, asking him to ask precisely the question he had asked.

Someone wanted the Thornbury door tested. Someone wanted to know what rattled.

The rain thickened. Gideon stood under a broken awning, protected his notebook with his body, and wrote:

*17 Salt Row. Girl answers like an interrogator. Boy honest, therefore dangerous. Mother present, ill, listening. Dispatch ink on courier's hand. Question put as instructed. Result: all three lied. Conclusion —*

He stopped. His pen hovered. Conclusion depended entirely on which side of the question he was working for, and he had spent three years refusing to say aloud, even to himself, which side that was.

Finally he wrote: *Conclusion pending. Watch.*

* * *

In the evening the census office smelled of wet wool and sealing wax. Gideon filed his day's returns with the clerk, who stamped them without reading them, which was standard, which was the entire tragedy of the republic in miniature: the records were meticulous and no one read them, until suddenly someone did, and then it was too late for everyone whose name was spelled correctly.

He collected his coat. He walked out into the dark, past the shuttered shops, around the edge of Miller's Square, where the last daylight-gray light still held in the western sky above the rooftops.

From the square's north corner you could see Salt Row, and the crooked spine of its roofs, and — he counted without meaning to, the way he counted everything — seventeen windows lit against the dark. One of them burned with a small steady flame behind gray lace. Old custom. A light to guide someone home.

He stood there longer than a census taker had any reason to, in the cold, with the rain turning to snow, watching a single candle in a single window in a city that had perhaps six weeks left to be a city at all.

Then he took out the oilcloth notebook, and beneath the entry for 17 Salt Row, in his small compressed hand, he added a line that appeared in no file, served no ministry, and answered to nothing but the debt he carried:

*Whatever she's carrying — find out before the others do.*

Across the square, unseen, the boy was scrubbing at something white on their doorframe.

Gideon didn't know it yet. But in four days, when he passed that way again and found the chalk mark gone — the mark he hadn't drawn — he would understand that the household at number seventeen had begun testing him right back.
