## 3. A Lie Told Well

The knock came at half past nine, which was wrong. Census men came in the morning, when the light through the door's fan window showed them a household whole and honest and half-asleep. Anyone who knocked after the second bell had stopped being a census man and started being something else.

Xanthe set down the darning needle, threaded it through the coat lining where they'd left off, and counted to six before answering. Six seconds: enough to seem unhurried, not enough to seem like someone who needed time.

Through the peephole — a nail hole drilled during the border troubles two winters back — they saw a tall man with a leather satchel and a slate-gray coat gone green at the cuffs. He wore the census office's brass badge on his lapel, but he wore it like a man wearing a borrowed name. His face was long and quiet and careful, the kind of face that would be easy to describe badly later, on purpose. Gideon Hale, the paper at the ministry said. District Four. Round of the Thornbury household scheduled for Tuesday. It was Thursday.

Xanthe opened the door three fingers' width, the chain still on.

"You're late for your round," they said. Not accusing. Observing, the way anyone would observe weather.

"Late for everything this month." The man's voice was low and even, a voice used to being written down. "Gideon Hale. Census. I know it's Thursday. The queue at the coal office ate my Monday and Tuesday both, and Wednesday the bridge was closed for the artillery movement. You heard the artillery."

Everyone had heard the artillery. It came up the river valley at night now, a soft industrial coughing, like some vast animal digesting the war.

"We're registered," Xanthe said. "My mother did the forms herself. She does all our paperwork — she doesn't trust me with it."

That was good. A small confession, self-deprecating, the sort of thing invisible people said to make themselves briefly human and therefore forgettable. Xanthe watched it land and watched it not land. The man's eyes had already moved past their shoulder into the room, cataloguing with the speed of habit: the stove, cold because coal was rationed to nothing; the medicine bottles on the sill; the mending basket; the coat over the chair back, mid-repair, its lining turned out.

His eyes rested on the coat one beat longer than eyes should rest on anything.

"May I come in? It's five questions. Ten if you argue."

"I don't argue."

"Then five."

* * *

He sat where Xanthe put him, on the hard chair by the window, and did not look around again, which was itself a kind of answer. Men who wanted to see a room looked. Men who already knew what was in a room didn't need to. Xanthe took the opposite chair and folded their hands in their lap, right hand over left, ink-stained knuckles down, and began the small warm work of lying.

"Household members present?"

"Three. Myself, my mother, my brother. Tam's eleven."

"Your mother's condition?"

"She'd tell you it's the damp. Her doctor tells her it's her lungs. Neither of them wins that argument." A flicker of a smile; let him see a family, ordinary, worn smooth by winter like every other family in Vessany. "She's sleeping. If you need her signature I can wake her, but she'll be cross about it, and you'll have earned a sick woman's temper over a form."

"No signature needed for presence." He wrote something — or made the motion of writing something; Xanthe couldn't see the page. "Occupation?"

"Clerk. Textile import house on Chandler's Row. Or what's left of it. The ships stopped coming in October."

"And before that?"

There it was. The first question that wasn't on any census form Xanthe had ever seen, delivered flat as a coin laid on a counter. *Before that.* As though occupation were a wound and he wanted the history of it.

"Same house," Xanthe said pleasantly. "Junior clerk, then clerk. I'm hoping for senior clerk when the war's over, but I try not to hope about things that far off. Do you?"

Something crossed his face — not amusement, exactly. Recognition, maybe, of another performer recognizing the stage.

"That wasn't one of my five questions," he said.

"No," Xanthe agreed. "It was mine."

He almost smiled. Then: "Household members born outside the district?"

And there it was, plain as a knife on a bread board. Xanthe kept their face doing what faces do when asked a boring question — the mild recalculation, the polite search of memory — while behind it every alarm they owned went off at once, silent and total, like a flooded engine room sealing its doors.

There was no such question. Xanthe had carried enough government paper to wallpaper a chapel; they knew the census schedule the way priests know liturgy. Household size. Ages. Occupations. Fuel sources. Livestock, absurdly, still, after two years of nobody owning livestock. But not birthplace. Birthplace questions had been struck from the schedule the same week the border districts were struck from the map — struck because the answer had become dangerous, because half of Vessany was born somewhere that no longer officially existed.

So why ask it?

"My mother," Xanthe said slowly, as if consulting real memory, which was the trick, always the trick — never invent, only rearrange. "Born in Delver's Hollow, but that's District Nine now, or it was until they redrew it. Does a redraw count? She'd say she was born in Vessany her whole life and Delver's Hollow was just where Vessany kept her."

"And your father?"

"Our father died when Tam was four." True. Let the truth do some work; it was the strongest material available and cost nothing here. "I don't remember much about his papers. Mother keeps them in the cedar box with the deeds."

"Mm." He wrote. This time Xanthe caught the angle of the page and saw that he was writing very little — a mark, maybe two. A man keeping a tally rather than a record. "Your brother. Born here?"

"Chandler Row, above the shop. The midwife was a Mrs. Ostrova, if you need a witness. She may be across the water by now."

"Most people will be across the water soon." He closed his ledger, and the small sound of it — the covers meeting, the elastic snapping around — sounded to Xanthe like a cell door taking an interest. He stood. "Thank you. That's my five."

"You counted generously."

"I find people talk more if they think the interview's over." He settled his satchel on his hip. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and turned back — and for a moment the census taker's blandness dropped away entirely, the way a coat slips off a shoulder, and Xanthe saw underneath it something that looked almost like regret, or warning, or both wearing the same clothes.

"One more thing, and then I really am done. Your hands."

Xanthe looked at their hands. They hadn't meant to; the instruction was to never look, looking was confession — but the words pulled their gaze down like a hook, and there it was, unavoidable, lit by the gray window light: the stains. Two fingertips of the left hand, the web of the right thumb, shadowed violet-black, the particular bruise-color of iron gall ink worked deep into the skin's whorls and kept there by days of handling. Clerk's ink sat on top of the skin and washed off Sunday nights. Dispatch ink was different. Dispatch ink was mixed with gum and fixed with heat, and it got into the fingerprints themselves, and everyone who worked with documents knew the difference the way farriers know a horse's mouth.

A courier's hands. Xanthe had scrubbed them that morning with lye and a pumice stone until the fingertips burned, and it hadn't mattered, because it never mattered; the stains lived below the skin's surface, in the layer where the body records what it has done.

"Textile house uses a lot of carbon copies," Xanthe said. "The ink transfers. Everyone in the order room has it."

"Yes," Gideon Hale said. "They do."

He held their eyes for exactly one second too long — not hostile, worse than hostile, *informed* — and then he opened the door and stepped out into the square, and the gray morning took him the way water takes a stone.

* * *

Xanthe stood at the window with the curtain angled open one inch and watched him cross the square. He did not hurry. He noted two other doors — chalked something on the frames, small marks at shoulder height, quick as a man marking laundry — and turned up Rope Walk toward the ministry annex without once looking back, which was the most alarming thing he'd done yet. Guilty men look back. Innocent men look back. Only men following a map don't.

Mother's voice came from the back room, thin and amused. "Was that the census?"

"It was."

"He didn't believe a word you said." A cough, mastered, folded away. "I could hear you through the wall. You were very good. That's how I could tell."

"Go to sleep, Mama."

"I'm composing my deathbed remarks. It passes the time." Another cough, longer this one. When it finished, the silence afterward had a new texture, and Xanthe pressed their forehead against the cold window glass and hated the sound of it with a hatred that had nowhere to go.

Tam came in from the scullery with grease to his elbows, smelling of the scrap heap. "Who was that?"

"Census."

"What did he want?"

"The usual. Who lives here, what we burn, whether we own a goat."

"We don't own a goat."

"Not anymore. War eats goats first."

Tam grinned, and the grin was so much their father's grin that Xanthe had to turn back to the window. Below, in the square, life performed its daytime pretense: women queuing at the pump, a cart of turnips, two militiamen sharing a cigarette with the boredom of men who already knew which side they'd end the war on. Ordinary. Sealed. A city going about the business of pretending it had a future.

And on the Thornbury doorframe, at shoulder height, a small chalk mark that had not been there at breakfast.

Xanthe saw it when they finally let the curtain fall — saw it in memory first, reconstructing the view downward from the window, and then went down and stood in the doorway and confirmed it with their own eyes. A single stroke, angled, with a short tail. Not a symbol Xanthe knew from any cipher book, which meant it belonged to somebody's private system, which meant it meant something specific, and the something specific was them.

Years of work had taught Xanthe the mechanics of being unseen: the forgettable face, the practiced stammer, the hands kept busy and the opinions kept nowhere. Invisibility was a craft, and they were good at it, good enough that their own handler sometimes forgot which city they were in. They had built a life inside that blindness the way snails build inside shells — and now a stranger had walked up with a brass badge and a borrowed list of questions and looked straight through the shell as though it were glass, and marked the door on his way out, patient as a debt collector.

Being seen, it turned out, felt nothing like being known. It felt like cold air on skin that had been covered a long time.

Xanthe scraped the chalk off with their thumbnail and stood holding the white dust, thinking. Options arranged themselves with courier efficiency: report the encounter to the drop box tonight; move the remaining dispatches out of the coat and into the flour tin; warn Mother to be sicker and Tam to be stupider if anyone asked. And beneath the logistics, quieter, an old instinct raising its hand: *find out who he is.* Not run from the lantern — walk toward it, count what it illuminated.

Because here was the arithmetic that wouldn't close. A census taker who asked unlisted questions was either hunting for the ministry or hunting around it. And a man who found a courier and did not arrest the courier was not being kind. He was being *patient*. He wanted something, and he had decided Xanthe was the shortest road to it.

Which meant the next visit was already scheduled somewhere in his careful ledger, and this time it wouldn't be five questions.

Xanthe brushed the chalk dust from their fingers, went upstairs, and began unpicking the stitches from the coat lining with steady hands — while across the square, in a third-floor window of the census annex, a lamp burned late, and a man sat writing not a report but a letter, and under the letter's final line drew, absently, in the same violet-black ink, a small chalk-colored sketch of a lantern.
