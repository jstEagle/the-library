## 4. The Lantern in the Window

The office of the District Census Bureau smelled of cold tallow and other people's lives.

Gideon Hale had learned to read a room by what it kept on its shelves, and this one kept everything: birth rolls bound in cracked leather, death registers stacked sideways like fallen dominoes, ration ledgers, property assessments, the great census books themselves with their ruled columns and their careful, damning entries. Eleven years of Vessany's population, counted and counted and counted again, as if arithmetic could hold a country together. He had entered four thousand names into those books since autumn. He knew, without looking, which of them had already fled, which were dead, and which — like the three Aldermann girls on Candle Street — had never existed at all except as ration allocations invented by a desperate mother with good penmanship.

He lit the desk lamp, turned down the wick so the light fell only on his own hands, and locked the door.

Then he unlocked it again. Old habit. A locked door told a visitor there was something worth locking. An unlocked one told them nothing at all, and Gideon Hale had spent nine years teaching himself to tell people nothing at all. He was, by every measurable standard, an unremarkable man: medium height, medium build, a face that clerks forgot before he reached the street. His badge said CENSUS. His boots said nothing. His handwriting was deliberately, painstakingly ordinary, because a man's hand betrayed him faster than his face — pressure, slant, the little flourishes of pride that survived even when a forger thought he'd killed them.

He took off his coat, folded it over the back of the chair, and sat.

Across the square, through the frost-furred window, the Thornbury house showed one lit window on the second floor. The rest of the façade was dark, shuttered, indistinguishable from the six other narrow houses pressed against it like teeth. But that window burned low and steady behind a curtain the color of old tea, and Gideon had watched it every night for two weeks now the way sailors watch weather.

*You saw me*, he thought. *And I saw you see me.*

He opened the bottom drawer.

* * *

The lantern sat under a false bottom he'd built himself, beneath a stratum of dead paperwork: closed files, pre-war tax disputes, the census supplement of a district that no longer answered roll calls. He lifted it out with both hands and set it on the desk, and the desk lamp caught the brass and threw a slow wheel of light across the ceiling — a lighthouse beam in miniature, turning above him in the dark, and for a moment Gideon closed his eyes and let himself be somewhere else entirely.

The Saltern Point light had gone dark in the first month of the war. He'd been the one to extinguish it — that was the irony he carried, the small stone in his boot that never wore smooth. Orders from the naval ministry: all coastal lights doused, deny the enemy gunners their bearings. He'd climbed the hundred and eleven steps with the keeper, an old man named Elias Wren who had tended that flame for forty-one years, and Gideon had stood by while Elias put his hands on the lamp housing one last time, palms flat, the way you'd touch the shoulder of someone dying.

"Forty-one years," Elias had said. "You know how many boats came home because of this light? I stopped counting at ten thousand. Now count the ones that won't."

Gideon had counted them anyway. He was still counting them.

Elias Wren drowned fourteen months later when the *Meridian* went down in the channel — not a warship, not a munitions run; a refugee transport, overloaded, unlit, steering blind through a snow squall because every lighthouse on the coast was dark by ministerial order. There were men pulled from the water who swore they'd seen a light where no light could be. Gideon chose to believe they'd seen the moon. It was easier than believing in ghosts doing the work the living had been forbidden to do.

The debt began there. Debts like that don't have numbers, but Gideon had found one anyway.

* * *

He unscrewed the lantern's fuel reservoir — cleaned, dry, wick removed — and drew out the oilskin packet inside.

It was not forged. That distinction mattered to him in the way that small distinctions matter to drowning men. Everything else about Gideon Hale was counterfeit: the badge, the authority, the patient clerk's smile he wore like a mask worn so long it had grown into the skin beneath. But the certificate in his hands was genuine — issued by the Ministry of Passage under seal, serial number 4471-C, entitling **the bearer** to one berth aboard any evacuation vessel departing before armistice, priority class, non-transferable. Signed by a deputy minister whose signature Gideon had verified three times against three different documents, because he could not afford for this thing to be flawed. It was, as far as he knew, one of fewer than two hundred such certificates in the entire occupied city, and the only one held by a census taker.

He had paid for it with nine years of accumulated leverage: small falsifications he'd never committed but could prove others believed he had, favors owed by frightened officials, a ledger of sins witnessed rather than sinned. The liaison office's passage clerk had traded it to him in a basement office in November, sweating, certain he was being blackmailed, which he was, though not for anything he'd actually done. Gideon had walked out with the certificate in his coat and vomited into the gutter two streets away, and then straightened up and gone back to work, because that was the arrangement he'd made with himself: he could do one dishonest thing, provided he did it thoroughly.

One berth. One life. Payment for ten thousand he hadn't saved.

The question — the question that had kept him awake in this chair more nights than his bed — was *whose* life.

He turned the certificate in the lamplight. The paper had gone soft at the creases from being unfolded too often. On the line marked BEARER, someone had once written a name in pencil and rubbed it out again so many times the fibers had worn to a pale shine, like a path worn across grass. Gideon knew every ghost that name had briefly been. A dockworker's widow from his rounds, coughing blood into her apron. Two sisters, fifteen and eleven, orphaned in the shelling of the East Terraces. A boy he'd never met whose father had once pulled Gideon from a canal ice when they were both nine years old and who had written to him — actually written, believing the badge — *you're the only man in the government I trust, Gid.*

He had erased each name within hours of writing it. Not because they were unworthy. Because he could not bear to choose, and cowardice dressed as prudence was still cowardice, and he knew that too.

"You're a fraud," he said aloud to the empty office. His voice sounded strange after a day of silence — rusted, smaller than he remembered. "A fraud with a lighthouse."

But the lantern had come to mean something in his hands, some nights. He'd bought it at the scrap market for three coins from a dealer who didn't know what it was — or did, and charged him accordingly. Saltern Point pattern, pre-war casting, the glass clouded but whole. He kept meaning to bury it somewhere, sink it in the canal, and instead he oiled the brass every Sunday and kept it under the floor of his lies like a heart kept beating in a jar.

* * *

He rose and went to the window, standing to one side of it, the way he always stood — a habit from his real work, whatever his real work was these days; even he had stopped being sure. Frost feathered the glass. Beyond it the square lay blue-white under a thin moon, the fountain at its center wearing a hood of ice, the chestnut trees along the north side holding up their bare black arms like men surrendering to nobody.

The Thornbury window still burned.

He'd first marked the house in October, routine rounds, the third week of the census. A household of three: mother, bedridden; daughter, listed as seamstress; son, thirteen, exempted from school by closure. Ordinary on paper. What had snagged him was smaller than paper. The daughter — Xanthe, the register said, an old name, a marsh-country name — had signed the household form with a left-slanting hand, modest loops, nothing memorable. And then six weeks later, on a follow-up visit, Xanthe had signed a ration amendment with a right-slanting hand, different loops, different pressure, a different person's handwriting wearing the same tired coat.

People lied to census takers constantly. They lied about ages and dependents and rooms sublet, and Gideon logged the lies without interest, the way a tide table logs the moon. But nobody changed their handwriting between visits unless they had two hands, two lives, two names — unless they were practiced at being other people. And practice like that didn't come from smuggling butter.

So he had watched. Not as a census taker; the census taker was done with the Thornbury file, closed and shelved. As whatever he was instead.

Tonight the curtain shifted. A silhouette crossed the lamplight — slim, quick, moving with that particular economy he'd come to recognize: no wasted motion, weight always balanced, a body trained out of its own habits. Xanthe paused at the window. For three heartbeats the silhouette stood framed against the glow, head tilted slightly, as if listening to the square itself.

Gideon did not move. Nine years of stillness held him like a hand.

Then the silhouette withdrew, and the light behind the tea-colored curtain dimmed to a coal-red ember, and the house went back to being six houses' twin.

"They know," Gideon murmured to the frost. "That's the trouble. You looked at me today like a man reading a form, and forms are what I am, and you read me anyway."

That afternoon, in the Thornbury parlor, he had asked the question that wasn't on any official form — *any member of the household born outside the district?* — and watched Xanthe's face do something extraordinary: nothing. Absolute, disciplined nothing. A stillness so complete it was itself a confession, because only the guilty and the professionally innocent can achieve it, and no one is professionally innocent.

He had also seen, before he left, the ink on Xanthe's fingers. Violet-black in the creases of the knuckles, the particular stain of ministry dispatch ink, the kind used for documents that mattered, the kind that took three days of scrubbing to fade. Seamstresses didn't get ink there. Seamstress got chalk on the fingertips, needle-pricks on the pads, thread-wear on the thumb.

He should have reported it. One line in the right column: *Occupant displays indicators consistent with courier activity. Recommend surveillance.* He wrote such lines every week. They were his trade, his cover, his contribution to the machinery grinding the city toward its surrender — and every one of them landed somewhere in a ministry that decided, with those words, whether a name became a file and a file became a cell.

Instead he had walked down the Thornbury steps, stood a moment in the wind, and drawn a small mark in chalk on the doorframe where the lintel met the post — a surveyor's mark, meaningless, deniable. A triangle with a bar through it. He'd chosen it from an old harbor code book: *light obscured; proceed with caution.*

A warning. He'd told himself it was a warning, though warnings to whom, about what, he could not have said under oath. Perhaps to whoever read the doorframes of this city — and somebody did; marks appeared and vanished on doors all winter like a silent correspondence conducted in frost. Perhaps to himself. *Light obscured. Proceed with caution. This one you cannot save and should not try.*

* * *

He went back to the desk and sat with the certificate flat before him, and did the arithmetic he did every night, the terrible patient arithmetic that never came out different.

One berth. Three Thornburys.

The mother would not survive a crossing — that was almost certain, the cough he'd heard through the parlor door had a wet iron sound to it, and the sea in winter spared no one. Taking her would likely kill her en route and spend the berth besides. Cold reasoning. He hated himself for the clarity of it, hated the census-taker part of himself that reduced lungs and love to probabilities.

The son, then. Thirteen. Tam. The boy hoarded scrap metal — Gideon had watched him drag a sledge of it through the market twice, haggling like a man three times his age, and there was something in the boy's fierce unlovely competence that reminded Gideon of every kid who'd ever survived a war by refusing to be a child. The boy had the best odds. The boy was also the easiest choice, and easy choices were how cowards laundered their preferences.

Which left the daughter. The courier. The woman with two handwritings and violet ink in her knuckles and a window that burned late over a table where she presumably copied things she would be shot for carrying.

If Gideon reported her, the berth would be wasted on whichever official claimed the credit, and the city would lose whatever she carried — and Gideon suspected, with the low constant dread that had become his baseline hum, that what she carried might matter more than any berth, maybe more than the whole crooked ledger of his double life. The ministries were signing something soon. Everyone below the fourth floor could feel it, the way animals feel weather. Papers moved differently now. Clerks whispered behind their hands. And couriers with forgettable faces were moving through the snow like corpuscles in a dying body, carrying what oxygen remained.

If Gideon did not report her, and she was caught anyway — by the occupiers' intelligence people, who were better at this than anyone and closing in everywhere — then his silence would have purchased nothing, and the certificate would rot in a lantern under a false bottom in an office full of dead names, and Elias Wren's ten thousand would grow by exactly one more.

There was a third option, of course. The one his spine thought about at three in the morning. Give her the berth. Walk across the square, knock, put the oilskin packet in those stained and capable hands, and say: *I know what you are, and I think you know what I am, and here is the only honest thing either of us owns.* Give the debt to her. Let ten thousand drowned souls ride out of the harbor in the body of a courier who might refuse it, or laugh at it, or turn him in to her handlers as a foreign agent — which, technically, by the standards of the republic whose census he falsely took, he was.

He imagined her face receiving that. The disciplined nothing breaking open into something human. He had been imagining it, with increasing specificity and increasing shame, for eleven days, and tonight the imagining had developed a warmth in the chest that felt less like strategy than like sickness, and he was self-aware enough to name it: loneliness, plain and old, the loneliness of a man who has not had an honest conversation since before the war and has begun to mistake surveillance for acquaintance. Watching her window nightly. Knowing her gait, her shopping hours, the scarf she wore looped three times. He was a creep and a spy and a penitent all wearing the same gray coat, and the worst of it was that all three of them were sincere.

The lamp guttered. Outside, far to the east, thunder rolled across a clear frozen sky, and Gideon counted without meaning to — one, two, three, four — artillery, then, out past the river, closer than last week. The war was walking toward the city at its own pace, patient as a census taker, and it would knock on every door eventually, and it did not use chalk.

Gideon folded the certificate along its worn creases, slid it back into the oilskin, fed it down into the lantern's dark belly, and screwed the reservoir home. He set the lantern back beneath the false floor, covered it with the stratum of the dead, and closed the drawer on it gently, like tucking in a child.

Then he took his coat, took his chalk, and let himself out into the square.

The snow squeaked under his boots. The Thornbury window had gone fully dark now, but smoke rose thin and silver from its chimney — someone awake, someone keeping a fire alive in the small hours, someone copying something by stove-light. He crossed halfway, stopped in the shadow of the fountain, and stood looking up at the house with his breath streaming and the chalk a small hard weight in his pocket like a second heart.

Tomorrow was a census day. His route ran down Marsh Lane and ended, by pure administrative coincidence he had arranged himself with three forged schedule amendments, at the Thornbury door.

*Proceed with caution,* the harbor code said. *Light obscured.*

He was done proceeding with caution. Tomorrow he would ask one question more — the question too many — and whatever broke, broke in the open, under both their eyes.

From up the lane, a small figure with a handcart watched him from a doorway, and slipped away before Gideon turned.

But that, Gideon did not see.
