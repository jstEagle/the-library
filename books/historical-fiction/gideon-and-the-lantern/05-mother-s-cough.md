## 5. Mother's Cough

The coal ran out on a Tuesday, and by Thursday the city had begun to lie about the cold.

I heard it in the queues — people saying *it's not so bad, really*, saying *we had winters like this when I was a girl*, their breath pluming white as they said it, their children's knuckles red as raw meat inside gloves worn to gauze. Vessany had always been good at lying to itself. It was practically a civic art. We lied about the war being nearly won, then about the war being nearly over, then about the difference between those two things mattering. Now we lied about the temperature, because the temperature was the one thing the occupiers hadn't thought to requisition yet.

The ration office on Corvane Street had a new sign in the window: COAL ALLOCATIONS SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW. The sign was fresh, the ink still sharp at the edges. *Pending review.* Somewhere in some ministry, some clerk was reviewing whether eleven thousand households deserved to be warm, and I suspected the review would conclude around the same time the armistice did.

I stood in front of that window longer than was safe. Couriers don't linger. Lingering is how faces get remembered, and my whole trade was built on being the kind of face that slid off memory like rain off glass. But I lingered anyway, because the medicine Mother needed came from the apothecary two doors down, and the apothecary heated his tinctures over a coal stove, and no coal meant no heat meant the tincture for her lungs would sit useless in its bottles while she coughed herself thinner by the day.

Inside, the apothecary — Herras, gray-bearded, hands stained amber from thirty years of resin and syrup — was wrapping a parcel with the exaggerated care of a man pretending business was normal.

"Xanthe." He didn't look up. "You're back."

"Twice this month. You should charge me rent."

"I should charge you double, is what I should do." He slid the parcel across, then kept two fingers on it. "The willow bark's gone. Everything from the southern depots is gone. What I have left is what you see, and what you see is half-strength and old stock." His voice dropped. "There's a woman on Mire Street selling pine resin lozenges off her stove. Crude, but they ease the chest. I'd send you there myself if I could leave the shop."

"How much?"

"For you?" He finally looked up, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with hours. "For you, the same as always. For everyone else, triple. Don't tell anyone I said that."

I paid him in the small silvered coins Tam had been polishing bright, took the parcel, and tucked it inside my coat against my ribs, where it sat warm as a second heartbeat. Then I went home through streets that were learning, block by block, how to be quiet.

* * *

Our flat was on the third floor of a building that leaned slightly east, as if craning to watch the harbor. You could hear the water from the kitchen window on windless days — the slap of hulls, the groan of mooring lines, the bells. Vessany was a city that had grown up around its fleet the way ivy grows around a wall, and even now, even starving, even occupied, the sound of ships was the sound of the republic breathing.

Mother was propped in the armchair by that window, wrapped in two blankets and the quilt Aunt Serafin had made before the war made widows of half the women we knew. She was small these days. She had been small all my life, but illness had taken whatever padding time had left and left her bird-boned, translucent at the wrists. Her eyes, though. Her eyes were exactly as they'd always been: dark, quick, missing nothing.

"You're shivering," she said, before I'd gotten the door fully shut.

"It's cold out."

"It's cold in here too, which is why I mentioned it." She held out her hand for the parcel, opened it with the efficiency of someone who had run a household through two shortages and a blockade, and examined the bottle without comment. She knew what half-strength looked like. She'd been doing arithmetic with medicine since before I was born.

"Where's Tam?"

"Market," she said. "He goes out with the handcart at dawn and comes back smelling like other people's chimneys." A pause. The cough came then — deep, wet, rooted somewhere the willow bark couldn't reach — and she rode it out with her eyes closed and her jaw set, refusing it the dignity of bending her forward. When it passed she breathed carefully, twice, and said, "He's hoarding again. Under his bed. Metal, mostly. Brass fittings, copper wire, a hinge he swears is solid bronze."

"He's bartering. It keeps him busy."

"It keeps him *out*, Xanthe. There's a difference, and mothers know it." She looked at me over the rim of the teacup she was warming between her palms. "You're both doing the same thing, you know. He collects metal because metal holds its shape. And you—" she gestured vaguely at my coat, at the whole architecture of my comings and goings, which she had never once asked about and never once failed to understand— "you collect errands. Everyone in this family is trying to hold a shape."

"The shape of a family that eats," I said.

"Mm." She sipped. Outside the window, a naval tug moved up the channel, low in the water, riding heavy. She watched it go with an expression I couldn't read, or didn't want to. "Eat something before you go out again. There's bread."

There was not much bread. There was never much bread. But there was some, and she had saved me the heel without being asked, the way she had every day of my life, and I ate it standing at the counter because sitting down felt like admitting how tired I was.

Tam came clattering up the stairs at dusk, fourteen years old and all elbows, cheeks scoured pink by the wind, hauling the handcart with the particular pride of a boy who has beaten the market to the good scrap. He dumped his haul on the newspaper spread by the door: pipe fittings, a dented kettle, coils of wire, and — I noticed with a lurch — a brass plaque the size of a book, engraved with lettering he'd already tried to scrub off.

"Where did that come from?"

"Demolition office on Quay Lane. They're tearing up the old customs house." He grinned, gap-toothed, triumphant. "Solid brass, Xanthe. This alone buys us coal for a week. Maybe two."

I turned it over in my hands. The engraving was faint under his scrubbing, but I could still make it out: *CUSTOMS HOUSE OF THE REPUBLIC OF VESSANY — ERECTED IN THE YEAR OF THE SECOND CONSOLIDATION.* A republic's name, scraped off its own brass by its own citizens, sold for warmth.

"Good find," I said, and gave him the smile he needed, and put the plaque down where I wouldn't have to look at it.

* * *

The dispatch came to me two nights later, wrapped in oilcloth inside a fish crate inside a cart of genuine, reeking herring, because the best hiding places are the ones that make couriers want to wash their hands.

My contact was a stevedore named Ollivan who worked the north piers and had the gift of looking bored in any weather, including artillery. He handed me the crate, I signed nothing — we never signed anything — and he said only, "Heavy cargo tonight. Handle it like it bites."

Everything I carried bit. That was the job. But I knew within four blocks that this one bit differently.

Couriers learn to read weight. Not the weight of paper — paper weighs nothing — but the weight of consequence, which transmits itself through the chain of custody like current through wire. The men who passed this crate along had handled it too carefully. The seal on the inner envelope was the ministry's black wax instead of the usual gray. And the routing mark, when I checked it under the streetlamp's dying gaslight in the mouth of Tanner's Alley, bore the cipher of the Admiralty itself, countersigned twice.

Codes sewn into coat linings are just thread until you deliver them. I delivered them. That night's delivery took me down through the dock district to a shuttered chandlery where a naval officer waited behind a counter stacked with rope that hadn't been sold in months. He was maybe fifty, weathered, with the permanent squint of a man who had spent his life reading horizons. He broke the black wax, read for a long time, and then set the pages down flat on the counter and pressed both palms on them, the way you'd press on a wound.

"Well?" I asked, because couriers aren't supposed to ask, and I asked anyway.

He looked at me for a moment — really looked, the way people do when they've decided you're furniture and then changed their minds. "Do you know what you brought me, courier?"

"Orders."

"Orders." He almost laughed. It came out as a cough of air. "This is the fleet disposition. Every ship. Every hull we have left, and where each one sits." He tapped the page. "And this paragraph here instructs me to see that none of them leave harbor. Scuttling charges at the sills. Block the channels with our own dead ships so nobody else can use them either."

The chandlery was very quiet. Through the walls I could hear the harbor doing what the harbor always did — water slapping, lines creaking, the far bell buoy tolling its slow idiot patience — and it sounded suddenly obscene, like laughter at a funeral that hadn't happened yet.

"They're sinking them," I said. "Our own—"

"That's the arithmetic of surrender, courier. If we can't keep them, no one does." He rolled the orders closed. His hands were steady, which told me he'd been grieving this for days already, probably since the first draft crossed his desk. "You want to know the worst part? The terms referenced in here — the annexation schedule, the corridor clauses — they cite dates. Dates next month. Which means the armistice isn't being negotiated anymore." He met my eyes. "It's written. It's been written for weeks. All that's left is the ink drying and the signatures, and men like me arranging our ships on the seabed like setting a table for guests we hate."

I walked home the long way, along the quay, because I couldn't make myself take the short way past the fleet.

They lay there in the dark roadstead — what was left of them. Two cruisers with patched plates. A scatter of gunboats. The old training barque *Meridian*, whose masts I had climbed as a child during the harbor festival, when the navy still let civilians aboard and the whole city smelled of tar and fried dough and pride. Floodlights swept them at intervals, and each sweep showed me hulls that would, within weeks, be opened to the sea on purpose. Deliberately. By their own crews, who loved them, who would stand on their decks and open the valves and step into the boats and row away while the water came in.

Grief arrived early that night. That's the thing nobody tells you about the fall of a country: you mourn it while it's still technically alive. You stand on a quay in the freezing dark, looking at ships that are still floating, in a republic that still has a name on its documents and a government that still issues orders, and you grieve — because you've seen the orders. Because grief doesn't wait for the official date. It moves ahead of events like weather moving ahead of the wind, and by the time the thing itself happens, you've already lived through it once, alone, in the dark, and all that's left on the appointed day is the paperwork.

I stood there until my feet went numb. Then I went home.

* * *

Mother was awake. Of course she was awake; she slept in fragments now, the cough breaking her nights into pieces, and she'd taken to keeping a candle lit because, she said, if she was going to be awake she might as well be able to see the room she was awake in.

She heard me come in the way she heard everything — no comment, no turning of her head, just a shift of attention like a compass needle finding north. I hung up my coat. I banked the stove with the last of the scrap wood Tam had traded for. I sat on the edge of her bed, and she studied my face in the candlelight with those quick dark eyes, and whatever she saw there made her set down her book.

"You look like your father after the blockade news," she said. "That particular gray."

"I'm tired, that's all."

"Mm." She reached out and took my hand. Her fingers were hot — fever-hot, always feverish now — and dry as paper. "Whatever you carry in that coat, Xanthe, and I have never asked and I am not asking now — I want you to remember something. Your father used to say a country isn't the ground. Ground is just where you happen to be standing. A country is the table you set for strangers. It's the language you argue in. It's—" the cough took her, briefly, and she mastered it, and continued as though uninterrupted— "it's the people who stay to pass the bread when everyone else is running for the door."

"That's a lot of wisdom for a woman with no coal."

"I'm economizing. Wisdom burns clean." She smiled, and for a moment the fever couldn't touch it, and she was twenty years younger and entirely herself. "Go to sleep. Whatever's coming, it'll still be coming tomorrow, and you'll be no use to anyone hollowed out."

I kissed her forehead — too warm, always too warm now — and went to my cot in the alcove. Through the wall I could hear Tam breathing in his room, deep and even, a boy's sleep, dreamless, with his hoarded brass beneath him like treasure. Through the window, faint, the harbor bells. Floating ships. For now, floating ships.

I lay in the dark and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the chandlery. The armistice was written. The fleet was condemned. The coal was gone and the medicine was half-strength and my mother's lungs were filling like the ships' hulls soon would, slow and certain and by design. Somewhere in a ministry, men were setting dates. Somewhere in a basement, my handler was waiting for my next report. And somewhere out in the sleeping city, a census taker with careful eyes had chalked our doorframe and walked away, and I still didn't know what the mark meant — only that marks like that are never made for nothing.

The candle guttered. The bells tolled. And in the morning, when I rose before light to check the street, the chalk mark on our doorframe had changed.

Someone had added a second stroke.
