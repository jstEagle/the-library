## 6. The Chalk Mark

I found it before anyone else did, because I was always the first one up. That was my job now — Xanthe was out before dawn half the week doing whatever it was they did at the ministry, and Mother slept in the gray hours when the cough let her, which was never long enough. So I rose, I banked the stove coals, and I checked the street.

That was my job too, though nobody had assigned it. I'd made it mine the way you make anything yours: by deciding nobody else would do it right.

The chalk mark sat on our doorframe at shoulder height, where a man might reach without stooping. A single diagonal stroke, white against the black paint, thin as a fingernail paring.

I stood on the step with the cold coming up through my boot soles and looked at it for a long time.

I knew what it meant. Everyone knew what chalk marks meant now, even if nobody said it out loud. The occupiers marked houses the way a butcher marks carcasses — this one for questioning, that one for requisition, another for nothing at all except the demonstration that they could touch whatever they liked. Old Vesker down the lane had a circle on his lintel for three days before two men in gray came and walked through his rooms and wrote things in a ledger and left again without explanation. He never learned why. Neither did anyone else. That was the point of it. You lived inside the question mark.

And I knew whose hand had put it there, because I'd watched him do it. Or I hadn't watched, but I'd seen him standing outside our door two days ago with his ledger under his arm and his census-taker's satchel buttoned wrong, asking Xanthe questions in a voice too soft to carry across the square. Gideon Hale. The man who counted us like spoons.

I wet my sleeve at the pump and scrubbed until my arm ached and the paint beneath went dull with moisture and the mark was gone. Gone, but not undone — he'd know it was gone. A man who marks doors comes back to look at his marks. I understood that much about how these things worked, and understanding it made me furious in a way that sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

He knew something about my family. He thought we were worth marking.

Fine. Then I would learn something about him.

* * *

The market on Foundry Row opened at eight, or pretended to, which in Vessany that winter amounted to the same thing. By half past, its four lanes were choked: women with ration cards held in their teeth while they dug for purses, old men selling clock parts off blankets, a boy of maybe ten turning somersaults between the stalls for coins that never came. The smell was coal smoke and brine and the sweet-rotten ghost of fruit that hadn't been seen in months.

I got there early enough to buy a twist of day-old bread — real currency now, better than currency — and posted myself near the pickle barrels with a view of the square. If Hale made his rounds in this district, he'd come here. Census men always worked the markets; that's where the people were, and where the people were, the counting happened.

I waited an hour. I ate the bread slowly and made myself into scenery, which is harder than it sounds. I am fourteen and I have my mother's red hair, which means I am many things but never invisible. So I became a boy fascinated by pickles. I examined those barrels within an inch of their lives.

He came at ten, from the north lane, walking like a man with an errand and not a patrol. No ledger today. Just the satchel, worn soft at the corners, hanging from his shoulder. He was taller than I remembered and moved differently than I remembered — census men shuffle, they pause, they consult. Gideon Hale walked the way water goes downhill. Directly.

I followed him.

I want to say I did it cleverly, and mostly I did, but there was one bad stretch along the tinker's row where the stalls thinned out and I had nothing to hide behind except a stack of crates and a man mending a chair, and I crouched behind the chair-mender for a full minute pretending to be riveted by caning work. He gave me a look. I gave him two coppers. He decided I was someone else's problem.

Hale cut south past the fish stalls, out of the market proper, down toward the harbor — which surprised me, I'll admit. I had him fixed in my head as a creature of doorways and ledgers. But he went down the harbor stairs two at a time like a man who'd grown up on boats, and came out on the customs quay where the clerks worked out of a tar-paper shed with a stovepipe sticking out of it like a burnt finger.

There was one clerk visible through the shed window, a fat man with a green eyeshade, and I watched Hale knock, wait, go in.

The shed's side wall had a gap where the boards had warped apart, and I have been skinny my whole life, and for once in my miserable life being skinny was useful. I flattened against the cold tar paper and put my eye to the gap.

They talked. I couldn't hear most of it — the wind off the water took the words and shredded them — but I didn't need to hear it, because I could see it.

I saw Hale open the satchel and take out a flat packet wrapped in oilcloth, and I saw him set it on the clerk's desk, and I saw the clerk glance out the window at the quay, and then open the packet with hands that shook just slightly, the way hands shake when they're touching more money than they usually see in a month.

Paper money. I couldn't count the notes from where I crouched, but I know what a thick packet looks like, and that was thick.

My heart went hard and small. *There it is.* The informer's trade, caught in the act. Buying names. Buying access. Buying *our names*, probably — because who else? Who else had he marked? Who else had he questioned with his soft voice and his questions that weren't on any form?

I pressed my face harder to the gap, memorizing everything — the clerk's face, the eyeshade, the exact way Hale stood with his weight on his left foot — building the testimony I would carry home. Xanthe had to know. Xanthe trusted everyone's surface, read every stranger's face for honesty and then believed what they read, and this man had sat at our table's edge and asked about household members born outside the district and Xanthe had answered, and all the while —

Then the clerk slid something back across the desk, and my whole testimony fell apart in my hands.

It was a document. I couldn't read it through the gap, but I could see enough: heavy paper, official proportions, a stamp in the corner the size of a plum. And I could see the clerk's face as he pushed it over, and I will remember that face longer than most faces I've known, because the clerk was not selling. The clerk looked frightened. Not greedy-frightened, not caught-frightened. Frightened the way you're frightened handing over something precious to somebody you hope deserves it.

Hale took the document. He didn't count the money first. He'd already paid — the packet stayed on the desk — and he took the paper like a man taking medicine, without relief, without triumph. He said something short. The clerk shook his head, said something longer. Hale said one word back, and even mangled by the wind it landed like a struck bell, and the clerk went quiet and nodded and looked away.

Then Hale put the document inside his coat, next to his body, the way you carry the thing that matters, and left the shed.

I peeled off the wall and got behind the pickle barrels before he came up the stairs, and I crouched there with my pulse slamming in my ears, trying to do sums that wouldn't finish.

Informer logic said: he bought a document with forged names in it, evidence, lists, something to hang us with. But informers don't pay. Informers are paid. Informers don't carry documents next to their hearts. And no dock clerk in occupied Vessany looks frightened for the sake of a man buying names.

So what had he bought?

A berth. The thought arrived whole, uninvited, terrible. An evacuation berth. They existed — everyone whispered about them, the passage-out papers stamped before the armistice sealed the borders forever, going for prices no honest family could pay, going sometimes for worse than money. One berth. One person out. And a fat clerk with shaking hands and a stamp the size of a plum.

But a berth for whom? Him? Fine. That's nothing to me. Let the census man save himself, it's a free country for eleven more weeks.

Except he'd asked about *us*. Except he'd marked our door. Except he'd sat across from Xanthe with that soft voice, and either the mark meant what marks mean — or it meant something else entirely, something I couldn't make sit still long enough to look at.

I hated him for the confusion alone. I could have managed hatred cleanly. Confusion curdled.

* * *

He didn't go home after the quay. He went to the chandler's on Rope Walk, and then to the chapel of the Seamen's Rest, where he stood for ten minutes among the memorial plaques with his hat off, and I watched him from the doorway of a cooper's yard and felt like a burglar in a church, which in a sense I was.

Then he came back through the square.

Our square. Past our house.

I shouldn't have followed him there. Even I knew that — there's following a man and there's leading him straight to your own front door, and only an idiot does the second. But he slowed in front of our gate, and I ducked behind the well housing with my heart somewhere up around my ears, and through the gap under the well's iron skirt I watched him stop.

He looked at the doorframe. At the place where the mark had been. He looked a long time, and then he took out a handkerchief — a handkerchief, like a gentleman, like a man with something on his conscience — and wiped his fingers on it though they were clean, and stood there in the falling light with his breath making little ghosts in the air.

And then he looked at the window.

Mother's window. The candle she keeps burning low at night because the doctor in better years told her warm light eased her breathing, which is nonsense medically and true in every other way. It was burning now, a small orange point in the blue dusk, and Gideon Hale stood in the street looking at it the way starving men look through bakery glass.

Not cataloguing. Not noting. *Longing.* I know longing when I see it; I've practiced it in mirrors, wanting things I couldn't name out loud — enough coal, my father back, to be taken seriously by people who pat my head. His face did what my face does.

He crossed himself. Quick, self-conscious, like a habit he was embarrassed by. I didn't even know the occupiers' men crossed themselves.

Then he walked on, and I stayed crouched behind the well until my knees screamed, doing arithmetic again, and this time the sums finished, and I wished they hadn't.

Because here's what added up: he wasn't hunting us. A hunter doesn't stand in the snow looking at your mother's candle like it's the last lit window in the republic. A hunter doesn't pay instead of being paid. Whatever Gideon Hale wanted from my family, it wasn't their names for a gray ledger.

It was something else. Something he was willing to spend a fortune at a customs shed to get, and stand in the cold to look at, and cross himself over.

And the mark — the second stroke, the one that changed overnight — was still out there on some wall or post where I hadn't found it yet, meaning something I couldn't read, addressed to someone I couldn't name.

I went in. Mother was awake, propped on her pillows, and she looked at my red hands and my wild eyes and said, "Tam, love, what did you scrub off the door?"

She knew. Of course she knew. She knows everything; it's her superpower, along with dying slowly in a country that's dying faster.

"Chalk," I said. "A census man's mark."

"Mm," said Mother, in the tone that meant she'd already gotten further than I had. She coughed, and rode it out, and looked at me with those eyes that had gone bright the way they do when the fever's up. "And what did the census man do today?"

I opened my mouth to tell her about the money and the shed and the frightened clerk.

And stopped. Because the answer, spoken aloud, was: *he bought a berth.* And if he bought a berth — one berth, the only kind that exists anymore, the rarest paper in Vessany — then the question that follows it arrives whether you speak it or not, and once it's in the room it never leaves:

*A berth for whom?*

For himself, I almost said. For himself, obviously.

But I had watched him look at the candle.

"He bought something at the docks," I said finally. "Something big. And then he came here, and looked at our door, and looked at your window, and left."

Mother was quiet a moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere across the city the evening guns went off, one, two, counting the hours like we needed counting.

"Well," she said, and pulled the blanket higher, and her voice was steady as a carpenter's level, "then I expect he'll be back."

She closed her eyes to sleep, and I sat by the stove banking the coals, and I thought about the third stroke that would be coming on some morning I hadn't checked yet, and what it would mean, and whether I'd have time to scrub it off before Xanthe saw it — or whether, this once, I ought to leave it exactly where it was.
