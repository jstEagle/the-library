## 7. Interrogation by Candlelight

The summons came the way the frost came that winter — quietly, and then all at once.

Xanthe found it folded inside a ration card at the coal office on Virey Street, written in a hand they knew better than their own: four letters of the old courier alphabet, the one used only for internal ministry traffic. *Kestrel. Basement. Ninth bell. Come alone, come plain.*

There was no signature. There never was.

Ninth bell. Two hours from now. Xanthe stood in the coal office queue with three other shivering citizens, watching the clerk weigh out black dust like it was sacrament, and let their face do what it had been trained to do for six years: nothing. Inside, something had begun to swing like a lamp in wind.

*Come alone, come plain.* That was standard. What was not standard was the summons itself. In six years of carrying the republic's secrets through its slow collapse, Handler Verel had summoned Xanthe to the basement exactly twice — once after a colleague vanished between relay points, once when the codes changed format entirely. Both times, someone else had not come back from the questioning.

Xanthe bought their coal, bought a twist of chicory root for Mother's cough — the apothecary's boy didn't meet their eyes anymore; nobody sold medicine in Vessany now, only substitutes that tasted of burnt straw — and walked home through streets gone blue with early dusk. Tam was in the kitchen scraping rust off a hinge he'd pried from somewhere, weighing it in his palm like a coin. Mother slept propped on pillows in the back room, her breath whistling faintly, a sound Xanthe had learned to hate more than artillery.

"You're going out," Tam said. Not a question. Fifteen years old and he read Xanthe the way sailors read weather.

"Errand."

"Your errands get longer every week."

"Prices go up. Have you eaten?"

"There was soup." He didn't look up from the hinge. "The census man came by again today. While you were out."

The cold followed Xanthe into the room then, though the stove was lit. "What did he want?"

"Wanted to know if anyone new had moved in. Wanted to count the beds. He counted four beds and wrote down three people, and then he stood there like he was waiting for me to say something wrong." Tam set down the hinge. His hands were steady; his voice wasn't, quite. "He asked where you were."

"And what did you say?"

"That my sibling worked nights. That I don't ask where." Tam's jaw tightened. "He smiled at me, Xanthe. Like we were friends. I hate how he smiles."

So did Xanthe. They thought of the chalk mark on the doorframe — scrubbed away now, though the ghost of it still showed if the light hit the stone sideways — and of Gideon Hale's eyes, patient and gray, missing nothing. The ink stains. *You handle documents.* A census taker who noticed ink the way a courier noticed exits.

And now the basement wanted them too. The walls were closing from both sides, and Xanthe stood precisely in the seam.

They kissed Mother's forehead — she stirred, murmured something about the laundry that had been hanging on the line in another country, another life — told Tam to bar the door, and went out into the ninth hour of the night.

* * *

The Ministry of Correspondence had once been a bank, and its basement still smelled of money: dry paper, brass fittings, the ghost of vaults. Now it held filing cabinets instead of gold, and the cabinets held the republic's remaining credibility, arranged alphabetically.

A guard Xanthe had never seen before took their name without writing it down, which meant it would be written down later, by someone else, somewhere else. He led them down a stairwell where the gaslight had been replaced by candles — the occupiers controlled the gas mains now, and the ministry's defiance consisted largely of burning wax it couldn't afford — and into a room with a table, two chairs, and a candelabra burning five tapers, as if light itself were being rationed down to its essentials.

Handler Verel sat at the table.

He was a small, precise man, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that made clerks trust him and made Xanthe's spine itch. He had recruited Xanthe six years ago — plucked them out of a mail-sorting house because they'd noticed an error in an official dispatch and, instead of reporting it, had simply corrected it and said nothing. *That,* Verel had said, *is either treason or genius. We'll find out which.*

Tonight his hands were folded on the table. There was no file in front of him. No papers at all.

"Sit down, Kestrel."

Xanthe sat. They kept their coat on. It was cold in the room, but also — the coat lining held things, and some animal part of them wanted those things close to their body tonight, even though tonight's dispatch had already been delivered two days ago and the lining held only a grocery list and a bus token from a city whose trams no longer ran.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"If I knew," Xanthe said, "you'd have wasted a candle."

Something flickered at the corner of Verel's mouth. Approval, or the memory of it. "A census taker," he said. "Gideon Hale. District registrar's office, seconded to the occupation census authority. Do you know him?"

"He's been making rounds in our quarter. Standard."

"Nothing about him is standard." Verel unfolded his hands and laid them flat. "Hale transferred into Vessany eleven weeks ago. Before that, his file places him in Corvane. Before *that*, the file goes blank for four years. Blank files don't happen to clerks, Kestrel. They happen to people whose employers pay to have the pages removed."

The candle flames leaned, all five together, as if a door had opened somewhere far above.

"You think he's foreign intelligence," Xanthe said.

"I think," Verel said softly, "that a man with a scraped history has spent three weeks asking questions in your district. And that of all the households in that district, he has visited yours *four times*. Four visits to a household of three registered occupants, one of whom is bedridden and one of whom is fifteen." He tilted his head. "Tell me, Kestrel. What does a census taker want with your family?"

"A count," Xanthe said. "That's generally the trade."

Verel watched them for a long moment. The silence in the room had texture; it pressed against the ears. Xanthe had been questioned before — by Verel, by border officers in three countries, once by a drunk occupier captain who'd found a ministry stamp in their satchel and been too stupid or too tired to care — and they knew the rhythm. The question repeated. Always the question repeated, wearing different clothes.

"Why did he come back today?" Verel asked.

"To count beds."

"How many do you have?"

"Four."

"How many people?"

"Three."

"And yet he wrote something in the margin of his form. My people recovered the carbon copy from the district office this afternoon." Verel reached at last into his breast pocket and drew out a thin sheet, translucent with age or handling, and laid it on the table facing Xanthe. Most of it was columns and checkmarks. But in the margin, in a small, careful hand — the same hand, Xanthe realized with a lurch, that had written *Do you handle documents?* across the bottom of a census form — was a single line.

*Household head employed in night document work. Verify.*

"You see the problem," Verel said.

It was not a question, so Xanthe did not answer it. Their heart was doing something complicated — beating fast and seeming, at the same time, to stop. *Verify.* The word sat on the page like a nail waiting for a hammer. And beneath the fear, unwelcome and unkillable, another feeling: Gideon Hale had looked at ink-stained fingers and written down the truth. Not a suspicion. Not an accusation. A fact, noted neutrally, filed under *verify* — the way an honest clerk notes an honest discrepancy. As if he weren't hunting them at all but simply... watching. Recording. Waiting.

Which was worse. Or was it better? Xanthe genuinely could not tell anymore, sitting in a candlelit basement being interrogated by their own side about a stranger's curiosity.

"What I see," Xanthe said slowly, "is a census taker doing arithmetic. Four beds, three people, a sibling who works nights. He wrote down what any fool could observe. If he were an agent, wouldn't he have written something useful?"

"If he were an agent," Verel said, "he'd write exactly that. The best ones look like bookkeeping." He took the carbon back, folded it, and burned it in the nearest candle, holding it until the flame kissed his fingertips. He never flinched. He never flinched at anything; it was the thing Xanthe most feared about him. "Now. The dispatch you carried Tuesday."

Here it was. The wire tightening. Xanthe kept their breathing level, kept their eyes on the burning paper. "Delivered. Signed for. You have the receipt."

"I have the receipt. What I don't have is confirmation that the contents stayed sealed between here and the signatory's desk." Verel looked up. "Hale's route through the market intersects yours. Twice last week, by my watchers' count. Coincidence is possible. This city is small now; everyone's routes intersect everything." A pause. "But agents don't find couriers by coincidence, Kestrel. They find them by pattern. And you, my dear, are the most regular irregular in Vessany. Same market day. Same bread queue. Same church you never pray in. You've survived by looking forgettable, but forgettable people are *predictable* people, and predictable people are findable people."

Every word landed like a step closer in an empty hall. Because he was right. Six years of tradecraft and Xanthe had grown comfortable, grown *rooted*, and roots showed above ground eventually.

"The final phase approaches," Verel went on, quieter now, almost gentle, which was somehow the most frightening register he owned. "There will be one more dispatch. Perhaps two. After that, none of this will matter — the ministry will be a memory, the codes will be scrap paper, and men like Hale will go home to wherever men like Hale come from. Until then, I need you clean. No routes deviated. No contacts made outside protocol. If the census taker speaks to you again—"

"He already spoke to me once."

The words left Xanthe's mouth before strategy could catch them. Instantly they understood the trap they'd built for themselves: concealment now would be worse than confession. Verel's watchers had likely seen the encounter anyway.

Verel went very still. "Tell me."

"Bread line. Three days ago. He asked about my hands — the ink. I told him I work in records. He asked nothing else. He let me go."

"*Let you go.*" Verel repeated the phrase as though tasting spoiled milk. "As if you were prey he declined, for the moment, to eat." He rose, crossed to the wall, and stood with his back to Xanthe, silhouetted against the candlelight, a small man casting a large shadow. "I'm going to tell you something I tell very few people, Kestrel, because I believe you're the best I have and because the next weeks will require you to understand exactly what kind of war this has become.

"This republic is finished. You know it, I know it, the signatories know it. What remains to be negotiated is the *manner* of the ending — how fast, how orderly, who gets out, who gets processed. The armistice terms are being drafted in that building across the square, and every intelligence service within four hundred miles wants to know what's in them before they're signed. The final dispatches moving through our network in the coming days are worth more than the entire ministry combined. Whoever holds them holds leverage." He turned around. In the candlelight his eyes were colorless. "If Hale is hunting our network — and he is — then he isn't interested in your family. He's interested in what passes *through* it. You are not a target, Kestrel. You are a door. And doors get broken."

Xanthe sat very still. Somewhere overhead, footsteps crossed the floor of the dead bank above them, unhurried, a janitor perhaps, or a minister, or a man drafting surrender.

"Am I free to go?" they asked.

"You were always free to go. That's the fiction that keeps us both polite." Verel opened a drawer and slid something across the table: a fresh pass, laminated, genuine-looking, bearing Xanthe's courier alias. "New identity supplement. Carry it separately from your others. And Kestrel—" he paused, and for one strange moment his voice lost its polish, revealing something older and wearier underneath, "—when the last dispatch comes, you'll know it. It travels differently. Heavier. When it comes, nothing else matters. Not your mother, not the boy, not the census man with his gray eyes. Nothing else matters. Can you promise me that?"

*Nothing else matters.* Xanthe thought of Mother's whistle-breath in the dark back room. Of Tam's hands, steady on stolen hinges. Of a chalk mark on a doorframe and a man across a square who watched their window at night — because Verel's watchers weren't the only ones; Xanthe had caught the silhouette twice now in the registrar's office across the way, a lamp burning late, a figure standing still.

"Yes," Xanthe said. It was the easiest lie they had ever told, and the heaviest.

Verel studied them a moment too long. Then he nodded, dismissed them with a flick of two fingers, and returned to his candles.

* * *

Outside, snow had started again — fine, granular, indifferent. Xanthe walked home the long way, running tradecraft on autopilot while the mind churned elsewhere.

Because here was the thing Xanthe could not put down, the splinter working deeper with every block: Verel had shown them the carbon copy. Had recited Hale's blank years, his transfer date, his intersection patterns. Verel knew *everything* about Gideon Hale.

Which meant the ministry had been watching Hale for weeks.

Which meant Verel's people had also been watching *everyone Hale watched* — including Xanthe — for just as long. The summons hadn't been triggered by today's discovery. Today's discovery was the pretext. The surveillance was the cause, and it predated the chalk mark, probably predated Hale's first knock.

Or — and here the splinter turned — Verel had none of that at all. The carbon copy might have been fabricated in this very basement, the blank years invented, the whole dossier theater staged to test whether Xanthe would defend the census taker or denounce him. To measure the exact weight of whatever it was growing, unspoken, between the courier and the man with the gray eyes.

Both readings were plausible. Both were poison. That was the design of basements like this one: you left knowing less than when you arrived, and knowing, at least, that you were known.

Xanthe passed the end of the square and stopped, just for a breath, in the shadow of a bombed archway. Across the open white space, the registrar's office was dark. But the Thornbury window — their own window, fourth floor, third from the corner — glowed faintly. Tam was still up. Keeping the lamp for them, the way he always pretended not to.

And beside it, fainter, lower, in the building opposite: a second light. One candle's worth. Behind it, unmistakable even at this distance and this angle, the still silhouette of a man who was not reading, not writing, not moving at all.

Watching the Thornbury window. Watching Xanthe come home.

Verel's agent, or Verel's invention, or neither — a man keeping a vigil over a family he had no earthly reason to care about. Three possibilities, and no way to choose between them except to walk across the square and ask.

Tomorrow, Xanthe decided, standing in the falling snow with duty pulling one arm and something far more dangerous pulling the other. Tomorrow there was a bread queue, and the same market day, and the same predictable, findable, unforgettable hour when their routes would cross again.

Tomorrow, one of them was going to stop pretending.
