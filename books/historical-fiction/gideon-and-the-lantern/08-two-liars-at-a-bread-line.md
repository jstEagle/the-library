## 8. Two Liars at a Bread Line

The queue outside Halloran's bakery began forming before dawn, a black thread of people stitched along the frozen street, and by the time the first gray light came over the rooftops it had become a country in miniature: the old pretending to be frail so they'd be pitied forward, the young pretending to be strong so no one would pity them at all.

Xanthe joined it two hundred places back, coat collar up, hands buried in pockets, wearing the face they wore everywhere now — the forgettable one, the one that said *I am nobody's errand*. Three days since the chalk mark on the doorframe. Tam had scrubbed it off before Xanthe even saw it, and that frightened them more than the mark itself. A warning you never read is a warning still waiting to happen.

They had counted exits out of habit. The alley behind the cooperage. The drainage grate that a child could slip through and an adult could not. The rooftop of the tannery if it came to running. None of it mattered, because halfway down the queue, four places ahead, wrapped in a census taker's gray wool coat, stood Gideon Hale.

He hadn't seen them yet. He was watching the bakery door with the patience of a man who had learned that everything in Vessany arrived late — bread, justice, spring.

Xanthe's heart performed a small, treacherous stumble and then resumed its work. *Steady. You are a queue. Queues are not afraid.*

* * *

Gideon felt the change in the line before he identified it — a subtle reordering of the air, the way one does when someone nearby is performing stillness too carefully. He knew that performance. He'd spent six years perfecting it himself.

He turned, unhurried, and there was the Thornbury courier, because that is what he had decided to call them in his head: not the second-born of 14 Lantern Row, not the ink-stained liar from the doorway, but the person who moved through occupied streets like a needle through cloth, carrying the republic's last secrets in its seams.

Their eyes met. Neither looked away first, which told him both of them were liars, and neither of them was good at it anymore.

"Well," Gideon said quietly, when the line shuffled them closer together, "the bread here is terrible. In case you were hoping for a better reason than surveillance."

"I don't need a reason to eat," Xanthe said.

"No," he agreed. "You need a reason to stand four places behind me in a queue that runs the length of Salt Street, though. There are two other bakeries within a mile that haven't been visited by the census this week."

"That's coincidence."

"Nothing in this city is coincidence anymore. Coincidence was requisitioned in the autumn." He said it without heat, almost gently, and that gentleness was worse than accusation. "I've been wondering something for three days, courier. Whether you followed me, or whether we're simply two instruments pointed at the same target by different hands."

"I'm not a courier."

"Ink on your fingers says otherwise. The same violet-gray as ministry dispatch ink. I catalogued forty-two households last month; only yours matched the seal wax on documents I'm not cleared to see, which means either you're laundering government correspondence or you're very dedicated to your writing. Poets don't get ration-priority stamps."

Xanthe looked at him then — fully, deliberately, letting the forgettable face fall away like a coat unbuttoned. "And what does a census taker know about seal wax? Census takers count noses. They don't catalogue inks. They don't ask about birth districts off-form. They don't chalk doors." A pause, surgical. "So let's stop pretending you're what your badge says. What are you?"

Around them the queue pressed close, deaf in the way crowds are deaf to quiet conversations, everyone conservating their attention for warmth and bread and their own private dread. Somewhere near the front a woman began coughing, deep and wet.

"Careful," Gideon murmured. "Accusations carry, in this cold."

"So do answers."

* * *

They got their loaves — half-loaves, dense and gray — and neither of them left. That was the strange part, Xanthe thought, following Gideon's nod toward the churchyard wall at the end of the square, sitting down on stone so cold it burned through wool with a man they had every professional reason to run from. Exhaustion did that. Two years of sleeping with one ear open, of measuring every friendship against its usefulness, of lying fluently in self-defense until the lies grew teeth — exhaustion made even danger feel like company.

Gideon broke his loaf and held half out.

"I don't take things from informers."

"It's bread, not a bribe. If I wanted to buy you, I'd have offered something worth having." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Take it. You look like a person who gives away more calories than they keep."

Xanthe took it, mostly because refusing meant admitting how long it had been since they'd eaten, and ate in silence while crows argued over the churchyard's bare linden trees.

"You first," Xanthe said finally. "You knocked on my door knowing things a census doesn't hold. You marked my frame. Either you were hunting me for someone, or you were hunting the thing I might be carrying for someone else, and either way—" They stopped. Breathed. The truth came out smaller than they expected, worn thin by three nights without sleep. "Either way I am so tired of being hunted that I would almost rather hear the answer than survive another day of guessing it."

Gideon studied them for a long moment. Then he reached inside his coat — Xanthe's whole body went rigid, hand moving toward the knife they weren't carrying anymore, had surrendered at a checkpoint weeks ago and mourned daily — and drew out not a weapon but a small brass key on a string. He turned it over in his gloved fingers.

"I'm going to tell you something stupid," he said. "Because you're right. I'm tired too. And because if you're going to ruin me, better it be for the truth."

* * *

He told it plainly, the way men tell things they've rehearsed alone a thousand times. He was not a census taker by origin. He had been born across the strait, in Corvane, and had come to Vessany eleven years ago with nothing but a name that opened no doors and a debt he couldn't speak of. The census work was cover — but not the occupiers' cover, and not the ministry's either.

"I work for myself," he said. "That's the confession. Everyone assumes I'm somebody's agent — the liaison office thinks I'm theirs, the ministry suspects I'm Corvani, and the neighbors think I'm just a dull little functionary who likes counting stairs. All three are wrong. I take the census because it puts me in every house in the district. It lets me find things."

"Find what?"

"People who are about to disappear. And ways to make sure some of them don't." He closed his fist around the key. "There's a certificate. An evacuation berth. Genuine — sealed, registered, valid until armistice seals the ports. One berth. One person, out, safe, before the gates close. It fell into my hands as payment for something I did that I'm not proud of, and I have spent four months deciding who deserves it, because I don't. I've watched this district like a shepherd counts sheep, and every family on my list has a mother, or a child, or a reason, and none of them is more deserving than the next, and it is making me insane."

The crows went on arguing. Xanthe sat very still.

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you're the only other person in Vessany carrying something that heavy," Gideon said. "I can see it on you. And because three days ago I put a chalk mark on your door to warn you — the liaison office flagged your household after my report went through their channels. My report. Which I wrote badly on purpose, omitting exactly enough to keep you unremarkable. But channels have a way of reading more than what's written." He looked at the bread in his hands as if disappointed by it. "I marked your door because I couldn't warn you any other way without hanging us both. Scrub it, and scrub it again if it comes back. Someone else may renew it."

Xanthe stared at him. The machinery of suspicion that had run day and night for two years ground suddenly against something it had no gear for: a man confessing to kindness disguised as menace.

"You're a spy who spies for strangers."

"I'm a coward who couldn't pick just one stranger to save, so I'm trying to save them all, badly." He laughed once, short and bleak. "Your turn, courier. Fair trade. What's sewn into your coat that makes the liaison office hungry?"

* * *

And here was the moment, Xanthe thought, where survival said *lie*. Say it's letters. Say it's medicine money. Say anything soft.

But the wall was cold, and the bread was honest in their stomach, and this gray-eyed liar across from them had just handed over the most dangerous thing either of them owned: the truth, unarmored.

"There's a code," they heard themselves say. "Not the one I carried last week — that's delivered and dead. The next one. The final one. I don't know its contents yet, only that when it comes, everything depends on who holds it when the signing happens. My handler trusts no one, least of all me, and the occupiers would burn half the district to intercept it, and I am one tired person with ink on my fingers standing between all of it and nothing." They pulled the collar tighter. "My mother is dying by inches in a house with no coal. My brother is eleven and already knows how to tail a man through a market, which is a sentence no child should make true. And I spend every day being invisible on purpose, and you're the first person in two years who's actually looked at me."

The words hung in the freezing air. Xanthe hadn't meant to say that last part. It had simply walked out.

Gideon was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had lost its careful flatness entirely.

"What's your name? Your real one. Not the one on the census."

"Xanthe."

"Gideon." He said it like a handshake. "Well, Xanthe. Here's what two professionals in collapse can offer each other: I know every household register, every false address, every empty attic in six districts, and every clerk whose stamp can be bought. You move through this city like smoke. If your code comes, and the wrong people start looking — and they will — I can build you a trail of ghosts so convincing they'll arrest three innocent phantoms before they get warm."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, when your code is delivered and done — if you're still standing — help me choose who takes the berth. You've got a family. You'll weigh it honestly. I can't anymore; I've been weighing it alone so long every name sounds the same."

It was madness. It was alliance with an unknown quantity, the exact thing two years of discipline forbade. Xanthe finished the bread, dusted the gray crumbs from their knees, and found that beneath the fear there was something unfamiliar and almost unbearable: relief. The specific relief of setting down a load in front of a witness.

"If you betray me," Xanthe said, "I will make sure the last thing anyone remembers about Gideon Hale is accurate and unflattering."

"Noted. If you betray me, I'll enter you into the census twice. Once as deceased." He stood, brushed off his coat, and for a moment the wind shifted and the morning light caught his face and he looked, briefly, like someone who had once been young and unguarded in a country that existed before the war. "Same queue tomorrow. Terrible bread. Better reasons."

He was ten steps gone when Xanthe called after him, low, urgent, the words carrying across the frost:

"Gideon. The chalk mark — someone renewed it this morning."

He stopped. Turned. And the ease drained out of his face like water through sand.

"I tore the page out of my book last night," he said slowly. "No one else knows that house exists on my route."

Behind him, at the far corner of the square, a man in a liaison office greatcoat folded his newspaper and began walking — not toward the bakery, but toward Lantern Row.
