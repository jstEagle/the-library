## 9. What the Lantern Holds

The lighthouse had not been lit in eleven years.

Gideon had learned that from the harbor registry, the way he learned everything — quietly, in margins, in the small print beneath the small print. The Vessany Point light was decommissioned in the first year of the war, its keeper pensioned off to a village that no longer existed, its lamp sold for brass. What remained was a stone tower with a rusted gallery rail and a spiral stair that complained under weight, standing at the elbow of the breakwater like an old soldier who had forgotten which war he'd fought in and now saluted everything out of habit.

He climbed it twice a week. He told himself it was for the view — the whole harbor laid out in miniature, the masts of ships that no longer came, the rooftops of the lower town where the Thornbury windows caught the last of the light each evening. He could see the square from up there. He could see, on clear nights, whether a candle burned behind the cracked pane of the third floor, and when it did, he would stand at the gallery rail with his hands behind his back like a man taking an honest inventory of his city, and he would feel like a thief.

Tonight there was a candle burning.

Tonight Xanthe Thornbury was climbing the stair behind him, breathing hard from more than the steps, one hand trailing the cold stone wall as though to reassure themselves the tower was real.

"You bring every census subject here?" Xanthe said. Their voice had the flat brightness they used for jokes — Gideon had catalogued that too, the way a man catalogues weather. "Or am I special?"

"You're a security risk," Gideon said. "That's nearly the same thing."

The lamp room opened above them like the inside of a bell. The great lens was gone; only its mounting remained, a skeletal cradle of iron ribs reaching toward the cupola, and in the center of it, absurd and domestic, sat the lantern.

It was nothing much to look at. A ship's lantern, brass gone green at the seams, glass panes fogged with salt, a handle curved like the neck of a patient bird. It hung from an iron hook where the great lamp's counterweight used to run, and it swung very slightly in the draft that came through the vent slats, ticking against its own hook like a clock that had lost track of the hour but refused to stop keeping time.

Xanthe stopped on the last step. Gideon watched them take the room in — the ribs of iron, the sea sound coming up through the stonework, the lantern turning on its hook — and he watched their face do something complicated and then put itself away.

"Why are we here?" Xanthe asked.

"Because you asked me what I was doing in the market," Gideon said. "And because I've decided you're going to hate me either way, and I'd rather you hated me knowing the truth."

He crossed the lamp room. His boots rang on the plates. He lifted the lantern down from its hook, and it was heavier than a lantern should be, and Xanthe saw him feel that weight, saw the way their whole body went still — courier-still, the stillness of a person whose livelihood is noticing things.

"The glass is original," Gideon said. "The rest isn't."

He set the lantern on the iron plinth where the lamp once stood, turned it so the door latch faced them, and took out his knife.

* * *

He had rehearsed this speech for six days. He had rehearsed it in the bathhouse, in the bread queue, in his office over the ledgers with the door barred at noon while the clerks were at lunch. In rehearsal it had been dignified. It had begun, he was fairly sure, with an account of the debt — the shape of a girl called Mirela Sandoval on a dock in the second winter of the war, a crate she should not have been sitting on, a patrol boat running without lights — and it proceeded through gratitude to confession to purpose, a clean architecture of words, load-bearing all the way down.

What came out instead, with the knife blade working at the lantern's false base, was:

"I killed someone."

The base plate came free with a squeal of old solder. Xanthe said nothing. Outside, far below, the sea worked at the breakwater stones with infinite patience, the same sentence over and over.

"Not with my hands," Gideon said. "With my forms. That's how it's done now, did you know? You don't need a rifle. You need a ledger and a stamp and a Tuesday afternoon." He drew out the packet that had lived in the lantern's hollow base for four months, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord. "There was a family on Corvel Street. Printer named Adel Ardo. He ran pamphlets in the first year — nothing I'd have read twice, honestly, bad prose, worse metaphors, the kind of thing men write when they've just discovered they're angry. Someone reported him. A neighbor wanting his shop, probably, or his wife, or nothing at all — people report each other for less now. And the liaison office needed quotas met for the relocation rolls, and I was the census man, and my signature moved the Ardos from Column A to Column B."

He unwrapped the oilcloth. His hands were steady, which seemed to him a moral failing.

"Column B was the transport to the eastern works. Fourteen people on my say-so. The Ardos had three children." He looked at the packet instead of at Xanthe. "Mirela Sandoval pulled two hundred and thirty people out by boat before they caught her. Two hundred and thirty people, and one census clerk who owed her his life from before the war, and I couldn't even do the arithmetic correctly until after the transports stopped running. After, everyone can do the arithmetic. That's the trick of it. Conscience keeps banker's hours."

"What's in your hands, Hale," Xanthe said. Not a question. Their voice had lost all its varnish.

Gideon turned the packet over. Beneath the oilcloth: cream-colored paper, heavy stock, the kind that hadn't been printed in Vessany since the second autumn. An official seal, embossed and real. A signature in violet ink. He laid it flat on the iron plinth in the gray window light.

An evacuation berth certificate. Berth One, Priority Class, vessel unspecified, port of embarkation unspecified, valid upon armistice declaration for passage of the bearer to any signatory nation accepting displaced persons. Stamped, countersigned, sealed. Genuine. There were perhaps forty such certificates left unissued in the whole of the Republic of Vessany, and every ministry that knew of them was lying about the number.

"This is why I took the census posting," Gideon said. "Not for the wage. Not for the access, though I've used both. Mirela — before they took her — she got this out of the harbor commission through channels I won't describe, and she gave it to me, and she said—" His throat closed on the memory of it: her chapped hands, her total lack of ceremony, the way she'd said it like instructions for fixing a pump. "She said, *give it to somebody worth the ink.*"

The lantern ticked on its hook. Somewhere below, a gull made its complaint.

"And so I requested assignment to the worst district in the city," Gideon said, "and I walked the rounds, and I kept my lists, and I looked at every household in the lower town and asked myself the only question that has mattered to me in three years of asking other questions." He straightened. "Who is worth the ink?"

* * *

Xanthe was looking at the certificate the way Gideon had seen starving people look through bakery windows — not with hunger exactly, but with a terrible adult knowledge of what hunger costs.

"It's genuine," Xanthe said.

"It's genuine."

"They'll honor it. At the docks. With everything falling apart."

"It's the one piece of paper in this country they cannot afford not to honor," Gideon said. "If word gets out that berth certificates are being refused, the corridor closes before it opens. Every occupying signatory signed onto it. It's the single thread of decency left in the whole arrangement, and even they know that if they snap it, there's nothing left to negotiate with but guns."

Xanthe picked it up. Their hands — ink-stained, chapped, steady as a surgeon's — held it at the edges like something consecrated. Gideon watched them read it top to bottom, then bottom to top, checking the seal, checking the countersignature, checking the watermark against the window light with an instinct so pure and practiced it hurt to watch. A lifetime of carrying dangerous paper distilled into one gesture.

"One bearer," Xanthe read aloud. "'Passage of the named bearer together with such effects as may be carried on the person.'" They lowered it. "One person. Not a family."

"One person."

Silence. Below them the harbor lay gray and empty, and beyond the mole the water darkened toward open sea, toward the shipping lanes where, in some few weeks or days, boats would leave and boats would not leave, and the difference between those two categories was being decided right now in rooms none of them would ever be allowed into.

"My mother is dying," Xanthe said.

It came out level. That was the thing about them, Gideon thought — they delivered the largest truths in the flattest voice, as if volume might crack the cargo. "Her lungs. It's the coal smoke, or it was always going to be her lungs, and the coal smoke simply got there first. She won't survive the occupation winters. She may not survive this one. There's a medicine — there was a medicine — and now there's a tea that smells like boiled rope, and Tam boils it for her every night and pretends it's working, and she lets him pretend, because she's kind, and kindness in this family looks a great deal like lying."

They looked at the certificate again.

"Tam is fourteen," they said. "Fourteen years old and he hoards scrap metal in a cache under the kitchen floor like a magpie with a grudge, and he follows strangers through markets, and he thinks I don't know about any of it, and he is entirely too young to bury a mother alone in a city that's about to fall." Their thumb moved along the certificate's edge. "And you're standing there looking at me like—"

"I'm not looking at you like anything."

"You're looking at me like a man watching someone do arithmetic he already did." Xanthe's voice frayed at last, one thread of it. "You know what I do. You've known since the day you saw my hands. So you know that I know how many people are in this district, and this city, and you've handed me one name's worth of salvation and asked me to hold it up against all of them, and you want to know what I'm going to do with it."

"No," Gideon said.

They both stopped.

"I didn't hand it to you to test you," Gideon said. "I'm not your handler, Thornbury. I'm not the ministry. I don't want to know what you'll do with it — if I wanted that, I'd have left it anonymous and watched your windows, which is what I did for four months, and I'm telling you now that watching was the coward's version and I'm done with it." He took a breath. The sea filled the pause. "I gave it to you because of the chalk mark."

Xanthe blinked. "The chalk—"

"The morning after I marked your door. You weren't the only house I marked — you know that, surely. Half the lower town was marked that week; the liaison office wanted a purge of the river district before the freeze. My marks sent eleven households to interrogation." He said it flatly, because dressing it up would be worse. "Ten of them came back in two days. Broken, fined, frightened, but back. Your brother scrubbed the mark off your door within an hour, and then he followed me through the fish market for forty minutes with a boat-hook hidden under his coat — yes, I saw it, I've been fourteen myself — and when he lost me at the customhouse, he went home, and he said nothing to you. Nothing. He carried it alone for three days rather than put fear on your shoulders."

Gideon looked out at the water.

"A boy who protects his family that well," he said, "in a household where the eldest carries the republic's secrets sewn into a coat lining, and the mother coughs blood into a rag and smiles about it — I made a decision at a market stall over a bribed dock clerk, and it's the only decision I've made with a clean conscience since the Ardos. I couldn't save two hundred and thirty people. I can't save this district. But Mirela told me to find somebody worth the ink, and I decided the Thornburys were worth the ink, and I've spent four months making sure of it, and now the paper is in front of you and I will not tell you whose name belongs on it. Because the moment I do, I'm just another clerk moving people between columns."

* * *

Xanthe turned away from him.

They walked to the seaward vent, certificate in hand, and stood with their back to him, and Gideon let them stand, because there are conversations that can only be conducted with a person's spine. Through the vent slats he could see the slice of harbor, the mole, the far shore where the occupiers' searchlights were beginning to wake for the evening, pale worms of light feeling along the hills.

He thought about the first time he'd seen Xanthe Thornbury — really seen them, past the forgettable face and the busy hands. A year ago, almost. The bridge over the millrace, in rain, and a figure ahead of him who slipped on the wet cobbles and caught themselves without dropping the parcel under their arm, without breaking stride, without appearing to notice — and who had then, in the same motion, glanced back and counted him: hat, badge, gait, threat-level, forgotten. Two seconds of work. Nobody moved like that unless they'd been trained by necessity, and nobody survived training by necessity without something enormous to protect.

He had gone home that night and written, in the margin of his census ledger, beside the Thornbury entry, a single word he later scratched out and could still read: *worth.*

"My mother," Xanthe said to the vent slats, "would never take it. You understand that. If I put her name on it, she'll laugh at me — she'll laugh, she laughs when she coughs now, it's obscene — and she'll say her life is spent and mine isn't, and she'll say it gently, which is how she wins arguments, and she'll win." A breath. "And if I give it to Tam, I'm sending a fourteen-year-old alone across the water to a country where he doesn't speak the language, to wait out a war with strangers, and if she dies here while he's crossing, he'll carry that the rest of his life, and I'll have chosen it for him."

"And if you keep it?" Gideon asked quietly.

Xanthe laughed — one syllable, no humor in it, the exact laugh they must have inherited from her. "If I keep it, I abandon the last code I'm carrying, and the last code decides whether thousands get out or nobody does, and I know that, and you know that, because you read my hands months ago and you've known ever since that I'm not a person who gets to have a berth."

They turned around. The light through the vent cut across their face, half-lit, half-shadowed, and their eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear.

"So thank you, Gideon Hale," they said, "for your clean conscience and your worthy hands. Thank you for handing the most impossible choice in Vessany to a courier three weeks before the armistice, wrapped in a dead lighthouse's lantern, because a smuggler told you to find somebody worth the ink." They held the certificate up between two fingers. It trembled very slightly, the only tremble they'd allowed themselves. "Because here is what you've actually done. You haven't given me a way to save one life. You've given me a reason to count, precisely, what every other life is worth. My mother's laugh. My brother's scrap-metal hoard. The corridor full of strangers I'll never meet. All of it on one sheet of cream paper, and whichever name I write, the others become arithmetic."

The lantern ticked against its hook. Tick. Tick. A clock that had lost track of the hour.

"I know," Gideon said. "I did the same counting four months ago, in this room, holding this paper. There's no clean way to hold it. There's only who holds it."

Xanthe folded the certificate along its crease — carefully, a courier's fold, corners aligned — and put it inside their coat, against their chest, where Gideon understood without asking they carried everything that mattered.

"We should go down," they said. "It's getting dark, and if anyone sees us—" They stopped. Their head turned a degree, toward the seaward vents, and Gideon saw their whole body change, go courier-still, listening.

Out on the water, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of an engine — a launch, throttled low, running without lights along the inside of the mole where no honest boat had any business being at dusk.

And beneath it, farther off but closing, the whistle of the harbor patrol, two notes, repeated — the signal, unused for a year, that meant the docks were being sealed early.
