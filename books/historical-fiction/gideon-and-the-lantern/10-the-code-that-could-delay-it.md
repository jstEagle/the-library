## 10. The Code That Could Delay It

The summons came the way all summons came now: folded twice, no seal, left under the loose brick by the cistern where children once hid marbles and couriers now hid their lives.

*X. — Ministry annex, hour of the closing bell. Come alone. Come ordinary. — V.*

Vellory. My handler. The man who had taught me that a courier's first loyalty was to the paper, not the people who wrote it or the people it was written for. I read the note twice, fed it to the stove, and watched the words curl into ash that smelled like every other burned instruction of the past three years.

Ordinary. That was the trick of me. I had spent so long being forgettable that I sometimes caught my reflection in shop glass and felt a flicker of genuine surprise, as if the face there belonged to a stranger kind enough to lend me its coat. A woman of no particular age, brown hair pinned without artistry, hands red from cold, walking with the slight apologetic haste of someone late for an errand she didn't want to explain. Nobody's target. Everybody's neighbor.

I buttoned that face on and went out into the snow.

* * *

The ministry annex had been a school before the war. I knew it as a school; Tam had nearly gone there, in the brief spring when the future still pretended to exist. Now the windows were papered over with maps and proclamations, and two sentries stood at the door in coats too big for them, boys wearing their fathers' war. One checked my papers without reading them. The other looked at my hands — everyone looked at courier's hands eventually — and saw only chilblains.

Down. Always down. The basements of Vessany had become the true seat of the Republic, a country migrating underground the way animals move before an earthquake. Candles in jars along the stair. The smell of wet stone, ink, and fear kept warm.

Vellory was waiting in the archive room, standing beneath the shelves of dispatch ledgers with his coat still on, which told me he wasn't staying. He looked older than he had in autumn. Everyone did. He was a narrow man, gray at the temples, with the careful hands of someone who had spent thirty years handling documents worth more than the men who carried them. He had been kind to me once, in the way a locksmith is kind to the key that works.

"Thornbury." Not Xanthe. Never names, in the basement country.

"Sir."

"You have the final packet."

Not a question, but I gave him nothing anyway. "I carry what I'm given."

"Don't perform for me tonight." He pulled out a chair, didn't sit. "Sit. Please."

That *please* woke something in me before any words did. Vellory said please perhaps once a year, and always before bad weather.

"The armistice terms are signed in draft," he said. "They'll be executed in six days at the Customs Hall. You know this. What you don't know—" he stopped himself, and I watched a man decide how much truth a subordinate could survive, "—what you may not know is that there are... discussions. Late ones. About a clause."

My heart was doing its work quietly, the way it had learned to. "Discussions," I repeated.

"Article Nine. The disposition of the fleet, the ports, the civilian registries." He moved along the shelf, trailing one finger over the ledger spines like a man reading braille. "The occupiers want the surrender clean. Clean means fast. Fast means the borders seal within hours of the signature, and everything inside them — ships, papers, people — becomes theirs to sort at leisure."

"Everything," I said, "meaning everyone."

"I said people, Thornbury. Don't make me say it plainer." His finger stopped on a ledger. "There is a minority position among our own signatories. Minister Corvane and her bloc. They've drafted language — an amendment, an annex, whatever legal fig leaf they can manage — that would condition the port surrenders on a guaranteed evacuation corridor. Forty-eight hours of open passage after signature. Civilian traffic only. Under neutral observation, if we can beg anyone neutral enough to watch."

Forty-eight hours. I could see it already, the arithmetic assembling itself behind my eyes the way figures assembled for Tam, unbidden and merciless: how many boats, how many souls per boat, how many crossings in two days if the weather held and the gunners on both sides held too. Thousands. Maybe ten thousand. Maybe more, if panic could be organized into a queue.

"That clause needs to reach Corvane," Vellory said. "Before the signing. In writing, verified, because verbal assurances at this stage are worth exactly the breath they're spoken with."

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because the packet you're carrying contains the certified text of that amendment." He turned from the shelves and looked at me directly, and I understood, finally, why he was still wearing his coat. "It was never meant for the liaison office, whatever the routing slip says. The routing slip was written by men who intend for Article Nine to die in transit. Lost courier, damaged dispatch, regrettable fog of war. It happens all the time."

The candles guttered in their jars. Somewhere above us a door slammed and the whole building seemed to flinch.

"You're asking me to reroute," I said carefully. "Off the official channel. To deliver government property to a signatory who isn't the addressee."

"I'm telling you what the paper carries, so that you can't claim ignorance later, whichever later finds you." He came close then, and lowered his voice below even the room's tolerance. "There are men in this ministry who would burn that page rather than let it reach Corvane, because a corridor delays the signature, and delay costs them protections they've already been promised privately. And there are men on the other side who would kill to stop it, because ten thousand witnesses leaving with their tongues intact is a worse indictment than any treaty. You are currently the most dangerous person in Vessany, Thornbury, and you did it by being nobody. Congratulations."

I thought about laughing. It wouldn't come. What came instead was the memory of Gideon Hale's voice in the bread queue, low and stripped of performance: *Everyone in this city is carrying something they'd die before showing.* He hadn't known how literally I'd take it.

"Why me?" I asked. "Why tell me? Why not send it yourself?"

"Because I have a family," Vellory said, simply, terribly. "And everything I do between this moment and the signature will be done to keep them on the right side of whatever line gets drawn. Including, if necessary, disappointing you." He straightened his cuffs — the gesture of a man putting his armor back on piece by piece. "Corvane receives petitions at her residence until the ninth hour. She trusts no channels but hand-delivery anymore. You know how to be a hand."

* * *

I walked home the long way, which was to say I walked nowhere near home.

Snow was falling again, the fat, indifferent flakes of a winter that had stopped pretending to be weather and become a condition. The city moved beneath it like something sleeping — shuttered stalls, a tram frozen on its tracks since November, laundry lines heavy with white. Here and there a window glowed, and behind each glow I found myself imagining faces, counting them, the terrible new habit of a woman who now knew exactly what a corridor was worth per hour.

One berth versus ten thousand souls.

The thought arrived without permission, wearing Gideon's lantern-pass like a face. One certificate hidden in a lighthouse. One life, chosen. And now this — a clause, a corridor, a mathematics so large it swallowed the individual whole and called the swallowing mercy. I had spent three years believing that what I carried mattered because it was small: a fold of paper, sewn into a lining, invisible. Weightless. Tonight the weight of it sat against my ribs like a keel.

Because here was the shape of it, plain as a ledger: if I delivered the clause, Mother almost certainly died in place. Her lungs were failing; the doctors had said it in the gentle circumlocutions doctors use — *the damp, the cold, the strain of these years* — but I had carried enough documents to translate euphemism fluently. Two days of open passage might save ten thousand strangers, and my mother would not be among them, because a boat deck in February would kill her as surely as the occupation would, and slower. If I let the clause burn — if I simply walked too slowly, took the wrong turning, became the lost courier the routing slip already predicted — the armistice signed quickly, the border sealed, and the berth Gideon had given us became not one escape among thousands but one escape, period. Tam on the water. Mother wrapped in her own blankets, in her own bed, in her own city, with me beside her until the end, however it ended.

One life held close, or ten thousand held at a distance.

I stopped in the lee of the customs house and pressed my palm flat against the cold stone, and hated myself with a thoroughness that surprised me, because some traitorous part of me had already begun negotiating. *She's dying anyway,* the part whispered. *You'd only be choosing the manner of it. Ten thousand, Xanthe. Children. Do the sum.*

Tam could do the sum. Tam, who hoarded scrap metal and bartered like a dockside merchant and looked at me lately with eyes too old for fifteen. Tam, whose berth I had already decided was his without ever saying it aloud, because love makes cowards of us in the specific ways we least forgive.

But Mother —

Mother, who had run this same city as a girl, who knew the name of every cobblestone's grandmother, who had made me learn the districts not as territory but as family. *You can't abandon a place you won't bother to know,* she used to say, hanging washing, and I had built a whole life on the inversion: *you can survive any place you refuse to know.* Anonymous. Forgettable. A coat borrowed from a stranger's reflection.

The clause in my lining was heavier than paper had any right to be. Certified text. Corvane. Ninth hour. Six days.

* * *

There was one more errand before dark, because even archangels of diplomacy need their mother's medicine.

The apothecary on Chandler's Row had nothing left worth the name — syrup of poppies cut with honey, licorice root, faith. He weighed it out in silence, and I paid in the coins Tam had polished, and while he wrapped the bottle in brown paper he said, without looking up, "They're saying the end comes quick, once it starts. Signing at the Customs Hall. My brother works the docks; he says the boats are already being counted."

"Counted by whom?"

"By everyone, miss. That's the trouble." He slid the bottle across. "Counted by the occupiers, who want them for themselves. Counted by the ministry, who want them empty or scuttled. Counted by every soul in this queue, who count them the way starving men count other people's bread." He glanced up at last, and his eyes were the eyes of a man performing his final professional act: honesty, free of charge. "If you ever get a choice between a boat and a promise, take the boat. Promises sink."

I carried the medicine home through streets that had heard the same rumor, that were already leaning toward the harbor the way plants lean toward light. At the corner by the fountain — dry, cracked, filled with snow — I saw a boy chalk something on the wall, and my hand went to my knife before my mind caught up: just numbers. A price. Scrap metal, tin, copper. Tam's economy, everywhere now, the city bartering its bones.

Home was warm, barely. Tam had gotten a fire going with scavenged coal and a chair leg, and Mother was propped on her pillows in the front room where she could see the window, because she liked to watch the snow even now, especially now, as if it were correspondence from somewhere.

"There she is," she said. "My important daughter."

"I'm nobody's daughter of consequence. I fetch syrup."

"Mm." She took the bottle with hands that shook only slightly, and drank the dose Tam had measured, and looked at me over the rim with those clear gray eyes that had never once in my life failed to find the lie in me. "You have your delivery face on."

"This is my only face."

"It's the other one. The one that walks through walls." She set the bottle down. "Sit a moment, Xanthe. Whatever you're carrying in that coat, set it down on the chair and sit."

For one vertiginous second I genuinely considered it — unbuttoning the lining, laying the certified clause of Article Nine on a kitchen chair next to the mending, telling her everything. The census taker. The lantern. The berth. The arithmetic. But her hands were trembling again, faintly, and some truths are a weight the sick shouldn't be asked to help carry.

"I sat all morning," I said. "Tam's been feeding me nonsense about scrap prices."

"They've doubled," Tam reported from the hearth, with pride. "Copper's insane. Everybody's wiring their houses for the occupiers' money."

Mother smiled at him, and then at me, and the smile was the old one, the complete one, and it undid me more than Vellory's *please* had. "Whatever it is," she said mildly, settling back into her pillows, "you'll do the right thing. You always confuse yourself terribly beforehand, but you arrive at it in the end. Your grandmother was the same. It's the Marchetti curse — a conscience with poor manners."

I kissed her forehead. It was warm. Everything about her was still warm, and I clung to that the way a drowning woman clings to a count of ten.

Later, after Tam slept, I sat by the window with the coat across my knees and my sewing kit open, ostensibly checking the stitches in the lining, actually holding council with myself. Across the square, one window burned yellow in the dark — Gideon's office, or his lodging, wherever the census taker went to be alone with his forms and his secrets. Once, I could have sworn, a silhouette crossed it and paused. Watching, or remembering to watch. With Gideon I had learned the difference hardly mattered.

Six days. Ninth hour. Corvane.

Ten thousand souls against one warm forehead, and the needle in my hand making its small, obedient stitches, sealing the fate I hadn't yet chosen deeper into the cloth.

That was when I heard the knock. Three soft raps at the street door, at an hour when no honest caller knocks — and through the window, down in the snow below, a lantern swung once, deliberately, side to side.

Gideon's signal. Which meant that whatever he had discovered, whatever had driven him through a curfew street to my door at midnight, had come to him the way the summons had come to me:

from the ministry.
