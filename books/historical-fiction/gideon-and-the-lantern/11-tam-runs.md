## 11. Tam Runs

The chalk mark came back.

I'd scrubbed it off the doorframe three days ago — worked at it with a wet rag until my knuckles ached and the gray stone showed clean, no ghost of it left. I'd stood back and told myself that was the end of it. Marks wash off. That's the whole point of marks.

But this morning, going out to the pump with the water pail, I saw the new one. Smaller. Neater. A single vertical stroke with a hook at the bottom, like an L lying down, drawn in white chalk at the height of a grown man's hand.

He'd come back. The census man. Gideon Hale, with his soft voice and his notebook and his questions that weren't on any form. He'd walked past our door in the dark or the fog or whatever hour men like him do their work, and he'd redrawn what I erased, and he hadn't even bothered to make it look like weathering.

That was when I stopped being scared and started being angry.

Anger is a funny thing in a city like Vessany in its last winter. There's not much room for it. Fear takes up most of the space, and cold takes the rest, and grief fills in the gaps like mortar between bricks. But anger found me that morning, standing on our step with a pail of water going cloudy in my hands, and it fit itself into some hollow place I hadn't known was empty, and it stayed.

Because here's what I knew by then. I knew Xanthe carried something. I didn't know what — nobody tells a fourteen-year-old anything in this family, which is its own kind of insult — but I knew the shape of it. I knew Xanthe went out before dawn and came back after curfew with ink stains they scrubbed too hard at, and I knew the coat with the strange heavy lining that never left the peg by the door even when the lining should have frozen stiff like every other coat in Vessany. I knew Mother watched Xanthe leave with her eyes open too wide, the way you watch someone walk a frozen river. And I knew the census man had asked his one question too many, and Xanthe had lied to him beautifully, and he had marked our door anyway.

So: either Gideon Hale was hunting my sibling, or he wanted what my sibling carried. Either way, he was a buyer or a seller, and both kinds of men deal in the same currency now.

Berths.

Everyone in Vessany talked about berths the way people used to talk about bread prices or the weather. The armistice was coming — everyone could feel it coming, the way you feel a storm in your teeth — and when it signed, the harbor would seal, and whoever wasn't on a boat would be inside the walls when they closed. Three berths. That's all I needed. One for Mother, whose cough had gone from a rattle to a wet tearing sound that I heard through the wall every night while I lay pretending to sleep. One for Xanthe, who would argue, who would say take yourself, take Mother, leave me — I knew my sibling well enough to write their refusal in advance. And one for me, which was the only part of the arithmetic that made sense to anyone else.

Three berths for information about the code. It seemed fair. It seemed more than fair. Men were paying in gold teeth and heirloom watches for less.

I want to be honest about what I did next, because everyone in this story keeps telling themselves pretty versions of what they did, and somebody has to say it plain: I didn't think about Xanthe. Not really. I thought about Mother's cough, and I thought about proving I wasn't a child, and underneath both of those I thought about how good it would feel to walk into that liaison office on Cordwainer Street and have a grown man in an occupier's uniform listen to me. Write down what I said. Take me seriously as a person with something to sell.

That's the truth. I was fourteen years old and invisible, and I mistook being dangerous for being seen.

* * *

The liaison office had been a tailor's shop before the war. You could still see it — the brass hooks along one wall where coats once hung waiting for alteration, the long mirror cracked diagonally, the fitting platform in the corner where now a desk sat piled with folders. The occupiers hadn't bothered repainting. Maybe they liked the irony. Maybe they didn't notice. They didn't notice most things; that was the great discovery of the occupation, the thing that surprised me more than anything else that winter. The men in the gray-green uniforms were not giants. They were tired clerks with rifles, mostly, cold and underfed and counting days just like us.

Except the one behind the desk that morning.

He had a name tag I couldn't read from where I stood in the queue of petitioners — a line of maybe fifteen people clutching papers, permits, grievances — and a face like a closed ledger. He wasn't Vessany-born. His accent, when he spoke, was the flat administrative accent of the capital across the strait, the accent from the radio broadcasts that Mother made Tam turn off, that she made me turn off, her hand shaking as she reached for the dial.

I'd rehearsed my opening on the walk over. *I have information about a diplomatic dispatch currently held within the city.* Formal. Adult-sounding. I'd practiced not letting my voice crack on *diplomatic.*

The queue moved slowly. An old woman ahead of me argued about a confiscated stove for eleven minutes and got nowhere and shuffled out looking smaller than when she'd come in. A man with a cartwright's forearms submitted a travel request and was told, without looking up, that travel requests were suspended. Everyone absorbed these small deaths quietly, the way we'd all learned to, and the clerk stamped things, and the mirror in the corner held all of us upside down and broken.

Then it was my turn, and my mouth went dry as paper, and I said my line anyway.

*I have information about a diplomatic dispatch currently held within the city.*

The clerk looked up. That was the first sign I'd miscalculated — he looked up. In twenty minutes of watching, I hadn't seen him look up at anyone. His eyes went past me to the door, to the street, back to me. And he said, very quietly, in that flat capital accent:

"Step around the side counter, please."

There is a moment — I've thought about it so many times since that it has worn smooth, like a stone handled for years — there is a moment when you understand you have made a mistake, and your body knows it before your mind finishes the sentence. My body knew. My feet wanted the door. But the door had a soldier beside it now, a soldier who hadn't been beside it when I came in, or maybe he always was and I'd been too busy rehearsing to see him, and either way the distance from the side counter to the door had become a very long distance indeed.

"Where did you get this information?" the clerk asked. He'd produced a form. Of course he'd produced a form. Everything in the occupation ran on forms; they'd brought pallets of them across the strait along with the rifles.

"Around," I said. "People talk."

"People talk." He wrote it down. *Subject claims hearsay origin.* I could read upside down; I'd learned it off Xanthe's discarded drafts years ago. "What is your name?"

And here is where I nearly lost everything, because I opened my mouth to give my real name — my actual name, the name attached to the household record of a certain census taker, the name written in a school registry and a ration book and God knew what other ledgers — and then some animal instinct clamped down on my tongue a half-second before the first letter escaped.

"Tomas Wren," I said. It was the name of a boy who'd died in the second year of the war, a boy from our street, a boy nobody in this office would ever check on because dead boys don't generate paperwork. I'd stolen a dead child's name and I felt nothing about it in that moment except relief.

"And this dispatch," the clerk said, pen moving. "You can identify its carrier?"

"No," I said. Too fast. "No. I just know it exists. I know what it's worth."

"Do you." He set the pen down. He folded his hands. And he smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, worse than any threat, because it was patient. "Boy. Do you know what happens to children who walk into liaison offices claiming to know about diplomatic dispatches?" He didn't wait for me to answer. "They are detained pending verification. Their households are detained pending verification. Their schools, their landlords, their neighbors — pending verification. Verification takes time. Time is something we have a great deal of, and your city has very little."

My ears went hot and then cold. *Their households.* Mother. Xanthe. I had walked in here carrying nothing but my own cleverness, and I had handed them a thread, and threads get pulled.

"Now," the clerk said pleasantly, "let's try again. Where do you live?"

And that is when the door opened and Gideon Hale walked in.

* * *

I heard him before I saw him — that mild, unhurried voice saying, "Excuse me, excuse me, official census business," and then he was through the queue and past the soldier, who half-raised a hand and then didn't finish raising it, because Gideon had done the trick of moving like a man who cannot be stopped without paperwork. He had a clipboard. He had his badge. He looked exactly like what he claimed to be, which I suppose is the definition of a good liar.

"Census reconciliation," he announced to the room at large, tapping the clipboard. "District Seven returns are due for audit and this office is holding two of my enumeration books. I've been sent for them." He said it to the soldier, to the clerk, to the ceiling — to everyone and no one, a man performing boredom so convincingly that boredom became contagious.

"We're conducting an interview," the clerk said.

"Are you." Gideon glanced at me then — one flick, half a heartbeat, eyes flat as a winter pond — and I understood that I was supposed to be nobody to him. A stranger. A child in a queue. "Is he on your enumeration list? Because if he's a district resident and you're interviewing him outside protocol, I'll need to note it. Cross-jurisdiction complaints go to the civil commission. They're backed up nine weeks."

The clerk's jaw tightened. Nine weeks. In nine weeks there would be no civil commission, no district seven, possibly no clerk. We all did the same arithmetic at the same moment, and I watched the clerk decide that I was not worth it.

"Take the boy out," he said finally, waving at the soldier. "He's wasting the office's time."

Gideon took my elbow. Firm, impersonal, a shepherd's grip. "Come along, then," he said loudly, in the voice adults use for delinquent children, and marched me past the queue, past the cracked mirror where I saw us both upside down — his face composed, mine burning — and out into the street, where the cold hit like a slap and kept slapping.

He didn't let go until we'd turned two corners. Then he stopped in the lee of a shuttered chandlery and rounded on me, and for the first time since I'd known him, Gideon Hale looked genuinely angry. Not the soft menace of the doorstep. Real anger, hot under the skin.

"What," he said, "in the name of every saint this city ever gave up on, did you think you were doing?"

"I was—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't build the speech. I watched you rehearse it on Cordwainer Street. You were mouthing it at shop windows. *Diplomatic dispatch,*" he said, dropping his voice to a furious whisper, "you said *diplomatic dispatch* to an occupying liaison officer. Do you know what they do to couriers' families? Do you know what they did to the Ferro family in March? They took the mother first, to make the son talk, and the son talked, and they took them both anyway, because a witness who talks is a witness who might un-talk."

"They'd have given me berths," I said, and hated how young my voice sounded. "Three berths. For the code. That's a fair trade, that's—"

"That's a trade with men who don't honor trades." He stepped closer. Snow was starting again, fine and dry, sifting down between the rooftops. "Listen to me, Tam. Listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to pretend this conversation never happened, and so are you. Your brother—" he caught himself, adjusted, "your *sibling* is carrying the only thing standing between this city and a closed border. Not selling it. Carrying it. Every day Xanthe walks out that door, Xanthe bets their life that the code reaches the right hands, and every day you do something like today, you add weight to the other side of the scale. You nearly handed them a thread to pull. *Tomas Wren*," he added, and something almost like grim humor crossed his face. "Dead boy's name. Quick thinking. Sloppy otherwise."

"How do you know he's dead?"

"I take censuses," Gideon said. "I know everyone who's dead in this district. It's most of what I know." He looked at me for a long moment, and the anger drained out of him and left something wearier behind, something that looked almost like recognition. "You did it for your mother. The cough. I know about the cough — I hear it from the square."

"You've been listening to our house?"

"I've been watching your house since the day I marked the door," he said simply. "Someone has to. The people who marked it before me weren't checking whether you'd erase it. They were confirming the address." He let that land. It landed hard; I felt it in my chest. "Go home, Tam. Go home and stay home. Let the people whose job it is to carry the heavy things carry them."

"And if they drop them?"

"Then we'll all find out what we're made of." He turned up his collar against the snow. "Walk behind me to the square. If anyone asks, I escorted a truant back toward the school ward. It's even mostly true."

He started walking. After a moment, I followed.

We didn't speak again. He kept ten paces ahead of me the whole way, a stranger escorting a stranger, and only at the mouth of the square did he pause, without turning around, and say — quietly enough that the snow nearly took it:

"For what it's worth, the trade you tried to make — three berths for one secret — it's the same trade everyone in this city is making right now, one way or another. You're not a fool, Tam Thornbury. You're just early. Try to survive being early."

Then he walked on toward the ministry quarter, and I stood in the falling snow with my heart still hammering and my stolen name still stuck in my throat like a fishbone, and I understood two things at once, the way you only understand things at fourteen, completely and uselessly.

The first was that Gideon Hale was not our enemy. I still didn't know what he was. But he had spent something in that office — credibility, safety, I'd learn later exactly how much — to pull a stupid boy out of a room with a patient man and a form, and men don't spend that on marks.

The second was that I had to tell Xanthe. Tonight. Before the clerk's office finished whatever verification they'd start anyway, before the thread I'd handed them got pulled taut. Because somewhere in that building was a sheet of paper with *Tomas Wren* written on it, and a description, and a date, and a connection waiting to be made to a household on a census list — a household already marked twice in chalk.

I went home the long way, doubling back twice like Xanthe had taught me without ever admitting they'd taught me, and I came through our door white-faced and snow-crusted, and Xanthe took one look at me and knew.

"What did you do?" they said.

And I opened my mouth, and for once in my life, I told the truth immediately.
