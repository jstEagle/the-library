## 12. The Handler's Offer

Tam told me everything in the space between one cough of his and the next — how he'd found the chalk mark before I'd ever noticed it, how he'd scrubbed it off with a wet sleeve and followed Gideon Hale through the fish market like a detective out of a serial, how he'd seen the money change hands at the dock clerk's window, and how — this was the part that made my stomach drop through the floorboards — he'd gone to the occupying liaison office on Channel Street with a proposition of his own.

"They said they'd give us three berths," he said. His voice cracked in the middle like a boy's and not a courier's. "Three. For you and Mother and me. All I had to do was tell them what I knew about the code."

I didn't shout. That surprised me most, afterward — that I didn't shout. I sat down across from him at the kitchen table where Mother's medicine bottles made a little amber forest, and I looked at my brother, fourteen years old, snow melting in his hair, and I understood with terrible clarity that he had done exactly what I would have done at fourteen, exactly what I had been doing for two years: he had tried to trade what he knew for the people he loved.

"Tam." I kept my voice very level. "What did you actually tell them?"

"Nothing real," he said quickly. "I swear it. I said there was a dispatch coming through the courier lines, something important, something that could hold up the signing. I didn't know anything true to tell them, so I — I made it bigger than it was. Made it sound like I was worth three berths." He swallowed. "Then the man behind the desk asked me to describe the courier who carried it. And I realized he wasn't going to let me leave the room until I did."

"And Gideon—"

"Gideon was there." Tam stared at his hands. "He came in right behind me. I thought — I thought he'd turned me in. But then he told them I was his census informant, that he was running me, that if they detained me they'd blow a months-long intelligence operation over one half-informed boy. He put his own name on it. His own badge." Tam's eyes came up, wet and furious with himself. "They let me walk because taking me would have cost *them* something. He made me expensive."

I closed my eyes. So Gideon Hale had stood in an occupying liaison office and spent his cover like coin to buy my brother's freedom — a stranger's brother, or not such a stranger anymore — knowing what that expenditure might cost him later. Knowing that men like the desk officer wrote everything down, and that a census taker protecting a courier's family drew a line straight back to the courier herself.

"You're never doing that again," I said.

"I know."

"I mean it, Tam. You don't go near any office, any queue, any man with a stamp. You are fourteen and your job is to be nobody."

"I know." And then, quieter, the age showing through: "I just wanted to fix it. You carry everything. Mother's medicine, the codes, all of it. I wanted to carry something for once."

Something in my chest turned over and refused to be angry anymore. I reached across the table and gripped his wrist, hard, the way our father used to grip ours when he meant *I love you* without saying it.

"You carried me home," I said. "You walked out of that office. That's more than most people manage this winter."

That was when the knock came at the door.

Three knocks, evenly spaced, unhurried. Nobody knocked like that in occupied Vessany — refugees pounded, occupiers battered, neighbors scratched softly and hoped you'd pretend not to hear. Three even knocks belonged to only one kind of man: a man who had never once in his life needed anything urgently, because the world rearranged itself around him instead.

Tam went white. I put a finger to my lips and moved to the door and opened it, and there in the stairwell gaslight stood Corvin Ashe, my handler, six years my senior in the ministry and twenty years my senior in the craft of looking unbothered, holding his hat in one hand and a paper-wrapped parcel in the other.

"Master Thornbury," he said, using the name I'd worn so long it fit like an old coat. "I brought coal. Real coal, not the slate they sell at the ration yards. May I come in?"

* * *

He gave the parcel to Tam with a smile, complimented the tidiness of our rooms, sat down carefully in the chair nearest Mother's door as though proximity to the sick were a courtesy, and declined tea. Then he sent Tam out — "Run along to Verrin's shop and see if they've any lamp oil, there's a good lad" — and waited until the door clicked shut before he folded his hands on his knees and became, visibly, a different man.

"The boy went to the liaison office yesterday," he said.

My blood went cold and stayed cold. "He's a child. Children do foolish things."

"He did a foolish thing that rang a bell I spent four years keeping silent." Ashe's voice never rose. It never had. In six years of working under him I had heard him shout exactly once, at a funeral, and I still wasn't certain it hadn't been grief rather than anger. "The occupiers keep records now. Cross-referenced records. Your address is attached to the courier route I have spent two years making look like nothing. Do you understand what nearly happened here?"

"Then why aren't I in a cellar somewhere being questioned by men who don't bring coal?"

He almost smiled. Almost. It came and went like weather. "Because I got to the record first. Because I am your handler, and handling is what I do." He leaned forward. "But that isn't why I've come, Xanthe. I've come because the final dispatch is moving. Yours."

The room seemed to tilt a degree. I kept my face still. Two years of practice; my face could have taught a course.

"It's in the city?" I said.

"You know it is. You've known since the navy order." He watched me with those patient gray eyes. "You're very good. Better than I told my superiors you were, which was already better than they believed. So let's not perform for each other. The dispatch you carry contains the armistice annex. Clause nine, specifically. You may not have read it — couriers who read die faster — but I suspect you have read it, because you're you. Shall I say it aloud anyway?"

I said nothing. He took that as permission.

"Clause nine permits either signatory to invoke a forty-eight-hour suspension of execution upon petition of any accredited ministry officer, pending third-party verification." He recited it flatly, like scripture memorized by someone who no longer believed in the church. "Forty-eight hours. Long enough, if invoked properly, to open an evacuation corridor through the eastern harbor. Boats, Xanthe. Thousands of berths instead of dozens. The difference between a city that surrenders its people and a city that surrenders its flag."

"And the ministry wants it delivered to the signatory who'll honor it," I said. "That's the mission. That's why I still exist."

"Yes," Ashe said. "That was the mission."

The word *was* landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"My superiors have reconsidered," he went on, in the same mild voice. "The delay clause embarrasses the signatories. It complicates the transfer of ministries. There are men — very senior men, men whose names you'd know — whose families are already booked on private transports leaving Thursday morning, ahead of the armistice, ahead of everything. If clause nine passes, the armistice slips two days. Those transports become irregular. Subject to inspection. Subject to seizure." He spread his hands, palms up, a gesture of terrible reasonableness. "You can see their difficulty."

I could see it with perfect clarity. I could see everything, suddenly, the whole rotten architecture laid open: every coded instruction, every safe house, every sacrifice I had absorbed into myself like soot — the courier who died on Wren Street, the network burned in autumn, the names I'd carried and never learned — all of it built to protect a machine whose operators intended, at the end, simply to step off it and sail away while the city drowned behind them.

"And you?" My voice came out strange, distant. "Where do you stand, Corvin?"

For a moment — one genuine moment — he looked tired. Old, even. He glanced toward Mother's door.

"My wife and daughter are in Kelvaren," he said. "Have been since spring. There's a transport Thursday. Two berths on it, in the name of a family that will never use them — I've seen to that. Immunity documentation as well, for whatever the occupiers' police decide to make of registered ministry personnel after the signing." He turned back to me. "Deliver the dispatch to me instead of to Ambassador Vell. I will see clause nine filed as lost in transit. You walk away clean, your mother gets treatment on the far side, your brother grows up in a country that isn't this one. Everyone you love lives."

"And everyone else drowns."

"The corridor isn't guaranteed," he said sharply, and for the first time something real came through, something urgent. "You think clause nine is salvation? I've watched stronger petitions die in committee. Forty-eight hours bought by a courier's memory against the weight of three governments — they'll find a reason to void it. They always find a reason. Meanwhile the sure thing walks out of this room on two feet: your family, whole, across the water, alive." He leaned in. "Xanthe. I have known you since you were nineteen and terrified and pretending not to be. You are the best instrument I ever built. Don't make me watch you break yourself against a wall for a corridor that exists on paper."

*An instrument.* Six years, and there it was, said aloud at last, in the kindest voice he owned. I looked at him — at the coal dust on his gloves, at the tiredness around his eyes that I chose, for six years, to read as conscience — and I felt something that wasn't hatred and wasn't grief but was some alloy of both, poured hot and left to set.

"When I was nineteen," I said, "you told me the republic was worth more than any one of us. You made me believe that serving it meant carrying whatever was asked and asking nothing back."

"I believed it then."

"And now you've got a Thursday boat."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the street crier called the evening ration numbers in a voice worn down to gravel.

"There's no version of this where you win," Ashe said quietly. "There's only versions where the people at your table live. Take the one on offer."

"I'll need until tomorrow to move my mother," I said. "She can't travel at night in this cold. Midday. I'll bring you the dispatch at midday, at the ministry, and we do the exchange there, papers first."

Relief moved through him like a current under ice. He believed me — or wanted so badly to believe me that belief stopped mattering. That was the thing about corruption wearing a familiar face: it had learned all your tells, and it trusted them, and trust was the one code I had never been trained to break.

He stood, settled his hat, paused at the door with his hand on the frame.

"For whatever it's worth," he said, not turning around, "if I could buy ten thousand berths instead of two, I would burn clause nine myself and sleep soundly. I'm not the villain of this, Xanthe. I'm just the man who counted, at the end, and found he had two."

Then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the stairs, even and unhurried to the last.

* * *

I sat at the table for a long time after. Through the window, across the square, the Thornbury-facing windows of Gideon Hale's rented office showed lamplight, then movement — a silhouette crossing, pausing, standing. Watching, or waiting, or merely existing in the small lit box of his own confinement. Two liars in a city of them, each holding something the other needed.

Because here was the arithmetic Ashe had handed me, whether he knew it or not: I had until midday. I had a dispatch I could not surrender and a clause I could not deliver through official channels, because the channels themselves had rotted from the top down. I had a mother who had already begun, in her quiet terrible way, arranging her own subtraction from our lives. I had a berth certificate — one — hidden in a defunct lantern across the square, in the keeping of a census taker who spent his cover to save my brother and asked nothing for it.

And I had, in my head, word for word, the only copy of clause nine that anyone in power intended should never see light.

Ashe thought he was buying the dispatch. The ministry thought the clause would die in transit. The occupiers thought a frightened boy had given them the shape of a secret.

They were all counting on the instrument to stay an instrument.

I pulled on my coat, checked that the codes sewn into its lining slept undisturbed, and looked once more at the lit window across the square. The silhouette had stopped moving. It stood very still, as if it too were doing arithmetic tonight — and as if, somehow, it had arrived at the same sum.

Midday, I had promised him. Twelve hours.

I went out into the snow and turned toward the lighthouse.
