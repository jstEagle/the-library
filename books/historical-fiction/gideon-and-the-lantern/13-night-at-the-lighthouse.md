## 13. Night at the Lighthouse

The shells came in from the east just after dark, and Xanthe ran for the only structure on the headland tall enough to be seen and foolish enough to offer shelter anyway.

They had been halfway back from the harbor office with a forged manifest folded against their ribs when the first flare went up, turning the snow a terrible chemical white. Then the sound — that long descending whistle Xanthe had learned to hear in dreams — and the impact somewhere behind the fish market, close enough that the ground bucked like a living thing. They didn't think. They ran for the lighthouse.

The door was already open. Gideon Hale stood in it, one hand outstretched, his face lit orange by something burning behind him.

"Get in," he said. "It's coming down all along the quay."

Xanthe took the hand without thinking about what it meant to take it, and then they were both inside and hauling the door shut against a wind full of ash, and the barrage rolled over the headland like weather, like judgment, like the end of the world conducted by competent professionals.

Inside, the lighthouse was nothing like Xanthe expected. They had imagined rust and gull droppings, a dead spiral of stairs. Instead there was warmth: a small iron stove, a crate of driftwood cut into lengths, a bedroll, a tin cup, a lamp turned low. And above them, filling the whole crown of the tower, the lantern itself — not dead at all but burning, its great lens throwing a slow wheel of light through the fogged glass, around and around, out over a sea that no ship was allowed to cross.

"You live here," Xanthe said, still breathing hard.

"I keep it." Gideon latched the door, checked it twice, dragged a heavy iron bracket across it that must have taken him an afternoon to rig. "Nobody lives anywhere anymore. But I keep this."

"Why light a beacon for a corridor nobody can use?"

He looked up at the lantern's slow rotation, and for a moment his face did something complicated — grief and guilt and a strange stubborn tenderness all fighting for the same ground. "Because the boats are going to come eventually," he said. "And because when everything else in Vessany is rubble, I wanted one thing still doing its job."

Another shell landed, closer. The whole tower shuddered; dust sifted from the joints of the stairs like flour from a torn sack. Xanthe braced against the wall and felt, absurdly, the manifest crinkle against their ribs — still there, still secret, still the most dangerous piece of paper in the republic. Outside, the war went about its work methodically, street by street, as if the city were being erased rather than captured.

Gideon pulled two crates together near the stove and sat down on one of them, and after a moment Xanthe sat on the other, and between them the stove ticked and breathed, and above them the light wheeled, and they waited out the bombardment the way people had waited out storms for ten thousand years — together, because alone was worse.

* * *

From Gideon's side of the silence, the arithmetic was unbearable.

He had watched Xanthe Thornbury for months across that square — the forgettable walk, the busy hands, the coat always a season wrong for the weather so nobody would look twice at what was underneath. He had knocked on their door with questions that weren't on any form. He had chalked their frame like a man marking prey. And every night since, he had climbed these stairs and sat under his stolen light and hated himself with a thoroughness that would have impressed the occupying army.

Now here they were three feet apart, sharing heat, and the shells were tearing apart the city where Xanthe's mother lay coughing and Tam hoarded scrap in some cellar, and Gideon thought: *I could have ended these people. One word into the right ledger.*

Instead he heard himself say, "I was a schoolteacher."

Xanthe's head came up. "What?"

"Before. In Corvenne, on the river. Thirty-one children, ages six to eleven. Arithmetic, penmanship, local history." He smiled, and it hurt his face, the first smile in weeks. "I was very strict about penmanship. There's a boy named Aldous who wrote his *g*'s backwards until June and I made him do a whole page every day, and when he finally got one right he held it up like he'd caught a fish."

Xanthe stared at him. The lamplight moved on their face, and Gideon watched the courier's mask try to reassemble itself and fail, and understood that he had just done something more dangerous than sheltering a spy. He had been seen.

"Why are you telling me that?"

"Because we might die in the next hour," Gideon said. "And if we die, I'd rather the last thing in my head be true."

* * *

The barrage slackened sometime past midnight, settling into that ugly irregular rhythm — one shell, then four minutes of ringing quiet, then another — that kept everyone awake and no one alive to anything but the waiting.

Xanthe turned their hands toward the stove. Ink-stained fingers, cracked knuckles, a scar across the left palm from a fence wire in some year that belonged to someone else. They tried to remember who that person had been, the one before the codes, before the sewn linings, before anonymity became a discipline practiced like scales.

"I sang," they heard themselves say.

Gideon looked over.

"At the Templedown market, Saturdays. My mother sold wool and I sang the buying songs, the call-and-response ones, you know them?" Xanthe hummed two bars before they could stop themselves — a rising third, a falling fifth, a tune built for shouting over crowds — and stopped, embarrassed, and found they didn't want to stop. "I had a voice on me. Mother said I'd sing my way to the capital someday. Tam used to sit on the sacks and drum on a flour tin, completely out of time, and I'd adjust the tempo so he could keep up, and neither of us ever admitted it."

"And now?"

"Now I carry paper that kills people quietly instead of loudly." Xanthe turned a hand over, studied the ink like a stain that had gone all the way through. "There's a dispatch I've been carrying for two days. You don't want to know what's in it. Or maybe you do, and that's exactly why I'll never tell you."

"I wasn't asking about the dispatch." Gideon's voice was quiet. "I was asking about the singing."

The question sat there, small and enormous. When had anyone last asked Xanthe a question whose answer cost nothing? Their handler asked for routes. The census taker had asked for names. Even their own thoughts had become inventories: what is carried, where is it safe, how long until signing.

"My throat still works," they said, and their voice betrayed something, and Gideon had the grace to look away and let them recover.

Outside, a shell fell into the harbor with a sound like the world swallowing. The water light in the windows shivered. Above them the beam swept round — dark water, dark sky, dark water — patient as a heartbeat that refused to consult the circumstances.

"Do you know why I became a census taker?" Gideon said, to the stove rather than to Xanthe. "It's the only occupation where asking questions about everyone is legal. I needed access to records. Names, addresses, household composition, movement histories." He paused. "You've guessed by now I'm not working for whoever printed my badge."

"I've guessed you're working for yourself," Xanthe said. "Which makes you either the most honest man in Vessany or the most dangerous."

"Both. Usually on the same day."

* * *

Somewhere in the small hours the barrage stopped entirely, and the silence that followed was so total it had texture, like snowfall, like held breath. The stove burned low. Gideon fed it another length of driftwood and the room filled with the smell of salt smoke, and Xanthe thought absurdly of their mother's kitchen, of wool and coal dust and the specific warmth of being nine years old with nowhere to be.

"Gideon." His name felt strange in their mouth, unpracticed. "On the census form. The question about births outside the district. Was that for your records or theirs?"

"For mine." He didn't flinch from it. "I was looking for someone. Someone who crossed the border eight years ago with a debt attached, and I thought — if I could find them, I could—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "There's a woman in Corvenne who hid me during the first conscription sweeps. Fed me out of her own ration book for a winter. She has a son she hasn't seen since. If I could find him a berth — a real one, a legitimate one — that would begin to pay it. That's all the espionage amounts to. A debt I can't stop counting."

Xanthe looked at him for a long moment. "You're the worst informer I've ever met."

"So I've been told." A ghost of a smile. "By a bread line, once."

The memory of that morning rose between them — the accusations hissed low over a shared loaf, two professionals stripped of their performances by sheer exhaustion, the astonishing experience of telling the truth to a stranger and finding it fit better than any lie. Since then there had been the lantern revealed, the berth certificate, the terrible bright hope of it. There had been Xanthe lying awake doing arithmetic that no conscience should have to do: one berth, three people, a corridor that might save thousands if a clause survived long enough to be spoken aloud.

"The berth," Xanthe said quietly. "You never told me who it was for. Originally. Before you gave it to me to decide."

"It was for anyone worthier than me," Gideon said. "That list turned out to be very long."

The stove ticked. The beam turned. And Xanthe found that they had shifted closer without deciding to — or Gideon had, or the room had shrunk, or the shells had rearranged the distance between all things in Vessany and left only this much gap, this handspan of warm air above a dying fire.

Their shoulders were almost touching. Not quite. The almost was louder than any shell.

"If we get out of this," Xanthe said, and their voice came out rougher than intended, "when the corridor opens — I'm going to sing again. At the dock. Badly, probably. Out of practice. Tam will drum on something and be completely out of time, and I'll slow down for him, and I won't admit it."

"I'll bring the census records," Gideon said. "Read everyone's name off as they board. Properly registered, properly counted." He turned his head, and his face was very close, lit from below by fire and from above by the slow sweep of the lantern. "Everyone accounted for. No one invisible."

"That sounds like the opposite of everything I've done for five years."

"Yes," he said. "Doesn't it sound like rest?"

Xanthe hadn't known they were crying until the tear reached their jaw. They didn't wipe it away. Under the circumstances, under the falling city, honesty seemed owed — to him, to the child at the market with the good voice, to the whole exhausted republic. Gideon's hand lay on the crate between them, palm up, open in a way that was not quite an offer and not quite an accident, and Xanthe looked at it and felt the almost stretch to its limit.

Their little finger moved. His answered. Half an inch of contact, warm skin against warm skin, the smallest possible treaty between two sovereign territories —

The shell hit the seawall.

The tower lurched. Glass rained somewhere below; the lantern fixture groaned on its gimbal; the beam swung wild and steadied. Both of them were on their feet without memory of standing, backs to the wall, hearts slamming, and when the ringing faded the handspan between them had widened again, the way distance does after artillery.

But the little finger remembered. Xanthe could feel it still, a point of heat like a coal carried in a bare hand, refusing to cool.

"Stay till dawn," Gideon said. "The roads will be watched all night. Nobody moves in this."

"There's a code I have to move by morning."

"I know. I'm not asking you to abandon it." He banked the stove, spread the bedroll, took the far end of it with a propriety so careful it was itself a kind of confession. "I'm asking you not to spend the hours before alone."

Xanthe lay down at the other end, boots off, coat still on, the manifest a hard rectangle against their ribs — and beneath it now, memorized clause by clause, the delay provision that could buy forty-eight hours for thousands, if it lived long enough to be spoken. They stared up through the darkness at the great lens turning overhead, and listened to Gideon breathe three feet away, and thought: *the war wants us to believe we are disposable. Tonight, refusing to believe it is the entire job.*

"Sing something," Gideon murmured, half asleep. "Quietly."

So Xanthe did — the market song, barely voiced, the rising third and falling fifth, a tune built for crowds reduced to one listener and a turning light. Somewhere past the middle of it they slept.

* * *

Dawn came grey and smoking. The seawall was gone; the fish market was gone; a chapel none of them attended stood roofless and improbably upright, like a man who has decided not to fall down today.

Xanthe stood at the lighthouse door pulling on their gloves, and Gideon stood beside them, and neither mentioned the night except in the way they stood — nearer than colleagues, farther than lovers, precisely calibrated to be deniable and undeniable at once.

"I'll find you before noon," Gideon said. "There's something I have to check at the registry. Something about the signing schedule."

Something in his voice made Xanthe turn. "What?"

"The signatory list changed overnight," Gideon said slowly. "One name removed. Added in different ink." He met Xanthe's eyes, and the tenderness of the night was still there, but beneath it now something cold and fast-moving. "Your handler's name is on it, Xanthe. Second from the bottom. Right above the line where the surrender gets signed."
