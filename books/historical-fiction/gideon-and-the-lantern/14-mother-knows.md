## 14. Mother Knows

The ceiling above her bed had become a country.

Sera Thornbury had mapped it the way she had once mapped coastlines as a girl — with patience, with attention, with the particular love one reserves for places no one else will ever visit. There was the water stain shaped like a swan over the eastern corner, fed by some pipe in the walls that wept when the temperature dropped. There was the hairline crack running from the light fixture to the window frame, branching each winter like a river delta. She knew every shadow it wore at every hour. In late afternoon, when the weak sun cleared the roofline of the chandler's across the lane and came through the frost-furred glass, the whole ceiling turned the color of old ivory, and she could pretend, briefly, that she was looking up at a sky instead of at the underside of a life.

She had been dying for three years. She had known it for two. The doctors had used gentler words — *a weakness of the heart*, *an inflammation that resists treatment* — but Sera had buried a husband and raised two children on the proceeds of knowing exactly what things were, and she did not require anyone's euphemisms now. Her lungs were filling by degrees, like a boat taking water too slowly to alarm the crew. Some mornings she woke surprised to find herself still moored. That surprise had curdled, months ago, into something quieter and more terrible: arithmetic. She counted her breaths the way she had once counted coins. She knew how many she had left the way a merchant knows his ledger is nearly closed — not to the last entry, but close enough to start putting things in order.

So she put things in order.

She had done it in the small ways first, the ways that could pass for an old woman's fussiness. She had Xanthe bring down the good linens from the cedar chest and fold them into the wardrobe where Tam could find them, telling him the chest's hinges wanted oiling, which was even true. She had written letters — seven of them, sealed, dated, addressed in her careful schoolroom hand — and hidden them inside the false bottom of her sewing basket, which neither of her children had ever thought to open because neither of them sewed. One was for Tam, to be read when he was old enough to ask what she had thought about all this. One was for Xanthe, to be read never, if she had any say in it, though she suspected she would not. The other five were for people who might need, someday, to be forgiven, or thanked, or reminded of debts the world's ledgers had lost track of. She had always believed that paper outlived intention. It was why she had married a courier's daughter to a republic, in a way — though she'd never told Xanthe that she'd seen it coming, that the day her child took the courier's oath at fourteen, all elbows and solemnity in a borrowed coat, Sera had gone home and wept into the dishwater, because she recognized the shape of the life being chosen. It was her husband's shape. And she had watched that shape walk out a door one October morning twenty-two years ago and not come back.

She understood everything. That was the cruelty and the comfort of a mind that stayed sharp while the body went: she understood everything, and could do nothing about most of it, and so had made a discipline of doing something about the rest.

She knew the coughing fits that emptied her in the small hours were worse this winter, and that the worsening was not only the illness. Coal ration cards had stopped meaning anything in December. The pharmacy on Cordwain Street had been stripped to its shelves — not looted, worse, *purchased*, by officers' wives with hard currency, before the officers themselves began leaving. She knew the medicine Xanthe brought home came from somewhere other than a pharmacy counter, because it arrived wrapped in waxed paper folded the way dispatches were folded, creased twice and tucked, and because Xanthe's hands had changed. Six years of carrying secrets will change a person's hands. They had grown still. Deliberate. When Xanthe set a cup of broth on the bedside table, they set it down the way a person lays a card they do not intend to show.

And she knew about the census man.

That knowledge had come to her the way most true things come to mothers: sideways, through the seams. She had heard Gideon Hale's voice through the floorboards the week he came to the door — a level, courteous voice, the kind she had learned to fear during the occupation of the eastern provinces in her own girlhood, because courteous men asking questions were never merely curious. She had heard the pause after his question about household members born outside the district. A pause like that has a texture. She had heard Xanthe answer it — smooth, warm, a little bored, a masterpiece — and she had lain in the dark with her hand pressed flat against her sternum and felt her heart do its uneven work, and had thought: *there. Now it begins.*

She did not know precisely what Xanthe carried. She had never asked, and Xanthe had never offered, and between those two silences there existed a marriage of tact that Sera considered among the great achievements of her life. But she could add. She had lived through the fall of the eastern provinces; she remembered the last month before the armistice there, the quality of the light in the streets, the way officials stopped meeting your eyes. Vessany had that light now. And her child walked through it every day carrying, whatever it was, the weight of it visible only in the set of their shoulders, in the way they slept sitting up in the chair beside her bed some nights rather than go down the hall where they couldn't hear her breathing.

The berth — well. The berth she had deduced more slowly, and less proudly, because the deduction required her to admit she had listened at more than floors. She had heard Xanthe and Gideon talking in the kitchen one night, low, the words worn soft by repetition, the way words get when two people have circled a thing for hours and finally sit down inside it. She heard *lantern*. She heard *one berth*, and *the signing*, and then a silence so long she thought they had left the room, and then Xanthe saying, in a voice Sera had not heard since they were nine years old and had broken their father's compass trying to fix it: *I don't know how to choose.*

Sera had turned her face to the wall and, for the first time since her husband died, allowed herself to cry without bothering to be quiet about it, because the house was empty except for the dying and the grieving, and neither much cares for decorum.

Afterward she had felt cleaner. That was the thing no one told you about grief done honestly — it aired the room.

Now, on a gray Tuesday with sleet ticking against the glass like a slow telegraph, she asked Xanthe to sit.

* * *

They came at once, of course. They always did. Her child — her tall, watchful, unreasonably brave child — crossed the sickroom the way they crossed the occupied city, without wasted motion, face arranged into mildness. They brought the basin for her morning washing without being asked. They had learned, somewhere in these months, to anticipate her, and she found she both loved and resented it: loved it as tenderness, resented it as rehearsal. People rehearse for endings.

"The slate needs wiping," she said, "and you've been avoiding my eyes for four days, which means either you've done something wicked or you're about to. Sit down and tell me which."

Xanthe laughed — a real one, startled out of them — and sat on the edge of the bed, and for a moment the courier fell away and there was just her gangly offspring, twenty years old and tired in a way twenty-year-olds should never learn the word for.

"I haven't done anything wicked," Xanthe said.

"You've decided something, though. Decisions sit on you the same way. Your father used to carry decisions like stones in his coat pocket; I could always tell when he'd made one because he'd stand in doorways deciding whether to tell me." She reached out and took their hand. It was cold. It was always cold now; the city had no coal and her child no gloves worth the name. "You've decided about the berth."

The change that came over Xanthe's face was small and total, like ice shifting on a pond. "Mother—"

"Hush. I've had weeks to think, and you've had days, so I'm ahead of you, and I intend to stay there; it's the only advantage left to me." Her voice was steady. She had practiced. She would not let it shake until the end, and maybe not even then. "Gideon's certificate. One passage out before the borders seal. You've spent four days trying to solve it like one of your ciphers — mother, brother, stranger — three lives, one seat, and whichever way you cut it you bleed. I know because I taught you arithmetic, and I know because I've watched you do sums at that table since before you could reach it standing."

"Tam," Xanthe said. It came out cracked. "It should be Tam. He's young, he's—"

"He's young," she agreed. "He's also been following that census man through the fish market for a week with all the subtlety of a church bell, which you may or may not know about, and which tells you exactly how safe he'd be on his own anywhere." She said it gently. She had decided to spend her capital freely now; hoarding was for people with time. "But it isn't going to be me, Xanthe. I want you to hear the whole thing before you argue, because you'll want to argue — you argue like your father, you open your mouth before your mind's finished loading — and I haven't the breath for a long debate. So."

Xanthe's jaw set. But they were a courier; they understood sealed orders. They waited.

"I am not taking the berth," she said, "because I am already going where the berth goes. Do you understand? Not today, perhaps not this month. But the boat leaves from the same shore, child, and mine is a shorter crossing. Every winter for three years I've known it. What I did not know — what I've been waiting to find out — was whether I'd have the grace to say so plainly while I still could. And I do. I have exactly enough breath left for honesty, and I mean to spend it better than I've spent anything in my life."

"Mother." Xanthe's hand had tightened on hers hard enough to hurt, and she let it hurt. "There are doctors across the corridor — the evacuation goes to Halverston, there's sanatoriums, there's—"

"There is air in Halverston, yes. And medicine, and clean sheets, and my son in a boarding house learning to eat alone at a table set for one." She smiled. She meant it to be wry and feared it came out tender, but perhaps tenderness was allowable; a mother is permitted one indulgence at her own funeral. "Listen to me. My life is spent. I don't say it as despair — I say it as a merchant says a ledger is balanced. I married a good man and lost him to a country that ate him and called it service. I raised two children in a house that leaked in the corners and never once in the middle, where it matters. I have seen a republic born and I am watching it die, and I find, examining the account, that I have had enough. More than enough. I have had *sufficiency*, which is more than most people ever get, and the great sin of the dying is to treat sufficiency like poverty."

"And if I'm wrong?" Xanthe whispered. "If you could cross, and live — five years, ten—"

"Then I would take five years in Halverston and spend them being afraid," she said. "Afraid in a clean room, with a window on a strange sea, wondering every hour what happened to my children in a city that was falling. You cannot cure what I have, Xanthe. Oh, the lungs, perhaps, for a while. But the rest of it — the part that has been leaving since the day your father didn't come home — no border crossing reaches that. And here is the thing I need you to carry, because you are the carrier in this family." She pulled herself up the pillow, and the effort cost her; she let them help, and hated needing it, and accepted it, because pride is cheap at the end and she had better currency to spend. "A choice like this isn't a loss. Everyone treats it like a loss — three lives, one seat, someone must be subtracted. But that's not the sum. The sum is: what does each life weigh, and where will it be spent best? Mine is nearly spent *here*. Spending it on a boat would be buying nothing. Tam's is barely begun and the world needs beginners desperately. Yours—" she stopped, and looked at her child for a long moment, at the ink stains faded almost to ghosts on their fingers, at the eyes that had learned stillness, at the mouth that still, when caught off guard, smiled exactly like their father. "Yours is the strange one. Yours is the one that could go either way, and that makes it precious beyond my reckoning. You can save a thousand people or one person or yourself, and I genuinely do not know which you'll choose, and I have made my peace with not knowing, because whatever you choose, you'll choose it awake. That's all any parent can make. Someone who chooses awake."

"I don't feel awake," Xanthe said. "I feel like I'm sleepwalking through the end of the world."

"Everyone at the end of the world feels that. The ones who tell you otherwise are selling something." She eased back against the pillows, breath rasping, and gestured toward the sewing basket on the dresser. "Bring me that. No — just the bottom tray. Lift it."

Xanthe hesitated, then did as they were told, and she watched their face as their fingers found the seam of the false bottom and lifted it, and found beneath it the bundle of letters and, laid across them, her wedding ring on its thin worn band.

"What's this?"

"Instructions," she said. "The ring goes to Tam, when you put him on the boat. Don't argue about the boat yet; I'm getting there. He'll pretend not to cry and then he'll cry, and you'll give him the ring and tell him it's from me and that I said—" her voice caught, just once, and she mastered it "—that I said he was the man of nowhere, that no one is the man of a house, that houses fall down, and that he should be the man of himself and let that be enough. Word for word. He'll hate it and keep it forever. Children keep the things that wound them cleanly."

"And the letters?"

"Debts and forgivenesses. You'll know when to use them, or you won't, and then burn them unread; they're not for the curious." She paused. "One of them is yours. Don't open it until—" she waved a hand, vague and merciless, "—until. You'll know the until when it arrives."

Xanthe sat holding the ring, turning it, and Sera watched the war happen behind their eyes — the courier wanting orders, the child wanting their mother, the citizen wanting absolution — and she reached up and touched their cheek, and they bent down slowly, like a drawbridge lowering, and let her.

"Here is the inheritance," she said into their hair. "Not the ring, not the letters. This: you were loved completely by somebody who had time to do it properly. Whatever happens in the next weeks — and things will happen, I've seen the light in the streets, I know what's coming — you start from that. Most people start from nothing and call it character. You start from a full account. Spend it boldly."

"Mother, I can't leave you."

"You aren't leaving me. You're staying, which is different and harder and exactly like you." She held them off at arm's length. "Now. The berth. Here is my ruling, and I'm your mother and I'm dying, which makes my rulings binding in every court that counts. The berth goes to Tam. Not because I matter less than he does — do not dare think it — but because I am finished and he is not, and because a woman should be permitted to complete her own work in her own house. My work was you two. Let me finish it here, in the bed where it was mostly done, with the swan-shaped stain over my head and my children within shouting distance. That is not sacrifice, Xanthe, whatever the ballads would call it if anyone ever sang about a woman named Sera Thornbury. It's just good bookkeeping."

Xanthe made a sound she had not heard since the funeral — a small, broken-off thing, half laugh, half something no one should have to hear their child make — and she gathered them in, arms thinner than they used to be but still, thank God, strong enough for this.

"The corridor," Xanthe said against her shoulder, muffled, wrecked, determined. "The code I'm carrying — there's a clause. If it reaches the right signatory before the signing, the armistice stalls. An evacuation corridor opens. Thousands, Mother. Not one berth. Thousands."

Sera went very still.

She had suspected her child carried something large. She had not imagined this. She lay back against the pillows and looked up at the ivory ceiling, at the swan-shaped stain, and felt — absurdly, indecently — like laughing, or singing, or standing up out of this bed that had held her for three years and walking to the window to see the street one more time. Her child. Her quiet, forgettable, invisible child, walking through the end of a republic with a thousand lives stitched into their coat lining, and calling it errands.

"Then you already know what you're going to do," she said. "You decided it before you came up the stairs. You just needed someone to tell you it was allowed."

"It could fail," Xanthe said. "The clause could fail, the signatory could refuse, and then I'll have spent the berth and the chance and gotten nothing."

"Then you'll have spent everything on a wager worthy of everything," she said. "Child, I have watched this city learn to look at its shoes for six years. If there's a chance — even a crooked, desperate, half-a-chance — to make ten thousand people look up instead, you take it, and you let the arithmetic be damned. That's the whole of the ethics I have to leave you. Love specifically. Spend boldly. Look up."

Outside, the sleet thickened against the glass, and somewhere down the lane a tram bell rang — the trams were still running, barely, stubbornly, the way everything here ran, on fumes and habit — and Sera Thornbury closed her eyes and listened to her child breathe beside her, and thought: *whatever comes, I got to see how it ends. How many people get that?*

"Send Tam up when he comes in," she said. "And Xanthe —"

They turned at the door. Frost on the glass behind them, light failing, ink-stained fingers, the borrowed coat, the whole improbable architecture of a person built out of her body and her husband's stubbornness and six years of secrets.

"When you deliver it," she said, "say it loudly."

Downstairs, the front door opened and closed, and boots came up the stairs two at a time, and Tam burst in smelling of scrap iron and river fog with news spilling out of him before he'd cleared the threshold — *the census man, the docks, Xanthe, you have to hear this* — and Sera Thornbury, listening, understood that the ending had begun arriving faster than anyone had planned.
