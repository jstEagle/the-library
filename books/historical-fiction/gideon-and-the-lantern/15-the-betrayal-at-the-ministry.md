## 15. The Betrayal at the Ministry

The summons came the way all summons came now: a slip of grey paper, no signature, only a time and an address. *Ministry of Continuance, Sub-level Two. Ninth hour.* The Ministry of Continuance — someone in the last government had a sense of irony that had outlived everything else, including, possibly, the government itself.

Xanthe dressed carefully that morning. Not well — well was a flag, and flags got you stopped at checkpoints — but *carefully*. The coat with the false lining stayed in its trunk; there was nothing left to sew into it. The final code lived now in three places: a sealed dispatch case chained to Xanthe's wrist, a copy locked in the handler's strongroom whose location Xanthe had never been told, and — though the handler did not know this, had never thought to ask, because couriers were instruments and instruments were not credited with memory — in Xanthe's head.

Nine years of carrying other people's words taught you things no one meant to teach. That seals could be forged, so you learned to read paper instead: weight, grain, the watermark's ghost when held against grey northern light. That orders changed, so you learned to listen at doorways without seeming to linger. And that the surest way to survive a document you could not afford to lose was to become one — to open the seal with steam in some stairwell, read the contents twice, close it so cleanly even the sender couldn't tell, and carry it thereafter in two places at once.

Clause Eleven of the armistice protocol was forty-one lines long. Xanthe had read it four times across three cities, once by candlelight in a customs house, twice in the back of a swaying mail coach, once in Mother's kitchen while Tam argued with a scrap merchant in the yard below. It was written in the turgid, self-important prose of men who believed they were ending history, and every line of it sat behind Xanthe's eyes like coins sewn into a lining.

*Sub-level Two. Ninth hour.*

They went down through the ministry's rear entrance, past sandbagged corridors where clerks who had once commanded fleets now burned files in washbasins. The air tasted of ash and wet stone. A guard checked the chain on Xanthe's wrist, found the seal unbroken, and waved them down a stair that grew colder with each step.

Handler Veyne was waiting at a table under a single caged bulb. He looked older than he had a week ago, which Xanthe would not have thought possible; the war had been eating him slowly for years and lately had picked up its pace. His coat hung on him. His hands, always immaculate, had ink on the knuckles.

"Courier." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Sit."

Xanthe sat. They set the dispatch case on the table between them, the chain still running from wrist to clasp, and waited. In the old days Veyne would have made conversation first — weather, rations, the particular quality of despair achievable only by mid-level civil servants. Today he said nothing at all, which told Xanthe more than anything he might have said.

"You understand what you've been carrying," Veyne said at last.

"I understand what I'm told to carry. It's safer that way. For both of us."

A thin smile touched Veyne's mouth and died there. "Yes. You were always the best of them. That's why it has to be you." He produced a key from his waistcoat, reached across, and unlocked the chain at Xanthe's wrist. The metal slid free and the sudden absence of its weight made Xanthe's arm feel like it might float away.

*It has to be you.* Xanthe turned the phrase over while Veyne broke the seal with a letter knife, unfolding the protocol with the tenderness of a man handling something already dead. *Has to be me for what?*

"The signing is tomorrow," Veyne said. "Eleven o'clock, the Hall of Concord, before anyone sensible has woken up. There will be no announcement. There will be no corridor. There will be an end, and then there will be order, and order is what survives people." He smoothed the pages flat. "You've done your part. The ministry will remember it."

"The clause," Xanthe said. "Clause Eleven."

Something flickered behind Veyne's eyes — surprise, quickly mastered, that a courier knew the number at all. "What about it?"

"It provides for a delay. Forty-eight hours, on petition of any signatory, to open an evacuation corridor." Xanthe kept their voice level, the voice they used at checkpoints, the voice that belonged to nobody. "I carried it. I'd like to know if it survived into the final text."

Veyne looked at them for a long moment. Then he did something Xanthe had never seen him do: he hesitated.

"You want the truth, courier? Very well. You've earned the truth, if nothing else." He drew a match from his pocket, struck it against the table's edge, and held it to the corner of the third page. "It didn't."

The paper caught. Flame walked up the page with terrible calm, browning the dense legal script to lace, and Veyne fed it to the next page, and the next, holding each until the fire took it, letting the ash fall into a tin basin that had clearly been set out for this purpose — that had been waiting, Xanthe understood, since before dawn.

"There was never going to be a corridor," Veyne said, watching the clauses curl and die. "The occupiers would never honor it, and asking would cost us concessions we can't spare. Better a clean surrender. Better mercy now than hope that gets ten thousand people shot on a frozen quay." He said it like a man reciting something he'd practiced in front of a mirror until it sounded like principle. "Clause Eleven was a vanity. Someone's conscience made flesh. Consciences are expensive this winter, and we are poor."

The basin filled with black feathers of ash. Xanthe watched Clause Eleven burn — the subsections, the provisos, the signature line reserved for the signatory who might have invoked it — and felt nothing move in their face, because nine years of invisibility is not a skill you lose in one morning, even now.

But underneath the stillness, something was turning over. Slowly. Like ice shifting on a river.

"You said it has to be me," Xanthe said. "Why?"

Veyne brushed ash from his cuff with two precise fingers. "Because you're discreet, you're loyal, and as of this moment you are the only person outside this room who knows the final text exists — which is now also false, since there is no final text. There is only the signing, and silence." He rose, took an envelope from the drawer beneath the caged bulb, and slid it across the table. "Your discharge. Full papers, exit stamp, valid until the border closes. Go home, courier. See to your mother. The republic thanks you, in whatever room they keep for such things."

Xanthe took the envelope. Their hands were steady. Their heart was not.

*He thinks I don't know it,* they thought, looking at the ash. *He thinks the words died with the paper. He thinks memory is a thing that belongs to ministries, filed and indexed and burned on command.*

Nine years. Four readings. Forty-one lines.

*Subsection Two: delay to be invoked upon oral petition of any signatory present, no written instrument required.*

They had argued about that subsection once, in a mail coach outside Drenmark, two ministry lawyers drunk on cold tea and their own importance, debating whether *oral* petition meant spoken aloud or merely unwitnessed, and whether a signatory could be coerced into silence after invoking it, and Xanthe, half-asleep against the window with a sealed case on their lap, had listened to every word because listening was the job, listening was the whole of the job, and no one had ever once thought to tell a courier to forget.

"I'll go home," Xanthe said. "See to my mother."

"Good girl," Veyne said, and then, catching himself — Xanthe had never given him a pronoun to work with, none of them ever had — "Good courier."

* * *

The stairs up from Sub-level Two were narrow and smelled of lamp oil. Halfway up, Xanthe stopped and stood very still in the dark, one hand on the cold stone, and let themselves feel it — all of it, all at once, because here in the dark there was no face to keep smooth.

Fury came first, and it was not hot. That was the strange thing. It wasn't the fury of the betrayed; it was the fury of the accountant finding the ledger cooked — cold, exact, itemized. *Item: one clause, forty-one lines, burned. Item: one corridor, withheld. Item: ten thousand names, unwritten, because better mercy now.* Item: a man who had preached duty to couriers for nine years lighting a match over it and calling the smoke *order*.

And under the fury, quieter, worse: relief. Shameful, animal relief at the envelope in their pocket, the exit stamp, the door standing open — and Xanthe stood in the stairwell and made themselves look at that too, because a courier who lied to everyone else ought at minimum not to lie to herself. She wanted to live. She wanted Tam to live. Somewhere in her chest the berth Gideon had shown her still glowed like coal, and Mother's face was on it, and she had spent three nights doing arithmetic that ended in her mother's lungs every time.

But Veyne was right about one thing, and Xanthe hated him for it: hope that fails on a frozen quay kills more people than despair ever does. If she was going to spend the clause in her head, she could not spend it like a gambler. It had to be spent like a courier — verified, timed, delivered to the one pair of hands that could use it.

*Any signatory present. No written instrument required.*

Signatories. She ran the list the way she'd run dispatch routes, over and over until the order was worn smooth. Presidio Corvane, who had resigned rather than sign the grain levy — gone east months ago. Minister Dulac, dead of his own hand before the first shell fell. General Aris Voss.

Voss.

The name landed like a dropped lantern. Voss, who had fought the handler's ministry for two years over convoy routes; Voss, whom Veyne had once described, over brandy Xanthe had pretended not to hear, as *"that sanctimonious bastard who thinks paper is sacred"*; Voss, who was scheduled — she had memorized the seating addendum along with everything else, because seating addenda told you who mattered and therefore who to watch — to sit at the signing table tomorrow at eleven o'clock, directly across from Veyne, with a pen in his hand and, by the letter of Clause Eleven, subsection two, the power to stand up and speak forty-eight hours into existence.

*No written instrument required.* Because the men who drafted it had trusted speech. Had believed, in the early bright days when the republic still thought it deserved itself, that a voice raised in a room full of witnesses was stronger than any seal.

She had a voice. She had never once used it for anything but vanishing.

Xanthe climbed the rest of the stairs.

* * *

Above ground, the city had gone quiet in the way it did before barrages, an held-breath hush, snow falling fat and slow through the smoke haze. Xanthe walked home the long way, past the bread line already forming in the dark, past shuttered shops with chalked prices no one believed anymore. At the corner of the square she stopped. The lighthouse stood against the grey sky like a held breath of its own, and behind it, small in the distance, a figure was watching the ministry doors — tall, still, census book under his arm, pretending with great diligence to be exactly what his badge said he was.

Gideon. Who had covered for Tam in the market with a lie smooth enough that Xanthe still didn't know its full shape. Who had shown her the lantern and the berth inside it and said, in a voice stripped of all his careful officialdom, *I took this posting to put it in worthy hands.*

She thought: *I could take Tam to the dock tonight. I could spend the berth and the envelope and be on the water by morning, and the clause would die in my head the way it died in Veyne's basin, and it would be mercy, it would be clean, it would be my mother breathing sea air by spring.*

She thought: *Forty-one lines.*

She thought of Mother's hands, folded on the blanket, steady in a way hers had never been. *My life is spent, child. Yours is not — and neither, I think, is your work.* Mother knew. Mothers always knew. She had looked at Xanthe two nights ago with those tired, terrible, clear eyes and said *you didn't come home from the ministry like a woman finished; you came home like a fuse,* and Xanthe had not denied it, because denying it to Mother would have been the one lie too far.

The snow thickened. Across the square, Gideon turned and, feeling her look, or the absence of her look, or whatever it was that had grown between two liars that needed no semaphore — he found her anyway, the way he always did, and lifted one hand slightly off his book. *Are you all right?* the gesture said.

Not yet, Xanthe mouthed, and pointed two fingers toward the lighthouse, and saw him understand: tonight. There were things he needed to know — the hour, the hall, the name Voss — and things she needed from him, because Clause Eleven required a signatory present and getting a courier into the Hall of Concord tomorrow required forged credentials, and there was no one alive better at making paper lie than a census man who had already forged one pass.

She walked home through the falling snow with the discharge envelope in her pocket like a coin she had decided not to spend.

In the kitchen, Tam looked up from a disassembled stove element spread across the table, saw her face, and set down his wrench. "Xanthe?"

"Pack a bag," Xanthe said. "Small. Everything you can't bear to lose goes in your coat, not the bag." She hung her wet outerwear and stood a moment with her hands open over the stove's weak warmth, feeling the last hours of being invisible drain out of her like heat. "By tomorrow night, one way or another, you're going to be on a boat. And I have an errand first."

"What kind of errand?"

"The kind they write clauses about." Xanthe looked at her brother — fourteen, scrap-savvy, furious, alive — and gave him the truth because he'd earned it and because tomorrow might not offer another chance. "I'm going to stop them signing the country away quietly. And then, Tam—" Her voice caught, and she let it. "Then I'm going to bring Mother down to the water myself, and we're all going to argue about berths one last time."

Outside, the snow kept falling on the ministry, on the hall where pens waited, on the ash Veyne had mistaken for the end of the matter — and in the head of one courier, forty-one lines burned brighter than any paper ever had.
