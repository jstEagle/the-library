## 16. Hunted Through the Snow

**Xanthe**

The knock came at half past three in the morning, three soft raps, the rhythm of a neighbor, and Xanthe knew before their feet touched the floor that it was not a neighbor. Neighbors in Vessany no longer knocked at half past three. Neighbors in Vessany had learned to sleep through everything.

They dressed in the dark, sewn coat over their shoulders, and lifted the floorboard in the pantry where Tam slept when the shelling came close. His eyes were open already. He was fourteen and had not properly slept in a month.

"Back stair," Xanthe whispered. "Mrs. Ovett's cellar. Don't come out for anyone wearing a uniform, and don't come out for anyone who isn't."

"Where will you be?"

"Being forgettable."

Tam's jaw set in a way that was their father's jaw, their father exactly, and Xanthe pressed two fingers briefly against it — the whole goodbye, the whole speech, compressed into a touch — and went out through the kitchen while the knocking came again, patient as snowfall.

The back stair was iced. Xanthe took it fast, hand on the wall, and dropped into the alley where the snow had drifted knee-deep against the coal shed. Behind them, above, the front door opened. Lamplight spilled down the steps. A voice, polite, unhurried, asked the person who answered — Mrs. Ovett, God keep her — whether anyone had gone out the back in the last quarter hour.

Xanthe did not wait for the answer. They went over the shed roof, along the wall, and dropped into the next street, where they became immediately, deliberately, no one: shoulders rounded, hands in pockets, a figure trudging toward the water queues like ten thousand other figures. The coat lining lay flat against their ribs. The code lived in their head now anyway — the handler had burned the paper, but paper had never been the true vessel — and still they carried the coat, because the coat was the last piece of the person they had been, and because a courier without a coat was a confession.

They had three hours until dawn. The armistice signed at nine. And somewhere behind them in the dark, two kinds of men were moving through the snow: the handler's, who wanted the clause in their head to die there, and the occupiers', who wanted it never to have existed at all.

**Gideon**

Gideon Hale had spent eleven years learning the city by its doors, and in one night he learned it by its windows instead — which were lit, which were dark, which had been dark for weeks and had gone suddenly bright with men inside them.

He sat in the census office with the lamps hooded and the ledgers open around him like a fortress of paper, and he built a person.

It was the work he was made for, though he had never thought of it that way. A census taker knew how a person was assembled: name, age, trade, district of birth, the number of children, the color of the door. He knew which records the occupiers had copied and which they hadn't — they had taken the district registers in the fall, all except the outhouses annex, which the clerk Voss had misfiled under *Ecclesiastical* and which Gideon had quietly declined to correct.

So the person he built would live in the outhouses annex.

He wrote the name in his own hand, in the ink the office used, with the slight rightward lean the office pens gave. *Merren Vail, widow, seamstress, born Corren's Ford, resident of the Weavers' Row annex since the second winter.* A real enough person. There had been a Merren Vail; she had died of the fever in October, and her page was blank below the death entry, and blank pages were the one honest currency left in Vessany.

He built her a trail. That was the harder craft. A person fleeing through a city left traces whether they meant to or not, and a hunter read traces the way Gideon read forms — by what was missing. So he made sure Merren Vail was missing in the right places. He marked her present at the Weavers' Row bread line on the fourteenth. He entered a ration chit issued at the Tanner's Gate station, torn out and stub retained. He noted, in the margin, in pencil, the kind of marginal observation a tired clerk makes: *says her sister keeps a room near the salt market — to verify.*

The salt market. Which would put any searcher, at four in the morning, walking away from the river district, away from the lighthouse, away from the signing hall, into a maze of collapsed tanneries where a hundred people could stand behind a wall and be no one at all.

He was blotting the page when the door below opened without a knock, and he knew the tread. Voss. The old clerk came up the stairs slowly, carrying a candle, and stood in the doorway looking at the ledgers spread everywhere, at the hooded lamps, at Gideon's face.

"They came for the Thornbury one," Voss said. "Two of ours and two of theirs. The house was empty."

"I heard nothing."

"You heard everything. You always do." Voss set the candle down. His hands were trembling, but his voice was not; he had spent forty years being unafraid of the wrong things, and had finally, in old age, gotten the list right. "The annex records, Hale. If they ask for the annex records—"

"Then they will find a seamstress named Merren Vail who died in October and whose ration chit says she was at Tanner's Gate on the fourteenth, and while they argue about which of those things is true, a courier will be walking through the tanneries." Gideon closed the ledger. "You misfiled it yourself, Voss. Under *Ecclesiastical.* I've always admired that."

Voss looked at him a long moment. "Your own exit papers," he said. "You have them still?"

"In a lantern."

"Then God keep the lantern lit." The old man took his candle and went down, and Gideon sat in the hooded dark with a dead woman's name in his hand and a living one in his heart, and began, methodically, to build a second trail — because the hunters would not all be fools, and fools were only the first wave.

* * *

**Xanthe**

The tanneries stank even in winter, a sweet-rotten ghost of themselves soaked into the brick, and the snow fell into the open roofs and lay unmarked on floors where no one had walked in months. Xanthe moved through them by the memory of a route Tam had drawn once in the ash of the hearth — the boy mapped everything, the boy had mapped the whole city in ash and never known he was drawing his sibling's escape — and came out at the salt market an hour before dawn with their feet soaked and their heart going like a fist on a door.

Gideon was there. He was standing at the mouth of Cooper's Lane under a lamp that had gone out, in a census taker's coat with the collar up, and when he saw Xanthe he did not wave or call out. He simply turned and walked, and Xanthe followed at the distance of strangers, and that — the ease of it, the fluency of it — was the most frightening and most steadying thing that had happened all night.

He led them down Cooper's Lane, through the arch of the old salt warehouse, into a cellar where blocks of salt were stacked like altars and a single storm lantern burned behind a curtain of sacking. Only when the sacking was drawn did he speak.

"They were at your house at half three."

"You watched."

"I watch everything. It's the trade." He crouched by the lantern, warming his hands, and looked up. "The handler's men took the west approach. The occupiers took the river road. They'll meet in the middle by six, and the middle is wherever you're standing. So from now on, you're not standing anywhere."

"I have until nine."

"You have until nine, and then you have to be inside a room with locked doors and a table, and the distance between here and there is the whole war." He pulled a folded paper from inside his coat — a ration chit, a real one, issued at Tanner's Gate — and a second paper behind it. "From this moment you are Merren Vail. Widow. Seamstress. If anyone asks, your sister keeps a room near the salt market and you're hiding from a husband's family who want your loom. Grief and cloth. No one in this city asks a second question about grief and cloth."

Xanthe took the papers. Their hands were shaking — not from the cold, or not only. "You built me out of a dead woman."

"Out of a woman who died of fever with no family to claim her page. It's the only honest forgery there is. I didn't invent her. I just — continued her." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't match his voice, and Xanthe understood suddenly what it cost him, the man of records, to make the records lie. He had spent his whole war being accurate. This was his treason, and he had committed it in his own handwriting.

"Gideon." They said his name the way you test ice. "If they catch you at this—"

"They'll catch *you* at it first, so let's not spend our fear on me." He stood. "The signing is at the customs hall. There's a courier entrance on the north side, for dispatch riders. Your old coat gets you to the door; your face gets you no further, because the handler will have your face on every clipboard by now. But Merren Vail delivering a widow's petition—" he almost smiled, "—petitions are still legal. Barely. It's the one door they haven't thought to nail shut, because no one has used it in a month."

"And once I'm through the door?"

"Once you're through the door, you're the only person in Vessany who can do what you're about to do, and I can't help you at all." He paused. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above, distant, the artillery had gone quiet, which was worse than the noise; the quiet meant the shelling had moved to the river, meant the corridor, meant the boats. "So I'll do the part I can. I'll be the man who misplaces you. Every record between here and that door will say you went east. Every ledger I can touch will point east, and I will stand in the office and be accurate about everything except you."

"Why?" Xanthe asked it plainly, because there was no time left for anything else. "You could tear up the trail and be safe. One word to the occupiers and you're on a boat with your papers and your pension and your eleven years of doors."

Gideon looked at them across the lantern, and the light moved on his face, and he did not give the answer Xanthe expected, the one from the lighthouse, the one about debts and worthy hands. He gave a smaller one, which was therefore larger.

"Because I've counted this city four times," he said. "Door by door. Name by name. I know how many people are in it. And I know that every one of them is in my handwriting somewhere — that I am, in a way that I can't explain to anyone who hasn't done it, made of them. I'm not going to hand over the one who got out of the ledger." He shouldered his satchel. "Come. The tanneries again, then the petition line. And Xanthe — walk like a widow. Slower. Widows have nowhere to be and everywhere to grieve."

* * *

**Xanthe**

They nearly died at the Tanner's Gate bridge.

It was nothing dramatic — that was the horror of it. A checkpoint of four men, three in occupier grey and one in the handler's ministry black, checking papers by lamplight with the snow coming down through the lamp's halo like static. The line moved. Xanthe stood in it as Merren Vail, hands folded, face slack with the particular exhaustion of grief, and let the cold make their nose run and their shoulders shake, because a weeping widow was a bore, and bores were waved through.

The man in black was checking faces, not papers. Xanthe felt his gaze go down the line and arrive, and pass, and — return.

"Name?"

"Merren Vail." Steady. The voice they used for dispatches, pitched down, roughened.

"Purpose at this hour?"

"My sister. Salt market. She lost her son in the fall and she doesn't—" and here Xanthe did the thing they had never done in eleven years of courier work, the thing that was the opposite of every discipline they owned: they let the truth out. "She doesn't eat unless someone sits with her. I've been walking across this city all night and I'm so tired that if you arrest me I'll probably thank you."

A silence. The lamp hissed. The man in black looked at the ration chit, at the face, at the chit again — and it was Gideon's chit, Gideon's ink, Gideon's careful dead woman — and then the grey-coat beside him said something bored in the occupiers' tongue, and the moment broke like ice under a boot, and the line moved, and Xanthe walked across the bridge with their heart in their throat and their feet in the snow and the whole clause of the armistice reciting itself behind their teeth like a rosary.

Gideon was waiting on the far side, at the mouth of the petition queue, being no one, a man with a clipboard and a cold. He fell into step without looking.

"The bridge?"

"The bridge."

"And?"

"I told the truth."

He glanced at them then, quickly, and something crossed his face that was not quite a smile — recognition, maybe, of a fellow craftsman seeing a new technique. "Did it work?"

"It worked because it was true. They're not looking for a tired widow, Gideon. They're looking for a courier, and a courier would have lied beautifully."

They walked. The queue for petitions was already forming outside the customs hall, a ragged hundred strong, holding their papers against their chests like icons, and the sky in the east had gone the grey of old dishwater, which meant an hour, which meant less. Gideon stopped at the queue's edge and handed Xanthe the last thing: a folded petition, a real one, a widow's plea for a dead son's back pay, in case anyone read past the first line.

"Once you're inside," he said, "I go back and burn Merren Vail out of the record before they can interrogate the paper. And then I—" he stopped. For the first time all night, the census taker did not know what came next in the form.

Xanthe took his hand. Bare hand, in the open, in the queue, in the snow — the most reckless thing either of them had done since the war began, and the easiest.

"If the clause holds," they said, "there'll be a corridor. And if there's a corridor, there'll be boats. And if there are boats—" they could not finish it; the arithmetic of it, the mother of it, the berth of it, stood in their throat.

"Then I'll be at the dock," Gideon said, "being accurate about who's on it. Go. Walk like a widow."

Xanthe walked like a widow, up the queue, toward the courier door, into the grey light of the last morning of the Republic of Vessany — and behind them, at the edge of the square, a man in ministry black turned away from a checkpoint and began, unhurried, to follow.
